## 1. Life

### Family, name, education
- Names: Bento (Portuguese), Baruch (Hebrew), Benedictus (Latin); "all three names mean 'blessed'". [source: SEP-S §1; WP-Baruch_Spinoza (Bento = boyhood/business name, Baruch = synagogue name, Benedictus = signature in correspondence)]
- Born **24 November 1632** in Amsterdam (SEP/IEP give only "1632"). Third child of Michael (d'Espinosa), a merchant, and Hannah Deborah, Michael's second wife; the family lived on the Vlooienburg island, near Rembrandt among others. [source: WP-Baruch_Spinoza (citing Nadler 1999, Israel 2023); IEP-S §1]
- The community was Portuguese-Jewish, of converso ("Marrano") stock: families coerced into Christianity in Iberia, who had kept a private Jewish identity; after the Portuguese Inquisition (est. 1536) many emigrated, some to Amsterdam, where they re-established themselves as Jews. [source: IEP-Rel §1; IEP-S §1]
- Mother died in 1638, just before he turned six. [source: IEP-S §1; WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- Uriel da Costa, related through his mother, was excommunicated twice by the community for denying the soul's immortality and the Torah's divine origin; in 1639/40 he was readmitted after prostrating himself so congregants stepped over him, and took his own life in 1640, when Spinoza was eight. [source: SEP-Pol §1.1; WP-Baruch_Spinoza; WP-Uriel_da_Costa]
- Educated at the congregation's Talmud Torah school (Hebrew, liturgy, Torah, commentaries incl. Rashi); he did **not** reach the upper levels devoted to Talmud, which trained rabbis. IEP-Rel: he certainly completed the first four levels, probably by about 14; whether he went further is disputed; some evidence he attended Rabbi Saul Levi Mortera's yeshiva Keter Torah. Possibly groomed for the rabbinate (SEP: "possible"). Left formal study (SEP: at 17) to work in the family importing business. [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-S §1; IEP-Rel §1; WP-Baruch_Spinoza] WRITER: say "left school in his teens to work in the family firm".
- Father died 1654; Spinoza ran the firm with his brother Gabriel. The business was ruined, partly by English seizures of cargo in the First Anglo-Dutch War. In March 1656 he had the city declare him an orphan (a legal minor) to escape his father's debts, breaking the synagogue rule that disputes be settled inside the community. [source: WP-Baruch_Spinoza (citing Nadler, Israel); IEP-Rel §1]
- Studied Latin with Franciscus van den Enden, an ex-Jesuit, physician, reputed freethinker and radical democrat, sometime between 1654 and 1657; through such circles (Collegiants and other dissenting Protestants) he met Descartes's philosophy. Around 1658 he audited classes at Leiden University. [source: IEP-S §1; WP-Baruch_Spinoza]

### The herem (27 July 1656)
- On **27 July 1656** (6 Av 5416) the leaders of the Talmud Torah congregation issued against him "the harshest writ of herem, ban or excommunication, ever pronounced by the Sephardic community of Amsterdam; it was never rescinded". He was 23. [source: SEP-S §1; WP-Baruch_Spinoza; IEP-Rel §1]
- **The reasons are not stated.** The writ cites only his "abominable heresies" and "monstrous deeds" (and testimony of witnesses) "but gives no specifics"; "There is no first-hand information from this period, and Spinoza does not address the incident in his surviving correspondence." [source: IEP-Rel §1; IEP-S §1; SEP-S §1; WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- Debate on the reasons: Nadler (SEP) says "an educated guess comes quite easy": he was probably voicing the views of his later works (denial of the soul's immortality, rejection of a transcendent providential God, denial that the Law was literally given by God or still binding). IEP-Rel warns "it is impossible to know how much of what they contain was in his mind at the time". IEP-S adds "perhaps, laxity in his observance". Other factors in the scholarship: the 1656 debt case breaking community rules; the community's fear of upsetting the Dutch authorities (the town council had told it to enforce observance of Jewish law). [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-Rel §1; IEP-S §1; WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- The writ curses him and forbids anyone to communicate with him, do business with him, read anything he writes, or come close to him. [source: IEP-S §1]
- The formula was not composed for him: Kasher and Biderman note that the Hebrew core of the text was a known formula, a Spanish translation of which had been brought to Amsterdam from Venice in 1618. The original writ is in Portuguese. [source: WS-1/WS-2 summarising Kasher & Biderman, https://www.tau.ac.il/~kasher/pspin.htm — page not opened]
- Never rescinded: in 2015 the Amsterdam congregation held a symposium on lifting it, but its rabbi (Haham Pinchas Toledano had earlier declined) ruled that it stands. [source: WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- He did not convert to Christianity or join any sect afterwards. A lost Spanish *Apologia* defending his views may have been written. [source: WP-Baruch_Spinoza]

### Lens-grinding, residences, works
- After 1656 he lived in Amsterdam and Leiden, supporting himself by teaching and learning lens-grinding; he made lenses, microscopes and telescopes, and Christiaan Huygens and others praised his lenses. He also depended on friends' gifts. [source: IEP-S §1; WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- **Rijnsburg** (near Leiden): by 1661, when his surviving letters begin (WP: moved 1660–61). There he wrote the *Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect* (unfinished) and the *Short Treatise on God, Man and His Well-Being* (circulated privately; survives only in Dutch; first published 1862). [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-S §1; WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- **Voorburg**, near The Hague, 1663. [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-S §1]
- **1663: *Principles of Descartes' Philosophy*** (full title per IEP: *René Descartes's Principles of Philosophy, Parts I and II, Demonstrated According to the Geometric Method by Benedict de Spinoza of Amsterdam*), with an appendix, *Metaphysical Thoughts*, and a preface by his friend Lodewijk Meyer warning that it was exposition, not endorsement. It grew out of lessons to a student. SEP and IEP: "the only work he published under his own name in his lifetime". [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-S §1]
- The *Ethics* was begun in the early 1660s; by 1665 parts circulated in draft among Amsterdam friends; he set it aside for the TTP. [source: IEP-S §1; SEP-S §1]
- **The Hague**, 1670 until death. [source: IEP-S §1; WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- Letters: none survive from before 1661; the editors of the *Opera Posthuma* removed personal matter. Correspondents included Henry Oldenburg (Secretary of the Royal Society), Huygens, Hudde, Willem van Blijenbergh (on evil). His seal bore *Caute* ("Caution") and a rose. [source: WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- In 1676 Leibniz visited him in The Hague. [source: WP-Baruch_Spinoza]

### The Theological-Political Treatise and its fate
- Context: Koerbagh's imprisonment for blasphemy (he had published, in Dutch and under his own name, views like Spinoza's) and death in prison in 1669 is "generally supposed" to have precipitated publication. [source: SEP-Pol §1.1]
- Published **anonymously in 1670**, in Latin, by Jan Rieuwertsz in Amsterdam with a false imprint ("Hamburg", publisher "Henricus Künraht"); Spinoza blocked a Dutch translation. [source: SEP-S §1; WP-Tractatus_Theologico-Politicus; WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- Reception: "published anonymously and to great alarm"; one critic called it "a book forged in hell by the devil himself" (SEP; WP-TTP gives "forged in hell by the apostate Jew working together with the devil"). Jakob Thomasius (Leibniz's teacher) attacked it in 1670; Velthuysen denounced it as the work of a dangerous atheist (Epp. 42–43). It earned Spinoza "a reputation for atheism, something he greatly resented". [source: SEP-S §1; SEP-Pol §1.2, §6; IEP-S §1; WP-TTP]
- Condemned by the Reformed Church's synod (WP: Dordrecht, 1673) and **officially banned in 1674**. [source: WP-Tractatus_Theologico-Politicus]
- **Heidelberg, 1673**: J. Louis Fabritius, writing for the Elector Palatine Karl Ludwig (letter dated 16 Feb. 1673), offered him the chair of philosophy, with freedom to philosophize provided he did not disturb the established religion. Spinoza's reply (Letter 48, 30 March 1673) said he had never wished to teach publicly, that teaching would take time from his own work, and that he did not know the limits of a freedom that must not disturb the public religion; he declined. [source: WS-7; WP-Baruch_Spinoza ("perhaps because ... it might curb his freedom of thought")]

### The de Witt murder, 1672
- 1672, the *rampjaar* ("disaster year"): French invasion; William III made stadholder; Grand Pensionary Johan de Witt resigned and, with his brother Cornelis, was killed by a mob. Spinoza admired de Witt. "According to one famous account, Spinoza had to be restrained by his landlord from taking a sign reading *ultimi barbarorum* ['ultimate of barbarians'] to the site of the massacre (Freudenthal 1899: 201)." The *Political Treatise* was written in the aftermath. [source: SEP-Pol §1.1; IEP-S §1] WRITER: present as an anecdote from one later report ("according to one account"), not as documented fact; Freudenthal 1899 is a collection of early biographical documents.

### Last years, death and posthumous works
- *Ethics* complete by 1675; he decided not to publish it. [source: IEP-S §1; WP-Spinoza's_Ethics ("written between 1661 and 1675")]
- At The Hague he worked on a *Compendium of Hebrew Grammar* (unfinished) and the *Political Treatise*. [source: SEP-S §1; WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- Died **21 February 1677** at The Hague, aged 44, "weakened by a respiratory illness" (IEP). [source: SEP-Pol §4.3.3; WP-Baruch_Spinoza; IEP-S §1]
- Cause of death: WP (citing Gullan-Whur 1998): "some form of lung affliction, probably tuberculosis and possibly complicated by silicosis brought on by grinding glass lenses". No contemporary diagnosis was found; the lens-dust/silicosis explanation is a later inference, always hedged. [source: WP-Baruch_Spinoza; IEP-S §1; WS-10/11 found only hedged popular repetitions] WRITER: "He died of a lung disease, probably tuberculosis; it is often suggested, without proof, that glass dust from lens-grinding made it worse."
- Buried in the Nieuwe Kerk, The Hague. [source: WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- He left no will; the manuscripts in his desk were sent to Amsterdam and published as ***Opera Posthuma*** (Latin; "B.D.S. Opera Posthuma") in 1677, with a Dutch version, *De Nagelate Schriften*; editors included Jarig Jelles and Lodewijk Meyer (WP also names Schuller and Bouwmeester for the letters); printer Rieuwertsz. One source (Spinoza Web) puts the release in the first weeks of 1678. [source: IEP-S §1; WP-Baruch_Spinoza; WS-15] (IEP misprints "Opus Posthuma"; the title is *Opera Posthuma*.)
- The posthumous works were banned throughout Holland in 1678 (WP: June 1678, the States of Holland, because they "contain very many profane, blasphemous and atheistic propositions"); they were also placed on the Catholic Index. [source: IEP-S §1; WP-Spinoza's_Ethics]

## 2. The geometrical method
- Full title *Ethica, ordine geometrico demonstrata* (*Ethics, Demonstrated in Geometrical Order*); written in Latin; five parts, each built like Euclid's *Elements*: definitions, axioms, propositions, demonstrations, scholia, corollaries. [source: IEP-S §2; WP-Spinoza's_Ethics]
- Part titles verified: I "On God" (SEP-S §2.1); II "On the Nature and Origin of the Mind" (*De Natura & Origine Mentis*, 1677 title page, WP image); IV "On Human Bondage, or the Powers of the Affects" (SEP-S §2.4). (Parts III and V titles not checked.)
- Why the method: IEP-S distinguishes Descartes's two methods (*Second Replies*, CSM II 110–11): analysis (the way of discovery, used in the *Meditations*) and synthesis (definitions, axioms, theorems, compelling assent); the *Ethics* uses synthesis because, starting from God, it shows how all things follow from God, and following that order approximates the knowledge in which happiness lies. IEP-M: some explain it by the 17th-century prestige of mathematics (Hobbes too tried to argue "geometrically"); others by its therapeutic aim: to "lead us, by the hand, as it were, to the knowledge of the human mind and its highest blessedness" (Ethics II preface). [source: IEP-S §2; IEP-M §1]

## 3. Substance monism (the chapter's main ARGUMENT)
Key definitions (Curley, as quoted in IEP-S §3a):
- I D3: "By substance I understand what is in itself and is conceived through itself, that is, that whose concept does not require the concept of another thing, from which it must be formed."
- I D4: "By attribute I understand what the intellect perceives of a substance, as constituting its essence."
- I D5: "By mode I understand the affections of a substance, or that which is in another through which it is also conceived."
- I D6: "By God I understand a being absolutely infinite, that is, a substance consisting of an infinity of attributes, of which each one expresses an eternal and infinite essence."
- Axiom I A1: "Whatever is, is either in itself or in another." [source: SEP-S §2.1]

The argument, step by step (IEP-M §3; IEP-S §3a; SEP-S §2.1):
1. Every substance has at least one attribute (an essence) (I D4). (Uncontroversial.)
2. **I P5**: "In Nature there cannot be two or more substances of the same nature or attribute."
   Sub-argument for P5 (IEP-M): (a) two substances would differ either in attributes or in modes (I P4); (b) a substance is prior in nature to its modes (I P1), so set the modes aside and consider the substances in themselves; (c) if they differ only in modes and share an attribute, then considered in themselves they are indistinguishable; (d) indistinguishable substances are identical; so no two substances share an attribute.
3. God has all possible attributes (I D6).
4. **I P11**: God "necessarily exists". Route: substances cannot produce one another (I P6), so a substance must be self-caused, i.e. its essence involves existence (I P7); hence God, a substance of infinite attributes, exists. Spinoza's demonstration: "if you deny this, conceive, if you can, that God does not exist. Therefore, by axiom 7, his essence does not involve existence. But this, by proposition 7, is absurd." IEP-M: unlike Anselm's and Descartes's versions, it does not reason from our *idea* of God and does not treat existence as a perfection.
5. Conclusion **I P14**: "Except God, no substance can be or be conceived." Any second substance would have some attribute, which God already has; by P5 two substances cannot share an attribute.
6. So **I P15**: "Whatever is, is in God, and nothing can be or be conceived without God." Everything else is a mode of God. [sources: IEP-M §3; IEP-S §3a; SEP-S §2.1]
- Significance: a break with the substance pluralism of almost all Western philosophers, including Descartes (many minds and bodies, plus God), and a rejection of classical theism (a creator distinct from the world). [source: IEP-S §3a]

Main objection (to P5): IEP-M §3a: "Spinoza's Argument for Substance Monism is generally deemed a failure by contemporary philosophers"; the most common attack is on P5. **Leibniz's objection**: two substances could not share *all* attributes, but could share one and differ by another (substance 1 has A and B, substance 2 has A and C), so they would be distinguishable yet share A. IEP-M says Leibniz first presented this "to Spinoza himself", who "did not find the objection persuasive" but "never offered an explicit reply".
Reply on Spinoza's behalf: Della Rocca (2002) argues from the **conceptual independence of attributes** (each attribute "must be conceived through itself", I P10): a shared attribute A would have to be distinguished by reference to B or C, which would make A conceived through another attribute, against P10. [source: IEP-M §3a (cites "Della Rocca 2002: 17–22 for a plausible solution ... based upon the conceptual independence of the attributes")] WRITER: state the reply in this general form.
- A related contemporary objection (Simon de Vries): Spinoza never shows that one substance can have several attributes; if each substance had one attribute, two attributes would mean two substances. "Spinoza did not clarify the issue in his response to de Vries." [source: WP-Spinoza's_Ethics, "Criticism"]
- Bayle (Dictionary, 1697) attacked Spinoza's notions of substance, mode and God at length; scholars (Labrousse) have judged that his criticisms "combat a phantom". [source: WS-12 (secondary summaries only); WP-Baruch_Spinoza for the 1697 Dictionary] (Bayle's specific examples: see NOT VERIFIED.)

## 4. God or Nature; naturans/naturata; attributes and modes
- **"Deus, sive Natura"**: SEP-S: the phrase "occurs in the Latin (but not the original Dutch) edition of the Ethics": "That eternal and infinite being we call God, or Nature, acts from the same necessity from which he exists" (**Part IV, Preface**). The friends who published the works left out "or Nature" in the Dutch version, "probably out of fear of the reaction". WP-Ethics: "God or Nature" appears **four times** in the *Ethics*. Also TTP ch. 3: "Whether we say ... that all things happen according to the laws of nature, or are ordered by the decree and direction of God, we say the same thing." [source: SEP-S §2.1; WP-Spinoza's_Ethics] (IV P4 dem. location: see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Ambiguity: the phrase can be read "as trying either to divinize nature or to naturalize God"; Nadler: "for the careful reader there is no mistaking Spinoza's intention" (naturalize God). [source: SEP-S §2.1]
- *Natura naturans* ("naturing Nature": God and his attributes, the active, productive side) and *Natura naturata* ("natured Nature": "whatever follows from the necessity of God's nature ... all the modes of God's attributes", I P29s). Terms already used in the *Short Treatise*. God's causation is **immanent**, not transitive (it does not pass outside God). Debate whether God is identical only with *naturans* or with both; Nadler thinks "the more likely reading" is both. [source: SEP-S §2.1; IEP-S §3b]
- Triangle analogy (I P17s1, Curley): "From God's supreme power, or infinite nature, infinitely many things in infinitely many modes, that is, all things, have necessarily flowed, or always follow, by the same necessity and in the same way as from the nature of a triangle it follows, from eternity and to eternity, that its three angles are equal to two right angles." [source: IEP-S §3b.i; SEP-S §2.1]
- I P16: "From the necessity of the divine nature there must follow infinitely many things in infinitely many modes (i.e., everything that can fall under an infinite intellect)." [source: SEP-S §2.1; IEP-M §3b]
- Infinitely many attributes; we know two: thought and extension. II P2: "Extension is an attribute of God, or God is an extended thing", widely (and, Nadler says, wrongly) read as making God a body, so "Spinozism" came to mean materialism. To Oldenburg he wrote that those who think he identifies God with Nature "taken as a kind of mass or corporeal matter ... are quite mistaken". [source: SEP-S §2.1–2.2; WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- Infinite modes (follow from an attribute's absolute nature: e.g. motion and rest under extension, infinite intellect under thought; often read as the laws of nature) vs finite modes (particular things, each caused by other finite modes). "Spinoza does little to explain" infinite modes. [source: IEP-S §3b.ii; SEP-S §2.1]
- Are modes properties of God? Curley (1969, 1988) denied it: modes depend on God only causally. "Curley's view has, however, proven unpopular"; "The dominant interpretation today is that modes are properties of the one substance" (Carriero, Melamed). [source: IEP-M §3b; SEP-Mod §1.2.4]

### The attributes debate (subjectivist vs objectivist)
- Ambiguities in I D4: "intellect" (finite or infinite?) and *tanquam* ("as if" or "as in fact" constituting the essence). [source: SEP-Att §1.8; IEP-M §4]
- **Subjectivist** reading, "which follows Hegel, and is given its paradigmatic expression by Wolfson" (*The Philosophy of Spinoza*, 1934): attributes are how the finite intellect perceives a substance whose essence is in itself simple; Wolfson: "to be perceived by the mind means to be invented by the mind" (vs. objectivism: "to be discovered by the mind") (Wolfson 1934, 146). Wolfson read Spinoza as "the last of the medieval Jewish rationalists", locating multiplicity in the mind, not in God. [source: SEP-Att §1.8.1]
- Objections: it makes our knowledge of God illusory, against II P47 (the mind has adequate knowledge of God's essence); it clashes with texts identifying God and the attributes (I P4, I P19, I P20c). Subjectivism "has fallen out of favor"; the **objectivist** reading "has become the standard". Della Rocca (2012) defends mind-dependent but not illusory attributes, from the Principle of Sufficient Reason. [source: SEP-Att §1.8.1]
- Objectivists (e.g. Curley 1988; possibly Gueroult 1968) hold that attributes are really distinct and each constitutes the essence of substance. Classification of Bennett differs: IEP-M calls Bennett (1984) a different "subjectivist" (attributes real and distinct but not fundamental); SEP-Att lists him with those who take the perceiving intellect to be the infinite one. [source: IEP-M §4; SEP-Att §1.8.2] WRITER: avoid labelling Bennett.

## 5. Necessity, determinism, no free will, no purposes
- I P29: "In nature there is nothing contingent, but all things have been determined from the necessity of the divine nature to exist and produce an effect in a certain way." I P33: "Things could have been produced by God in no other way, and in no other order than they have been produced." [source: SEP-S §2.1; SEP-Mod intro; IEP-S §3b.iii]
- Spinoza called his views on necessity and possibility the "principal foundation" of the *Ethics* (Ep. 75). [source: SEP-Mod intro]
- No miracles: "Nothing happens in nature that does not follow from her laws" (TTP ch. 6, Shirley). [source: SEP-S §3.1]
- **Debate: necessitarianism or "only" determinism?** Necessitarian reading (the actual world is the only possible world; e.g. Garrett 1991, 2018). Non-necessitarian reading (Curley 1969; Curley and Walski 1999): finite modes follow partly from infinite modes (laws) and partly from other finite modes (I P28: the chain of finite causes has no first member), so no finite thing exists necessarily; support: II A1 ("from the order of Nature it can happen equally that this or that man does exist or that he does not exist"). Cost: no explanation why this whole series exists rather than another, which Curley concedes. Leibniz raised the related "gap" problem (how can the finite follow from the infinite?) a year after Spinoza's death. [source: SEP-Mod §1.2.3–1.2.5, §2]
- Free will denied: II P48: "In the Mind there is no absolute, or free, will, but the Mind is determined to will this or that by a cause that is also determined by another, and this again by another, and so to infinity." III P2s: "men believe themselves free because they are conscious of their own actions and are ignorant of the causes by which they are determined." (Curley) [source: SEP-S §2.4; IEP-S §5a]
- Man is not "a dominion within a dominion" (III Preface, Curley): "Most of those who have written about the Affects ... seem to conceive man in nature as a dominion within a dominion. For they believe that man disturbs, rather than follows, the order of nature, that he has absolute power over his actions, and that he is determined only by himself." He will treat human actions "just as if it were a Question of lines, planes, and bodies." [source: SEP-S §2.4; IEP-S §5]
- **Appendix to Part I** (teleology as prejudice): "All the prejudices I here undertake to expose depend on this one: that men commonly suppose that all natural things act, as men do, on account of an end; indeed, they maintain as certain that God himself directs all things to some certain end, for they say that God has made all things for man, and man that he might worship God." Finding eyes, teeth, plants, animals and sun useful, people infer rulers of nature who made them for us. The falling-stone example ends: they "will not stop asking for the causes of causes until you take refuge in the will of God, i.e., the sanctuary of ignorance." Teleology "turns nature upside down", putting effect before cause. [source: SEP-S §2.1]
- Against an anthropomorphic God (I P15s): "those who feign a God, like man, consisting of a body and a mind, and subject to passions." [source: SEP-S §2.1]

## 6. Mind and body: parallelism
- **II P7**: "The order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things." Demonstration: it follows from I A4 ("The knowledge of an effect depends on, and involves, the knowledge of its cause"). II P7s: "a mode of extension and the idea of that mode are one and the same thing, but expressed in two ways"; "A circle existing in nature and the idea of the existing circle, which is also in God, are one and the same thing, which is explained through different attributes." [source: IEP-S §3b.iv; SEP-S §2.2]
- Argument against interaction (IEP-S §3b.iv): if a body caused an idea, knowledge of the idea would involve knowledge of extension (by I A4); it does not; so causes and effects lie within one attribute. Hence no mind–body interaction, but strict correlation, grounded in their being one thing.
- The human mind is the idea of the human body (II P11–13); every body has a corresponding idea, so in a sense all things are "minded" (IEP: "he does not consider the human mind to be unique"); minds differ as bodies differ in complexity (II P13s). [source: IEP-S §4a; SEP-EM §4]
- Result: "the so-called mind-body problem does not, technically speaking, arise" (Nadler), Spinoza's answer to Descartes's problem. [source: SEP-S §2.2] Cross-ref the published *Descartes* chapter, which says "*Spinoza* and *Leibniz* give rival answers to Elisabeth's problem".
- Three kinds of knowledge: imagination/opinion ("random experience": "confused and mutilated", II P29c), reason (common notions, adequate ideas), and intuition (third kind). "It is of the nature of Reason to regard things as necessary, not as contingent" (II P44); reason sees things "under a species of eternity" (*sub specie aeternitatis*). II P47: "The human Mind has an adequate knowledge of God's eternal and infinite essence." [source: SEP-S §2.3]

## 7. Conatus, affects, bondage, freedom
- **III P6**: "Each thing, as far as it can by its own power, strives to persevere in being." III P7: this striving "is nothing but the actual essence of the thing." Debated: is conatus teleological? Is the III P4–6 argument valid? (Garrett 2002 defends it.) [source: IEP-S §5b; IEP-M (conatus section)]
- Three primary affects: **joy** (*laetitia*; passage to greater perfection/power: III P11s "By Joy ... I shall understand that passion by which the Mind passes to a greater perfection"), **sadness** (*tristitia*; passage to lesser), **desire** (conatus with consciousness of it). Love = joy with the idea of an external cause; hate = "sadness with the accompanying idea of an external cause". Some translations use "pleasure"/"pain". [source: SEP-S §2.4; IEP-S §5c]
- Actions vs passions: we act when we are the "adequate cause"; passions arise from outside. There are active joy and active desire, but no active sadness. [source: IEP-S §5c; SEP-S §2.4]
- Values: III P9s: "we judge it to be good because we strive for it, will it, want it, and desire it", not the reverse. [source: IEP-FW §4]
- "We are driven about in many ways by external causes, and ... like waves on the sea, driven by contrary winds, we toss about, not knowing our outcome and fate" (III P59s). [source: SEP-S §2.4]
- **Bondage** (IV Preface): "lack of power to moderate and restrain the affects I call Bondage. For the man who is subject to affects is under the control, not of himself, but of fortune, in whose power he so greatly is that often, though he sees the better for himself, he is still forced to follow the worse." Reason alone does not restrain an affect; only a stronger affect can (IV P7, IV P14). [source: SEP-S §2.4; IEP-S §5d]
- Ethical egoism: IV P18s: "reason demands that everyone love himself, seek his own advantage ... and absolutely, that everyone should strive to preserve his own being as far as he can." Yet reason makes people agree: "insofar as men live according to the guidance of reason, they must always agree among themselves" (IV P35). IV P28: "Knowledge of God is the mind's greatest good; its greatest virtue is to know God." [source: IEP-S §6b–c; SEP-S §2.5]
- Pity, humility and repentance are not virtues (IV P50, 53, 54); suicide is weakness, "completely conquered by external causes" (IV P18s). [source: IEP-Mor]
- **The free man** (end of Part IV, IV P67–73): a model of human nature to aspire to, not literally attainable ("man is necessarily always subject to passions", IV P4c); he always acts honestly (IV P72), lives in society (IV P73), declines favours from the ignorant (IV P70). Debate on his status: Garrett 1990, Nadler 2006 and 2015, Kisner 2011, Homan 2015. [source: IEP-Mor; IEP-FW §4]
- **IV P67** (Curley): "A free man thinks of nothing less than of death, and his wisdom is a meditation on life, not on death." Nadler's rendering: "The free person thinks least of all of death, and his wisdom is a meditation not on death but on life." [source: WS-4/WS-5 (Curley's *Spinoza Reader*; Julie Klein, "Spinozan Meditations on Life and Death", citing Curley); WP-Spinoza's_Ethics for the first clause and the P67 number]
- Freedom through knowledge (Part V): an affect that is a passion ceases to be one once we form a clear idea of it; understanding that things are necessary lessens their hold (V P6s: grief at a loss eases once one sees "this good could not, in any way, have been kept"; no one pities infants for not speaking). "the power of the mind is defined by knowledge alone" (V P20s). Stoic in spirit (Nadler; IEP: "back to the Stoics and ultimately to Socrates"). [source: SEP-S §2.5; IEP-S §6a]
- **Intellectual love of God** (*amor Dei intellectualis*): arises from the third kind of knowledge; constant; it is "the very love by which God loves himself"; it is blessedness. But "He who loves God cannot strive that God should love him in return" (V P19). Nadler: Spinoza here takes "his cue from Maimonides's view of human eudaimonia". [source: IEP-S §6d; SEP-S §2.5; WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- **Eternity of the mind** (V P23): "The human mind cannot be absolutely destroyed with the body, but something of it remains which is eternal." Not personal immortality: memory and imagination perish (V P34s: people "confuse it with duration, and attribute it to the imagination, or memory"); the eternal part is the idea of the body's essence and the adequate ideas the mind has; "the more the mind understands things by the second and third kind of cognition, the greater the part of it that remains" (V P38d). Debate: Bennett (1984) called the doctrine "rubbish"; Hübner and others defend its consistency with II P7; Nadler (2002) stresses that it is far from traditional immortality; debt to medieval Jewish views (Klein; Nadler; Ravven & Goodman). [source: IEP-S §6e; SEP-EM §4]
- Last words of the *Ethics* (V P42s, Curley): "If the way I have shown to lead to these things now seems very hard, still, it can be found. And of course, what is found so rarely must be hard. For if salvation were at hand, and could be found without great effort, how could nearly everyone neglect it? But all things excellent are as difficult as they are rare." [source: IEP-S §6f]

## 8. The Theological-Political Treatise (1670)
- Subtitle's aim: to show that "the freedom to philosophize can not only be granted without injury to piety and the peace of the Commonwealth, but that the peace of the Commonwealth and Piety are endangered by the suppression of this freedom." [source: SEP-S §3]
- Natural history of superstition: fear and hope drive popular religion; clergy exploit them. [source: SEP-S §3.1; IEP-S §1]
- **Prophecy as imagination**: prophets were pious, morally upright people with vivid imaginations, not superior intellects; "no one needed to have a more perfect mind in order to prophesy, but only a more vivid imagination" (TTP 1, Curley vol. 2 p. 85). This breaks with Maimonides (*Guide* 2.36–38), for whom a prophet needs both great intellect and great imagination. Prophets give no authority on science or philosophy. [source: IEP-Rel; SEP-S §3.1]
- Election of the Hebrews: political and temporary, not intellectual or moral; "at the present time there is nothing whatsoever that the Jews can arrogate to themselves above other nations" (TTP 3, Shirley p. 45). Ceremonial law bound only the ancient Hebrew state. [source: SEP-S §3.1]
- **Authorship of Scripture**: Moses did not write all, or even most, of the Pentateuch (Moses in the third person; his death and later events narrated; places named by later names); Spinoza thought Ezra the Scribe the likely compiler of the books through Kings; the text is partly "mutilated"; canonization by the Pharisees in the 2nd century BCE. Not new in itself (Abraham ibn Ezra in the 12th century and Hobbes had suggested it) but no one had argued it so boldly or drawn such conclusions. [source: SEP-S §3.1; also SEP-Hobbes via early-modern--hobbes notes: "Hobbes and Spinoza often get a good deal of credit for developing this approach"]
- **Method of interpretation**: "the method of interpreting Scripture is no different from the method of interpreting Nature" (TTP 7, Shirley p. 87): meaning (authorial intention) from Scripture itself and its history, not from what we take to be true; against Maimonides (*Guide* 2.25), who reinterpreted Scripture to fit demonstrated truth. [source: SEP-S §3.1; IEP-Rel]
- Core message: "To know and love God, and to love one's neighbor as oneself"; Scripture teaches obedience, not knowledge; faith and philosophy are separate spheres. Worshippers of the letter "are beginning to worship likenesses and images, that is, paper and ink" (TTP 12, Shirley pp. 145–6). Minimal "catholic faith" (TTP 14). [source: SEP-S §3.1]
- The state: state of nature with natural right = power; a contract hands right to a sovereign (Hobbes's influence explicit); democracy is "the most natural" form; the sovereign controls outward religion (so "it is misleading to proclaim Spinoza as a proponent of the separation of church and state"), but inner belief is an inalienable right. [source: SEP-S §3.2]
- TTP 20 (Shirley p. 223): "Everyone is by absolute natural right the master of his own thoughts, and thus utter failure will attend any attempt in a commonwealth to force men to speak only as prescribed by the sovereign despite their different and opposing opinions." Freedom "is of the first importance in fostering the sciences and the arts" (TTP 20, Shirley p. 226); seditious speech is excluded. Chapter 20's title alludes to Tacitus, "rare are the happy times when we may think what we wish and say what we think". [source: SEP-S §3.2; SEP-Pol §6]
- Difference from Hobbes (Ep. 50 to Jarig Jelles): "I always preserve natural Right unimpaired". [source: early-modern--hobbes notes / SEP-Pol §2.1] (Consistent with the published *Hobbes* chapter.)

## 9. Political Treatise (unfinished)
- Written after 1672; begins by echoing Machiavelli's critique of utopian theorizing (TP 1.1; cf. *Prince* 15); designs for monarchy, aristocracy and democracy; he "had just begun the first of what would likely have been two chapters on democracy when he died". His model democracy excludes foreigners, those not *sui iuris* (women, children, servants) and those not living "respectably"; the exclusion of women on grounds of "natural" inequality (TP 11.4) is criticized today (Gatens 1996). Published in the *Opera Posthuma* to "relative indifference". Feuer's claim of an anti-democratic turn after 1672 is rejected by Steinberg. [source: SEP-Pol §1.2, §4.3, §6; IEP-S §1]

## 10. Spinoza and Judaism (cross-ref *Jewish Philosophy: from Saadia to Maimonides*)
- Steeped in Maimonides and Gersonides (IEP-S). Crescas: his treatment of the infinite and his view of God as "the place of the world" helped Spinoza make extension an attribute of God; Crescas's determinism probably influenced him; Crescas is "one of the few medieval philosophers that Spinoza quotes with sympathy"; scholars dispute the extent of Jewish influence. [source: IEP-S §1; SEP-Cresc §4 and conclusion]
- SEP-Maim: "For a naturalist like Spinoza, Maimonides is too willing to dismiss science and take refuge in traditional concepts like creation and divine volition." [source: SEP-Maim §8] (Consistent with the published Jewish Philosophy chapter.)
- Wolfson read him as "the last of the medieval Jewish rationalists". [source: SEP-Att §1.8.1]
- Kabbalah thesis: Elijah Benamozegh (1863) argued Kabbalah was the main source; Miquel Beltrán points to Abraham Cohen de Herrera. Minority view. [source: WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- Later Jewish reception: "first modern Jew"/"first secular Jew" debates; Salomon Rubin, who translated the *Ethics* into Hebrew, saw him as a new Maimonides; secular Zionists (Ben-Gurion) claimed him as a proto-Zionist on the basis of a passing TTP 3 remark; scholars disagree. Nadler: Spinoza himself thought that without the Law the Jews lacked a source of identity. [source: WP-Baruch_Spinoza]

## 11. Reception
- "Spinozist" as a slur: Nadler: condemned since his excommunication for a "God existing in only a philosophical sense"; for a century after his death philosophers read him largely in secret. [source: SEP-S §2.1; WP-Spinoza's_Ethics]
- Leibniz and Pufendorf thought his views on right and law more dangerous than Hobbes's (no divine legislator). Bayle, Locke and Toland knew his case for the freedom to philosophize. [source: SEP-Pol §6]
- Bayle (1697) linked his "atheism" to a Chinese sect ("Foe Kiao", i.e. Buddhism, from Jesuit reports); Kant later paired him with Laozi as "pantheists"; 19th-century Indologists (Goldstücker, Max Müller) likened his substance to Vedānta's Brahman while noting he could not have known it. Comparisons, not influence. [source: WP-Baruch_Spinoza] (Global angle; cross-ref Vedānta chapter.)
- **Pantheism Controversy**: in 1780 at Wolfenbüttel Lessing supposedly told Jacobi he was a Spinozist (WP: he "knew no philosophy ... save Spinozism"); Lessing died 1781; Jacobi and Mendelssohn corresponded from 1783; Jacobi published *Über die Lehre des Spinoza in Briefen an den Herrn Moses Mendelssohn* in **1785** (2nd ed. 1789, 3rd 1819); Mendelssohn replied 1786. For Jacobi, consistent rationalism leads to Spinozism, i.e. fatalism and atheism. The ensuing "Spinoza renaissance" made the *Ethics* a touchstone for German philosophy. [source: SEP-Jac §2, §3.1; WP-Pantheism_controversy]
- Hegel: "to be a follower of Spinoza is the essential commencement of all philosophy" (*Lectures on the History of Philosophy*, as quoted in SEP-Att §1.8.1); and "Spinoza is made a testing-point in modern philosophy, so that it may really be said: You are either a Spinozist or not a philosopher at all" (*Lectures*, Haldane & Simson trans., vol. 3, 1896, p. 283 per a quotation site). [source: SEP-Att; WP-Baruch_Spinoza; WS-8] WRITER: quote the "testing-point" sentence with "in the Haldane–Simson translation"; "Either Spinozism or no philosophy" is a paraphrase.
- Novalis called him "the God-intoxicated man". [source: WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- Plekhanov called Marxism "a variety of Spinozism". [source: SEP-Pol §6]
- **Einstein**: in April 1929 Rabbi Herbert S. Goldstein of New York cabled to ask whether he believed in God; Einstein's German reply (reported in the *New York Times*, ~25 April 1929) is translated in WP as "I believe in Spinoza's God who reveals himself in the orderly harmony of what exists, not in a God who concerns himself with the fates and actions of human beings"; *Time* has "lawful harmony of all that exists". [source: WP-Baruch_Spinoza; WS-9] WRITER: give one version and say "in one translation".
- 20th century: Deleuze called him "the prince of philosophers"; Althusser, Balibar, Negri, Matheron, Gueroult, Macherey in France; Wittgenstein's *Tractatus* title alludes to the TTP; Strauss, *Spinoza's Critique of Religion*. [source: WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- Netherlands: on the 1000-guilder note; the Spinoza Prize. [source: WP-Baruch_Spinoza]

## Debates
- **Why was he excommunicated?** Reasons unstated; Nadler infers his later heresies; IEP-Rel says we cannot know; financial and legal breach (1656 orphan petition) and the community's caution toward Dutch authorities are also cited. [SEP-S; IEP-Rel; WP-Baruch_Spinoza]
- **Is the monism argument sound?** "Generally deemed a failure" by contemporary philosophers (IEP-M); Leibniz's shared-attribute objection vs Della Rocca's reply from the conceptual independence of attributes. [IEP-M §3a]
- **Attributes**: subjectivist (Hegel, Wolfson 1934; Della Rocca's mind-dependent variant) vs objectivist (now standard; Curley, Gueroult). [SEP-Att; IEP-M]
- **Modes as properties vs causal effects**: Curley (causal only) vs majority (inherence: Carriero, Melamed). [IEP-M §3b; SEP-Mod]
- **Necessitarianism**: Garrett (full necessitarian) vs Curley/Walski (strong determinism with genuine alternatives). [SEP-Mod]
- **"Pantheist"?** The label dates from Toland (1705) (SEP-Pan). Pantheist reading: God = all of Nature (Nadler and others identify God with naturans + naturata). Curley (and Savan, Mason) identify God with *natura naturans* only (laws/principles), so finite things are effects, not parts, of God, which some use to deny pantheism. Gueroult and Carlisle: **panentheism** (the world is "in" God). Hartshorne: "classical pantheism". Nadler's own view: Spinoza is not a pantheist because pantheism is still a theism that treats nature with worshipful awe, which Spinoza rejects: "Spinoza's philosophy is an atheism" (Nadler 2026, quoted in WP). [SEP-S §2.1; SEP-Pan; WP-Baruch_Spinoza; WS-14]
- **Eternity of the mind**: "rubbish" (Bennett 1984) vs consistent with parallelism (Hübner; Garrett 2018 ch. 9; Nadler 2002). [SEP-EM §4]
- **Status of the "free man"**: attainable ideal or exemplar only (Garrett, Nadler, Kisner, Homan). [IEP-Mor; IEP-FW]
- **Radical Enlightenment**: Jonathan Israel (*Radical Enlightenment*, 2001; *Enlightenment Contested*, 2006; *Democratic Enlightenment*, 2011) argues Spinoza "and Spinozism were in fact the intellectual backbone of the European Radical Enlightenment everywhere" and that this leads to modern liberal democracy. Critics (Theo Verbeek, Harvey Chisick, Anthony La Vopa, Antoine Lilti, Samuel Moyn, Dan Edelstein) admire his reading but reject the radical/moderate binary and the Spinoza-as-origin thesis (Johnson Kent Wright's summary: "a false idol—Spinoza, supposed demiurge of modernity"). Steinberg (SEP-Pol): Spinoza's influence on French democratic thought came mainly through his naturalism, not his (realist, arguably anti-revolutionary) political arguments. [WP-Jonathan_Israel; SEP-Pol §6; WS-13]
- **Separation of church and state?** Nadler: "misleading" to call him a proponent, since the sovereign controls outward religion. [SEP-S §3.2]
