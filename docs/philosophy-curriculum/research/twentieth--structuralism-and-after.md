## Saussure and the linguistic model
- Ferdinand de Saussure, born Geneva 26 November 1857, died 22 February 1913; Swiss linguist; PhD Leipzig 1880; taught at the École pratique des hautes études in Paris, then from 1892 at the University of Geneva (Sanskrit and Indo-European) [source: WP-Sau].
- At 21 he published the *Mémoire sur le système primitif des voyelles dans les langues indo-européennes* (1878/1879), whose reconstructed "sonant coefficients" were later confirmed after Hittite was deciphered (the laryngeal theory) [source: WP-Sau].
- He "hardly published anything during his lifetime" on general linguistics [source: WP-Sau].
- AUTHORSHIP CAVEAT: the *Cours de linguistique générale* (1916) was published after his death, compiled by his former students Charles Bally and Albert Sechehaye (with the assistance of Albert Riedlinger) from notes on his Geneva lectures; Saussure did not write it as a book [source: WP-Sau; WP-CGL]. The lecture dates are given as 1907–1911 (WP-Sau) or 1906–1911 (WP-CGL): say "between 1906/7 and 1911" or "in three courses of lectures before the First World War". A manuscript of Saussure's own notes found in Geneva in 1996 was published as *Writings in General Linguistics* (trans. Carol Sanders and Matthew Pires, OUP 2006) [source: WP-CGL; WP-Sau]. Roy Harris wrote *Reading Saussure* (1987) and later works on how interpreters reshaped him [source: WP-Sau bibliography; WS].
- English translations: Wade Baskin (New York: Philosophical Library, 1959; later ed. Perry Meisel & Haun Saussy, Columbia UP 2011) and Roy Harris (La Salle: Open Court, 1983) [source: WP-Sau; WP-CGL].
- *Langue/parole*: the distinction between language (*la langue*, the system) and speech (*la parole*, individual occurrences of language use); Saussure's "speech circuit" [source: WP-Sau; WP-CGL].
- The linguistic sign is two-sided: the signifier (a linguistic form, e.g. a word's sound-image) and the signified (the concept); the word "tree" refers not to a physical tree but to the psychological concept [source: WP-Sau].
- Arbitrariness: Saussure took for granted that "No one disputes the principle of the arbitrary nature of the sign" (Baskin, p. 68); the link between signifier and signified is conventional, though he allowed onomatopoeic exceptions [source: WP-Sau].
- Value is differential: concepts gain value by contrast with related concepts; meaning (signification) is distinguished from value [source: WP-Sau].
- Synchrony/diachrony: language can be studied as it changes through time (diachronically) or as a system at one moment (synchronically); his chess analogy — a spectator joining a game in progress needs only the present position of the pieces, not their history [source: WP-CGL; WP-Sau].
- The *Course* is "generally regarded as the starting point of structural linguistics" [source: WP-CGL]. Saussure's "semiology": a science studying "the life of signs within society" [source: WP-Sau].
- Foucault read Saussure's "structuralist account of linguistic sense"; Derrida's early thought drew on Saussure [source: SEP-F §2; IEP-D §1].
- Charles Sanders Peirce developed a theory of signs independently of Saussure (cross-ref *American Pragmatism*) — consistent with the published Pragmatism chapter.

## Jakobson and the Prague school (brief)
- The Prague linguistic circle began in 1926 as a group of linguists, philologists and literary critics; members included the Russian émigrés Roman Jakobson, Nikolai Trubetzkoy and Sergei Karcevskiy, with Vilém Mathesius as instigator and first president; programmatic "Prague Theses" 1929; disbanded 1952 after the 1948 Communist coup [source: WP Prague_linguistic_circle].
- Roman Jakobson (Moscow 1896 – Cambridge, Massachusetts, 18 July 1982); with Trubetzkoy "in effect" founded modern phonology; at Harvard from 1949 [source: WP Roman_Jakobson].
- In wartime New York, at the École Libre des Hautes Études, Jakobson met and collaborated with Lévi-Strauss; through his "decisive influence on Claude Lévi-Strauss and Roland Barthes" he was pivotal in carrying structural analysis beyond linguistics [source: WP Roman_Jakobson; WP-LS; WP-Str].

## Lévi-Strauss
- Claude Lévi-Strauss, born Brussels 28 November 1908 to French-Jewish parents; died 30 October 2009 (aged 100) [source: WP-LS].
- Visiting professor of sociology at the University of São Paulo; he and his then wife Dina Lévi-Strauss, a trained ethnographer, lived and worked in Brazil 1935–1939, doing fieldwork in the Mato Grosso and Amazon (Guaycuru, Bororó, Nambikwara) — his only ethnographic fieldwork [source: WP-LS]. (Include Dina: she was a visiting professor of ethnology in her own right.)
- 1941: New School for Social Research, New York; co-founder with Maritain, Focillon and Jakobson of the École Libre des Hautes Études [source: WP-LS].
- *Les structures élémentaires de la parenté* (*The Elementary Structures of Kinship*) 1949 (his state-doctorate thesis; English 1969 by Bell, von Sturmer and Needham): kinship is based on **alliance** between groups formed through exchange of women in marriage (against Radcliffe-Brown's descent model); the incest taboo as the rule requiring exchange outside one's line; reviewed favourably by Simone de Beauvoir [source: WP-LS]. SEP-LA: the 1949 book "helped launch the French structuralist movement that flourished during the 1950s and 1960s, an orientation that challenged the theoretical primacy of existentialism in France" [source: SEP-LA §1].
- "The Structural Study of Myth", *Journal of American Folklore* 68 (270): 428–44 (1955) [source: WP-LS bibliography]. Method: break versions of a myth into minimal units ("mythemes") and arrange them in bundles; myths are built from binary oppositions. His Oedipus analysis pairs "the overrating of blood relations" with "the underrating of blood relations", and the autochthonous origin of humans with its denial; myth's work is to associate an irreconcilable opposition with a reconcilable one, giving the illusion that the former is resolved [source: WP-LS]. There is "no one 'authentic' version of a myth" [source: WP-LS].
- *Tristes Tropiques* (Plon, 1955): memoir of his 1930s travels mixing ethnography and philosophical meditation; made him one of France's best-known intellectuals [source: WP-LS; WP Tristes_Tropiques].
- *Anthropologie structurale* (1958; *Structural Anthropology*, 1963); chair of social anthropology at the Collège de France 1959–1982; elected to the Académie française 14 May 1973 [source: WP-LS].
- *La Pensée sauvage* (1962; English *The Savage Mind* 1966, later *Wild Thought*): the title puns on *pensée* (thought / pansy); it concerns "mind in its untamed state as distinct from mind cultivated or domesticated for the purpose of yielding a return", not "the mind of savages" [source: WP The_Savage_Mind].
- **Bricolage**: savage thought is like the *bricoleur*, who makes do with "whatever materials are at hand", versus the engineer who designs tools and materials for a project [source: WP The_Savage_Mind; WP-LS].
- **Debate with Sartre**: the second half of *The Savage Mind* (a theory of history) engaged Lévi-Strauss "in a heated debate with Jean-Paul Sartre over the nature of human freedom", setting a structuralist account of agency against Sartre's existentialism [source: WP-LS]. Structuralism rose in France "in the wake of existentialism, particularly in the 1960s" [source: WP-Str]. (Cross-ref *Existentialism*: Sartre's *Critique of Dialectical Reason* 1960 is the work at issue — that link is from memory; the WP pages read do not name the book.)
- *Mythologiques* I–IV (1964–1971; *The Raw and the Cooked* etc.) traced myths from South America to the Arctic [source: WP-LS].
- Lévi-Strauss called Montaigne "the pioneer of cultural relativism" — already used in the published *Renaissance Philosophy* chapter (consistent).

## Structuralism spreads: Barthes, Lacan, Althusser
- SEP-LA: "Lévi-Strauss 'structuralized' anthropology, as Roland Barthes did for literary-cultural studies and Louis Althusser for Marxism", and Lacan likewise structuralized Freudian psychoanalysis [source: SEP-LA §1].
- Roland Barthes (Cherbourg, 12 November 1915 – Paris, 26 March 1980; struck by a laundry van on 25 February 1980 opposite the Collège de France) [source: WP Roland_Barthes].
- *Mythologies* (1957): 53 short essays written 1954–56, mostly first in *Les Lettres nouvelles*, on how bourgeois culture "naturalizes" its values; essays include "The World of Wrestling"; the essay "Myth Today" analyses a *Paris Match* cover (no. 326, July 1955) of a young Black soldier in French uniform saluting; English abridged 1972 (tr. Annette Lavers), complete edition 2012 (Richard Howard and Lavers) [source: WP Mythologies_(book)].
- "The Death of the Author" (1967): against explaining a text by its author's intentions and biography; the "death of the author" is the "birth of the reader"; first published in English in the US magazine *Aspen* (nos. 5–6) [source: WP The_Death_of_the_Author; WP-PS].
- Jacques Lacan (13 April 1901 – 9 September 1981), psychiatrist and psychoanalyst; from the late 1940s/1950s took up Saussure, Lévi-Strauss and Jakobson; his "return to Freud" and "most famous dictum": "the unconscious is structured like a language" (*l'inconscient est structuré comme un langage*); 1953 "Rome Discourse" as founding manifesto; the Symbolic order; *Écrits* (900 pages) [source: SEP-LA §1, §2.1.2]. SEP-LA notes that a 1959–60 "turn" in Lacan is controversial.
- Louis Althusser (1918–1990): *For Marx* and *Reading Capital* (both 1965), identified with the "structuralism" trend; "Ideology and Ideological State Apparatuses" (1970): ideology "interpellates" (hails) individuals as subjects via institutions such as family, schools, church; critics called his work idealist, Stalinist, dogmatist "or excessively structuralist"; in November 1980 he strangled his wife Hélène Rytmann-Légotien and was judged unfit to stand trial [source: SEP-AL intro, §§1, 4]. Cross-ref *Marx* (published chapter has the 1845 "epistemological break" — consistent).
- Althusser and Hyppolite taught Foucault at the ENS; Foucault joined the Communist Party in 1950 under Althusser's influence and left in 1952 [source: SEP-F §2; IEP-F §1]. Althusser and Hyppolite invited/recommended Derrida to teach at the ENS (from 1964) [source: SEP-D §1; WP-JD].

## "Post-structuralism": the label
- The French figures of the 1960s "work with concepts developed during the structuralist revolution in Paris in the 1950s and early 1960s ... For this reason they are often called 'poststructuralists'" and they cite May 1968 as a watershed [source: SEP-PM intro].
- Writers often called post-structuralist (Barthes, Derrida, Foucault, Deleuze, Baudrillard) — "many theorists who have been called 'post-structuralist' have rejected the label"; scholars "rarely label themselves as post-structuralists" [source: WP-PS]. Derrida "distanced himself from post-structuralism" [source: WP-JD].
- Foucault was "a major figure in two successive waves of 20th century French thought–the structuralist wave of the 1960s and then the poststructuralist wave" [source: IEP-F intro]; SEP-F: "associated with the structuralist and post-structuralist movements". WP-MF: Foucault rejected the label structuralist [source: WP Madness_and_Civilization: "a label Foucault himself always" rejected].
- That the term is an American/Anglophone coinage hardly used in France: supported only by search snippets (a 2025 *Kritike* article; a Manchester UP 2021 chapter "Lost in the Post" on post-structuralism between France and the US; François Cusset's *French Theory* thesis) (WS). Safe wording: "a label applied largely by English-speaking readers, which most of those so labelled rejected".
- The Johns Hopkins colloquium "The Languages of Criticism and the Sciences of Man", Baltimore, 18–21 October 1966, organised by René Girard and Richard Macksey, Ford Foundation-funded; speakers included Barthes, Lacan, Hyppolite, de Man, Derrida; published 1970 as *The Structuralist Controversy* [source: WP-SSP; WP-PS].

## Foucault: life
- Paul-Michel Foucault, born Poitiers 15 October 1926; father a surgeon [source: SEP-F §1; IEP-F §1; WP-MF].
- ENS 1946; taught by Merleau-Ponty, mentored by Althusser, prepared with Hyppolite; depression and a suicide attempt at the ENS, "generally agreed to have resulted from Foucault's difficulties coming to terms with his homosexuality"; degrees in philosophy and psychology (licence de psychologie 1949, diplôme de psycho-pathologie 1952) [source: IEP-F §1; SEP-F §3.1].
- Taught psychology at the ENS from 1951; his students included Derrida [source: IEP-F §1].
- Posts in Uppsala (1955), Warsaw (1958), Hamburg (1959); *History of Madness* written mostly then [source: IEP-F §1; SEP-F §3.1].
- Clermont-Ferrand 1960–66; met Daniel Defert 1960, his partner for life; University of Tunis 1966–68 (missed most of May 1968 in Paris, but witnessed Tunisian student unrest in March 1968) [source: IEP-F §1; WP-MF].
- Head of philosophy at the experimental Vincennes campus (Paris VIII) 1968–69, with Badiou, Rancière, Balibar [source: IEP-F §1; WP-MF].
- Collège de France: elected (WP-MF: November 1969; SEP-F: 1969; IEP-F: 1970 — WP-MF explains: elected Nov. 1969, inaugural lecture December 1970, *L'ordre du discours*); chair in the "History of Systems of Thought" [source: SEP-F §1; WP-MF].
- February 1971: co-founded the Groupe d'information sur les prisons (GIP) with Pierre Vidal-Naquet and Jean-Marie Domenach [source: WP-MF; SEP-F §1].
- Died in Paris of AIDS-related illness on **25 June 1984** [source: SEP-F §1; WP-MF; IEP-F gives 26 June — that is the date *Libération* announced the death per WP-MF; use 25 June]. First public figure in France to die of AIDS complications; Defert founded the charity AIDES [source: WP-MF].
- He forbade posthumous publication; the lectures (1970–84) were nevertheless published; vol. 4 of *History of Sexuality* (*Les aveux de la chair*) published 2018 [source: SEP-F §§3.6, 4; WP The_History_of_Sexuality].
- Sartre "had no personal influence on Foucault"; Foucault mocked Sartre's starting from the subject as "transcendental narcissism" and rejected Sartre's role of "universal intellectual" [source: SEP-F §2].

## Foucault: works and ideas
- Influences: Canguilhem and Bachelard's history of science (discontinuities, "Kuhnian avant la lettre"), Saussure, Lacan, Dumézil; Bataille and Blanchot; Nietzsche [source: SEP-F §2; IEP-F §3].
- Foucault inverts Kant: instead of asking what in the apparently contingent is necessary, ask "what, in the apparently necessary, might be contingent"; "Each of his major books is a critique of historical reason" [source: SEP-F §3].
- *Folie et déraison: Histoire de la folie à l'âge classique* (1961), his doctoral thesis; English abridged *Madness and Civilization* (tr. Richard Howard; WP infobox gives 1964 for the English edition — other sources 1965; say "the 1960s"), full *History of Madness* tr. Jonathan Murphy and Jean Khalfa (Routledge, 2006) [source: SEP-F bibliography; IEP-F §3a; WP Madness_and_Civilization]. Thesis: the "liberation" of the mad by Pinel and Tuke and the idea that madness is mental illness was not clear progress but a cover for moral control [source: SEP-F §3.1]. The "Great Confinement" of the 17th century [source: WP Madness_and_Civilization].
- *The Birth of the Clinic* (1963) [source: SEP-F §3.1].
- *Les mots et les choses* (Gallimard, April 1966; *The Order of Things*, tr. Alan Sheridan, 1970), subtitle "An Archaeology of the Human Sciences"; became a bestseller; opens with an analysis of Velázquez's *Las Meninas* [source: SEP-F §3.2; IEP-F §1, §3d; WP The_Order_of_Things].
- **Episteme**: "the stable ensemble of unspoken rules that governs knowledge", subject to historical breaks; two breaks — the start of the Classical age (17th c.) and of the modern age (turn of the 19th c.) [source: IEP-F §3d]. Renaissance knowledge as resemblance; Classical knowledge as representation (map model); with Kant, "man" as both transcendental subject and empirical object — "there was no epistemological consciousness of man as such" before (Sheridan tr. [1970: 309]) [source: SEP-F §3.2].
- *The Archaeology of Knowledge* (1969; tr. Sheridan 1972): archaeology studies the rules of "discursive formations" that operate beneath individual consciousness; the "statement"; the "archive" [source: SEP-F §3.3; IEP-F §3e]. "What is an Author?" (1969) [source: IEP-F §3e].
- **Genealogy**: from Nietzsche's genealogy of morals; shows a system of thought was "the result of contingent turns of history, not the outcome of rationally inevitable trends"; first deployed in *Discipline and Punish*; a "history of the present" [source: SEP-F §3.3; IEP-F §4]. Foucault's essay "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" (1971) — consistent with published *Nietzsche* chapter.
- *Surveiller et punir: Naissance de la prison* (1975; *Discipline and Punish: The Birth of the Prison*, tr. Alan Sheridan, 1977) [source: SEP-F bibliography; WP-DP].
- It opens by contrasting the public torture and execution of Robert-François Damiens (who stabbed Louis XV with a penknife at Versailles on 5 January 1757; executed by dismemberment at the Place de Grève on 28 March 1757) with a regimented early-19th-century prison timetable (WP-DP names Mettray) [source: WP-DP; WP Robert-François_Damiens].
- "to punish less, perhaps; but certainly to punish better" (Sheridan [1977: 82]) [source: SEP-F §3.4].
- Three techniques of disciplinary power: hierarchical observation, normalizing judgment, the examination; the examination combines "the deployment of force and the establishment of truth" ([1977: 184]) — "a prime example of what Foucault calls power/knowledge" [source: SEP-F §3.4].
- Power/knowledge is not Bacon's "knowledge is power" (knowledge as an instrument of an independent power): "in knowing we control and in controlling we know" [source: SEP-F §3.4]. IEP-F: power-knowledge was introduced "as a replacement for the Marxist notion of ideology" [source: IEP-F §4b].
- **Docile bodies**: disciplinary techniques make bodies of prisoners, soldiers, workers and schoolchildren "more useful and at the same time easier to control" [source: SEP-F §3.4; WP-DP].
- **Panopticon** (cross-ref *Bentham, Mill and Utilitarianism*): Bentham's prison design in which each inmate is always visible from a central tower but never knows whether he is being watched, so behaves as if always seen; "the paradigmatic architectural model of modern disciplinary power"; Bentham never built it [source: SEP-F §3.4]. Willey Reveley drew Bentham's plan in 1791 [source: WP Panopticon]. Foucault came on it while studying 18th-century hospital architecture [source: WP Panopticon].
- Discipline spreads to factories, hospitals, schools: a "carceral" network; and it was not the decision of a central agency — techniques developed for innocuous purposes converged [source: SEP-F §3.4; WP-DP].
- IEP-F's corrective: the book's main conclusion is that the prison's "objective purpose" is "to produce criminality and recidivism", with reform part of the system; "Though Foucault is often presented as a theorist of 'panopticism,' this is not the central claim of the book" [source: IEP-F §4a].
- *La volonté de savoir* (1976; *The History of Sexuality*, vol. 1, tr. Robert Hurley, 1978; UK *The Will to Knowledge*): rejects the **repressive hypothesis** (that Victorian sexuality was silenced); instead discourse on sex multiplied (medical, juridical, psychological); confession secularised in doctors, therapists, psychiatrists; power "is not essentially repressive but productive" [source: SEP-F §3.5; IEP-F §4b].
- Power is relational, to be studied from the bottom up as a "microphysics of power"; "where there is power, there is always resistance"; resistance is "the odd term in the relations of power" (Hurley [1978: 96]) [source: SEP-F §3.5].
- **Biopower**: unlike sovereign power (the right to kill, power by "deduction"), biopower "endeavors to administer, optimize, and multiply [life], subjecting it to precise controls and comprehensive regulations" (Hurley [1978: 137]); population statistics, public health, insurance, urban planning; norms gain at the expense of law [source: SEP-F §3.5]. (Consistent with the History chapter *Quantitative Data*.)
- Lectures: *Security, Territory, Population* (1977–78) and *The Birth of Biopolitics* (1978–79) — "governmentality", "the way in which one conducts the conduct of men" ([2008: 186]); neoliberalism (Gary Becker's human capital) [source: SEP-F §4].
- Later ethics: *The Use of Pleasure* and *The Care of the Self* (both 1984; English 1985, 1986); ethics as the relation to oneself; "aesthetics of existence"; subjects "are not just docile bodies, but actively refuse, adopt and alter forms of being a subject"; late lectures on "care of the self" and *parrhesia* [source: SEP-F §§3.6, 4]. Pierre Hadot's *Philosophy as a Way of Life ... from Socrates to Foucault* links this (see *Hellenistic Philosophy* notes).
- Iranian revolution: in 1978 *Corriere della Sera* sent him to Tehran days after Black Friday; he met Shariatmadari and Bazargan, visited Khomeini; "His articles expressed awe of Khomeini's Islamist movement, for which he was widely criticised in the French press, including by Iranian expatriates"; he replied that Islamism would be a major force the West must treat with respect [source: WP-MF; IEP-F §1]. A reassessment: Behrooz Ghamari-Tabrizi, *Foucault in Iran: Islamic Revolution after the Enlightenment* (Minnesota, 2016) [source: WP-MF bibliography].
- Chomsky–Foucault debate: Eindhoven University of Technology, Netherlands, 22 October 1971 (broadcast 28 November 1971), moderated by Fons Elders, on whether there is an innate human nature; Chomsky defended human nature and justice grounded in reason; Foucault rejected a universal basis for justice; Chomsky later: "He struck me as completely amoral" [source: WP Chomsky–Foucault_debate; WP-MF].
- 1977 age-of-consent petition: Foucault signed a petition to lower the homosexual age of consent; in 1978 argued children could consent [source: WP-MF]. (Mention only if relevant; sensitive.)

## ARGUMENT 1 (step by step): Foucault's genealogy of disciplinary power
Reconstruction from SEP-F §3.4–3.5 and IEP-F §4:
1. Standard histories present the move from torture (Damiens, 1757) to imprisonment as humanitarian progress.
2. But the new penal practices aim not at revenge but at "correcting" deviance by norms: they observe, rank against norms ("normalizing judgment") and examine.
3. These techniques also produce knowledge (records, cases, criminology, psychiatry); the examination unites "the deployment of force and the establishment of truth". So in the human sciences power and knowledge cannot be separated: power/knowledge.
4. The same techniques spread from prison to army, factory, hospital and school (Panopticon as the model: visibility induces self-surveillance), producing "docile bodies" — useful and controllable.
5. Since no central agent designed this, power is dispersed, relational and productive (it makes subjects, e.g. "the delinquent"), not only repressive.
6. Conclusion: what presents itself as gentler, enlightened, scientific treatment is also a more effective form of control — "to punish less, perhaps; but certainly to punish better" — and its claimed necessity is contingent, hence open to change.
Objections:
- **Fraser (normative confusion)**: Foucault's work offers "empirical insights" but is "normatively confused" (Fraser 1989, 31); he cannot say why domination should be resisted: "Only with the introduction of normative notions could he begin to tell us what is wrong with the modern power/knowledge regime and why we ought to oppose it" (1989, 29) [source: SEP-FP §3.5]. Original essay: "Foucault on Modern Power: Empirical Insights and Normative Confusions", *Praxis International* 1 (Oct. 1981): 272–287; reprinted as ch. 1 of *Unruly Practices* (1989) (WS). NB "empirically insightful and normatively confused" is a paraphrase of the title, not a quotation.
- **Habermas (performative contradiction / crypto-normativism)**: in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985; tr. Frederick Lawrence 1987) he argues that Nietzsche, Heidegger, Derrida and Foucault "commit a performative contradiction in their critiques of modernism by employing concepts and methods that only modern reason can provide"; Foucault does not subject his own genealogy to genealogical unmasking: "Foucault cannot adequately deal with the persistent problems that come up in connection with an interpretive approach to the object domain, a self-referential denial of universal validity claims, and a normative justification of critique" (Lawrence tr., 286) [source: SEP-PM §9]. WP-MF: Habermas called Foucault's thinking "crypto-normativist", covertly reliant on Enlightenment principles [source: WP-MF].
- **Charles Taylor**, "Foucault on Freedom and Truth", *Political Theory* 12 (2) (May 1984): 152–83 (reprinted in Hoy ed., *Foucault: A Critical Reader*, 1986): "Foucault disconcerts"; the concept of power belongs to a field from which freedom and truth cannot be excluded (domination implies something it imposes on), so a power without them is incoherent (WS, secondary summaries; Paul Patton's 1989 reply in *Political Studies* defends Foucault) — use cautiously.
- **Agency (feminist)**: Linda Martín Alcoff and Seyla Benhabib: making the subject an effect of power denies the agency feminism needs; Nancy Hartsock: Foucault views power "from above", from the colonizer's perspective, and neglects structural inequality — "domination, viewed from above, is more likely to look like equality" (1996, 39) [source: SEP-FP §3.5; IEP-FF].
- **Historians**: Peter Gay — Foucault "breathed fresh air into the history of penology" but was overall negative; David Garland — critics converge on "Foucault's overestimation of the political dimension"; Fred Alford — Foucault "mistaken the idea of prison ... for its practice" [source: WP-DP]. On Bentham, the SEP's Bentham entry, citing Janet Semple (1993), says Foucault "hardly does justice to the intricacies" of the project (already in published *Utilitarianism* chapter).
Replies:
- Foucault holds that power is always met by resistance and is "an unstable network" [source: SEP-F §3.5]; government involves giving reasons, so critique as questioning the reasons for being governed is itself a form of resistance [source: SEP-F §4].
- The later work adds the subject's active self-formation: subjects "are not just docile bodies" [source: SEP-F §3.6].
- That Foucault treats autonomy and other normative notions as mere effects of power "has been contested recently in the literature on Foucault; see Allen 2008a and Oksala 2005"; Amy Allen replies to the agency critique (2008a chs. 2–3) and to Hartsock (1996, 1999) [source: SEP-FP §3.5].
- Postmodernists answered Habermas with "rhetorical counter-strategies"; SEP-PM notes that Habermas "takes postmodernism seriously and does not, like other critics, reject it as mere nonsense" [source: SEP-PM §9].

## Derrida: life
- Born Jackie Élie Derrida, 15 July 1930, El-Biar (Algiers), French Algeria, into a Sephardic Jewish family; the Vichy "Jewish laws" interrupted his schooling [source: SEP-D §1; WP-JD].
- Failed the ENS entrance twice, entered 1952; passed the agrégation on the second try, 1956 (Deleuze passed him in the courtyard: "My thoughts are with you, my very best thoughts") [source: SEP-D §1]. (IEP-D says he was accepted "at the age of 19" — contradicts SEP's 1952; use SEP.)
- During the Algerian War taught soldiers' children 1957–59 instead of military service; Sorbonne 1960–64; ENS from 1964 on Althusser's and Hyppolite's recommendation [source: WP-JD; SEP-D §1].
- 1963 "Cogito and the History of Madness" criticised Foucault's reading of Descartes, causing "a rupture between Derrida and Foucault, which will never fully heal" [source: SEP-D §1]. Foucault and the Mitterrand government helped free him after his 1981 arrest in Czechoslovakia [source: WP-JD].
- 1967 "annus mirabilis": *Writing and Difference*, *Voice and Phenomenon* (*Speech and Phenomena*), *Of Grammatology* [source: SEP-D §1; IEP-D].
- 21 October 1966: "Structure, Sign, and Play in the Discourse of the Human Sciences" at Johns Hopkins (published 1967 as ch. 10 of *Writing and Difference*): structuralism still holds onto a "center" ("God", "being", "presence", "man") exempt from play; on Lévi-Strauss's bricolage, we are all *bricoleurs* using inherited tools; one of the earliest critiques of structuralism in non-structuralist terms [source: WP-SSP; WP-PS].
- EHESS director of studies from 1983; first director of the Collège international de philosophie 1982–84; regular US posts (Johns Hopkins, Yale; UC Irvine from 1986/87) [source: SEP-D §1; WP-JD].
- Died of pancreatic cancer (diagnosed 2002) in Paris: SEP-D says 8 October 2004; WP-JD says "in the early hours of 9 October 2004". Say "October 2004" or "8/9 October".
- Honorary degrees include Cambridge 1992; Adorno Prize 2001 [source: WP-JD].

## Derrida: deconstruction
- "Deconstruction" is probably adapted from Heidegger's *Destruktion* in *Being and Time* [source: SEP-D §5; IEP-D].
- Target: the "metaphysics of presence" or "logocentrism": privileging presence, speech, the origin; "Phallogocentrism points towards the patriarchal significance of this privileging" [source: IEP-D §2a]. Derrida's own statement: "All metaphysicians, from Plato to Rousseau, Descartes to Husserl" proceed by conceiving "good to be before evil, the positive before the negative, the pure before the impure ..." — "the metaphysical exigency" (*Limited Inc* 236) [source: IEP-D §2a].
- Phonocentrism: Plato, Rousseau, Saussure and Lévi-Strauss denigrate writing as the sign of a sign; Saussure: "language and writing are two distinct systems of signs: the second exists for the sole purpose of representing the first" [source: IEP-D §3]. Derrida: "writing is not a sign of a sign, except if one says it of all signs, which would be more profoundly true" (OG 43); "arche-writing" [source: IEP-D §3].
- *Différance* (with an "a"): combines differing (spatial) and deferring (temporal); the a/e difference is inaudible in French, so it needs writing; "neither a word, nor a concept" [source: IEP-D §3c; SEP-D §5].
- The supplement (Rousseau): "writing is the supplement par excellence since it proposes itself as the supplement of the supplement, sign of a sign" (OG 281); Rousseau's laments over masturbation in the *Confessions* (OG 153–4) [source: IEP-D §3e]. Much of *Of Grammatology* Part II reads Rousseau's *Essay on the Origin of Languages* via a "logic of supplementarity" [source: WP-OG]. Cross-ref *Enlightenment and Rousseau*.
- *Of Grammatology*: Minuit 1967; English by Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak, Johns Hopkins UP 1976 (corrected 1997, revised 2016); Spivak bought the book in 1967 and translated it with a celebrated preface; she met Derrida in 1971 [source: WP-OG; WP Gayatri_Chakravorty_Spivak].
- "*il n'y a pas de hors-texte*" occurs in the Rousseau essay of *Of Grammatology* (Part II, "...That Dangerous Supplement...", p. 158 in the English) [source: WP-JD; WS citing Deutscher]. More literal rendering "there is no outside-text"; Spivak's translation is commonly cited as "there is nothing outside the text" with a bracketed alternative (exact bracket wording NOT verified — see below). Derrida (Afterword to *Limited Inc*, 1988, p. 136): the formula, "which for some has become a sort of slogan, in general so badly understood, of deconstruction ... means nothing else: there is nothing outside context. In this form, which says exactly the same thing, the formula would doubtless have been less shocking" [source: WP-JD].
- Searle's 1994 parody of deconstructive tactics uses this very gloss as an example of retreating to a truism [source: WP-SD].

## ARGUMENT 2 (step by step): deconstruction of the speech/writing hierarchy
From SEP-D §5 (the "two phases", from *Positions* 1971/72 and the Preface to *Dissemination*) and IEP-D §§2–3:
1. Western metaphysics ("Platonism") structures things by oppositions that are hierarchies: intelligible/sensible, essence/appearance, soul/body, speech/writing, good/evil [source: SEP-D §5].
2. Speech is privileged because the speaker seems present to his meaning ("hearing-oneself-speak"); writing is demoted as a derivative sign of a sign that works in the speaker's absence [source: IEP-D §3; SEP-D §3].
3. But every feature used to demote writing — repeatability in the absence of its author and addressee, referring only to other signs, meaning deferred — holds of speech too: "all writing ... must be able to function in the absence of every empirically determined addressee"; signs refer to signs without end [source: IEP-D §3].
4. Phase one: reverse the hierarchy (writing is as "original" as speech). Phase two: re-inscribe the formerly inferior term as the "resource" of the opposition itself — "arche-writing", *différance*, the "trace", "supplement", "pharmakon" (old names or "paleonyms") [source: SEP-D §5].
5. Underlying argument: even present experience contains repetition (memory and anticipation), so "we can have no experience that does not essentially and inseparably contain these two agencies of event and repeatability"; pure presence is never given [source: SEP-D §3].
6. Conclusion: the opposition cannot be sustained as a pure hierarchy; meaning is never fully present — but deconstruction is not mere negation: it works from within the text, "faithful" to it while finding its internal tensions [source: IEP-D §2].
Objections:
- **Searle (1977)**: "Reiterating the Differences: A Reply to Derrida", *Glyph* 1 (1977), answering Derrida's "Signature Event Context" (Montreal conference August 1971; *Marges* 1972; English in *Glyph* 1, 1977): Derrida misread Austin, whose theory had a narrow goal justifying the exclusion of "parasitic" speech; he confused the type–token distinction; Searle "agreed ... that intentionality presupposes iterability" [source: WP-SD; WP Limited_Inc; SEP-D §1]. Derrida's 90-page "Limited Inc a b c ..." (*Glyph* 2, 1977) mocks Searle as "Sarl"; the 1988 book *Limited Inc* (Northwestern) omits Searle's essay because Searle refused permission [source: SEP-D §1; WP Limited_Inc]. Searle in the *New York Review of Books* (2 Feb 1984): "the low level of philosophical argumentation, the deliberate obscurantism of the prose, the wildly exaggerated claims"; and (1983) relayed Foucault's alleged private description of Derrida's style as "*obscurantisme terroriste*" [source: WP Limited_Inc].
- **Habermas** (PDM 1985): Derrida levels the distinction between philosophy and literature, moving logic into rhetoric to evade self-reference: "Whoever transposes the radical critique of reason into the domain of rhetoric in order to blunt the paradox of self-referentiality, also dulls the sword of the critique of reason itself" (Lawrence tr., 210); *différance* as "Dionysian messianism", meaning infinitely deferred (180–81) [source: SEP-PM §9].
- **Cambridge 1992**: some Cambridge academics, mostly not philosophers, proposed an honorary doctorate; opposed by Professor of Philosophy Hugh Mellor; a letter (to *The Times*, per SEP-D) by Barry Smith and others — eighteen philosophers from many countries including Quine, David Armstrong, Ruth Barcan Marcus and René Thom — said Derrida's work "does not meet accepted standards of clarity and rigour", likened it to "tricks and gimmicks similar to those of the Dadaists", and concluded: "Academic status based on what seems to us to be little more than semi-intelligible attacks upon the values of reason, truth, and scholarship is not, we submit, sufficient grounds for the awarding of an honorary degree in a distinguished university"; the ballot went 336 to 204 in Derrida's favour [source: WP-JD; SEP-D §1 ("A group of analytic philosophers wrote an open letter ... to the Times of London"); IEP-D §1].
- Philosophers of phenomenology (J. Claude Evans 1991; also Bernet, Brough, Mohanty, Zahavi) criticised his reading of Husserl [source: SEP-D §1].
Replies:
- Derrida (*Limited Inc* Afterword, 1988): "il n'y a pas de hors-texte" means "there is nothing outside context"; what Austin treats as parasitic (citation, non-serious use) is an "essential possibility" of all language — "one neither can nor ought ... to exclude the possibility of transgression" [source: WP-JD; WP-SD].
- IEP-D: Searle's criticisms were "perhaps unfairly at times"; the antipathy shows "that deconstruction challenges traditional philosophy in several important ways" [source: IEP-D §1]. Commentators read the exchange as "a series of elaborate misunderstandings" or a win for one side [source: WP-SD].
- On self-reference: SEP-PM notes that Habermas's close reading "testifies to their intelligibility" [source: SEP-PM §9].
- Habermas and Derrida later reconciled; in 2003 Derrida co-signed Habermas's text against the Iraq war (already in published *Critical Theory* chapter, from WP Jürgen_Habermas) — consistent.

## Derrida: ethics and politics
- 1990s "turn" to politics and religion: "Force of Law" (1989–90; the "mystical foundation of authority"): three aporias of law and justice — "the epoche of the rule", "the ghost of the undecidable", "the urgency that obstructs the horizon of knowledge"; "A decision that did not go through the ordeal of the undecidable would not be a free decision"; justice is "impossible" and always "to come" [source: SEP-D §5].
- *Spectres de Marx* (Galilée, 1993; *Specters of Marx*, tr. Peggy Kamuf), first given as lectures at the "Whither Marxism?" conference (University of California, Riverside); against Fukuyama's triumphalism; "democracy to come"; a "New International"; deconstructed Marxism as "a new messianism" [source: SEP-D §1; WP Specters_of_Marx]. Cross-ref *Marx*.
- Hospitality: conditional versus unconditional hospitality ("A Word of Welcome", 1996, on Levinas) [source: SEP-D §1; WP-JD]. *The Gift of Death* (1992; Abraham and Kierkegaard) [source: IEP-D; consistent with *Schopenhauer and Kierkegaard* notes]. *Rogues* (2003): the most democratic states can be the most "roguish" [source: SEP-D §1].
- Third definition of deconstruction ("Et Cetera", 2000): gift, hospitality, death "can be possible only as impossible" [source: SEP-D §5].

## Deleuze (and Guattari)
- Gilles Deleuze, born Paris 18 January 1925, died by suicide 4 November 1995; older brother arrested for resistance and died on the train to Auschwitz [source: SEP-DE §1; WP Gilles_Deleuze].
- First book on Hume, *Empiricism and Subjectivity* (1953) — a provocation when peers focused on "the three 'H's" (Hegel, Husserl, Heidegger) [source: SEP-DE §1].
- *Nietzsche and Philosophy* (1962): genealogy as Nietzsche's genetic method, will to power as his principle of difference; anti-Hegelian focus on affirmative "active" forces [source: SEP-DE §§1–2; consistent with published *Nietzsche* chapter].
- *Difference and Repetition* (1968), primary doctoral thesis (secondary: *Spinoza and the Problem of Expression*); seeks the genesis of real experience, not conditions of possible experience; a "philosophy of difference"; "I feel myself to be a pure metaphysician" [source: SEP-DE intro, §3.1].
- Taught at Vincennes/Paris VIII from 1969 to 1987 [source: SEP-DE §1].
- Met Félix Guattari (1930–1992), radical psychoanalyst and militant, in 1969 (SEP-DE §1; the SEP intro says 1968 — internal inconsistency; say "in 1968–69") [source: SEP-DE; WP Félix_Guattari].
- *Capitalism and Schizophrenia*: *Anti-Oedipus* (1972; English 1977 tr. Hurley, Seem, Lane), a "succès de scandale"; "desiring-production" crisscrossing Marx and Freud; "body without organs" (from Artaud) [source: SEP-DE §4.1; WP Anti-Oedipus]. Foucault wrote a preface to the English edition [source: IEP-F §1].
- *A Thousand Plateaus* (1980; tr. Brian Massumi, 1987), written as a "rhizome" — "allowing immediate connections between any of its points" — in fourteen "plateaus" [source: SEP-DE §4.2; WP A_Thousand_Plateaus]. "The tree is filiation, but the rhizome is alliance, uniquely alliance. The tree imposes the verb 'to be,' but the fabric of the rhizome is the conjunction, 'and... and... and...'" (tr. Massumi, "Introduction: Rhizome") [source: WP Rhizome_(philosophy)].
- *What is Philosophy?* (1991): philosophy as the creation of concepts [source: SEP-DE intro].
- Lyotard called Deleuze a "library of Babel" [source: SEP-DE intro].
- Critics: feminists Alice Jardine (1985) and Luce Irigaray on "becoming-woman"; Alain Badiou (1997): Deleuze is a philosopher of the One, not the multiple; Žižek, Hallward [source: SEP-DE §6].

## Lyotard and the postmodern condition
- Jean-François Lyotard, born Vincennes 10 August 1924, died of leukaemia in Paris, April 1998 (21 April per WP) [source: SEP-LY §1; IEP-LY §1; WP].
- Taught philosophy at a lycée in Constantine, Algeria; joined Socialisme ou Barbarie (with Castoriadis and Lefort) in 1954, writing on the Algerian war [source: SEP-LY §1; IEP-LY §1b].
- *La Condition postmoderne: rapport sur le savoir* (Minuit, 1979), written at the request of the Council of Universities of Quebec's provincial government; English *The Postmodern Condition: A Report on Knowledge*, tr. Geoff Bennington and Brian Massumi (Manchester/Minnesota, 1984); his best-known work "often to his chagrin" [source: SEP-LY intro; IEP-LY bibliography; WP The_Postmodern_Condition].
- "The term 'postmodern' came into the philosophical lexicon" with this book (already in use in the arts) [source: SEP-PM §2; SEP-LY].
- Grand narratives named: "the dialectics of Spirit, the hermeneutics of meaning, the emancipation of the rational or working subject, or the creation of wealth" [source: SEP-LY §3.2; SEP-PM §2]. IEP-LY's two main metanarratives: history as progress toward emancipation; knowledge as progress toward totalisation [source: IEP-LY].
- Other theses: knowledge becomes information in computerised societies; legitimation by "performativity" (efficiency); Wittgensteinian language games; paralogy; against consensus as the goal of discourse (aimed at Habermas: Lyotard 1984 [1979], 65–66) [source: SEP-PM §2, §9].
- *The Differend* (1983): a differend arises when a conflict between genres of discourse cannot be judged without silencing one side; context after the Shoah [source: SEP-LY §3.3].
- Critics later used "postmodernism" as a pejorative umbrella (Kristeva, Foucault, Derrida, Deleuze) for supposed "epistemological and moral nihilism"; SEP-LY argues Lyotard offers "not an 'anything goes' relativism" [source: SEP-LY intro].

## Critics and uses
- **Habermas**, *Der philosophische Diskurs der Moderne: Zwölf Vorlesungen* (1985; *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity: Twelve Lectures*, tr. Frederick Lawrence, 1987) — "the most prominent and comprehensive critic of philosophical postmodernism"; defends "argumentative reason in inter-subjective communication"; charges performative contradiction and an "illicit aestheticization of knowledge and public discourse" [source: SEP-PM intro, §9; WP The_Philosophical_Discourse_of_Modernity]. Habermas died 14 March 2026 (per published *Critical Theory* chapter).
- **Sokal affair**: physicist Alan Sokal's hoax article "Transgressing the Boundaries: Towards a Transformative Hermeneutics of Quantum Gravity" appeared in *Social Text* 46/47, Spring/Summer 1996 ("Science Wars" issue) [source: WP Sokal_affair].
- *Impostures intellectuelles* (Odile Jacob, 1997) by Sokal and Jean Bricmont; English *Fashionable Nonsense: Postmodern Intellectuals' Abuse of Science* (US, Picador 1998; UK *Intellectual Impostures*) [source: WP Fashionable_Nonsense; SEP-DE bibliography]. SCOPE: chapters on Lacan, Kristeva, Irigaray, Latour, Baudrillard, Deleuze and Guattari, Virilio (plus Bergson in the French edition only); the authors say they do not analyse postmodern thought in general but misuse of maths and physics. Derrida appears only for one remark at the 1966 conference, and Foucault is not among the targets [source: WP Fashionable_Nonsense]. Derrida's reply in *Le Monde*: "Sokal and Bricmont Aren't Serious" [source: WP Fashionable_Nonsense]. SEP-DE says Deleuze knew the finitist history of the calculus "despite Sokal and Bricmont's 1999 intimations otherwise" [source: SEP-DE §6.3].
- Searle (1990): "The spread of 'poststructuralist' literary theory is perhaps the best-known example of a silly but non-catastrophic phenomenon" [source: WP-PS].
- **Feminist uses**: Judith Butler, *Gender Trouble: Feminism and the Subversion of Identity* (Routledge, 1990): gender is performative, sustained by repeated acts; draws on Foucault's analysis of subjection: "juridical systems of power produce the subjects they subsequently come to represent" (1990, 2); feminist critique must ask how "the category of 'women', the subject of feminism, is produced and restrained by the very structures of power through which emancipation is sought" (1990, 2) [source: SEP-FP §3.5; SEP-F §3.5; WP Gender_Trouble]. Butler uses she/her and they/them pronouns [source: WP Gender_Trouble].
- **Postcolonial uses**: Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak (born Calcutta 24 February 1942; Columbia) — "Can the Subaltern Speak?" in Cary Nelson and Lawrence Grossberg (eds.), *Marxism and the Interpretation of Culture* (Urbana: University of Illinois Press, 1988), 271–313; it opens by criticising a Foucault–Deleuze exchange for claiming the oppressed "can speak and know their conditions" and ignoring the "epistemic violence of imperialism"; through the British ban on sati, "the subaltern as female is even more deeply in shadow"; "widely viewed as the watershed text in postcolonial feminism" [source: SEP-FP §3.6; WP Subaltern; WP Spivak]. (The Foucault–Deleuze exchange is "Intellectuals and Power", 1972 — title from memory, not verified.)
- Edward Said, *Orientalism* (1978): Western representations of the "Orient" as a discourse tied to power; foundational for postcolonial studies; method derived from Derrida and Foucault [source: WP Orientalism_(book); SEP-FP §3.6 "postcolonial theory is deeply (though not uncritically) influenced by poststruturalism, particularly the work of Foucault and Jacques Derrida"].
- Women in the movement: Julia Kristeva, Hélène Cixous (who called the 1960s generation "the incorruptibles"), Luce Irigaray, Sarah Kofman (Nietzsche — in published chapter), Dina Lévi-Strauss [sources: SEP-D §2; SEP-DE §6.1; WP-LS].

## Debates
- **Is Foucault normatively confused?** Fraser, Habermas ("crypto-normativist", performative contradiction), Taylor (power needs freedom and truth) vs Allen 2008 and Oksala 2005, and the late Foucault on critique and self-formation [sources: SEP-FP §3.5; SEP-PM §9; WP-MF; SEP-F §§3.6, 4].
- **Is panopticism the point of *Discipline and Punish*?** Popular reading vs IEP-F (the central claim is that prisons produce delinquency); historians (Semple on Bentham; Garland, Alford, Gay on evidence) [sources: IEP-F §4a; WP-DP; SEP Bentham via published *Utilitarianism* chapter].
