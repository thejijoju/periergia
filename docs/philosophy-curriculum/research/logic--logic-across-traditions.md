## 1. Framing: is "logic" one subject across cultures?

- Gillon (SEP-Ind §1): logic "seeks to distinguish good reasoning from bad"; one may study reasoning from an ontic perspective (when do facts require other facts), an epistemic one (when does knowledge of some facts permit knowledge of another), a dialectic one (when must someone who accepts some statements accept another), or a linguistic one (using the forms of linguistic expressions to identify forms of inference). "The study of reasoning in India has been from the ontic, epistemic and dialectic perspective, and not from the linguistic perspective, the perspective best known to modern thinkers." [source: SEP-Ind §1] — A strong organising idea for the whole chapter.
- Gillon also: "inference and argument are but two sides of the same coin: an argument can be thought, and hence become an inference; an inference can be expressed, and hence become an argument." [source: SEP-Ind §1]
- Tillemans (SEP-Dhk §3.1): since Stcherbatsky's two-volume *Buddhist Logic* (1930/32), the Buddhist theory of reasons is often called "logic", "a term that sometimes has the unwanted effect of leading readers to think that good reasons for Buddhists are simply those that are formally valid. In fact, the notion of formal validity … is not itself explicitly discussed by Buddhist theoreticians". [source: SEP-Dhk §3.1]
- Phillips (SEP-Gan §5): "inference throughout Nyāya is part of epistemology. Something about the world is known by any veritable inference. An inference must have premises that are themselves known. Nyāya philosophers do not distinguish between a formally valid deductive argument (which may or may not have false premises) and deductive arguments that are 'cogent'". [source: SEP-Gan §5]
- Bocheński: *Formale Logik* (Freiburg/Munich: Karl Alber, 1956); English tr. Ivo Thomas, *A History of Formal Logic* (Notre Dame, 1961; 2nd ed. 1970). [source: SEP-Ind bibliography] It has a chapter on Indian logic; Tillemans: "Not inappropriately, the great historian of logic, Innocentius Bocheński, in the chapter on Indian Logic in his History of Formal Logic, stated that the Hetucakra thus suggested an awareness of formal considerations." [source: SEP-Dhk §3.1] A 1962 review reports that Bocheński treats four varieties of logic — ancient, scholastic, mathematical and Indian — as aspects of one logic [WS-6; secondary, weaker]. (Use "four varieties" only with "according to a contemporary review"; the "two original varieties" wording was NOT found.)
- Ganeri: "Introduction: Indian Logic and the Colonization of Reason" is the INTRODUCTION to Jonardon Ganeri (ed.), *Indian Logic: A Reader* (Richmond: Curzon/Routledge, 2001), which reprints essays from Colebrooke's 1824 essay on Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika onward [WS-3, WS-4; SEP-Ind bibliography confirms "Ganeri, Jonardon (ed.), 2001, Indian logic: a reader, Richmond: Curzon"]. It is NOT a stand-alone book. Related: Ganeri, "The Hindu Syllogism: Nineteenth-century perceptions of Indian logical thought", *Philosophy East and West* (1996): after H. T. Colebrooke's 1824 "discovery" of the "Hindu syllogism" (his term for the Nyāya-sūtra five-step schema), European logicians showed considerable interest, but later writers downplayed Indian rationalist traditions, feeding a myth that Indian thought is arational [WS-4, abstract only]. Do not attribute more specific arguments to Ganeri's introduction than this.
- China: SEP-CLL: the Mohists "made no attempt at investigating formal logic independently of their interests in analyzing the semantics of the terms of language (ming, or 'names')"; they "developed a clear theory of inference … but argued that the rules governing inference are partly determined by the semantics of individual terms and terms in combination, as opposed to rules merely operating on an argument's logical form." [source: SEP-CLL intro]
- SEP-CLL: "logic as a unique object of study or discipline disentangled from other philosophical pursuits was historically awakened in Chinese philosophical thought only after the introduction of Western philosophical notions and methods in the 16th and 17th centuries" (Matteo Ricci arrived 1583, arranged a translation of Euclid with Xu Guangqi); the demise of Mohism "appears to have squelched philosophical interest in the study of language and logic mostly until modern times, when it was revived in 1894" by Sun Yirang (Graham). Caution "to avoid anachronistically imposing notions" (Garrett 1993). [source: SEP-CLL §1]
- SEP-CLL: much scholarship of the past 50 years has sought similarities with Western logic (Zong 2000) or claimed a formal programme comparable to Aristotle or the Stoics (Zhang and Liu 2007); Harbsmeier (1998): "The aim of logical analysis was to establish a correct description of the world". [source: SEP-CLL §3]
- Fraser (SEP-MC intro): the later Mohists "decisively refute the once widespread view that ancient Chinese thinkers were concerned exclusively with ethics, moral psychology, and nature mysticism and uninterested in language, epistemology, and logic." But (SEP-MC §7; SEP-Moh §5): "They do not investigate formal logic or deductive inference, nor formulate an explicit notion of logical consequence"; they apply versions of excluded middle and non-contradiction, notions of "permissibility" (*kě*) and "perversity" (*bèi*), and "a rigorous system of quantifiers". [source: SEP-MC §7; SEP-Moh §5]
- Hansen's thesis (SEP-Moh §4): Hansen (1985, 1992) proposed that early Chinese theorists address not semantic truth but the pragmatic question "whether a certain way of using words is appropriate"; criticised by Graham 1989 and Harbsmeier 1998; Fraser: "Construed as the claim that the Mohists are wholly unconcerned with or lack a concept corresponding to semantic truth, Hansen's thesis is unconvincing", since "this" (*shi*), "fitting" (*dang*) and "so" (*ran*) overlap enough with semantic truth; the Mohists also allow that a claim meeting their standards may still fail to fit the "facts" (*qing*). [source: SEP-Moh §4] SEP-CLL: Hansen (1983) "even rejects the view that early Chinese philosophers were concerned with articulating theories of truth". [source: SEP-CLL §3]
- Arabic logic's "Islamic" character: Street: "Arabic logic can be said to be Islamic in two senses, both—in my opinion—of limited significance": the conquests made Arabic the language of learning, but "the religion of the conquerors played no significant role in the development of the subject"; and after the 13th century logic found a place in madrasa education. He prefers the term "Arabic logic". [source: SEP-AIL intro]

## 2. Aristotle's syllogistic (cross-ref *Aristotle*, *Arguments*)

- Organon: "The ancient commentators grouped together several of Aristotle's treatises under the title Organon ('Instrument')": *Categories, On Interpretation, Prior Analytics, Posterior Analytics, Topics, On Sophistical Refutations*. The title "reflects a much later controversy about whether logic is a part of philosophy (as the Stoics maintained) or merely a tool used by philosophy (as the later Peripatetics thought)"; Aristotle never uses the term. [source: SEP-AL §2] Bobzien: collected and ordered "by later Peripatetics … because they considered logic not a part but rather an instrument of philosophy". [source: SEP-AncLog §2] (No source read names Andronicus of Rhodes as the editor — do not name him.)
- Bobzien: "Aristotle is the first great logician in the history of logic. His logic was taught by and large without rival from the 4th to the 19th centuries CE." (sic: "CE" in SEP; the published Aristotle chapter copies it.) [source: SEP-AncLog §2]
- Eubulides of Miletus (mid-4th c. BCE) "is on record as the inventor of both the Liar and the Sorites paradox"; Plato's *Euthydemus* "contains a large collection of contemporary fallacies". [source: SEP-AncLog intro, §1]
- Figures: by position of the middle term — first figure: middle is subject of one premise and predicate of the other; second: predicate of both; third: subject of both. [source: SEP-AL §5.1; SEP-AncLog §2.4]
- The count: Bobzien: Aristotle "went systematically through the fifty-eight possible premise combinations and showed that fourteen have a conclusion following of necessity"; he rejects "thirty-four premise combinations" by counterexample. CAVEAT: 14 + 34 = 48, and 3 figures × 16 premise pairs = 48; "fifty-eight" in SEP appears to be a slip. Safest wording: "fourteen valid combinations; the rest rejected by counterexample". [source: SEP-AncLog §2.4]
- The fourteen moods: first figure Barbara, Celarent, Darii, Ferio; second Camestres, Cesare, Festino, Baroco; third Darapti, Felapton, Disamis, Datisi, Bocardo, Ferison. The names are later mnemonics: the vowels give the A/E/I/O types of premises and conclusion. [source: SEP-AncLog §2.4]
- Perfect vs imperfect: a deduction is perfect (*teleios*) if it "needs no external term in order to show the necessary result" (24b23–24), imperfect if it "needs one or several in addition…" (24b24–25). First-figure deductions are perfect ("evident"); second and third are completed by "reduction" (*anagein*) to the first. [source: SEP-AL §5.2; SEP-AncLog §2.4]
- Three methods: conversion (*antistrophê*: from AeB infer BeA; AiB → BiA; AaB → BiA); reductio ad impossibile (*apagôgê*) for the two moods that conversion cannot handle (Baroco and Bocardo); exposition (*ekthesis*), whose status as proof is debated. [source: SEP-AncLog §2.4; SEP-AL §5.6.4 names Baroco and Bocardo]
- Metatheorem: Aristotle reduced the particular first-figure moods to second-figure ones, "thus de facto reducing all syllogisms to Barbara and Celarent". "From a modern perspective, Aristotle's system can be understood as a sequent logic in the style of natural deduction". [source: SEP-AncLog §2.4; SEP-AL §5]
- Metatheory: Lear: "Aristotle shares with modern logicians a fundamental interest in metatheory". [source: SEP-AL §1]
- Modal syllogistic (Prior Analytics 1.8–22): premises necessary (N), possible/contingent, or assertoric; Smith: it "appears to be much less satisfactory and is certainly far more difficult to interpret". "Problem of the two Barbaras": Aristotle accepts Barbara NAN (necessary major, assertoric minor, necessary conclusion) but rejects Barbara ANN; Theophrastus instead adopted the rule that the conclusion follows the "weakest" premise ("peiorem rule" — that Latin label not in source; avoid). Bobzien: "As Aristotle's theory is conceivably internally inconsistent, the formal models that have been suggested may all be unsuccessful"; Malink 2013 offers a full reconstruction. [source: SEP-AL §5.6; SEP-AncLog §2.5]
- "Kant … even held that nothing significant had been added to Aristotle's views in the intervening two millennia"; Prantl drew the corollary that any later logician who said anything new was "confused, stupid, or perverse". [source: SEP-AL §1, §6]
- Galen (2nd c. CE) tried to synthesise Peripatetic and Stoic logic and claimed to introduce a third kind, the "relational syllogism"; Aristotelian syllogistic came to be called "categorical", the Peripatetic adaptation of Stoic syllogistic "hypothetical". [source: SEP-AncLog §6]

## 3. Stoic logic (cross-ref *Hellenistic Philosophy*)

- Diodorus Cronus and his pupil Philo ("sometimes called 'Philo of Megara'"), later 4th to mid-3rd c. BCE, "conceived of logic as a logic of propositions"; no writings survive. [source: SEP-AncLog §4]
- Philo's conditional: false "when and only when its antecedent is true and its consequent is false"; Philo "may be credited with introducing truth-functionality into logic"; resembles material implication but can change truth-value over time. Diodorus: a conditional is true "if it neither was nor is possible that its antecedent is true and its consequent false" — "reminiscent of strict implication". Both lead to versions of the paradoxes of material and strict implication, "a fact the ancients were aware of" (Sextus, M 8.109–117). [source: SEP-AncLog §4] (Arguments already used the paradoxes; mention only.)
- Chrysippus' conditional (a third view): true "when the contradictory of the consequent is incompatible with its antecedent" (D.L. 7.73). The Philonian conditional was expressed as "not both p and not q". [source: SEP-AncLog §5.2] — So three rival conditionals: Philo (truth-functional), Diodorus (at all times), Chrysippus (incompatibility).
- Diodorus' Master Argument: incompatibility of (i) every past truth is necessary, (ii) the impossible does not follow from the possible, (iii) something is possible which neither is nor will be true; the argument has not survived. [source: SEP-AncLog §4]
- Zeno of Citium (335–263 BCE) studied with Diodorus. Chrysippus of Soli (c. 280–207) "is without doubt the second great logician in the history of logic"; "if the gods used any logic, it would be that of Chrysippus" (D.L. 7.180); "over 300 books on logic"; only two badly damaged papyri survive, plus fragments (D.L. book 7; Sextus PH 2, M 8). [source: SEP-AncLog §5]
- Sayables (*lekta*): "the underlying meanings in everything we say and think, but—like Frege's 'senses'—also subsist independently of us"; complete vs deficient ("writes" prompts "who?"); assertibles (*axiômata*) are complete sayables with a truth-value, which can change over time ("it is day" true when it is day). [source: SEP-AncLog §5.1]
- Universal affirmatives rephrased as conditionals: "Every A is B" → "If something is A, it is B" (S.E. M 9.8–11). Negation is prefixed: "Not: Dion is walking"; Stoics' rule that the controlling particle comes first is "an implicit bracketing device similar to … Łukasiewicz' Polish notation". [source: SEP-AncLog §5.1–5.2]
- Stoic disjunction "is exclusive and non-truth-functional": true "when necessarily precisely one of its disjuncts is true". [source: SEP-AncLog §5.2]
- System: "a substructural backwards-working Gentzen-style natural-deduction system" of five indemonstrables plus four rules (*themata*); first thema = antilogism, third = a cut rule; only first and third survive. Modes use ordinals: "If the 1st, the 2nd. But not: the 2nd. Therefore not: the 1st." [source: SEP-AncLog §5.3–5.4]
- Bobzien's caveat (already in Arguments): indemonstrables were defined by meta-linguistic descriptions; "there are thirty-two modes corresponding to the five meta-linguistic descriptions"; the universal assumption that five modes represent them "is false and not supported by textual evidence". [source: SEP-AncLog §5.4] (Published Hellenistic chapter gives *p/q* schemas "in a modern simplification" — consistent if labelled.)
- Stoic logic "faded into oblivion" between the 3rd and 6th c. CE, "to be resurrected only in the 20th century, in the wake of the (re)-discovery of propositional logic". [source: SEP-AncLog §6]
- Liar and Sorites among Chrysippus' topics (D.L. 7.189–199). [source: SEP-AncLog §5] (Leave detail to *Paradoxes*.)

## 4. India I: Nyāya and the five-membered argument

- Nyāya-sūtra by Gautama/Akṣapāda "(c. 2nd ce)", redaction thought by some to be 3rd c. CE; mostly about epistemology and debate. Caraka-saṃhitā, "Classical India's earliest medical text", "conjectured by some to have been redacted in its current form at the beginning of first century ce"; its argument passages: CS 1.11.21–22, 3.4.1–14, 3.8.16–67. [source: SEP-Ind §3.1.3]
- Five members: both CS 3.8.31 and NS 1.1.32 give five parts, four with identical names; only Caraka illustrates. [source: SEP-Ind §3.2] Sanskrit names (IEP-Ny): *pratijñā* (thesis), *hetu* (reason/probans), *udāharaṇa* ("illustration of concomitance"), *upanaya* ("application of the rule"), *nigamana* (conclusion); "In practice, the five-membered 'syllogism' is often truncated into three steps". [source: IEP-Ny] (The stock example is already in Arguments; use the Sanskrit names and the history instead.)
- From analogy to deduction (Gillon): earliest example (Caraka) analogical; earliest deductive version in the *Fāng biàn xīn lùn* (*Upāya-hṛdaya*), whose author surprisingly REJECTS it; the *Rúshí lùn* (*Tarka-śāstra*) endorses a deductive form, rejects analogical ones, and states the "three forms (tri-rūpa)" of a good reason; Vasubandhu's *Vāda-vidhi* simplifies to thesis–reason–example ("That which has H has S, like d"), and is "absolutely clear that the example statement expresses an indispensability relation (a-vinā-bhāva)". [source: SEP-Ind §3.2]
- Tarka-śāstra example (deductive): "Sound is not eternal / because, arising due to an effort, it arises immediately / If a thing is eternal, (then) it does not arise due to an effort. For example, space … / Sound too is not that way / Therefore, sound is not eternal." [source: SEP-Ind §3.2]
- Earliest mention of the three forms: a brief passage in the non-logical *Shùn zhōng lùn* (attributed to Asaṅga), which REJECTS the criterion as "thought to be of a non-Buddhist origin" (H. Ui 1929, via Katsura). [source: SEP-Ind §3.2] → The three-mark criterion predates Dignāga.
- Vyāpti ("pervasion"): Gaṅgeśa's standard terms — *pakṣa* (inferential subject; the mountain), *sādhana/hetu* (prover; smokiness), *sādhya* (probandum; fieriness), *vyāpti* ∀x(Hx → Sx), "Wherever smoke, there fire"; *dṛṣṭānta* (example: a kitchen hearth; and a known absence of both). [source: SEP-Gan §5]
- Jain view on members: Jains accept the five-statement Nyāya model (example: "[Thesis] Sound is impermanent, [Evidence] Because it is a product. [Account] Whatever is such is alike, like a pot. [Application] And this is a product. [Conclusion] Therefore it is impermanent.") but hold only the first two necessary; Siddhasena: without the thesis it would be "like observing an archer without knowing what his target is". "Jaina philosophers are traditionally considered as the ones to go the furthest" in reducing the argument. [source: SEP-Jain §3.3]

## 5. India II: Buddhist logic — Dignāga and Dharmakīrti

- Dignāga: Gillon "c. 5th – 6th century ce"; Gorisse 480–540; WP c. 480–c. 540 (born near Kanchi per Tāranātha). "A pivotal figure in the history of Indian logic." Works: *Nyāya-mukha* (Chinese translation; a Sanskrit MS known in Tibet, unedited), *Hetu-cakra-ḍamaru* ("The drum wheel of reason", Tibetan only) and *Pramāṇa-samuccaya*, four of whose six chapters are on inference or argument. [source: SEP-Ind §4.1; SEP-Jain §3.3; WP-Dignāga]
- Inference for oneself (*svārtha-anumāna*, the cognitive process) vs for another (*parārtha-anumāna*, its verbal expression) — titles of chapters 2 and 3 of the *Pramāṇa-samuccaya*. [source: SEP-Ind §4.1] Gorisse: an inference for others "is metaphorically called" inference, as opposed to inference for oneself, "a genuine type of cognition". [source: SEP-Jain §3.3]
- Dignāga's canonical form (Katsura's reconstruction): "thesis: Sound is non-eternal / reason: because it is immediately connected with an effort / similarity example: That which is immediately connected with an effort is observed to be non-eternal, like a pot / dissimilarity example: That which is eternal is observed not to be immediately connected with an effort, like space." No application or conclusion. Uses *pakṣa* instead of *pratijñā*. [source: SEP-Ind §4.1]
- The three conditions (*trairūpya*) as formulated "from Dharmakīrti on" (SEP-Dhk §3.1): (1) *pakṣadharmatva*: the subject is ascertained as having the reason-property; (2) *anvayavyāpti* (pervasion as co-presence): the reason is present only in similar instances (*sapakṣa*); (3) *vyatirekavyāpti* (co-absence): the reason is wholly absent from dissimilar instances (*vipakṣa*). Dharmakīrti uses the particle *eva* to sharpen each (NB 2.5): "the reason's definite existence in the subject; … existence in subject-like things only; … utter non-existence in subject-unlike things". [source: SEP-Dhk §3.1; SEP-Ind §4.2]

### STEP-BY-STEP: the Wheel of Reasons (*hetucakra*)
- Gillon: "Perhaps most original in Dignāga's work … is what he called the wheel of reasons (hetu-cakra), an equivalent alternative to the three forms of reason". "It comprises a three by three matrix": the reason (H) occurs in all, none or some of the subject-like things (sapakṣa), and in all, none or some of the subject-unlike things (vipakṣa). "Dignāga identified the syllogisms corresponding to the top and bottom cases of the middle column as good syllogisms and those corresponding to the other cases as bad." [source: SEP-Ind §4.1]
- Table (WP-Hetucakra, "adapted from Matilal, 1998: p. 9"), + = all, ± = some, – = none: 1 (+sap, +vip); 2 (+sap, –vip); 3 (+sap, ±vip); 4 (–sap, +vip); 5 (–sap, –vip); 6 (–sap, ±vip); 7 (±sap, +vip); 8 (±sap, –vip); 9 (±sap, ±vip). Only 2 and 8 are sound — i.e. the reason is ABSENT FROM EVERY dissimilar case and present in at least one similar case. 4 and 6 are "contradictory" pseudo-reasons; 5 is "uniquely deviating" (*asādhāraṇa*) [source: WP-Hetucakra; consistent with SEP-Ind]. The remaining corners (1, 3, 7, 9) fail because the reason occurs in some dissimilar case (traditionally "inconclusive" — that label NOT verified in a read source; avoid or hedge).
- Worked reading (for the writer to build): with thesis "sound is non-eternal", reason "produced": produced things are found among non-eternal things (pots) and never among eternal things (space) → cell 2, good. The Mīmāṃsā-style "sound is eternal because audible": audible things other than sound do not exist, so the reason occurs in no similar case and no dissimilar case → cell 5, bad (Gillon discusses this exact example; see objection below). [source: SEP-Ind §4.1; Fallacies chapter already uses "eternal because audible" as a *counterbalanced* reason, so present it here as the Hetucakra's "uniquely deviating" case and note the cross-reference.]
- Bocheński: the Hetucakra, with operators "partial presence", "complete presence", "complete absence", yields "nine types of reasons, types that are abstracted from content and subject matter", suggesting "an awareness of formal considerations"; R. S. Y. Chi, *Buddhist Formal Logic* (1969; SEP-Dhk cites Chi 1984 reprint), went further. [source: SEP-Dhk §3.1; SEP-Ind bibliography]
- OBJECTION 1 (deductive or not?): "a rather lively debate": is Dignāga's canonical syllogism deductive or inductive? Reasons for doubt: the word "observed" (*dṛṣṭa*) in the example statement, and the retention of example phrases, which "have no bearing on the deductive validity". Gillon replies: "observed" is attached to the main clause, not the relative clause ("every instance of H is observed to be S", not "every observed H is S"), and if factive it leaves truth-conditions unchanged; examples exclude valid but unpersuasive arguments such as "Sound is eternal / because it is audible / Whatever is audible is eternal", where audibility is coextensive with sound so there is "no independent empirical evidence"; Dignāga "rules out the syllogism as a bad syllogism, rather than, as we would, accept it as a valid syllogism with an unpersuasive premiss." Others: Hayes (1980; 1988) reads Dignāga as developing inductive reasoning; Oetke (1994; 1996) defeasible reasoning (Taber 2004 criticises Oetke). [source: SEP-Ind §4.1]
- OBJECTION 2 (redundancy/inconsistency): the second and third forms are contrapositives, so one is "logically superfluous"; Dignāga acknowledges their equivalence (PS 2.5). If *sapakṣa* excludes the subject and *eva* is restrictive, "the three forms are inconsistent" (Gillon 1999). [source: SEP-Ind §4.1–4.2]
- OBJECTION 3 (Jain): "Jaina authors show that the three conditions of Dignāga are neither necessary, nor sufficient, and that what ultimately counts is that the evidence-property cannot be thought of otherwise than in the presence of the target-property" — *anyathānupapatti*, "impossible otherwise". [source: SEP-Jain §3.3]
- Dissent on originality: Anacker (2005): the hetucakra "sometimes held to be the first complete Indian formulation of what constitutes the validity and invalidity of an argument, is in fact nothing of the kind: it is a pedagogic device mapping out in detail what Vasubandhu's criteria already presuppose." [source: WP-Hetucakra quoting Anacker 2005, *Seven Works of Vasubandhu*]
- Who first? Whether the deductive canonical syllogism "spread from Dignāga to Praśastapāda, or from Praśastapāda to Dignāga, or from some other person to the two of them both has yet to be decided"; it "came to be adopted virtually by every classical Indian thinker and … made its way to China, Korea and Japan through the spread of Buddhism". [source: SEP-Ind §4.2]
- Dharmakīrti (c. 7th c. per Gillon; dates disputed per PUB-mahayana): *Pramāṇa-vārttika* — inference chapter (*svārtha-anumāna*) 340 verses with autocommentary; argument chapter (*parārtha-anumāna*) 285 verses; also *Pramāṇa-viniścaya*, *Nyāya-bindu*, *Hetu-bindu*, *Vāda-nyāya*. [source: SEP-Ind §4.2]
- The problem of the universal premise: knowing "whatever has H has S" seems to require already knowing the conclusion. Īśvarasena grounded it in non-perception; Dharmakīrti (PV svārtha vv. 13–25) objected that non-observation of an H-without-S is no guarantee; his answer: the pervasion is guaranteed by causation (*tadutpatti*) or identity (*tādātmya*). Gillon: "Dharmakīrti's solution does not work" (Gillon 1991; Steinkellner 2015 dissents). [source: SEP-Ind §4.2]
- Dharmakīrti's inference-for-others is two-membered: "All Cs are Bs, like D. A is C." The conclusion is omitted, and in the *Vādanyāya* stating it becomes "a 'point of defeat' (nigrahasthāna)" — because an inference-for-others only shows "provers" and "a conclusion cannot prove itself". Tillemans: parārthānumāna and Aristotelian syllogisms rest on "considerably different philosophies of logic". [source: SEP-Dhk §3.2] — Excellent contrast with Aristotle's syllogism, whose conclusion is essential.
- Good reasons require more than validity: soundness, ascertainment of the three characteristics, and the opponent's "desire to know" (*jijñāsā*): "formal", "factual", "epistemic" and "rhetorical considerations". [source: SEP-Dhk §3.1]
- Prasaṅga (consequence): "It would follow absurdly that A would be B, because of being C", leading by contraposition (*prasaṅgaviparyaya*) to "A is not C" — a reductio turning on modus tollens. [source: SEP-Dhk §3]
- Śaṅkarasvāmin (c. 6th c.), Dignāga's pupil, wrote the manual *Nyāya-praveśa* ("Beginning logic"). [source: SEP-Ind §4.2]

## 6. India III: Navya-Nyāya and the Jains

- Gaṅgeśa ("Great Professor", *mahôpādhyāya*), Mithilā, NE India, "first half of the fourteenth century"; Bhattacharya (1958) gives 1300 and 1350 as limits of his flourishing. Work: *Tattvacintāmaṇi*, "Jewel" — Phillips's translation title *Jewel of Reflection on the Truth about Epistemology* (3 vols, Bloomsbury 2020); WP renders it "Thought-Jewel of Reality". Four chapters, one per knowledge source: perception, inference, analogy, testimony. "Traditionally taken to inaugurate 'New' Logic, Navya Nyāya", but "the distinction between 'Old' and 'New' Nyāya is problematic"; some scholars make Udayana the originator. The Jewel displaced the commentary tradition on the Nyāya-sūtra. [source: SEP-Gan intro, §1; WP-Navya-Nyāya]
- Dating disagreement: WP-Navya-Nyāya says the school was "founded in the 13th century by … Gaṅgeśa"; SEP-Gan: first half of the 14th century; SEP-EMI: Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika merged "after 12th century". PUB-classical-indian-schools: "usually placed in the fourteenth century (older estimates say the thirteenth)" — consistent; keep that wording.
- Bhattacharya (1958): the inference chapter "is by far the most popular, though the most intricate"; Navya-Nyāya studies "flowed through a large number of channels cut by single sentences or phrases of this part". [source: SEP-Gan §1]
- Gaṅgeśa rejected twenty-four candidate definitions of pervasion before endorsing his own; his test case: "This tree possesses a monkey-conjunction, since it is this tree" (monkey in the branches, not at the roots). [source: SEP-Gan §5.1]
- Induction: repeated observation "even hundreds of times" is insufficient; earthen things scratchable by iron — "diamonds … are a counterexample". Gaṅgeśa answers the doubter: "It is the doubter's own behavior that proves the lie to the doubt". (Indian-dissenters already uses Phillips's paraphrase "the skeptic himself relies on such generalizations…" — avoid repeating.) [source: SEP-Gan §3–5]
- *Tarka* types recognised by Gaṅgeśa: self-dependence (begging the question), mutual dependence, circularity, infinite regress, unwanted consequence… [source: SEP-Gan §5.2]
- Technical language: Navya-Nyāya authors "develop a precise technical language through the employment of which many traditional philosophical problems could be clarified and resolved"; used also "in poetics, linguistics, legal theory"; the school developed for about four centuries, peaking with Raghunātha, Jagadīśa and Gadādhara; 17th–18th-c. manuals simplified it, notably Annambhaṭṭa's *Tarkasaṃgraha* (*Manual of Reason*), nicknamed *Bāla-gādādharī*. [source: SEP-EMI §1]
- Example of the technical language (SEP-EMI): "(This) pot is not in the room" vs "(Any) pot is not in the room" are the same sentence in Sanskrit; Nyāya distinguishes specific absence (*viśeṣābhāva*) from generic absence (*sāmānyābhāva*) by the "delimitor of counterpositiveness" (*pratiyogitāvacchedaka*): this-pot-hood vs pothood. Pervasion "where there is smoke there is fire" is contraposed and expressed as a generic absence of smoke wherever there is a generic absence of fire (Ingalls 1951: 54–5). [source: SEP-EMI]
- De Morgan's laws in Navya-Nyāya: "absence of (both A and B) = (absence of A) or (absence of B), and absence of (either A or B) = (absence of A) and (absence of B)" (Ingalls 1951: 63–67) — "recognisably versions of De Morgan's Laws". [source: SEP-EMI] — Good comparative point (De Morgan, 1847).
- Ingalls: Daniel H. H. Ingalls Sr. (1916–1999), American professor of Sanskrit; *Materials for the Study of Navya-Nyāya Logic* (Harvard UP, 1951). [source: WP-Daniel_H._H._Ingalls_Sr.; SEP-Gan bibliography]
- Matilal: *The Navya-Nyāya Doctrine of Negation* (Harvard UP, 1968); *Epistemology, Logic, and Grammar in Indian Philosophical Analysis* (1971); *Logic, Language, and Reality* (1985); *The Character of Logic in India* (ed. Ganeri & Tiwari, SUNY 1998, posthumous); *The Central Philosophy of Jainism (anekānta-vāda)* (1981). [source: WP-Navya-Nyāya; SEP-Ind bibliography; SEP-Jain bibliography]
- Jain logic: systematic analysis of inference begins with Siddhasena Mahāmati (710–780 CE; *Nyāyāvatāra*, "Guide of Logic") and Akalaṅka (720–780); continues to Vādi Devasūri (1143) and Yaśovijaya (1638–1688), *Jainatarkabhāṣā* ("Manual of Jain Logic"). Jain authors referenced the Nyāya-sūtra and engaged Dignāga and Dharmakīrti. [source: SEP-Jain §3.3] (Dates per Gorisse; others differ.)
- Seven modes of predication: Samantabhadra's *Āptamīmāṃsā* "amongst the first to teach" the first four (Āmī 14: "An entity is somehow possessed of the character 'being', somehow … 'non-being', somehow … both, and somehow indescribable"); seven modes: exists; does not exist; exists and does not exist; inexpressible; exists and inexpressible; does not exist and inexpressible; exists, does not exist and inexpressible. [source: SEP-Jain §3.4.3] IEP-Jain renders each with "Perhaps a is F…". [source: IEP-Jain] (Full list already in Indian-dissenters; here mention only, framed as a logic of perspective-indexed predication.)
- Gorisse: the Jain doctrines of many-sidedness are "probably their major contribution to philosophy". [source: SEP-Jain §3.4.4]

## 7. China: Mohists, the School of Names, Xunzi

- Canons: compiled "most likely between the late 4th and mid 3rd century B.C."; six books conventionally called the *Mò Biàn* ("Mohist Dialectics"): Canons and Explanations, "Greater Selection" (*Dà Qǔ*), "Lesser Selection" (*Xiǎo Qǔ*); origin of the titles unknown; Canons begin with 87 definitions. Fraser: texts "the most difficult and corrupt in all the classical Chinese literature" (already quoted in PUB-hundred-schools). [source: SEP-MC §1–2]
- *Biàn* defined in the Lesser Selection (Fraser tr.): "Disputation (biàn 辯), by it we clarify divisions between this (shì 是) and not-this (fēi 非) to examine the guidelines of order and disorder; clarify points of sameness and difference to discern the patterns of names (míng 名) and stuff (shí 實); and settle benefit and harm to resolve uncertainty and doubt". [source: SEP-MC §7]
- Four techniques of the Lesser Selection (Fraser tr.): Analogy (*pì* 譬): "Presenting other things and using them to clarify it." Paralleling (*móu* 侔): "Placing expressions side by side and jointly proceeding." Pulling (*yuán* 援): "Saying, 'You are so, how is it that I alone cannot be so?'" Pushing (*tuī* 推): "On the grounds that what they don't accept is the same as what they do accept, propose it." Paralleling "has no obvious counterpart in other logical traditions". [source: SEP-MC §7.2]
- Paralleling examples (NEW; not in Arguments): "White horses are horses; riding white horses is riding horses. Black horses are horses; riding black horses is riding horses" → "Brown horses…", "Yellow dogs are dogs; feeding yellow dogs is feeding dogs." And the other pattern: "Reading books isn't books; liking reading books is liking books"; "Being about to fall into a well isn't falling into a well; stopping someone about to fall into a well is stopping someone from falling into a well" → by analogy "Holding there is fate isn't fate; rejecting holding there is fate is rejecting fate" (an anti-fatalist conclusion). Form: "x is not y, Fx is Fy". [source: SEP-MC §7.2]
- The Mohists' own warning (Fraser tr.): "Things have respects in which they are similar, yet it doesn't follow that they are completely similar. Parallels between expressions are correct only up to a point. … Hence expressions in analogies, paralleling, 'pulling,' and 'pushing' become different as they proceed, become dangerous as they change direction, fail when taken too far, and leave their roots as they flow, and so one cannot fail to be cautious and cannot invariably use them." [source: SEP-MC §7.3]
- Quantifier asymmetry (NEW; Graham tr.): "Loving people requires loving all people without exception … Not loving people does not require loving no people at all … Riding horses does not require riding all horses without exception; it is (rather) riding some horses … But not riding horses does require riding no horses at all". (Xiao Qu, tr. Graham 2003 [1978]) — i.e. the same grammatical form hides "all" in one case and "some" in the other. [source: SEP-CLL §3]
- Quantifiers defined: Canon A43 defines "all" (*jin*) via "none" (*mo*): "All is none not being so"; the Greater Selection: "Some is not all". [source: SEP-CLL §3]
- Fǎ and analogy: judgement as distinguishing kinds by "models" (*fǎ*); "extending kinds" (*tuī lèi*); the "difficulty of extending kinds". [source: SEP-MC §§1, 7.1]
- School of Names: disputation "rooted partly in the practice of litigation, partly in the rhetoric used by court advisors"; "Primarily a type of analogical argumentation … often took the form of citing a precedent, analogy, or model (fa, also 'law')". [source: SEP-SN §1]
- Gongsun Long (c. 320–250 BCE), retainer of the Lord of Pingyuan (d. 252 BCE) in Zhao; anecdotes in Zhuangzi and *Annals of Lü Buwei*; cited the Mohist "all-inclusive care" to King Hui; "John Doe has three ears" debate with Kong Chuan (plurals unmarked: "has ear(s)" plus left and right). Of the *Gongsunlongzi* (5 dialogues + late introduction; Han History lists 14 scrolls), Graham argued three dialogues are later forgeries; "Probably only the 'White Horse,' the essay 'Indicating Things,' and a bit of another dialogue are genuine pre-Han texts." [source: SEP-SN §6]

### STEP-BY-STEP: "White horse is not horse" (new angles beyond PUB-hundred-schools)
- The text has five arguments. (Args 1 and 2 already in PUB-hundred-schools; use Arg 3 or 4 and the interpretations.) Argument 3 (Fraser tr.): "Horses indeed have color; thus there are white horses. Supposing horses had no color, and there were simply horses and that's all, how could we pick out the white horses? So white is not horse. White horse is horse combined with white. Is horse combined with white the same as horse? So I say: White horse is not horse." Argument 4 trades on construing "brown horse is horse" as identity ("flying things entering a pond, inner and outer coffins in different places"). [source: SEP-SN §6.1]
- Gongsun Long's own defence via Confucius and the King of Chu's lost bow ("A person lost a bow, a person will find it… Why must it be 'Chu'?"): Confucius "took Chu people to be different from what's called 'people'"; Kong Chuan replies that omitting "Chu" broadens the referent. Harbsmeier: the book's editors took the theme to be how the extension of "person"/"horse" varies when modified. [source: SEP-SN §6.1]
- Interpretations (SEP-SN §6.1): universals (Fung 1958, Cheng 1983 — now broadly rejected among European and American scholars, "since no ancient Chinese philosopher held a realist doctrine of universals"); kind and identity relations (Cikoski 1975, Harbsmeier 1998); part-whole relations (Hansen 1983, Graham 1989); how phrase extensions differ from constituents (Hansen 1992); use/mention (Thompson 1995); Fraser: ambiguity between predication and identity, with an implicit Leibniz's-law principle in Argument 2. Graham: "the difficulty of finding an angle of approach from which the arguments will make sense" (1989: 82). [source: SEP-SN §6.1]
- Hansen's MASS-NOUN hypothesis: Hansen, "Mass Nouns and 'A White Horse Is Not a Horse'", *Philosophy East and West* (1976), developed in *Language and Logic in Ancient China* (Ann Arbor: University of Michigan Press, 1983): classical Chinese nouns behave like mass nouns ("horse" like "water"), so "white horse" names the mereological sum of white-stuff and horse-stuff, which is not the same as horse-stuff; he argues there is no Platonic realism in ancient China. Graham endorsed the mereological reading without the mass-noun hypothesis; critics note linguistic work finding mass/count distinctions in classical Chinese. [WS-2 (secondary summaries: HKU preface, encyclopedia entries) — weaker; SEP-SN confirms only "part-whole relations (Hansen 1983, Graham 1989)"; SEP-CLL bibliography confirms the 1983 book.] Present as "Hansen's influential proposal", not settled.
- Xunzi on the white horse: the *Xunzi* "does cite a version of his white horse sophism in a list of incorrect uses of names (22.3)". [source: SEP-SN §6] Goldin: Xunzi divides the sophists' paradoxes "into three typological categories" and concludes: "All heretical theories and aberrant sayings depart from the correct Way and are presumptuously crafted according to these three categories of delusion" (Xunzi 22.3d). [source: SEP-Xun §7]
- Xunzi "Rectifying Names" (*Zhengming*, Book 22): Goldin: "the primary concern of the chapter is morality, not linguistics" (Fraser 2016); "Names have no inherent appropriateness. We designate them [by some word] in order to name them" (22.2g) — sounds like Saussure but is not about the same questions; naming is the king's task ("When one who is a king determines names…", 22.1c). [source: SEP-Xun §7] IEP: Xunzi "defended a modified conventionalism"; "One subject that was certainly not part of Xunzi's program of study is logic"; "no patience for the dialectical games". [source: IEP-Xun §4] Contrast SEP-CLL: Cua (1985) holds "methods of disputation reach their highest point in the writings of Xunzi" — a DEBATE in miniature.
- Graham's translation of Xunzi 22 (quoted in SEP-CLL): "…Therefore the wise made for them apportionments and distinctions and instituted names to point out objects, in the first place in order to clarify noble and base, secondly to distinguish same and different. … This is the purpose of having names." (tr. Graham 1989) [source: SEP-CLL intro]
- Hui Shi: ten theses (already in PUB-hundred-schools). Fraser: several focus on indexicals (Hansen 1992: 262); thesis 5 on "the same" and "different" may be the key. [source: SEP-SN §5]

## 8. Arabic logic (cross-ref *Falsafa*, *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes…*)

- Tradition "from the mid-eighth century down to today"; foundational figures Alfarabi (d. 950), Avicenna (d. 1037), Averroes (d. 1198). [source: SEP-AIL intro]
- Abū Bishr Mattā ibn Yūnus (d. 940), Syriac Christian, added translations of the *Poetics* and *Posterior Analytics* from Syriac "in the early 900s". [source: SEP-AIL §1.2]
- The debate: "a disputation on the relative merits of grammar and logic convened for the amusement of the Vizier"; Sīrāfī "confounds him with a series of grammatical subtleties"; Mattā's reply begins "This is grammar, and I have not studied grammar. The logician has no need of grammar, whereas the grammarian does need logic…" and continues "Now, the meaning is more exalted than the expression, and the expression humbler than the meaning." Translation: Abed 1991: xvi (minor changes, Street); full translation in Margoliouth 1905. Street: Mattā's view "weathered Sîrâfî's storm of criticism badly". [source: SEP-AIL §1.2 and note 5] (The first sentences are already quoted in PUB-falsafa; use the last sentence if quoting.)
- Date: 932, before the vizier in Baghdad [source: WP-Abu_Bishr_Matta, citing Margoliouth 1905]. Margoliouth dated it to AH 320, when Abū l-Fatḥ Ibn al-Furāt was vizier; the account is in the eighth "Night" of Abū Ḥayyān al-Tawḥīdī's *Kitāb al-Imtāʿ wa-l-Muʾānasa* ("Book of Enjoyment and Conviviality"), recorded at Ibn Saʿdān's request, mainly via ʿAlī ibn ʿĪsā al-Rummānī; scholars doubt it is verbatim (Yāqūt accused Tawḥīdī of "romancing"). [WS-7; Margoliouth 1905 JRAS 37: 79–129]
- Significance DEBATE: important (Elamrani-Jamal 1983; Endress 1986; Mahdi 1970) vs relatively insignificant (Frank 1990; Street agrees with Frank). [source: SEP-AIL note 6]
- Some went further than Sīrāfī: Ibn aṣ-Ṣalāḥ (d. 1245): "the access to something bad is also bad". [source: SEP-AIL §1.2]
- Al-Fārābī (c. 870–c. 950): logic indispensable for law and theology; wrote *The Short Treatise on Reasoning in the Way of the Theologians*, interpreting the jurists' analogies (*qiyâsât*) "as logical syllogisms". [source: SEP-AIL §1.2; SEP-FarL intro] Hodges: he "presents Aristotle's logic as a collection of methods for exercising persuasion, regulating debate, discovering truth and achieving certainty". [source: SEP-FarL intro] (Universal-grammar view already in PUB-falsafa.)
- Avicenna's break: Strobino: "directly or indirectly—the most influential logician in the Arabic tradition"; "Arabic logic accordingly divides into two areas and periods: pre-Avicennan and post-Avicennan (Street 2004)"; after him "Aristotle and the Peripatetic tradition almost entirely disappear from the scene". Works: *Šifāʾ* (*The Cure*, 1020s), *Nağāt* (*Salvation*, early views c. 1014), *Išārāt wa-tanbīhāt* (*Pointers and Reminders*, 1030s, most influential). [source: SEP-ISL intro, §1]
- Avicenna on Aristotle's modal mixes: "most of what Aristotle's writings have to say about the modal mixes are tests, and are not genuine opinions" (*Qiyâs* 204.10–12); later he writes of problems "arising through negligence"; the learned men of Shiraz asked why his syllogistic "differs so radically from Aristotle's". [source: SEP-AIL §1.3]
- Temporal reading: every categorical proposition is modalised, temporally or alethically; "absolute" propositions carry an elided "at least once" (a-proposition: "every J is at least once B"; e-proposition: "no J is always B"); consequences: the contradictory of an absolute is not an absolute, absolute e-propositions do not convert, second-figure syllogisms with absolute premises are sterile. He rejected absolute e-conversion with counterexamples "no horse is sleeping" (from Aristotle) and "no man is laughing" (his own). [source: SEP-AIL §§1.3, 2.3] Two readings, substantial/referential (*ḏātī*: as long as the subject exists) vs descriptional (*waṣfī*: as long as it is described by the subject term). [source: SEP-ISL §3.1.1] He replaced categorical/hypothetical with connective (*iqtirânî*) and repetitive (*istithnâʾî*) syllogisms. [source: SEP-AIL §1.3]
- Ibn Taymiyya called *Pointers* "the Koran of the philosophers" (Michot 2000). [source: SEP-AIL §1.3]
- Al-Ghazālī: Ibn Khaldūn: early Muslims "greatly disapproved" of logic; "ever since Ghazâlî (d. 1111) and Fakhraddîn ar-Râzî (d. 1210), scholars have been somewhat more lenient". Ghazālī argued logic "was entirely free of metaphysical presuppositions injurious to the faith": "speculation in juristic matters … is not distinct from speculation in philosophical matters … but only in terms of where it takes its premises from" (*Miʿyâr* 28.2–4). In the *Mustasfâ*: "he who does not comprehend [logic] is not to be trusted at all in his sciences." Street calls his logic "too dilute to be recognizably Farabian or Avicennan". Titles in Street: *Touchstone for Speculation* (Miḥakk, 1966 ed.) and *Yardstick of Knowledge* (Miʿyār, 1961 ed.). [source: SEP-AIL §1.4.1] (PUB-ghazali renders *Miʿyār al-ʿilm* as *Standard of Knowledge* — both are translations of the same title; keep consistent with the published chapter.)
- *Al-Qisṭās al-mustaqīm* ("The Correct Balance"; ed. Chelhot 1959 [source: SEP-Ghaz bibliography]): Ghazālī extracts five "balances" (logical forms) from Qur'anic verses — three "balances of equivalence" (greater, middle, lesser: in effect the three syllogistic figures), a balance of concomitance (conditional) and a balance of opposition (disjunctive) — claiming God taught them to the prophets, Abraham included (Q 6:76, 6:83). [WS-5; weaker: secondary summaries and Ghazālī's own text via ghazali.org] The work's anti-Ismāʿīlī setting is plausible but NOT verified in a scholarly source read here.
- Averroes: trained in Fārābī's logic; "One of the worst things a later scholar can do is to deviate from Aristotle's teaching … this is what happened to Alfarabi in his logical texts" (*Maqâlât* 175.6–8); returned to the modal logic four times. [source: SEP-AIL §1.4.2]
- Later manuals: Ibn Khaldūn credits the new style to Fakhr ad-Dīn ar-Rāzī (d. 1210) and Afḍal ad-Dīn al-Khūnajī ("on whom Eastern scholars rely even now"); Khūnajī's *Disclosure of Secrets about the Obscurities of Thoughts* (*Kashf al-asrār*, ed. El-Rouayheb 2010). Khūnajī's death date given as both 1248 and 1249 within SEP-AIL — say "d. 1248/9". Najm al-Dīn al-Kātibī (1204–1276 CE, AH 600–675, per WP; Street lists him with Abharī and Ṭūsī as "among the greatest logicians working anywhere in the thirteenth century") wrote the *Shamsiyya*; Abharī the *Īsāghūjī*; these "became mainstays of the madrasa teaching of logic, studied from the late thirteenth century down to the present day"; Kātibī helped at the Maragha Observatory (founded 1259). [source: SEP-AIL §1.5; WP-Najm_al-Din_al-Qazwini_al-Katibi for dates]
- A later fatwā declared logic "not mixed with philosophy" (Abharī, Kātibī, Khūnajī, Taftāzānī) a *farḍ kifāya*, a communal religious duty, since defending the faith in *kalām* depends on it. [source: SEP-AIL §1.5.3]
- After c. 1350 traditions crystallised regionally (Ottoman, Persian, Indian); Ottoman logicians after 1600 worked on relational syllogisms (El-Rouayheb 2010b). [source: SEP-AIL §1.6]

### STEP-BY-STEP: Ibn Taymiyya's argument that the syllogism is no better than analogy
- Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328, Damascus); *Refutation of the Logicians* (*al-Radd ʿalā al-manṭiqiyyīn*), begun under house arrest in Alexandria 1309–1310, completed in Damascus; aimed at Aristotelian logic "as reformulated by Ibn Sīnā". [source: SEP-IT §1, §3.2]
- P1. Avicennan logic holds that analogy (*qiyās al-tamthīl*) gives only probability because the cause (*ʿilla*) of the shared feature cannot be ascertained with certainty, whereas a demonstrative categorical syllogism (*qiyās al-shumūl*) with certain premises gives certainty. P2. But the middle term of a syllogism plays exactly the role of the cause in an analogy: date wine (*nabīdh*) is forbidden by analogy to grape wine because both intoxicate; equally "every date wine is an intoxicant; every intoxicant is forbidden; therefore, date wine is forbidden". P3. Knowing that intoxication necessitates the ruling is "no different from the equally difficult problem of identifying the middle term". P4. Certain knowledge of universal propositions about the extramental world (e.g. "all flames burn") is impossible without complete induction. C. "Analogy … and the categorical syllogism … are equivalent. Certainty … and probability … differ only according to the subject matter" (*Manṭiqiyyīn* 116/157). Further: certainty can be reached without the syllogistic form, and with one premise ("every created thing … entails the existence of the Creator"); to require the form "is mere pedantry". [source: SEP-IT §3.2]
- Objection/reception: El-Rouayheb (2016): Ibn Taymiyya's "imprecision and the fact that he gives later logical developments minimal attention may help explain why pre-modern logicians did not interact with his criticism"; he addressed the narrower claim that no certain acquired assent is possible except via the syllogism. [source: SEP-IT §3.2] Background: he denied extramental universals ("Shared meanings and universal notions … exist only in the mind"). [source: SEP-IT §2]
- Comparative point (writer's, flagged as such): his claim that deduction rests on analogy echoes the Mohist and early Indian treatment of argument as analogical.

## 10. Medieval Latin logic (cross-ref *Scotus, Ockham…*, *Early Scholasticism*)

- Properties of terms: theory "evolved out of the work of Anselm and Abelard at the turn of the twelfth century", developed through the 13th–14th c.; William of Sherwood (Oxford, 1240s): four properties, "signification, supposition, copulation, and appellation" (tr. Kretzmann); Peter of Spain and Lambert of Auxerre represent Paris. Read: "none of the medieval 'properties' matches exactly any modern notion". [source: SEP-Terms intro, §1]
- Supposition: material ("'man is a monosyllable'", Sherwood), simple (for the universal/concept), personal (for ordinary objects). Ockham: simple supposition is for "an intention of the soul"; Buridan eliminated simple supposition, folding it into material. Personal supposition divides into determinate, confused and distributive, and merely confused, defined by "descent" to singulars (Burley, Ockham): in "Every man is an animal", "man" has confused and distributive supposition, "animal" merely confused; one may infer "Every man is this animal or that animal and so on". Slogan: *talia subiecta qualia predicata permiserint* ("subjects are such as predicates permit"). [source: SEP-Terms §3] — Comparative hook: medieval descent ≈ quantifier scope; compare the Mohists' "loving people / riding horses".
- Obligationes: "a genre of logic that was concerned especially with" coherence: a respondent must defend an obviously false sentence without contradiction; flourished from the 13th c.; "During the peak period in the fourteenth century, many or perhaps even most European logicians wrote a dedicated text on obligations"; Walter Burley's treatise c. 1302; Roger Swyneshed 1330–35; Ockham's *Summa logicae* c. 1323. [source: SEP-Obl intro, §1]
- Consequentiae: treatises on consequence appear in the 14th c.; "the first sustained attempt at adopting a sentential/propositional perspective since the Stoics"; "unlike Stoic logic, which had little historical influence", they are background to modern logic; "the medieval concept of consequentia … is the main precursor of the modern concept of logical consequence". [source: SEP-Cons intro] "Formal consequence" phrase early in Simon of Faversham (1280s). [source: SEP-Cons §2.3]
- Buridan's *Tractatus de Consequentiis* (1330s per Hubien): definition by necessary truth-preservation; counterexample "No sentence is negative, therefore no donkey is running" (the premise falsifies itself whenever produced, so the simple definition counts it valid; Buridan rejects it, noting its contrapositive "Some donkey is running, therefore some sentence is negative" is invalid); formal consequence = "valid in all terms retaining a similar form" (TC 22–23); Book III suggests "third-figure syllogisms are more foundational than first-figure" (a "radical subversion of Aristotelian orthodoxy"); he accepts that from the impossible anything follows. [source: SEP-Cons §3.3]

## 11. The formal revolution (keep short; cross-ref *Leibniz*, *Formal Logic*, *Frege, Russell…*, *Pragmatism*)

- Leibniz's names for his programme: *characteristica universalis* ("lingua characterica universalis" was Trendelenburg's term), "alphabet of human thoughts", *calculus philosophicus*, *calculus ratiocinator*, *spécieuse générale*. [source: SEP-LLI] (Other Leibniz facts: PUB-leibniz.)
- Boole, *The Mathematical Analysis of Logic* (1847); De Morgan, *Formal Logic* (1847) and "On the Syllogism, No. IV, and on the Logic of Relations" (1864). [source: SEP-Peirce bibliography] For Boole/De Morgan/Peirce detail use `research/logic--formal-logic.md` §11 (verified there).
- Frege, *Begriffsschrift, eine der arithmetischen nachgebildete Formelsprache des reinen Denkens* (1879). Schröder's 1880 review: Frege's system is "less of a 'general characteristic' and more of a calculus ratiocinator", already achieved by Boole; Frege replied (1883) he aimed to "express contents", not an abstract logic like Boole's. [source: SEP-Frege §1; SEP-LLI]
- Smith: the rise of modern logic after Frege and Russell "brought with it a recognition of the many serious limitations of Aristotle's logic", yet modern-trained scholars "have come to view Aristotle with new respect … for the remarkable similarity in spirit". [source: SEP-AL §1] Łukasiewicz, *Aristotle's Syllogistic from the Standpoint of Modern Formal Logic* (2nd ed. 1957); Corcoran, "Completeness of an Ancient Logic" (JSL 1972). [source: SEP-AL bibliography]

## Debates
- Is "logic" one subject across cultures? Gillon: India studied reasoning ontically, epistemically and dialectically, not linguistically/formally; Tillemans: formal validity not explicitly discussed by Buddhists, and "logic" can mislead; Bocheński (and Chi) saw formal awareness in the Hetucakra. [SEP-Ind; SEP-Dhk]
- Was Dignāga's canonical syllogism deductive (Gillon), inductive (Hayes), or defeasible (Oetke; criticised by Taber)? [SEP-Ind §4.1]
- Is the Hetucakra an original advance or a "pedagogic device" restating Vasubandhu (Anacker)? Did the deductive syllogism pass from Dignāga to Praśastapāda or vice versa? (undecided) [WP-Hetucakra; SEP-Ind §4.2]
- Did Dharmakīrti solve the problem of knowing the universal premise? Gillon: no; Steinkellner 2015: dissents. [SEP-Ind §4.2]
- "Old" vs "New" Nyāya: is Gaṅgeśa a revolution? Phillips: "At no time is there a decisive revolution"; some name Udayana the originator. [SEP-Gan]
- Did ancient China have logic? Fraser: Mohists refute the "uninterested in logic" view but lack logical consequence and treat argument as analogical; Zhang & Liu (2007) see a formal programme; Harbsmeier sees deduction from general propositions; Hansen denies concern with semantic truth (Graham, Harbsmeier, Fraser object); SEP-CLL warns against anachronism. [SEP-MC; SEP-Moh; SEP-CLL]
- White horse: universals (Fung; now largely rejected), part-whole (Hansen 1983, Graham 1989), mass nouns (Hansen 1976/1983), extensions of phrases (Hansen 1992), kinds/identity (Cikoski, Harbsmeier, Fraser), use/mention (Thompson 1995); or a court entertainer's spoof (Harbsmeier, Fraser). [SEP-SN; WS-2]
- Xunzi: logician or anti-logician? Cua: disputation "reach[es] its highest point" in Xunzi; IEP: logic "certainly not part of Xunzi's program"; Goldin: the essay's concern is morality. [SEP-CLL; IEP-Xun; SEP-Xun]
- Significance of the 932 Mattā–Sīrāfī debate: important (Elamrani-Jamal, Endress, Mahdi) vs insignificant (Frank, Street); its historicity (Tawḥīdī's "romancing", Margoliouth). [SEP-AIL note 6; WS-7]
- Aristotle's modal syllogistic: coherent (Malink 2013's reconstruction) or "conceivably internally inconsistent" (Bobzien)? Two Barbaras disputed since Theophrastus. [SEP-AL; SEP-AncLog]
- Authorship of Maimonides' *Treatise on Logic*: Davidson against, Stroumsa for. [WS-1]
- Stoic logic's influence: SEP-Cons says Stoic logic "had little historical influence"; Bobzien (2021, in PUB-birth-of-analytic-philosophy) argues Frege borrowed from it; SEP calls her case cumulative with "no smoking gun".
