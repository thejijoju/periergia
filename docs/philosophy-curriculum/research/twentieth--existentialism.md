## The label "existentialism"
- SEP-EX: the word was "first introduced by Marcel in 1943"; "only Sartre and Beauvoir explicitly self-identified as 'existentialists'" [source: SEP-EX intro].
- WP-EX: coined by the French Catholic philosopher Gabriel Marcel "in the mid-1940s"; when Marcel first applied it to Sartre "at a colloquium in 1945, Sartre rejected it"; Sartre then publicly adopted the label in his lecture of 29 October 1945 [source: WP-EX, citing Cooper 1990, Flynn 2006, Daigle 2006, Fulton 1999]. **Date of coinage: say "in 1943–45" or "during the Second World War / mid-1940s"; SEP says 1943, WP says mid-1940s and 1945 for the first application to Sartre.**
- Beauvoir's own account (memoir *The Force of Circumstance*): Sartre at first rejected the label as implying a system — "My philosophy is a philosophy of existence; I don't even know what Existentialism is"; Beauvoir was irritated that critics called her novel *The Blood of Others* an "Existentialist novel"; "But our protests were in vain. In the end, we took the epithet that everyone used for us and used it for our own purposes" (Beauvoir 1963, tr. R. Howard [1992, 38]) [source: SEP-EX note 1].
- Marcel's philosophy was called "Christian Existentialism" (most famously in Sartre's "Existentialism is a Humanism"), "a term he initially endorsed but later repudiated" [source: SEP-MA intro]. WP-EX: Marcel later preferred "Neo-Socratic" [source: WP-EX].
- Camus "although he more than once denied it, a philosopher"; "he forcefully separated himself from existentialism"; Sartre accepted the "existentialist" label "that Camus rejected" [source: SEP-C intro, §1]. In a November 1945 interview with Jeanine Delpech (*Les Nouvelles Littéraires*) Camus said he did "not believe sufficiently in reason to believe in a system" [source: SEP-C §1].
- Heidegger: in December 1946, answering questions from Jean Beaufret (letter of 10 November 1946), Heidegger wrote the "Letter on Humanism", reworked and published 1947, distancing himself from Sartre and existentialism [source: WP-LH; IEP-EX §3d ("he repudiates any possible connection of his philosophy with the existentialism of Sartre")]. SEP-EXc: "in 1946 Heidegger would repudiate the retrospective labelling of his earlier work as existentialism" [source: SEP-EXc §1].
- Jaspers also resisted: his *Philosophie* avoided the title "Existential Philosophy" despite using *Existenzphilosophie*; later preferred "philosophy of reason"; "Heidegger resented being described as an existentialist, and Jaspers, at least after 1933, resented being identified with Heidegger" [source: SEP-JA].
- Many so-called existentialists "for instance Camus and Heidegger—repudiated the label" [source: SEP-EXc §1].
- SEP-EX lists the movement's overlapping themes, since there is "no unifying doctrine": nihilism; engagement vs detachment; existence precedes essence; freedom; authenticity; ethics [source: SEP-EX intro]. Note: "existentialists" include secular and religious thinkers, defenders and critics of radical freedom [source: SEP-EX intro].
- Context: "the existentialist moment" (Baert 2015) after WWII, the camps and Hiroshima/Nagasaki [source: SEP-EX intro].

## Antecedents (cross-reference, keep brief)
- Groundwork laid by Kierkegaard and Nietzsche in the 19th century and by Husserl, Heidegger and Jaspers in the 20th, plus Ortega y Gasset and Unamuno; literary forerunners include Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Kafka, Rilke [source: SEP-EX intro].
- Kierkegaard and Nietzsche "came to be seen as precursors"; neither used the word [source: SEP-EXc §1; WP-EX]. Periergia's *Schopenhauer and Kierkegaard* already quotes SEP that "father of existentialism" "obscures at least as much as it reveals" — keep that caution.
- Dostoevsky's *Notes from Underground* (1864): the underground man rebels against a picture of humans as determined "piano keys" [source: SEP-EX §4.1].
- Heidegger's line "The 'essence' of Dasein lies in its existence" (*Being and Time*, 1927, Macquarrie & Robinson, SZ 42) is the origin of the principle that Sartre "repackaged" as "existence precedes essence" [source: SEP-EX §3].
- Jaspers's "limit" or "boundary situations" (*Grenzsituationen*) — "when everything that is said to be valuable and true collapses before our eyes" (*Philosophie* vol. 2, 1932) [source: SEP-EX §5.1].
- Karl Jaspers 1883–1969, psychiatrist turned philosopher in the early 1920s [source: SEP-JA].
- Miguel de Unamuno (29 September 1864 – 31 December 1936); major philosophical essay *Tragic Sense of Life* (1913) [source: WP Miguel_de_Unamuno].

## Sartre: life and works
- Jean-Paul Sartre, born 21 June 1905, died 15 April 1980, Paris [source: WP-JPS; SEP-S 1905–80].
- École Normale Supérieure; failed the agrégation at first, then came first in 1929; Beauvoir second, at 21 [source: SEP-S §1; SEP-B §1].
- Taught at Le Havre 1931–36 while writing *Nausea* [source: SEP-S §1]. Encountered phenomenology c. 1933, studied at the French Institute in Berlin [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-SA §1].
- *Nausea* 1938 [source: SEP-S intro].
- Meteorologist in the war; captured by the German army in 1940 and imprisoned for just under a year [source: SEP-S §1].
- *Being and Nothingness* (*L'Être et le Néant*) 1943; English translations by Hazel Barnes (1956) and Sarah Richmond (2018) [source: SEP-S intro; WP-BN].
- *No Exit* (*Huis clos*), first performed at the Théâtre du Vieux-Colombier, May 1944 [source: WP-NE]. (SEP-S dates it "1947" — the English publication; use 1944.)
- *Les Temps modernes*: first issue October 1945 (WP-LTM: 15 October 1945); the name was chosen in December 1944, after Chaplin's *Modern Times*; first board Sartre (director), Aron, Beauvoir, Leiris, Merleau-Ponty, Ollivier, Paulhan [source: WP-JPS; WP-LTM; SEP-MP §1]. (SEP-S says "commencing from 1943", which is wrong.)
- Refused the Légion d'honneur in 1945 [source: WP-JPS].
- *Anti-Semite and Jew* (*Réflexions sur la question juive*) 1946, composed "very quickly in 1944" [source: SEP-S §7; WP-JPS]. "Black Orpheus" (*Orphée noir*), preface to a 1948 anthology of Négritude poetry [source: SEP-S §7].
- *Search for a Method* 1957; *Critique of Dialectical Reason* vol. 1 ("Theory of Practical Ensembles") 1960; vol. 2 posthumous in French 1985; both in English only by 1991 [source: SEP-S §6]. Key concepts: praxis, the practico-inert, seriality (the bus queue), the "group in fusion" [source: SEP-S §6].
- "Never a member of the French Communist Party"; "we were convinced at one and the same time that historical materialism furnished the only valid interpretation of history and that existentialism remained the only concrete approach to reality" (*Search for a Method*, tr. Barnes [1963: 21]) [source: SEP-S §6]. Supported the PCF in the 1950s until the 1956 Soviet invasion of Hungary; later supported the Maoists [source: WP-JPS]. In 1977 he claimed no longer to be a Marxist [source: IEP-SA §1].
- Nobel Prize in Literature 1964: on 14 October 1964 he wrote to the Nobel Institute asking to be removed from the list and warning he would decline; the letter went unread; prize announced 22 October; he declined, saying he always declined official honours and that "a writer should not allow himself to be turned into an institution" [source: WP-JPS]. SEP-S phrases it as "Sartre's attempt to refuse the Nobel Prize" [source: SEP-S intro].
- *The Words* (*Les Mots*, 1964), autobiography; *The Family Idiot* (Flaubert, 1971–2) [source: SEP-S].
- Became almost completely blind in 1973 [source: WP-JPS].
- Funeral 19 April 1980: WP-JPS "50,000 Parisians"; SEP-S "estimates … range from 50–100,000" — give "tens of thousands (estimates 50,000–100,000)". Buried in Montparnasse Cemetery [source: WP-JPS; SEP-S §1].
- De Gaulle, responding to calls to prosecute Sartre for civil disobedience, reportedly said you don't arrest Voltaire [source: SEP-S intro] (anecdote; attribute as "reportedly").
- Foucault called the late Sartre's work a "magnificent and pathetic attempt of a man of the nineteenth century to think the twentieth century" (1966) [source: SEP-S intro].

## Sartre: *Being and Nothingness* (1943) — ontology, freedom, bad faith, the look
- Distinction between being-for-itself (*pour-soi*, consciousness, which negates and transcends) and being-in-itself (*en-soi*, the "pure plenitude" of things) [source: SEP-S §4.1].
- The for-itself is "a being which is what it is not and is not what it is" (Barnes [1956: 79]) [source: SEP-S §4.1].
- Nothingness: Pierre's absence "pervades the whole café" when I expected him; contrasted with merely thinking that the Duke of Wellington is absent; destruction and fragility exist only for a being "by whom nothingness comes into the world" [source: SEP-S §4.1; WP-BN].
- Freedom: we are "condemned" to be free — "in his hyperbolic phrase" (SEP-Sf §… "Ontology"); "We are 'condemned to be free,' which means that we can never simply be who we are but are separated from ourselves by the nothingness of having perpetually to re-choose" [source: SEP-Sf; SEP-EXc §2.2]. The phrase appears in both *Being and Nothingness* (Barnes's glossary: the For-itself is "condemned to be free") and the 1945 lecture [source: WP-BN; SEP-EX §4]. See Quotations for exact wording status.
- Anguish (*angoisse*): consciousness of freedom; translated "anguish" by both Barnes and Richmond [source: SEP-S §1; SEP-EXc].
- Facticity: no factual state, "even poverty", determines consciousness to apprehend it as a lack; it is "the apprehension of the revolution as possible" that gives the worker's suffering its value as motive (Barnes [1956: 433, 437]) [source: SEP-S §4.1].
- The hiker who gives in to fatigue: fatigue becomes a motive only when made the focus of attention; things could have been otherwise, so Sartre says a choice was made [source: SEP-S §4.1].
- Bad faith (*mauvaise foi*): either denying or over-identifying with facticity or transcendence; most commonly over-identifying with facticity, as if I were a thing in-itself ("I am what I am"); bad faith is "an immediate, permanent threat to every project of the human being" (Barnes [1956: 116]) [source: SEP-EX §5.5].
- Critique of Freud: positing a censor that both knows and does not know merely relocates the paradox of a "liar" and "lied to" in one consciousness [source: SEP-S §4.2; WP-BN].
- **The waiter, step by step**: (1) his movements are "a little too precise, a little too rapid", he plays at being a waiter like an automaton; (2) he is trying to make himself a waiter the way an inkwell is an inkwell (in-itself); (3) but he can only do so because he is a free for-itself choosing this role, so the project presupposes the freedom it denies; (4) Sartre: "There is no doubt I am in a sense a café waiter … But if I am one, this cannot be in the mode of being-in-itself. I am a waiter in the mode of being what I am not" (Barnes [1956: 60]) [source: SEP-S §4.2; IEP-SA §3 (BN 59); WP-BN quoting the passage]. Alternative reading of the example: Bernasconi 2007 [source: SEP-S §4.2].
- **The woman on a first date, step by step**: (1) she ignores the sexual intent of her companion's compliments, taking them as addressed to her as a pure consciousness; (2) when he takes her hand she leaves it in his, "neither consenting nor resisting – a thing"; (3) she thereby postpones the choice to accept or refuse; (4) she plays on her double reality — treating her hand as a mere thing (facticity) while treating herself as pure transcendence detached from her body [source: WP-BF]. (Only WP describes this example; SEP/IEP read do not. Use as Sartre's example, but do not quote beyond "neither consenting nor resisting – a thing".)
- No account of good faith in BN beyond a final footnote promising an ethics; sincerity ("being what one is") is itself impossible for the for-itself [source: SEP-S §4.2].
- **The look** (*le regard*): the keyhole example — absorbed in peeping, I hear footsteps and suddenly apprehend myself as an object for another: shame, a "pre-moral" experience; this is Sartre's answer to the "reef of solipsism" — the Other is encountered, not inferred [source: SEP-S §4.3].
- "It is useless for human reality to seek to escape this dilemma: one must either transcend the Other or allow oneself to be transcended by him. The essence of the relations between consciousness is not the Mitsein (being-with); it is conflict" (Barnes [1956: 429]) [source: SEP-S §4.3]. "Conflict is the original meaning of being-for-others" (Barnes [1956: 475]) [source: SEP-EX §6.1].
- The chapter on concrete relations with others is "essentially a restatement of the 'master-slave' stage of Hegel's struggle for recognition without the possibility of its overcoming" [source: SEP-S §4.3] (cross-ref *German Idealism*).
- "to be dead is to be prey for the living" (Barnes [1956: 543]) [source: SEP-S §1].

## Sartre: "Existentialism Is a Humanism" (lecture, 1945; book 1946)
- Delivered in Paris in late October 1945 to a packed audience at the Club Maintenant; WP-EX gives **29 October 1945**; SEP-Sf (Flynn) gives **28 October 1945** — WS (Aeon) also gives "the evening of October 29". Use 29 October with "some sources say 28" [source: WP-EX; SEP-Sf §1; WS].
- It came two weeks after the launch of *Les Temps modernes*; Camus's *Combat* reported it on its front page as "Too many attend Sartre lecture" [source: WP-EX; WS (Aeon)].
- Published 1946 by Nagel as *L'existentialisme est un humanisme*; English 1948 (Methuen) as *Existentialism and Humanism*, tr. Philip Mairet; later tr. Carol Macomber (Yale 2007); Bernard Frechtman's translation used by SEP-EX [source: WP-EIH; SEP-Sf bibliography; SEP-EX].
- It offered an accessible version of BN and answered Marxist and Christian critics [source: SEP-S intro].
- **Sartre's later attitude**: "the transcript of this lecture was the only publication that Sartre openly regretted seeing in print" [source: SEP-Sf §1]. WS (Aeon) "Sartre later renounced the lecture". Phrase as "Sartre later regretted its publication" (not "disowned its ideas").
- IEP-EX calls it "lovely, if oversimplifying" [source: IEP-EX §3d].
- Thesis: "existence precedes essence" (Frechtman [2001, 292]) is the first principle [source: SEP-EX §3]. Sartre also uses the formula in BN [source: SEP-S §4.1].
- The paper-knife: an artisan makes a paper-knife from a concept of what it is for, so its essence precedes its existence; theists picture God creating humans the same way; for an atheist, there is no such designer, so the human being first exists and only afterwards defines himself [source: WS (marxists.org/Mairet text summary)]. (Treat exact wording as unverified; paraphrase.)
- "There are no excuses behind us nor justifications before us" (Frechtman [2001, 296]) [source: SEP-EX §4].
- "I am obliged to want others to have freedom at the same time that I want my own freedom" (Frechtman [2001, 306]) [source: SEP-EX §6].
- Anguish: feeling of responsibility not only for oneself but for all humanity ("In fashioning myself, I fashion Man"); abandonment: no God to prescribe how to live; despair: acting without relying on possibilities beyond one's control [source: WP-EIH]. (Secondary summary; paraphrase.)
- The student's dilemma: a pupil whose brother had been killed by the Germans in 1940 and whose father was a collaborator had to choose between staying with his mother (who lived only for him) and leaving to join the Free French; no ethical doctrine (Christian or Kantian) could decide; Sartre told him he was free and must choose — i.e. invent [source: WS (LitCharts summary)]. Exact wording ("You are free, therefore choose—that is to say, invent") NOT verified.
- Sartre described existentialism (in a 1945 lecture) as "the attempt to draw all the consequences from a position of consistent atheism" [source: WP-EX citing James Wood's intro to Penguin *Nausea*].
- Flynn: Sartre's thesis that freedom is the implicit object of every choice (which would disqualify an "authentic Nazi") is a claim "he makes but does not adequately defend in his Humanism lecture" [source: SEP-Sf].

## Sartre: "Hell is other people"
- From *No Exit* (1944): "L'enfer, c'est les autres", spoken by Garcin near the end, after realising there are no instruments of torture — the three dead characters torment one another [source: WP-NE]. SEP-EX quotes it in Gilbert's translation as "Hell is—other people" (1944 [1989, 45]) and links it to the conflict of being-for-others [source: SEP-EX §6.1]. Paul Bowles's translation: "Hell is just – other people" [WS].
- Sartre's own gloss, in a spoken preface to a recording of the play (1964 Deutsche Grammophon recording per WP-NE; often dated 1965): the phrase "has always been misunderstood"; he did not mean that relations with others are always poisoned but that when relations are twisted, others' judgement is what we use to know ourselves, so others become hell [source: WS (laculturegenerale.com summary); WP-NE for the recording]. Date: say "in the mid-1960s".

## The argument for radical freedom (for step-by-step reconstruction)
Premises/steps (Sartre, BN; SEP-S calls the overall argument "an inference to the best explanation" [source: SEP-S §4.1]):
1. We encounter negations directly in experience — Pierre's absence, destruction, fragility, questions that admit a "no" [SEP-S §4.1].
2. Being-in-itself is pure positivity: "there is not less after the storm, just something else" [SEP-S §4.1].
3. So negation must come into the world through a being that can detach itself from what is — consciousness (the for-itself), which "is what it is not and is not what it is" [SEP-S §4.1].
4. This power of negation is freedom: no factual state can of itself cause consciousness to respond to it in one way; we choose (usually pre-reflectively) its meaning and the ends we adopt [SEP-S §4.1].
5. Anguish is the direct phenomenological evidence of this freedom [SEP-S §4.1].
6. Conclusion: human reality is free in an ontological sense and responsible for its situation — "condemned" to freedom [SEP-S §4.1; SEP-Sf].
- Clarification Sartre makes: freedom is not power to achieve anything; the for-itself is always in a factical situation; his view is "not a voluntarist or capricious account" [SEP-S §4.1].
- Objection 1 (begging the question): the metaphysical distinction between for-itself and in-itself "might seem to 'beg the question' by assuming what it purports to establish"; reply: Sartre argues realism and idealism cannot account for negation, and appeals to anguish — an inference to the best explanation [SEP-S §4.1].
- Objection 2 (situation/facticity; Merleau-Ponty, *Phenomenology of Perception* 1945): "Against the Sartrean position that freedom is either total or null", freedom emerges only against our "universal engagement in a world" with meanings not of our choosing, and "acquired habits and the sedimented choices of our lives have their own inertia"; situation "does not eliminate freedom but is precisely the field in which it can be achieved" [SEP-MP §3]. Beauvoir likewise speaks of "sedimentation" resisting change [WP-EX].
- Reply / development: Sartre's *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960) adds the practico-inert and seriality, social sedimentation "that had only minimally featured" in BN [SEP-S §6]. IEP-EX: Sartre was "influenced in the meantime by the criticisms of Merleau-Ponty and de Beauvoir" when writing *Notebooks for an Ethics* (late 1940s) and became less pessimistic about relations with others [IEP-EX §3d].
- Objection 3 (grounding ethics): if values are chosen, what rules out an "authentic Nazi"? BN offers only a footnote promising an ethics; EIH asserts that willing my freedom entails willing others' freedom ("I am obliged to want others to have freedom…") but, per Flynn, does not adequately defend it [SEP-S §4.2; SEP-Sf; SEP-EX §6]. Reply: SEP-EX argues existentialism is not moral nihilism — an ethics of owning freedom and of authentic being-for-others / reciprocity (Beauvoir) [SEP-EX intro, §6]; *Notebooks for an Ethics* (1947–48, published 1983; tr. Pellauer 1992) attempted the ethics [SEP-EX bibliography; SEP-S §4.2].
- Objection 4 (Heidegger): "the reversal of a metaphysical statement remains a metaphysical statement" — Sartre only reverses Plato's essence-before-existence and stays within metaphysics [source: WP-LH quoting the Letter; IEP-SA §1].
- Objection 5 (Freud): Wollheim and Baldwin argued Sartre's critique of the unconscious misreads Freud [source: WP-JPS]; SEP-S: "It is debated whether or not this is a reasonable treatment of Freud" [SEP-S §4.2].

## Sartre: politics and anti-colonialism
- Racism is a form of bad faith (believing in/constructing essences); *Notebooks* suggest all oppression rests on bad faith; we all profit from colonial exploitation even if not settlers [source: SEP-S §7].
- "Black Orpheus" (1948): Sartre "called for an anti-racist racism" and saw Négritude as a step toward revealing race itself as bad faith; Fanon's reception was "mixed": in *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952) Fanon argues this undermined his own lived experience [source: SEP-S §7]. WS: Sartre cast Négritude as the negative/weak moment of a dialectic leading to the universal (class); Fanon's verdict that Sartre "has destroyed black zeal" [WS — secondary].
- Rapprochement with Fanon "culminated" in Sartre's preface to *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961), "where also Sartre appears to endorse a counter-violence" [source: SEP-S §7]. Fanon met Sartre in Rome for a three-day meeting in 1961 and Sartre agreed to write the preface [source: WP-FF].
- After 1967, because of Sartre's support for Israel in the Six-Day War, Fanon's widow Josie Fanon had the preface removed from new editions (Howard University interview, 1978) [source: WP-WE].
- Homi Bhabha, foreword to the 2004 English edition, criticised Sartre's preface for narrowing reading to violence [source: WP-WE].

## Beauvoir: life
- Simone de Beauvoir born 9 January 1908, Paris; died 14 April 1986 aged 78; buried with Sartre in Montparnasse Cemetery, wearing Nelson Algren's ring [source: SEP-B §1].
- Rejected God at fourteen; family lost class status in 1919; diplôme on Leibniz 1928–29; agrégation 1929, aged 21, the youngest to pass, second to Sartre (on his second attempt); "the eighth woman to pass the agrégation" (SEP-B) [source: SEP-B §1; IEP-B].
- 1929 pact with Sartre: "essential" love with "contingent loves" disclosed to each other; never married; both "have also been criticized for their sexual ethics" [source: SEP-B §1].
- Controversy (state carefully): relationships in the 1930s–40s with younger women, including former pupils Olga Kosakiewicz, Natalie Sorokin and Bianca Bienenfeld (Lamblin), some of whom also became involved with Sartre; Lamblin later wrote she felt deceived when Beauvoir's letters/war diary were published [source: WP-SB]. In 1941 Sorokin's mother filed a complaint; after an investigation Beauvoir lost her teaching licence (suspended 1943, reinstated 1945) and never taught again [source: WP-SB; IEP-B ("dismissed from teaching again in 1943" after "a parental complaint … for corrupting one of her female students")]. SEP-B says she was "dismissed from her post in 1941 by the Nazis" — conflicting; IEP-B mentions both a 1941 dismissal and the 1943 one. Safest: "in 1943, after a complaint by a pupil's mother, she was removed from teaching under the Vichy regime".
- Co-founded and co-edited *Les Temps modernes* (1945) [source: SEP-B §1; IEP-B].
- *She Came to Stay* 1943; *Pyrrhus and Cinéas* 1944 (first philosophical essay); *The Blood of Others* 1945; *All Men Are Mortal* 1946; *The Ethics of Ambiguity* 1947; *America Day by Day* 1948; *The Second Sex* 1949; *The Mandarins* 1954 (Prix Goncourt); *The Long March* 1957; memoirs 1958–63 (4th vol. 1974); *A Very Easy Death* 1964; *The Coming of Age* (*La Vieillesse*) 1970; *Adieux* (on Sartre's last years) [source: SEP-B §1].
- Declared herself a feminist in a 1972 *Nouvel Observateur* interview; wrote and signed the Manifesto of the 343 (1971) [source: SEP-B §1].
- With Gisèle Halimi, *Djamila Boupacha* (1962), on an Algerian woman tortured by the French army [source: SEP-B §10].
- She refused to call herself a philosopher, and was often treated as Sartre's disciple [source: SEP-B intro, §2].

## Beauvoir: *Pyrrhus and Cinéas* (1944) and *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947)
- *Pyrrhus and Cinéas* opens from Plutarch's dialogue; Part I ends: "A man alone in the world would be paralyzed by … the vanity of all of his goals. But man is not alone in the world" (PhilW 115); others' freedom cannot be touched directly, so I must *appeal* to others to take up my projects; the appeal needs others who are free and materially equal; "We are … condemned to violence", violence being "the mark of a failure which nothing can offset" (PhilW 138) [source: SEP-B §4].
- *The Ethics of Ambiguity* drops the inner/outer distinction and argues I can be alienated from my freedom; human ambiguity = we are subjects for ourselves and objects for others; we never coincide with ourselves or become God; she rejects absolutes (God, Humanity, the classless society) that justify sacrificing present people for the future [source: SEP-B §5].
- Against Dostoevsky's "if God is dead everything is permitted": without God to pardon us, we are "totally and inexcusably responsible" [source: SEP-B §5].
- "to will oneself moral and to will oneself free are one and the same decision" (tr. Frechtman) [source: IEP-B].
- **Argument, step by step** (SEP-B §5; IEP-B): (1) We begin as children in the "serious world" of ready-made values — metaphysically privileged but mystified; (2) adolescence demands that we take responsibility for freedom; (3) we can flee freedom (bad faith) by remaining in the serious world; (4) types: the sub-man and the serious man refuse freedom; the nihilist, the adventurer and the (maniacally) passionate man misread it; (5) the serious man subordinates his freedom to an external idol ("the Cause", "Fame", "Power") — "the ultimate example of bad faith" (IEP-B); (6) the adventurer comes close to morality but cares only for his own freedom, so can become a tyrant's tool or a tyrant (IEP-B); (7) acknowledging ontological freedom is necessary but not sufficient: ethical freedom must "embrace the ties that bind me to others" and work for the material and political conditions of others' freedom; (8) so I cannot affirm my own freedom without affirming others'; evil is the denial of freedom; to treat adults as children is "immoral and evil"; the oppressed (exploited, enslaved, terrorised) are not in bad faith in submitting [source: SEP-B §5; IEP-B]. Exemplary figures: the artist-writer [SEP-B §5].
- Objection: it is too abstract, defining morality apart from social context — Beauvoir's own later self-criticism in *Force of Circumstance*; reply: *The Second Sex* materially situates the analysis [source: SEP-B §7]. Objection (from *Pyrrhus*): if persuasion fails, violence is permitted — an "uneasy" conclusion [SEP-B §4].

## Beauvoir: *The Second Sex* (1949)
- Conceived in 1946 at the Deux Magots café, prompted by Michel Leiris's *L'âge d'homme*: "What has it meant to me to be a woman?" [source: SEP-B §7]. Extracts first appeared in *Les Temps modernes* [source: SEP-B §1; WP-LTM].
- Central claim: "woman is the Other, man is the essential being"; the myth of the "eternal feminine" (Vol. I: facts and myths; Vol. II: lived experience) [source: SEP-B §7].
- Immanence vs transcendence: women are anchored to the repetitive, passive realm of immanence and forced to relinquish transcendence; Beauvoir argues for equality while recognising sexual difference; "the body is a situation" [source: SEP-B §7].
- The Other is based on Hegel's master–slave dialectic, but women, unlike other oppressed groups, lack a shared history and are dispersed among men; the man–woman relation is a "primordial Mitsein" (SS:2010, 9) [source: SEP-B §7]. Her reading of Hegel was shaped by Kojève [source: SEP-B §2] (consistent with Periergia's *German Idealism*).
- "Hence woman makes no claim for herself as subject because she lacks the concrete means, because she senses the necessary link connecting her to man without positing its reciprocity, and because she often derives satisfaction from her role as the Other" (SS:2010, 10, Borde & Malovany-Chevallier) [source: SEP-B §7].
- The famous line opens Volume II: "On ne naît pas femme: on le devient" (SS:1949) [source: SEP-B §7; IEP-B ("Book II begins with…")]. See Quotations for translations.
- Credited by many with introducing the sex–gender distinction; whether she did, and whether that reading is right, is debated (Butler vs Toril Moi and Nancy Bauer) [source: SEP-B §7; WP-SS].
- Race: Richard Wright influenced her account of oppression and she refers to him in the book, "yet her treatment of race in the book is vexed"; critics (e.g. Spelman 1988, Simons, Deutscher, hooks) say she generalises from a white bourgeois perspective; she also argued women's subjection differs in kind from racial subjection [source: SEP-B §6, §7, §14].
- Reception: sold about 22,000 copies in its first week; placed on the Vatican's Index; Camus "was furious … saying I had ridiculed the French male" (Beauvoir, 1974 interview); received more as an affront to sexual decency than as philosophy; later a foundation of second-wave feminism [source: WP-SS; SEP-B §2].
- Translations: H. M. Parshley (a zoologist with no background in philosophy), Knopf 1953 — omits around 15% of the text, mistranslates central philosophical concepts and depoliticises it [source: SEP-B §2/§1; WP-SS]. Toril Moi's critique "While we wait: The English translation of *The Second Sex*" (*Signs* 27:4, 2002) [source: WP-SS]. Beauvoir requested a new translation in a 1985 interview [source: WP-SS]. New unabridged translation by Constance Borde and Sheila Malovany-Chevallier: Jonathan Cape (UK) November 2009, Knopf (US) 2010; praised for restoring cuts but criticised (Moi; NYT review; Nancy Bauer) for style, literalism and errors [source: WP-SS; SEP-B bibliography; WS].
- Translated into 40 languages; Spanish translation banned in Francoist Spain in 1955 [source: WP-SS].

## Beauvoir: influence on Sartre (debate) and *Old Age*
- Debate: were ideas in BN (1943) taken from *She Came to Stay* (1943)? Kate and Edward Fullbrook, *Simone de Beauvoir and Jean-Paul Sartre: The Remaking of a Twentieth-Century Legend* (Basic Books, 1994) argue Beauvoir originated ideas credited to Sartre, using letters and Sartre's *War Diaries* to show Sartre read the novel's manuscript in February 1940 [source: SEP-B §2, §14; WS (Kirkus)]. Margaret A. Simons, *Beauvoir and The Second Sex: Feminism, Race, and the Origins of Existentialism* (Rowman & Littlefield, 1999) [source: IEP-B bibliography]. SEP-B: these arguments "eventually … abated" and scholars turned to her broader originality; her diaries show "Sartre often relied on Beauvoir's intellectual acuity"; Beauvoir herself insisted her philosophy merely elaborated Sartre's [source: SEP-B §1–2]. Michèle Le Doeuff, "Simone de Beauvoir and Existentialism" (1980) [source: SEP-B §14].
- *The Coming of Age* (*La Vieillesse*, 1970; English 1972 — published in the UK as *Old Age*, tr. Patrick O'Brian per SEP-EX bibliography "P. O'Brian"): like *The Second Sex*, treats the old as Other; old age as a situation of Otherness is not inevitable but produced by culture, myths and economics; the old man "looks … like one of a 'different species'" (1970 [1972], 231) [source: SEP-B §12; SEP-EX bibliography]. (UK title *Old Age* not verified in a source read — WP page 404; use *The Coming of Age*.)

## Camus: life
- Albert Camus, born 7 November 1913 in Mondovi (now Dréan), French Algeria; *pied-noir*; mother Catherine Hélène Sintès, of Spanish (Balearic) descent, deaf and illiterate; studied philosophy at the University of Algiers [source: WP-AC].
- Member of the Algerian branch of the French Communist Party in the mid-1930s, expelled for refusing its line of playing down French colonialism; journalist on *Alger républicain* (Oct 1938–Jan 1940); June 1939 reports on famine in Kabylie; tuberculosis disqualified him from military service [source: SEP-C §4].
- Planned triptych of the absurd (novel, essay, play) sent from Algeria to Paris in September 1941: *The Stranger* published June 1942, *The Myth of Sisyphus* October 1942, *Caligula* later [source: SEP-C §3].
- "Letters to a German Friend" (1943–44); March 1944 succeeded Pascal Pia as editor of *Combat*, the main underground newspaper of the non-Communist left; one of few to protest Hiroshima (1945); opposed the death penalty for collaborators; "Neither Victims nor Executioners" (1946) [source: SEP-C §4].
- *The Plague* 1947; *The Rebel* (*L'Homme révolté*) 1951; *The Fall* 1956 [source: SEP-C; WP The_Rebel_(book)].
- Nobel Prize in Literature 1957 at 44 — second-youngest laureate after Kipling, first literature laureate born in Africa [source: WP-AC; SEP-C intro].
- Died 4 January 1960, aged 46, in a car crash at Villeblevin near Sens, in Michel Gallimard's Facel Vega; buried at Lourmarin; Sartre wrote a tribute praising his "stubborn humanism" [source: WP-AC; SEP-C intro].

## Camus: *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942) — the absurd
- The absurd = the "divorce" between human consciousness, with its "wild longing for clarity" (MS 21), and "the unreasonable silence of the world" (MS 28); for Camus absurdity is not a property of the world or of us alone but of our relation to the world (contrast Sartre, for whom absurdity/contingency is ontological) [source: SEP-C §3.3].
- The absurd feeling arises when "the stage sets collapse. Rising, streetcar, four hours in the office or the factory, meal, streetcar, four hours of work, meal, sleep, and Monday Tuesday Wednesday Thursday Friday Saturday and Sunday according to the same rhythm …" (MS 12–13) [source: SEP-C §3.1].
- "Philosophical suicide": Camus writes against Shestov, Kierkegaard, Jaspers, Heidegger and Husserl: "they deify what crushes them and find reason to hope in what impoverishes them. That forced hope is religious in all of them" (MS 24); "starting from a philosophy of the world's lack of meaning, it ends up by finding a meaning and depth in it" (MS 42) [source: SEP-C §3.3]. (SEP-C does not use the phrase "philosophical suicide"; it is the title of a chapter of the essay — NOT verified from a source read; use as "what Camus calls philosophical suicide" only if checked.)
- Responses rejected: suicide and hope; the answer: live "without appeal", in revolt, lucidity; "die unreconciled and not of one's own free will" (MS 55); "The absurd is lucid reason noting its limits" (MS 49) [source: SEP-C §3.2–3.3].
- Revolt, freedom, passion: Camus's three consequences are standard but not stated as a triad in SEP-C read; SEP-C summarises his answer as "Full consciousness, avoiding false solutions such as religion, refusing to submit, and carrying on with vitality and intensity" [source: SEP-C §3.4].
- Sisyphus: "His scorn of the gods, his hatred of death, and his passion for life won him that unspeakable penalty in which the whole being is exerted toward accomplishing nothing" (MS 120); the descent is "the hour of consciousness … he is superior to his fate. He is stronger than his rock" (MS 121); "Sisyphus, proletarian of the gods, powerless and rebellious, knows the whole extent of his wretched condition: it is what he thinks of during his descent" (MS 121); "Happiness and the absurd are two sons of the same earth. They are inseparable" (MS 122); "The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy" (MS 123) [source: SEP-C §3.4; IEP-EX §3f also quotes MS 121].
- Meursault (*The Stranger*) kills "an Arab on a beach in Algiers", is guillotined; becomes conscious like Sisyphus [source: SEP-C §3.4, §4]. Kamel Daoud's retelling from the victim's family's side calls it a "philosophical crime" [source: SEP-C §4.1].
- Camus on Sartre's *Nausea* (1938 review): its theories and images "don't add up to a work of art" [source: SEP-C §3.1]. Sartre reviewed *The Stranger* ("Camus's *The Outsider*") linking Camus with Pascal, Rousseau and Nietzsche [source: SEP-C §1].

### The absurd argument, step by step (for reconstruction)
1. Human beings cannot help seeking meaning, unity and clarity — the "nostalgia for unity" [SEP-C §3.2–3.3].
2. The world gives no answer: "This world in itself is not reasonable, that is all that can be said" (MS 21) [SEP-C §3.2].
3. The absurd is the confrontation of (1) and (2) — neither alone [SEP-C §3.3].
4. If we are to stay true to the premises we must keep both terms alive; suicide abolishes the questioner, and "hope" (religious or "philosophical suicide") abolishes the silence of the world [SEP-C §3.2–3.3].
5. Therefore the consistent response is to live in lucid revolt, without appeal, and the absurd "dictates" not death but life [SEP-C §3.2, §3.4]. *The Rebel* restates the logic: absurdist reasoning's "final conclusion" is "the repudiation of suicide"; "To say that life is absurd, consciousness must be alive" (R 6, tr. changed) [SEP-C §4.1].
- Objection 1 (Jeanson 1947, "Albert Camus ou le mensonge de l'absurdité"): absurdist philosophy is "a contradiction in terms", an anti-rational posture that ends in silence [SEP-C §1]. Reply (Sagi 2002): Camus speaks not as an irrationalist but as one trying rationally to understand the limits of reason [SEP-C §3.2].
- Objection 2 (Aronson/SEP-C): Camus asserts rather than argues the world's meaninglessness ("truisms", MS 18); and he moves from scepticism/nihilism to a normative stance — "a certain sleight of hand, as the philosopher gives way to the artist" [SEP-C §3.1, §3.5]. Reply: Camus explicitly sought the "consequences" not proofs of the absurd, appealing to common experience; Sagi sees his happiness as close to Aristotle's realisation of human capacities; the principle of consistency gives objective validity [SEP-C §3.1, §3.5, §4.1].
- Objection 3: suicide is an act, not a "question"; reply: for Camus "the primary result of philosophy is action, not comprehension" [SEP-C §3.1].

## Camus: *The Rebel* (1951) and the break with Sartre (1952)
- "I revolt, therefore we are" (R 22) — revolt creates solidarity [SEP-C §4.1]. From suicide to murder: "the age of ideologies" [SEP-C §4.1]. Against revolution: Communism leads to "logical crime"; measure (*mesure*), "Mediterranean" thought [SEP-C §4.2]. "In the Marxian perspective … a hundred thousand deaths is a small price to pay for the happiness of hundreds of millions" (Camus 1991, 130) [SEP-C §4.2].
- Aronson's critique: *The Rebel* focuses solely on Communist violence and ignores colonialism, the Holocaust and Hiroshima; "the philosophy of revolt became Cold-War ideology"; it targets Sartre's turn to history [SEP-C §4.2].
- The break: Sartre gave the review to Francis Jeanson; Jeanson's "Albert Camus ou l'âme révoltée" appeared in *Les Temps modernes* no. 79, May 1952; Camus replied with a letter addressed to "Monsieur le directeur" (dated 30 June 1952), published with replies by Sartre and Jeanson in no. 82, August 1952; Camus and Sartre never spoke again [source: WS (multiple: Partisan Review, Aronson, Kulkarni); SEP-S §1 ("reception of Camus' book in *Les Temps Modernes* in 1951–2 caused Sartre and Camus to very publicly fall out (see Aronson 2004)")]. (One WS source places Jeanson's review in June; most say May.)
- Sartre to Camus: "Vous êtes devenu la proie d'une morne démesure qui masque vos difficultés intérieures. … Tôt ou tard, quelqu'un vous l'eût dit, autant que ce soit moi" [source: WP-LTM] (English rendering in WP is loose; quote French or paraphrase).
- The quarrel made Camus and Sartre "respectively, the leading intellectual voices of the anti-Communist and pro-Communist left" [source: SEP-C intro].

## Camus and Algeria; the "justice and my mother" remark
- Camus's Kabylie reports were "among the first detailed articles ever written by a European Algerian describing the wretched living conditions of the native population" [source: SEP-C §4].
- The remark: at a meeting with students at Stockholm (the student union/maison des étudiants), 12 December 1957 — two days after the Nobel ceremony of 10 December, NOT in his Nobel lecture — answering an Algerian student. Commonly cited wording (from Finkielkraut, *Un cœur intelligent*, 2009): "En ce moment, on lance des bombes dans les tramways d'Alger. Ma mère peut se trouver dans un de ces tramways. Si c'est cela la justice, je préfère ma mère." *Le Monde*'s report rendered it "Je crois à la justice, mais je défendrai ma mère avant la justice", a wording Camus reportedly confirmed in a letter of 17 December 1957; the short form "between justice and my mother, I choose my mother" is a later truncation [source: WS (herodote.net, laconnaissanceouverte blog, cercle-k2) — secondary]. WP-AC gives the English "People are now planting bombs in the tramways of Algiers. My mother might be on one of those tramways. If that is justice, then I prefer my mother" but wrongly places it "during his 1957 Nobel Prize acceptance speech" and calls it "often-misquoted" [source: WP-AC]. Critics called the response reactionary/colonialist [source: WP-AC].

## Fanon
- Frantz Omar Fanon, born 20 July 1925, Martinique (Fort-de-France); died 6 December 1961, Bethesda, Maryland (NIH), of double pneumonia while under treatment for leukaemia; the CIA arranged his travel; admitted under the name Ibrahim Omar Fanon; buried in Algeria (Aïn Kerma) [source: WP-FF; SEP-FA intro; IEP-FA].
- Served in the Free French forces (5th Antillean Marching Battalion), wounded near Montbéliard, awarded the Croix de Guerre; Aimé Césaire was a teacher and influence [source: WP-FF].
- Studied medicine and psychiatry at Lyon; *Black Skin, White Masks* began as a dissertation ("Essay on the Disalienation of the Black") whose rejection led him to publish it as a book [source: WP-FF]. Published 1952 (Seuil), when Fanon was 27 [source: SEP-FA §1].
- 1953: *chef de service* at the Blida-Joinville psychiatric hospital, Algeria (SEP-FA misprints "Bilda"); joined the FLN after the war began on 1 November 1954; resignation letter to the Resident Minister, 1956; expelled January 1957; moved to Tunis; worked on *El Moudjahid*; ambassador to Ghana for the Algerian provisional government [source: WP-FF; IEP-FA; SEP-FA intro]. (WP-FF: Blida "1953–1956"; deportation January 1957 — consistent with Periergia's *Health in Africa* chapter.)
- *A Dying Colonialism* (*L'an V de la révolution algérienne*, 1959); *Toward the African Revolution* (posthumous, 1964); *Écrits sur l'aliénation et la liberté* (2015; English *Alienation and Freedom*, 2018) [source: SEP-FA intro, bibliography].
- *Black Skin, White Masks*: method "existential-phenomenological" (Lewis Gordon) but also Négritude, psychoanalysis, Hegel, Marxism — "one of the more engaging areas of Fanon-interpretation" [source: SEP-FA §1]. Introduction: "the zone of non-being"; Black people locked in blackness, white people in whiteness; subjectivity as "a yes that vibrates to cosmic harmonies" (Philcox 2008: 2) [source: SEP-FA §1].
- Chapter 5, "L'expérience vécue du Noir": a white child points at Fanon: "Look, a Negro!"; Fanon revisits Sartre's account of the look, which "fixes" the other; the "epidermal racial schema", reworking Merleau-Ponty's body schema [source: SEP-FA §1; WP-BSWM]. Translation debate: Markmann (Grove 1967) titled it "The Fact of Blackness"; SEP-FA: *expérience vécue* translates *Erlebnis*, "properly rendered in the Richard Philcox translation as 'lived-experience'" (Grove 2008); David Macey (*Radical Philosophy* 95) called "The Fact of Blackness" unfortunate because Fanon's point is that there is no "fact" of blackness, only lived experience; *nègre* spans 'negro' to the slur [source: SEP-FA §1; WP-BSWM; WS (Macey)]. Philcox's exact chapter title NOT verified (commonly "The Lived Experience of the Black Man").
- Fanon contrasts anti-Semitism (fear of alleged Jewish power) with anti-Black racism (contempt for alleged weakness), against Sartre's *Anti-Semite and Jew* [source: SEP-FA §1].
- Chapter 7 "The Black Man and Hegel": suspicion of recognition modelled on whiteness; footnote: the colonial master wants work, not recognition (cross-ref *German Idealism*) [source: SEP-FA §1; WP-BSWM].
- Criticised as sexist and homophobic (e.g. remarks on rape fantasy and "the Negrophobic man is a repressed homosexual") [source: WP-BSWM].
- *The Wretched of the Earth* (*Les damnés de la terre*, Maspero 1961, published shortly before his death; English tr. Constance Farrington 1963, Richard Philcox 2004): "On Violence" — violence as precondition of liberation, shocking colonizer and colonized into recognising the colonized's humanity; national consciousness and national culture; the pitfalls of a national bourgeoisie; psychiatric case studies [source: SEP-FA §4–5; WP-WE; IEP-FA]. The book was censored in France [source: WP-FF]. Title echoes the "Internationale"; Shatz (2024) traces it to Jacques Roumain's poem "Sales nègres" [source: WP-WE].
- Gordon: *Fanon and the Crisis of European Man* (1995), *What Fanon Said* (2015) [source: WP Lewis_Gordon; SEP-FA (which gives the first title wrongly as "...European Humanity")].

## Others briefly
- **Merleau-Ponty** (1908–1961): met Sartre at the ENS; resistance group Socialisme et Liberté with Sartre (1940–41); co-founded *Les Temps modernes*, political editor, resigned December 1952 over political disagreements set off by the Korean War, left the board 1953; *Adventures of the Dialectic* (1955) attacked Sartre's "ultrabolshevism"; Beauvoir replied with "Merleau-Ponty and Pseudo-Sartreanism" (1955), "opening a rift … that would never entirely heal" [source: SEP-MP §1]. His critique: Sartre's dualism of being and nothingness leaves "no middle ground for history or praxis" [source: SEP-MP §6].
- **Gabriel Marcel** (1889–1973): philosopher, drama critic, playwright (some thirty plays), musician; converted to Catholicism 1929 (prompted by François Mauriac); "problem" vs "mystery" — "A problem is something which I meet … But a mystery is something in which I am myself involved" (*Mystery of Being*, 1949 trans.); "being" vs "having"; "creative fidelity", hope; Gifford Lectures, Aberdeen 1949–50 (*The Mystery of Being*); William James Lectures, Harvard 1961–62; Ricoeur his most famous student [source: SEP-MA]. "Existence and Objectivity" (1925), *Metaphysical Journal* (1927) [source: WP-EX]. Opponent of Sartre's atheistic existentialism [source: IEP-MA].
- **Jaspers**: see above; *Philosophie* (1932), boundary situations [source: SEP-EX §5.1; SEP-JA].
- **Unamuno**: *Tragic Sense of Life* (1913) [source: WP Unamuno]; listed by SEP-EX among the movement's groundwork.
- **Richard Wright** (4 September 1908 – 28 November 1960): moved to Paris; close to Sartre, Beauvoir, Merleau-Ponty; existentialist phase in *The Outsider* (1953); saw himself as working through Kierkegaard (dread, despair) [source: WP Richard_Wright_(author); WP-BE]. Friend of Beauvoir; influenced her account of oppression [source: SEP-B §6–7].
- **Black existentialism**: SEP-EX: Fanon's *Black Skin, White Masks* "helped give birth to Africana critical theory or 'black existentialism' (Gordon 2000)"; existentialism informed Ellison, Wright, Du Bois, King, Malcolm X [source: SEP-EX intro, §2.4]. Lewis R. Gordon: *Bad Faith and Antiblack Racism* (1995); ed. *Existence in Black: An Anthology of Black Existential Philosophy* (Routledge 1997); *Existentia Africana: Understanding Africana Existential Thought* (Routledge 2000) [source: WP Lewis_Gordon; SEP-EX bibliography]. William R. Jones (1970s) used Camus's absurd against black theology; Steve Biko's Black Consciousness echoes Beauvoir's "one becomes" [source: WP-BE]. Du Bois's double consciousness has been "revisited by many scholars as a notion rooted in existentialist thought" [source: WP-BE] (cross-ref *Philosophy in an Age of Empire*).
- **Japan**: Nishitani Keiji studied with Heidegger 1937–39 and made nihilism his central problem, answering it with Buddhist emptiness (*Religion and Nothingness*, tr. Jan Van Bragt, 1982); Tanabe studied with Heidegger and wrote the first article on him (1924); Kuki Shūzō's work on contingency combined "logical rigor and existential insight"; see Steven Light, *Shūzō Kuki and Jean-Paul Sartre: Influence and Counter-Influence in the Early History of Existential Phenomenology* (1987) [source: SEP-KY]. SEP-EX notes "deep affinities with the nonduality of Buddhism" (Loy 2018; Kalmanson 2020) [source: SEP-EX §7.5].
- **Latin America**: Heidegger and Sartre "constituted the dominant philosophical force" for a generation; existentialism gained a foothold in the 1950s–early 1960s; Carlos Astrada (Argentina) and Vicente Ferreira da Silva (Brazil), influenced by Heidegger; Astrada: humans have no determinate essence; in Mexico the Hyperion Group (Hiperión) "briefly took up existentialist themes, abandoning them within a few short years (Sánchez 2016)" [source: SEP-LA §3.3].
- Psychotherapy (Laing, May, Frankl, Yalom) and theology (Barth, Tillich, Buber) [source: SEP-EX intro].

## Consistency with published Periergia texts
- *History of Philosophy* overview: "Sartre: existence precedes essence, radical freedom, bad faith; Beauvoir, whose *The Second Sex*, 1949 — 'one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman' — founded modern feminist philosophy; Camus on the absurd" — consistent (Parshley wording). "Founded modern feminist philosophy" is the overview's evaluative claim; SEP-B calls it "trailblazing" and "one of the most important feminist texts of the twentieth century". No contradiction.
- *Schopenhauer and Kierkegaard*: cites Sartre's *Existentialism Is a Humanism* as "(1946)" (publication year) — consistent if this chapter says "lecture 1945, published 1946". Its caution about "father of existentialism" matches SEP-EX/SEP-EXc ("precursors").
- *German Idealism*: Beauvoir used the lord–bondsman via Kojève; Fanon's footnote (master wants work not recognition) — consistent with SEP-B §2, §7 and SEP-FA §1. It promises cross-reference to this chapter.
- *Phenomenology* (published): Sartre's objection to Heidegger on death (BN 1943) — consistent; that chapter's notes record Merleau-Ponty co-founded *Les Temps modernes* in October 1945 and left in December 1952 (matches SEP-MP).
- *History: Decolonization*: "Fanon, born in Martinique in 1925, served in the Free French army, and headed a service at a psychiatric hospital in Algeria before joining the FLN"; "Sartre's preface celebrated violence"; "some scholars argue translations made Fanon sound like an apostle of violence" — consistent with WP-FF, SEP-S §7 ("appears to endorse a counter-violence"), Bhabha's foreword. ## Debates
- Who counts as an existentialist; whether the term names a movement (1940s–50s France) or a broader tendency reaching back to Kierkegaard (or Socrates) [source: WP-EX; SEP-EX intro; SEP-EXc §1].
- Heidegger as existentialist? He repudiated it; IEP-EX argues he can still be classified as one [source: IEP-EX §3d; SEP-EXc].
- Radical vs situated freedom: Sartre vs Merleau-Ponty (and Beauvoir's "sedimentation"); recent readings (Rae 2024) argue Sartre avoids both determinism and indeterminism [source: SEP-S §4.1; SEP-MP; WP-EX].
- Is Sartre's account of others hopelessly pessimistic ("adolescent paranoia", Marcuse 1948) or a serious contribution to the problem of other minds? [source: SEP-S §4.3].
- Whether Sartre's critique of Freud is fair [source: SEP-S §4.2; WP-JPS].
- Can existentialism ground an ethics? (authentic Nazi problem; Beauvoir's ethics of ambiguity; Webber's "categorical imperative of authenticity") [source: SEP-Sf; SEP-EX §6; SEP-EXc].
- Beauvoir's originality and influence on Sartre (Fullbrooks 1994; Simons 1999; Le Doeuff 1980) vs her own self-description as Sartre's elaborator [source: SEP-B §2, §14].
- Does *The Second Sex* introduce the sex/gender distinction? (Butler vs Moi, Bauer) [source: SEP-B §7; WP-SS]. Its treatment of race [SEP-B §7].
- Parshley vs Borde & Malovany-Chevallier translation (Moi's critiques of both) [source: SEP-B; WP-SS].
- Was Camus a philosopher? (Camus said no; Sartre, Srigley, Sagi treat him as one; Aronson finds assertion rather than argument) [source: SEP-C §1, §3].
- *The Rebel* and Algeria: Camus's anti-Communism as Cold War ideology (Aronson) vs moral clarity on political violence (Foley; Berman) [source: SEP-C §4]; the "mother" remark as reactionary/colonialist (Sherman, Marshall) vs misquoted [source: WP-AC; WS].
- Fanon's method (existential-phenomenological per Gordon; Négritude/psychoanalysis/Marxism per Rabaka; post-structuralist per Bhabha) [source: SEP-FA §1]; Fanon on violence — "apostle of violence" vs violence as precondition of a new humanism; Sartre's preface as distorting (Bhabha) [source: SEP-FA §4; WP-WE].
- Sartre's "Black Orpheus" (race as a negative moment toward the universal) vs Fanon's insistence on lived Black experience [source: SEP-S §7; SEP-FA §1].
