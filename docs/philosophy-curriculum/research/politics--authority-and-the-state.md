## 1. The problem: obligation, authority, legitimacy, justification
- SEP-PO follows "the traditional practice of equating political obligation with a moral duty to obey the law of one's country or state"; "many answers have been given, and none now commands widespread assent", and a number of contemporary philosophers "deny that a satisfactory theory of political obligation either has been or can be devised." [source: SEP-PO intro]
- The phrase "political obligation" is "apparently no older than" T. H. Green's *Lectures on the Principles of Political Obligation*, delivered at Oxford in 1879–80; Green's task was "to discover the true ground or justification for obedience to law". [source: SEP-PO §1]
- Sophocles' *Antigone* (first performed around 440 BCE) raised the problem; Plato's *Crito* gave Socrates' response "some forty years later". [source: SEP-PO §1]
- Political obligation is usually taken to be **content-independent** (a duty to obey the law as such, because it is the law; Hart 1982), **general** (covering all the laws and all subjects) and **particular** (owed to one's own state: Simmons's "particularity requirement", 1979, p. 31). [source: SEP-PO §2.3]
- Prudence is not the issue: "You ought to obey the law because you will suffer if you do not" is a reason, but not the moral reason Green sought. [source: SEP-PO §2.3]
- Legal vs political obligation: a "legal obligation" can be a descriptive social fact; it gives a moral reason only if there is a political obligation. People in a tyranny have legal obligations but may have no moral duty to obey. [source: SEP-PO §2.2]
- Obligation vs duty (Hart 1958; Brandt 1964): obligations are voluntarily incurred, duties need not be. Rawls uses the distinction: most citizens have no general *obligation* to obey but a *natural duty* to support just institutions (Rawls 1999, p. 97). [source: SEP-PO §2.1]
- **Descriptive vs normative legitimacy.** Weber: legitimacy is participants' belief ("Legitimitätsglaube"); "the basis of every system of authority, and correspondingly of every kind of willingness to obey, is a belief, a belief by virtue of which persons exercising authority are lent prestige" (Weber 1964: 382). Three sources: tradition, charisma, legality. [source: SEP-Leg §1]
- Normative legitimacy is tied either to justifying coercive power (Rawls 1993, Ripstein 2004) or to justifying authority, i.e. the right to rule (Raz 1986). De facto authority claims the right to rule and is acquiesced in; legitimate authority actually has it. [source: SEP-Leg §1]
- Rawls's "liberal principle of legitimacy": "political power is legitimate only when it is exercised in accordance with a constitution (written or unwritten) the essentials of which all citizens, as reasonable and rational, can endorse in the light of their common human reason" (Rawls 2001: 41). [source: SEP-Leg §3]
- Legitimacy vs justice: for Rawls, institutions can be legitimate but unjust, not the converse; legitimacy "makes weaker demands than justice". Pettit (2012): a just state imposes a social order promoting non-domination; a legitimate one imposes it "in an appropriate way". [source: SEP-Leg §1]
- **Legitimacy vs justification (Simmons).** The state's *justification* depends on its moral defensibility (showing that having a state is morally better than not, Simmons 2001: 125); its *legitimacy* (its right to impose duties on particular people) depends on their actual consent. Justification is necessary but not sufficient for legitimacy; there is no general duty to obey one's own state, but that does not make all states "necessarily illegitimate" (2001: 137). [source: SEP-Leg §2.2] (Simmons's essay collection is *Justification and Legitimacy*, Cambridge 2000 per WP; SEP cites it as 2001 — give 2000/2001 or omit year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._John_Simmons])
- Edmundson (1998): legitimacy may justify a state's issuing directives without creating even a prima facie duty to obey them. Dworkin (1986: 191) also separates legitimate authority from a duty to obey. [source: SEP-Leg §§1, 2.2]
- Bernard Williams's "basic legitimation demand" (2005): providing security is necessary but not sufficient; the state must also justify how it provides it. Tommie Shelby (2007): oppressed minorities may rightly see institutions as lacking legitimacy because the order is unjust. [source: SEP-Leg §1]

## 2. History in brief: Crito, divine command, contract, utility, Kant
- 399 BCE: Socrates convicted of impiety and corrupting the youth; friends arranged escape; he refused, arguing that escape would break his "agreements and commitments" and "mistreat" friends, country and the laws (Crito 54c, Grube). [source: SEP-PO §1.1]
- SEP-PO finds four seeds of later theories in the *Crito*: (1) long residence = agreement (consent); (2) birth, nurture, education owed to the laws, with the father analogy (membership/associative, also gratitude); (3) fair play: "if we leave here without the city's permission, are we mistreating people whom we should least mistreat?" (50a); (4) utility: "do you think it possible for a city not to be destroyed if the verdicts of its courts have no force but are nullified and set at naught by private individuals?" (50b). [source: SEP-PO §1.1]
- "Bedrock principle": "neither to do wrong or to return a wrong is ever right, not even to injure in return for an injury received" (Crito 46b, 49d). The personified Laws tell Socrates to "persuade or obey" them (51b–c); he was "wronged not by us, the laws, but by men" (54c). SEP-Soc notes irony: since 403/2 BCE it had been a crime even to propose a law conflicting with the newly inscribed laws. [source: SEP-Soc]
- Crito vs Apology debate: in the *Apology* (29c–30c) Socrates says he would not obey a verdict ordering him to stop philosophising; whether this contradicts the *Crito* is debated (Young 1974; Kraut: persuasion must be seriously possible, else disobedience may be permissible; Penner 1997, Woozley 1979 and others read stricter obedience; Gallop 1998: Socrates accepts the result, not necessarily the Laws' argument). Cicero read the *Crito* as grounding service from gratitude. Hume called it the only ancient text with the idea of an implicit promise of loyalty. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crito; earlier notes in ancient--socrates-and-the-sophists.md mention Grote's problem]
- Divine command: Romans 13:1–2, "For there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God. Therefore he who resists the authorities resists what God has appointed..." Responses allowing disobedience: office vs officer (John Chrysostom, 4th c.), disobedience vs active resistance (Luther), conflicting authorities (Reformation). These pressures pushed Protestants toward consent of the governed (Skinner). [source: SEP-PO §1.2]
- Utilitarianism "has found few adherents" among believers in a general obligation; sophisticated attempts (Sartorius 1975, Hare 1976) are widely judged unsuccessful. [source: SEP-PO §1.4]
- Kant: leaving the state of nature is a moral imperative, not a matter of consent: "When you cannot avoid living side by side with all others, you ought to leave the state of nature and proceed with them into a rightful condition"; the original contract is "the Idea of this act, in terms of which alone we can think of the legitimacy of a state"; subjects may oppose injustice "by complaints … but not by resistance" (Kant 1991 [1797], pp. 121–2, 127, 130, as cited in SEP-PO; translator not named there). [source: SEP-PO §1.5] Kant accepted Hume's point that states often begin in violence and made the contract a test: each law should be one all could have consented to (*Theory and Practice* 8:294). [source: SEP-Leg §2.2]

## 3. The social contract compared (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau) — keep brief, cross-link
- SEP-PO: the classic expressions of the contract theory of obligation remain *Leviathan* (1651) and Locke's *Second Treatise* (1690). [source: SEP-PO §1.3]
- **Hobbes:** state of nature = "a warre of every man against every man", driven by "restless desire for Power after power" (chs 11, 13); people covenant to obey a sovereign with power to make, enforce and interpret law ("sovereignty by institution"); conquerors also gain authority ("sovereignty by acquisition"). Either way subjects consent to whoever has effective power. SEP-PO: few now accept that "agreement at the point of a sword" creates moral (not prudential) obligation, and in Hobbes "it is not clear that consent is really the key" — rather "we have an obligation to obey anyone who can maintain order." [source: SEP-PO §1.3] Political obligation "ends when protection ceases" (SEP hobbes-moral, via Hobbes notes). See *Hobbes* notes for quotations.
- "Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words, and of no strength to secure a man at all" — *Leviathan* ch. 17 (1651 spelling). Found by search in the Standard Ebooks, Saylor and SparkNotes texts; **WS only** (the Hobbes chapter notes had this as unverified; now weakly verified). [source: WS: https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/thomas-hobbes/leviathan/text/chapter-17]
- **Locke:** free and equal individuals set up government to escape the "inconveniencies" of the state of nature; two stages (form a society; then establish government), which lets Locke argue for a right of revolution without a return to the state of nature; mere submission to a conqueror is not consent. [source: SEP-PO §1.3]
- Locke on consent: full membership requires **express consent** (II.122); **tacit consent** is given by enjoying property or even "walking along the highways" (SEP-LP's paraphrase; inheritance II.120). [source: SEP-LP §4] SEP-PO: Locke "stretch[es] the notion of tacit consent too far" in II.119, where the "very being of anyone within the territories" expresses submission. [source: SEP-PO §1.3]
- Locke §119 wording (WS only): "[E]very man, that hath any Possession, or Enjoyment, of any part of the Dominions of any Government, doth thereby give his tacit Consent", extending to a lodging for a week or "barely travelling freely on the highway". Use singular "Possession". [source: WS: https://carneades.pomona.edu/2019f-Political/14.LockeHumeConsent.html]
- Simmons on Locke: it is hard to see how walking on a street or inheriting land is a "deliberate, voluntary alienating of rights" (Simmons 1993, 69); Locke's logic pushes toward "philosophical anarchism". Pitkin (1965): consent matters less than it seems because natural law fixes what government may do ("hypothetical consent"). [source: SEP-LP §4]
- **Rousseau:** the problem is to find "a form of association" in which each citizen would "obey only himself" (SC I.6, SEP-R's fragments; translator not named). "Man is born free, but is everywhere in chains" (SEP-R; Cole: "Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains"); those subject to the general will are "forced to be free" (I.7). [source: SEP-R §3.3] The full Cole sentence of I.6 ("...may still obey himself alone, and remain as free as before") was **not verified** (see NOT VERIFIED).
- Rousseau's contract differs from the other two: obligation comes from obeying a law one gives oneself as a member of the sovereign people (moral freedom), not from transferring rights to a separate ruler; sovereignty cannot be represented. Details and quotations in *Enlightenment and Rousseau* notes §8b. [source: SEP-R §3]
- Rousseau's Second Discourse: the first, fraudulent contract proposed by the rich (see Rousseau notes §8a) — useful as the first "critique of the contract from inside".
- **General problems of contract theory (SEP-PO §1.3):** historical or hypothetical? If historical, few have actually contracted; if hypothetical, "a hypothetical contract is no contract at all" (Dworkin 1977, p. 151). Hume was "the first to raise the decisive objection" to historical contracts: the consent of one's ancestors does not bind oneself (SEP-CT, citing Hume 1777, pp. 470–1). [sources: SEP-PO §1.3; SEP-CT]
- Contract theory beyond Europe: the sibling chapters handle Pateman's *The Sexual Contract* (1988: a prior "original pact" among men to dominate women; the contract is a means of patriarchy, not its end) and Mills's *The Racial Contract* (1997, inspired by Pateman: a prior contract determines who counts as a full person able to "contract in"). Mention only. [sources: IEP-SC §§4–5; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sexual_Contract; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Racial_Contract]

## 4. ARGUMENT A (step by step) — Hume against tacit consent, "Of the Original Contract"
- Date: **disputed in the sources.** SEP-Auth, SEP-Leg, IEP-Hume and the Pomona handouts give **1748**; SEP-PO gives **1752**; SEP-CT cites the **1777** edition. Standard view: first published 1748 (in *Three Essays, Moral and Political*), later included in *Essays, Moral, Political, and Literary* (Part II, Essay XII). Safe wording: "first published in 1748". [sources: SEP-Auth bibliography "Hume, David, 1748 [1965]"; SEP-Leg bibliography "Hume, David, 1748"; IEP-Hume ("his 1748 essay"); SEP-PO §1.3 ("published in 1752"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essays,_Moral,_Political,_and_Literary]
- Reconstruction of the target (consent theory, as SEP-Auth frames it): P1. If someone voluntarily resides in a state's territory and benefits from its rule of law, he knows or ought to know that others expect his obedience. P2. So continued voluntary residence can reasonably be interpreted as committing him to obey. C. Residence is tacit consent, which grounds obligation. [source: SEP-Auth §2]
- Hume's objection, step by step:
  1. Consent requires a free choice; tacit consent must still engage the will ("Even a tacit contract requires that the will be engaged"). Native subjects do not think they consented; they think they were born to obey, and governments punish disloyalty in people who gave no promise. [source: SEP-HumeM §12]
  2. Leaving one's country is, for most people, ruinously costly. "Can we seriously say that a poor peasant or artisan has a free choice to leave his country, when he knows no foreign language or manners, and lives from day to day by the small wages which he acquires?" [source: IEP-Hume (quotes it directly)]
  3. Analogy: someone "carried on board while asleep" who "must leap into the ocean and perish the moment he leaves her" does not consent to the captain's authority by staying (SEP-PO's quotation of Hume, ed. Hendel 1953, p. 51). [source: SEP-PO §1.3; SEP-Auth §2]
  4. So continued residence cannot be read as consent; the consent theory fails for almost everyone.
  5. Hume's positive view: allegiance rests not on promise or contract but on "the general social value of having a government" (utility, interest), which runs parallel to, and is independent of, the duty to keep promises. History shows governments founded on conquest and usurpation. Rebellion against "a cruel tyranny" is permitted. [sources: SEP-HumeM §12; IEP-Hume]
- Note on the popular label: Hume's man is **"carried on board while asleep"**, i.e. kidnapped; the "press-ganged sailor" is a later gloss, not Hume's wording. [source: SEP-PO §1.3 quotation]
- **Objection to Hume (Beran 1987, via SEP-Auth):** people often make binding commitments to avoid terrible costs (insurance; combatants laying down arms on condition of safety), so a terrible alternative does not void consent. [source: SEP-Auth §2]
- **Reply / Hume's second point:** even if residents should know obedience is expected, that shows the consent argument is "an idle wheel": what really obligates is the public benefit of the state and the importance of compliance to producing it. Simmons: the "interpretation problem" (why residence should *mean* consent) remains; Brilmayer and Wellman: tacit consent begs the question, since a group with no authority cannot demand that people submit or leave a territory it claims. [source: SEP-Auth §2]

## 5. Simmons and the critique of consent (and the other theories)
- A. John Simmons (b. 4 May 1950), American political philosopher; taught at the University of Virginia from 1976; *Moral Principles and Political Obligations* (Princeton, 1979); *On the Edge of Anarchy: Locke, Consent, and the Limits of Society* (Princeton, 1993). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._John_Simmons]
- Critics such as Hume and Simmons (1979, chs 3–4) do not deny that consent can create obligation; they claim too few people have given actual express or tacit consent of the right kind, and hypothetical consent cannot fill the gap. [source: SEP-PO §4.1]
- Simmons's conclusion is "a posteriori" philosophical anarchism: no defence of a general obligation has succeeded, so only the few who have expressly committed themselves (e.g. by an oath of citizenship) have something like a general obligation (Simmons 1979, 2001; also Smith 1973; Raz 1979; L. Green 1988). [source: SEP-PO §3.1]
- Philosophical anarchists "do not take the illegitimacy of states to entail a strong moral imperative to oppose or eliminate states" (Simmons 2001: 104). [sources: SEP-PO §3; SEP-An §2]
- Consent theory's defenders: Beran (1987) wants formal procedures for express consent, with dissenters free to leave, secede or live in a reserved territory (critics: the options would not make consent voluntary). Plamenatz and Steinberger (2004): voting, calling the police, using libraries etc. count as consent. Murphy: "surrender of judgment is a kind of consent". Gilbert: obligations from "joint commitment", which need not be voluntary. Estlund (2008) "normative consent": refusing to consent when one ought to is void, so one is bound anyway. [source: SEP-PO §4.1]
- Carole Pateman's *The Problem of Political Obligation* (2nd edn, University of California Press, 1979) is a consent-critical, participatory-democratic study (bibliographic entry only). [source: SEP-PO bibliography]

## 6. Fairness (fair play)
- Hart's formulation, "Are There Any Natural Rights?" *Philosophical Review* 64 (1955): "when a number of persons conduct any joint enterprise according to rules and thus restrict their liberty, those who have submitted to these restrictions when required have a right to a similar submission from those who have benefited by their submission" (p. 185). [source: SEP-PO §4.2; bibliography]
- Rawls adopted it as the "duty of fair play" in "Legal Obligation and the Duty of Fair Play" (1964, in S. Hook ed., *Law and Philosophy*). [source: SEP-PO §4.2]
- Argument: those who participate in a reasonably just, mutually beneficial cooperative practice owe a fair share of its burdens to fellow participants; free riders wrong them even if the scheme survives; since obeying the law is the principal form of cooperation in a polity, members owe obedience. [source: SEP-PO §4.2]
- **Nozick's objection** (*Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, 1974, pp. 90–95, the example at p. 93): neighbours set up a public address/entertainment system, each adult assigned a day to run it; you occasionally hear and enjoy it but never agreed; when your day comes, must you take a turn? Fair play says yes; the right answer is "surely not". So the principle would let others put us under obligations just by conferring benefits on us. [sources: SEP-PO §4.2; SEP-Auth §2 ("neighborhood public address system", "loudspeaker")]
- Simmons's objection (1979): receiving benefits ≠ accepting them; states produce non-excludable goods (order, defence) one cannot refuse; few accept them knowingly as products of a cooperative scheme, and most see them as "purchased (with taxes) from a central authority" (1979, p. 139). [source: SEP-PO §4.2]
- Replies: Nozick's case is beside the point because passive receipt doesn't make one a participant (Dagger 1997) or the benefits are "of relatively little value" (Klosko); many people participate without full awareness (Dagger, Besson); obligations needn't be voluntary (Arneson 1982; Klosko). Klosko's conditions: goods must be "(i) worth the recipients' effort in providing them; (ii) 'presumptively beneficial'; and (iii) have benefits and burdens that are fairly distributed" (2004, p. 39). George Klosko, *The Principle of Fairness and Political Obligation* (1992; 2nd edn 2004) and *Political Obligations* (OUP 2005). [source: SEP-PO §4.2 and bibliography]
- Counter-replies: Simmons says modern states are too large and impersonal to be cooperative schemes, and Klosko's view is a "disguised Natural Duty theory" (2005, p. 190); fairness may be "a powerfully conservative principle" (Normore 2010). [source: SEP-PO §4.2]
- Socrates' "mistreating people whom we should least mistreat" (Crito 50a) is an early fairness argument. [source: SEP-PO §1.1]

## 7. Gratitude
- Argument (SEP-Grat §5.3): (1) The state benefits its subjects. (2) So subjects owe gratitude. (3) This includes not harming the state. (4) Disobeying the law harms the state. (5) So subjects should obey. Ancient pedigree (depending on reading of the *Crito*). Advantage: explains particularity, and refugees' obligations to generous host states (D'Cruz 2014). [source: SEP-Grat §5.3]
- Objections: a state may not be the right kind of benefactor (doubts it acts from benevolence, or sacrifices; Simmons 1979: 185–188); taxes are forced, and "Someone who forces benefits on me, and forces me to pay a share for them, may thereby undermine his desert of gratitude" (Klosko 1989; Wellman 1999); unjust laws forfeit gratitude; and (4) fails — secret disobedience need not harm, and civil disobedience against unjust laws may help the state. Fallback: some gratitude obligations of "wider latitude", not obedience (McConnell 1993). Replies: Walker (1988) — we feel gratitude to schools; McConnell — we owe repayment to corporations. [source: SEP-Grat §5.3]

## 8. Natural duty of justice
- Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (1971): a natural duty of justice "requires us to support and to comply with just institutions that exist and apply to us" (1999 rev. edn, p. 99). Natural duties bind us as moral agents without any voluntary act. [sources: SEP-PO §4.4; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Theory_of_Justice]
- Developed by Waldron (1999), Christiano (*The Constitution of Equality*, 2008), Wellman (2005: Samaritan duty of easy rescue), Stilz (*Liberal Loyalty*, 2009: Kantian freedom as independence). Shared argument: justice is underdetermined, needs coordination, and people reasonably disagree; only a common legal order can settle "for practical purposes what justice consists in" (Christiano); "There is no way other than general compliance with a single authoritative set of rules to secure peace and protect basic moral rights" (Wellman 2005, p. 45). Many require the order to be democratic and rights-respecting. [source: SEP-PO §4.4]
- **Main objection, particularity (Simmons 1979, pp. 31–5; 2005):** why must I discharge a duty to support just institutions through *my* state's, rather than others' that are more just or more needy? Replies: proximity and interaction (Waldron); free-riding on fellow citizens (Wellman); justice is a way of relating to those one interacts with, not an outcome to promote anywhere. [source: SEP-PO §4.4]

## 9. Associative (membership) obligations — Dworkin
- Associative obligations arise from occupying a role in a socially salient relationship (parent, friend, citizen), whether chosen or not. Particularity follows automatically. [source: SEP-PO §4.3]
- Ronald Dworkin, *Law's Empire* (Harvard, 1986): "political association, like family and friendship and other forms of association more local and intimate, is in itself pregnant of obligation" (p. 206); associative political obligations arise only in a "community of principle", committed to treating all members with equal concern and respect (pp. 213–215). [source: SEP-PO §4.3]
- John Horton (*Political Obligation*, 1992; 2nd edn 2010): a polity, "like the family, [is] a relationship into which we are mostly born", whose constitutive obligations "do not stand in need of moral justification" (1992, pp. 150–51). Yael Tamir: "deep and important obligations flow from identity and relatedness" (1993, p. 99). [source: SEP-PO §4.3]
- Objections: citizens lack the intimacy that grounds family obligations (Jeske 2001: the notion of relationship "stretched beyond recognition"); membership alone would bind the oppressed members of unjust polities (Dagger 2000); conditions like Dworkin's make the theory collapse into consent or natural duty (Simmons 2001; Wellman). Replies: value need not require intimacy (Scheffler 2018); unjust "associations" are not genuine ones; Renzo's quasi-voluntarism (exit at reasonable cost). [source: SEP-PO §4.3]
- Pluralism: Klosko (2005), Jonathan Wolff, Knowles (2010) argue for multiple principles. [source: SEP-PO §5]

## 10. ARGUMENT B (step by step) — Wolff's philosophical anarchism, and Raz's reply
- Robert Paul Wolff (27 Dec 1933, New York – 6 Jan 2025), taught at Harvard, Chicago, Columbia (1964–71), then the University of Massachusetts Amherst (later in Afro-American Studies). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Paul_Wolff]
- *In Defense of Anarchism* (Harper & Row, 1970; 80 pp.); five editions, latest University of California Press 1998. Parts: "The Conflict between Authority and Autonomy", "The Solution of Classical Democracy", "Beyond the Legitimate State". Praise from Murray Rothbard "chagrined" Wolff. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_Defense_of_Anarchism]
- Definitions: authority is "the right to command, and correlatively, the right to be obeyed" (p. 4, 1998 edn); "To claim authority is to claim the right to be obeyed" (1970: 6). [sources: SEP-PO §3.1; SEP-Auth §1]
- Reconstruction:
  1. Authority = the right to command and to be obeyed; to accept authority is to do something *because* one is told to (content-independently). [SEP-PO §3.1; SEP-Auth §2.4]
  2. Every rational agent has a "primary obligation" of autonomy, "the refusal to be ruled" (p. 18): freedom plus responsibility to decide for oneself what is right in each case. [SEP-PO §3.1]
  3. Acting on a command because it is commanded, rather than on one's own moral judgement of its merits, violates autonomy. [SEP-Auth §2.4]
  4. So autonomy and authority are incompatible, except in a unanimous direct democracy (where one obeys only laws one has willed) — "so restricted in its application that it offers no serious hope of ever being embodied in an actual state" (1970: 55). [SEP-An §2.2]
  5. Conclusion: "If all men have a continuing obligation to achieve the highest degree of autonomy possible, then there would appear to be no state whose subjects have a moral obligation to obey its commands. Hence, the concept of a de jure legitimate state would appear to be vacuous, and philosophical anarchism would seem to be the only reasonable political belief for an enlightened man." (1970: 17) [SEP-An §2.2]
- Wolff does not deny *de facto* legitimate states; the anarchist "will never view the commands of the state as legitimate, as having a binding moral force" (1970: 16). Wolff claims Kantian inspiration (Kant himself was no anarchist). [sources: SEP-An §2.2; SEP-PO §1.5]
- Opening of Part I (WS only): "The defining mark of the state is authority, the right to rule. The primary obligation of man is autonomy, the refusal to be ruled." [source: WS: https://freethoughtblogs.com/stderr/2016/09/04/sunday-sermon-in-defense-of-anarchism-3 ; second sentence's p. 18 confirmed by SEP-PO]
- **Objections:** (a) autonomy need not "invariably override all other values" (Horton); (b) autonomy as a capacity must be developed, and authority (including political) can foster it (Dagger 1997); (c) Wolff's argument would also condemn promises and contracts as constraints on autonomy, so Simmons calls it a "failed attempt" (2001, p. 111); (d) Reiman (1972) noted its a priori, absolute character. Matthew Noah Smith (2011) tried to rescue it via the moral status of the self. [sources: SEP-PO §3.2; SEP-An §2.2]
- **Raz's reply: the service conception** (*The Morality of Freedom*, Clarendon Press, Oxford, 1986). [sources: SEP-Auth §2.4; SEP-Leg §2.1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Raz]
  - Normal Justification Thesis: "The normal way to establish that a person has authority over another involves showing that the alleged subject is likely to better comply with the reasons which apply to him (other than the alleged authoritative directive) if he accepts the directives of the alleged authority as authoritatively binding and tries to follow them, rather than by trying to follow the reasons which apply to him directly" (Raz 1986: 53; SEP-Leg wording; SEP-Auth has "likely better to comply ... applies to him").
  - Dependence Thesis: directives "should be based on reasons which already independently apply to the subjects" (1986: 47). Example: we already have reason to contribute a fair share; authority sets a fair, efficient tax scheme.
  - Pre-emption Thesis: an authority's requirement "is a reason for its performance which is not to be added to all other relevant reasons when assessing what to do, but should exclude and take the place of some of them" (1986: 46).
  - Together = the "service conception" (1986: 56). Reply to Wolff: it can be irrational, even immoral, to insist on acting on one's own judgement when following an authority would make one conform better to reason; one can autonomously decide to defer (a higher-order exercise of autonomy). Coordination is "a major, if not the main, factor" in political legitimacy (Raz 2009: 153).
  - Consequence: authority is **piecemeal** — binding only those who would do better by deferring, and only in those domains; Raz denies any existing state has a general duty of obedience. Independence condition: where acting on one's own judgement matters more than getting it right (e.g. choosing friends), authority fails even if NJT is met.
  - Objections: pre-emption seems irrational (Hurd 2001: one cannot rationally ignore the particular facts); Darwall (2010): a financial adviser may satisfy the NJT yet has no standing to *demand* compliance, so NJT gives no right to rule. Raz replies that the power to impose duties is distinct from a right to rule correlating with accountability (Raz 2010: 290). [SEP-Auth §§2.4.1–2.4.2]
- Joseph Raz (born Joseph Zaltsman, Haifa, 21 Mar 1939 – London, 2 May 2022); *The Morality of Freedom* won the 1987 W. J. M. Mackenzie Book Prize (Political Studies Association). Other works: *The Authority of Law* (1979). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Raz]

## 11. Political anarchism (classical)
- "Anarchism" from Greek *arché* (first principle, ruling power); anarchy = rule by no one. [source: SEP-An §1]
- **William Godwin**, *Enquiry Concerning Political Justice* (published 14 Feb 1793). "government, even in its best state, is an evil" and we should have "as little of it as the general peace of human society will permit" (bk III, ch. VII, pp. 185–6); "despotism is as perennial as anarchy is transitory" (bk VII, ch. V, p. 736). [sources: SEP-An §2.1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enquiry_Concerning_Political_Justice]
- **Pierre-Joseph Proudhon** (15 Jan 1809, Besançon – 19 Jan 1865); a printer; *What Is Property?* (*Qu'est-ce que la propriété?*, 1840) with the slogan "property is theft!"; "one of the first authors to explicitly affirm anarchism". Quote: "The government of man by man (under whatever name it be disguised) is oppression. Society finds its highest perfection in the union of order with anarchy." (Proudhon 1840, 1876 English edition p. 286). Broke with Marx (*The Poverty of Philosophy*). [sources: SEP-An §2.1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Joseph_Proudhon]
- **Mikhail Bakunin**: "If there is a State, there must be domination of one class by another and, as a result, slavery; the State without slavery is unthinkable—and this is why we are the enemies of the State." (1873; 1990 edn p. 178). [source: SEP-An §1.1]
- **Peter Kropotkin** (9 Dec 1842 – 8 Feb 1921), Russian geographer, anarchist communist; *The Conquest of Bread* (1892); *Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution* (1902; essays first in *The Nineteenth Century*, 1890–96), arguing that cooperation is as much a factor of evolution as competition. 1910 encyclopedia (Britannica) article on anarchism; denied anarchists argued a priori. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Kropotkin; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_Aid:_A_Factor_of_Evolution; SEP-An §§2.1–2.2]
- **Emma Goldman** (1869, Kovno/Kaunas – 14 May 1940); published *Mother Earth* from March 1906; *Anarchism and Other Essays* (1910, Mother Earth Publishing), including "Anarchism: What It Really Stands For"; jailed 1917 for opposing the draft; deported to Russia December 1919; *My Disillusionment in Russia* (1923). Quote: "I believe that Anarchism is the only philosophy of peace, the only theory of the social relationship that values human life above everything else... Every institution today rests on violence" (1913; 1998 edn p. 59). [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emma_Goldman; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarchism_and_Other_Essays; SEP-An §5] (WP gives her birth day inconsistently, 27 June vs 7 June: give only the year.)
- Christian and religious anarchism: Tolstoy (1894) refuses allegiance; anarchist threads in Taoism, Sufism, bhakti (Ramnath 2011). Gandhi: "I myself am an anarchist, but of another type" (1916). [source: SEP-An §§1.2, 5]
- Political vs philosophical anarchism: political anarchists hold states are necessarily immoral and should be abolished; philosophical anarchists deny political obligation without demanding abolition. [source: SEP-PO §3]

## 12. Confucian rule by virtue and *li* vs Legalist rule by *fa* — keep comparative
- Mandate of Heaven (*tianming*): the Zhou legitimised their conquest of the Shang (c. 1046 BCE) by claiming Heaven had transferred its mandate because of Shang depravity and misrule; Heaven became a moral judge of rulers' virtue (Allan 1984). By the Warring States, Heaven was closer to an abstract normative order. [sources: SEP-ChSP §1; SEP-Men §1]
- *De* (Virtue) originally a king's political charisma; subjects "would willingly follow a ruler with Virtue, without the need for coercion." [source: SEP-Men §1]
- Analects 2.3 (Slingerland 2003): "If you try to guide the common people with coercive regulations and keep them in line with punishments, the common people will become evasive and will have no sense of shame. If, however, you guide them with Virtue, and keep them in line by means of ritual, the people will have a sense of shame and will rectify themselves." [source: SEP-Men §1]
- Mencius: "climbing a tree in search of a fish" (1A7) for conquering by war. [source: SEP-Men §1]
- Mencius 7B14 (IEP translation): "The people are to be valued most, the altars of the grain and the land ... next, the ruler least." 5B9: ministers of royal blood "admonish. If after repeated admonishments he still will not listen, they depose him". [source: IEP-Men §4]
- **Mencius 1B8** (IEP translation): King Xuan of Qi (r. 319–301 BCE) asks whether Tang and Wu rebelled against and killed Jie and Zhou; "Is it permissible for a vassal to murder his lord?" Mencius: "One who robs co-humanity [ren] you call a 'robber'; one who robs the right [yi] you call a 'wrecker'; and one who robs and wrecks you call an 'outlaw.' I have heard that [Wu] punished the outlaw Zhou – I have not heard that he murdered his lord." A rectification of names: a ruler who destroys *ren* and *yi* is no longer a ruler. [source: IEP-Men §4] (WP's rendering: "I have merely heard of killing a villain Zhou, but I have not heard of murdering [him as] the ruler" — see Confucianism notes.)
- Shun's legitimacy (5A5): "Heaven gave it to him; human beings gave it to him" — Heaven's acceptance shown through the people's satisfaction; "Tian does not speak – it simply reveals through deeds and affairs". IEP: Mencius endorses a "right of revolution" but "is no democrat"; "a type of benevolent dictatorship". [source: IEP-Men §§3–4]
- **Debate:** Bai Tongdong (2008, "A Mencian Version of Limited Democracy") reads Mencius as a foundation for democracy; Justin Tiwald (2008, "A Right of Rebellion in the Mengzi?") cautions against overestimating his championing of popular will, stressing Mencius's "essentially procedural requirement that the ruler be properly designated for the task" (Tiwald 2008: 274). Loubna El Amine (2015) argues political order, not moral edification, is the Confucian end. [sources: SEP-Men; SEP-ChSP §1]
- Xunzi: "There are people who create order; there are no standards creating order of themselves" — the character-centred view against rule by standards alone. [source: SEP-ChSP §4]
- **Legalist (*fa*) view:** Pines prefers "the *fa* tradition"; "Legalism" is "quite inaccurate" (see Hundred Schools notes). [source: SEP-ChLeg §1]
- *Fa* must be public and impartial: "laws are applicable equally to every subject, and even the ruler himself should strictly observe them"; transparency "so that everyone within his frontiers, including the lowly and base, will hear and understand it" (Han Feizi 38.8.2). Pines: *fa* "is not a tool of intimidation and suppression as is often imagined (despite the notorious advocacy of harsh punishments)". Impartiality (*gong*) vs selfishness (*si*). [source: SEP-ChLeg §2.3]
- Shang Yang (Book of Lord Shang, Pines's translation): "When the people are weak, the state is strong. Hence the state that possesses the Way devotes itself to weakening the people" (20.1); "To prevent wrongdoing and stop transgressions, nothing is better than making punishments heavy. When punishments are heavy and [criminals] are inevitably captured, then the people dare not try [to break the law]. Hence, there are no penalized people in the state" (17.3); goal: "eradicate punishments with punishments" (18.1). Harsh punishments even for minor or "about to be committed" offences; mutual responsibility among neighbours and kin; mandatory denunciation. Likes and dislikes "are the root of rewards and penalties" (9.3). Even Sima Qian granted his success in making Qin's people law-abiding (Shiji 68). [source: SEP-ChLeg §3.1]
- Against Confucian "worthies": "one is ennobled before one has any merits; one is punished before one has committed a crime" (Book of Lord Shang 25.1). Shen Dao: if the ruler "abandons the standard (*fa*) and relies on himself to govern", identical merits are rewarded differently and resentment arises (Harris 2016: 120). [source: SEP-ChLeg §4]
- Han Feizi ch. 40: the system is for the average ruler, for whom positional power (*shi*) matters more than virtue; ch. 49: "The sage does not try to practice the ways of antiquity or to abide by a fixed standard, but examines the affairs of the age". [source: SEP-ChSP §2.9] Two handles (reward, punishment), *fa/shu/shi*: see Hundred Schools notes.
- Comparison point for the chapter: both traditions ask what makes rule *work* and be *rightful*; neither frames it as consent. Confucians ground legitimacy in virtue/Heaven's mandate shown in the people's welfare (with a limited right to depose a tyrant); *fa* thinkers ground order in impersonal, public, impartial standards backed by reward and punishment. Pines and SEP-ChSP warn against reading "Legalists" as mere totalitarians and "Confucians" as anti-law.

## 13. Islamic political thought on just rule and obedience
- **Al-Fārābī** (d. c. 950; dates in *Falsafa* notes): *The Opinions of the People of the Virtuous City* (also called *The Perfect State*) and *The Political Regime* may be among his latest works. Draws on Plato's *Republic* and *Laws* (the *Politics* apparently not available in Arabic). The excellent city (*madina fadila*) is one "in which people aim through association at co-operating for the things by which felicity in its real and true sense can be attained" (*Perfect State* V.15.3). Its natural ruler is at once "philosopher, prophet, supreme ruler, lawgiver, and imam"; if no single person has all the qualities, several with complementary qualities rule together. "Immoral cities" pursue status, honour, domination; "No one at all among the inhabitants of these cities gains happiness" (*Political Regime*). He envisages a multi-ethnic, multi-religious empire rather than a Greek city-state (Orwin 2017). Legitimacy here is perfectionist: rule is rightful insofar as it guides citizens to happiness. [sources: SEP-Far; SEP-FarSR §§2, 4.1–4.2]
- **Ibn Taymiyya** (1263–1328, Damascus): *Law-Guided Public Policy* (*al-Siyāsa al-Sharʿiyya*), drafted around 1310. Religion "cannot do without temporal power"; public authority's purpose is "to reform the religion of the people" so "that religion is entirely for God" (Siyāsa 30, 33); he gives no blueprint for an ideal Islamic state but urges officials to reform within existing structures. He argued the Mamlūks must fight the Ilkhānid Mongols (converted 1295) because they did not follow all the laws of Islam. His relations with rulers "ranged from silence to open rebellion" (WP). [sources: SEP-IT §§1, 5; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Taymiyya]
- **Ibn Khaldūn** (27 May 1332, Tunis – 17 March 1406, Cairo); *Muqaddimah* written 1377 as the introduction to the *Kitāb al-ʿIbar*; standard English translation by Franz Rosenthal (3 vols, Princeton, 1958). *ʿAṣabiyya* = group feeling, social cohesion, strongest among nomads; it carries a group to "royal authority" (*mulk*), then decays in sedentary luxury; dynasties "have a natural life span like individuals", rarely lasting beyond three generations of about forty years each. Government is "an institution which prevents injustice other than such as it commits itself" (Gellner called this the best definition in political philosophy); "injustice ruins civilization". Best polity: the caliphate under revealed law; second, rule by rational justice for worldly welfare; worst, tyranny that usurps property. He dismissed al-Fārābī's "virtuous city" as useless. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Khaldun; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muqaddimah; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asabiyyah] (No SEP or IEP entry on Ibn Khaldūn exists: plato.stanford.edu/entries/ibn-khaldun/ is 404; Wikipedia only, so state cautiously.)
- Ovamir Anjum, *Politics, Law and Community in Islamic Thought: The Taymiyyan Moment* (Cambridge, 2012). [source: SEP-IT bibliography]

## 14. Civil disobedience and the duty to obey unjust laws
- **Rawls's definition** (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971, §55): civil disobedience is "a public, nonviolent, conscientious yet political act contrary to law usually done with the aim of bringing about a change in the law or policies of the government" (1971 p. 364; 1999 edn p. 320). Exact wording **WS-verified**; SEP-CD paraphrases it as "a public, non-violent and conscientious breach of law undertaken with the aim of bringing about a change in laws or government policies" and calls it "the most widely accepted account". It addresses "the sense of justice of the majority of the community" (1999, 320). [sources: SEP-CD intro, §1.2; WS: https://eugenefds.substack.com/p/john-rawls-on-civil-disobedience]
- Features on the Rawlsian view: fidelity to law, publicity (openly, with warning, after Hugo Bedau 1961), non-violence, non-evasion (accepting punishment). Rawls's justification conditions: (1) serious, long-standing injustice (esp. violation of equal basic liberties: segregation yes, economic inequality no), (2) last resort, (3) coordination with other minorities (1999, 326–9). Critics (incl. Delmas, *A Duty to Resist: When Disobedience Should be Uncivil*, 2018) find this too narrow. [source: SEP-CD §§1, 3]
- **Thoreau** (1817–1862): refused his poll tax for years in protest at slavery, the extermination of Native Americans and the Mexican War; jailed in 1846 by constable Sam Staples (one night: released when "someone interfered, and paid that tax"); lectured on "The Rights and Duties of the Individual in relation to Government" (twice in 1848); published as "Resistance to Civil Government" in Elizabeth Peabody's *Æsthetic Papers* (1849); retitled "Civil Disobedience" in 1866, four years after his death (Sophia Thoreau may have chosen the title — Fedorko 2016; Dawson 2007 argues Thoreau's authority over the edits). For Thoreau "civil" meant relating to citizens and government, not politeness. [sources: SEP-CD §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Disobedience_(essay); SEP Thoreau "a night in jail" https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/thoreau/]
- Thoreau quotes (WP block quotes; 1849 text): "I heartily accept the motto,—'That government is best which governs least;' ... Carried out, it finally amounts to this, which I also believe,—'That government is best which governs not at all;' and when men are prepared for it, that will be the kind of government which they will have." / "Under a government which imprisons any unjustly, the true place for a just man is also a prison." The "governs least" motto is often misattributed to Jefferson (Monticello calls it spurious). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Disobedience_(essay)]
- **Gandhi**: *satyagraha* from Sanskrit *satya* (truth) + *āgraha* (holding firmly); coined in a 1906 *Indian Opinion* competition in South Africa (Maganlal Gandhi proposed "Sadagraha"; Gandhi altered it). Gandhi: "Truth (satya) implies love, and firmness (agraha) engenders and therefore serves as a synonym for force. I thus began to call the Indian movement Satyagraha, that is to say, the Force which is born of Truth and Love or non-violence, and gave up the use of the phrase 'passive resistance'" (*Satyagraha in South Africa*). "Its root meaning is holding on to truth, hence truth-force. I have also called it love-force or soul-force" (Statement to Disorders Inquiry Committee, 5 Jan 1920). He denied deriving civil disobedience from Thoreau: the South African resistance "was well advanced before I got the essay" (letter to P. Kodanda Rao, 10 Sep 1935). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyagraha] (WP's lead also says "as early as 1919" — inconsistent; use the 1906–08 South African origin.)
- In 1907 Gandhi published a synopsis of Thoreau in *Indian Opinion*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Disobedience_(essay)]
- *Hind Swaraj* (1909), written in Gujarati aboard SS *Kildonan Castle*: "You can govern us only so long as we remain the governed" (as quoted in WP; check translation). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hind_Swaraj_or_Indian_Home_Rule]
- Salt March: 12 March – 6 April 1930, Gandhi and 78 satyagrahis to Dandi, 24 days, breaking the Salt Act on 6 April. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_March]
- Gandhi on fasts: hunger strikes coercive; Satyagrahic fasts persuasive and non-violent. [source: SEP-CD §1.2]
- **King, "Letter from Birmingham Jail"**, dated **16 April 1963**, replying to "A Call for Unity" by eight white Alabama clergymen; begun in the margins of a newspaper. Published as "Letter from Birmingham City Jail"; in *Why We Can't Wait* (1964). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_from_Birmingham_Jail]
- King quotes (WP, citing King 1964 pp. 69–77): "Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere." / "There are two types of laws: just and unjust. I would be the first to advocate obeying just laws. One has not only a legal but a moral responsibility to obey just laws. Conversely, one has a moral responsibility to disobey unjust laws. I would agree with St. Augustine that 'an unjust law is no law at all.'" / "A law is unjust if it is inflicted on a minority that, as a result of being denied the right to vote, had no part in enacting or devising the law." / "I submit that an individual who breaks a law that conscience tells him is unjust, and who willingly accepts the penalty of imprisonment in order to arouse the conscience of the community over its injustice, is in reality expressing the highest respect for law." / "'Wait' has almost always meant 'Never.'" King draws on Aquinas: a law not rooted in "eternal law and natural law" is unjust; one that "uplifts human personality" is just. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_from_Birmingham_Jail]
- "One who breaks an unjust law must do so openly, lovingly, and with a willingness to accept the penalty." — **WS only** (several reprints; an earlier draft added a parenthesis about "the white mothers ... in New Orleans"). [source: WS: https://www.nationalreview.com/corner/an-unjust-law-is-no-law-at-all-mlks-letter-from-a-birmingham-jail/amp/]
- King on coercion: "The purpose of our direct action program is to create a situation so crisis packed that it will inevitably open the door to negotiation" (Letter); "Nonviolent coercion always brings tension to the surface" (King 1968, 137). [source: SEP-CD §1.2]
- King: "I became convinced that noncooperation with evil is as much a moral obligation as is cooperation with good. No other person has been more eloquent and passionate in getting this idea across than Henry David Thoreau." (Autobiography.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Disobedience_(essay)]
- Locating civil disobedience: between legal protest and conscientious refusal / uncivil disobedience / revolution. It presupposes the agent's "general respect for their regime". [source: SEP-CD intro]
- Duty to obey unjust laws: on all the theories above, the obligation is at most prima facie/pro tanto; Raz: never conclusive (SEP-PO §2.3). Rawls: a natural duty to comply with just institutions "applies" to laws within a nearly just constitution, so injustice of a particular law does not by itself license disobedience (SEP-CD §3). Fairness theorists: fair-play obligation does not bar civil disobedience to transform the scheme (SEP-PO §4.2). Gratitude: civil disobedience against unjust laws may benefit the state (SEP-Grat). Hume: rebellion against "a cruel tyranny" permitted (SEP-HumeM). Locke: "appeal to heaven" (Locke notes). Mencius 1B8 (above).

## Debates
- **Is there any general political obligation?** Defenders split among consent, fair play, association, natural duty and hybrids; philosophical anarchists (Wolff a priori; Simmons, Raz, L. Green a posteriori) deny a general duty; none "now commands widespread assent". [source: SEP-PO]
- **Legitimacy vs justification**: Simmons separates them; Rawls ties legitimacy to justifying coercion; Raz to authority; realists (Williams) to a political "basic legitimation demand". [source: SEP-Leg]
- **Was Hobbes's theory really a consent theory?** SEP-PO doubts it ("an obligation to obey anyone who can maintain order"). [source: SEP-PO §1.3]
- **How much Locke differs from Hobbes** is "a matter of scholarly dispute". Simmons vs Pitkin on the role of consent in Locke. [sources: SEP-PO §1.3; SEP-LP §4]
- **Rousseau: liberty or totalitarianism?** — see Rousseau notes §8c; SEP-An notes some read Rousseau close to "a posteriori philosophical anarchism" (Bertram).
- **Fair play vs Nozick/Simmons** (above); **associative vs voluntarist** (Dworkin/Horton vs Simmons/Wellman); **natural duty vs particularity**.
- **Raz vs Darwall** on whether the service conception yields a right to rule; **Hurd** on the rationality of pre-emption. [source: SEP-Auth]
- **Mencius and democracy**: Bai vs Tiwald; El Amine on a distinct political sphere; Mou Zongsan's critique. [sources: SEP-Men; SEP-ChSP]
- **Was "Legalism" totalitarian?** Pines stresses impartial, public *fa* binding even the ruler, against the "people-basher" image. [source: SEP-ChLeg]
- **What counts as civil disobedience?** Rawls/Bedau's narrow, civil, publicity-and-punishment model vs broader accounts (Brownlee, Delmas, Scheuerman: Gandhi and King tolerated coercion); King's "nonviolent coercion". [source: SEP-CD]
- **Crito vs Apology** on obedience (Grote's problem; Kraut vs Penner/Woozley). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crito; SEP-Soc]
- **Who actually coined "satyagraha", and was Gandhi influenced by Thoreau?** Gandhi denied the latter. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyagraha]

## Surprises / commonly believed but contradicted
- The label "press-ganged sailor" is not Hume's; his man is kidnapped while asleep.
- SEP-PO dates "Of the Original Contract" 1752, but SEP-Auth, SEP-Leg and IEP give 1748.
- Thoreau's essay was titled "Resistance to Civil Government" (1849); "Civil Disobedience" is the posthumous 1866 title, and Thoreau's "civil" meant "of citizens", not "polite".
- "That government is best which governs least" is Thoreau quoting a motto, not Jefferson (spurious attribution per Monticello via WP).
- Gandhi explicitly denied taking civil disobedience from Thoreau.
- Pines: *fa* law was meant to bind the ruler too and to be public; the "Legalist = terror" picture is partial.
- Wolff's anarchism does not deny de facto states or require abolishing them; Simmons, its leading a posteriori ally, calls Wolff's a priori version "a failed attempt".
- Raz, the main defender of legitimate authority, denies any existing state has a general duty of obedience.
