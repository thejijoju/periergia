## The Vienna Circle: people, dates, places
- A group of "scientifically trained philosophers and philosophically interested scientists" who met under the "(nominal) leadership" of Moritz Schlick for often weekly discussions during academic terms "in the years from 1924 to 1936" [source: SEP-VC §2.1]. WP-VC: meetings regularly 1924–1936 at the University of Vienna; from winter term 1924 at the Mathematical Institute, Boltzmanngasse 5, by Schlick's personal invitation, after his students Waismann and Feigl suggested a regular "evening circle" [source: WP-VC]. Discussions were on Thursday evenings [source: SEP-VC §2.2].
- Schlick (born Berlin 14 April 1882; doctorate under Max Planck in physics, 1904) was appointed in 1922 to Mach's old chair, Philosophy of the Inductive Sciences, at Vienna [source: SEP-S §1; SEP-VC §2.1; WP-MS]. Hahn supported the appointment [source: WP-VC].
- Pre-history: a "First Vienna Circle" (the label is Haller's) of Philipp Frank, Hans Hahn and Otto Neurath met from 1907 in Viennese coffeehouses to discuss Mach, Poincaré, Duhem and the new logic; it stopped about 1912 when Frank took Einstein's former chair in Prague [source: WP-VC; SEP-VC §3.1].
- Members named in the 1929 manifesto (per SEP-VC): Schlick, the mathematician Hans Hahn, the physicist Philipp Frank, the social scientist Otto Neurath, "his wife, the mathematician Olga Hahn-Neurath", the philosopher Viktor Kraft, the mathematicians Theodor Radakovic and Gustav Bergmann, and, "since 1926", Rudolf Carnap; further members from Schlick's students (Friedrich Waismann, Herbert Feigl, Marcel Natkin) and Hahn's students (Karl Menger, Kurt Gödel) [source: SEP-VC §2.1]. Frank lived in Prague throughout; Carnap moved to Prague in 1931 [source: SEP-VC §2.1].
- Gödel: SEP-VC counts him among the members recruited from Hahn's students; WP-VC lists him in the "inner circle" by regular attendance. Calling him merely an "attendee" understates the sources; safer: "the young logician Kurt Gödel, a regular in the Circle" [source: SEP-VC §2.1; WP-VC]. He announced his incompleteness result in the discussion at the Königsberg conference, September 1930 [source: SEP-VC §2.2; WP Kurt_Gödel].
- Membership is blurred: "Not all of those who ever attended the discussions can be called members" [source: SEP-VC §2.1]. Menger and Kraft later wanted to be known only as sympathisers; Reidemeister and Edgar Zilsel were sympathisers throughout [source: SEP-VC §2.1].
- Other participants not listed in the manifesto included Béla Juhos, Josef Schächter, Walter Hollitscher, Heinrich Neider, **Rose Rand**, Josef Rauscher, **Käthe Steinhardt**, Robert Neumann, Felix Kaufmann and Egon Brunswik; loosely associated were Paul Lazarsfeld and **Marie Jahoda** [source: SEP-VC §2.1]. WP-VC's "periphery" also lists Else Frenkel-Brunswik and the mathematician Olga Taussky-Todd [source: WP-VC].
- Visitors included Carl Gustav Hempel (from Berlin), A. J. Ayer (UK), W. V. O. Quine, Ernest Nagel, Charles Morris (USA), Jørgen Jørgensen, Eino Kaila, Arne Naess (Scandinavia), H. A. Lindemann (Argentina) and **Tscha Hung (Hong Qian) from China** [source: SEP-VC §2.1; SEP-LE §1].
- Popper "was never a member or associate of the Circle, though he studied with Hahn in the 1920s and in the early 1930s discussed its doctrines with Feigl and Carnap" [source: SEP-VC §2.1]. SEP-LE: he "did not however, regularly attend meetings of the Vienna Circle and generally considered himself an outsider" [source: SEP-LE §3].
- Wittgenstein was never a member (see the *Wittgenstein* chapter); from 1927 he met Schlick, Waismann, Carnap and Feigl [source: WP-VC].

## Women of the Circle (where they actually were)
- **Olga Hahn-Neurath** (20 July 1882 – 20 July 1937): mathematician and philosopher, sister of Hans Hahn; blind from 1904 (age 22); in 1911 the third woman ever to graduate in philosophy at Vienna; main mathematical interest Boolean algebra; a regular participant in the Circle's discussions; listed as a member in the manifesto. Married Otto Neurath (WP-OHN: 1912; WP-VC: 1911 — sources disagree). After February 1934 fled via Poland and Denmark to the Netherlands; died in The Hague on her birthday in 1937 [source: WP-OHN; SEP-VC §2.1; SEP-N §1 (Olga was Neurath's second wife, died 1937)].
- **Rose Rand** (born Rozalia Rand, Lemberg/Lviv, 14 June 1903 – Princeton, 28 July 1980): logician; as a PhD candidate a regular participant who "kept records of these discussions", most active 1930–35; doctorate (on Kotarbiński) completed 1937/38 but, as a Jew, barred from her profession; emigrated to London in 1939 with Susan Stebbing's help; worked as a nurse and in a metal factory; Popper helped her get a small grant for Oxford (1950–54); moved to the USA in 1954, living on temporary posts and translating Polish and Russian logicians [source: WP-RR; SEP-VC §2.1].
- **Susan Stebbing** (1885–1943), first woman to hold a philosophy chair in the UK, "played a major role in introducing logical positivism into Britain": met Schlick at the 1930 Oxford congress, invited Carnap to lecture in London in 1934 (published as *Philosophy and Logical Syntax*), sat on the organising committee of the Unity of Science congresses (Paris 1935, Cambridge 1938). She accepted the "logical" and the "empiricism" but rejected the "positivism", i.e. the repudiation of metaphysics; criticised Carnap in "Logical Positivism and Analysis" (British Academy, March 1933), rejecting methodological solipsism on "the best of grounds … namely, that I know it to be false" (1933a: 77). She also spent time and money rescuing refugees [source: SEP-ST §§1, 4; WP Susan_Stebbing].
- **Marie Reidemeister** (later Marie Neurath): Neurath's assistant in Isotype; fled with him to England in 1940; co-edited and co-translated his *Philosophical Papers* (1983) [source: SEP-N §1; WS: https://ebooks.mpdl.mpg.de/ebooks/Record/EB000713591/TOC].

## Public face: Ernst Mach Society, manifesto, journal, congresses
- Verein Ernst Mach (Ernst Mach Society/Association): founding session November 1928; Schlick president, Hahn a vice-president, Neurath and Carnap in its secretariat; originally proposed by the Austrian Freidenkerbund (Free Thinkers' Association); for popular lectures on scientific philosophy [source: SEP-VC §2.2]. Dissolved early in 1934 for political reasons (WP-VC: by the Schuschnigg regime, after the Austrian Civil War) [source: SEP-VC §2.1; WP-VC].
- The name "Vienna Circle" was invented by Neurath [source: SEP-VC §2.2; WP-VC citing Frank 1949].
- Manifesto, 1929: *Wissenschaftliche Weltauffassung. Der Wiener Kreis* (Vienna: Wolf), signed by Carnap, Hahn and Neurath, dedicated to Schlick. Translation of the title: SEP-VC: "The Scientific World Conception: The Vienna Circle"; WP-VC: "The Scientific Conception of the World: The Vienna Circle"; IEP-VC: "A scientific world-view". Literally "scientific world-conception". Full English translation in Stadler and Uebel (eds.) 2012 [source: SEP-VC §2.2 and bibliography; WP-VC; IEP-VC]. Its publication coincided with the "First Conference for the Epistemology of the Exact Sciences", Prague, mid-September 1929, organised with the Berlin Society [source: SEP-VC §2.2].
- Schlick himself "was critical of the manifesto of 1929" and gave his own vision in "The Turning Point in Philosophy" (1930) [source: SEP-VC §2.2].
- *Erkenntnis*: in 1930 the Circle and the Berlin Society took over *Annalen der Philosophie* and restarted it as *Erkenntnis*, co-edited by Carnap and Reichenbach [source: SEP-VC §2.2; IEP-VC; WP Erkenntnis (published under that name 1930–1938; IEP-VC says to 1940)].
- Book series: *Schriften zur wissenschaftlichen Weltauffassung*, ed. Schlick and Frank (1928–1936/37), which published Popper's *Logik der Forschung*; *Einheitswissenschaft* ("Unified Science"), ed. Neurath, 1933–1939 [source: SEP-VC §2.2; IEP-VC; WP-VC].
- Congresses: Königsberg 1930; Prague "Pre-Conference" 1934; International Congresses for the Unity of Science in Paris (1935, 1937), Copenhagen (1936), Cambridge, England (1938), Cambridge, Mass. (1939), "all in the main organized by Neurath"; a last gathering in Chicago, 1941 [source: SEP-VC §2.2].
- *International Encyclopedia of Unified Science*: from 1938, Neurath editor-in-chief, Carnap and Charles Morris co-editors; only 10 monographs by Neurath's death in 1945; wound up in 1970 at 20 monographs as "Foundations of the Unity of Science" — "notably containing Thomas Kuhn's *Structure of Scientific Revolutions*" [source: SEP-VC §2.2]. (Irony worth using: Kuhn's book, often taken as the movement's nemesis, appeared in its own encyclopedia — Kuhn 1962 is listed as vol. II no. 2 [source: SEP-LE bibliography].)
- The Berlin Society for Empirical (from 1931 Scientific) Philosophy: Hans Reichenbach, Kurt Grelling, Walter Dubislav, Kurt Lewin, Richard von Mises, etc.; "the other point of origin of logical empiricism" [source: SEP-VC §2.1; WP Berlin_Circle]. Hempel (born near Berlin 8 Jan 1905, d. 1997) studied with Reichenbach in Berlin [source: SEP-H §1]. Reichenbach (1891–1953) left Berlin in 1933 for Istanbul, and in 1938 went to UCLA [source: SEP-R; SEP-LE §3].

## Dispersal, murder, emigration
- SEP-VC's chronology: 1931 Feigl to the USA (WP-VC says he left Austria in 1930); 1934 Hahn died (24 July 1934, after surgery), Neurath fled to Holland (to the UK in 1940); 1935 Carnap to the USA (Chicago from 1936; SEP-C); 1936 Schlick murdered; 1937 Menger to the USA, Waismann to the UK; 1938 Frank, Kaufmann, Brunswik, Bergmann to the USA, Zilsel and Rand to the UK, Schächter to Palestine; 1940 Gödel to the USA [source: SEP-VC §2.1; WP-VC; WP Hans_Hahn].
- From 1934 "anti-semitism institutionalized and irrationalism increasingly dominating public discourse"; after Schlick's murder "only a small rump" met for two more years; the 1938 Anschluss ended the Circle's activity in Austria [source: SEP-VC §2.1; WP-VC].
- Schlick's murder: 22 June 1936, on the main staircase of the University of Vienna on the way to his lecture, shot (four bullets) by Johann (Hans) Nelböck, a former philosophy student "who had been threatening him for several years" and had been diagnosed paranoid schizophrenic; motives disputed — "inter alia anti-Semitism (although Schlick was not a Jew) and defense of metaphysics", or (Nelböck's other version) jealousy over a student, Sylvia Borowicka. Nelböck was sentenced to 10 years and released on probation after two years, after the Anschluss. The press turned it into a nationalist, anti-Jewish *cause célèbre* [source: SEP-S §11; WP-MS]. (IEP-VC's "killed by a Nazi sympathizer student" is too simple — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Influence in exile: Carnap at Harvard, Chicago and UCLA; Feigl at Iowa and Minnesota; Frank at Harvard; Reichenbach and Hempel; their students Putnam and Salmon; "logical empiricism strongly influenced the post-World War II development of analytic philosophy of science" — whereas Waismann had little influence in the UK and Neurath died marginalised there (Oxford, 22 December 1945) [source: SEP-VC §2.1; SEP-N §1].
- Neurath: head of the Central Planning Office in the Bavarian socialist government in 1919; active 1921–1934 in socialist politics in "Red Vienna" (housing, adult education); founded the Social and Economic Museum of Vienna and the Isotype picture language [source: SEP-N §1].

## Politics: the "left wing" and Schlick
- SEP-VC: over the liberalisation of the verification criterion (c. 1931), "the so-called left wing with Carnap, Hahn, Frank and Neurath first distinguished itself from the 'more conservative wing' around Schlick" (Carnap's own phrases, 1936/37 and 1963) [source: SEP-VC §3.1]. SEP-S puts the protocol-sentence split as left wing (Carnap, Hahn, Neurath) vs Schlick and Waismann [source: SEP-S §8]. SEP-VC also speaks of "the non-formalist left Vienna Circle" (Neurath, Frank) [source: SEP-VC §3.4].
- The "left wing" label in SEP is primarily philosophical (anti-foundationalism, liberalised empiricism, deflationist anti-metaphysics), not only political [source: SEP-VC §§3.1, 3.3, 3.6]. Politically: Neurath and Hahn were socialists [source: WP-VC]; the Circle linked its anti-metaphysics to "public engagement for scientific Enlightenment reason in the ever-darkening political situation"; some members opposed *völkisch* holism [source: SEP-VC §§1, 2.3]. Schlick was a correspondence theorist who "showed little enthusiasm" for the manifesto's constructivism [source: SEP-VC §3.6].

## "Logical positivism" or "logical empiricism"?
- SEP-VC uses "logical empiricism (or logical positivism or neopositivism)" interchangeably [source: SEP-VC intro].
- SEP-LE: no principled boundary can be drawn "along doctrinal or sociological lines (Uebel 2013)"; usually "logical empiricism" is the wider term; the Berlin group never called themselves positivists but used the word of "unnamed Viennese"; "'Positivist' is a term usually applied by opponents"; "The one philosopher who would have unhesitatingly described himself as (having been) a logical positivist was A.J. Ayer" [source: SEP-LE §1]. WP Berlin_Circle: Reichenbach insisted on "logical empiricism" to distinguish his view [source: WP Berlin_Circle].
- Neurath coined the term "logical empiricism" in 1931 ("Physikalismus") [source: WP-VC]. (Single source.)

## Core doctrines (minimal consensus)
- Logic and mathematics analytic/tautological (following Frege–Russell logicism and extending Wittgenstein's view of logical truth); synthetic statements cognitively meaningful iff empirically testable "in some sense"; no synthetic a priori — relativity showed Euclidean geometry, Kant's example, to be false of physical space; metaphysics rejected as neither analytic nor empirical; normative ethics' unconditional norms likewise without cognitive standing; "unified science"; philosophy as second-order "critique of language" (the linguistic turn) [source: SEP-VC §2.3].
- Mach's dictum, accepted early: "where neither confirmation nor refutation is possible, science is not concerned" (Mach 1883) [source: SEP-VC §3.1].
- Metaphysical sentences were not denied all meaning: they could have an "expressive" or emotive surplus, but no cognitive meaning [source: SEP-VC §3.1].

## ARGUMENT 1: the verification principle, its versions and its fate (step by step)
- Origin in the Circle: Wittgenstein discussed "The sense of a proposition is [the method of] its verification" with Schlick and Waismann on 22 December 1929 and 2 January 1930; Waismann's "Theses" presented it as Wittgenstein's view; in the Circle it served "mainly as a demarcation criterion against metaphysics" [source: SEP-VC §3.1].
- Distinguish (SEP-VC): verificationism as a *theory of meaning* (meaning = method of verification) vs as a *criterion of cognitive significance*; "the former entails the latter, but not vice versa" [source: SEP-VC §3.1].
- Reconstruction (from SEP-VC §3.1, SEP-A §3.1, WP-V):
  - P1. Every cognitively meaningful sentence is either analytic (true or false by meaning/logic alone) or empirically verifiable.
  - P2. Metaphysical sentences ("the Absolute…", "the Nothing nothings", God-talk) are neither.
  - C. So they are cognitively meaningless — not false, but pseudo-statements.
- Strong (conclusive) version: Wittgenstein's criterion "required conclusive verifiability" (in principle, not in practice) [source: SEP-VC §3.1].
- Objection 1 — universal laws: "Everybody had noted that Wittgenstein's criterion rendered universally quantified statements meaningless" — so scientific laws would be nonsense [source: SEP-VC §3.1; WP-V]. Replies: Schlick (1931), following Wittgenstein, treated laws as *rules* for forming verifiable singular statements; Hahn (1933) and the left wing weakened the criterion; Carnap, *Testability and Meaning* (1936–37), replaced verification by "mere confirmability" / partial testability [source: SEP-VC §3.1]. ("Liberalization of empiricism" is Carnap's phrase.)
- Objection 1b — existential statements: strong falsification-style criteria count "unicorns exist" in, strong verification counts "all swans are white" out (Popper's complaint) [source: IEP-PS §2].
- Ayer, *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936): all propositions analytic or else strongly or weakly verifiable; strong = truth conclusively ascertainable; weak = an observation statement deducible from the proposition together with auxiliaries, but not from the auxiliaries alone [source: SEP-A §3.1]. Defect: any P conjoined with "if P then O" yields O [source: SEP-A §3.1].
- Ayer's second edition (1946): a statement is *directly* verifiable if an observation statement or if, with other observation statements, it entails an observation statement not derivable from those alone; *indirectly* verifiable if, with other premises, it entails directly verifiable statements not derivable from those premises alone, and those premises "do not include any statement that is not either analytic, or directly verifiable, or capable of being independently established as indirectly verifiable" (LTL 2nd ed., p. 17) [source: SEP-A §3.1].
- Objection 2 — Church's refutation (Alonzo Church, review of LTL, *Journal of Symbolic Logic* 14 (1949): 52–53): take logically independent observation statements O1, O2, O3 and any S whatever. Then (¬O1 & O2) ∨ (O3 & ¬S) is directly verifiable (with O1 it entails O3); S is then indirectly verifiable (with it, S entails O2) — unless O2 follows from the disjunction alone, in which case ¬S is directly verifiable. Either S or ¬S passes, so the criterion admits everything [source: SEP-A §3.1; WP-V].
- Objection 2 generalised (Hempel 1950, 1951): by 1950 Hempel conceded it was "useless to continue to search for an adequate criterion of testability in terms of deductive relationships to observation sentences"; Carnap's translatability criterion, once liberalised for theoretical terms, admits "some set of metaphysical propositions"; Hempel concluded the unit of significance is whole theories, a matter of degree [source: SEP-VC §3.1].
- Objection 3 — self-refutation: the principle is neither analytic nor empirically verifiable, so by its own lights meaningless (pressed e.g. by Putnam 1981) [source: SEP-VC §3.6; WP-V]. Replies: it was offered "not as an empirical claim but as a meta-theoretical proposal for how to delimit empiricist languages" (Carnap 1936/37 §27); Hempel called it "a linguistic proposal"; Ayer later wrote it "is to be regarded, not as an empirical hypothesis, but as a definition"; Reichenbach a stipulation [source: SEP-VC §3.6; WP-V]. SEP-VC concedes some members (Schlick) may have understood it in a way open to the charge [source: SEP-VC §3.6].
- Ayer's later admission: he had been vague whether the criterion was "weak" (demarcating sense from nonsense) or "strong" (method of verification gives the meaning) [source: SEP-A §3.1].
- Verdict: "For most critics … the problem of ruling out metaphysical statements while retaining the terms of high theory remained unsolved"; the criterion disappeared from discussion "since the early 1960s (other than as an example of mistaken positivism)"; "the attempt to show metaphysics strictly meaningless once and for all did not succeed" [source: SEP-VC §§3.1, 3.6].

## Meaning versus metaphysics: Carnap on Heidegger
- Carnap, "Überwindung der Metaphysik durch logische Analyse der Sprache", *Erkenntnis* 2 (1932): 219–241; English "The Elimination of Metaphysics through Logical Analysis of Language", trans. Arthur Pap, in Ayer (ed.), *Logical Positivism* (1959), 60–81 [source: SEP-VC bibliography (spells "Papp"); WS: https://philarchive.org/archive/TEOv1]. Literal title: "Overcoming Metaphysics through Logical Analysis of Language" [source: WP-V].
- Target: Heidegger's "Das Nichts selbst nichtet", from the inaugural lecture *Was ist Metaphysik?* (Freiburg, 24 July 1929) [source: SEP-CT §1; WP What_Is_Metaphysics?]. Renderings: "The Nothing itself nihilates" (SEP-CT, Leitgeb & Carus); "The Nothing itself nothings" (Pap's translation of Carnap, per WS); "Nothingness nothings" (WP-V).
- Carnap's diagnosis: such sentences contain meaningless words ("the absolute", "thing in itself") or are ill-formed in logical syntax though grammatical in natural language; "nothing" is not a name or predicate but expresses negated existential quantification; they at best convey a *Lebensgefühl* (attitude to life), better expressed in art — Carnap cites Nietzsche's *Zarathustra* as a positive example [source: SEP-CT §1; WS (philarchive) for the quantifier point].

## Protocol-sentence debate and Neurath's boat
- Question: the content, form and status of the evidence statements of science [source: SEP-VC §3.3].
- Neurath, "Protokollsätze", *Erkenntnis* 3 (1932): 204–214; "Protocol Statements" in Neurath, *Philosophical Papers 1913–1946* (Reidel 1983), 91–99, edited and translated by Robert S. Cohen and Marie Neurath [source: SEP-N bibliography; SEP-VC bibliography; WS: https://ebooks.mpdl.mpg.de/ebooks/Record/EB000713591/TOC]. An earlier translation, "Protocol Sentences", by George Schick, is in Ayer (ed.), *Logical Positivism* (1959), p. 199 [source: WS (hist-analytic/archive.org listings)].
- Neurath's protocols are physicalist, third-person, with the observer's name, time and place embedded; none is beyond revision [source: SEP-N; WP-V].
- Schlick, "Über das Fundament der Erkenntnis" ("On the Foundation of Knowledge"), *Erkenntnis* 4 (1934): *Konstatierungen* (affirmations) such as "here now blue" are incorrigible "unshakeable points of contact between knowledge and reality", but cannot be written down without losing their meaning; all scientific propositions remain hypotheses. Neurath replied ("Radical Physicalism and the 'Real World'", 1934) that this was metaphysics [source: SEP-S §8]. SEP-VC: Schlick "explicitly defended the idea of foundations", yet conceded all scientific statements fallible, "so his position on foundationalism was by no means the traditional one"; what his foundations amounted to is disputed (Oberdan vs Uebel) [source: SEP-VC §3.3].
- Carnap's *Aufbau* basis was methodologically solipsist ("elementary experiences" and one relation, "recollection of similarity"); in "On Protocol Sentences" (1932) he allowed both phenomenalist and physicalist bases (first quiet use of tolerance) and later abandoned the phenomenal language for practical purposes as "suitable for soliloquy only" (1936/37) [source: SEP-VC §3.3; SEP-C §3].
- Popper's "basic statements" were designed to avoid the protocol-sentence picture: accepted by "a free decision" of the community; science stands on piles driven into a swamp, "not down to any natural or 'given' base" (*LSD* 2002: 94) [source: SEP-P §4].

## Carnap's works (dates)
- Born 18 May 1891, Ronsdorf (now Wuppertal); Frege's student at Jena (1910–1913, two courses); died 14 Sept 1970 (Santa Monica per WP) [source: SEP-C §2.1; WP Rudolf_Carnap; Frege chapter notes].
- Came to Vienna as *Privatdozent* in 1926 (brought by Schlick and Hahn); *Der logische Aufbau der Welt* (1928; trans. Rolf A. George, *The Logical Structure of the World*, 1967); *Scheinprobleme in der Philosophie* (1928) [source: SEP-C; WP-VC; SEP-Q bibliography]. Revisionist reading: the *Aufbau* aimed at structural objectivity, not foundationalism (Friedman 1987, 1992; Richardson 1990, 1998) — though Carnap himself later placed it "very close to foundationalism" and Uebel argues for a foundationalist "aberration" around 1929/30 [source: SEP-VC §3.3].
- *Logische Syntax der Sprache* (Vienna: Springer, 1934; trans. Amethe Smeaton, *The Logical Syntax of Language*, 1937) [source: SEP-Q bibliography].
- Principle of Tolerance (LSS §17): "we are not in the business of setting up prohibitions but of arriving at conventions… In logic there are no morals. Everyone is welcome to set up his logic, i.e., his form of language, as he pleases. If he wants to discuss it with us, though, he needs to state his intentions clearly, and give syntactical specifications rather than philosophical considerations." (wording as quoted in SEP-C §1.3; the Smeaton 1937 text is usually quoted with "it is not our business to set up prohibitions" — not verified, see below) [source: SEP-C §1.3].
- Chicago from 1936 for nearly twenty years; then UCLA [source: SEP-C §5; SEP-VC §2.1].
- "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" (1950): *internal* existence questions (asked within a linguistic framework, e.g. "are there infinitely many numbers?" in ZF set theory — trivially yes) vs *external* questions (about the reality of the framework's entities), which taken literally make no sense and are better read as practical questions of which framework to adopt. It answered critics of his intensional semantics, including Quine, Neurath and Ryle (whose 1949 review of *Meaning and Necessity* was "perhaps the nastiest"). Quine rejected the distinction as resting on analyticity [source: SEP-C §7].

## Ayer and *Language, Truth and Logic*
- Alfred Jules Ayer, born London 29 Oct 1910, died 27 June 1989; Eton, Christ Church Oxford (1929); Ryle sent him to Vienna; attended the Circle January–March 1933 (with Schlick, Waismann, Hahn, Menger, Gödel, occasionally Neurath, and Quine present; Carnap already in Prague); his German was poor: "[o]n the whole I have got very little out of them all" (to Ryle, 19 Feb 1933) [source: SEP-A §1; WP A._J._Ayer].
- *LTL* written at 24, published 1936 (London: Victor Gollancz); second edition 1946 with a new introduction; "a best seller after World War II"; "represents logical positivism to many English speakers" [source: SEP-A intro, §1; SEP-LE §3; WP-LTL].
- Ayer saw himself continuing Locke, Hume and Russell; analytic propositions = tautologies [source: SEP-A intro; WP-LTL].
- Emotivism (briefly; cross-ref *Metaethics: Are There Moral Facts?*): moral utterances are not verifiable and so express feelings rather than state facts; not subjectivism (which would make them truth-apt reports of feelings); some took emotivism as a *reductio* of verificationism; in the 1946 introduction Ayer said his emotivism would survive the demise of his positivism; C. L. Stevenson developed it independently (*Ethics and Language*, 1944) [source: SEP-A §8].

## ARGUMENT 2: Popper's falsificationism (step by step)
- Popper born Vienna 28 July 1902, parents of Jewish origin; briefly Marxist in 1919; worked in Adler's clinics; heard Einstein lecture; trained as cabinetmaker; PhD 1928 under Karl Bühler ("Zur Methodenfrage der Denkpsychologie"); New Zealand (Canterbury) 1937–45; LSE 1946, professor of logic and scientific method 1949; knighted 1965; died 1994 [source: SEP-P §1; IEP-PS §1].
- *Logik der Forschung*: printed 1934 with the imprint 1935, in Schlick and Frank's series; Popper's own English rewrite *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, London: Hutchinson, 1959 [source: WP-LSD; SEP-LE; SEP-IP bibliography; IEP-PS §1].
- "Demarcation problem": the German term *Abgrenzungsproblem* introduced in a short 1933 text — "the request for a criterion to differentiate between 'empirical-scientific' and 'metaphysical' assertions"; in 1935 between "the empirical sciences on the one hand, and mathematics and logic as well as 'metaphysical' systems on the other". Pseudoscience was not mentioned in these texts; only from a 1957 lecture did Popper say he had worked on "science and pseudo-science" since 1919 [source: SEP-PS §4.2].
- The motivating contrast (Popper's later account): Einstein's theory made risky predictions (light deflection, Eddington 1919), whereas Freud's and Adler's theories were "simply non-testable, irrefutable. There was no conceivable human behaviour which could contradict them" (*Conjectures and Refutations* 1963: 37); the drowning-child example (1974: 985) [source: SEP-P §2; IEP-PS §2a].
- Reconstruction (SEP-P §3; SEP-IP §6; SEP-PS §4.3):
  - P1. Scientific laws are universal statements ("All As are X" = "No A is not-X").
  - P2. No finite set of observations can verify a universal statement (Hume's point).
  - P3. One genuine counter-instance can refute it, by modus tollens: "an exception, far from 'proving' a rule, conclusively refutes it."
  - C1. So the mark of science is not verifiability but falsifiability: statements "must be capable of conflicting with possible, or conceivable observations" (Popper 1962, 39).
  - C2. Science proceeds by bold conjectures and attempted refutations; theories that survive are "corroborated", not confirmed or made probable.
- Unlike the positivists, Popper's criterion is *not* a criterion of meaning: metaphysical statements may be "far from meaningless" (1974, 978–979) [source: SEP-PS §4.3; IEP-PS §2].
- Popper on induction: accepts Hume's critique and "goes beyond it in arguing that induction is never actually used in science"; all observation is theory-laden [source: SEP-P §3]. Hume's argument "establishes for good that all our universal laws or theories remain forever guesses, conjectures, [and] hypotheses" (1974, 1019) [source: IEP-PS §2d]. (Consistent with the *Berkeley and Hume* chapter.)
- Basic statements: singular existential ("There is an X at Y"), intersubjectively testable, accepted by convention: "Experiences can motivate a decision … but a basic statement cannot be justified by them—no more than by thumping the table" (*LSD* 2002: 87–88); jury analogy (2002: 92) [source: SEP-P §4].
- Objection A — Duhem–Quine: theories are never tested in isolation; a failed prediction can be blamed on an auxiliary hypothesis [source: IEP-PS §2b]. Reply: Popper allows auxiliary hypotheses that yield new falsifiable predictions (Neptune posited to explain Uranus) but forbids *ad hoc* ones; Marxism "immunized itself against the most blatant refutations" (1974, 985) [source: IEP-PS §2b; SEP-SM §3.3]. Later Popper spoke of degrees of testability (1985) [source: SEP-SM §3.3].
- Objection B — Lakatos: there are no Popperian crucial tests; had Galle not found Neptune, Newtonian physics would not have been abandoned; theories are "tenaciously protected from refutation by a vast 'protective belt' of auxiliary hypotheses" (Lakatos 1978: 4); judge research programmes as progressive or degenerating ("sophisticated methodological falsificationism"). Lakatos: on Popper's criterion "a theory may be scientific even if there is not a shred of evidence in its favour" (1981, 117). Also: "If a theory is falsified, it is proven false; if it is 'falsified' [in Popper's conventionalist sense], it may still be true" (1978: 24) [source: SEP-P §12; SEP-PS §4.5].
- Objection C — Kuhn: Popper characterises "the entire scientific enterprise in terms that apply only to its occasional revolutionary parts"; "it is normal science, in which Sir Karl's sort of testing does not occur, rather than extraordinary science which most nearly distinguishes science from other enterprises"; puzzle-solving is the criterion (astronomy vs astrology) (Kuhn 1974, 802–804). Popper's reply: astrologers solve puzzles too; Kuhn replaces "a rational criterion of science by a sociological one" (1974, 1146–1147) [source: SEP-PS §4.4]. Kuhn's theory-ladenness objection to agreed basic statements [source: IEP-PS §3].
- Objection D — corroboration smuggles induction back: many unrefuted hypotheses remain; why rely on the corroborated one for prediction? (Salmon 1981); if every universal hypothesis has probability zero, corroborated and falsified theories are equally improbable (Urbach 1984); Jeffrey: falsifying conclusively is as hard as verifying [source: SEP-IP §6; IEP-PS §3].
- Objection E — probabilistic hypotheses: a statement assigning a probability strictly between 0 and 1 is logically consistent with any finite set of observations, so strictly unfalsifiable; Popper called this the "problem of decidability"; in practice it is handled by methodological/statistical decision rules (Mayo: they "can indirectly falsify hypotheses by adding a methodological falsification rule") [source: WS: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1202.4792 (Briggs, secondary); WP Falsifiability §Statistical theories]. Thin sourcing: present only briefly.
- Objection F — actual pseudosciences (astrology, psychoanalysis) have been tested and failed, so falsifiability does not mark them off (Culver and Ianna 1988; Grünbaum 1984 on Freud) [source: SEP-PS §4.3; SEP-P §12].
- General replies: falsificationism is a normative methodological proposal, not a description of all practice; Popper never held a falsified theory must be abandoned at once, especially without a rival; David Miller (1998) defends critical rationalism [source: IEP-PS §3].
- Natural selection: Popper called Darwinism "not a testable scientific theory, but a metaphysical research program" (1976, 168), then retracted in a 1977 Darwin College lecture (testable though "difficult to test", 1978, 344) [source: SEP-PS §4.3].
- Popper's claim to have killed logical positivism: SEP-LE: "Later he claimed to have 'killed' logical positivism" [source: SEP-LE §3]. *Unended Quest: An Intellectual Autobiography* (1976; first as "Autobiography of Karl Popper" in Schilpp (ed.) *The Philosophy of Karl Popper*, 1974) has a section titled "Who Killed Logical Positivism?" (TOC p. 98) [source: WP-UQ; WS (archive.org TOC)]. Exact sentence not verified — see below.
- *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (London: Routledge, 1945, 2 vols: "The Spell of Plato"; "The High Tide of Prophecy: Hegel, Marx, and the Aftermath"); written in New Zealand during the war, prompted by the Anschluss [source: WP-OS; SEP-P §1]. Cross-ref: the *Plato* and *German Idealism* chapters already present Popper's charges and their rebuttals (Vlastos; Avineri/Knox; "most commentators now agree that Popper was wrong" about Hegel) — consistent.

## ARGUMENT 3: Quine, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (step by step)
- Quine: born Akron, Ohio, 25 June 1908; Harvard PhD 1932 on *Principia Mathematica*; 1932–33 Sheldon fellowship to Vienna, Warsaw and Prague (with Carnap); Harvard 1936–78; died Boston 25 Dec 2000 [source: SEP-Q §1].
- Presented at the American Philosophical Association (Eastern Division) meeting, December 1950; published *Philosophical Review* 60 (1951): 20–43; revised reprint in *From a Logical Point of View* (1953), 20–46 [source: IEP-QA §2; SEP-Q bibliography]. Morton White's "The Analytic and the Synthetic: An Untenable Dualism" made a related case first; Quine had doubts since 1933 and in 1934 gave admiring lectures on Carnap [source: IEP-QA §§1–2].
- The two dogmas: (i) belief in a fundamental cleavage between analytic truths (true by meaning, independently of fact) and synthetic truths; (ii) reductionism, "the belief that each meaningful statement is equivalent to some logical construct upon terms which refer to immediate experience" — in subtler form, that "each statement, taken in isolation from its fellows, can admit of confirmation or infirmation at all" [source: SEP-AS §3.4; SEP-D (quoting 1953, 20, 41)].
- Circle of synonymy and analyticity (IEP-QA §2): (1) Analytic = "true by virtue of meanings"; but meanings are obscure ("meaning is what essence becomes when it is divorced from the object of reference and wedded to the word"). (2) Logical truths are fine; the problem is the second class, e.g. "No bachelor is married", which becomes a logical truth only by putting synonyms for synonyms — so analyticity needs synonymy. (3) Definition? Except for explicit conventional abbreviation, "definition rests on synonymy rather than explaining it". (4) Interchangeability *salva veritate*? Only in a language with "necessarily", but that adverb presupposes we "have already made satisfactory sense of 'analytic'". (5) Carnap's semantical rules for artificial languages? They either use "analytic" undefined or pick an arbitrary class; "we might just stop tugging at our bootstraps altogether". (6) Conclusion: the distinction has not been drawn; it is "an unempirical dogma of empiricists, a metaphysical article of faith" (that last phrase NOT VERIFIED here — see below).
- Holism (against dogma ii): "our statements about the external world face the tribunal of sense experience not individually but only as a corporate body" (SEP-AS and SEP-D print a comma after "individually", citing the 1953 reprint p. 41; the exact punctuation of the 1951 text could not be checked) [source: SEP-AS §3.4; SEP-D]. Quine's 1953 footnote credits Duhem [source: SEP-D].
- The fabric/field passage: "The totality of our so-called knowledge or beliefs, from the most casual matters of geography and history to the profoundest laws of atomic physics or even of pure mathematics and logic, is a man-made fabric which impinges on experience only along the edges. Or, to change the figure, total science is like a field of force whose boundary conditions are experience. A conflict with experience at the periphery occasions readjustments in the interior of the field." (1951, 42–3) [source: SEP-U §2].
- "The unit of empirical significance is the whole of science" (1951, 42) [source: SEP-U §2].
- "Any statement can be held true come what may, if we make drastic enough adjustments elsewhere in the system" (1953, 43); and conversely "no statement is immune to revision" (1951, 43) — even logic (Quine mentions revising the law of excluded middle to simplify quantum mechanics — standard, but not checked here) [source: SEP-D; SEP-Q §3.3].
- Last line: Carnap and Lewis take a pragmatic stand on choosing language forms but their pragmatism stops at the analytic–synthetic boundary; "In repudiating such a boundary I espouse a more thorough pragmatism" (1951, 46) [source: SEP-Q §3.1; pragmatism chapter notes].
- "Web of belief": the phrase is Quine's later metaphor (Quine and Ullian, *The Web of Belief*, 1970) applied retrospectively; "Two Dogmas" itself speaks of a "man-made fabric" and "field of force" [source: SEP-CF bibliography; SEP-U; SEP-AS §3.6.1].
- Quine's positive explanation of apparent apriority: logic and arithmetic lie near the centre of the web, involved in almost all our theory, so their rejection is unimaginable — hence they seem necessary and a priori [source: SEP-Q §3.2].
- SEP-Q's reading: the deepest disagreement is that Quine rejects Carnap's Principle of Tolerance — choice of language and choice of theory are on the same epistemological footing [source: SEP-Q §§3.1, 3.3].
- Objection 1 — Grice and Strawson, "In Defense of a Dogma", *Philosophical Review* 65 (1956): 141–58: so widely shared and projectible a distinction (people agree on indefinitely many new cases) is unlikely to have no basis; Quine's standard of clarity would make discourse about meaning — even understanding — impossible [source: SEP-AS §3.5; SEP-Q §3.3]. Reply (SEP-Q): scepticism about meanings is not scepticism about meaningfulness; Quine's standard is naturalistic explanation [source: SEP-Q §3.3].
- Objection 2 — Putnam ("The Analytic and the Synthetic", SEP dates it 1965 [1975]): there are trivially analytic "one-criterion" words (bachelor) — but such analyticities "cut no philosophical ice…bake no philosophical bread and wash no philosophical windows"; Putnam's case studies ("straight line", "kinetic energy" revised after Einstein) actually support Quine's point that framework principles get revised; Putnam (1978) also said Quine's universal-revisability claim needs modification [source: SEP-AS §§3.3, 3.5, 3.6.2, 5; SEP-VC §3.2]. (Many sources date Putnam's paper 1962 — Minnesota Studies; not checked.)
- Objection 3 — Carnap's reply: he was explicating analyticity for constructed formal languages, not natural language, where precision makes the circularity complaint irrelevant ("Meaning Postulates" 1952; "Meaning and Synonymy in Natural Languages" 1955; replies in Schilpp 1963); his analyticity is framework-relative, so universal revisability "misses its mark"; Quine retorted that meaning postulates are identified only by appearing on a list headed "meaning postulates" [source: SEP-VC §3.2; SEP-C §7].
- Current assessment (a named debate): for decades it was "widely agreed that Quine had 'won'", and "Two Dogmas" became "perhaps the best-known and most widely cited paper in all analytic philosophy"; since the Carnap revival of the 1980s–90s the mainstream view (Creath) is that "Quine at least partially misunderstood Carnap and that the two philosophers were largely talking past each other"; Quine rightly hit the *Aufbau*'s radical reductionism, which Carnap had already given up [source: SEP-C §7; SEP-VC §3.2 (Stein 1992: their programmes were different)].
- Quine's later concession: a sentence is analytic for a speaker "if he learned the truth of the sentence by learning to use one or more of its words" (1991, 270); "I recognize the notion of analyticity in its obvious and useful but epistemologically insignificant applications" (1991, 271) [source: SEP-Q §3.3].
- Naturalised epistemology: "Epistemology Naturalized" (in *Ontological Relativity and Other Essays*, 1969): "Why not settle for psychology?" (1969b: 75); "Epistemology, or something like it, simply falls into place as a chapter of psychology and hence of natural science" (1969b: 82); objection (Jaegwon Kim 1988): this abandons normativity — "a purely descriptive, causal-nomological science of human cognition" [source: SEP-NE §§2, 3.1].
- Indeterminacy of translation, *Word and Object* (MIT Press, 1960), ch. 2: a field linguist hearing a native say "Gavagai" when a rabbit passes cannot settle by any behavioural evidence whether it means "rabbit", "undetached rabbit part", "rabbit stage" or "rabbithood"; Quine distinguishes indeterminacy of reference ("inscrutability") from holophrastic indeterminacy; it is not scepticism about successful communication, judged "by smoothness of conversation" (1990a, 43) [source: SEP-Q §6.2; WP-WO]. *Word and Object* popularised Neurath's boat [source: WP-NB].

## Legacy and decline
- Passmore, "Logical Positivism", in P. Edwards (ed.), *The Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (1967), vol. 5, 52–57: "Logical positivism, then, is dead, or as dead as a philosophical movement ever becomes." (p. 57). Creath: Passmore meant specific doctrines, read through Ayer, and conceded that "the spirit which inspired the Vienna circle" persisted — "It still does" [source: SEP-LE §5].
- Reasons for decline (SEP-VC): failure to formulate the criterion; "increasing opposition to the distinctions between analytic and synthetic statements and observational and theoretical terms"; Carnap's approach came to seem "too formalist in execution and too deflationary in ambition"; Kuhn's socio-historical approach superseded Popper's in many eyes [source: SEP-VC §3.1; SEP-P §1]. By 1960 many former participants defined themselves against it [source: SEP-LE §1].
- Lasting legacy: philosophy of science as a discipline ("In the US nearly all philosophers of science can trace their academic lineages to Reichenbach"); Carnap's shaping of Bayesian probability (Zabell); the revival of metaphysics after 1967, though not of the kind they combated; Carnapian explication and "conceptual engineering" (Creath, Chalmers, Haslanger) [source: SEP-LE §5]. Carnap's ideas "are presently enjoying a major revival" [source: SEP-C intro].
- Revisionist scholarship: "more than four decades" of history of philosophy of science (Friedman 1999 *Reconsidering Logical Positivism*; Richardson 1998; Uebel 1992, 2007; Stadler; Carus 2007) has "unearthed much previously neglected variety and depth"; the charge of foundationalism against the Circle as a whole "constitutes a total misunderstanding … if it does not bespeak wilfull ignorance" (Uebel) [source: SEP-VC intro, §§1, 3.3]. Friedman, *A Parting of the Ways: Carnap, Cassirer, and Heidegger* (2000) [source: SEP-VC bibliography].
- Hempel's ravens paradox (briefly; Hempel 1937, 1945): by Nicod's criterion a black raven confirms "All ravens are black"; a non-black non-raven (white shoe, green apple) confirms the logically equivalent "All non-black things are non-ravens"; equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by the same evidence; so a white shoe confirms "All ravens are black" — Goodman's "prospects for indoor ornithology" (1955, 71) [source: SEP-CF §1.2; SEP-H §3.1].
- Global reach: logical empiricism had centres "in England, France, Scandinavia, at several universities in the U.S., and even China" [source: SEP-LE §1]. Tscha Hung (Hong Qian, 1909–1992) wrote his doctorate under Schlick (1934) on causation in modern physics, attended the Thursday meetings, returned to China in 1937 and taught the Circle's philosophy at Tsinghua and later Peking University until his death in 1992 [source: WS: https://philarchive.org/archive/JIATVC (Yi Jiang, "The Vienna Circle in China: The Story of Tscha Hung"); SEP-VC §2.1]. (Single secondary source for the dates.)

## Debates
- **Was the Vienna Circle foundationalist and reductionist?** Standard textbook picture (phenomenalist *Aufbau*, incorrigible protocols) vs revisionists (Friedman, Richardson: *Aufbau* about structural objectivity; Uebel: the left wing pioneered anti-foundationalism, only "a vaguely foundationalist faction around Schlick"). Uebel also sees a brief foundationalist "aberration" in Carnap c. 1929/30 [source: SEP-VC §3.3].
- **Is the verification principle self-refuting?** Putnam: yes; Carnap/Hempel/Ayer: it is a proposal, explication or definition, not a factual claim; SEP-VC leaves open whether Schlick's version escapes [source: SEP-VC §3.6; WP-V].
- **Did Quine refute Carnap?** Long-dominant view: Quine "won"; revisionists (Creath, Stein, Ricketts, Richardson, Carus): they were "largely talking past each other"; SEP-Q (Hylton & Kemp): the real disagreement is over the Principle of Tolerance [source: SEP-C §7; SEP-VC §3.2; SEP-Q §3].
- **Logical positivism vs logical empiricism**: labels interchangeable for most (Uebel, Creath); Reichenbach insisted on "logical empiricism"; only Ayer embraced "logical positivist" [source: SEP-LE §1; WP Berlin_Circle].
- **How far was the Circle inspired by Wittgenstein's *Tractatus*?** (see *Wittgenstein* notes) — SEP-VC warns against overestimation; verificationism entered the Circle via Wittgenstein's 1929–30 conversations, though Carnap had a weaker criterion already in 1928 [source: SEP-VC §3.1].
- **Popper's demarcation**: falsifiability vs Kuhn's puzzle-solving vs Lakatos's progressive research programmes vs multicriterial approaches; whether it really separates pseudoscience (astrology was tested and refuted) [source: SEP-PS §§4–5].
- **Naturalised epistemology**: replacement naturalism (Quine) vs normativity objection (Kim) vs moderate naturalism (Goldman) [source: SEP-NE].
- **Schlick's murder: politics or madness?** Paranoia, ideology (anti-metaphysics, anti-Semitism), personal jealousy — sources keep it open [source: SEP-S §11; WP-MS].

## Consistency with published Periergia texts
- *History of Philosophy* overview: "meaningful statements are either analytic or empirically verifiable; metaphysics is literally nonsense — … the verification principle failed its own test; Quine … dissolved the analytic/synthetic distinction and holism replaced foundations". Tension: (a) "failed its own test" is presented as settled, whereas SEP-VC treats the self-refutation charge as disputed (the criterion as a proposal); the weightier failure was technical (Church, Hempel). (b) "dissolved" overstates current scholarship (Creath: Quine and Carnap talked past each other; Quine later accepted a limited analyticity). (c) "holism replaced foundations" ignores that Neurath and the left Circle were already anti-foundationalist (Neurath's boat, 1913/1932). Not factual errors so much as simplifications the chapter should nuance.
- *Wittgenstein* (this branch): Circle read the *Tractatus* line by line; from 1927 Schlick and others met him; never a member; Grayling vs SEP-VC on influence; Carnap "should have so completely misunderstood the last sentences" — consistent with these notes. The chapter's "from 1927 Moritz Schlick and others met Wittgenstein" matches WP-VC.
- *Frege, Russell and the Birth of Analytic Philosophy* (this branch): Carnap was Frege's student (1910–13); Beaney's claim that the linguistic turn was taken properly by the *Tractatus* and Carnap in the early 1930s — consistent with SEP-VC's "the Vienna Circle took the so-called linguistic turn".
- *Kant*: non-Euclidean geometry objection associated with Russell and Carnap; commentators (Friedman, Shabel) oppose it — consistent with SEP-VC §2.3 (relativity showed Euclidean geometry false of physical space). The chapter may note the Circle's rejection of the synthetic a priori was partly driven by relativity.
- *Berkeley and Hume*: "Popper accepted that the problem is insoluble but denied that science uses induction … Critics reply that his 'corroboration' smuggles inductive support back in" — consistent with SEP-IP §6 and SEP-P §3.
- *American Pragmatism*: "Two Dogmas" ends "In repudiating such a boundary I espouse a more thorough pragmatism"; C. I. Lewis taught Quine — consistent (SEP-Q §3 notes Lewis's influence "perhaps underestimated"). Peirce's maxim "often compared with the logical positivists' verification principle" — consistent.
- *Plato*, *German Idealism*, *Nineteenth-century overview*, *Enlightenment and Rousseau*: Popper's *Open Society* readings and their critics — consistent; no conflict.
- *Classical Indian Schools*: Arnold compares Kumārila with Popper on falsification — a possible cross-reference, no conflict.
- *What Is Philosophy?*: "Quine saw it as continuous with the empirical sciences" — consistent with SEP-Q intro.
- No philosophy-of-science branch chapter (Section I) is written yet.
