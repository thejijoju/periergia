## 1. Life

- Born 5 April 1588 (Old Style) at Westport, now part of Malmesbury, Wiltshire; Malmesbury is about 30 miles east of Bristol. Died 4 December 1679 at Hardwick Hall, a Cavendish house, "still associated after seventy years" with the family. [sources: SEP-H §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- IEP-HM: born "the year the Spanish Armada made its ill-fated attempt to invade England"; lived "to the exceptional age of 91". [source: IEP-HM §1]
- Premature birth / "twins" story: WP says he was born prematurely when his mother heard of the coming Armada and that Hobbes "later reported that 'my mother gave birth to twins: myself and fear'", citing the *Vita carmine expressa* (Molesworth, *Opera Latina* vol. I, p. 86). The source is Hobbes's **Latin verse autobiography**, *Vita carmine expressa*, composed 1672–73 and first published 1679 (National Archives catalogue description of the Chatsworth manuscript, via WS). An early English verse translation (1680, EEBO A44004, via WS) renders it "Did bring forth Twins at once, both Me, and Fear." Carlo Ginzburg's lecture also identifies the source as the Latin verse autobiography (WS). So: the famous line is genuine in substance, but it is Hobbes's Latin, written c. 1672 in his eighties, and "fear and I were born twins" is a paraphrase, not his words. The Latin wording itself was **not** read (see NOT VERIFIED). [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes; WS: https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/r/575fdd7d-d72c-413e-9563-ed0393fa8105 ; https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/A44004.0001.001/1:2?rgn=div1]
- Father, also Thomas, was "a somewhat disreputable local clergyman" who left Malmesbury in 1604 after a fight in a churchyard with another clergyman, Richard Jeane (Aubrey: "Hobs stroke him and was forced to fly for it"). Aubrey's anecdote of the vicar crying out "Trafells is troumps" (clubs are trumps) in his sleep at church after playing cards all Saturday night. Very little is known of his mother (WP: not even her name). [sources: SEP-H §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- His studies were paid for by his uncle Francis Hobbes, a glover. [source: SEP-H §1]
- Oxford: Magdalen Hall (predecessor of Hertford College). SEP: left Malmesbury "in 1602 or 1603"; WP: went up "between 1601 and 1602". SEP: graduated from Oxford February 1608. WP (citing Malcolm's ODNB article, 2004): completed his BA "by incorporation at St John's College, Cambridge, in 1608". Spelling: SEP writes "Magdalene Hall", WP "Magdalen Hall". Safe wording: "studied at Magdalen Hall, Oxford, from about 1602, graduating BA in 1608". [sources: SEP-H §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- 1608: became tutor to William Cavendish (1590–1628), later 2nd Earl of Devonshire, beginning a connection with the family that lasted most of his life. The job gave him books and intellectual contacts. [source: SEP-H §1; WP]
- Grand tour with the young Cavendish, 1610–15 (Venice: met Fulgenzio Micanzio, associate of Paolo Sarpi). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- Around 1620 he worked for a time as secretary to Francis Bacon (WP: amanuensis, translating some *Essays* into Latin). [sources: SEP-H §1; WP]
- Thucydides: translation of the *History of the Peloponnesian War*, the first English translation made directly from the Greek (WP). SEP and IEP date its publication 1629; WP says "in 1628". Catalogue records (via WS) give the title *Eight Bookes of the Peloponnesian Warre*, entered in the Stationers' Register on 18 March 1628 and printed with a 1629 title page for Henry Seile. (The 18 March 1628 entry probably falls in 1629 by modern reckoning, since the English legal year then began on 25 March. That reconciliation is the researcher's inference, not stated by a source.) Use "published 1629". Hobbes called Thucydides "the most politic historiographer that ever writ" (WP). [sources: SEP-H §1; IEP-HM §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes; WS: https://collections.salisburycathedral.org.uk/006189059]
- Euclid anecdote (Aubrey): Hobbes became fascinated with geometry "at the age of forty" after looking at a copy of Euclid's *Elements*, not believing a proposition, and tracing its demonstration back through the propositions on which it depended (SEP-H paraphrase). Aubrey's wording (via WS from the Clark edition of *Brief Lives*): he was forty before he looked on geometry; in a gentleman's library Euclid's *Elements* lay open at "47 El. libri I" (Book I, proposition 47); "'By G—,' sayd he, 'this is impossible!'". The source is Aubrey (anecdote), not Hobbes. [sources: SEP-H §4; WS: https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/47787/pg47787-images.html (Aubrey, *Brief Lives*, Clark ed., Gutenberg 47787)]
- 1628: the 2nd Earl died of plague (June 1628) and his widow dismissed Hobbes. 1629–30: tutor to Gervase Clifton, mostly in Paris. Then tutor to William Cavendish, 3rd Earl of Devonshire (the son of his first pupil). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- Mid-1630s continental tour: met Marin Mersenne in Paris (Hobbes's own autobiographies mention this). The Galileo meeting: WP says he "visited Galileo Galilei in Florence while he was under house arrest ... in 1636". SEP: the claim rests on **Aubrey** ("When he was at Florence ... he contracted a friendship with the famous Galileo Galilei"), and "curiously Hobbes's autobiographical writings do not mention this". Present the Galileo visit as reported by Aubrey, c. 1636. [sources: SEP-H §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- Mersenne circle: Mersenne was "in good terms with Hobbes", published one of his optical treatises and Sorbière's French translation of *De Cive*. [source: SEP-Mer]
- Back in England 1637. *The Elements of Law, Natural and Politic* circulated in manuscript in 1640 (by the end of the Short Parliament), dedicated to William Cavendish (later Duke of Newcastle). It was the first work following his systematic order from mind and language up to politics. A pirated printing appeared in 1650 in two parts, *Human Nature* and *De Corpore Politico*. [sources: SEP-H §1; SEP-M §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- Exile: left England late in 1640 (WP: November 1640, when the Long Parliament met, fearing he was in disfavour because of the treatise) and stayed in Paris until 1651. SEP: he was associated with the royalists "and might also have had reason to fear punishment because of his defence of absolute sovereignty". [sources: SEP-H §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- In Paris: Mersenne's circle; Pierre Gassendi "seems to have been a particular friend". [source: SEP-H §1]
- *De Cive* (1642) was his first published book of political philosophy, finished November 1641 (WP), in three parts "Liberty", "Empire" and "Religion". It was conceived as the third part of the *Elements of Philosophy* (*De Corpore* 1655, *De Homine* 1658, *De Cive*). Enlarged edition printed by Elzevir in Amsterdam (printing began 1646 per WP; the WP *Bellum* article dates the revised edition 1647). English translation *Philosophicall Rudiments concerning Government and Society* 1651; whether Hobbes made it is disputed (Warrender for, Malcolm against). [sources: SEP-H §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellum_omnium_contra_omnes]
- 1647–48: mathematics tutor to Charles, Prince of Wales (the future Charles II) in Paris. IEP-HM: "math tutor to the future King Charles II". 1647: near-fatal illness lasting six months. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes; IEP-HM §1]
- *Leviathan* was written late in the exile and published in London in 1651 (WP: mid-1651). Its title-page engraving was by Abraham Bosse, made after discussion with Hobbes: a crowned giant made of over 300 small figures, holding sword and crozier, under the Latin of Job 41:24 (Vulgate numbering) "Non est potestas Super Terram quae Comparetur ei". [sources: SEP-H §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leviathan_(Hobbes_book)]
- After *Leviathan*'s "secularist spirit" angered Anglicans and French Catholics and cut his ties with the exiled royal court, he returned to London "in winter 1651" (WP, following the 1911 *Britannica*), submitted to the Council of State and lived privately. SEP: "not returning until 1651". The return is sometimes dated early 1652 (winter 1651/52); "returned to England at the end of 1651" is safe. [sources: SEP-H §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- *De Corpore* 1655 (logic, language, method, metaphysics, mathematics, physics); *De Homine* 1658 (mostly optics/physiology). IEP-HM dates *De Homine* 1657. Use 1658, as SEP and WP do. [sources: SEP-H §1; IEP-HM §1; WP]
- Restoration (1660): Charles II granted him a pension of £100; "Hobbism" became a byword. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- 1666: the House of Commons brought in a bill against atheism and profaneness. On 17 October 1666 the committee was empowered to receive information about books tending to atheism, "in particular ... the book of Mr. Hobbes called the *Leviathan*". Hobbes burned some papers. He studied the law of heresy and argued (Appendix to the 1668 Latin *Leviathan*) that after the High Commission's abolition no court of heresy remained and that only opposing the Nicene Creed could be heresy. Afterwards he could not get a licence to publish on human conduct in England. IEP-HM: religious figures "made moves in Parliament that apparently led Hobbes to burn some of his papers". [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes; IEP-HM §1]
- Latin *Leviathan*, Amsterdam 1668, with significant changes on the Trinity and the nature of God. [source: SEP-H §1]
- *Behemoth* (history of the civil wars / the Long Parliament) was written in the late 1660s (WP: 1668). Charles II refused permission to publish it (Malcolm, *Correspondence*). Pirated editions appeared in the 1670s, and William Crooke's authorised edition followed in 1682. SEP-M says "published 1679" (presumably a pirated printing), IEP-HM "published 1682", SEP-H "published posthumously". Safe: "written c. 1668, published posthumously (authorised edition 1682)". [sources: SEP-H §1; SEP-M §1; IEP-HM §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behemoth_(Hobbes_book)]
- 1670s: verse translations of the *Odyssey* and *Iliad*. [source: SEP-H §1]
- Last years at the Cavendish house of Chatsworth with the 1st Duke of Devonshire. Many manuscripts were found there. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]

## 2. Controversies (Wallis, Bramhall, Boyle, Descartes)

- Wallis: *De Corpore* (1655) contained an erroneous squaring of the circle. John Wallis (with Seth Ward) attacked it, and the quarrel ran "nearly a quarter of a century". Hobbes never admitted the error. Titles include Hobbes's *Stigmai, or Marks of the Absurd Geometry, Rural Language, Scottish Church Politics, and Barbarisms of John Wallis* (1657). The standard study is Jesseph, *Squaring the Circle* (1999). SEP: in mathematics Hobbes "is best remembered for his repeated unsuccessful attempts to square the circle". [sources: SEP-H intro, §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- Bramhall (free will): Bishop John Bramhall, an Arminian, had debated Hobbes (in Paris) and wrote down his views. Hobbes's reply was printed without his consent with "an extravagantly laudatory epistle" (*Of Liberty and Necessity*, 1654). Bramhall printed the whole exchange in 1655 (*A Defence of the True Liberty of Human Actions*). Hobbes answered with *The Questions concerning Liberty, Necessity and Chance* (1656). Bramhall's *Castigations* (1658) had the appendix *The Catching of Leviathan the Great Whale*. Hobbes's *Answer* (written 1668, published 1682) calls God a "corporeal spirit". [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes; SEP-H §1, §5; SEP-M §1]
- Bramhall called *Leviathan* a "Rebell's Catechism" (because of the subject's retained rights). [source: SEP-M §9]
- Hobbes on liberty (classical compatibilism): freedom is finding "no stop, in doing what he has the will, desire, or inclination to doe" (Leviathan ch. 21; SEP-Comp cites the Flathman & Johnston Norton edition, p. 108). He is the paradigm "classical compatibilist" alongside Hume. [source: SEP-Comp §2.1]
- Boyle: debates about the experimental physics of the Royal Society (Shapin & Schaffer). [source: SEP-H §1]
- Clarendon thought Hobbes supported Cromwell, while anti-royalists thought him a royalist. [source: SEP-H §5]

## 3. Hobbes and Descartes (overlap with the sibling *Descartes* chapter)

- 1640: Hobbes sent Mersenne comments on Descartes's *Discourse* and *Optics*. Descartes replied via Mersenne, and Hobbes answered. 1641: Hobbes's objections were published with the *Meditations* as the **Third Set of Objections**, with Descartes's replies. (WP: a further exchange on other works "succeeded only in ending all correspondence between the two".) [sources: SEP-H §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- Other objectors were Caterus, Mersenne's theologians, Gassendi (Fifth), Arnauld, Bourdin. Mersenne gathered the sets. [sources: SEP-D; SEP-Mer]
- Mutual attitudes: "a curious mixture of respect and dismissal". Descartes thought Hobbes better at moral philosophy than elsewhere, but with "wicked" views there (letter of 1643). He worried Hobbes was "aiming to make his reputation at my expense, and by devious means" (1641 letter to Mersenne, in Malcolm's *Correspondence*). They are said to have met once, in 1648, and "did not get along well" (Martinich 1999). Aubrey: they "mutually respected one another", but Hobbes thought Descartes should have stuck to geometry. [source: SEP-H §1]
- Third Objections, verified points:
  1. **Thinking thing may be a body.** Hobbes argued that from "I am thinking" it does not follow that the thinker is immaterial: a thing that thinks may be "something corporeal", since we seem to conceive subjects of acts only as corporeal, as Descartes's own wax example shows. Descartes replied that Hobbes confused the thinking thing with its acts or powers and called the inference without reason. [source: WS summary of the Objections and Replies text (earlymoderntexts.com and a UC course PDF) and of Duncan, "Hobbes's Materialism in the Early 1640s" — weaker, check against CSM II before quoting]
  2. **Dreaming.** Hobbes objected to the waking-continuity test in Meditation VI that (a) it implies someone "can know he is awake without knowledge of the true God", against Descartes's own thesis, and (b) one could dream the continuity too. Descartes: "an atheist can infer that he is awake on the basis of memory of his past life" but cannot know the criterion is sufficient without knowing he was created by a non-deceiving God (AT 7:195–6). "A dreamer cannot really connect his dreams with the ideas of past events, though he may dream that he does" (AT 7:196, CSM 2:137). [source: SEP-DE]
  3. **Ideas are images; we have no idea of God (or the soul).** Hobbes held ideas to be images and denied we have an idea of God. Descartes replied that by "idea" he means "whatever is immediately perceived by the mind" (AT VII 181; CSM II 127). [sources: SEP-DI; WS summary for "no idea of the soul/God"]
  4. **Innate ideas.** Descartes to Hobbes: "when we say that an idea is innate in us, we do not mean that it is always there before us ... We simply mean that we have within ourselves the faculty of summoning up the idea" (AT VII 189; CSM II 132). [source: SEP-DI]
  5. **Natural light.** Descartes: "As everyone knows, a 'light in the intellect' means transparent clarity of cognition" (AT 7:192, CSM 2:135). [source: SEP-DE]
  6. **Nominalism and reasoning.** In his reply Descartes objected that Hobbes's view of reasoning as linking names could not explain how the same truths are expressed in different languages (CSM 2.126). Leibniz and Henry More made similar complaints. [source: SEP-H §2.3]
- Hobbes's "gross errors" argument in *De Corpore* 3.4 targets inferences from being able to *consider* thinking without body to thought existing without body, perhaps aimed at Meditation VI. SEP notes Descartes's argument relies on *clear and distinct* conceivability, a distinction "Hobbes's argument seems blind to". [source: SEP-H §3]
- Translations above: CSM = Cottingham, Stoothoff, Murdoch, *Philosophical Writings of Descartes* vol. 2 (Cambridge 1984), as used by SEP.

## 4. Materialism, mechanism, mind

- By the time of *Leviathan* and *De Corpore* Hobbes held that human beings, minds included, are entirely material. "This was not a popular or widely-held position at the time." [source: SEP-H §3]
- Sensation is a chain of motions: the object presses on the sense organ, the motion continues to the "brain and heart", and there it causes "a resistance, or counter-pressure, or endeavour of the heart to deliver itself; which endeavour, because outward, seemeth to be some matter without. And this seeming, or fancy, is that which men call sense" (Leviathan 1.4, Curley). [source: SEP-H §2.1]
- Imagination is "decaying sense": "after the object is removed, or the eye shut, we still retain an image of the thing seen, though more obscure than when we see it" (Leviathan 2.2). Imagination and memory are "the same thing" under two names (2.3). Understanding is imagination raised by words or other signs; "a dog by custom will understand the call ... of its master" (2.10). There is no further Cartesian immaterial faculty. [source: SEP-H §2.1]
- Sources of his empiricism: all ideas derive from sensation. Hume's impressions/ideas resemble Hobbes's "decaying sense", and SEP suggests one "might well speak of Hobbes, not Locke, as the first of the British empiricists". [source: SEP-H §2.1, §6]
- Leviathan's Introduction compares the human body and the state to machines (automata): "why may we not say, that all Automata (Engines that move themselves by springs and wheeles as doth a watch) have an artificiall life?" (quoted, 1651 spelling, in WP; the WP block quote is truncated, so check the end against an edition). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leviathan_(Hobbes_book)]
- "Insignificant speech": "incorporeal substance" is like "incorporeal body", a contradiction. Names "whose significations are contradictory and inconsistent; as this name, an incorporeal body, or (which is all one) an incorporeal substance". SEP cites this as "Hobbes 1655, 4.20–1", but the passage reads like *Leviathan* ch. 4 §§20–21. Probably a citation slip in SEP; cite it as *Leviathan* ch. 4 only after checking an edition. [source: SEP-H §3]
- SEP's judgement: Hobbes "clearly was a materialist about the natural world, but the explicit arguments he offers for the view seem rather weak". Perhaps he trusted the progress of science (he admired William Harvey). [source: SEP-H §3]
- Even spirits are bodies: "the Universe is All, that which is no part of it, is Nothing ... Nor does it follow from hence, that Spirits are nothing: for they have dimensions, and are therefore really Bodies" (Leviathan xlvi.15, 1651 spelling as quoted in SEP-MC). [source: SEP-MC]
- God corporeal? In the *Elements of Law* (11.2–3) Hobbes gives a cosmological argument: we know only that God, "first cause of all causes", exists, since "we can have no conception or image of the Deity". Attributes like "spirit" or "omniscient" signify reverence, not description. The same three views recur in *Leviathan* (11.25, 12.6–9). Later (Appendix to the 1668 Latin *Leviathan*; *Answer to Bishop Bramhall*) he calls God a "corporeal spirit", meaning at least that God is extended, a body mixed through the world. SEP calls this "strange", but "on the surface an odd theism rather than atheism". [source: SEP-H §5]
- Margaret Cavendish attended "Cavendish Circle" meetings (1640s) with Hobbes, Descartes, Mersenne, Gassendi and Digby. She developed her own non-Hobbesian materialism, in which thought is a basic feature of bodies and not motion in the brain, and refused to make God a body. Politically she sided largely with Hobbes. [sources: SEP-MC; SEP-H §6]
- Gassendi's view of pre-social humans driven by fear and desire, and his mechanism, resemble Hobbes's (SEP-Gas calls him "his acquaintance"). [source: SEP-Gas]

## 5. Language, nominalism, reason as reckoning

- Nominalism: "the only universal things are names" (Elements 5.6–7; Leviathan 4.6–8; De Corpore 2.9). "Tree" names each tree, and there is no universal tree or universal idea. Common names apply because of similarities, but how we draw the boundaries of kinds involves an arbitrary element of decision. [source: SEP-H §2.3]
- Leviathan wording (1651 spelling, via WP): "there being nothing in the world Universall but Names". "Words are wise mens counters, they do but reckon by them: but they are the mony of fooles, that value them by the authority of an Aristotle ... or any other Doctor whatsoever" (WP gives this with ellipses; Leviathan ch. 4). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leviathan_(Hobbes_book)]
- Leibniz's verdict: Hobbes is "a super-nominalist", making truth depend on definitions and hence on human will ("Preface to an Edition of Nizolius", 1670, Loemker trans.). [source: SEP-H §2.3]
- Reasoning as computation: "By reasoning I understand computation. And to compute is to collect the sum of many things added together at the same time, or to know the remainder when one thing has been taken from another. To reason therefore is the same as to add or to subtract" (De Corpore 1.2, Martinich trans.). A syllogism is "an addition of three names, just as a proposition is of two" (4.6). [source: SEP-H §2.4]
- Leviathan ch. 5 parallel: "REASON, in this sense, is nothing but Reckoning (that is, Adding and Subtracting) of the Consequences of generall names agreed upon, for the marking and signifying of our thoughts" (1651 text has "Substracting"). [source: WS summary of the Toronto Pressbooks 1651 edition, LitCharts, SparkNotes — weaker]
- Leibniz endorsed reasoning-as-computation ("Of the Art of Combination", 1666). Haugeland's line that Hobbes was "prophetically launching Artificial Intelligence" (1985) is judged by SEP "at least rather over-dramatic". [source: SEP-H §2.4]
- Contempt for scholastics: in *Behemoth* Peter Lombard and Duns Scotus wrote like "two of the most egregious blockheads in the world". [source: SEP-H §3]

## 6. Psychology and motivation

- Assumptions (SEP-M §3): people are roughly equal in powers, generally "shun death", have limited benevolence and partiality, are sensitive to slights, use "good" and "bad" for their own preferences, and are curious and anxious about the future, which inclines them to religion. [source: SEP-M §3]
- Appetite and aversion: "'good' and 'bad' have no meaning other than to describe our appetites and aversions" (IEP-SC paraphrase of Hobbes's subjectivism). Reason acts as "Scouts, and Spies, to range abroad, and find the way to the things Desired" (Leviathan ch. 8, quoted IEP-SC). [source: IEP-SC §2a]
- "So that in the first place, I put for a generall inclination of all mankind, a perpetuall and restlesse desire of Power after power, that ceaseth onely in Death" (Leviathan ch. 11). Only the phrase from "a perpetuall" to "Death" is verified (1651 spelling, via WS; modernised: "a perpetual and restless desire of power after power, that ceaseth only in death"). The opening words are from memory and are not verified. [source: WS summary (Hanover College excerpt; Stephen Hicks PDF)]
- "No man giveth but with intention of good to himself; because gift is voluntary; and of all voluntary acts, the object is to every man his own good" (Leviathan, quoted by IEP-PE and IEP-Egoism as the classic egoist text). [source: IEP-PE §2]

## 7. The state-of-nature argument (step by step)

Reconstruction (from SEP-M §§3–5 and IEP-HM §4; Leviathan ch. 13–14):
- P1 (Equality). People are roughly equal in body and mind, so anyone can kill anyone, by stealth or by joining with others. Hence no natural authority. IEP paraphrase: "Even the strongest must sleep; even the weakest might persuade others to help him kill another" (Leviathan xiii.1–2). That line is **IEP's paraphrase**, not Hobbes's wording. [source: IEP-HM §4; SEP-M §3, §11]
- P2 (Right of nature). Each person has a liberty right to preserve herself and to judge what that requires. Since almost anything might be judged necessary, this becomes in practice "a right to all things", "even to one another's body" (Leviathan xiv.4). [sources: SEP-M §3; IEP-HM §4]
- P3 (Scarcity and uncertainty). People compete for scarce goods and cannot know others' intentions.
- P4 (Three causes of quarrel). "First, Competition; Secondly, Diffidence; Thirdly, Glory". The first "maketh men invade for Gain; the second, for Safety; and the third, for Reputation" (Leviathan ch. 13, 1651 spelling via Wikisource as summarised by WS). Diffidence (distrust) makes anticipation, striking first, rational. The vainglorious minority provokes pre-emptive defence. [sources: IEP-HM §4 (Leviathan xiii.3–9); SEP-M §4; WS: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Leviathan_(1651)/Chapter_13]
- C1. "Hereby it is manifest, that during the time men live without a common Power to keep them all in awe, they are in that condition which is called Warre; and such a warre, as is of every man, against every man" (Leviathan ch. 13; 1651 spelling via WS of Wikisource. Modernised wording identical in WP *Bellum omnium* and *State of nature* articles, the latter citing Curley). [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellum_omnium_contra_omnes; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_nature; WS]
- War is not only fighting but a known "Will to contend by Battle" (WP *Bellum*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellum_omnium_contra_omnes]
- C2. In such a condition, "no place for Industry ... no Arts; no Letters; no Society; and which is worst of all, continuall feare, and danger of violent death; And the life of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short" (see Quotations for full text and spelling). [sources: SEP-M §3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- C3. In the state of nature "nothing can be Unjust. The notions of Right and Wrong, Justice and Injustice have there no place" (Leviathan xiii.13, modernised in IEP-HM). [source: IEP-HM §4]
- "bellum omnium contra omnes": the Latin phrase is from ***De Cive*** (Preface, and ch. 1 §12 as "bellum omnium in omnes"), **not** *Leviathan*. *Leviathan* says "warre of every one against every one", "war ... of every man against every man". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellum_omnium_contra_omnes]
- De Cive viii.1: men considered "as if they had just emerged from the earth like mushrooms and grown up without any obligation to each other" (IEP, Tuck/Silverthorne trans. recommended). [source: IEP-HM §4]
- **Objection 1: historically false.** Hobbes's own replies: (a) all sovereigns are in this state towards one another (hence his status as the representative "realist" in international relations); (b) many peoples were formerly in it, and "the savage people in many places of America" still are (Leviathan XIII); (c) "most significantly", it is recognisable to anyone whose state has collapsed into civil war. The state of nature is an abstraction, but something like it is a "perpetually present possibility". [source: SEP-M §5]
- Hobbes's appeal to experience (reply to doubters, ch. 13): the reader is to consider that "when taking a journey, he armes himselfe, and seeks to go well accompanied; when going to sleep, he locks his dores" (1651 spelling via WS of Saylor/Hanover excerpts; the continuation is not verified). [source: WS]
- **Objection 2: families, not isolated individuals (feminist and other critics).** Children owe obedience to parents in exchange for nurture, so the primitive units include families, plus bonds of affection, clan and religion. SEP's reply: competition, diffidence and glory may be just as disastrous among small groups. [source: SEP-M §5]
- **Objection 3 (Rousseau): Hobbes describes socialised people with the laws removed.** Rousseau, in the *Discourse on Inequality*, held that previous accounts "such as Hobbes'" took civilised humans and simply removed laws and government. The faculties Hobbes assumes (property, foresight, seeing all others as threats) develop historically. Natural man is "isolated, timid, peaceful, mute", and self-preservation is not the only principle (there is also pity). [source: IEP-R] Rousseau's own conjectural history nonetheless has a "Hobbesian insecurity" stage leading to a state that entrenches inequality. [source: SEP-R]
- **Objection 4: pessimistic picture of human nature.** SEP-M: although "many readers have criticized Hobbes's state of nature as unduly pessimistic, he constructs it from a number of individually plausible empirical and normative assumptions". IEP-HM's central theme is that the problem "cannot be avoided simply by taking a less selfish view of human nature", because the core problem is private **judgment** (disagreement), not selfishness. [sources: SEP-M §3; IEP-HM intro, §4]
- Open interpretive question (SEP-M §5): is the war caused by (i) short-sighted irrationality, (ii) fully rational agents trapped in a prisoner's-dilemma-like structure, or (iii) irrational passions (pride, spite) of some or all? "A matter of continuing debate". Abizadeh (2011) proposes a "disagreement theory" of the causes of war. [source: SEP-M §5 and bibliography]

## 8. Laws of nature, covenant, the Foole (Leviathan ch. 14–15)

- Hobbes lists **nineteen** laws of nature; the first two are politically crucial, and the third (keep covenants) grounds justice. [source: IEP-HM §5a]
- First law (Leviathan xiv.4, modernised in IEP-HM): "Every man ought to endeavor peace, as far as he has hope of obtaining it, and when he cannot obtain it, that he may seek and use all helps and advantages of war." [source: IEP-HM §5a]
- Second law (xiv.5, IEP-HM; WP *State of nature* identical): "That a man be willing, when others are so too, as far-forth as for peace and defense of himself he shall think it necessary, to lay down this right to all things, and be contented with so much liberty against other men, as he would allow other men against himself." [sources: IEP-HM §5a; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_nature]
- The sum of the laws is "not to treat others in ways we would not have them treat us". They are "precepts", "conclusions" or "theorems" of reason, "eternal and immutable". They bind in conscience always but in action only when it is safe. [source: SEP-M §6]
- Hobbes: "the true doctrine of the Lawes of Nature is the true Morall philosophie". [source: SEP-M intro]
- Inalienable right: "a man cannot lay down the right of resisting them that assault him by force to take away his life, because he cannot be understood to aim thereby at any good to himself" (Leviathan ch. 14, modernised; via WS of the Hanover excerpt). Hence a covenant not to defend oneself is void. [source: WS]
- The Foole (Leviathan ch. 15, Curley 15.5): "The Foole hath sayd in his heart, there is no such thing as Justice" (echoing the Psalm's "The fool hath said in his heart, there is no God"; opening phrase verified only via WS). The Foole says it is sometimes "most reasonable" to break a covenant when the other party has already performed. Hobbes's reply: breaking covenant tends to self-destruction, because others will not admit a known covenant-breaker into future covenants or defensive alliances. [sources: SEP-GE §(Reconciliation Project); WS]
- Game-theoretic restatements: the Foole's "opportunity game" (one party has performed and the other can perform or exploit). Sugden (1986) and Skyrms (1998) read Hobbes's reply to the Foole as a proto-folk-theorem argument about indefinitely repeated games. Kavka (1984) named the "Reconciliation Project" (showing that morality and prudence coincide). [source: SEP-GE]
- Kinch Hoekstra, "Nothing to Declare: Hobbes and the Advocate of Injustice" (1999), and Springborg, "Hobbes's Fool the Insipiens, and the Tyrant-King" (2011) are on the Foole. [source: SEP-M bibliography]
- Covenants without the sword: "Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words" is a famous line but was **not** verified this session (see NOT VERIFIED). IEP-HM notes the problem of making the first move. If the state of nature is so distrustful, why would anyone disarm first? Hobbes concedes "there is scarce a commonwealth in the world whose beginnings can in conscience be justified" (Review and Conclusion, 8). [source: IEP-HM §5a]

## 9. Authorization and the sovereign (Leviathan ch. 16–18, 20–21, 29)

- Sovereignty by **institution** (mutual covenant to obey a common authority) and by **acquisition** (covenanting with a conqueror for protection). They are equally legitimate, and the motive in both is fear. "Political obligation ends when protection ceases." [source: SEP-M §7]
- Authorization: in *Leviathan* each covenants to "own and authorize" all the sovereign's public acts. This differs from the *Elements* and *De Cive*, where subjects simply lay down rights in favour of the sovereign. [source: SEP-M §9]
- Authorization formula (ch. 17): "I Authorise and give up my Right of Governing my selfe, to this Man, or to this Assembly of men, on this condition, that thou give up thy Right to him, and Authorise all his Actions in like manner" (1651 spelling). The **first clause** is verified via WS (sources disagree over "this"/"his Assembly"). The "on this condition..." continuation is from memory and is **not verified**. [source: WS]
- "This is the Generation of that great LEVIATHAN, or rather (to speake more reverently) of that Mortall God, to which wee owe under the Immortall God, our peace and defence" (ch. 17; via WS of the Toronto 1651-spelling edition. The final words "our peace and defence" are not verified). [source: WS]
- Because subjects are authors of the sovereign's acts: "he that complaineth of injury from his sovereign complaineth that whereof he himself is the author, and therefore ought not to accuse any man but himself, no nor himself of injury because to do injury to one's self is impossible" (modernised, as quoted in WP; WP gives no chapter — ch. 18 is the researcher's assumption, check). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- Responsibility: actions done in obedience "without the inward approbation, are the actions of the sovereign, and not of the subject" (Leviathan xlii.106) — SEP-M reads this as reassuring Christians that they will not be damned for obeying. [source: SEP-M §9]
- Absolutism: sovereignty must be neither divided nor limited. Legislation, adjudication, enforcement, taxation, war-making and control of doctrine are interlocked. Limiting the sovereign starts a regress of "authorities", or relocates absolute sovereignty. [source: SEP-M §8] Quote: "For what is it to divide the power of a commonwealth, but to dissolve it; for powers divided mutually destroy each other" (Leviathan xxix.12; cf. De Cive xii.5). [source: IEP-HM §5]
- Three forms (monarchy, aristocracy, democracy) differ "not in the difference of power, but in the difference of convenience or aptitude to produce the peace and security of the people". Hobbes prefers monarchy on "mild pragmatic grounds" (public and private interest most closely united; fixed succession). He concedes that which form is best is the one thing not proven with certainty. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leviathan_(Hobbes_book) (quotation, modernised; WP gives no chapter number — ch. 19 assumed, check); SEP-M §8; IEP-HM §5]
- Retained rights ("true liberties of subjects", ch. 21): a right of self-defence even against the sovereign, and "seemingly broad resistance rights" where family or even honour are at stake. These are debated as the "Achilles' heel" of the theory. Freedom "depends on the silence of the laws" (xxi.18). [sources: SEP-M §9; IEP-HM §5]
- The Latin *Leviathan* (1668) coined "auctoritas non veritas facit legem" (authority, not truth, makes law), ch. 26. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leviathan_(Hobbes_book) (note to lead)]
- Legal positivism's roots lie in Hobbes and Hume, but even Hobbes "required that law actually be able to keep the peace, failing which we owe it nothing". [source: SEP-LegP]
- Political position (IEP-SC): Hobbes rejected both divine right (Filmer) and shared King-and-Parliament sovereignty. He was "both radical and conservative": authority rests on the interests of equals, yet must be absolute. [source: IEP-SC §2a]

## 10. Religion (Leviathan Parts III–IV; atheism; Erastianism)

- Religion takes up "roughly half" of *Leviathan*. SEP-M advises readers to read Parts Three and Four, not just the excerpted Parts One and Two. [sources: SEP-M §1, §10; IEP-HM]
- Part III "Of a Christian Commonwealth": no one can infallibly know another's claimed revelation, so religious power is subordinate to civil power (the sovereign is head of the church). This is the Erastian conclusion, though WP does not use the word "Erastian", so label it as standard terminology. The sword-and-crozier frontispiece shows the union of the two powers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leviathan_(Hobbes_book)]
- Dreams and revelation: "To say he [God] hath spoken to him in a dream is no more than to say that he dreamed God spake to him" (Leviathan 32.6, Curley). [source: SEP-H §5]
- Miracles defined (37.7) as "a work of God (besides his operation by the way of nature, ordained in the creation), done for the making manifest to his elect the mission of an extraordinary minister for their salvation". His scepticism about miracle reports is weaker than Hume's but similar. [source: SEP-H §5]
- Critical reading of Scripture (e.g. the Pentateuch as a later compilation): "Hobbes and Spinoza often get a good deal of credit for developing this approach". [source: SEP-H §5]
- Part IV "Of the Kingdom of Darkness": not hell, but the darkness of ignorance. The kingdom of darkness is "nothing else but a confederacy of deceivers that, to obtain dominion over men in this present world, endeavour, by dark and erroneous doctrines, to extinguish in them the light" (WP, modernised, truncated). Chiefly aimed at the Roman Church (purgatory, ghosts and fictitious miracles, ch. 46). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leviathan_(Hobbes_book)]
- Reasons critics gave for calling him an atheist (Mintz 1962): the universe is body, God is body, the Pentateuch is a compilation, the Trinity is Moses, Jesus and the Apostles, few miracles after biblical times, witchcraft a myth, the Church must submit to Leviathan. Many contemporaries still thought he believed in God. Cudworth (1678) thought he denied God. [source: SEP-H §5]
- Hobbes: "atheism, impiety, and the like are words of the greatest defamation possible" (Answer to Bramhall, Molesworth p. 282). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- Bishop Brian Duppa on *Leviathan*: "many things said so well that I could embrace him for it, and many things so wildly and unchristianly, that I could scarce have so much charity for him, as to think he was ever Christian". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leviathan_(Hobbes_book)]

## 11. Women, the family, race

- Hobbes "was one of the earliest western philosophers to count women as persons when devising a social contract". Women are naturally equal (equally vulnerable and capable of dominating) and equally free, so their consent is needed. This contrasts with Filmer's patriarchalism. He rejected Salic law, held that women can be sovereigns, and said authority is "neither male nor female". [source: SEP-M §11]
- Maternal dominion: "in the state of nature, dominion over children naturally belongs to the mother", with the Amazons adduced as evidence. Leviathan ch. 20 (1651 spelling, via WS of the Toronto edition): "For in the condition of meer Nature, where there are no Matrimoniall lawes, it cannot be known who is the Father"; "If there be no Contract, the Dominion is in the Mother". The Amazon example: they contracted with neighbouring men so that daughters remained with them, so "the dominion of the females was in the mother" (WS, modernised edition). [sources: SEP-M §11; WS]
- Tension: in civil society Hobbes speaks of families as "fathers", "servants" and "children", justifying this by saying fathers, not mothers, founded societies. There is a lively debate between egalitarian/feminist readings and readings stressing women's ultimate exclusion (Pateman 1989; Slomp 1994; Sreedhar 2012, 2019 on the Amazons; Hirschmann & Wright eds. 2012). [source: SEP-M §11 and bibliography]
- Race: he invokes the "savages" of America, yet denies innate differences: without leisure, "what do we differ from the wildest of the Indians?" [source: SEP-M §11]

## 12. Influence and reception (cross-refs)

- SEP-M: Hobbes is "now widely regarded as one of a handful of truly great political philosophers". Leviathan rivals Plato, Aristotle, Locke, Rousseau, Kant and Rawls. His method (justification by what rational, free, equal persons would agree to) was hugely influential, while his absolutist conclusions "have served mostly as a foil". [source: SEP-M intro]
- IEP-SC: social contract theory "is given its first full exposition and defense by Thomas Hobbes"; then Locke and Rousseau, then Rawls and Gauthier. [source: IEP-SC intro]
- Locke: "near descendant". In the *Second Treatise* he held the state of nature preferable to an absolute sovereign's arbitrary power. Locke never names Hobbes, and the *First Treatise* attacks Filmer. SEP-H: Locke's anti-nativism, attention to language, openness to materialism and scepticism about revelation are Hobbesian themes (Rogers 1988). Straussians read Locke as close to Hobbes. [sources: SEP-M §3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_nature; SEP-H §6; SEP-LP]
- Spinoza: Dutch Hobbesians (Velthuysen, the De la Court brothers) were conduits. Spinoza to Jarig Jelles (Epistle 50): "As far as Politics is concerned, the difference you ask about, between Hobbes and me, is this: I always preserve natural Right unimpaired, and I maintain that in each State the Supreme Magistrate has no more right over its subjects than it has greater power over them". The translation is not named in the passage; SEP-Sp's bibliography uses Curley's *Collected Works*. [source: SEP-Sp §1.2, §2.1]
- Rousseau: Hobbes is among his "most immediate influences". The *Social Contract* rejects Hobbes's representative sovereignty ("to hand over one's general right of ruling oneself ... is a form of slavery") but shares his view that individuals keep no natural rights against the community. Rousseau's "natural freedom" recalls Hobbes's right of nature. [source: SEP-R]
- Leibniz engaged most with Hobbes among canonical philosophers from Descartes to Kant (nominalism, conatus, reasoning as computation). He wrote him letters in the 1670s, probably unanswered. Hume modelled the *Treatise* on the *Elements of Law* (Russell). [source: SEP-H §6]
- Cross-tradition hook: Periergia's published *Confucius, Mencius and Xunzi* chapter compares Xunzi's account of ritual to "Hobbes's state of nature" (superficially). Martinich (2011) compares the sovereign in Hanfeizi and Hobbes; Schwitzgebel (2007) compares Mencius, Xunzi, Hobbes and Rousseau. [sources: supabase/content/philosophy--history-of-philosophy--ancient-beginnings--confucianism.sql; SEP-M bibliography]

## Debates
- **Was Hobbes a psychological egoist?** The traditional reading says yes: IEP-SC presents his theory of motivation as "Psychological Egoism", and IEP-PE says Hobbes and Bentham are "arguably" the only major figures to endorse it explicitly, citing "No man giveth but with intention of good to himself". Against it: Bernard Gert, "Hobbes and Psychological Egoism", *Journal of the History of Ideas* 28 (1967) 503–520, argued Hobbes is not a psychological egoist, defined as holding that men never act to benefit others or from belief in rightness (except perhaps in the *Elements*), while granting that Hobbes thought most actions of most people self-interested (details via WS; the bibliographic data is from SEP-M). SEP-M: "The formerly dominant view that Hobbes espoused psychological egoism as the foundation of his moral theory is currently widely rejected." IEP-HM: "most scholars now accept that Hobbes himself had a much more complex view of human motivation". [sources: IEP-SC §2a; IEP-PE §2; SEP-M intro and bibliography; IEP-HM intro; WS]
- **What kind of moral theory?** Most scholars read personal relativism or subjectivism. Others read divine command theory, virtue ethics, rule egoism or projectivism. "Too ambiguous to have garnered any general consensus" (SEP-M). [source: SEP-M intro]
- **Laws of nature: prudential theorems or divine commands (Taylor–Warrender thesis)?** A. E. Taylor ("The Ethical Doctrine of Hobbes", *Philosophy* 1938, reprinted in K. C. Brown ed., *Hobbes Studies*, 1965) and Howard Warrender (*The Political Philosophy of Hobbes: His Theory of Obligation*, 1957) argued that obligation in Hobbes is independent of his psychology and rests on the laws of nature as **God's commands** (Martinich 1992 is in the same family). Critics: the laws would not bind atheists, the textual evidence is thin, and Hobbes calls them "conclusions, or theorems" that are only "improperly" called laws. Rival readings: Watkins (hypothetical imperatives), Nagel 1959, Gauthier (prudential/egoist). SEP-NL (Murphy) nonetheless counts Hobbes a "paradigmatic natural law theorist" who held "that the laws of nature are divine law (Leviathan, xv, ¶41)". [sources: SEP-H §5 (Warrender 1957, Martinich 1992 vs Nagel 1959, Darwall 1994); SEP-M §6; SEP-M bibliography (Brown 1965 contains Taylor, Warrender, Watkins, Plamenatz); SEP-NL; WS (Taylor 1938 title and the critics' points)]
- **Game-theoretic readings.** Barry (1965), Gauthier (1969), Hampton (1986), Kavka (1986) and Taylor (1987; first name not given in the passage read) gave game-theoretic accounts of the war in the state of nature. Vanderschraaf, Moehler (2009: best modelled as an **assurance game**, not a prisoner's dilemma) and Chung (2015) continue them. Hampton and Kavka read the creation of the commonwealth as a Nash bargaining problem. Hampton's dilemma: if war comes from passions, contractors will break the contract; if from prisoner's-dilemma rationality, rational actors will not comply with the contract either. Gauthier's answer is "constrained maximization" (*Morals by Agreement*, 1986), which critics say moralises rationality (Moehler 2020). [sources: SEP-GE; SEP-CT §4; SEP-M bibliography]
- **Atheist or (odd) theist?** Esoteric readings: Curley (1992) points to a circle (miracles are judged by doctrine, prophets by miracles); Jesseph (2002) argues the material God does not add up; Strauss-style reading between the lines. Against: Hobbes said many dangerous things openly, and his corporeal-God view looks sincerely defended. Tuck and Pocock are also cited on his religion. [sources: SEP-H §5; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes]
- **Do retained rights undo absolutism?** If each subject judges whether protection has failed, have we left the state of nature? Bramhall's "Rebell's Catechism". Some modern commentators call it the theory's "Achilles' heel". [source: SEP-M §9]
- **Responsibility under authorization:** if subjects "own" all sovereign acts, are they morally responsible? One resolution denies that Hobbesian authorization carries responsibility (SEP-M names no proponent in the text; see Lloyd 2016 in its bibliography). [source: SEP-M §9]
- **Feminist or patriarchal?** See §11. [source: SEP-M §11]
- **Method: Zabarellan (Aristotelian resolution–composition, via Padua/Galileo) or geometric (analysis/synthesis)?** Possibly both (Hattab 2014; Adams 2019). [source: SEP-H §4]
- **Is the separation of powers a refutation?** IEP-HM argues that Hobbes's arguments for indivisible sovereignty "are not only weak; they are simply refuted by the (relatively) successful distribution of powers in modern liberal societies". This is an IEP author's verdict, to be presented as such. [source: IEP-HM §5–6]
