## 1. Philosophy of technology: background

### The field and the "neutrality thesis"
- Ernst Kapp was the first to use the term "philosophy of technology" (1877). 20th-century critics of technology include Heidegger (1954), Günther Anders (1956), Arnold Gehlen (1957), Marcuse (1964), Habermas (1968), Hans Jonas (1979) and Andrew Feenberg (1991, 1999); also Lewis Mumford (1934), Jacques Ellul (1954), Langdon Winner (1977, 1980, 1983) and Albert Borgmann (1984). Carl Mitcham (*Thinking Through Technology*, 1994) called this "humanities philosophy of technology". [source: SEP-Tech §1]
- SEP: these founding figures were "almost all openly critical of technology"; later scholars (Don Ihde's "postphenomenology", 1979, 1993; Peter-Paul Verbeek 2000/2005, technology as "mediation") adopted a more neutral view. [source: SEP-Tech §1]
- The "instrumental view" yields the **neutrality thesis**: technology is a neutral instrument that can be put to good or bad use by its users; "During the twentieth century, this neutrality thesis has been criticized." The SEP says the view that technology is value-laden "seems the majority position". [source: SEP-Tech §§3.1, 3.2.1]
- Two trends in ethics of technology: away from technological determinism ("technology … develops autonomously and irrevocably") toward seeing development as shaped by human choices. [source: SEP-Tech §3]

### Heidegger, "The Question Concerning Technology"
- Themes first developed in the lecture "Das Ge-stell" (usually "Enframing"), delivered **1 December 1949 in Bremen**, second of four lectures titled "Insight into What Is" ("Einblick in das, was ist", with "The Thing", "The Danger", "The Turning"). A revised, expanded version, "Die Frage nach der Technik", was delivered **18 November 1953 in Munich** and first published **1954** in *Vorträge und Aufsätze* (Pfullingen: Neske). English: William Lovitt (trans.), *The Question Concerning Technology and Other Essays*, Harper & Row, **1977**, pp. 3–35. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Question_Concerning_Technology ; SEP-Tech bibliography; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestell]
- Heidegger starts from the "instrumental and anthropological definition of technology": "[t]echnology is a means to an end [and] a human activity" — he calls this "correct" but not yet true to technology's essence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Question_Concerning_Technology (quoting Lovitt 1977, pp. 4–5)]
- Contrast: older craft (*poiēsis*, "bringing-forth", the silver chalice example) vs modern technology's "challenging-forth" (*Herausfordern*); examples: the Rhine (hydroelectric plant), the forester ordered by the paper industry. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Question_Concerning_Technology]
- Key terms: *Bestand* ("standing-reserve"; Wrathall prefers "stock") — things show up as replaceable, interchangeable items "on call"; *Gestell* — the gathering of all modes of "setting-upon"; default translation "enframing"; Kisiel proposes "syn-thetic com-posit(ion)ing", Wrathall "inventory". [source: SEP-Heid §5.2.1]
- Heidegger calls the technological mode of revealing "the supreme danger" (GA7: 31 / QCT 26). The danger is not primarily lethal machines but that this way of revealing crowds out every other. [source: SEP-Heid §5.2.2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Question_Concerning_Technology]
- Interpretive debate on what the danger is: Dreyfus — the culture's dedication to "flexibility and efficiency … just for the sake of flexibility and efficiency themselves" drives out marginal practices like friendship ("Friendship becomes networking"); Iain Thomson — "calculative thinking" reducing entities to "intrinsically meaningless resources on standby for optimization"; Pippin — denial of meaningfulness; Wrathall — "optionalization of everything". [source: SEP-Heid §5.2.2]
- Criticism: Feenberg says Heidegger is "unrealistically essentialist" ("one and only one 'essence' of technology") and leads to fatalism (Belu & Feenberg 2010). Defenders (Dreyfus, Thomson, Wrathall): changing background practices shifts our possibilities, so it is not fatalism. [source: SEP-Heid §5.2.2]
- Heidegger "himself denied that his discussion of technology constituted a philosophy of technology"; technology's grip is a symptom of a wrong attitude toward Being "on the rise for almost 25 centuries". [source: SEP-Tech §1]
- Biographical caveat: Heidegger was elected Rector of Freiburg in April 1933, joined the Nazi Party on 1 May 1933, resigned the rectorship April 1934 but remained a party member until the end of the regime. [source: SEP-Heid §1 / §3]

### Winner, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" and the Moses bridges
- Langdon Winner, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?", *Daedalus* 109(1), Winter 1980, pp. 121–136; reprinted in MacKenzie & Wajcman (eds), *The Social Shaping of Technology* (1985; 2nd ed. 1999); adapted in *The Whale and the Reactor* (University of Chicago Press, 1986). [source: SEP-Tech bibliography; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langdon_Winner]
- Two ways artifacts have politics: (1) design or arrangement of a particular artifact "becomes a mechanism for settling the affairs of a community" (Moses bridges are his chief example); (2) "inherently political" technologies that either *require* or are *strongly compatible with* particular social relations (SEP's examples: railroads, nuclear energy needing authoritative management). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langdon_Winner ; SEP-Tech §3.6]
- The Moses claim (from Robert Caro's *The Power Broker*, 1974): Moses built low overpasses on Long Island parkways to keep buses — hence poorer and Black New Yorkers without cars — off the parkways to Jones Beach. Moses's aide Sidney Shapiro is cited by Caro (p. 952) as saying low bridges were used to make it harder for future legislatures to admit commercial vehicles. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Moses]
- **The dispute.** Bernward Joerges, "Do Politics Have Artefacts?", *Social Studies of Science* 29(3) (1999): 411–431, questioned the example; in the same issue Steve Woolgar and Geoff Cooper, "Do Artefacts Have Ambivalence? Moses' Bridges, Winner's Bridges and Other Urban Legends in S&TS", 29(3): 433–449, called the bridge story an "urban legend". Historian Kenneth T. Jackson also denied nefarious intent. Wikipedia: excluding commercial vehicles and using low bridges "were standard on earlier parkways, where they had been instituted for aesthetic reasons", though Moses "appears to have made greater use of low bridges". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Moses ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langdon_Winner ; WS-5 (Joerges bibliographic details) https://ideas.repec.org/a/zbw/espost/71061.html]
- Teaching point: the dispute is over the historical example and intent, not over Winner's general thesis (which the SEP treats as influential). Joerges's detailed argument was NOT read; describe it only as questioning the example's accuracy and the role of intent. [source: WS-5]

### Jonas, *The Imperative of Responsibility*
- Hans Jonas (10 May 1903, Mönchengladbach – 5 Feb 1993); Jewish; Heidegger's student who in 1964 publicly repudiated him over Nazism; his mother died in Auschwitz. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Jonas]
- *Das Prinzip Verantwortung: Versuch einer Ethik für die technologische Zivilisation* (Insel/Suhrkamp, Frankfurt, **1979**); extended English edition *The Imperative of Responsibility: In Search of an Ethics for the Technological Age* (University of Chicago Press, **1984**), trans. Jonas with David Herr. [source: SEP-Tech bibliography; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Jonas]
- SEP: Jonas argued technology requires an ethics with responsibility as the central imperative because (1) technology now lets humanity destroy the earth and itself (atomic weapons) and re-engineer nature and humanity (biotechnology), and (2) modern technology is driven by social and political forces that escape individual control. [source: SEP-Tech §3.2.2]
- Imperative (wording as given by Wikipedia, citing Tirosh-Samuelson & Wiese 2008, p. 135): "Act so that the effects of your action are compatible with the permanence of genuine human life". The common fuller form adds "on earth". **[single source for exact wording]** [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Jonas]
- Jonas's essay "The Heuristics of Fear" (in Kranzberg ed., *Ethics in an Age of Pervasive Technology*, 1980). Commentators treat the "heuristics of fear" as the book's central device and credit the book with laying groundwork for the precautionary principle, though Jonas does not use that term. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Jonas ; WS-16 (weak)]
- Credited with catalysing the environmental movement in Germany. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Jonas]

### Value-sensitive design (VSD)
- Developed by Batya Friedman and Peter Kahn at the University of Washington "starting in the late 1980s and early 1990s"; book: Batya Friedman and David G. Hendry, *Value Sensitive Design: Shaping Technology with Moral Imagination* (MIT Press, 2019). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_sensitive_design ; SEP-Tech bibliography]
- Method: iterative "tripartite" investigations — conceptual (stakeholders, values, value conflicts), empirical, technical; distinguishes direct and indirect stakeholders. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_sensitive_design ; SEP-Tech §3.2]
- Related: "design for values" (van den Hoven, Vermaas & van de Poel 2015), "privacy by design". [source: SEP-Tech §3.2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_sensitive_design]
- Criticisms: Le Dantec et al. (a predetermined list of values may miss values that emerge from cases); Manders-Huits (VSD's definition of values as "what a person or group of people consider important in life" conflates preferences with moral values); Reijers & Gordijn propose virtues instead (after Shannon Vallor). Friedman & Hendry reply that VSD now commits to at least human well-being, justice and dignity. SEP adds that "ethics cannot be guaranteed through design", since use, maintenance and retirement matter too. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_sensitive_design ; SEP-Tech §3.2]

## 2. Privacy and surveillance

### Warren and Brandeis (1890)
- Samuel D. Warren and Louis D. Brandeis, "The Right to Privacy", *Harvard Law Review* 4 (5), 193–220, **15 December 1890**; apparently written mainly by Brandeis; 7,222 words. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Right_to_Privacy_(article)]
- Occasion: "instantaneous photography" and newspaper gossip ("Gossip is no longer the resource of the idle and of the vicious, but has become a trade…"). Prosser's story that a society wedding (Warren's daughter's) triggered it is disputed; the article says it was general society-page coverage. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Right_to_Privacy_(article)]
- Grounded the right in "inviolate personality" and "the right to one's personality" (1890: 205, 207), "the dignity … of the individual"; SEP calls "the right to be let alone" "the first definition of informational privacy". [source: SEP-Priv §§1.2, 3.2]
- The phrase "the right to be let alone" was borrowed from Judge Thomas Cooley's *Treatise on the Law of Torts* (1st ed. 1879; Warren and Brandeis cited the 2nd ed., 1888). [source: WS-19 https://journals.library.brandeis.edu/index.php/blj/article/download/537/159/2003] **[WebSearch only]**
- Influence: Roscoe Pound (1916) said they added "nothing less than … a chapter to our law"; Nimmer: "perhaps the most famous and certainly the most influential law review article ever written". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Right_to_Privacy_(article)]
- Reductionist challenge: Judith Jarvis Thomson (1975) held privacy is a cluster of other rights (property, bodily security); Thomas Scanlon replied directly. [source: SEP-Priv §2.1]
- Feminist critique: MacKinnon saw privacy as shielding domestic abuse; Anita Allen (1988) defends a reconstructed privacy as valuable for women. [source: SEP-Priv §2.4]
- Democratic value: Ruth Gavison (1980) — privacy supports moral autonomy, a precondition of democracy. [source: SEP-Priv §4.4]

### Contextual integrity (Nissenbaum)
- Helen Nissenbaum, "Privacy as Contextual Integrity", *Washington Law Review* 79 (2004): 101–139; book *Privacy in Context: Technology, Policy, and the Integrity of Social Life* (Stanford University Press). Year: Wikipedia's citation gives **2009**; the brief and SEP cite **2010** (2010 is the copyright/edition year commonly cited) — use "2009/2010" or "2010" with caveat. [source: SEP-ITP bibliography; SEP-Priv §3 ("Nissenbaum (2010) defines privacy as the adequate flow of information"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_Nissenbaum]
- Four claims: privacy concerns appropriate flows of information; appropriate flows conform to contextual informational norms; norms have five parameters — **data subject, sender, recipient, information type, transmission principle**; norms evolve and are evaluated by effects on interests, by ethical/political values, and by **how well they serve the context's own ends and purposes** (the most distinctive). Contrasts with privacy-as-control and privacy-as-secrecy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contextual_integrity]
- Example structure: medical information flowing from patient to doctor under confidentiality is appropriate; the same information flowing to an employer or advertiser violates contextual norms though nothing "secret" was taken. Van den Hoven's "informational injustice": information from one sphere (health care) used in another (commerce) changes meaning and may discriminate; linked to Nissenbaum and to Walzer's spheres of justice. [source: SEP-ITP §1.4]
- Other moral reasons for data protection (van den Hoven 2008): prevention of harm; informational inequality; informational injustice and discrimination; encroachment on moral autonomy and human dignity ("chilling effect"). [source: SEP-ITP §1.4]

### Bentham's Panopticon and Foucault
- Jeremy Bentham conceived the panopticon ("all seeing") in letters while visiting his brother Samuel in Krichev (Russian Empire, now Belarus), arriving early 1786; it applied Samuel's ideas on watching workers; drawings by Willey Reveley (1791); *Panopticon, or The Inspection House* (1791). Key idea: inmates "could be seen but never knew when they were watched". Bentham thought the design applicable to factories, asylums, hospitals, schools; the manager himself was to be open to public inspection ("Who guards the guards?"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panopticon]
- Bentham's description (1791): "…hence the sentiment of a sort of invisible omnipresence." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panopticon]
- Michel Foucault, *Surveiller et punir: Naissance de la prison* (Gallimard, **1975**); English *Discipline and Punish: The Birth of the Prison*, trans. Alan Sheridan (Pantheon, **1977**). Opens with the execution of Damiens vs the timetable of Mettray. Discipline produces "docile bodies". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discipline_and_Punish]
- Foucault: the Panopticon "must be understood as a generalizable model of functioning … the diagram of a mechanism of power reduced to its ideal form" (Sheridan trans.). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panopticon]
- **Interpretive caution:** IEP: "Though Foucault is often presented as a theorist of 'panopticism,' this is not the central claim of the book"; the central theme is discipline as a historical form of power (from 17th-century soldiering via prison, factory and schools), and "power-knowledge". [source: IEP-Fou §3a]
- Deleuze's "societies of control" (1992) presents power as more fluid, spread through digital technologies of private corporations. [source: SEP-Tech §3.6]

## 3. Algorithmic bias and fairness

### COMPAS and ProPublica (2016)
- COMPAS = Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions; created 1998 by Northpointe (became **equivant** in January 2017); used in New York, Wisconsin, California, Broward County (Florida) etc. for bail, sentencing and parole. Does not use race as an input. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COMPAS_(software); SEP-AF §1.1]
- Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson et al., "Machine Bias", *ProPublica*, **23 May 2016**; risk scores for more than 7,000 people arrested in Broward County. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COMPAS_(software) ; WS-14]
- ProPublica's key claim (quoted in SEP): "The formula was particularly likely to falsely flag black defendants as future criminals, wrongly labeling them this way at almost twice the rate as white defendants. White defendants were mislabeled as low risk more often than black defendants." [source: SEP-AF §1.1]
- Northpointe's reply: William Dieterich, Christina Mendoza and Tim Brennan, "COMPAS Risk Scales: Demonstrating Accuracy Equity and Predictive Parity" (Northpointe, **8 July 2016**) — scores were equally predictive (calibrated) for Black and white defendants. [source: WS-14 https://www.equivant.com/?p=2091]
- SEP: "a Black person and white person who were each given the same score were equally likely to recidivate" (predictive parity), yet among those who did not reoffend, the Black person was significantly more likely to have been scored high risk (unequal false positive rates). Wikipedia: "Counterintuitively, both statements are true." [source: SEP-AF §1.1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COMPAS_(software)]
- Base rates in the data: recidivism 51% among Black defendants vs 39% among white defendants (Chouldechova). **[WebSearch only]** [source: WS-2]
- *State v. Loomis* (Wisconsin Supreme Court, July 2016): judges may consider COMPAS scores at sentencing, with warnings about the tool's "limitations and cautions". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COMPAS_(software)]
- Dressel and Farid (2018): COMPAS was somewhat more accurate than individual laypeople but less accurate than groups of them; later work found algorithms do better under more realistic conditions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COMPAS_(software)]

### The impossibility results, stated precisely
- Definitions (binary prediction, groups A and B):
  - **Calibration / predictive parity** (Northpointe's measure): among people given the same score (or labelled high-risk), the proportion who actually reoffend is the same in each group. Formally, P(Y=1 | S=s, A=a) = P(Y=1 | S=s, A=b) for every score s; "well-calibrated" adds that this probability equals s. [source: SEP-AF §1.2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairness_(machine_learning)]
  - **Error-rate balance / equalized odds** (ProPublica's measure): the false positive rate (share of people who did *not* reoffend who were labelled high-risk) and the false negative rate (share of those who *did* reoffend who were labelled low-risk) are equal across groups. Kleinberg et al. call these "balance for the negative class" and "balance for the positive class". [source: SEP-AF §1.2; WS-1]
  - Note for the writer: SEP-AF §1.2 defines the false positive rate as erroneous positives "out of the total number of people predicted to have the trait" — that is actually the false discovery rate (1 − positive predictive value). Use the standard definition above (denominator = all actual negatives), which matches Wikipedia's confusion-matrix formulation (separation = equal TN/(TN+FP) and TP/(TP+FN)). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairness_(machine_learning)]
- **Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan**, "Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores", arXiv:1609.05807 (submitted **19 Sept 2016**), published in Proceedings of Innovations in Theoretical Computer Science (ITCS) **2017**. They formalise three conditions — calibration within groups, balance for the positive class, balance for the negative class — and prove that "except in highly constrained special cases, there is no method that can satisfy these three conditions simultaneously"; even approximate satisfaction requires data approximating a special case. Per SEP, the special cases are perfect prediction and equal base rates ("An algorithmic tool cannot satisfy both predictive parity and equalized odds when base rates are different unless the tool is perfectly accurate"). [source: WS-1 https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.05807 ; SEP-AF §1.2]
- **Chouldechova**, "Fair prediction with disparate impact: A study of bias in recidivism prediction instruments", *Big Data* 5(2), **2017** (arXiv 1703.00056, Feb 2017; earlier short version arXiv 1610.07524, Oct 2016). Result: if recidivism prevalence differs between groups, an instrument satisfying predictive parity **cannot** have equal false positive *and* false negative rates across groups; the higher-prevalence group will generally have a higher FPR and lower FNR. She argues unequal error rates can produce disparate impact when high-risk scores bring harsher treatment. [source: WS-2 https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.00056 ; SEP-AF §1.1 (cites "Chouldechova 2016: 5")]
- Name: "impossibility result" / "Kleinberg-Chouldechova impossibility theorem". [source: SEP-AF §1.1]
- Wikipedia general statement: independence, separation and sufficiency cannot all hold "except in specific rhetorical cases"; in particular, separation and sufficiency cannot both hold when the sensitive attribute and the outcome are not independent. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairness_(machine_learning)]
- Philosophical responses: Green & Hu (2018): "[l]abeling a particular incompatibility of statistics as an impossibility of fairness generally is mistaking the map for the territory". Brian Hedden (2021) argues **calibration within groups** is the only necessary statistical condition, since otherwise "the same risk score would have different evidential import for the two groups"; Hu (2025) objects that calibration fails across all intersecting groups unless the tool is perfectly accurate; Benjamin Eva (2022) proposes "base rate tracking"; Hellman notes the "treat like cases alike" maxim supports both sides; the airport-screening example (treating different risks alike can be fairness) after Schauer 2003. Hedden canvasses eleven statistical measures. [source: SEP-AF §§1.1–2.1]
- Sources of machine bias (IEP, after Springer et al. 2018): data bias, computational/algorithmic bias, outcome bias (feedback loops, e.g. predictive policing sending more police where more crime was recorded, after O'Neil 2016). [source: IEP-AI §2c]

## 4. Automation and work

### Luddites
- English textile workers, 1811–1816, beginning in Nottingham and spreading to Yorkshire and Lancashire; named after the legendary "Ned Ludd" (said to have smashed two stocking frames in 1779). Concerns: pay, labour-saving, child labour, conditions, product quality. Suppressed by law and military force, including executions and transportation; a mass trial at York in January 1813. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luddites]
- Luddites "were not opposed to the use of machines per se (many were skilled operators…)"; Malcolm Thomis (1970): attacks "did not imply any necessary hostility to machinery as such"; Eric Hobsbawm ("The Machine Breakers", *Past & Present*, 1952) called machine-wrecking "collective bargaining by riot"; 12,000 troops were deployed, more than Wellington took to Portugal in 1808. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luddites]

### Keynes, "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" (1930)
- Published in *The Nation and Athenaeum*, October 1930 (originally a lecture); reprinted in *Essays in Persuasion*. Wikipedia's Keynes article says "a lecture later published in 1931" — the essay appeared in the *Nation and Athenaeum* in 1930 and in *Essays in Persuasion* in 1931. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes ; WS-4 (Quote Investigator)]
- Keynes popularised "technological unemployment", calling it "only a temporary phase of maladjustment". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_unemployment]
- Predicted that within about a century the "economic problem" might be solved; SEP-EAI: he expected an "age of leisure" around 2030 assuming growth; "we have already reached the level he anticipated for 2030, but we are still working." SEP-Work: Keynes observed productivity gains had not reduced work time, which he attributed to a work ethic that stymies our capacity to enjoy leisure. [source: SEP-EAI §2.9.4; SEP-Work §2]

### Frey and Osborne and the 47% figure
- Carl Benedikt Frey and Michael A. Osborne, "The Future of Employment: How Susceptible Are Jobs to Computerisation?", Oxford Martin School working paper dated **17 September 2013**; journal version *Technological Forecasting and Social Change* 114 (**2017**): 254–280. Estimated computerisation probabilities for **702** occupations; about **47% of total US employment** in the high-risk category. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_unemployment ; WS-18 (REF impact case study)]
- Frey and Osborne later stressed the figure measures technical feasibility, "over some unspecified number of years", not predicted job losses. [source: WS-18 https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/opinion/view/404] **[WebSearch only]**
- Critiques: OECD study (2016, by Arntz, Gregory and Zierahn — authors not verified this session): across 21 OECD countries only about **9%** of jobs were automatable on a task-based approach (6% South Korea, 12% Austria); another study: heterogeneity of tasks within occupations lowered US at-risk occupations from 38% to 9%; Bruce Chapman: studies ignore new jobs. Bruegel (2014) applied the method to the EU and got 54%. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_unemployment]
- Economics (SEP-EAI): automation's effects are "job polarisation" (Goos, Manning & Salomons 2009) — growth at high- and low-skill ends, pressure on mid-skill jobs; farming employed over 60% of the workforce in Europe and North America in 1800, about 5% by 2010; Hinton 2016: "We should stop training radiologists now" — not borne out a decade later. SEP (revised Mar 2026): "Unlike in 2020, an impact of AI systems on the labour market is now clearly visible"; AI investment "ca. 0.5 trillion US$ in 2025"; "for the moment, it appears that the overall impact of AI is that of generating more employment (World Economic Forum 2025), but there are indications that this will change." **[dated to SEP revision of 27 Mar 2026; single source for the investment figure]** [source: SEP-EAI §2.9.4]

### Basic income
- Precursors: Thomas Paine, *Agrarian Justice* (1797); Thomas Spence, *The Rights of Infants* (1797); Milton Friedman's negative income tax (*Capitalism and Freedom*, 1962); Basic Income European Network founded 1986 (later BIEN). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_basic_income]
- Experiments: Finland, 2,000 unemployed people, two years from January 2017 (extension refused April 2018); Stockton, California, 125 residents, 2019–21; GiveDirectly in Kenya (12-year pilot); Ontario pilot from July 2017. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_basic_income]
- Philosophical debate: the **reciprocity / fair-play objection** — those who benefit from social cooperation ought to contribute by working (Becker 1980; Stuart White 2003); replies: conditions for just reciprocity do not obtain for some groups (Tommie Shelby 2012 on the "ghetto poor"), or an obligation to work breaches state neutrality among conceptions of the good — Philippe van Parijs (1991, the article known as "Why Surfers Should Be Fed") and Levine (1995). Van Parijs and Vanderborght (2017) defend basic income as immune to unemployment. [source: SEP-Work §§3–4]

### Meaningful work and a post-work future
- Goods of work beyond wages: social goods and community (Gheaus & Herzog 2016); Estlund: the workplace is "the single most important site of cooperative interaction and sociability among adult citizens outside the family"; Aristotelian self-realisation; Marx — work objectifies human powers, alienation under capitalism (1844). [source: SEP-Work §2]
- Meaningful work: dispute whether subjective, objective or both; Kantian accounts ground it in autonomy (Schwartz 1982, Bowie 1998, Roessler 2012); Rawls: lack of meaningful work undermines self-respect, so a just society may need to be an "employer of last resort". [source: SEP-Work §2]
- Anti-work tradition: Bertrand Russell (1932, "In Praise of Idleness") on the value of leisure; Hannah Arendt (1958). [source: SEP-Work §2]
- Post-work: some welcome it as liberating (Danaher, *Automation and Utopia*, 2019; Livingston 2016; Chamberlain 2018; James 2018); others fear loss of a central arena of human goods (Deranty 2015), inequality (Frase 2016), or the psychological transition (Cholbi 2018). [source: SEP-Work §4]

## 5. Responsibility for autonomous systems

### Responsibility gap (Matthias 2004)
- Andreas Matthias, "The responsibility gap: Ascribing responsibility for the actions of learning automata", *Ethics and Information Technology* 6(3) (**2004**): 175–183. [source: SEP-CR bibliography; IEP-AI references]
- Claim (SEP): "the more complex computer technologies become and the less human beings can directly control or intervene in the behavior of these technologies, the less we can reasonably hold human beings responsible". Argument: we normally blame the operator if the machine works as specified, the manufacturer for construction flaws; control is a necessary condition of responsibility; with learning automata neither can in principle predict future behaviour; so neither is (fully) responsible. Examples include a Mars vehicle, an adaptive elevator, a lung-cancer diagnosis system and a pet robot. [source: SEP-CR §1; WS-19 (examples and control premise)]
- Background: the "problem of many hands" (Nissenbaum 1994; Jonas 1984; Friedman 1990) — Therac-25 radiation overdoses of six patients in the 1980s, contributing to three deaths (Leveson and Turner 1993); the 737 MAX crashes (late 2018, early 2019). [source: SEP-CR §1]

### Sparrow, "Killer Robots" (2007) — candidate argument for step-by-step reconstruction
- Robert Sparrow, "Killer Robots", *Journal of Applied Philosophy* 24(1) (**2007**): 62–77. [source: SEP-CR bibliography; IEP-AI references]
- SEP-EAI attributes the "responsibility gap" for autonomous weapon killings "esp." to Sparrow 2007, "meaning that neither the human nor the machine may be responsible". [source: SEP-EAI §2.7.4]
- Structure (WS-6, summarising the paper and Champagne & Tonkens' reading): asks who is responsible when an autonomous weapon commits what would otherwise be a war crime; considers (a) the programmer/designer, (b) the commanding officer who ordered its use, (c) the machine itself; none is satisfactory. Programmers and commanders cannot fairly be blamed for genuinely autonomous choices they could not predict or control; the machine cannot be responsible because responsibility requires that the agent can be punished (made to suffer), which a non-sentient robot cannot. Conclusion: since fighting justly requires that someone be answerable for deaths, deploying such weapons is unethical. [source: WS-6 https://robsparrow.com/wp-content/uploads/Killer-robots.pdf (not read in full); SEP-CR §3 ("they cannot suffer and thus cannot be punished (Sparrow 2007; Asaro 2011)")]
- Objections/replies: (1) **meaningful human control** — keep humans "in" or "on the loop" (Santoni de Sio & van den Hoven 2018), originally coined in the LAWS debate; (2) Champagne & Tonkens' "blank check" responsibility — a high-ranking person voluntarily accepts responsibility in advance; (3) Simpson & Müller (2016): not every event must have a responsible party; the real issue is the just distribution of risk; (4) sceptics deny any new gap: Daniel Tigard, "There is No Techno-Responsibility Gap" (*Philosophy & Technology*, 2020), Königs 2022, Da Silva 2024; (5) "retribution gap" (Danaher 2016). Reply on behalf of Sparrow/Matthias: meaningful control may be illusory at machine speed, and accepting blame in advance does not make it *deserved*. SEP-EAI: "It remains an open question whether the advent AI systems truly challenge our allocations of responsibility, or rather just generates temporary confusion." [source: SEP-EAI §2.7.4; SEP-CR §5; IEP-AI §2b; WS-6]
- Santoni de Sio & Mecacci (2020) identify further gaps, including an "active responsibility gap" (forward-looking duties). [source: SEP-CR §5]

### Autonomous vehicles, trolley dilemmas and the Moral Machine
- First death in a car on "autopilot" (Tesla Model S) May 2016; first pedestrian killed by an experimental self-driving car (Uber) **March 2018** — the system classified the victim as "unknown", then "vehicle", then "bicycle". [source: IEP-AI §2b]
- About 1 million people killed on roads per year (SEP's round figure). [source: SEP-EAI §2.6.2]
- Doubt that real cars face trolley problems (Nyholm & Smids 2016; Himmelreich 2018 "Never Mind the Trolley"; Goodall 2016 "Away from Trolley Problems and Toward Risk Management"). SEP: "It is doubtful … that an actual driver or autonomous car will ever have to solve trolley problems." [source: SEP-EAI §2.6.2; IEP-AI §2b]
- **Moral Machine** (MIT Media Lab, Iyad Rahwan's Scalable Cooperation group; idea of Rahwan, Azim Shariff, Jean-François Bonnefon; built by Edmond Awad and Sohan Dsouza). Deployed June 2016, active to July 2020. Edmond Awad et al., "The Moral Machine experiment", *Nature* 563: 59–64 (**October 2018**). About **40 million decisions** from millions of participants in **233 countries and territories**, ten languages. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_Machine ; IEP-AI references; WS-8]
- Findings: strong global preferences for sparing humans over animals, more lives over fewer, the young over the old; three cultural clusters (labelled Western, Eastern, Southern in the paper; Wikipedia describes them as Christian-majority North America/Europe; East Asian and Islamic countries; Latin America and Francophone countries); the preference for sparing the young was weaker in the Eastern cluster; respondents not a representative sample, and Rahwan urged "extreme caution". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_Machine ; WS-8]
- **German Ethics Commission** on Automated and Connected Driving (Federal Ministry of Transport and Digital Infrastructure), chaired by former constitutional judge **Udo Di Fabio**, presented **20 ethical guidelines on 20 June 2017**. Rule 1: automated driving is ethically required if it causes fewer accidents than human driving. Rule 9 (as quoted in coverage): "In the event of unavoidable accident situations, any distinction between individuals based on personal features (age, gender, physical or mental constitution) is strictly prohibited"; "General programming to reduce the number of personal injuries may be justifiable. Those parties involved in the generation of mobility risks must not sacrifice non-involved parties." Awad et al. note the clash with the Moral Machine's preference for sparing the young. [source: WS-7 https://sciencemediacenter.de/en/angebote/ethik-kommission-zum-automatisierten-fahren-legt-richtlinien-vor-17042 ; WS-8 (NPR) https://www.kqed.org/science/1933667/should-self-driving-cars-have-ethics] **[rule wording from news coverage; the report itself was not read]**
- Teaching point: the Moral Machine records preferences, not what is right; Germany's rule 9 rejects some popular preferences on grounds of equal dignity.

## 6. Long-term AI risk

### Early warnings
- Samuel Butler, "Darwin among the Machines" (1863); Karel Čapek's play *R.U.R.* (1920) introduced the word "robot". Turing (1951 lecture): "At some stage therefore we should have to expect the machines to take control, in the way that is mentioned in Samuel Butler's *Erewhon*." [source: SEP-EAI §2.8.1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_risk_from_artificial_intelligence]
- **Norbert Wiener** (1894–1964), founder of cybernetics, "Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation", *Science* 131 (3410): 1355–1358, **6 May 1960**: "If we use, to achieve our purposes, a mechanical agency with whose operation we cannot interfere effectively […] we had better be quite sure that the purpose put into the machine is the purpose which we really desire." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_alignment ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norbert_Wiener]
- **I. J. Good** (Irving John Good, born Isadore Jacob Gudak, 9 Dec 1916 – 5 Apr 2009), Bletchley Park cryptologist with Turing, later at Virginia Tech, consultant to Kubrick's *2001*. "Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine" (**1965**). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I._J._Good ; IEP-AI §2g]

### Bostrom, *Superintelligence* (2014)
- Nick Bostrom, *Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies* (Oxford University Press, **2014**); #17 on the NYT science best-seller list, August 2014. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superintelligence:_Paths,_Dangers,_Strategies]
- **Orthogonality thesis**: "Intelligence and final goals are orthogonal axes along which possible agents can freely vary. In other words, more or less any level of intelligence could in principle be combined with more or less any final goal." (Bostrom 2012, 73; cf. 2014, 105–109). It denies the Kantian-style view that greater rationality brings better moral understanding. [source: SEP-EAI §2.8.2]
- **Instrumental convergence**: "Several instrumental values can be identified which are convergent in the sense that their attainment would increase the chances of the agent's goal being realized for a wide range of final plans and a wide range of situations…". Steve Omohundro's "basic AI drives" (2008): self-preservation, goal-content integrity, self-improvement, resource acquisition. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_convergence ; SEP-EAI §2.8.2]
- Paperclip maximiser: first in March 2003 (Yudkowsky, Extropians list; Bostrom, "Ethical Issues in Advanced Artificial Intelligence", 2003); Bostrom says it is an illustration, not a prediction. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_convergence ; IEP-AI §2g]
- "Control problem": how humans could control a superintelligent system (Bostrom 2014, 127ff); since control seems very hard, the focus is on aligning values at design stage. [source: SEP-EAI §2.8.2]
- Critics of the argument: Müller and Cannon (2022) argue orthogonality plus instrumental convergence incoherently assume two notions of intelligence; Dung (2024) defends orthogonality; Floridi (2016) calls the singularity a "myth"; Andrew Ng (2015): worrying about it is "like worrying about overpopulation on Mars". Cappelen, Goldstein and Hawthorne argue dismissers advance "equally speculative 'survival stories'". [source: SEP-EAI §2.8.2; IEP-AI §2g; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_risk_from_artificial_intelligence]
- "Fanaticism": the x-risk literature is often tied to the view that a very large utility, even at low probability, overrides medium-size certain utility (Wilkinson 2022). Cross-refer to *Consequentialism* for longtermism. [source: SEP-EAI §2.8.2]

### Russell, *Human Compatible* (2019) and the alignment problem
- Stuart J. Russell, *Human Compatible: Artificial Intelligence and the Problem of Control* (Viking, **2019**). Argues the "standard model" (optimise a fixed, human-specified objective) is dangerously misguided. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Compatible ; IEP-AI references]
- Three principles (p. 173), meant for developers, not to be coded in: "1. The machine's only objective is to maximize the realization of human preferences. 2. The machine is initially uncertain about what those preferences are. 3. The ultimate source of information about human preferences is human behavior." Uses inverse reinforcement learning. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Compatible ; IEP-AI §2g]
- Russell's "King Midas" framing: "you get exactly what you ask for, not what you want." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_alignment]
- Alignment definition: steering AI systems toward intended goals, preferences or ethical principles; proxy goals and "reward hacking"/specification gaming. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_alignment]
- "Whose values?": Iason Gabriel (2020) proposes Rawlsian fair principles that could command an overlapping consensus. [source: IEP-AI §2g]
- Brian Christian, *The Alignment Problem: Machine Learning and Human Values* (W. W. Norton, 6 Oct 2020). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Alignment_Problem]

### Near-term vs long-term harms debate
- SEP-EAI frames it as "short-term" vs "long-term" AI ethics; notes that "some of the discussion about long-term risk is moving away from XRisk and superintelligence, returning to general considerations of risk from AI"; "Even philosophers who … think that there are compelling reasons to reject the arguments for XRisk, should acknowledge that they might be wrong." ## Arguments for step-by-step reconstruction

### A. The responsibility-gap argument against autonomous weapons (Sparrow 2007, building on Matthias 2004)
1. It is permissible to use a weapon in war only if someone can justly be held responsible for the deaths it causes (a condition of just war / jus in bello).
2. If an autonomous weapon kills wrongly, the candidates for responsibility are the programmer/designer, the commanding officer who deployed it, and the machine itself.
3. The programmer is not responsible: the system's choices are its own (learned, not specified), so they could not be predicted or controlled (Matthias's control condition).
4. The commander is not responsible for the same reason: deploying a system whose targeting choices are autonomous does not give control over them.
5. The machine is not responsible: being responsible requires being an apt target of praise and punishment, and a machine that cannot suffer cannot be punished.
6. So no one can justly be held responsible (a "responsibility gap").
7. So deploying fully autonomous weapons is impermissible.
- **Objection** (meaningful human control; Santoni de Sio & van den Hoven 2018; or "blank check" responsibility, Champagne & Tonkens): premises 3–4 are false if we design systems and institutions so that humans keep meaningful control or formally accept responsibility in advance; and (Tigard 2020; Simpson & Müller 2016) responsibility can be distributed or the issue recast as fair distribution of risk, as with other risky technologies.
- **Reply:** If humans keep enough control to be responsible, the weapon is not fully autonomous, so the argument still rules out *full* autonomy; and pre-accepted responsibility makes someone *liable*, not *blameworthy* for choices they could not foresee, which is exactly the gap. SEP-EAI leaves it open whether the gap is real or "just generates temporary confusion".
[sources: SEP-EAI §2.7.4; SEP-CR §§1, 3, 5; IEP-AI §2b; WS-6; WS-19]

### B. The fairness impossibility argument
1. A risk score is fair to two groups only if it is calibrated within each group (a given score means the same reoffending probability in each). [Northpointe; Hedden]
2. It is fair only if error rates are balanced (innocent members of each group equally likely to be flagged; guilty members equally likely to be missed). [ProPublica]
3. Theorem (Kleinberg et al. 2016; Chouldechova 2017): when base rates differ between groups and prediction is imperfect, no score satisfies both.
4. Base rates differ and prediction is imperfect in COMPAS (51% vs 39%).
5. So no recidivism score for these groups can be fair by both standards; one must choose, or abandon a purely statistical test of fairness.
- **Objection** (Hedden 2021): premise 2 is false — only calibration is required; unequal error rates reflect different base rates, not unfair treatment.
- **Reply:** if base rates themselves reflect unjust history or biased data (more policing producing more recorded arrests), calibrated scores entrench injustice; and Green & Hu say calling a statistical incompatibility an "impossibility of fairness" is "mistaking the map for the territory" — fairness is a moral notion, not a single statistic. [sources: SEP-AF §§1–2; IEP-AI §2c; WS-1; WS-2]

### C. Contextual integrity against "nothing to hide"/"it's public anyway" (optional)
1. Privacy is violated when information flows in breach of the norms of the context in which it was shared (Nissenbaum).
2. Norms are specified by subject, sender, recipient, information type and transmission principle.
3. Aggregating or re-routing information already "public" (e.g. location traces) can change recipient and transmission principle.
4. So such flows can violate privacy even though no secret was revealed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contextual_integrity ; SEP-ITP §1.4]

## Surprises (for the writer)
- The Moses-bridge story, the standard example of "artifacts with politics", is historically contested (Joerges 1999; Woolgar & Cooper 1999).
- Foucault's best-known image (the panopticon) is, per IEP, not the book's central claim.
- "The right to be let alone" is Judge Cooley's phrase, which Warren and Brandeis credited.
- Luddites were not opposed to machinery as such.
- The famous Good quote is usually truncated; the original adds "provided that the machine is docile enough to tell us how to keep it under control".
- The 47% figure was about technical feasibility over an unspecified period, not a forecast of job losses.
- COMPAS: both ProPublica's and Northpointe's numerical claims are true; the disagreement is about which fairness measure counts.
- GDPR was adopted in 2016, not 2018 (SEP-ITP says "spring 2018").
- Bentham's panopticon idea came from his brother Samuel's factory-supervision scheme in Belarus.
