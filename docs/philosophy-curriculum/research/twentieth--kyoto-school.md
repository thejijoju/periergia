## The label "Kyoto School" and who belongs
- The Kyoto School (*Kyōto-gakuha*) is "a group of 20th century Japanese philosophers who drew on the intellectual and spiritual traditions of East Asia, those of Mahāyāna Buddhism in particular, as well as on the methods and content of Western philosophy" [source: SEP-KY intro].
- Founding figure: Nishida Kitarō (1870–1945); the two other most prominent members are Tanabe Hajime (1885–1962) and Nishitani Keiji (1900–1990); the name reflects their association with Kyoto (Imperial) University [source: SEP-KY §1].
- Unlike Plato's Academy or the Frankfurt School's Institute for Social Research, the Kyoto School never founded an institution or official organisation (until the Nishida Philosophy Association, 2003); Nishida reportedly always encouraged independent thinking and never intended to found a "school" [source: SEP-KY §2.1; WP-KS].
- **Who coined the name — correct the brief's assumption.** Tosaka Jun did NOT coin the label from nothing. Tsuchida Kyōson (1891–1934), a former student of Nishida, wrote of "the so-called Kyōto School, the founder of which is Nishida" in *Contemporary Thought of Japan and China* (English, 1927); the Japanese version (published first, 1926) says *Kyōto-ha*; "so-called" implies the phrase was already in use; Tsuchida treated Tanabe as an excellent representative [source: SEP-KY §2.1 (citing Crespín Perales 2022, 2023); WP-KS].
- Tosaka Jun (1900–1945), a student of Nishida and Tanabe, in a 1932 essay "The Philosophy of the Kyoto School", used the term still current, *Kyōto-gakuha*; he included Nishida, Tanabe and Miki Kiyoshi, and attacked the School as the epitome of "bourgeois philosophy in Japan", an idealism ignoring material conditions and social praxis [source: SEP-KY §2.1; WP-KS citing Heisig 2001, p. 4]. Safe wording: "Tosaka gave the School its lasting name in 1932, though the looser label *Kyōto-ha* was already circulating by 1926."
- Ironically Tosaka himself, with Miki, is now counted by some as the "left wing" of the Kyoto School (Hattori 2004) [source: SEP-KY §2.1].
- After the war the name became politically charged: Nishitani recalled (1977) that "The name 'Kyoto School' is a name journalists used in connection with discussions that friends of mine and I held immediately before and during the war"; by then Americans used it "purely" for a school of thought [source: SEP-KY §2.1 (NKC XI, 207)].
- Since the 1970s the name recovered its philosophical sense, first in the West, where Religious Studies departments received the School's philosophies of religion while largely ignoring the political debates; the politics returned in the West in the 1990s (notably *Rude Awakenings*, Heisig & Maraldo eds.) [source: SEP-KY §§1, 2.1].
- Definitions differ: Fujita Masakatsu (following Takeda Atsushi) defines it as "the intellectual network that was centered on Nishida and Tanabe" and those directly influenced by them, so includes Tosaka and Miki; Ōhashi Ryōsuke defines it by a shared thought, "nothingness", and earlier excluded Miki for his Marxism [source: SEP-KY §2.2].
- John Maraldo identifies six criteria scholars have used to include/exclude members: (1) connection with Nishida; (2) Kyoto University; (3) stance toward Japanese/Eastern traditions; (4) stance toward Marxism, the nation-state and the Pacific War; (5) stance toward Buddhism and religion; (6) stance toward absolute nothingness (Maraldo 2005) [source: SEP-KY §2.2].
- Heisig, following Takeuchi Yoshinori, "triangulates" the School around Nishida, Tanabe and Nishitani (Heisig 2001, 3–7, 275–78) [source: SEP-KY §2.2].
- Tosaka's point, often repeated: without Tanabe's critical appropriation of Nishida there would be no "school", only disciples of "Nishida philosophy" [source: SEP-KY §2.2]. Nishida and Tanabe "infamously stopped speaking to one another"; Nishitani and Tosaka stayed on good terms despite differences [source: SEP-KY §2.2].
- Ōhashi's generations: first — Nishida, Tanabe; second — Hisamatsu Shin'ichi (1889–1980), Nishitani, Kōsaka Masaaki (1900–1969), Shimomura Toratarō (1900–1995), Kōyama Iwao (1905–1993), Suzuki Shigetaka (1907–1988); third — Takeuchi Yoshinori (1913–2002), Tsujimura Kōichi (1922–2010), Ueda Shizuteru (1926–2019) [source: SEP-KY §2.3].
- Abe Masao (1915–2006), a student of Hisamatsu, was "an important representative of the Kyoto School and contributor to inter-religious dialogue in North America", less known in Japan [source: SEP-KY §2.3].
- SEP-NK's alternative grouping: Tanabe often considered co-founder; the "fuzzy set" includes Marxist-leaning Miki and Tosaka, "more nationalistic students like Kōyama Iwao and Kōsaka Masaaki", and close disciples Mutai Risaku and Shimomura; D. T. Suzuki, Hisamatsu, Watsuji and Kuki on the fringes; third generation Takeuchi, Ueda, Abe [source: SEP-NK §11].
- **Associates, not members:** D. T. Suzuki (1870–1966), Nishida's schoolmate and friend, introduced him to Zen practice, but was not an academic philosopher nor at Kyoto University: "a closely related thinker" [source: SEP-KY §2.3; WP-KS]. Watsuji Tetsurō (1889–1960) and Kuki Shūzō (1888–1941) were both brought to Kyoto by Nishida but "remained too independent" for the inner circle; Davis calls them "first rate philosophers" whose work "outshines" many full members [source: SEP-KY §2.3]. Carter (SEP-WA §1): Watsuji "is not usually thought of as being a member", but it "would be a mistake to exclude him from some sort of honorary membership".

## Nishida Kitarō: life
- Born 19 May 1870 in Unoke, on the Sea of Japan north of Kanazawa (now Kahoku, Ishikawa), site of a large museum (designed by Andō Tadao); died 7 June 1945 in Kamakura [source: SEP-NK §1; SEP-KY §2.3; WP-NK]. WP-NK: died aged 75 of a renal infection; D. T. Suzuki organised his funeral [source: WP-NK].
- Dropped out of high school with D. T. Suzuki in 1890; entered Tokyo Imperial University as a "limited status" student 1891; graduated 1894 with a thesis on Hume's theory of causation; taught at provincial schools; "more intent on formal meditation practice under Zen masters in Kyoto" [source: SEP-NK §1].
- Zen: Nishida finally passed the *mu* kōan of the *Gateless Barrier* "after nearly a decade of intense practice"; he told Nishitani it was early his "impossible desire" to bring Zen and philosophy together [source: SEP-KY §3.2 (citing Yusa 2002; NKZ XIX, 224–25)].
- *An Inquiry into the Good* (*Zen no kenkyū*), begun 1905, published January 1911 (Kōdōkan); won him a post at Kyoto Imperial University in 1910, professor 1914 [source: SEP-NK §1; WP-NK works list]. (Cross-ref *Philosophy in an Age of Empire*, which covers pure experience; consistent.)
- He secured Kyoto posts for Tanabe, Watsuji and Kuki, and attracted Nishitani, Miki and Tosaka; retired (professor emeritus) and moved to Kamakura in 1929 [source: SEP-NK §1]. (WP-KS says Nishida held the chair 1913–28 and Tanabe succeeded him in 1928 [source: WP-KS; WP-HT].)
- Family losses: his first wife and four of his eight children died before him [source: SEP-NK §1].
- Self-description: "I have always been a miner of ore; I have never managed to refine it" (Nishida 1958, Preface; tr. Robert Schinzinger) [source: SEP-NK intro].
- Nishida helped bring Einstein to Japan in 1922 [source: SEP-NK §11].

## Nishida: from pure experience to place (basho)
- Pure experience (1911): experience in its original form "precedes the differentiation into subject experiencing and object experienced"; "The moment of seeing a color or hearing a sound" is prior to judgment (Abe & Ives tr., p. 3); influenced by William James and Ernst Mach but stresses non-individuated experience; Nishida later worried about its "latent psychologism" and dropped the term [source: SEP-NK §§2.1, 8.2]. Cross-ref *Philosophy in an Age of Empire* and *American Pragmatism* (the pragmatism chapter does not currently mention Nishida).
- Middle stages: *Intuition and Reflection in Self-Awareness* (1917) — self-awareness (*jikaku*), "a self reflecting itself within itself"; then "absolute free will" (*The Problem of Consciousness* 1920; *Art and Morality* 1923) [source: SEP-NK §§2.2, 8.2; SEP-KY §3.3].
- The essay "Place" (*Basho*) appeared in *Tetsugaku Kenkyū* no. 123, **June 1926** [source: WP-NK works list]; it was collected in *From That Which Acts to That Which Sees* — dated **1927** by SEP-NK §3, but "his 1926 book" by SEP-KY §§3.1, 3.3. Safe wording: "the 1926 essay 'Place', collected in *From That Which Acts to That Which Sees* (1927)". Many scholars see this book as the beginning of "Nishida philosophy" proper [source: SEP-KY §3.1].
- The book's preface ends with the programmatic lines: Western culture "thought form as being and the giving of form as good. However, does there not lie hidden at the base of our Eastern culture … something which sees the form of the formless and hears the voice of the voiceless? … it is my wish to provide this quest with a philosophical foundation" (NKZ IV, 6; Davis's translation) [source: SEP-KY §3.1].
- Colleague Sōda Kiichirō criticised the unclear first formulation of *basho* (October 1926) [source: SEP-NK §10; WP-NK].
- Nishida inverts Aristotle: instead of the grammatical subject that can never be a predicate (the *hypokeimenon*, substance), consciousness is the "transcendental predicate" that can never be a subject [source: SEP-NK §3].
- Three *topoi*, ontologically: the place of being (the world of nature); the place of relative nothingness (the field of consciousness, no-thing relative to the things it is conscious of, but still opposed to being); the place of absolute nothingness, which "underlies both sides of the opposition". "His premise is that the meaning of 'to be' is 'to be within'" [source: SEP-NK §3].
- *Zettai* (absolute) literally means "severing of opposition", i.e. without an opposing other; *sōtai* (relative) means "mutual opposition"; so the true absolute must embrace, not stand over against, the relative [source: SEP-KY §3.3; SEP-NK §3].
- Absolute nothingness is not a transcendent being, nor a Hegelian World Spirit; "absolute nothingness at once transcends everything and is that by which everything is constituted" (NKZ IX, 6) [source: SEP-KY §3.3; SEP-NK §7 gives a variant wording].
- One motive: only a place of absolute nothingness lets "truly individual" beings and genuine I–thou encounters exist, since "there is no universal [of being] whatsoever that subsumes the I and the thou" (NKZ VI, 381) [source: SEP-KY §3.3].
- Maraldo's three stages (heuristic; Heisig rejects discrete stages): (1) consciousness — pure experience, self-awareness, absolute will; (2) place (1927–c.1932); (3) the dialectical historical world (from c.1932 to 1945) [source: SEP-NK §8.2].
- Later key terms: "absolutely contradictory self-identity" (*zettai mujunteki jikodōitsu*), the title of an essay in *Shisō* no. 202, **March 1939** [source: WP-NK works list; SEP-KY bibliography (2025 multilingual edition of "this important 1939 text")]; "action-intuition" or "enactive intuition" (*kōi-teki chokkan*), "seeing a thing by becoming it", exemplified by artistic creation and scientific experiment; the historical world moves "from the created to the creating" out of an "absolute present" [source: SEP-NK §§4, 4.1; SEP-KY §3.3].
- WP-NK: the logic of *basho* aims to overcome the subject–object split of Aristotle's subject logic and Kant's predicate logic through "absolutely contradictory self-identity", "a dynamic tension of opposites that, unlike the dialectical logic of Hegel, does not resolve in a synthesis" [source: WP-NK; WP-KS] (WP characterisation; consistent with SEP-NK §4: "holds together differences without sublating them into a higher unity").
- 1934 statement: "Reality is being and at the same time nothingness … the self-identity of what is absolutely contradictory" (NKZ VII, 441; Davis tr., cf. Nishida 1970, tr. Dilworth) [source: SEP-KY §3.3].
- Nishida's own East/West contrast: Western culture takes the ground of reality to be being, Eastern to be nothingness (essay on "types of culture", NKZ VII, 429–33) [source: SEP-KY §3.1; SEP-NK §9]. Retrospectively: "It is not that I conceived of my way of thinking in dependence on Mahāyāna Buddhism; and yet it has come into accord with it" (NKZ XIV, 408) [source: SEP-KY §5.1].
- Last completed essay, "The Logic of Place and the Religious Worldview" (*Bashoteki ronri to shūkyōteki sekaikan*), composed February–April 1945, completed about two months before his death; English "The Logic of Topos and the Religious Worldview", tr. Michiko Yusa, *The Eastern Buddhist* 1986–87 [source: SEP-NK §1; WP-NK works list; SEP-KY bibliography].
- In it God/the absolute "contains its own absolute self-negation within itself" (NKZ XI, 397); neither pantheism nor transcendent theism; God is "immanently transcendent"; the relation of finite self and absolute is "inverse correspondence/correlation" (*gyakutaiō*): the closer the self approaches God, the stronger the difference; draws on D. T. Suzuki's "logic of *soku-hi*" ("is and is not") from the Prajñāpāramitā/Diamond Sutra; "I am myself by knowing my own death" (NKZ XI, 420) [source: SEP-KY §§3.3, 3.7; SEP-NK §5].
- Influence: Nishida's *basho* inspired physicist Yukawa Hideki's theory of "elementary domains"; architect Andō Tadao; psychiatrist Kimura Bin; ecologist Imanishi Kinji [source: SEP-NK §11].

## ARGUMENT 1 (for step-by-step reconstruction): Nishida's logic of place
Reconstruction from SEP-KY §3.3 (Davis's "rough outline" of the 1926 "Place" essay) and SEP-NK §3:
1. Whatever is, is related to other things; any relation between A and B requires a third term, a place C "in which their relation is situated" ("to be is to be within") [SEP-KY §3.3; SEP-NK §3].
2. The first candidate for C is physical space; but things are determined by more than location, so C is better understood as a field of judgment, a universal or category (red and blue appear and contrast as colours "within the same color field") [SEP-KY §3.3].
3. Categories are unified in consciousness, which correlates colour, size, shape and so on into the perception of one particular apple: so the field of consciousness is a more concrete place [SEP-KY §3.3]. Consciousness itself can never be an object/grammatical subject: it is a "transcendental predicate" — a "relative nothingness", no-thing relative to its objects [SEP-NK §3].
4. But the subjective field of consciousness has a limit: "As Kant demonstrated", it reflects things only as they appear under its categories, not as they are; and it is still set over against objects (so the nothing is still relative to being) [SEP-KY §3.3; SEP-NK §3].
5. So there must be an ultimate place where subject and object, persons and things, encounter each other and "come to be and pass away" — "the place, not just of intellectual judgments, but of birth and death" [SEP-KY §3.3].
6. That ultimate place cannot itself be a being (else it would need a further place, and it would be opposed to other beings); it must be what is not opposed to anything — "the place of true [absolute] nothingness", a "groundless ground" that envelops all beings while letting them determine themselves [SEP-KY §3.3; SEP-NK §3.1].
- Conclusion: reality is the self-determination of absolute nothingness; individuals are its "focal points" [SEP-NK §§3.1, 7].
- Helpful analogy (Maraldo's, not Nishida's): every text has a context, which can be made a text in a further context; this does not end in infinite regress but points to "an ultimate context of a different order" — absolute nothingness [SEP-NK §3.2].
- **Main objection (Tanabe, 1930)**: in "Requesting Instruction from Professor Nishida" (THZ IV, 305–328) Tanabe charged that the place of absolute nothingness (a) rests on a religious or mystical intuition, crossing illegitimately from philosophical reason to supra-rational experience, and falls into "a kind of Plotinian 'emanationism'"; and (b) by making nothingness an unchanging, enveloping "place" above the dialectical interaction of beings, it "ends up falling back into a metaphysics of being after all"; nothingness should instead be thought as the movement of "absolute mediation"/"absolute negation" [source: SEP-KY §3.4]. Many see this 1930 essay as the origin of the Kyoto School as more than Nishida's disciples [source: SEP-KY §3.4].
- **Reply (Nishida)**: his idea involves religious experience but is neither mystical ecstasy nor "thought in the direction of substance, as is Plotinus' One": "it is not a matter of the self-determination of being, but rather the self-determination of nothingness" (NKZ VI, 154); only a self-negating nothingness, not a transcendent One, affirms the world of the many — "because this is absolute nothingness … the mountain is mountain, the river is river" (NKZ V, 182) [source: SEP-KY §3.4]. And substantively he moved: under criticism from Tanabe, Miki and Tosaka in the early 1930s he developed the historical, dialectical world, the historical body and action-intuition [source: SEP-NK §§4, 10; SEP-KY §3.4].
- Further objections: SEP-NK §10 asks how a system in which wholes mirror themselves in their parts can account for "fallibility, unpredictability, distortion or violence", and where the author stands in it. IEP-NO §5 (Finn): if absolute nothingness is "defined in contrast to relativity, namely, as absolute", is it really beyond relative definition? "It seems that we have stumbled upon contradictions and binaries again"; Nishida calls it the "place of absolute nothing" (*zettai mu no basho*) [source: IEP-NO §5]. Scholars of Buddhism may object that Nishida's Buddhism stayed at the level of personal experience rather than the Abhidharma, Madhyamaka, Yogācāra, Tiantai and Huayan philosophical traditions [source: SEP-KY §3.3].
- Nishitani's later assessment: Tanabe's criticisms "often fail to do justice to Nishida's thought", but "were not without their point" [source: SEP-KY §3.4 (Davis summarising NKC IX)].

## Tanabe Hajime
- Born 3 February 1885, Tokyo; died 29 April 1962, (Kita-)Karuizawa; studied mathematics, then philosophy, at Tokyo Imperial University; taught at Tōhoku from 1913; Order of Culture [source: WP-HT].
- Invited by Nishida to Kyoto as associate professor 1919; in Germany 1922–23, under Riehl in Berlin then Husserl in Freiburg, where he befriended the young Heidegger and Oskar Becker; back in Japan 1924 [source: WP-HT]. Upon returning in 1924 he "was the first scholar in the world to write an article on Heidegger's thought" [source: SEP-KY §3.1]; WP-HT's bibliography lists "A New Turn in Phenomenology: Heidegger's Phenomenology of Life", *Shisō* no. 36, October 1924 [source: WP-HT]. (Consistent with the published *Phenomenology* chapter.)
- Succeeded Nishida in 1928 [source: WP-HT; WP-KS]; studied Hegel intensively (*Hegel's Philosophy and the Dialectic*, 1932) [source: WP-HT; SEP-KY §3.5].
- **Logic of species** (*shu no ronri*): first essay "The Logic of Social Existence" (*Tetsugaku Kenkyū*, November 1934–January 1935); "The Logic of Species and the World Schema" (1935); "The Logic of National Existence" (October–December 1939) [source: WP-HT bibliography]. Conceived "in critique of Bergson and Nishida" as an account of how ethnic specificity (the "species") mediates the particular individual and universal humanity; adapting Hegel, the nation-state could embody and rationalise a people's specificity, as a concrete universal [source: SEP-KY §4.5]. English: "The Logic of the Species as Dialectics", tr. David Dilworth and Taira Sato, *Monumenta Nipponica* 24:3 (1969) [source: WP-KS].
- The "critical lapse": Tanabe proposed that the "relative absolute" of the Japanese nation-state could be a "supreme archetype" for other nations (THZ VI, 232–33). Heisig: "a step that was fatal but really unnecessary, if not outright inconsistent with the principles of his logic … nothing in the logic of the specific allows any one instance to become an archetype for the others. It is as if Tanabe were quoting himself out of context" (Heisig 2001, 136–37) [source: SEP-KY §4.5].
- WP-HT: his wartime letters to students going to war use the regime's terms, and "even more damning are his essays written in defense of Japanese racial and state superiority" (citing Hubbard in Unno & Heisig) [source: WP-HT]; WP-KS: Tanabe "bears the greatest brunt of the criticism" for bringing the logic of species into politics [source: WP-KS].
- Tanabe also took part, with Nishitani but not Nishida, in secret meetings for the Navy, February 1942–1945 (see Ōshima memos below) [source: SEP-KY §4.4].
- **Metanoetics**: lecture "A Way to Repentance: Metanoetics" (*Zangedō*) given October 1944; book *Philosophy as the Way of Metanoetics* (*Zangedō to shite no tetsugaku*), Iwanami, April 1946 [source: WP-HT bibliography; SEP-KY §4.5 says "the first parts of which were delivered as lectures in 1944"]. *Zange* = repentance/confession; metanoesis from Greek *metanoia* [source: WP-HT; WP Metanoetics].
- English: *Philosophy as Metanoetics*, tr. Takeuchi Yoshinori with Valdo Viglielmo and James W. Heisig, University of California Press, 1986 [source: SEP-KY bibliography (Takeuchi trans., 1986); WS-7 (UC Press 1986; with Viglielmo and Heisig; Chisokudō 2nd rev. ed. 2016)]. (WP-KS gives April 1987 — a reprint/paperback; use 1986.)
- Content: reason, through Kantian antinomies, is driven to "absolute critique"; it must repeatedly die to its own self-power and be reborn through "Other-power" (*tariki*, the term of Shinran's Shin Buddhism) [source: SEP-KY §§3.4, 4.5]. It was "a personal self-critique", a call to national self-critique, and above all "an 'absolute critique' of human rationality" [source: SEP-KY §4.5].
- Tanabe's quoted formulations (Takeuchi tr.): "Because the absolute subject of Other-power is absolute nothingness … it must be thoroughly mediated by the relative self. In contrast to a mere 'self-identity of absolute contradictories', only that which entails the absolute existential mediation of the death and resurrection of the self can be called absolute nothingness" (Tanabe 1986, 8) [source: SEP-KY §3.4]. Davis comments the dismissal of Nishida is "hardly convincing", since Nishida too speaks of death and resurrection of the self [source: SEP-KY §3.4].
- Limits of the repentance: "one looks through that work in vain for any admission of guilt for particular actions or statements that he had made" (Heisig 2001, 151) [source: SEP-KY §4.5].
- In 1951 Tanabe wrote that as the war intensified, "weak-willed as I was, I found myself unable to resist … which is a shame deeper than I can bear"; more than ten philosophy graduates died on the battlefield, "for which I feel the height of responsibility and remorse" (as quoted in Heisig, "The Self That Is Not a Self", in Unno & Heisig 1990, p. 284; translator presumably Heisig) [source: WP-HT] (WP-only; verify against Unno & Heisig before quoting).
- Later Tanabe moved among Shin Buddhism, Christianity (*Demonstratio of Christianity*) and finally Zen [source: SEP-KY §5.1; WP-HT].

## Nishitani Keiji
- Born 27 February 1900, Ishikawa Prefecture; died 24 November 1990, Kyoto [source: WP-KN]. Studied under Nishida and Tanabe at Kyoto; dissertation (1924) "Das Ideale und das Reale bei Schelling und Bergson" [source: WP-KN].
- Memoir on his youth: "it was a period absolutely without hope … My decision then, to study philosophy was in fact – melodramatic as it might sound – a matter of life and death" (quoted in Davis ed., *Oxford Handbook of Japanese Philosophy*) [source: WP-KN] (WP-only).
- Began with Bergson, Schelling, Nietzsche and the German mystics; studied with Heidegger in Freiburg **1937–39**, when Heidegger was turning to nihilism; Heidegger "frequently invited him to his house to learn about Zen" [source: SEP-KY §3.5; WP-KN]. (WP-KN: he had a scholarship to study with Bergson, who was too ill [source: WP-KN].) Consistent with published *Existentialism* chapter.
- First book *A Philosophy of Elemental (Radical) Subjectivity* (1940); *Worldview and View of the Nation* (*Sekaikan to kokkakan*, 1941); *Nihilism* (*Nihirizumu*, Kōbundō, 1949) [source: WP-KN works list; SEP-KY §§3.7, 4.3]. English of *Nihilism*: *The Self-Overcoming of Nihilism*, tr. Graham Parkes with Setsuko Aihara, SUNY 1990 [source: SEP-KY bibliography]. Consistent with the published *Nietzsche* chapter.
- Held the chair of philosophy and religion at Kyoto, 1943 (WP-KN) — WP-KS gives other dates (1955–63); treat as uncertain [source: WP-KN; WP-KS].
- *Religion and Nothingness* = *Shūkyō to wa nanika* ("What is religion?"), 1961; English tr. Jan Van Bragt, University of California Press, **1982** [source: SEP-KY bibliography; WP-RN]. (Van Bragt's WP page and some citations give 1983 — likely a printing; use 1982.)
- Three fields: the field of (reified) "being and [dualistic] consciousness" → the "field of nihility" (*kyomu*, relative nothingness, "vacuous nothingness") → the "field of emptiness" (*kū no ba*, *śūnyatā*) [source: SEP-KY §3.5]. WP-KN calls the first the "Field of Consciousness" [source: WP-KN].
- His programme: "overcoming nihilism by way of passing through nihilism" (NKC XX, 192) [source: SEP-KY §§3.5, 4.5]; Heisig quotes him: "The fundamental problem of my life … has always been, to put it simply, the overcoming of nihilism *through* nihilism" [source: WP-KN citing Heisig 2001].
- Nishitani judged that Nishida begins from a standpoint where ego-consciousness is already broken through, and so neglects the existential path to it; Nishida's philosophy "failed to adequately address" nihilism [source: SEP-KY §3.5].
- On Western thinkers: Nietzsche reached only "a standpoint of relative absolute nothingness"; in Heidegger "traces of the representation of nothingness as some 'thing' that is nothingness still remain" (Van Bragt tr., 1982, pp. 66, 96) [source: SEP-KY §3.1].
- Unlike most of the School, the later Nishitani prefers the Buddhist term "emptiness" (*kū*, *śūnyatā*) to Nishida's "absolute nothingness" (*mu*) [source: SEP-KY §3.2].
- Nishitani refused the label "natural theologian of Zen", though he spoke from "the standpoint of Zen" [source: SEP-KY §1].

## ARGUMENT 2 (alternative): Nishitani's way through nihilism to emptiness
1. Modern subjects reify themselves as egos and objectify things for technological manipulation (the field of consciousness/being); science and humanism produce this [SEP-KY §3.5].
2. This standpoint collapses into reactive nihilism: "God as the highest being is dead" (heeding Nietzsche's madman), and self and things lose reality [SEP-KY §3.5].
3. Fleeing nihilism (back to reified being) is no solution; one must "step back" into the field of nihility and pass through it [SEP-KY §3.5].
4. But nihility is still a relative nothingness — it negates being from outside, so it remains defined against being [SEP-KY §§3.2, 3.5].
5. Only by "trans-descending" beneath nihility to the field of emptiness, where emptiness "empties itself even of the standpoint that represents it as some 'thing' that is emptiness", are self and things affirmed again, neither nullified nor reified, "on their own home-ground" [SEP-KY §§3.2, 3.5].
- Key quotation (Van Bragt tr.): emptiness "is to be realized as something united to and self-identical with being" (*Religion and Nothingness*, 1982, p. 97; NKC X, 109–10) [source: SEP-KY §3.2].
- Buddhist background: Nāgārjuna's "emptiness of emptiness" and the warning against "emptiness-sickness" (*kūbyō*); the *Heart Sutra*'s "form is emptiness; emptiness is form" [source: SEP-KY §3.2]. Cross-ref *Mahāyāna Philosophy* (published: "śūnyatā means not 'nihility' but 'the lack of autonomous existence'" — consistent with Nishitani's nihility/emptiness contrast).

## Ueda Shizuteru and the third generation (brief)
- Ueda Shizuteru (1926–2019), Nishitani's student, Eckhart scholar (PhD Marburg) and lay Zen master; leader of the Nishida revival from the 1980s; "twofold being-in-the-world"; formula "I, not being I, am I" (*ware wa, ware narazu shite, ware nari*); Eckhart's nothingness, he argues, still points to a higher being [source: SEP-KY §§3.1, 3.6, 3.7; WP Shizuteru_Ueda].
- Hisamatsu Shin'ichi: studied with Nishida, Zen at Myōshin-ji from 1915; taught at Kyoto 1943–49; teacher of Abe Masao; founded the FAS Society; spoke of absolute nothingness as "Oriental nothingness" (Hisamatsu 1960) [source: WP Shin'ichi_Hisamatsu; SEP-KY §3.1].

## Watsuji Tetsurō
- Born 1 March 1889, Himeji (Hyōgo), son of a physician; died 26 December 1960, Tokyo [source: WP-TW; SEP-WA §1]. Early books on Nietzsche (1913) and Kierkegaard (1915); his graduation thesis on Nietzsche was rejected, so he wrote one on Schopenhauer [source: SEP-WA §1].
- Influenced by Natsume Sōseki; from 1918 (*Revival of Idols*) turned from advocacy of Western individualism to critique; *Pilgrimages to the Ancient Temples in Nara* (1919); "single-handedly" brought Dōgen out of obscurity (*Shamon Dōgen* in *Studies in the History of the Japanese Spirit*, 1926/1935) [source: SEP-WA §1; WP-TW].
- Invited by Nishida and Hatano Seiichi to Kyoto (lecturer in ethics) in 1925; Europe 1927–28 (about fourteen months; returned summer 1928 because his father died); read *Being and Time* in Berlin in early summer 1927, "among the very first" readers; professor at Kyoto 1931; chair of ethics at Tokyo Imperial University 1934–49 [source: SEP-WA §§1–2; WP-TW].
- *Ethics as the Study of Man/Humanity* (*Ningen no gaku to shite no rinrigaku*): **1934** (WP-TW works list, Iwanami) vs "completed 1935" (SEP-WA §1). Use "1934" with WP, or "mid-1930s".
- *Fūdo* (Climate), Iwanami **1935**; English *Climate and Culture: A Philosophical Study*, tr. Geoffrey Bownas (1961) [source: WP-TW; SEP-WA bibliography]. Three types of culture: pastoral, desert, monsoon [source: WP-TW]. Augustin Berque prefers "milieu" to "climate" [source: SEP-WA §2].
- *Rinrigaku* (Ethics), three volumes 1937, 1942, 1949 [source: SEP-WA §1; WP-TW]. English (first half of vol. 1): Yamamoto Seisaku and Robert E. Carter, SUNY 1996 [source: SEP-WA bibliography; WP-TW].
- **Ningen and aidagara**: *ningen* is written *nin* (person) + *gen* (space, between); a human being is both individual and social — "we are individuals, and yet we are not just individuals … and we are social beings, but we are not just social beings" (Carter's paraphrase); *gen/ken* goes back to *aida*/*aidagara*, betweenness, the network of relationships [source: SEP-WA §3]. *Rinri* (ethics) = *rin* (fellows, system of relations) + *ri* (reason, principle) [source: SEP-WA §3]. "The study of ethics is the study of human being" [source: SEP-JP §5.3].
- Double negation: the individual negates the group to become an individual; then negates its own separateness, returning to the whole — "the negation of negation"; "The annihilation of the self … constitutes the basis of every selfless morality since ancient times" (Watsuji 1996, 225, Yamamoto & Carter tr.) [source: SEP-WA §§3–4]. Ethics "consists of the laws of social existence" (Watsuji 1996, 11) [source: SEP-WA §3]. "Betweenness is emptiness, and emptiness is betweenness" (Carter) [source: SEP-WA §4].
- Robinson Crusoe and Hobbes's state of nature dismissed as abstractions: we are born into relationships, beginning with the mother [source: SEP-WA §3].

## ARGUMENT 3 (alternative, links to Phenomenology chapter): Watsuji's critique of Heidegger in *Fūdo*
1. Heidegger's *Being and Time* founds human existence on temporality [SEP-WA §2].
2. Temporality alone can be lived in isolation: "As temporal beings, we can exist alone, in isolative reflection" (Carter); so to emphasize our being in time is "to discover human existence on the level only of individual consciousness" (Watsuji 1961, 9, Bownas tr.) [SEP-WA §2].
3. But human beings have a "dual character", individual and social; and the social is spatial: we exist "out in" a shared world — e.g. we do not first meet cold as an external object; "we are already out in the cold", and we feel it with others ("we all talk about the weather") [SEP-WA §2].
4. So "space must be regarded as linked with time" (Watsuji 1961, 9); climate/milieu is "the agent by which human life is objectivised" (1961, 14) [SEP-WA §2].
- Conclusion: an adequate account of existence must be spatial and social (*ningen*, betweenness), not individual and temporal.
- Objections: (a) the climatic typology risks determinism and national stereotyping (not stated in these terms in sources read — NOT VERIFIED as a sourced objection, see below); (b) the political objection that Watsuji's whole culminates in the state (next section). Published *Phenomenology* chapter already quotes the "level only of individual consciousness" line — consistent.

## Watsuji and the state; "Japanese spirit"
- The double negation culminates in "the restoration of the absolute totality", and Watsuji argues the state "takes on the authority of totality"; LaFleur: "by valorizing the state as that entity that has the moral authority … to 'turn private things into the public domain,' Watsuji provides a smooth rationale for a totalizing state" (LaFleur 1994, 458) [source: SEP-WA §§5–6].
- Wartime works: *Sonnō shisō to sono dentō* (The Idea of Reverence for the Emperor and Its Tradition, 1943), "one of the works for which Watsuji was branded a right-wing, reactionary thinker"; *Nihon no shindō* (The Way of the Japanese Imperial Subject) with *Amerika no kokuminsei* (The National Character of the United States), 1944 — this volume was ordered banned from sale by SCAP during the Occupation; postwar *Kokumin tōgō no shōchō* (The Symbol of National Unity, 1948) [source: SEP-WA §1; WP-TW].
- In the 1944 essay he contrasted Japanese readiness for self-sacrifice "if required by the Emperor" with Western egoism and utilitarianism; praised the bushidō ideal of "the absolute negativity of the subject" [source: SEP-WA §5].
- Detractors dominated 1945–c.1975, admirers from the 1980s (LaFleur) [source: SEP-WA §5]. Carter's partial defence: Rinrigaku vol. 3 insists no nation has the correct path, rejects cultural assimilation of one nation by another and the idea of a universal state; fault lies in "collapsing of the descriptive and the prescriptive" [source: SEP-WA §5]. *Sakoku: Japan's Tragedy* (1950) blamed the war on isolationism; LaFleur suggests a 1943 lecture to Navy officials warned against the course taken [source: SEP-WA §5].
- WP-TW: his wartime theories provided support for nationalism, "a fact which, after the war, he said that he regretted" [source: WP-TW] (WP, uncited; contrast LaFleur's speculation that Watsuji felt no need to recant — sources disagree).

## Kuki Shūzō, Miki Kiyoshi, Tosaka Jun (brief)
- Kuki Shūzō (15 February 1888 – 6 May 1941); son of Baron Kuki Ryūichi; close to Okakura Kakuzō; converted to Catholicism 1911; in Europe about seven/eight years (SEP-JA "seven-year stay"; WP "eight years") studying with Rickert, Husserl, Heidegger (Marburg 1927–28), knew Bergson and engaged the young Sartre as French tutor [source: SEP-JA §8; WP-SK].
- *The Structure of Iki* (*"Iki" no kōzō*): first draft Paris 1926; published 1930 (Iwanami) after his 1929 return [source: SEP-JA §8; WP-SK]. *Iki* (refined style, from Edo pleasure-quarter erotics) has three moments: coquetry (*bitai*), brave composure (*ikiji*), resignation (*akirame*) [source: SEP-JA §8]. English: Hiroshi Nara, *The Structure of Detachment* (2004); John Clark, *Reflections on Japanese Taste* (1997) [source: SEP-KY bibliography; SEP-JA bibliography].
- *The Problem of Contingency* (1935); first Japanese book-length study of Heidegger (1933) [source: WP-SK]. Leslie Pincus, *Authenticating Culture in Imperial Japan* (1996), reads Kuki as a cultural nationalist; Parkes (SEP-JA) rejects her claim he was a fascist [source: SEP-JA §8; SEP-KY bibliography].
- Miki Kiyoshi (5 January 1897 – 26 September 1945): studied with Rickert (1922) and Heidegger at Marburg (1923), Paris 1924; *Study of the Human Being in Pascal* (1926); arrested 1930 (funds to the Communist Party); in 1937 (date disputed) headed the cultural division of Konoe's Shōwa Research Association think tank; arrested again 1945 for sheltering the fugitive Takakura Teru; died of nephritis in Toyotama Prison on 26 September 1945, forty days after the war ended [source: WP-MK]. *The Logic of Imagination* (unfinished; vol. 1 1939), English tr. John W. M. Krummel (Bloomsbury 2024) [source: WP-MK; SEP-KY bibliography].
- Tosaka Jun (27 September 1900 – 9 August 1945): co-founded the Society for the Study of Materialism (1932), banned 1938; arrested 1938 under the Peace Preservation Law; died in Nagano prison the day of the Nagasaki bombing [source: WP-JT].
- SEP-KY: "Both Tosaka Jun and Miki Kiyoshi, the key figures of what is sometimes called the 'left wing of the Kyoto School,' died in prison in 1945 as a result of their intellectual resistance" [source: SEP-KY §4.1] (note WP's more specific grounds for Miki's last arrest; and Miki's collaboration with the Shōwa Research Association complicates a pure "resistance" label).

## Women in modern Japanese philosophy (sources support a short paragraph)
- SEP-JP §5.5: Asian women have been "doubly invisible" (Arisaka 2000); Murasaki Shikibu is now recognised as Japan's first known female philosopher (Wawrytko 2023), "while modern authors such as Yosano Akiko, Hiratsuka Raichō, and Yamakawa Kikue (JPS 2011) have been acknowledged as philosophers as well" — i.e. they are included in *Japanese Philosophy: A Sourcebook* [source: SEP-JP §5.5]. WP-HR cites the Sourcebook pp. 1148–58 for Hiratsuka [source: WP-HR].
- Hiratsuka Raichō (10 February 1886 – 24 May 1971): Zen practitioner; founded *Seitō* (Bluestocking), Japan's first all-women literary magazine, 1911, opening "In the beginning, woman was the sun"; founded the New Women's Association with Ichikawa Fusae, 1920 [source: WP-HR].
- Yosano Akiko (7 December 1878 – 29 May 1942): poet ("Thou Shalt Not Die", 1904, to her brother in the Russo-Japanese War); in the 1918–19 motherhood-protection debate argued for women's economic independence against Hiratsuka's call for state support of mothers; Yamakawa Kikue (3 November 1890 – 2 November 1980), socialist feminist, also took part; Yamakawa headed the Labour Ministry's Women's and Minors' Bureau 1947–51 [source: WP-YA; WP-YK; WP-HR].

Women in the School's later scholarship: Yusa Michiko (Nishida biographer), Yoko Arisaka (critic of Nishida's universalism), Keta Masako and Hanaoka (Kawamura) Eiko (fourth-generation affiliates), Erin McCarthy (feminist reading of Watsuji) [source: SEP-KY §§2.3, 4.2; bibliography].

## The wartime controversy: the record
- SEP-KY's framing: in the face of an unjust regime, philosophers had three options besides complicity: withdraw, resist openly, or attempt "immanent critique or cooperative correction"; Nishida, Tanabe and Nishitani took the third — Ōhashi's "cooperative resistance" or "anti-establishment cooperation" (*hantaiseiteki kyōryoku*) [source: SEP-KY §4.1]. Davis concedes their writings "were more or less successfully co-opted by the extreme nationalism that they were more or less trying to reorient" [source: SEP-KY §4.1].
- Nishitani's retrospective self-justification: he tried "from within to open up a path for overcoming this extreme nationalism" (NKC IV, 384) [source: SEP-KY §4.1].
- **Nishida's 1941 address to the emperor** warned: "Any totalitarian system that negates outright the role of the individual is but an anachronism" (NKZ XII, 271; tr. Yusa 1994) [source: SEP-KY §4.2]. SEP-NK: the address "advocated academic freedom and a place for each nation in the global world" [source: SEP-NK §1].
- Nishida's *The Problem of Japanese Culture* (lectures 1938; book 1940) sold some 60,000 copies [source: SEP-NK §1; WP-NK (March 1940)]. Former student Prime Minister Konoe Fumimaro was among officials who appealed to him [source: SEP-NK §1].
- **"Principles for a New World Order" (1943)**: Yatsugi Kazuo of the Center for National Strategy asked Nishida for a rationale for the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere; Nishida reportedly exploded in anger, then agreed; his text was "edited and 'simplified'" by a sociologist go-between; he was disappointed it had no effect on Tōjō [source: SEP-KY §4.2]. In it he insists the co-prosperity sphere must not entail ethnocentrism, expansionism, imperialism, colonialism or totalitarianism (NKZ XII, 432–33), yet writes "in order to build a particular world, a central figure that carries the burden of the project is necessary. In East Asia today there is no other but Japan" (NKZ XII, 429; tr. Arisaka 1996, 102) and "The Imperial House is the beginning and the end of our world, as the absolute present that embraces past and future" (NKZ XII, 430) [source: SEP-KY §4.2]. English: Yoko Arisaka, "The Nishida Enigma: 'The Principle of the New World Order'", *Monumenta Nipponica* 51:1 (1996), 81–106 [source: SEP-KY bibliography].
- Nishida used the slogans "all the world under one roof" (*hakkō ichiu*) and "imperial way" (*kōdō*); Ueda Shizuteru calls this a "tug-of-war over meaning" that Nishida lost; Yusa: he "took up the jargon and slogans of the day and sought to redeem them" [source: SEP-KY §4.2]. Right-wing critics found Nishida too abstract or insufficiently supportive; leftist critics after 1945 found him "profusely nationalistic" [source: SEP-NK §1].
- **Chūōkōron round tables**: Kōsaka Masaaki, Nishitani, Kōyama Iwao and the historian Suzuki Shigetaka held three discussions, published in *Chūōkōron* and then as the book *Sekaishi-teki tachiba to Nihon* (The World-Historical Standpoint and Japan, Chūōkōronsha 1943); English: David Williams, *The Philosophy of Japanese Wartime Resistance* (Routledge 2014), "avowedly interpretive and controversial" [source: SEP-KY §4.3 and bibliography]. Dates: first session 26 November 1941 (two weeks before Pearl Harbor), published January 1942; second published April 1942; third session 24 November 1942, published January 1943 [source: WS-1 (Williams; Tu 2006)] — WS-only, flagged. SEP-KY gives "1941–43".
- In them the participants called modern Japan the turning point of world history, criticised Western imperialism's hypocrisy, insisted Japan's "leading role" not be imperialist, but also whitewashed Japanese aggression in China and suggested "Japanizing" or "half-Japanizing" some "more superior" Asian ethnic groups (Kōsaka et al. 1943, 262–63, 337) [source: SEP-KY §4.3].
- Nishitani's *Worldview and View of the Nation* (1941): the Japanese nation would be the new "focal point of world history" after Rome and Britain, but its mission was a world with "no specific center", possible only if Japan became a self-negating "nation of non-ego" (NKC IV, 285–300) [source: SEP-KY §4.3].
- Imperial Way ideologues attacked the round tables as "ivory-tower speculations that risked reducing the Empire to simply one more category of world history"; further printings were reportedly stopped by censors [source: SEP-KY §4.5 (Horio 1994)].
- **"Overcoming Modernity" (*Kindai no chōkoku*) symposium**, held 23–24 July 1942 under the Literary World (*Bungakukai*) group, hosted by Kawakami Tetsutarō; participants included Bungakukai writers and critics (Hayashi Fusao, Kamei Katsuichirō, Miyoshi Tatsuji, Nakamura Mitsuo, Kobayashi Hideo), Japanese Romantic School figures, and Kyoto School members including Nishitani and Suzuki Shigetaka [source: WS-2 (details flagged WS-only); SEP-KY §4.3 for Nishitani's debate with Kobayashi]. Published in *Bungakukai*, autumn 1942 (sources differ: Sept–Oct vs Oct–Nov), book 1943 [source: WS-2]. So: **not a purely Kyoto School event**. English: Richard F. Calichman (ed. and tr.), *Overcoming Modernity: Cultural Identity in Wartime Japan* (Columbia UP, 2008) [source: SEP-KY bibliography; WS-2].
- The Kyoto participants argued for overcoming modernity by going through it, against Kobayashi's call to return to the pre-modern classics; Nishitani: "There is no turning back to the way things were … Our tradition must be appropriated from the direction in which we are heading" (NKC VIII, 183; Parkes tr. 1990, 179) [source: SEP-KY §4.3].
- **Ōshima memos**: notebooks of Ōshima Yasuma (Tanabe's student) published by Ōhashi Ryōsuke in 2001 (*Kyōto-gakuha to Nihon-kaigun*) record secret meetings of Kyoto School members (Tanabe, Nishitani; not Nishida) for a moderate faction of the Navy, February 1942 to just before war's end; Ōshima's 1965 account says the aims were first to avoid war, then to end it quickly (including toppling Tōjō's cabinet), then postwar planning — though only the second is detailed in the notebooks [source: SEP-KY §4.4].
- **After the war**: Nishitani: "During the war we were struck on the cheek from the right; after the war we were struck on the cheek from the left"; "Nishitani and others were purged for several years from their university positions" [source: SEP-KY §4.5]. Heisig: banned from public office by the occupation authorities in July 1946 [source: WP-KN citing Heisig 2001]. The "Big Four" of the round tables (Kōsaka, Nishitani, Kōyama, Suzuki) were removed from Kyoto University; Kōsaka and Nishitani later reinstated [source: WS-5 (weak source: japanese-wiki-corpus; flag)].

## Debates
- **Was the Kyoto School's wartime thought fascist/imperialist ideology, or "resistance from within"?** Name it as a live debate:
  - Critics: Tetsuo Najita and H. D. Harootunian (1998): the School's philosophies of history were "a thinly disguised justification … for Japanese aggression and continuing imperialism"; "no group helped defend the state more consistently and enthusiastically … and none came closer … to defining the philosophic contours of Japanese fascism" ("Japan's Revolt against the West", in *Modern Japanese Thought*, ed. Wakabayashi, CUP 1998, 238–39) [source: SEP-KY §4.1]. Harootunian, *Overcome by Modernity* (Princeton 2000): the symposium's "only destination … was the place where Japan itself had been overcome by modernity" — though he concedes it "prefigured precisely all of those doubts and obsessions concerning subjectivity, cultural difference, and even racism" now global (p. 94) [source: SEP-KY §4.3]. Bernard Faure, "The Kyoto School and Reverse Orientalism" (1995) [source: SEP-KY §5.1 and bibliography]. Peter Dale, *The Myth of Japanese Uniqueness* (1986), on *nihonjinron* [source: SEP-KY §5.1]. Leslie Pincus (1996) on Kuki [source: SEP-KY bibliography]. Naoki Sakai, "Modernity and Its Critique: The Problem of Universalism and Particularism", in Miyoshi & Harootunian (eds.), *Postmodernism and Japan* (Duke 1989), 93–122 [source: WS-4] — content of his argument not read (WS-3 reports he sees Western universalism and Japanese particularism as two sides of one coin; flagged). Robert Sharf, "The Zen of Japanese Nationalism", *History of Religions* 33:1 (1993), and Brian Victoria, *Zen at War* (1997), on Zen institutions' and D. T. Suzuki's support for the war (broader Zen context, not the philosophers specifically) [source: WS-8].
  - Middle/critical-but-careful: Yoko Arisaka (1999): "the chief claim of the defenders—that Nishida's philosophical 'universalism' is incompatible with nationalist ideology—fails because universalist discourse was used both as a tool of liberation and oppression in Japan's case"; she adds a nation claiming to bear a universal principle "should be familiar from recent American experience" [source: SEP-KY §4.2]. Critics may say Nishida questions Eurocentrism "only by way of shifting the locus of the concrete universal to Japan" [source: SEP-KY §4.2]. SEP-NK §6: a "charitable reading" (Japan should become one nation among equals) vs "a more critical reading" (falsely absolutising Japan) [source: SEP-NK §6].
  - Defenders/contextualisers: James Heisig and John Maraldo (eds.), *Rude Awakenings* (1994/1995 — SEP gives 1994; WS-8 says 1995); Heisig: "One has … to ignore the greatest bulk of the writings of these thinkers to arrive at the conclusion that anything approaching or supporting the imperialistic ideology of wartime Japan belongs to the fundamental inspiration of their thought" (Heisig 2001, 6); he warns against both "side-steppers and the side-swipers" (Heisig 1990) — though Heisig also criticises them (Heisig 2023) [source: SEP-KY §§4.1, 4.5]. Graham Parkes, "The Putative Fascism of the Kyoto School and the Political Correctness of the Modern Academy" (*Philosophy East and West* 47:3, 1997) [source: SEP-KY bibliography]. Ueda Shizuteru's "tug-of-war over meaning" (1994) [source: SEP-KY §4.2]. Christopher Goto-Jones, *Political Philosophy in Japan: Nishida, the Kyoto School, and Co-Prosperity* (Routledge 2005), reading Nishida's politics as neither ultranationalist nor liberal but a development of Mahāyāna thought; ed. *Re-politicising the Kyoto School as Philosophy* (2008) [source: SEP-KY bibliography]. David Williams, *Defending Japan's Pacific War* (2005), a "revisionist" defence centred on Tanabe as "post-White" political philosopher [source: SEP-KY bibliography]. Ōhashi's "cooperative resistance" thesis and the Ōshima memos [source: SEP-KY §4.4]. Maraldo, "The War Over the Kyoto School" (*Monumenta Nipponica* 61:3, 2006) reviews Goto-Jones and Williams [source: SEP-KY bibliography].
  - Davis's own verdict (one side): "many philosophers worldwide today view the political misadventures of the Kyoto School as lamentable footnotes to the main text", while "a faction of polemical intellectual historians" persists [source: SEP-KY §4.5]. A chapter should attribute this view to Davis, not state it as consensus.
- **Is Tanabe the co-founder or merely Nishida's critic?** Tosaka: no school without Tanabe; Davis: Tanabe often seen "justly or unjustly, as more of a dialectical counterpoint than an independent alternative" [source: SEP-KY §§2.2, 3.5].
- **Is "Eastern nothingness vs Western being" a useful contrast?** Heisig: the School's "East" is "something of an invention … one constellation of a heritage too long and too plural to be represented fairly by Japan" (Heisig 2001, 271–72); Maraldo: Nishida's problems "are universal" and "put 'East' and 'West' to rest" [source: SEP-KY §5.1].
- **Is Watsuji's ethics collectivist?** Carter: many read him as giving priority to the social, but his theory insists on a balanced dual nature [source: SEP-WA §3]; LaFleur: his state theory gives "a smooth rationale for a totalizing state" [source: SEP-WA §6]. Whether Buddhist emptiness is the key to his system: LaFleur yes; Dilworth: "not essentially a Buddhistic or religious one" [source: SEP-WA §6].
- **Is Nishida a Buddhist philosopher?** Davis: misleading to call Nishida's or Tanabe's thought simply "Eastern" or "Buddhist"; Nishida: not dependent on Mahāyāna, "and yet it has come into accord with it" [source: SEP-KY §5.1].
