## 1. What ontology is (framing)

- Ontology "as etymology suggests, is the study of being, of what there is": What entities or kinds of entity exist? Abstract entities (sets, numbers) as well as concrete ones ("people and puddles and protons")? Properties or universals as well as particulars? **Meta-ontology** asks about the nature and method of ontology itself, e.g. what a theory is committed to. [source: SEP-OC intro]
- Aristotle described his subject variously as "first philosophy", "the study of being qua being", "wisdom" or "theology". "Qua" means roughly "in so far as": the study of beings *as* beings, not some special object called "being qua being". [source: SEP-ArMet §1]
- Aristotle never used the word "metaphysics". At least a century after his death an editor, "in all probability" Andronicus of Rhodes, titled the fourteen books *Ta meta ta phusika*, "the ones after the physical ones" (i.e. after the *Physics*). [source: SEP-Met §1; WP-Metaphysics_(Aristotle) says Andronicus arranged the works in the 1st c. BCE]
- **The word "ontology" is early modern.** Jacob Lorhard's *Ogdoas Scholastica* (St Gallen, 1606) contains "ontologia", "probably appearing for the first time ever in a book"; he uses it synonymously with "metaphysica" (the title ends "Metaphysices, seu Ontologiae"). Rudolph Goclenius's *Lexicon Philosophicum* (Frankfurt, 1613), p. 16: "ontologia, philosophia de ente" ("ontology, the philosophy of being"). A 2nd edition of Lorhard's book, *Theatrum Philosophicum*, also appeared in 1613 (Basel). Lorhard (d. 19 May 1609) and Goclenius were both in Marburg in 1607 and "probably met". [source: WP-Jacob_Lorhard; WP-Rudolph_Goclenius ("the term itself was first introduced by Jacob Lorhard in *Ogdoas Scholastica* (1606)")] WP-Ontology: the Greeks did not use the term; "coined by philosophers in the 17th century". Christian Wolff (1679–1754) was influential in establishing ontology as a distinct discipline. [source: WP-Ontology] Caveat: "first ever" is hedged ("probably") in WP; say "the earliest known printed use".
- The problem of universals "in general is a historically variable bundle of several closely related ... metaphysical, logical, and epistemological questions, ultimately all connected to the issue of how universal cognition of singular things is possible" (worked example: how do we know Pythagoras' theorem holds for all possible right triangles, having seen only finitely many?). [source: SEP-UM intro]

## 2. The problem of universals: the one-over-many argument (NEW; set out step by step)

- Worked example (IEP): an apple and a ruby are both red; "their common redness results from sharing a universal. If they are both red at the same time, the universal, red, must be in two places at once." Universals are **repeatable / multiply exemplifiable**; individuals (particulars) are not ("in only one place at a time"). [source: IEP-Univ intro, §1a]
- SEP-Prop: "The most traditional task for which properties have been appealed to is to provide a solution to the so-called 'one over many problem' via a corresponding 'one over many argument'. This traces back at least to Socrates and Plato (e.g., *Phaedo*, 100c–d) and keeps being rehearsed (Russell 1912: ch. 9; ... Armstrong 1978a: ch. 7; Loux & Crisp 2017: ch. 1)." Things "are many ... and yet they are somehow one". [source: SEP-Prop §1.2]
- Reconstruction for the chapter (from SEP-Prop §1.2 and IEP-Univ §§1c, 2):
  1. Many numerically distinct things are genuinely alike (same shape, colour, weight); we apply one general term ("red", "spherical") to all of them, and not arbitrarily.
  2. Such agreement needs an explanation in how the things are, not in our conventions ("it is the world, and not our conventions, that make predications true or false").
  3. The best explanation is that there is one thing, a property/universal, that each of them has.
  4. So universals exist. (SEP-Prop: the argument is an "abductive inference to the best explanation" and "not foolproof".)
  - Supporting arguments: properties as meanings of predicates (*Phaedo* 78e; Russell 1912); apparent quantification over properties ("this apple is red; this tomato is red; hence there is something that this apple is and this tomato also is"); quantification over properties in science (inverse-square law, temperature as mean kinetic energy); laws of nature as relations between universals; Plato's epistemic argument that knowledge needs unchanging objects. [source: SEP-Prop §1.2; IEP-Univ §1b]
  - **Main objection (Quine; "ostrich"/austere nominalism):** predicates do not carry ontological commitment; only singular terms and bound variables do; "a is red" needs no entity "redness" (Quine 1948). Nothing needs explaining; some predicates just apply. Armstrong (1978) called this "ostrich nominalism", "originally a pejorative jab"; Devitt replied in "'Ostrich Nominalism' or 'Mirage Realism'?" (1980). [source: SEP-Prop §1.2; SEP-Nom §4.2; SEP-Prop bibliography]
  - **Second objection (parsimony/strangeness):** universals would be wholly present in many places at once; Plato's Forms are outside space and time and causally inert, so it is mysterious how we know them; and "if qualitative resemblance ... can be accounted for without universals ... we should do without them". [source: IEP-Univ §§1a, 2c]
  - **Realist reply:** nominalist alternatives either leave resemblance brute or smuggle in a universal (resemblance) (see §5). [source: IEP-Univ §3]
- The map: **realists** accept mind-independent universals; **conceptualists** say generality lies in concepts in minds; **nominalists** accept only particulars (and words). [source: IEP-Univ intro; SEP-Prop §1.3] Two senses of "nominalism" today: denial of universals vs denial of abstract objects; they overlap but differ (an argument against universals is often about shareability; one against abstracta about non-spatiotemporality and knowability). [source: SEP-Nom §1]
- **Transcendent (ante rem) vs immanent (in rebus) realism.** Plato: universals exist even uninstantiated ("before the things"); Aristotle, "in opposition to his master", held they exist only if instantiated ("in things"). Contemporary champions: Russell (1912) for the first, Armstrong (1978a) for the second. [source: SEP-Prop §1.3] IEP names these "Extreme Realism" (Plato) and "Strong Realism" (Aristotle-inspired); the latter "is immune to the Third Man Argument". [source: IEP-Univ §2]
- Medieval labels: universals *ante rem* (divine ideas), *in re* (features in things), *post rem* (concepts). Nearly all medievals accepted divine ideas but none accepted Plato's mind-independent eternal Forms; Klima warns the realist/conceptualist/nominalist classification is "crude". [source: SEP-UM §1]

## 3. Plato's Forms and the Third Man (CROSS-REF *Plato*; brief here)

- In the *Parmenides* (Third Man at 132a–b) an aged Parmenides attacks the theory of Forms. "Plato never refers to any argument as the 'Third Man'. The moniker derives from Aristotle" (e.g. *Metaphysics* 990b17 = 1079a13, 1039a2; *Sophistical Refutations* 178b36 ff.). [source: SEP-Parm §4.3]
- Most commentators: the regress uses three principles, **One-over-Many**, **Self-Predication** (the F is F), **Non-Identity** (no form is identical with what partakes of it); L1, L2, L3 … an infinite hierarchy of forms of largeness. The vast literature was "initiated by" Vlastos (1954), *Philosophical Review* 63: 319–49. Commentators differ over why the regress is vicious (an epistemic reading: explanation must come to an end — Vlastos, Fine) and which premise Plato should drop. [source: SEP-Parm §4.3; IEP-Univ references] The *Plato* chapter already sets this out step by step (with Rickless's view) — do not repeat; use it to motivate Aristotle's immanent alternative.
- IEP's version in apple terms: the Form of Red must itself be red to make apples red; then its redness needs a Red2, and so on; so "we'll never be able to explain why our original apple is red". "The Third Man Argument threatens only Extreme Realism." [source: IEP-Univ §2c]

## 4. Aristotle's immanent realism (CROSS-REF *Aristotle*; brief)

- In the *Categories*, "said of" a subject relates more general to less general within a category (man is said of a particular man; animal of man; transitive, 1b10); things "in" a subject cannot exist on their own (a shade of colour). Individual substances (this man, that horse) are "primary substances" (*prôtai ousiai*) "for without them ... nothing else would exist" (argument at 2a35–2b7). "Universals" (*ta katholou*) are what is "said of many". [source: SEP-ArMet §2]
- In *Metaphysics* Book Zeta he reopens "what is substance?" — already in the *Aristotle* chapter with the controversy whether forms are universal or particular. Do not repeat.
- Trope precursor debate: in the *Categories*, quality comes in a universal and particular variety ("pallor" and "this pale", *to ti leukon*); Owen (1965) read "this pale" as a perfectly shareable determinate shade, the traditional reading (Ackrill 1963; Kampa & Wilkins 2018) as a particular "bit" of pallor (a trope). [source: SEP-Trope §1]

## 5. Medieval debate (CROSS-REF *Augustine*, *Neoplatonism*, *Early Scholasticism*, *Falsafa*, *Scotus, Ockham…*; mostly brief)

- **Porphyry** (234?–305? CE), born in Tyre; with Plotinus in Rome 263–268; left for Sicily in 268; Plotinus died 270. [source: SEP-Porph] The *Isagoge* ("Introduction" to Aristotle's *Categories*) was composed in Sicily in 268–270 and sent to Chrysaorius, "according to all the ancient commentators" (Ammonius, Elias, David). [source: WP-Isagoge] Standard introduction to logic "in Byzantium, the Arabic world and, through Boethius' translations, in the Latin West". [source: SEP-Porph]
- Porphyry's three questions, Spade's translation (*Five Texts*, 1994, p. 1): "I shall beg off saying anything about (a) whether genera and species are real or are situated in bare thoughts alone, (b) whether as real they are bodies or incorporeals, and (c) whether they are separated or in sensibles and have their reality in connection with them. Such business is profound, and requires another, greater investigation." [source: SEP-UM §3]
- **Boethius** (c. 480–524) wrote two commentaries on the *Isagoge*, the first on Marius Victorinus's translation, the second on his own. [source: SEP-UM §§1, 3]
- **Boethius's dilemma (NEW here; set out step by step — Klima's reconstruction):** A universal must be common to many "in its entirety", "simultaneously", and must "constitute the substance of its particulars".
  1. A universal exists either in reality or only in the mind.
  2. If in reality, it is either one being or many beings.
  3. Not one being: what constitutes a thing's substance shares its act of being; one act of being cannot be identical with the many distinct acts of being of its particulars at once.
  4. Not many beings (e.g. the collection of individual animalities): those animalities would need a further genus, generating "super-animalities" and so on to infinity.
  5. So it exists only in the mind.
  6. But a universal in the mind is an understanding of a thing "otherwise than the thing is", and "what is understood otherwise than the thing is is false" (Spade, *Five Texts* p. 23). So universal thoughts would all be false, and universal knowledge (e.g. geometry) impossible.
  - **Boethius's reply:** the principle is ambiguous. Representing a thing *to be* otherwise than it is is false; representing it *in a different manner* from how it exists is not (a written sentence faithfully represents a spoken one). Universals exist in sensible things but are understood apart from them. [source: SEP-UM §§3–4] (The *Augustine* and *Early Scholasticism* chapters give the conclusion only.)
- **Roscelin** (c. 1050–c. 1121): universals are mere words (*voces*). The phrase "breath of the voice" (*flatus vocis*) comes from Anselm's attack on "dialecticians who think universal substances are nothing but *flatus vocis*" (WP quotes the Latin). [source: WP-Roscellinus; already in *Early Scholasticism*]
- **Abelard** (1079–21 April 1142): SEP calls him "arguably, the greatest logician of the Middle Ages and ... equally famous as the first great nominalist philosopher"; his metaphysics is "the first great example of nominalism in the Western tradition", though "nominalism—or, better, irrealism" (also about propositions, events, wholes, etc.). [source: SEP-Ab intro, §2] IEP: "credited as the founder of nominalism for his claim that a universal is a name (*nomen*) or significant word (*sermo*)", and with inspiring the *nominales*. [source: IEP-Ab §2] — On the "sermonism" label: the word *sermo* is verified (IEP); the label "sermonism" itself is not in any source read (see NOT VERIFIED).
- Abelard's argument against real universals (Socrates and Brunellus the ass; contraries in one thing) and his *status* (the common cause of imposition is "being a man", *esse hominem*, "a cause which is a non-thing", compared to "the ship was wrecked because the pilot was absent") — fully in *Early Scholasticism*; cross-ref only. [source: SEP-UM §6; SEP-Ab §2]
- **Avicenna** (980–1037) on the common nature: "Horsehood ... has a definition that does not demand universality. Rather it is that to which universality happens. Hence horsehood itself is nothing but horsehood only. For in itself it is neither many nor one, neither is it existent in these sensibles nor in the soul..." — *Metaphysica* (Latin) V, 1, trans. Spade 1985, as quoted SEP-UM §7 (note 36). Aquinas's *On Being and Essence* c. 2 develops the "absolute consideration" of a nature. Klima's analogy: the same nature in many individuals is like the same book in many copies — no "universal book" over and above the copies. [source: SEP-UM §7] The *Falsafa* chapter gives the Latin *equinitas ergo in se est equinitas tantum*; consistent.
- **Avicenna's essence/existence distinction:** existence is not part of a thing's essence/quiddity (*māhiyya*); knowing what a thing is does not tell you that it is; every thing other than the Necessary Existent is "in itself only possible"; "everything which has a quiddity distinct from being (*anniyya*) is a caused thing" (*Ilāhiyyāt* VIII, 4). [source: SEP-ISM §3] Already in *Falsafa* — cross-ref.
- **Scotus** (1263/66–1308): a realist; the extra-mental "common nature" (*natura communis*) is "indifferent" to existing in any number of individuals, exists outside the mind only in particulars, "contracted" by each one's haecceity (*haecceitas*); humanity itself is common and repeatable and "ontologically prior to any particular exemplification" (*Ordinatio* 2, d. 3, pars 1, qq. 1–6, trans. in Spade 1994). [source: SEP-Scot §3.3] The word "haecceity" was coined by his followers (WP-Haecceity). Formal distinction, "less than numerical unity" — in *Scotus and Ockham* and the medieval overview; cross-ref.
- **Ockham** (c. 1287–1347): does NOT argue against universals from the razor; he holds the realist theories of his day "outright incoherent". Only universal concepts (and derivatively words) are universal, "predicable of many"; he moved from the *fictum* theory to the *intellectio* theory (a universal concept is the act of thinking of several things at once). Two ontological categories only, substance and quality (plus theological exceptions for relation). [source: SEP-Ock §§4.2–4.3]
- **The razor wording:** "must not multiply entities beyond necessity" — "Although the sentiment is certainly Ockham's, this particular formulation is nowhere to be found in his texts." His fullest formulation allows positing entities if supported by reason (self-evident premises), experience, or Scripture. [source: SEP-Ock §4.1] Ockham wrote *Numquam ponenda est pluralitas sine necessitate* (Sentences commentary, I d. 27 q. 2 K) and, in *Summa Logicae* i.12, *Frustra fit per plura quod potest fieri per pauciora*; *Entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem* is "absent in his extant works" and traced to John Punch (1639), who called it a "common axiom"; the phrase "Ockham's razor" (*novacula occami*) is claimed by Libert Froidmont (1649). "Pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate" was a scholastic commonplace (it appears in Scotus). [source: WP-Occam's_razor] Already in *Scotus and Ockham*; cross-ref and use as a meta-ontology point (parsimony as a theoretical virtue: SEP-Nom §2.2; IEP-Univ §2c).

## 6. The modern menu (NEW; worked examples and objections)

- **Predicate/austere nominalism**: a is red because "is red" is truly said of a; leaves it "a brute fact". IEP: this refusal "has even landed Predicate Nominalism the label 'Ostrich Nominalism.'" [source: IEP-Univ §3a; SEP-Nom §4.2]
- **Class nominalism**: redness just is the class of red things (or of possible red things: Quinton 1957, Lewis 1983). [source: SEP-Nom §4.2]
- **Resemblance nominalism**: classification is governed by primitive resemblance facts (Rodriguez-Pereyra 2002). [source: SEP-Nom §4.2] Book: Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra, *Resemblance Nominalism: A Solution to the Problem of Universals* (Oxford: OUP, 2002); he is Professor of Metaphysics at Oxford (Oriel). [source: WP-Gonzalo_Rodriguez-Pereyra]
  - Objections (IEP, worked examples): (1) **coextension**: if everything with a heart has a kidney, the resemblance classes coincide, so "having a heart" = "having a kidney" — false; (2) **imperfect community / wrong respect**: the ripe red apple also resembles an unripe green apple of the same kind, so the "red" class lets in non-red things, and restricting resemblance to "the right way" is circular; (3) **resemblance regress**: resemblance itself looks like a universal, so the view collapses into realism (classically Russell's point). [source: IEP-Univ §3b] Note: IEP's objections are to a simple version; Rodriguez-Pereyra's book is designed to answer them (not verified in detail here — say only that he defends the view against these objections).
- **Trope theory**: tropes are particular property-instances (this rose's redness), "things like the particular shape, weight, and texture of an individual object"; two objects "share" a shade of green when each has a greenness-trope and the tropes "exactly resemble each other". [source: SEP-Trope intro] "The father of the contemporary debate on tropes was D. C. Williams (1953a; 1953b ...)": a one-category ontology of tropes, bundle theory of concrete particulars, resemblance-class theory of universals — "the so-called 'standard' view". [source: SEP-Trope §1] Williams, "On the Elements of Being: I", *Review of Metaphysics* 7(1): 3–18; "II", 7(2): 171–92 (1953). [source: SEP-Trope bib; WP-Trope] Donald Cary Williams (28 May 1899 – 16 January 1983), Harvard; his work was studied closely in Sydney (Armstrong, Keith Campbell). [source: WP-Donald_Cary_Williams] Williams's "aboriginal" sense of "abstract": "partial, incomplete, or fragmentary, the trait of what is less than its including whole" (Williams 1953a: 15). [source: SEP-Trope §2] Keith Campbell, *Abstract Particulars* (Blackwell, 1990). [source: IEP-Univ refs]
  - Motivations: perception ("I don't see color in general, but the color of this object") and causation (the stove's temperature-trope burns your hand); but SEP says these give weak support because states of affairs could do the same. [source: SEP-Trope intro, §4]
  - Objection: trope resemblance is itself left brute: "The very relation we sought to understand reappears as our answer." [source: IEP-Univ §3c] Reply: tropes avoid resemblance nominalism's bad classes, since each trope has only one nature, and resemblance can itself be treated as tropes. [source: IEP-Univ §3c]
- **Conceptualism**: generality is in concepts; objection — what makes "red" correctly apply to a and b but not c? Either brute (back to predicate nominalism) or a shared feature (back to realism): it "teeters" between the two. [source: IEP-Univ §3d]
- IEP's conclusion: "A consensus does seem to be emerging ... that two genuine contenders are left: Strong Realism and Trope Nominalism." (Present as IEP authors' judgement.) [source: IEP-Univ §4]
- **Armstrong** (D. M. Armstrong, 8 July 1926 – 13 May 2014): immanent realism — universals exist, but no uninstantiated Platonic universals; *Universals and Scientific Realism* (2 vols, CUP, 1978: vol. I *Nominalism and Realism*, vol. II *A Theory of Universals*); *What Is a Law of Nature?* (CUP 1983); *Universals: An Opinionated Introduction* (Westview, 1989); *A World of States of Affairs* (CUP 1997). States of affairs (a particular's instantiating a universal) are "*the* fundamental structures in reality". [source: WP-David_Malet_Armstrong; SEP-Prop bib] SEP-Prop: Armstrong is "the best-known contemporary supporter" of a minimalist (sparse) conception: properties exist only if instantiated, are instantiated in space-time, and their existence "must be sanctioned by microphysics". [source: SEP-Prop §2] Laws of nature as necessitation relations between universals (Armstrong, Tooley, Dretske; "all ravens are black" example from Mumford). [source: WP-Armstrong]
- **Sparse vs abundant** (Lewis 1983, 1986 following Bealer 1982): sparse properties "cut nature at its joints" and are found a posteriori by science (negative charge, spin; not caloric); abundant ones include "grue", "disliked by Socrates". [source: SEP-Prop §2]
- Newer strict nominalisms: mereological (redness = fusion of all red things), reistic (Brentano), causal, grounding, second-order nominalism (Jones 2018; Trueman 2020). [source: SEP-Nom §4.2]
- Bradley's regress (relations of instantiation need further relations) "deeply worries ontologists". [source: SEP-Prop §2.3] Useful link to Nyāya's "self-linking" inherence (§10).

## 7. Abstract objects (CROSS-REF *Reason, Experience and Innate Ideas* for Benacerraf; brief)

- Paradigms: numbers and pure sets are abstract (if they exist); rocks, trees and people concrete; there is "no standard account" of how to draw the line. Other candidates: properties, propositions, types, possible worlds. [source: SEP-AO §1] The "way of negation": abstract = lacking features of paradigm concreta (Frege-inspired: non-mental and non-sensible; commonly non-spatiotemporal, causally inert). [source: SEP-AO §3.5]
- The abstract/concrete distinction "is not an ancient one"; Plato's Forms were "causes par excellence", whereas abstract objects are supposed causally inert; originally a distinction among words (abstract noun "whiteness" v. concrete "white"), transposed to ideas in the 17th c. (Locke's general triangle, *Essay* IV.vii.9). [source: SEP-AO §2.1]
- Arguments for abstracta: "seven is a prime number" is true; Fregean (singular terms in true sentences refer), truthmaker and Quinean routes; Quine–Putnam indispensability (Putnam 1971). [source: SEP-Nom §3; SEP-OC §1.1]
- Arguments against: Benacerraf 1973's epistemic challenge ("places them beyond the reach of the better understood means of human cognition", p. 668), Field's version (1980, 1989); the Eleatic principle (to exist is to be causally active); parsimony; Benacerraf 1965's non-uniqueness problem (is 2 {{0}} or {0,{0}}?). [source: SEP-Nom §2] Benacerraf is in *Reason, Experience and Innate Ideas* — one sentence and a link.
- Fictionalism: Hartry Field, *Science without Numbers: A Defence of Nominalism* (Princeton UP / Blackwell, 1980). [source: SEP bibliographies]

## 8. Quine's criterion of ontological commitment (NEW; core argument)

- "On What There Is", *Review of Metaphysics* 2(5), September 1948, pp. 21–38; reprinted in *From a Logical Point of View* (1953). [source: WS-2; SEP-OC cites "Quine 1948: 33"; WP-Plato's_beard gives RM 2(5), 1948]
- Criterion (verbatim): "A theory is committed to those and only those entities to which the bound variables of the theory must be capable of referring in order that the affirmations made in the theory be true." (Quine 1948: 33.) [source: SEP-OC §1.1] (Also quoted in the published *Analytic Philosophy since 1950*.) "Quine's criterion ... has dominated ontological discussion in analytic philosophy since the middle of the 20th century; it deserves to be called the orthodox view." [source: SEP-OC §1.1]
- Slogan: "To be, as Quine said, is to be the value of a variable (Quine 1948)." [source: SEP-Fict] WS gives the slogan as "[t]o be is to be the value of a variable" (Quine 1948: 34), and the longer 1948 sentence "To be assumed as an entity is, purely and simply, to be reckoned as the value of a variable." [source: WS-2, WS-3] — page not checked against the text; cite without page.
- Method: regiment the theory in first-order logic; names eliminated (Russell's theory of descriptions; "Socrates" → predicate "is-Socrates"/"Socratizes"); avoid commitment by finding an acceptable **paraphrase** without the questionable entities (Quine 1948: 32). Criterion is descriptive (what a theory says there is), distinct from Quine's prescriptive method (accept the best theory, then accept its commitments). [source: SEP-OC §§1.1–1.2]
- Quine on predicates: ordinary subject–predicate sentences carry no commitment to properties; hence the criterion served "a traditional nominalist agenda". [source: SEP-OC §1.1; SEP-Prop §1.2] But Quine's own ontology: physical objects (the content of any portion of space-time) and **sets** (needed for mathematics); no properties, propositions or possibilia because their identity criteria are unclear — "no entity without identity" (1969: 23). [source: SEP-Q]
- **The Pegasus puzzle ("Plato's beard") — set out step by step** (premises from SEP-NEO; Quine's wording from WP/WS):
  1. "Pegasus does not exist" is true, so meaningful.
  2. If meaningful, its singular term "Pegasus" is meaningful.
  3. A meaningful singular term denotes something.
  4. If "Pegasus" denotes something, then "Pegasus does not exist" is false.
  5. So if it is true it is false. [source: SEP-NEO]
  - Quine: "This is the old Platonic riddle of nonbeing. Nonbeing must in some sense be, otherwise what is it that there is not? This tangled doctrine might be nicknamed Plato's beard; historically it has proved tough, frequently dulling the edge of Occam's razor." [source: WP-Plato's_beard quoting Quine 1948; WS-1]
  - **Two bad answers**: McX (Pegasus is an idea in the mind — "an elementary confusion" between Pegasus and the Pegasus-idea) and Wyman (Pegasus "has his being as an unactualized possible"; SEP calls Wyman "the fictional Meinongian"). [source: WS-2; SEP-OC §1.x] Quine: "to give Wyman the word 'exist'. I'll try not to use it again; I still have 'is'" (Quine 1948: 23). [source: SEP-OC] Against Wyman's possibles: "the possible fat man in that doorway; and, again, the possible bald man in that doorway" — are they one possible man or two? (no identity criteria). [source: WS-2] His "overpopulated universe ... offends the aesthetic sense of us who have a taste for desert landscapes". [source: WS-1 (fragment "It offends the aesthetic sense of us who have a taste for desert landscapes, but this is not the worst of it.")]
  - **Quine's answer**: Russell's theory of descriptions — treat "Pegasus" as a description ("the winged horse captured by Bellerophon"/"the thing that pegasizes") and paraphrase "Pegasus does not exist" as "it is not the case that something is uniquely F"; the singular term disappears, so premise 3 fails to apply. SEP-NEO gives the strategy with "the flying horse from Greek mythology" and Vulcan. [source: SEP-NEO; WP-Plato's_beard ("Russell's theory of 'singular descriptions' ... is supposed to 'detangle' Plato's beard")] (The verb "pegasizes" itself NOT verified — see below.)
  - **Objections**: (a) Meinongians (Parsons 1980, Zalta 1983) and Priest (2005, quantifier "utterly ontologically neutral") reject the "there is"="exists" identification. [source: SEP-NEO] (b) The criterion is "hostage" to disputed translations into logic; different paraphrases give different commitments. [source: SEP-OC intro] (c) Ideological commitment (primitive predicates) may matter as much as ontology (Sider 2011). [source: SEP-OC §1.1]
- Opening of the essay (WS-1 fragments, verified only via search snippet): "A curious thing about the ontological problem is its simplicity. It can be put in three Anglo-Saxon monosyllables: 'What is there?'" The well-known continuation ("It can be answered, moreover, in a word—'Everything'") was NOT confirmed (see below).

## 9. Carnap's internal/external distinction and the Quine–Carnap debate

- Carnap, "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology", *Revue Internationale de Philosophie* 4(11) (1950): 20–40; reprinted in *Meaning and Necessity*, 2nd edn (1956): 205–221. Written "calmly" in response to Ryle's "nastiest and most vituperative" review of *Meaning and Necessity*. [source: SEP-Carnap §7 and bibliography]
- **Internal** existence questions are asked within a specified language framework and get straightforward answers ("are there infinitely many numbers?" in Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory: trivially yes). **External** questions ("whether something exists period") "have no cognitive significance" taken literally; read charitably they are practical questions of framework choice ("Shall we introduce this form of expression into our language?"). [source: SEP-Carnap §7; supplement H]
- Carnap's tolerance (ESO: 221): "To decree dogmatic prohibitions of certain linguistic forms instead of testing them by their success or failure in practical use, is worse than futile ... Let us be cautious in making assertions and critical in examining them, but tolerant in permitting linguistic forms." [source: SEP-Carnap supplement H]
- Quine rejected the internal/external distinction because it relied on the analytic/synthetic distinction (accepting a framework = accepting its analytic sentences). Whether it really needs analyticity is now debated (Yablo 1998; Thomasson 2014). For years Quine was thought to have "won"; since the 1980s–90s Carnap revival the picture "looks rather different". [source: SEP-Carnap §7; supplement H]
- Neo-Carnapian deflationism: Yablo, Thomasson (2009, 2014, 2016), Hirsch (2011), Hofweber (2016); the collection Chalmers, Manley and Wasserman (2009). [source: SEP-Carnap supplement H] Already summarised in *Logical Positivism* and *Analytic Philosophy since 1950*; here teach it as the meta-ontological rival to Quine.

## 10. Meta-ontology, easy ontology, grounding (brief)

- David J. Chalmers, David Manley and Ryan Wasserman (eds.), *Metametaphysics: New Essays on the Foundations of Ontology* (Oxford: OUP, 2009); contains Chalmers, "Ontological Anti-Realism" (pp. 77–129) and Schaffer, "On What Grounds What" (pp. 347–383). [source: SEP bibliographies (Gr, Nom, Mer)]
- **Easy ontology** (Amie Thomasson, *Ontology Made Easy*, OUP 2015): existence questions are answered easily, by empirical evidence or "merely by the rules of use" of general terms — when "application conditions" are fulfilled (Thomasson 2015, 86, 89–95); she takes the approach to be inherited from Carnap. [source: SEP-AO §3.7.1] Thomasson b. 4 July 1968, Dartmouth. [source: WP-Amie_Thomasson] Worked example for the chapter: from "there are five chairs" infer "there is a number of chairs" — present as illustrative of the easy approach only (not quoted from a source).
- **Grounding**: Schaffer: "metaphysics as I understand it is about what grounds what" rather than what exists (2009: 379); Fine: "if considerations of ground were abolished, then very little of the subject would remain" (2012a: 41). Grounding is a constitutive (not causal) "in virtue of" relation (worked example: the truckers are on strike in virtue of picketing). [source: SEP-Gr intro] Schaffer's approach is "neo-Aristotelian": contentious entities (composites, abstracta, fictional characters) exist and the real question is which are fundamental; Swiss-cheese holes as derivative; "priority monism" (the cosmos is the one fundamental entity). [source: WP-Jonathan_Schaffer] Nominalism can also be redefined as a thesis about fundamentality rather than existence. [source: SEP-Nom §1]

## 11. Do tables exist? Mereology and the special composition question (NEW; step-by-step)

- Van Inwagen posed the **Special Composition Question** (SCQ) in a 1987 article and in *Material Beings* (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1990): under what conditions do some things compose something? He credits Hestevold (1981) with an earlier formulation. Form of answer: "for any xs ... there is a further material object, y, composed of those xs if and only if ____". [source: IEP-MC §2; SEP-Mer §4.1; WP-Peter_van_Inwagen bibliography (1990)]
- Answers: **nihilism** (composition never occurs; only simples; "there really are no tables. There are only fundamental physical simples spatially arranged" — Unger, Dorr, Cameron; Rosen and Dorr 2002; Sider 2013); **universalism** (any things whatever compose something, so "trout-turkeys" and "umbrella-laptops" exist — Lewis, Sider; "mereological universalism"); **restricted** answers, incl. van Inwagen's **organicism**: composition occurs only when the activity of the xs "constitutes a life" (1990: ch. 9), so there are elementary particles and living organisms but no tables or chairs; "there are tables" is acceptable as "there are simples arranged tablewise". [source: SEP-Mer §4.5; WP-Mereological_nihilism; WP-Peter_van_Inwagen] The labels "nihilism" and "universalism" follow van Inwagen 1990. [source: SEP-Mer §4.5 and n. 45]
- SEP-Mer: nihilism "reflects a venerable form of ontological minimalism that can be traced back to Greek atomism and early Buddhist nominalism"; on Buddhist minimalism and contemporary mereology see Westerhoff (2013), Jones (2021), Siderits (2022, ch. 4). [source: SEP-Mer §4.5 n. 44] — a good bridge to §13.
- **Argument from vagueness (Lewis 1986 *On the Plurality of Worlds*: 211–13; Lewis 1991; Sider 2001 §4.9) — step by step:**
  1. If composition is restricted, it must follow some intuitive principle (contact, bonding, life …).
  2. Any such principle is vague: there are sorites series of cases from clear composition to clear non-composition.
  3. But whether something exists cannot be vague ("oddness comes in degrees whereas parthood and existence cannot be a matter of degree").
  4. So any sharp cut-off would be arbitrary/brute: "no restriction on composition can be vague, but unless it is vague, it cannot fit the intuitive desiderata" (Lewis 1986b: 213).
  5. So composition is either never restricted in the existence of sums (universalism) or never happens (nihilism). [source: SEP-Mer §4.5]
  - Objections: Merricks 2005, Nolan 2006, Smith 2006, Korman 2010/2015; universalism is counter-intuitive. Reply: our discomfort with trout-turkeys reflects "psychological biases" with no bearing on the world's structure. [source: SEP-Mer §4.5]
- **Overdetermination argument (Merricks, *Objects and Persons*, 2001) — step by step:** a baseball shatters a window; the atoms "arranged baseballwise" are causally sufficient; if the baseball also caused it, the shattering would be overdetermined; overdetermination is objectionable; a baseball, if it existed, would have causal powers; so there are no baseballs. Merricks himself is a quasi-nihilist (he accepts human beings). Replies: accept systematic overdetermination (unpalatable to most); Thomasson argues the argument wrongly treats the baseball as something over and above its parts. [source: IEP-MC] (Title *Objects and Persons* not verified — cite "Merricks 2001".)
- Objection to organicism: vagueness of "life" (when does an inhaled carbon atom become part of the cat?). [source: WP-Mereological_nihilism]
- Material constitution (statue and clay) belongs to *Identity, Change and Persons* — do not steal.

## 12. Indian realism: Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika (CROSS-REF *Classical Indian Schools*)

- *Padārtha* = "thing denoted by a word" (category). Vaiśeṣika (and Nyāya, which adopted it) lists **substance (*dravya*), quality (*guṇa*), action (*karma*), universal (*sāmānya* or *jāti*), individuator (*viśeṣa*), inherence (*samavāya*)**, with **absence (*abhāva*)** added later (by Śrīdhara, Udayana, Śivāditya per WP). [source: IEP-Nyaya §2; WP-Padārtha; WP-Vaisheshika] Nyāya's own scheme has sixteen *padārthas*, the second (*prameya*) including the Vaiśeṣika categories. [source: WP-Padārtha] Praśastapāda's *Padārthadharmasaṃgraha* (c. 4th c. per WP) is the next major Vaiśeṣika work after the *Vaiśeṣika-sūtra*. [source: WP-Vaisheshika] The *Nyāya-sūtra* is attributed to Gautama (c. 200 CE per IEP; dating very uncertain). [source: IEP-Nyaya §1]
- Qualities are **tropes**: "Qualities (*guṇa*) are property tropes which qualify substances. Unlike universals they are not repeatable." The red of a fire hydrant is particular; the universal redness inheres in it. Universals inhere in substances (pot-hood), qualities (redness) and motions. Nyāya argues universals are needed for recurring experience, for language, and for causal regularities (relations between universals). [source: IEP-Nyaya §2b, 2d] → Striking point for the chapter: Nyāya combines tropes AND universals (universals inhere in tropes).
- **Udayana's six "impediments" to a genuine universal** (otherwise a mere *upādhi*, imposed property): (i) must have more than one instance (no "spacehood"); (ii) two universals with exactly the same instances are one; (iii) cross-division: two apparent universals that share an instance without one being subsumed under the other are both mere *upādhis* ("the most controversial", suggesting natural kinds); (iv) no universal whose acceptance leads to infinite regress ("universal-hood"); (v) none for individuators; (vi) none for inherence (regress). [source: IEP-Nyaya §2d] Udayana 11th c. [source: PN epistemology--reason-and-experience]
- **Inherence (*samavāya*)** relates qualities, actions, universals and individuators to substances, universals to qualities and actions, "and ... wholes to their parts" ("my car, a substance, is a single entity, which inheres in its various parts. Thus, your touching just one part of my car is enough to justify the claim 'you touched my car'"). Nyāya makes inherence "self-linking" to block Bradley-type regresses, which Śaṅkara (c. 8th–9th c.) pressed in India. [source: IEP-Nyaya §2e] Individuators (*viśeṣa*) "may be conceived as roughly analogous to haecceities". [source: IEP-Nyaya §2f]
- Perception of universals: Uddyotakara's six sense–object connections include "inherence in what inheres in what is conjoined" (sense faculty → universal in a quality-trope). [source: IEP-Nyaya §1]
- B. K. Matilal (modern) speculated that Nyāya's doctrine of non-overlapping universals (*jāti*, also "caste") might reflect a Brahmanical ideal of no "intermixture of classes" — already in *Modern Chinese and Indian Philosophy*. Mahāyāna chapter: since *jāti* means both "universal" and "caste", Dharmakīrti's attack undercut caste metaphysics. Note link with Udayana's cross-division rule (iii).
- **Wholes (*avayavin*)**: For Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika a whole is "a genuinely new thing that inheres in its parts" (the *Classical Indian Schools* chapter). Nyāya's arguments (WS-4, weak): there is a whole "because we can hold, pull, etc."; if only atoms existed, nothing would be perceived, since atoms are imperceptible; the regress of parts stops at partless atoms. [source: WS-4 — treat as unverified detail; the general position is verified in IEP-Nyaya §2e and SEP-MIB]

## 13. Buddhist nominalism and anti-holism (CROSS-REF *Early Buddhism*, *Mahāyāna*)

- Reductionism: "just as words such as 'chariot' are used only when certain parts are arranged together ... so also an individual (*satto*) is nothing but a conventional designation that applies to the five aggregates" (Questions of King Milinda). [source: SEP-MIB] Abhidharma distinguishes conventional (*saṃvṛti*) from ultimate (*paramārtha*) expressions; apparent wholes are reduced to momentary *dharmas*. [source: SEP-Abhidharma (https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/abhidharma/, Noa Ronkin); SEP-Chinese-metaphysics (https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-metaphysics/)] The chariot belongs to *Early Buddhism* (Vajirā, Nāgasena) and *Identity* — cite only.
- Vasubandhu (4th–5th c. CE) in the *Twenty Verses*: an external object would be a whole (*avayavin*) over and above its parts, separate atoms, or an aggregate; wholes are rejected by "the Buddhist reductionist principle which says that only the component parts of an entity are real"; atoms joined on six sides have parts; a partless atom could not give "light on one side and shade on the other". [source: SEP-Vasu §4.2; SEP-MIB] Already set out step by step in *Mahāyāna*; here only as the Buddhist reply to Nyāya's *avayavin*.
- **Dharmakīrti against universals** (Dharmakīrti dated c. 6th or 7th c.; Krasser 2012 mid-6th c.; Balcerowicz 2016 550–610; agnosticism "still seems to be warranted"). [source: SEP-Dk n. 3–4] *Pramāṇavārttika* I.152 (Tillemans's translation): "It [i.e., the universal present in one instance] does not go [to another], it was not there [before the instances], nor is it there after [them], nor does it have parts. [And even when in other places] it does not leave the previous locus. Oh my! It's just one disaster after another." [source: SEP-Dk §1.1] Paṇḍit Aśoka's *Sāmānyadūṣaṇa*: "One can clearly see five fingers in one's own hand. One who commits himself to a sixth general entity fingerhood, side by side with the five fingers, might as well postulate horns on top of his head." (trans. in Chakrabarti and Siderits's introduction to Siderits, Tillemans, Chakrabarti 2011). [source: SEP-Dk §1.1, n. 8]
- **Dharmakīrti's one-many argument — step by step** (SEP-Dk §1.1):
  1. If a universal is really present in several particulars, it shares their natures.
  2. Those natures are many and different.
  3. So the universal would itself be many — not one universal.
  4. If instead it is a "radically separate entity" (*arthāntara*) that stays one, it is not really the nature of the things, contrary to our saying several things have the same property.
  5. Nyāya's link, inherence, is "an occult entity, posited ad hoc and without any discernible effects".
  6. Criterion of the real: what has "causal powers" (*arthakriyāsamartha*); universals have none. So universals are not real.
  - **Nyāya reply** (IEP): universals are perceived through inherence in instances, are needed for language and causal law, and inherence is self-linking (no regress); Udayana's rules show universals are not posited "ad hoc" for every predicate. [source: IEP-Nyaya §§1, 2d–e] SEP-Dk: realists "will predictably maintain that they are untouched because universals are not the kind of things that need to be in one place at one time". [source: SEP-Dk §1.1] — parallels the IEP "strangeness objection" in the West (§2).
- **Apoha** ("exclusion"): Dignāga (c. 480–c. 540), *Pramāṇasamuccaya* ch. 5 and *Sāmānyaparīkṣā*: "A word talks about entities only as they are qualified by the negation of other things"; blue things are "non-non-blue" (*anyāpoha*). Quasi-universals, since absences are less real. Kumārila (fl. c. 660, Mīmāṃsā) and Uddyotakara (Nyāya, c. 6th c.) charged circularity: to understand non-non-blue you must already understand blue. Dharmakīrti's reply (tu quoque) and his causal version (perceptions of blue things cause the "same judgment"; a forerunner of causal theories of reference — Kripke, Putnam). Modern formal reconstructions: Herzberger 1975, Siderits 1995/2005; parallel with Saussure. [source: SEP-Dk §2.2; SEP-Kum intro; WP-Udyotakara] Already in *Mahāyāna* (quotation, objection and reply) — cross-ref and keep to two or three sentences.
- Kumārila on universals and inherence: criticised inherence as regress-generating and held universal and particular to be aspects of one thing; linguistic universals (and phonemes) eternal. [source: SEP-Kum §3.2; PN reason-and-experience notes]

## 14. China: names and kinds (CROSS-REF *Hundred Schools*, *Logic across Traditions*)

- The "School of Names" (*ming jia*) is a Han archivists' label for diverse Warring States thinkers (Deng Xi, Yin Wen, Hui Shi, Gongsun Long); "a taxonomical fiction". [source: SEP-SN intro] Gongsun Long c. 320–250 BCE, retainer to the Lord of Pingyuan in Zhao. [source: SEP-SN §6]
- "White horse is not horse": an early influential interpretation (Fung 1958; Cheng 1983) read it as about universals, but "There is now a fairly broad consensus, at least among European and American scholars, that the text is unlikely to concern universals, since no ancient Chinese philosopher held a realist doctrine of universals." Alternatives: kind and identity relations (Cikoski; Harbsmeier), part–whole (Hansen 1983; Graham 1989), extension of phrases (Hansen 1992), use/mention (Thompson 1995). [source: SEP-SN §6.1] Already in *Logic across Traditions* and *Hundred Schools*.
- "Discourse on Pointing at Things" (*Zhiwu lun*): "an enigmatic discussion on reference and the referent"; paradox "when no thing is not the pointed-out, to point out is not to point out". [source: WP-Gongsun_Long] (SEP gives no reconstruction; keep to one sentence.)
- **Mohist kinds (*lèi* 類)**: three types of names — "all-reaching" (*wù*, "thing"), "kind" names (horse) that "proceed to" all relevantly similar things, and "personal" names; using a name says a thing is "the same" (*tóng*) as other things of that kind. Four kinds of sameness: identity, part–whole (*tǐ*/*jiān*), being together, and similarity of kind. [source: SEP-MC] Fraser: the realism/conceptualism/nominalism taxonomy "is unhelpful in understanding classical Chinese thought ... the various positions ... all tend to fall roughly under the label of nominalism, appealing neither to universals nor to concepts"; better plotted from realism about kind distinctions (later Mohists lean toward "a brand of realism": the world fixes the similarities) through Xunzi's "pragmatic conventionalism" to the *Zhuangzi*'s perspectivism. [source: SEP-MC]
- The Mohist Canons discuss "mereological ontology". [source: SEP-MC intro]

## 15. Islamic world (CROSS-REF *Falsafa*, *Al-Ghazālī and Averroes*)

- Kalām physics was atomistic (from the Muʿtazila — Abū al-Hudhayl, al-Jubbāʾī — not only Ashʿarites); atoms are minimal space-occupying units with no parts; differences between bodies come from **accidents** inhering in homogeneous atoms; "no less than twenty-two different kinds of accidents", incl. colour, life and "ceasing to exist". Pines (1936) thought early atoms point-like; Dhanani (1994) argues both early and later atoms are like Epicurean minimal parts. Earlier theologians held engenderment (*tawallud*); "most later theologians adopted an occasionalist worldview", reserving all agency to God. [source: SEP-AIN §1; WP-Atomism] Al-Ashʿarī lists three accounts of time. [source: SEP-AIN §1.2]
- Avicenna's essence/existence and "horseness is just horseness": §5 above.
- Interesting contrast for the chapter: kalām is a radical "only atoms and accidents" ontology driven by theology (God's continuous creation), unlike Western nominalism's parsimony motive.

## Debates
- **How to read the Third Man**: which of One-over-Many, Self-Predication, Non-Identity Plato should drop; whether the problem is epistemic (Vlastos, Fine) or logical; whether Plato thought it fatal. [source: SEP-Parm §4.3] (Plato chapter covers this.)
- **Was Aristotle a trope theorist?** "this pale" in the *Categories* as a trope (Ackrill; Kampa & Wilkins) or a shareable determinate shade (Owen 1965). [source: SEP-Trope §1]
- **What is Abelard's *status*?** Klima: a real non-thing; Guilfoy: no item in the ontology at all (per the *Early Scholasticism* chapter). And: "nominalist" or better "irrealist" (King/SEP). [source: SEP-Ab §2]
- **Ockham: nominalist or conceptualist?** IEP calls his version "conceptualism" (per *Scotus and Ockham* chapter); SEP calls it nominalism (Panaccio's characterisation). [source: SEP-Ock §4]
- **Realism v. trope nominalism v. resemblance nominalism** today: IEP authors see an emerging two-way contest (Strong Realism v. Trope Nominalism); SEP-Nom treats nominalism as "still a popular view" with many varieties; SEP-Prop openly "take[s] for granted" realism. [source: IEP-Univ §4; SEP-Prop §1.3]
- **Transcendent v. immanent realism**: recent defences of transcendentism (Alvarado 2020, Berman 2020, Tugby 2022); argument that immanentism makes grounding circular (Alvarado, Costa, Raven) with replies (Imaguire 2021; Giordani & Tremolanti 2022). [source: SEP-Prop §1.3]
- **Quine v. Carnap**: was Quine's victory real? Mainstream view since the 1980s–90s revival is more favourable to Carnap; whether internal/external needs analyticity (Yablo 1998; Thomasson). [source: SEP-Carnap §7; supplement H]
- **Is ontology substantive?** Quinean/neo-Aristotelian (Schaffer: grounding; Sider: structure) v. deflationists (Thomasson, Hirsch, Yablo). [source: SEP-Carnap supplement H; SEP-Gr; WP-Schaffer]
- **SCQ**: nihilism v. universalism v. organicism; the vagueness argument and its critics (Merricks 2005, Korman). [source: SEP-Mer §4.5; IEP-MC]
- **Apoha**: top-down (Dignāga; Matilal, Herzberger, Siderits) v. bottom-up causal (Dharmakīrti) readings; whether circularity objection is answered (Hugon 2011 v. Hale 2011). [source: SEP-Dk §2.2]
- **White horse**: about universals (Fung) or not (current Western consensus); kinds, part–whole, mass nouns (Hansen, contested). [source: SEP-SN §6.1]
- **Kalām atoms**: point-like (Pines 1936) or minimal extended parts (Dhanani 1994). [source: SEP-AIN §1]
- **Dharmakīrti's dates**: 6th v. 7th century. [source: SEP-Dk n. 3]

## Surprises (commonly believed, contradicted by sources)
- The word "ontology" is not ancient: earliest known printed use is Lorhard's *Ogdoas Scholastica* (1606), then Goclenius (1613); "metaphysics" is an editor's title, not Aristotle's word. [WP; SEP-Met]
- Aristotle, not Plato, named the "Third Man". [SEP-Parm]
- Ockham did not argue against universals with his razor (he thought realism incoherent), and never wrote "entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity". [SEP-Ock; WP]
- "Flatus vocis" is Anselm's hostile description, not Roscelin's own phrase. [WP-Roscellinus]
- Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika already had tropes (qualities are unrepeatable property-instances) *and* universals inhering in them — a combination modern Western debates usually treat as rivals. [IEP-Nyaya]
- Udayana's criteria for a genuine universal anticipate the sparse/abundant distinction: not every predicate names a universal. [IEP-Nyaya; SEP-Prop]
- Dharmakīrti's causal account of apoha resembles modern causal theories of reference (Kripke, Putnam). [SEP-Dk]
- Classical Chinese thought, per Fraser, did not have a realism-about-universals debate at all; the white horse is probably not about universals. [SEP-MC; SEP-SN]
- Quine was a nominalist about properties but a realist about sets (and so abstract objects). [SEP-Q]
- Mereological nihilism ("there are no tables") is a serious, growing position, and is traced by Varzi to Greek atomism and early Buddhist nominalism. [SEP-Mer]
