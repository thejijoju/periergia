## 1. Life and works

- Born 22 April 1724, Königsberg, capital of East Prussia (now Kaliningrad, Russia); a major commercial centre, military port and "relatively cosmopolitan university town". Died 12 February 1804 in Königsberg, "just short of his eightieth birthday". [sources: SEP-K §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant]
- Baptized "Emanuel", later spelled "Immanuel"; fourth of nine children. Father a master harness maker; mother (Anna Regina Reuter) daughter of a harness maker, better educated than most women of her class. Parents Pietist (Johann Georg and Anna Regina, per IEP); mother died 1737, father 1746. [sources: WP-Immanuel_Kant; SEP-K §1; IEP-K §1]
- Pietist Collegium Fridericianum ages 8–15 (graduated summer 1740); reacted against its "forced soul-searching", took refuge in Latin classics. Kuehn: the parents' artisan values ("hard work, honesty, cleanliness, and independence") mattered more than their Pietism. [source: SEP-K §1 citing Kuehn 2001; WP]
- University of Königsberg (the Albertina) from 1740 (aged 16); favourite teacher Martin Knutzen (1713–1751), a Pietist influenced by Wolff and Locke, who introduced him to Newton. [sources: SEP-K §1; WP]
- First publication *Thoughts on the True Estimation of Living Forces*: SEP dates it 1747; WP says published 1749 (written 1745–47). Give "1747 (printed 1749)" or avoid the year. [sources: SEP-K §1; WP]
- Private tutor outside Königsberg after leaving c. August 1748; **returned August 1754**; began teaching at the Albertina 1755 as unsalaried lecturer (Privatdozent), paid by students, until 1770; average about 20 hours of lectures a week (logic, metaphysics, ethics, mathematics, physics, physical geography). [sources: WP (Kuehn 2001: 94, 98); SEP-K §1; IEP-K §1]
- **Nebular hypothesis:** *Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens* (1755), "written and published anonymously"; developed what became the nebular hypothesis; the printer went bankrupt and the book had little immediate impact. Kant credited a 1751 review of Thomas Wright's *An Original Theory* (1750) as inspiration. Laplace independently proposed a similar model in 1796. (Swedenborg proposed parts of a nebular theory in 1734.) [sources: SEP-K §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Natural_History_and_Theory_of_the_Heavens ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebular_hypothesis]
- Latin dissertations 1755: *On Fire* (Magister) and *New Elucidation of the First Principles of Metaphysical Cognition* (licence to teach); *Physical Monadology* 1756. Three papers on the 1755 Lisbon earthquake in 1756. Turned down for the logic-and-metaphysics chair in 1758 (petitioned Empress Elizabeth during the Russian occupation, 1758–62). [sources: SEP-K §1; WP]
- 1762–64 works: *Only Possible Argument in Support of a Demonstration of the Existence of God* (1762–3); Prize Essay (*Inquiry Concerning the Distinctness of the Principles of Natural Theology and Morality*), second to Moses Mendelssohn, published 1764; *Negative Magnitudes* (1763); *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and the Sublime* (1764), "mainly with alleged differences in the tastes of men and women and of people from different cultures"; his interleaved copy's notes show Rousseau's "deep influence" on his moral thought in the mid-1760s. *Dreams of a Spirit-Seer* (1766) satirises Swedenborg and rationalist metaphysics. [source: SEP-K §1]
- 1770: chair of logic and metaphysics at age 46 (had declined Erlangen 1769 and Jena 1770); sublibrarian from 1766. Inaugural Dissertation (*On the Form and Principles of the Sensible and Intelligible World*, 1770): distinguishes sensibility and understanding; space and time "not something objective and real" but "subjective and ideal" (2:403); a short-lived "Platonism" of an intelligible world. He gave Germany's first anthropology course. [sources: SEP-K §1; IEP-K §2a]
- **The "silent decade":** IEP-K: after 1770 Kant "published hardly anything for more than a decade (this period is referred to as his 'silent decade')". SEP-K: he "spent a decade working on the *Critique of Pure Reason* and published nothing else of significance between 1770 and 1781". Letter to Marcus Herz, 21 February 1772, poses the key question: how can a representation "refer to an object without being in any way affected by it" (10:130–131). [sources: IEP-K §1; SEP-K §1, §2.2]
- **Critique of Pure Reason**: 1st edn (A) 1781, Riga, publisher Johann Friedrich Hartknoch; 2nd, substantially revised edn (B) 1787. Cited by A/B pagination. [sources: SEP-K; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critique_of_Pure_Reason]
- Early reviews few and "uncomprehending"; the Göttingen review (Garve's text, drastically shortened and edited by Feder, published anonymously January 1782) likened transcendental idealism to Berkeley's idealism; Kant replied in the *Prolegomena*. [sources: SEP-K §3.1 and n. 9; SEP-TI §2.1]
- *Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics* 1783; *Idea for a Universal History* and "What is Enlightenment?" 1784 (cross-ref *The Enlightenment and Rousseau*); *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* 1785; *Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science* 1786; B edition 1787; *Critique of Practical Reason* 1788; *Critique of the Power of Judgment* 1790 (Kant then announced the end of his critical enterprise, 5:170); *Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason* 1793; "Theory and Practice" 1793; *Toward Perpetual Peace* 1795; *Metaphysics of Morals* (Doctrine of Right; Doctrine of Virtue) 1797; *Conflict of the Faculties* and *Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View* 1798. Critical period 1781–1798. Retired from teaching 1796. [sources: SEP-K §1; IEP-K §1]
- **Religion and the censorship:** the second of the planned essays was denied the imprimatur by the Berlin censors; Kant combined the essays into a book and got it approved as philosophy (IEP: through the philosophical faculty at Jena). Under Frederick William II (minister Wöllner), in October 1794 a royal rescript reprimanded him and prohibited further writings that "distort and disparage" Christianity; Kant promised "as your Majesty's most loyal subject" to refrain from public discourse on religion, and later held the promise lapsed when the king died (1797). He told the story in *Conflict of the Faculties* (1798). [sources: SEP-Rel §3.6, §3.7.1; IEP-Rel §1; IEP-K §1]
- Reinhold's *Letters on the Kantian Philosophy* (1786); Reinhold given a chair at Jena 1787; Fichte's *Attempt at a Critique of All Revelation* (1792, anonymous) was taken for Kant's; Kant publicly repudiated Fichte in 1799 (12:370–371). [source: SEP-K §1]
- Last years: *Opus Postumum* notes unfinished; mental decline "precipitous around 1800". Last words reported as "Es ist gut" ("It is good") (Vorländer). IEP: thousands attended the funeral of "the sage of Königsberg". Never married (considered marriage twice). Neighbours setting their watches by his walks: WP reports it only as "It was said". [sources: SEP-K §1; WP; IEP-K §1]

## 2. The project of the first Critique; the Copernican revolution

- Kant defines metaphysics via "the cognitions after which reason might strive independently of all experience"; the Critique seeks a "decision about the possibility or impossibility of a metaphysics in general" (Axii). "Our age is the age of criticism, to which everything must submit" (Axi). [source: SEP-K §2, Guyer–Wood]
- Copernican passage (B xvi–xviii, Guyer–Wood as quoted in SEP-K): "Up to now it has been assumed that all our cognition must conform to the objects; but all attempts to find out something about them a priori through concepts that would extend our cognition have, on this presupposition, come to nothing. Hence let us once try whether we do not get farther with the problems of metaphysics by assuming that the objects must conform to our cognition … This would be just like the first thoughts of Copernicus …". [source: SEP-K §2.2]
- "we can cognize of things a priori only what we ourselves have put into them" (Bxviii); cognition "reaches appearances only, leaving the thing in itself as something actual for itself but uncognized by us" (Bxix–xx); "I had to deny knowledge in order to make room for faith" (Bxxx). (IEP-Meta, in another translation: "annul knowledge in order to make room for faith".) [sources: SEP-K §2.2; IEP-Meta]
- **Was the analogy apt? (debate).** SEP-K (citing Bird 2006: 31): analogous because both require "contributions from the observer to be factored into explanations of phenomena, although neither reduces phenomena to the contributions of observers alone". Critics: the analogy reverses Copernicus' logic (Victor Cousin 1854: Kant "supposed … that he himself was the centre, and that all moved round him"; M. Engel, *Kant-Studien* 54, 1963). I. Bernard Cohen, *Revolution in Science* (1985), ch. 15 "Kant's Alleged Copernican Revolution" (pp. 237–253) calls it a "myth". Rockmore: **Kant never used the phrase "Copernican revolution"** himself (he speaks of Copernicus' "first thoughts"/"primary hypothesis"). [sources: SEP-K §2.2 and n. 6; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernican_Revolution]
- **Dogmatic slumber:** *Prolegomena* Preface (4:260). Verified wording (SEP-HC authors' own translation): "I freely admit that it was the remembrance of David Hume which, many years ago, first interrupted my dogmatic slumber and gave my investigations in the field of speculative philosophy a completely different direction." The German *Erinnerung* is rendered "remembrance" (Hatfield), "recollection" (Beck), and also as "admonition"/"reminder" (WS-1: a Cambridge excerpt brackets "admonition by [or remembrance of: Erinnerung]"; translation discussed by Beck and Guyer). [sources: SEP-HC §1; WS-1 https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/kant-prolegomena-to-any-future-metaphysics/2C3FDA7B9FC9C568405B9FC71EAF5D08 ; https://api.philpapers.org/rec/ANDKHA]
- When was he woken? SEP-HC: two alternatives — the late 1750s–mid 1760s (German translation of Hume's *Enquiry* 1755) or the mid-1770s (Beattie's *Essay*, German 1772, quoting the *Treatise*); the authors prefer the first. Kant could read Hume only in translation. [source: SEP-HC §1]
- **A rival account by Kant himself:** letter to Garve, 1798 (12:257–258): it was "the antinomy of pure reason" that "first aroused" him "from the dogmatic slumber". [source: IEP-TI §3.a.iii]
- IEP-K: Hume's problem about causal necessity "generalized to all metaphysical knowledge pertaining to necessity". Hume's fork is the background (cross-ref *Berkeley and Hume*). [source: IEP-K §2b]

## 3. Analytic/synthetic, a priori/a posteriori, the synthetic a priori

- A priori = justification independent of experience; Kant ties apriority to necessity and "strict universality" (B3–4): "Experience never gives its judgments true or strict, but merely assumed or comparative universality (through induction)… Necessity and strict universality are thus secure criteria of an a priori cognition" (SEP-HC's translation; example: "all bodies are heavy" has only empirical universality). All cognition "begins with" experience but not all "arises from" it (B1). [sources: SEP-J §2.2.1; SEP-HC §2]
- Analytic vs synthetic: standard gloss (IEP-K) "all bachelors are unmarried" (analytic) vs "this ball right here is red" (synthetic). Hanna (SEP-J) warns that Kant's criterion is that an analytic judgment's denial entails a contradiction (A151/B190–191); containment is at best sufficient, and Kant's distinction is not the same as the later, Quine-attacked one. [sources: IEP-K §2b; SEP-J §2.2.2]
- Four combinations; analytic a posteriori impossible; synthetic a priori claimed in mathematics, physics and metaphysics (B14–18); the Critique's question is "how are synthetic a priori judgments possible?" (B19). [source: SEP-J §2.2.3]
- **7 + 5 = 12** (B15, Cambridge wording via IEP-K): "The concept of twelve is by no means already thought merely by my thinking of that unification of seven and five, and no matter how long I analyze my concept of such a possible sum I will still not find twelve in it." Geometry: triangle constructed in pure intuition; "to construct a concept means to exhibit a priori the intuition corresponding to it" (A713/B741). [source: IEP-K §3]
- Leibniz cross-ref: Kant adopts Leibniz's notion of necessary truths as analytic but denies arithmetic is analytic; Frege later called Kantian analyticity "taking out of the box again what we have just put into it". [sources: SEP-J §2.2.2; Periergia *Leibniz* notes]
- "Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind" (A51/B75). [source: IEP-K §2c; IEP-Meta]

## 4. Transcendental Aesthetic: space and time as forms of intuition (ARGUMENT)

- Intuition = singular, immediate representation (sensibility, passive); concept = general (understanding, active). [source: IEP-K §2c]
- **Metaphysical Exposition of space** (B edn; SEP-ST's translation): (1) "Space is not an empirical concept which has been derived from outer experiences" — to represent things as "outside and alongside one another" "the representation of space must already underlie them" (A23/B38); (2) "Space is a necessary a priori representation that underlies all outer intuitions. One can never forge a representation of the absence of space, though one can quite well think that no things are to be met within it" (A24/B38–9); (3) space is not a general concept but a pure intuition: "one can represent only one space", many spaces are "parts of one and the same unique space" (A25/B39); (4) "Space is represented as an infinite given magnitude" (B39–40). [source: SEP-ST §3.2–3.3; IEP-TI §3.a.i]
- **Transcendental Exposition / argument from geometry** (B40–41): premises — geometrical truths are necessary (so a priori) and synthetic; conclusion — our representation of space must be an a priori intuition, which is possible only if it "has its seat solely in the subject … as the form of outer sense in general" (B41). A regressive transcendental argument (Strawson 1966: 277–8 makes it central; Allison 2004 weights the metaphysical exposition more; Shabel 2004 resists the regressive reading). [sources: IEP-TI §3.a.i; SEP-ST §4.1; IEP-K §2d.i]
- Conclusions (A26/B42): "Space represents no property at all of any things in themselves, nor any relation of them to one another"; space is spoken of "only from the human standpoint"; "transcendental ideality" with "empirical reality" (A28/B44). Time parallel: "time is not something that would subsist for itself or attach to things as an objective determination" (A32/B49). [sources: SEP-ST §4.1; IEP-TI §3.a.ii; SEP-K §3]
- Suggested step-by-step reconstruction (built from IEP-K and IEP-TI): P1 Geometry is known a priori (necessary). P2 Geometry is synthetic. P3 Synthetic a priori cognition of objects requires that the objects be given in a pure (non-empirical) intuition. P4 A pure intuition that precedes objects is possible only if it is a form of our sensibility. C1 Space is a form of our sensibility. C2 (the "conclusions") Space is no property of things in themselves.
- **Objection 1: the "neglected alternative" / "Trendelenburg's gap."** Grant that our representation of space is an a priori intuition; it does not follow that things in themselves are not spatial — they might be both. Influential version: Adolf Trendelenburg, *Logische Untersuchungen* (3rd edn 1870, vol. 1: 156–168); anticipated by Pistorius (1786, 1788); heated 19th-century debate (Vaihinger 1892). IEP-K puts it: "Why can't it be both?" **Replies:** Kant's distinctive account of intuition (immediate, sensibility-dependent) blocks the gap (Allais 2015: 145–204; Willaschek 1997; also Falkenstein 1995; Allison 2004: 128–132); and Kant's indirect argument from the Antinomies (if space and time were things in themselves, reason would prove contradictions). IEP-TI notes the antinomy reply may not address space and time "as such". [sources: IEP-TI §3.a.ii–iii; IEP-K §2d.i]
- **Objection 2: non-Euclidean geometry.** A "long-standard story (perhaps originally promoted by Bertrand Russell in his *Principles of Mathematics* and by Rudolph Carnap in his *Philosophical Foundations of Physics*)" holds that modern logic, "the discovery of non-Euclidean geometries", and the formalisation of mathematics make Kant's intuition-based theory "obsolete or irrelevant". **Reply:** contemporary commentators are "broadly united in opposing" this story and reconstruct Kant in his own context (Friedman, *Kant and the Exact Sciences*, 1992; Parsons; Hintikka). Kant's view does presuppose that we represent space "as an infinite Euclidean magnitude" (SEP-ST). Kant's philosophy of mathematics also inspired Brouwer's intuitionism. [sources: SEP-Math §3.1; SEP-ST §3.3]

## 5. The categories, the Transcendental Deduction, apperception

- Twelve categories in four groups of three (IEP-Meta's table): Quantity — unity, plurality, totality; Quality — reality, negation, limitation; Relation — inherence and subsistence (substance/accident), causality and dependence (cause/effect), community (reciprocity); Modality — possibility–impossibility, existence–nonexistence, necessity–contingency. Derived in the "metaphysical deduction" from the table of logical forms of judgment (e.g. categorical judgment ↔ substance/accident; hypothetical ↔ cause/effect). [sources: IEP-Meta §4; IEP-K §2e; SEP-K §4.2]
- Goal of the Transcendental Deduction: show categories are "objectively valid" because "through them alone is experience possible" (A93–94/B126). SEP-K: "the central argument of the Critique … and one of the most complex and difficult texts in the history of philosophy." Rewritten for B. [source: SEP-K §4]
- **"The I think must be able to accompany all my representations; for otherwise something would be represented in me that could not be thought at all, which is as much as to say that the representation would either be impossible or else at least would be nothing for me" (B131–132; Guyer–Wood via SEP-K).** [source: SEP-K §4.1]
- Against Locke: self-consciousness "does not yet come about by my accompanying each representation with consciousness, but rather by my adding one representation to the other and being conscious of their synthesis" (B133). [source: SEP-K §4.1]
- Argument sketch (IEP-K): unified experience → unity of apperception → ability to distinguish subjective from objective → representing objects by rules → the rules are the categories; hence categories apply to all objects of experience. A "transcendental argument". The understanding is "the law-giver of nature": "we ourselves bring into the appearances that order and regularity that we call nature" (A125). [sources: IEP-K §2e; SEP-K §4.3]
- Analogies of Experience: First (substance persists; conservation), Second (every event has a cause — Kant's answer to Hume), Third (community/reciprocal interaction). [source: IEP-K §2f]
- **Refutation of Idealism** (added in B, 1787, B274ff.): consciousness of the determinate temporal order of my inner states requires something permanent in space outside me; so the "problematic idealism" (Descartes) of inferring outer objects from inner states rests on a false premise. Kant was annoyed by reviews comparing him to Berkeley. Its success is "a matter of heated debate". [sources: IEP-K §2f; SEP-TI §2.4.5]

## 6. Transcendental idealism and things in themselves (DEBATE)

- Core claims (SEP-K §3): we experience only appearances; space and time are not things in themselves or their determinations; they are the subjective forms of human intuition; they are empirically real. Key text A42/B59–60 ("What may be the case with objects in themselves and abstracted from all this receptivity of our sensibility remains entirely unknown to us."). Kant called it "critical idealism" in the *Prolegomena* (4:293). [source: SEP-K §3 and n. 7]
- "Transcendental" = conditions of the possibility of experience; "transcendent" = beyond experience. [source: IEP-K §2g]
- **Two-objects (two-world)** reading: traditional; goes back to the Garve–Feder review; dominant in Kant's lifetime. Recent proponents (SEP-K n. 10): Strawson 1966, Aquila 1983, **Guyer 1987**, Van Cleve 1999, Jankowiak 2017. Objections: knowing that unknowable things exist and affect us (Jacobi 1787: "without that presupposition I could not enter into the system, but with it I could not stay within it"); scepticism; incoherence of non-spatiotemporal "affection". [source: SEP-K §3.1]
- **Two-aspects (one-world)** reading. Recent proponents (SEP-K n. 11): Bird 1962/2006, Prauss 1974, **Langton 1998**, **Allison 2004**, Allais 2015. Two versions: (a) metaphysical/property-dualist — **Langton**: things in themselves = substances with intrinsic properties; appearances = their relational/extrinsic properties; "Humility": we cannot know intrinsic properties (*Kantian Humility*, 1998); (b) epistemological — **Allison**: two standpoints, the human one and that of an intuitive intellect (*Kant's Transcendental Idealism*, 1983; rev. edn 2004). Langton explicitly rejects Allison's epistemic reading. [sources: SEP-K §3.2; SEP-TI §4.4]
- Stang (SEP-TI §5.2): Langton's view can be read as identity or non-identity; she "in fact opts for a non-identity reading". So the debate is three-cornered, not "Allison vs Guyer/Langton" (see Surprises). [source: SEP-TI §4.4, §5.2]
- IEP-K: common objection to two-world readings — too close to Berkeley; to one-world readings — trivialising Kant; intermediate positions exist. SEP-K: "there is no such thing as the standard interpretation". [sources: IEP-K §2d.ii; SEP-K §3]

## 7. Transcendental Dialectic

- Reason's three ideas: soul, world (as totality), God; "transcendental illusion" = reason's inescapable tendency to posit objects for them. [source: IEP-K §2g]
- **Paralogisms** (rational psychology): from "I think" rationalists inferred the soul is a substance, simple, self-identical, distinct from the external world; Kant: mistaking the mere thought of the I for a cognition of it as object; no intuition of the I itself, so no knowledge of the soul. [source: IEP-K §2g.i]
- **Four antinomies** (A426–461/B454–489): (1) beginning in time / bounds in space vs none; (2) composites made of simples vs not; (3) causality through freedom vs only natural causality; (4) a necessary being belongs to the world vs not. First two "mathematical", last two "dynamical". Mathematical: thesis and antithesis both false (contraries, not contradictories); the world is neither determinately finite nor infinite but "indefinitely" large. Dynamical: both can be true (phenomena vs things in themselves) (A528–532/B556–560). Antinomy = an indirect argument for transcendental idealism. [sources: IEP-TI §3.a.iii; IEP-K §2g.ii; IEP-Meta]
- First antinomy wording (Kemp Smith-type translation as given in WP): Thesis "The world has a beginning in time, and is also limited as regards space." Antithesis "The world has no beginning, and no limits in space; it is infinite as regards both time and space." Thesis proof (WS-6, translation as in the source PDF, likely Kemp Smith): assume no beginning; then "up to every given moment an eternity has elapsed"; but "the infinity of a series consists in the fact that it can never be completed through successive synthesis"; so an infinite elapsed series is impossible and a beginning is necessary. Antithesis proof: assume a beginning; then an empty time preceded the world, and nothing can come to be in empty time (only the "empty time" premise was seen in WS-6). Objections: Broad (1955) — illegitimate use of the "successive synthesis" definition of infinity; the proof treats synthesis as a mental act and so begs the question against a realist; Allison reads the synthesis claims as conceptual truths about infinity. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kant%27s_antinomies ; WS-6 https://homepages.uc.edu/~martinj/The%20Infinite/Kant%20-%20First%20Antinomy.pdf , https://stafforini.com/pdfs/broad-1955-kant-mathematical-antinomies.pdf]
- **Ideal of pure reason:** God as *ens realissimum*; only three possible proofs — ontological, cosmological, physico-theological — and none succeeds. Anticipated in *Only Possible Argument* (1763). [source: IEP-K §2g.iii]
- **Ontological argument:** "existence is not a real predicate"; a real predicate "goes beyond the concept of the subject and enlarges it" (A598/B626); existence is the mere "positing" of a thing; the hundred thalers/dollars: "what would exist would not be the same as what I had thought in my concept" (A600/B628). SEP-Rel: the CPR contains **four** distinct objections (first two treat "God exists" as analytic, A594–597/B622–625; last two as synthetic). The term "ontological argument" dates from Kant's time. Russell (1946: 586): easier to be persuaded ontological arguments are no good than to say what is wrong with them. [sources: SEP-Rel §3.1.2.1; SEP-OA intro; IEP-K §2g.iii]
- **Cosmological argument** parasitic on the ontological; "nest of dialectical presumptions" (A609/B637); Leibniz's modal argument as emblem: "If something exists, an absolutely necessary being also has to exist. Now I myself, at least exist; therefore, an absolutely necessary being exists" (A604/B633). [source: SEP-Rel §3.1.2.2]
- **Physico-theological (design) argument**: also ultimately rests on the ontological, but Kant treats it with sympathy: it "always deserves to be mentioned with respect" (A623/B651). [source: SEP-Rel §3.1.2.3]
- Cross-refs: Anselm and Gaunilo (*Early Scholasticism*), Descartes's Fifth Meditation (*Descartes*), Aquinas's cosmological ways (*Thomas Aquinas*).

## 8. Moral philosophy: the Groundwork (ARGUMENT)

- Good will: the only thing good without qualification/limitation; it would "shine like a jewel" even if it achieved nothing (G 4:393; IEP wording: "like a jewel, it would still shine by itself, as something that has its full worth in itself"). [sources: SEP-M §2; IEP-K §5a]
- Duty and moral worth; respect for the moral law; maxims = subjective principles (examples from CPrR: "to let no insult pass unavenged", "to increase my wealth by every safe means", 5:19, 27). Hypothetical vs categorical imperatives. [sources: SEP-M §§2–4; SEP-K §5.4]
- **Formulas (Gregor wording via SEP-M/SEP-HM, which cite Gregor 1996):**
  - Universal Law (FUL): "act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law" (G 4:421). Law-of-nature variant (Gregor 1997 per WS-3): "act as if the maxim of your action were to become by your will a universal law of nature" (4:421).
  - Humanity (FH): "So act that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means" (G 4:429).
  - Autonomy: "the Idea of the will of every rational being as a will that legislates universal law" (G 4:432).
  - Kingdom of Ends: "act in accordance with the maxims of a member giving universal laws for a merely possible kingdom of ends" (G 4:439).
  - Kant: these are "basically only so many formulations of precisely the same law, each one of them by itself uniting the other two within it" (G 4:435); how they are equivalent is disputed. [sources: SEP-M §§5–9; SEP-HM §2; WS-3]
- Four Groundwork examples: suicide (perfect duty to self), lying promise (perfect to others), developing talents (imperfect to self), helping others (imperfect to others). Contradiction in conception → perfect duty; contradiction in the will → imperfect duty. Four-step procedure following O'Neill (1975, 1989) and Rawls (1980, 1989). [source: SEP-M §5]
- **False promise step by step (SEP-M):** (1) Maxim: "I will make lying promises when it achieves something I want" (to get needed money). (2) Universalise as a law of nature: everyone must try to deceive by promising whenever it gets them what they want. (3) Conceive it: in such a world no practice of giving one's word could exist, and everyone knows it. (4) But the maxim needs that practice ("a necessary means … that a practice of taking the word of others exists"). So one conceives "a world containing promises and a world in which there can be no promises" — a contradiction in conception; hence a perfect duty never to act on it. Note: no self-contradiction in the maxim itself; the contradiction appears only on universalisation. CPrR parallel: the deposit example — "everyone may deny a deposit which no one can prove has been made … would annihilate itself since it would bring it about that there would be no deposits at all" (5:27). [sources: SEP-M §5; SEP-K §5.4]
- Readings of the contradiction: logical vs practical (Korsgaard: universal adoption would defeat the maxim's purpose). [source: WS-4 summaries of Korsgaard 1986 and Kleingeld]
- **Objection: empty formalism (Hegel).** Hegel (*Philosophy of Right* §135) charges Kant's ethics with "empty formalism": the test is indiscriminate/presupposes content (e.g. that property, the deposit, already exists). Kantian replies: the test does generate contradictions, hence content; plus the Humanity formula supplies material content. Freyenhagen, "Empty, Useless, and Dangerous? Recent Kantian Replies to the Empty Formalism Objection", *Hegel Bulletin* 32/63 (2011) 163–186, calls it a "stand-off". [sources: WS-7 https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/hegel-bulletin/article/empty-useless-and-dangerous-recent-kantian-replies-to-the-empty-formalism-objection/45E26FB48598BE5E43EA2EDAA9476FC8 ; https://repository.essex.ac.uk/2643/] (§135 text itself not read.)
- **Objection: false positives and false negatives.** False negatives: innocuous maxims seem to fail (e.g. a course handout's "Don't be a farmer" for the will test); false positives: wrong maxims seem to pass if made very specific (a false promise to a named person on a particular date). Allison's reply: such examples are not genuine maxims. Kleingeld (2019) says these have "classic Kantian rebuttals" but a separate "Latitude Problem" (convenience killing) has seemed insurmountable. [source: WS-4 https://iris.unimore.it/retrieve/e31e124f-b22c-987f-e053-3705fe0a095a/BAGNOLI%202021%20The%20Categorical%20Imperative%20OSNE%20proofs.pdf ; https://carneades.pomona.edu/2023-186e/18.Allison.html] (summaries only)
- **The murderer at the door.** "On a Supposed Right to Tell Lies from Benevolent Motives" (*Über ein vermeintes Recht aus Menschenliebe zu lügen*), *Berlinische Blätter* 1 (1797), replying to Benjamin Constant (*Des réactions politiques*). Kant: "a lie always harms another; if not some human being, then it nevertheless does harm to humanity in general, inasmuch as it vitiates the very source of right" (Abbott translation via WP). Wood calls it "famous (or infamous)"; Varden (2010) argues it is misread and belongs to Kant's legal philosophy (Doctrine of Right). Korsgaard, "The Right to Lie: Kant on Dealing with Evil" (*Philosophy & Public Affairs* 15:4, 1986): the maxim of lying to a deceiver passes FUL, but the Humanity formula seems to forbid it. Jens Timmermann wrote the first monograph, *Kant and the Supposed Right to Lie* (CUP; WP 2025, a review suggests 2024) — he argues Kant largely had it right. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_a_Supposed_Right_to_Tell_Lies_from_Benevolent_Motives ; WS-5]
- Humanity formula does not forbid using people as means, only as *mere* means (the taxi driver vs the horse); respect = Darwall's "recognition respect"; persons cannot lose their humanity by misdeeds. [source: SEP-M §6]
- Kingdom of Ends gained favour with Rawls (1971) and Hill (1972). [source: SEP-M §8]

## 9. Autonomy and freedom

- "Autonomy" = giving the law to oneself; the "fundamental idea" of Kant's philosophy (SEP-K). Reciprocity thesis (Allison 1990): "freedom and unconditional practical law reciprocally imply each other" (5:29). [source: SEP-K §5.1, §5.4]
- Third antinomy resolution: natural causality for appearances, possible freedom for things in themselves; we cannot know theoretically that we are free. Freedom is "the keystone" (5:3–4). The **fact of reason**: consciousness of the moral law (5:31) "proves" the "practical" reality of freedom; gallows example (5:30): a man ordered by his prince to give false testimony "judges … that he can do something because he is aware that he ought to do it". ("Ought implies can" is a later label, not Kant's phrase.) [sources: SEP-K §5.2–5.3; IEP-K §2g.ii]
- Problem: if only the noumenal self is free, how is the phenomenal self responsible? Coherence doubtful on either reading of transcendental idealism. [source: SEP-K §5.2]
- Postulates of practical reason: God, freedom, immortality; the highest good. [source: SEP-K §6; IEP-K §5c]
- "Two things fill my mind with ever new and increasing admiration and reverence …: the starry heavens above me and the moral law within me" (CPrR 5:161; translation as given in WP, translator not stated). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant]

## 10. Critique of the Power of Judgment (1790): beauty and the sublime

- Four moments of the judgment of taste (SEP-A): (1) quality — pleasure that is **disinterested** (does not depend on or generate desire for the object), unlike the agreeable and the good; (2) quantity — claims **universal validity**, but "not based on concepts"; cannot be proved; "beauty is not a concept of the object" (§38); (3) relation — "purposiveness" without a determinate purpose; "merely formal purposiveness"; the expression "purposiveness without a purpose" is one "widely used by commentators"; (4) modality — **necessity**, "exemplary", based on a "common sense" (*sensus communis*, §20). Free play of imagination and understanding. [sources: SEP-A §2.1; IEP-K §7a]
- Criticisms: disinterestedness (we do care about preserving beautiful things — Crawford 1974); aesthetic formalism (influential via Hanslick in music); Dickie's "myth of the aesthetic attitude" (1964); Wollheim's "empty cognitive stock". [source: SEP-A §2.4]
- Sublime: mathematical (imagination fails to comprehend magnitude; reason's idea of totality; "indicates a faculty of the mind which surpasses every standard of sense", §26) and dynamical (nature as "a power that has no dominion over us", §28; examples: overhanging cliffs, thunder clouds, volcanoes, hurricanes). Pleasure mixed with displeasure. [source: SEP-A §2.7]
- Beauty as "symbol of morality" (§59); genius and aesthetic ideas; judgment bridges "the great gulf" between nature and freedom. [sources: SEP-A §2.8; IEP-K §7b–c]

## 11. Political philosophy: Perpetual Peace and right

- Universal principle of right: "Any action is right if it can coexist with everyone's freedom in accordance with a universal law" (6:230). Republicanism; Kant borrows "general will" from Rousseau. [source: IEP-K §6b]
- *Toward Perpetual Peace* (1795): six **preliminary articles** (8:343–47): no peace treaty with secret reservations for future war; no acquisition of one state by another; abolition of standing armies; no national debt for external affairs; no forcible interference in another state's constitution ("no state shall forcibly interfere in the constitution and government of another state", 8:346); no acts in war that make future peace impossible. Three **definitive articles**: (1) every state's civil constitution shall be republican (8:348) — the people who bear war's costs decide on war; (2) a federation/league of free states (8:354); (3) cosmopolitan right limited to "universal hospitality" (8:357). [sources: SEP-SP §8; IEP-K §6c]
- League vs world republic: the world republic is an "idea"; the voluntary league is a "negative surrogate" (8:357). Byrd & Hruschka (2010) argue Kant favoured a state of states; Caranti (2022) the league. Doyle (1983) linked the first article to the democratic peace thesis. [source: SEP-SP §8]
- Hospitality: right to visit/offer commerce, not to settle; shelter in emergencies (sometimes read as basis for refugee duties); "a violation of right on one place of the earth is felt in all" (8:360). In the 1790s Kant strongly criticised European colonisation (settlement only by uncoerced, informed contract; 6:354; 8:358–60). [source: SEP-SP §9]
- Cross-ref History "Peace" chapter: *Perpetual Peace* (1795) later linked to democratic-peace theories. Consistent.

## 12. Race (careful; DEBATE)

- "Of the Different Races of Human Beings" (*Von den verschiedenen Racen der Menschen*): published 1775 as an announcement of his physical geography lectures, revised 1777. Monogenesis: all humans from a common "lineal root genus", with "seeds" and "dispositions" triggered by climate; four races ("noble blond" of northern Europe; "copper red" of America; "black" of Senegambia; "olive-yellow" of Asian India); interfertility as evidence of one species. [sources: SEP-Race (Bernasconi & Lott 2000: 8–22); WS-9]
- "Determination of the Concept of a Human Race" (*Bestimmung des Begriffs einer Menschenrace*), *Berlinische Monatsschrift*, November 1785; criticised by Georg Forster (1786); Kant's reply "On the Use of Teleological Principles in Philosophy" (1788). Herder also part of the exchange. Kant lectured on anthropology (and physical geography) for decades. [sources: WS-9 https://www.theoriesofrace.com/8 ; https://periodicos.sbu.unicamp.br/ojs/index.php/kant/article/view/8672613 ; WP-Immanuel_Kant]
- *Observations* (1764), §4, in Guyer's Cambridge translation (Frierson & Guyer eds., 2011; translation first in *Anthropology, History, and Education*, 2007) as quoted by WP: "The Negroes of Africa have by nature no feeling that rises above the ridiculous. Mr. Hume challenges anyone to adduce a single example where a Negro has demonstrated talents …"; "So essential is the difference between these two human kinds, and it seems to be just as great with regard to the capacities of mind as it is with respect to color." Also the remark on a "Negro carpenter": "this scoundrel was completely black from head to foot, a distinct proof that what he said was stupid." Kant draws on Hume's footnote to "Of National Characters" (cross-ref *Berkeley and Hume*). [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant ; WS-8 https://ndpr.nd.edu/news/immanuel-kant-observations-on-the-feeling-of-the-beautiful-and-sublime-and-other-writings/]
- Two strands of literature (Lu-Adler 2023, 2n4): critics — **Emmanuel Chukwudi Eze**, "The Color of Reason: The Idea of 'Race' in Kant's Anthropology" (1995); **Charles Mills**, "Kant's *Untermenschen*" (2005; also *Black Rights/White Wrongs*, OUP 2017: Kant "sanitized for public consumption"); **Robert Bernasconi** (2001 "Who Invented the Concept of Race? Kant's Role in the Enlightenment Construction of Race"; 2002 "Kant as an Unfamiliar Source of Racism") — Bernasconi: Kant "supplied the first scientific definition of race"; defenders of Kant's broader philosophy — Louden (2000), Hill & Boxill (2001), Kleingeld (2007), Allais (2016, "Kant's Racism"). Eze credited with bringing the issue to light among Western philosophers in the 1990s. [sources: SEP-Race; WP-Immanuel_Kant]
- **Kleingeld**, "Kant's Second Thoughts on Race", *Philosophical Quarterly* 57 (2007): Kant "did defend a racial hierarchy until at least the end of the 1780s" but changed his mind before *Toward Perpetual Peace* (1795); there and in the *Metaphysics of Morals* he "becomes more egalitarian with regard to race" (2007: 586), rejecting colonialism, chattel slavery and the slave trade. Reiterated 2019 ("On Dealing with Kant's Sexism and Racism") and 2025 ("Anti-Racism and Kant Scholarship", *Mind* 134). Rauscher (SEP-SP): "By the mid-1790s … Kant appears to have given up beliefs about racial inferiority and no longer discusses it in his lectures." [sources: SEP-Race; SEP-SP §9–10; WP]
- **Huaping Lu-Adler**: "Kant and Slavery—Or Why He Never Became a Racial Egalitarian", *Critical Philosophy of Race* 10 (2022): Kleingeld's paper had a "narrative-shaping" effect; Kant's late remarks reflect worry that slavery and colonialism impede progress and "perpetual peace", treating racial slavery as a "disinterested philosopher of history" rather than a moral problem; *Kant, Race, and Racism: Views from Somewhere* (OUP 2023): Kant held "a consistent inegalitarian position" (2023: 18), and was central as scholar and teacher in shaping the prevailing view of race. [source: SEP-Race]
- Zorn (WP): many notorious quotations come from the 1777–88 exchange with Herder and Forster; Kant argued for the unity of the human species against Forster's view that races were distinct species. Present as one scholar's caution, not a verdict. [source: WP-Immanuel_Kant]
- Diderot rejected Kant's new anthropology of races for a monogenetic view; Herder rejected the concept of race (cross-ref *The Enlightenment and Rousseau*). [source: Periergia Enlightenment notes, SEP-D, SEP-Her]

## 13. Women in Kant

- *Observations* §3: women predominantly have a feeling for the beautiful, men for the sublime (with caveats: "we must also remember that they are not all alike"). A learned woman with a head full of Greek "like Madame Dacier", or who disputes about mechanics like the Marquise du Châtelet, "might as well even have a beard" (Goldthwait translation, p. 78, as quoted in secondary literature; WS-10). [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observations_on_the_Feeling_of_the_Beautiful_and_Sublime ; WS-10 https://philarchive.org/archive/MIKKOM]
- Citizenship: in "Theory and Practice" (1793) and the Doctrine of Right (6:315), voting requires being "one's own master"; women (for unspecified "natural" reasons), children and economic dependants are "passive" citizens with the rights of freedom and equality but no vote; passive citizens must be able to work their way up to active status — though women could not. Kant admits "It is, I admit, somewhat difficult to determine what is required in order to be able to claim the rank of a human being who is his own master." [source: SEP-SP §2, §4]
- Marriage (Doctrine of Right): reciprocal possession of each other's sexual capacities; reciprocity "restores" personality (6:278); yet husbands' legal rule over wives held not unjust; some contemporaries objected. Scholars sifting this: Herman 1993, Wilson 1998, Hay 2013, Varden 2020 (*Sex, Love, and Gender: A Kantian Theory*). [source: SEP-SP §5]
- Rauscher: Kant's views are "considered sexist and racist universally today and even out of step with some of his more progressive colleagues". [source: SEP-SP §10]

## 14. Influence

- Reinhold, Fichte, then Schelling and Hegel (German Idealism; cross-ref chapter 34); Schopenhauer read the thing in itself as Will (overview). Kant's philosophy of mathematics provoked Frege, Russell, Husserl and inspired Brouwer. Rawls and Habermas strongly influenced by Kant's moral philosophy. SEP-K: "the central figure in modern philosophy", who "set the terms for much of nineteenth and twentieth century philosophy". [sources: SEP-K intro, §1; SEP-Math §3.1; WP-Immanuel_Kant; Periergia overview]

## Surprises
- Kant never used the phrase "Copernican revolution"; Cohen calls it a "myth" and critics say the analogy runs backwards.
- Kant gave two accounts of what woke him: Hume (*Prolegomena* 1783) and the antinomy (letter to Garve, 1798).
- Langton is a one-world (property) reader who opposes Allison; she is not on Guyer's two-world side.
- SEP's main Kant entry misdates Wolff's death (1750 for 1754).
- Kant's first publication date: SEP 1747 vs WP 1749.
