## 1. What "Enlightenment" means; periodisation; one or many

- Kant, "An Answer to the Question: What is Enlightenment?" (*Beantwortung der Frage: Was ist Aufklärung?*), published in the December 1784 issue of the *Berlinische Monatsschrift* (eds Friedrich Gedike and Johann Erich Biester), replying to a question posed a year earlier by the Reverend Johann Friedrich Zöllner; several leading intellectuals replied; Kant's is the most famous. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_Is_Enlightenment%3F]
- In 1783 Moses Mendelssohn described Enlightenment as a process by which man was educated in the use of reason (Outram, via WP). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment]
- SEP-E: for Enlightenment thinkers themselves "the Enlightenment is not an historical period, but a process of social, psychological or spiritual development, unbound to time or place"; Kant identifies enlightenment "with the process of undertaking to think for oneself". [source: SEP-E intro]
- SEP-E's framing: the heart of the Enlightenment is the "loosely organized activity of prominent French thinkers of the mid-decades of the eighteenth century, the so-called 'philosophes' (e.g., Voltaire, D'Alembert, Diderot, Montesquieu)", but there is a "renowned Scottish Enlightenment (key figures are Frances [sic] Hutcheson, Adam Smith, David Hume, Thomas Reid)" and a German *Aufklärung* (Wolff, Mendelssohn, Lessing, Kant). D'Alembert called his age "the century of philosophy par excellence". SEP-E takes its "primary origin" to be the scientific revolution. [source: SEP-E intro]
- SEP-E: the devolution of the French Revolution into the Terror in the 1790s, with the rise of Romanticism, "can serve as a convenient marker of the end of the Enlightenment, conceived as an historical period." [source: SEP-E intro]
- Periodisation has no consensus: some date the start to Descartes (1637), many in France to Newton's *Principia* (1687); French historians usually place the *Siècle des Lumières* between 1715 (start of Louis XV's reign) and 1789; most scholars end it with the French Revolution or the start of the Napoleonic Wars (1804); recent work extends it in time and globally. "Scholars have never agreed on a definition of the Enlightenment or on its chronological or geographical extent." The English term "the Enlightenment" emerged only in the late 19th century; French *Lumières* was used first by Dubos in 1733. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment]
- Israel's thesis: Jonathan Israel (b. 1946; IAS Princeton 2001–2016) distinguishes a "radical Enlightenment", founded on a rationalist materialism first articulated by Spinoza, leaning to religious scepticism and republican/democratic government, from a "moderate Enlightenment" (Descartes, Locke, Wolff) seeking accommodation with traditional power and faith. He argues Spinoza and Spinozism "were in fact the intellectual backbone of the European Radical Enlightenment everywhere". Trilogy: *Radical Enlightenment* (2001), *Enlightenment Contested* (2006), *Democratic Enlightenment* (2011); also *A Revolution of the Mind* (2009), *Revolutionary Ideas* (2014, where he calls Robespierre's Jacobin ideology "an obsessive Rousseauiste moral Puritanism steeped in authoritarianism…"). [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Israel ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment]
- Critics of Israel: Johnson Kent Wright (H-France Forum) reports critiques by Theo Verbeek, Harvey Chisick, Anthony La Vopa, Antoine Lilti, Samuel Moyn and Dan Edelstein, who reached a "strikingly unanimous verdict": that Israel serves "a false idol—Spinoza, supposed demiurge of modernity—and an unsustainable principle—the idea of an umbilical connection between metaphysical monism and political radicalism". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Israel]
- Pocock's "Enlightenments": J. G. A. Pocock (7 Mar 1924 – 12 Dec 2023), *Barbarism and Religion* (1999–2015), vol. 1 titled *The Enlightenments of Edward Gibbon, 1737–1794* (1999). John Robertson, *The Case for the Enlightenment: Scotland and Naples 1680–1760* (Cambridge, 2005), explicitly challenges "the recent tendency to fragment the Enlightenment … into multiple Enlightenments", argues against following Pocock, and finds the unity in a shared commitment to understanding and advancing human betterment, articulated in the language of political economy (comparison centred on Hume and Vico). [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._G._A._Pocock ; WS: https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/C94B96C2BF6E3CC00860C1A419B37AE3 , https://muse.jhu.edu/article/257100]
- So: three positions to present as a debate — one Enlightenment with a common core (Robertson; Gay's "family" of philosophes is a different, stylistic unity per a reviewer), several national/confessional Enlightenments (Pocock), and two rival Enlightenments, radical vs moderate (Israel). [sources: as above]

## 2. The philosophes and the *Encyclopédie*

- Full title *Encyclopédie, ou dictionnaire raisonné des sciences, des arts et des métiers* ("Systematic Dictionary of the Sciences, Arts and Crafts"); published 1751–1772; originally conceived (prospectus 1745) as a French translation of Ephraim Chambers's *Cyclopaedia* (1728). [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclop%C3%A9die ; SEP-D §1.2]
- Size: SEP-E: 28 volumes (17 of text, 11 of plates) over 21 years, "over 70,000 articles", "over 140 contributors". WP: about 71,818 articles and 2,784 plates; 17 text volumes 1751–1765, 11 plate volumes 1762–1772; first pressrun 4,225 copies; some 25,000 sets in various editions sold by 1789. [sources: SEP-E §1.5; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclop%C3%A9die]
- Dedication/orientation: the *Encyclopédie* "is dedicated to three empiricists (Francis Bacon, John Locke and Isaac Newton)" (SEP-E's phrase), signalling the ascendancy of empiricism; its orientation is "decidedly secular and implicitly anti-authoritarian"; title page: "a society of men of letters". [source: SEP-E §1.2, §1.5]
- D'Alembert's *Preliminary Discourse* (1751): knowledge originates in the senses; its "Figurative System of Human Knowledge" divides understanding into memory, reason and imagination; it marks a shift from Descartes's rationalism toward the empiricism of Locke and Newton. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preliminary_Discourse_to_the_Encyclopedia_of_Diderot]
- Diderot's prospectus November 1750; his article "Encyclopédie" in vol. V (1755). Jesuit opposition (Berthier, *Journal de Trévoux*). The abbé de Prades affair (1752) led to a temporary suspension of the privilege; vols II–VI appeared 1751–56. After Damiens's attack on Louis XV (1757) and vol. VII, controversy led **d'Alembert to resign**, leaving Diderot in sole control; Helvétius's *De l'esprit* (July 1758) was the "final blow". [source: SEP-D §1.2]
- Suppression: in 1759, after vol. VII, on the initiative of the Parlement of Paris, the government revoked/suspended the privilege; work continued "in secret" with highly placed protectors (Malesherbes, Madame de Pompadour); vols VIII–XVII appeared together in 1765 with a false "Neufchastel" imprint; the plates were not subject to the ban. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclop%C3%A9die ; SEP-D §1.2; SEP-V §1.6 ("in 1759 the work's publication privilege was revoked in France")]
- D'Alembert's departure: SEP-D puts it in the 1757–58 controversy; WS sources (Liberty Fund, encyclopedia.com) say 1758, after his article "Genève"; WP's Encyclopédie article and the Periergia History chapter say "until 1759". Safe: "d'Alembert withdrew in 1758". [sources: SEP-D §1.2; WS: https://oll.libertyfund.org/people/jean-le-rond-d-alembert , https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/science-and-technology/mathematics-biographies/jean-le-rond-dalembert]
- Voltaire contributed articles from vol. 5 (1755) and fused the *Encyclopédie*'s enemies into a single "infamy"; the contributors became known as a "coterie of philosophes". [source: SEP-V §1.6]
- On slavery the *Encyclopédie* was "ambivalent": some articles reported it matter-of-factly or defended it; Jaucourt attacked it in "Traite des nègres". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclop%C3%A9die]

## 3. Voltaire

- François-Marie Arouet, 21 November 1694 – 30 May 1778 (SEP-V gives 1694–1778). Bastille 16 May 1717 – 15 April 1718 for a satirical verse; adopted the name "Voltaire" in 1718. Literary debut *Oedipe* (1718). [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaire ; SEP-V §1.1]
- England 1726–29, after an affair of honour with the Chevalier de Rohan (WP: Rohan had him beaten, then had him imprisoned in the Bastille, 17 April 1726). Met Swift, Pope, Gay through Bolingbroke; did not meet Newton (d. 1727) but knew Clarke and the Newtonians. [sources: SEP-V §1.2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaire]
- *Letters on the English*: published first in English in 1733 (*Letters Concerning the English Nation*), then in French in 1734 as *Lettres philosophiques*; 24 letters, including on Quakers, Parliament, inoculation, Bacon, Locke, Newton. The French edition was printed without approval, publicly burned by the royal hangman, and a lettre de cachet was issued; Voltaire fled with Émilie du Châtelet to Cirey. SEP-V: historians "still scratch their heads" about why it caused such furor. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_on_the_English ; SEP-V §1.4]
- SEP-E: Voltaire in the *Letters* (1734) embraced Newton's physical system in preference to Descartes's; SEP-V: the book used "an opposition between Newton and Descartes" and fused "Bacon, Locke, and Newton into an English philosophical complex". *Éléments de la philosophie de Newton* 1738 (definitive 1745). [sources: SEP-E §1.1; SEP-V §1.4]
- Cirey 1734–1749 with Du Châtelet (see §9). After her death (1749) he went to Frederick the Great's court (1750; WP: Potsdam), quarrelled with Maupertuis (satire 1752), left; settled near Geneva (Les Délices 1755), then permanently at Ferney from early 1759 until 1778. [sources: SEP-V §1.5–1.6; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaire]
- "Écrasez l'infâme!" — SEP-V translates "Crush the infamy!"; SEP-E "Crush the infamous", a sign-off in his letters, and says *l'infâme* "refers to the Church, not to religion as such"; WP: refers to abuses of power by royal and religious authorities and clerical superstition/intolerance. Voltaire was a deist who "carries deism across the channel to France". [sources: SEP-V §1.6; SEP-E §2.3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaire]
- *Candide, ou l'Optimisme*, published simultaneously in five countries no later than 15 January 1759; banned by Geneva's Grand Council and Paris administrators by end of February 1759. Satire of Leibnizian optimism through the tutor Pangloss ("best of all possible worlds"); triggered by the Lisbon earthquake (1 November 1755) and the Seven Years' War. Ends with Candide telling Pangloss "we must cultivate our garden" (*il faut cultiver notre jardin*); the Turk's work keeps them free of "three great evils: boredom, vice, and poverty". SEP-V: *Candide* is "a serious attack on Leibnizian philosophy". Cross-ref *Leibniz*. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candide ; SEP-V §1.6]
- *Poème sur le désastre de Lisbonne* (1756): 180 lines against Pope's "whatever is, is right" and Leibnizian optimism; 30,000–50,000 killed. Rousseau answered in a letter Voltaire received on 18 August 1756 (Providence; particular evils serve the general good); Rousseau believed *Candide* was Voltaire's rebuttal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Po%C3%A8me_sur_le_d%C3%A9sastre_de_Lisbonne]
- Calas affair: Jean Calas (1698 – 10 March 1762), Protestant merchant of Toulouse; his son Marc-Antoine found dead 13–14 October 1761; the Parlement of Toulouse sentenced him (9 March 1762) to be broken on the wheel; executed 10 March 1762, protesting innocence. Voltaire's campaign: Louis XV annulled the sentence in 1764; formal exoneration 1765 (on a procedural defect), family paid 36,000 livres. *Treatise on Tolerance* 1763. WP's Calas lead says "exonerated in 1764"; body gives annulment 1764, exoneration 1765. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Calas ; SEP-V §1.6]
- *Treatise on Tolerance* (1763) passage (translator not named by WP): "I say that we should regard all men as our brothers. What? The Turk my brother? The Chinaman my brother? The Jew? The Siam? Yes, without doubt; are we not all children of the same father and creatures of the same God?" [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaire]
- *Dictionnaire philosophique* 1764–1770 (SEP-V). Voltaire also wrote hostile passages on Jews (WP: 30 of 118 articles in the *Dictionnaire* concern Jews/Judaism, described negatively; Peter Gay vs Arthur Hertzberg debate on how to read this), was a polygenist (separate origins of races), yet in *Candide* the slave of Surinam says "at what price we eat sugar in Europe". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaire]
- Final return to Paris early 1778, feted; died weeks later; first person reburied in the revolutionary Panthéon (1791). [source: SEP-V §1.7]

## 4. Montesquieu

- Charles-Louis de Secondat, baron de La Brède et de Montesquieu; born at La Brède near Bordeaux, died of a fever in Paris on 10 February 1755. Birth date: SEP-M "January 19th, 1689"; WP 18 January 1689 (use "January 1689"). Président à mortier of the Parlement of Bordeaux (1716), presiding over its criminal division (which "administered various punishments including torture"). [sources: SEP-M §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montesquieu]
- *Persian Letters* 1721, anonymous (authorship an open secret), instant success. Usbek and Rica travel to Europe 1711–1720; satire of France through outsiders' eyes; and a dark harem plot: Usbek, apparently enlightened, is a despot at home; Roxana's final letter (Letter 161): "I may have lived in servitude, but I have always been free. I have amended your laws according to the laws of nature" (translation as quoted in SEP-M). [source: SEP-M §3]
- Académie française 1728; travel incl. two years in England, admired the English system; *Considerations on … the Romans* 1734; *The Spirit of the Laws* 1748 (anonymous); *Defense* 1750; placed on the Index in 1751. [source: SEP-M §1]
- Three forms of government: republics (democratic or aristocratic), monarchies, despotisms; democracies require civic virtue; laws vary with climate, soil, population etc. [sources: SEP-E §2.1; SEP-M §4]
- Liberty = "a tranquillity of mind arising from the opinion each person has of his safety" (SL 11.6); "constant experience shows us that every man invested with power is apt to abuse it … it is necessary from the very nature of things that power should be a check to power" (SL 11.4), hence separation of executive, legislative and judicial power; judicial power "so terrible to mankind … becomes, as it were, invisible" (11.6). Shklar: "a liberalism of fear". Influenced Madison and the US Constitution; also Beccaria. [sources: SEP-M §4.2; SEP-E §2.1]
- Climate theory: cold constricts fibres (people vigorous, bold, less sensitive: "you must flay a Muscovite alive to make him feel", SL 14.2); heat relaxes them; not hereditary, not deterministic; laws should counteract the worst effects. [source: SEP-M §4.3]
- Slavery: "the state of slavery is in its own nature bad" (SL 15.1); contemptuous of religious and racist justifications; yet in very hot countries slavery is "more reconcilable to reason" (15.7), though he hopes "there is not that climate upon earth where the most laborious services might not with proper encouragement be performed by freemen" (15.8). [source: SEP-M §4.3]
- The irony passage (SL 15.5): a satirical list of pro-slavery arguments presented without comment, including that sugar would be too expensive without slave labour. Nugent translation (WS, constitution.org): "Sugar would be too dear if the plants which produce it were cultivated by any other than slaves", preceded by "The Europeans, having extirpated the Americans, were obliged to make slaves of the Africans, for clearing such vast tracts of land." Voltaire later wrote that Montesquieu "was almost always right against the fanatics and the promoters of slavery." [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montesquieu ; WS: https://constitution.org/2-Authors/cm/sol_15.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaire]

## 5. Diderot, d'Holbach, La Mettrie, Condorcet

- Diderot (1713–1784), born at Langres, son of a cutler. *Pensées philosophiques* burned July 1746. After the *Lettre sur les aveugles* (1749; the blind mathematician Saunderson) he was imprisoned at Vincennes from July 1749 for three months, released November 1749. [source: SEP-D §1.1]
- Materialism: by the *Lettre sur les aveugles* he moves toward materialist metaphysics; *Le Rêve de D'Alembert* (trilogy of dialogues, unpublished in his lifetime) is a masterpiece of "vital materialism"; in the article "Spinosiste" the "modern Spinozists" hold that "matter is sensitive" (shown by the egg, inert, becoming a sensing being by heat alone) and "only matter exists". SEP-E: in Diderot "atheism is partly supported by an expansive, dynamic conception of nature". [sources: SEP-D §2.3; SEP-E §2.3]
- Late work: contributions to the abbé Raynal's *Histoire … des deux Indes* (final edition), including "imagined dialogues about the horrors of the European imperial slave system spoken by oppressed Africans"; *Supplément au voyage de Bougainville* (Tahitians vs Europeans); a "passionate abolitionist"; rejected Kant's new anthropology of distinct races in favour of a monogenetic view. Visited Catherine the Great 1773–74. [source: SEP-D §1.4]
- Raynal's *Histoire philosophique et politique des établissements et du commerce des Européens dans les deux Indes*: published anonymously, Amsterdam 1770; Diderot estimated to have written between a third and a fifth (Curran 2019); third edition 1780 more violent; banned and burned by the public hangman 29 May 1781; Raynal exiled. Over 30 French editions 1770–1787. Toussaint Louverture read it and was inspired by a passage predicting slave revolt (C. L. R. James, *The Black Jacobins*); Raynal (WP) warned "the Africans only want a chief, sufficiently courageous, to lead them on to vengeance and slaughter". Turgot complained it treated "slavery as abominable" while "wanting slaves". [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histoire_des_deux_Indes ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- D'Holbach: Paul-Henri Thiry, born 1723 at Edesheim (d. 1789 per WP title); his coterie met for about thirty years from the early 1750s, many members avowed atheists; published anonymously *Le Christianisme dévoilé*, *Système de la nature* (1770, under the name "Mirabaud" — WP) and *Le Bon Sens*; *System of Nature* and *Common Sense* condemned by the Parlement of Paris and publicly burned; answered by Voltaire and Frederick the Great among others. [source: SEP-H §1]
- La Mettrie (23 Nov 1709 – 11 Nov 1751), physician, *L'Homme machine* (*Man a Machine*), mental processes caused by bodily/brain organisation; found refuge at Frederick the Great's court. Publication year not settled in sources read (see NOT VERIFIED). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julien_Offray_de_La_Mettrie]
- Condorcet: Marie-Jean-Antoine-Nicolas de Caritat, marquis de Condorcet, 17 Sept 1743 – 28/29 March 1794 (SEP-C header gives March 28, its text says he was arrested March 27 and "found dead in his prison cell on March 29" at Bourg-la-Reine, cause unknown). Proscribed 1793 after opposing the Jacobin constitution; wrote the *Esquisse* (*Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind*) during eight months in hiding; published 1795 by his widow Sophie de Grouchy (SEP-C abstract dates it "(1794)", i.e. written; SEP-E and WP: published posthumously 1795). Ten epochs; SEP-E: "the paradigmatically Enlightenment view of history of the human race as a continual progress to perfection". [sources: SEP-C §2–3; SEP-E §2.1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sketch_for_a_Historical_Picture_of_the_Progress_of_the_Human_Mind]
- Condorcet on slavery: *Réflexions sur l'esclavage des nègres* (1781); president of the Société des Amis des Noirs (January 1789). On women: 1790 essay "On the admission of women to the rights of citizenship"; attributed women's limitations to education and circumstances: "I hope that anyone who attacks my arguments will do so without using ridicule or declamation, and above all, that someone will show me a natural difference between men and women on which the exclusion could legitimately be based" (Condorcet 1790, trans. in McLean and Hewitt 1994, as quoted in SEP-C). SEP-C: "exceptional even for an enlightened thinker". [source: SEP-C intro, §1]
- Sophie de Grouchy learned English to translate the 7th edition of Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments* (published 1798 with her *Letters on Sympathy*). [source: SEP-C §3 and bibliography]

## 6. Scottish Enlightenment: Hutcheson, Smith, Reid, Ferguson (Hume cross-ref)

- SEP-S18: "Philosophy was at the core of" the Scottish Enlightenment; major figures Hutcheson, Hume, Smith, Reid and Ferguson; others Carmichael, Turnbull, Kames, Beattie, Blair, Millar, Dugald Stewart. [source: SEP-S18 §1]
- Hutcheson (1694–1746): Irish by birth, educated and later professor at Glasgow; *Inquiry into the Original of our Ideas of Beauty and Virtue* (1725), *Essay on the Nature and Conduct of the Passions, with Illustrations on the Moral Sense* (1728); moral sense theory; influenced Smith, Reid and Hume. SEP-E: Shaftesbury and Hutcheson "the two founders of modern moral sense theory". [sources: SEP-Hut intro; SEP-E §2.2]
- Adam Smith: baptised 5 June 1723 (OS) at Kirkcaldy, Fife; died Edinburgh 17 July 1790. Studied under Hutcheson at Glasgow; chair of logic and rhetoric 1751, moral philosophy 1752. *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* 1759 (six authorised editions in his lifetime; the 2nd and 6th (1790) significantly revised); *Wealth of Nations* 1776 (cross-ref Economics). [sources: SEP-S18 §9; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Smith]
- Sympathy: arises "when we imagine how we would feel in the circumstances of others" — a "projective" account, versus Hume's "contagion" account (Fleischacker's terms); imagined feelings never fully match, so spectators and agents adjust; this mutual adjustment produces the "awful" virtues of self-command and the "amiable" virtues of humanity. [source: SEP-Sm §2]
- Impartial spectator: we judge by the feelings "a well-informed and impartial spectator would have" (TMS 129, 135); "A feeling … is worthy of moral approval if and only if an impartial spectator would sympathize with that feeling" (SEP-Sm's formulation). IEP-Sm: "a theory of conscience"; self-deceit is "the source of half the disorders of human life" (TMS III.4.6); "He desires not only praise, but praiseworthiness" (TMS III.2.2). [sources: SEP-Sm §2; IEP-Sm]
- Smith reviewed Rousseau's *Discourse on Inequality* for the *Edinburgh Review* (SEP-R; Griswold 2017/2018). SEP-Sm reports the interpretive debate: Smith as endorsing commercial society (Berry, McCloskey), as sharing Rousseau's worries about corruption (Dwyer), or as critiquing Rousseau (Hanley 2009). [sources: SEP-R §6; SEP-Sm §2]
- Thomas Reid: born Strachan, Aberdeenshire, 26 April (OS) / 7 May 1710; died 7 October 1796. Minister at New Machar 1737; King's College Aberdeen 1752; *An Inquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense* 1764; then the Glasgow moral philosophy chair, replacing Adam Smith (WP); resigned 1781; *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* 1785, *Essays on the Active Powers of Man* 1788. Co-founder of the Royal Society of Edinburgh 1783. [sources: SEP-Reid intro; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Reid]
- Reid vs the "ideal theory": Reid's target, "The Way of Ideas" or "Ideal Theory", drawn from Locke, Berkeley, Hume and others back to Plato and Aristotle, holds that mental representations ("ideas") mediate all experience; Reid calls this "all fiction, without any foundation in nature" (IHM 2.4), unscientific and anti-Newtonian, leading to scepticism; he holds that the immediate objects of perception are external things (direct realism). Common-sense first principles have "the consent of ages and nations, of the learned and unlearned" (cf. EIP 6.4); to deny them is "not only false but absurd". SEP-E: Reid defends "naïve realism as a defense of common sense over against the doctrines of the philosophers". [sources: SEP-Reid §1.1, §2; SEP-E §1.2]
- Adam Ferguson (1 July 1723, Logierait – 22 Feb 1816): Gaelic-speaking chaplain to the Black Watch (1745/6); succeeded Hume as Advocates' Librarian; Edinburgh chairs of natural philosophy 1759–64 and pneumatics and moral philosophy 1764–85; *An Essay on the History of Civil Society* (1767). He doubted that progress had increased the ratio of happiness to unhappiness, and attacked Hobbes, Rousseau and Hume for speaking ignorantly of a state of nature: there are no records of a pre-social human condition; humankind has always lived in society, "hence the state of nature is a social state" (SEP-S18's paraphrase); he proposed history, travellers' accounts and myths instead of contract theory; credited as a founder of sociology. [sources: SEP-S18 §11; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Ferguson]

## 7. Rousseau: life

- Born Geneva 28 June 1712 (baptised 4 July); mother Suzanne Bernard died nine days later; father Isaac a watchmaker and citizen of Geneva. Apprenticed to an engraver (1725); left Geneva 1728 at 16; Mme de Warens; converted to Catholicism in Turin, April 1728 (losing Genevan citizenship); the stolen-ribbon episode; Lyon tutor 1740; Paris 1742 (numerical musical notation rejected by the Academy of Sciences); met Diderot; secretary to the French ambassador in Venice; Paris from 1744/45, music articles for the *Encyclopédie*. [sources: SEP-R §1; IEP-R §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau]
- Thérèse Levasseur from 1745 (married 1768, IEP). Five children, all left at the foundling hospital shortly after birth — "an almost certain sentence of death in eighteenth-century France" (SEP-R); Voltaire later used this against him. In *Confessions* Book IX he wrote "I trembled at the thought of intrusting them to a family ill brought up, to be still worse educated. The risk of the education of the foundling hospital was much less" (old translation quoted in WP, translator not named); in a 1751 letter to Mme de Francueil he had pleaded poverty. [sources: SEP-R §1; IEP-R §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau]
- Vincennes "illumination" (1749): walking to visit the imprisoned Diderot, read the Dijon Academy's prize question; experienced the thought "that humankind is good by nature but is corrupted by society". *Discourse on the Sciences and Arts* (First Discourse) won the Dijon prize (1750); published 1751 (SEP-R; IEP dates "1750"). Question (IEP): "Has the restoration of the sciences and arts tended to purify morals?" Answer: no. [sources: SEP-R §1; IEP-R §1, §3]
- Opera *Le Devin du village* (1752); Querelle des Bouffons. Reconverted to Calvinism and regained Genevan citizenship 1754. *Discourse on the Origin and Foundations of Inequality* (Second Discourse) 1755, for the Dijon question "What is the origin of inequality among men, and is it authorized by the natural law?"; did not win; about four times the length of the first. *Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre* (1758) cemented the break with the Encyclopédistes. [sources: SEP-R §1; IEP-R §1]
- *Julie, ou La Nouvelle Héloïse* 1761 (bestseller). 1762: *The Social Contract* (April, IEP) and *Emile* (May). *Emile* was condemned in Paris; both condemned in Geneva for religious heterodoxy (the Savoyard Vicar's "Profession of Faith"); books burned, arrest warrants; renounced Genevan citizenship May 1763; *Letters from the Mountain* (1764). [sources: SEP-R §1; IEP-R §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emile,_or_On_Education]
- Hume: Rousseau crossed to England with Hume, arriving London 13 January 1766; stayed at Wootton Hall, Staffordshire (from 22 March 1766). Horace Walpole's hoax letter "from Frederick the Great" (published in a London paper 3 April 1766) and press attacks convinced Rousseau of a plot; SEP-R: "wrongly convinced that Hume was at the center of a plot against him". Hume published his account in French (October 1766) and English (November). Returned to France 1767. [sources: SEP-R §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau]
- *Confessions* (written 1764–70; published posthumously, IEP "several years after his death"); *Rousseau, Judge of Jean-Jacques: Dialogues*; *Reveries of the Solitary Walker*; *Considerations on the Government of Poland* (1770–71). Died 2 July 1778 at Ermenonville (IEP gives July 3 — use 2 July); remains moved to the Panthéon 11 October 1794. SEP-R: "the pioneer of modern autobiography". [sources: SEP-R §1; IEP-R §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau]
- Kant: a portrait of Rousseau was the only image in Kant's house; "legend has it" Kant missed his daily walk only when reading *Emile* (SEP-R flags as legend). [source: SEP-R §6]

## 8. Rousseau: the arguments

### 8a. Discourse on Inequality — step by step (sources: SEP-R §2–3; IEP-R §3)
1. Method: strip away what socialisation added to find natural man. Rousseau says the state of nature may never have existed (citing revelation), yet treats some stages as observable; Neuhouser (2014) reads the story as a philosophical device separating natural from artificial elements of psychology; Kelly (2006) contests this. Debate. [SEP-R §2; IEP-R §3]
2. Against Hobbes: previous accounts "have taken civilized human beings and simply removed laws, government, and technology" (IEP's summary). War of all against all requires property, foresight, recognition of others as threats, perhaps language — faculties that develop historically. Natural man is "isolated, timid, peaceful, mute, and without the foresight to worry about what the future will bring". [IEP-R §3]
3. Two pre-rational principles: *amour de soi* (self-love, drive to self-preservation) and *pitié* — "an innate repugnance to see his fellow suffer" (Second Discourse, *Collected Writings* vol. II p. 36, as quoted by IEP). Pity restrains self-interest; for Rousseau it is merely instinctual, not yet moral. [SEP-R §2, §2.1; IEP-R §3]
4. Humans differ from animals by freedom (ability not to be governed solely by appetite) and *perfectibility* (capacity to learn). These make development — and the fall — possible. [SEP-R §2]
5. Stages: temporary co-operation (hunting); settled communities, families; sexual competition and comparison give rise to *amour-propre* (comparative self-love, the need for recognition, often "pride" or "vanity"); then agriculture and metallurgy, division of labour, private property, inequality between owners and non-owners. [SEP-R §2; IEP-R §3]
6. Property and the founding of civil society (Cole's translation, WS-verified wording): "The first man who, having enclosed a piece of ground, bethought himself of saying This is mine, and found people simple enough to believe him, was the real founder of civil society." (A different, untranslated-attribution version in WP: "The first man who, having fenced in a piece of land, said 'This is mine', and found people naïve enough to believe him, that man was the true founder of civil society. From how many crimes, wars, and murders … Beware of listening to this impostor; you are undone if you once forget that the fruits of the earth belong to us all, and the earth itself to nobody.") [sources: WS: https://www.scribd.com/document/394702363/2-Anthropology-Rousseau , https://www.millerpolitics.com/rousseaudiscourse.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau]
7. Interdependence + inflamed *amour-propre* = domination, dissimulation, alienation (the European minister who must flatter). [SEP-R §2]
8. A Hobbesian war of rich and poor leads the rich to propose a contract that entrenches their property: "All ran to meet their chains thinking they secured their freedom, for although they had enough reason to feel the advantages of political establishment, they did not have enough experience to foresee its dangers" (Second Discourse, *Collected Writings* vol. II p. 54, as quoted by IEP). SEP-R: "In an echo of Locke and an anticipation of Marx", a class state. Conclusion: inequality is not natural; it is a product of history, entrenched by a fraudulent contract. [IEP-R §3; SEP-R §3]
- Objection 1 (incoherence of "natural goodness", SEP-R): if society is composed entirely of naturally good beings, how does evil get a foothold? And Rousseau says morality is not natural, so "good by nature" cannot mean morally good. Reply (SEP-R): natural goodness is negative ("the absence of evil"); corruption comes from the structure of interdependence and comparison, not from wicked individuals. [SEP-R §2]
- Objection 2 (Ferguson, 1767): there are no records of a pre-social human condition; humans have always lived in society, so the state of nature is a social state; use history and travellers' reports, not conjecture. Reply: on the "device" reading (Neuhouser), Rousseau's story is not historical history at all but an analysis separating natural from artificial; Rousseau himself denied it was a reconstruction of history "as it actually was". [SEP-S18 §11; SEP-R §2]
- Objection 3 (is *amour-propre* simply evil?): older readings (Charvet 1974) treat it as wholly negative; Dent (1988) and Neuhouser (2008) read it as both the cause of the fall and the promise of redemption, capable of benign forms (via *Emile* and the *Social Contract*); McLendon (2019) pushes back. [SEP-R §2]

### 8b. The Social Contract (1762) — step by step
- Full title *Du contrat social; ou, Principes du droit politique*; edited/printed in Amsterdam. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Social_Contract]
- Opening line, Book I ch. 1. Cole (1913): "Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains. One thinks himself the master of others, and still remains a greater slave than they." (WS: Wikiquote/Standard Ebooks listings of Cole.) IEP (Masters–Kelly *Collected Writings* vol. IV p. 131): "Man was/is born free; and everywhere he is in chains." SEP-R: "Man is born free, but is everywhere in chains" (no translator named). Rousseau then says he can make this condition legitimate. [sources: WS: https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/The_Social_Contract , https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/jean-jacques-rousseau/the-social-contract/g-d-h-cole/text/chapter-1-1 ; IEP-R §4; SEP-R §3.3]
- The problem: find "a form of association" in which each, uniting with all, "obey[s] only himself" and remains as free as before (SEP-R's quotation fragments, SC I.6). Renouncing freedom "deprive[s] one's actions of all morality" (SC I.4). [SEP-R §3, §3.3]
1. Each associate alienates all his rights to the whole community; because each gives all, conditions are equal for all and no one has an interest in making them burdensome. [SEP-E §2.1; SEP-R §3.1]
2. The general will — the collective will of the citizen body directed to the common good — is the source of law; law must come from all and apply to all, and may not name individuals. [SEP-R §3, §3.1; IEP-R §4]
3. General will vs will of all (SC II.3, Masters–Kelly translation via IEP): "There is often a great deal of difference between the will of all and the general will. The latter looks only to the common interest; the former considers private interest and is only a sum of private wills. But take away from these same wills the pluses and minuses that cancel each other out, and the remaining sum of the differences is the general will." [IEP-R §4]
4. In obeying law each obeys a will that is his own, so remains free. Exchange of natural freedom (unlimited right to all things, cf. Hobbes) for civil freedom (secured sphere under law), plus "moral freedom": "obedience to a law that one has prescribed to oneself" (SC I.8). Outvoted citizens (SC IV.2 — SEP-R says "Book 4 chapter 3"; see NOT VERIFIED) discover they were mistaken about the general will. [SEP-R §3.3]
5. "Forced to be free" (SC I.7), Cole (WS): "whoever refuses to obey the general will shall be compelled to do so by the whole body. This means nothing less than that he will be forced to be free", continuing that this secures him "against all personal dependence" (SEP-R quotes the last phrase). [sources: WS: https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/jean-jacques-rousseau/the-social-contract/g-d-h-cole/text/chapter-1-7 ; SEP-R §3.3]
6. Sovereignty cannot be represented; handing one's right of self-rule to another is "a form a slavery" (SEP-R paraphrase); yet sovereign (people making general laws) is distinguished from government (executive), and Rousseau favours an elective aristocracy for government; he is pessimistic that governments will usurp sovereignty. The legislator (Lycurgus-like) must form citizens who can will good laws — a puzzle and a regress. Civil religion (final chapter): existence of a supreme being, afterlife, sanctity of the contract; tolerate all who tolerate; those who say there is no salvation outside their church cannot be citizens. [SEP-R §3.2, §3.4, §3.5]
- Citizens are exclusively male; women "subject to the authority of the state but have no voice in the determination of the general will" (SEP-R §3.4).

### 8c. The totalitarian-reading debate (present as a debate)
- Hegel (Philosophy of Right) and Benjamin Constant blamed the general will for the Terror; SEP-R notes Hegel misrepresents the general will as an overlap of private wills, and in the *Encyclopedia Logic* shows he knew this was not Rousseau's view. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_will ; SEP-R §6]
- J. L. Talmon (1916–1980), *The Origins of Totalitarian Democracy* (1952): the general will leads to "totalitarian democracy". Popper and Russell read Rousseau similarly; Isaiah Berlin's 1952 BBC lectures (published 2002 as *Freedom and Its Betrayal: Six Enemies of Human Liberty*, ed. Henry Hardy) put Rousseau among six "enemies of human liberty"; in "Two Concepts of Liberty" (1958) Berlin lists Rousseau among thinkers who conflated positive liberty with rational action knowable by an elite. Lester Crocker (1968) also pressed the thesis. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_will ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Concepts_of_Liberty ; WS: https://contemporarythinkers.org/isaiah-berlin/book/freedom-betrayal-six-enemies-human-liberty/]
- Replies: Ralph Leigh (1963) and Maurice Cranston rejected Talmon's thesis; J. S. Maloy (2005): "The totalitarian thesis in Rousseau studies has, by now, been discredited as an attribution of real historical influence." Rawls called his two principles of justice an effort to "spell out the content of the general will" (Cohen 2010, p. 2); Joshua Cohen, *Rousseau: A Free Community of Equals* (2010), reads the general will democratically; Neuhouser (1993, "Freedom, Dependence and the General Will") explains "moral freedom" and freedom as independence from personal dependence. SEP-R: the "forced to be free" passage implies a "republican freedom" — law protects me from subjection to any particular person's will. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_will ; SEP-R §3.3, §6]
- Interpretive spectrum (SEP-R §3.1): a "democratic" conception (what citizens decide in assembly) vs a "transcendent" one (the common interest apart from what anyone wants) (Bertram 2012), plus a will of individuals toward the common good (Canon 2022); epistemic-democracy readings via Condorcet's jury theorem (Grofman and Feld 1988). Possible upshot: "a posteriori philosophical anarchism" (no actual state legitimate). SEP-R: Rousseau has inspired liberal, communitarian, republican, deliberative-democratic and (to hostile writers) authoritarian readings.

### 8d. Emile, Sophie, Wollstonecraft
- *Emile* (1762), five books; "negative education", child-centred, tutor manipulates environment; Book IV contains the Savoyard Vicar's Profession of Faith (reason for condemnation; placed on the Index); Book V on Sophie. Opening: "Everything is good as it leaves the hands of the Author of things…" (WP fragment). [sources: SEP-R §5; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emile,_or_On_Education]
- Sophie's education aims at her role as wife; she is to be submissive to Emile; Rousseau says women are cleverer and excel in practical reason (IEP). WP quotes Book V (translator unnamed): women should be "passive and weak", are "made specially to please man", though "man ought to please her in turn". SEP-R: "a rather extreme misogyny"; he does not extend his insight that apparent slavishness results from unjust subjection to women. [sources: IEP-R §5; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emile,_or_On_Education ; SEP-R §3.4]
- Wollstonecraft (27 April 1759 – 1797), *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) attacks Rousseau's *Emile* among existing pedagogies; after quoting a passage of *Emile*: "I shall make no other comments on this ingenious passage, than just to observe, that it is the philosophy of lasciviousness"; "taught from their infancy that beauty is woman's sceptre, the mind shapes itself to the body, and, roaming round its gilt cage, only seeks to adorn its prison". She proposed civil and political rights, women's own elected representatives, women physicians. Also *Vindication of the Rights of Men* (1790) against Burke. [sources: SEP-W §1, §3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Vindication_of_the_Rights_of_Woman]

### 8e. Rousseau's place
- SEP-R: Rousseau's own view of philosophers was "firmly negative" (post-hoc rationalizers of self-interest). IEP: the First Discourse is "perhaps the greatest example of Rousseau as a 'counter-Enlightenment' thinker", the Second Discourse "representative of Enlightenment thought". Everdell (1971/1987), Melzer (1996) and Garrard (2003) make Rousseau the founder of the Counter-Enlightenment, contradicting Berlin's view of him as an (erratic) philosophe. A debate. [sources: SEP-R intro; IEP-R §3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-Enlightenment]
- Rousseau and Hobbes: Rousseau rejects Hobbes's representative sovereign but shares his view that individuals keep no natural rights against the sovereign. Rousseau sometimes claimed he was only restating Locke (Brooke 2009). [source: SEP-R §3.4, §6]
- Rousseau and Machiavelli: reported (WS, secondary only) that in SC III.6 and its footnote Rousseau called *The Prince* "the book of republicans", Machiavelli having veiled his love of liberty while attached to the Medici. Not verified in a primary text. [WS: https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/9568/4/FullText.pdf ; https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/9780429020773/chapters/10.4324/9780429020773-5]

## 9. Who the Enlightenment excluded — and who took part anyway

- SEP-E: "It is striking how unenlightened many of the Enlightenment's celebrated thinkers are concerning issues of race and of gender … the rights of women and of non-white people are generally overlooked … (Mary Wollstonecraft's *Vindication* … is a noteworthy exception) … Enlightenment writings are rife with rank ethno- and Eurocentrism, often explicit." It cites Eze, *Race and the Enlightenment: A Reader*. [source: SEP-E §2.1]
- Salonnières: in the 18th century salons led by Madame Geoffrin, Mlle de Lespinasse and Madame Necker; women chose guests and topics and directed discussion. Dena Goodman, *The Republic of Letters*: "The salonnières were not social climbers but intelligent, self-educated, and educating women who adopted and implemented the values of the Enlightenment Republic of Letters"; her Habermasian reading criticised by Pekacz. Debate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salon_(gathering)]
- Émilie du Châtelet: born Paris 17 December 1706; met Voltaire 1733; Cirey; *Institutions de physique* (1740) wedding Newtonian mechanics to Leibnizian metaphysics; translation and commentary of Newton's *Principia*, unpublished until 1759, "still the only complete French translation ever published" (SEP-V) / "still the leading French translation" (SEP-Ch); died 10 September 1749 after childbirth (daughter born 4 September). Cross-ref Women Philosophers chapter. [sources: SEP-Ch intro; SEP-V §1.3, §1.5]
- Olympe de Gouges (born Marie Gouze, Montauban, 7 May 1748 – guillotined 3 November 1793): *Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen*, written 14 September, published 15 September 1791, dedicated to Marie Antoinette. Art. I: "Woman is born free and remains equal to man in rights. Social distinctions may only be based on common utility." Art. X: women have the right to mount the scaffold, so must also have the right to mount the speaker's rostrum. Also anti-slavery: *Zamore et Mirza* (1788), *Réflexions sur les hommes nègres* (1788). Executed for seditious writings (federalism / anti-Montagnard posters), not explicitly for feminism (WP). [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympe_de_Gouges ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Woman_and_of_the_Female_Citizen ; SEP-C §4]
- Hume's footnote: "Of National Characters" first appeared 1748 without it; the footnote was added in the 1753–54 *Essays and Treatises on Several Subjects* (SEP-Race: "1754 edition"; others: 1753). Text as quoted in SEP-Race (from Zack 2002): "I am apt to suspect the negroes and in general all the other species of men (for there are four or five different kinds) to be naturally inferior to whites. There never was a civilized nation of any other complexion than white, nor even any individual eminent either in action or speculation." Revised late (SEP-Race: 1776 edition; Garrett: posthumous 1777 edition) to restrict the claim to "negroes". Popkin and Zack read the 1754 version as polygenist; Valls denies either version is. [sources: SEP-Race §on Hume; WS: https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/GARHRR , https://muse.jhu.edu/article/15093/summary]
- Kant: "Of the Different Human Races" (1775, rev. 1777): monogenesis, four races from a common root, triggered "seeds"; a racial hierarchy. Debate: Eze (1995), Mills (2005), Bernasconi (2001, 2002) critical; Louden, Hill & Boxill, Kleingeld (2007: Kant became "more egalitarian with regard to race" by *Toward Perpetual Peace*, 1795, rejecting colonialism and slavery), Allais (2016); Lu-Adler (2022–23) argues Kant held a consistent inegalitarian position. Cross-ref Kant chapter. [source: SEP-Race]
- Anti-slavery voices: Montesquieu's irony; Jaucourt's "Traite des nègres"; Diderot in Raynal's *Histoire des deux Indes*; Condorcet's *Réflexions* (1781) and Amis des Noirs; Gouges. (See §§2, 4, 5.)
- Herder (1744–1803) criticised the very concept of race and denounced colonial exploitation; Forster (SEP-Her) argues Herder's "pluralist cosmopolitanism" is "a good deal purer than Kant's", which was undermined by "racism, antisemitism, and misogyny" — Forster's interpretation, flag as such. [source: SEP-Her]
- Anton Wilhelm Amo (c. 1703 – c. 1759): Nzema, born at Axim (Gold Coast, now Ghana); taken to Amsterdam c. age four by the Dutch West India Company (whether enslaved is unknown) and given to the dukes of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel; baptised 29 July 1708. Entered Halle's law school 1727; disputation *De jure Maurorum in Europa* ("On the Rights of Moors in Europe", 1729) — manuscript lost, summary in the university's *Annals* (1730). Doctorate at Wittenberg 1734: *De humanae mentis apatheia* — accepts mind–body dualism but argues sensation belongs to the living body, not the immaterial mind (argument: whatever feels lives… is a divisible body; the mind is not a divisible body; so the mind does not sense). Lectured at Halle as "Antonius Guilielmus Amo Afer"; *Treatise on the Art of Philosophising Soberly and Accurately* (1738); moved to Jena (WP gives 1736 and 1740 in different places); after his patron's death (1735) and a public lampoon in Halle, returned to the Gold Coast c. 1747; reportedly at Fort San Sebastian, Shama, in the 1750s; probably died c. 1759. English translation: Menn and Justin E. H. Smith (OUP, 2020). Berlin's Mohrenstraße renamed Anton-Wilhelm-Amo-Straße, August 2025. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton_Wilhelm_Amo]
- Haitian Revolution as a test: slave revolt began night of 21 August 1791; independence 1 January 1804. Susan Buck-Morss, "Hegel and Haiti", *Critical Inquiry* 26:4 (2000), pp. 821–865, expanded as *Hegel, Haiti, and Universal History* (Pittsburgh, 2009): Hegel must have known of the Saint-Domingue revolt through contemporary reports, and his master–slave dialectic should be read in that context; universal history must be understood as shaped by events outside Europe. This is Buck-Morss's argument (contested; present as such). [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Buck-Morss ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]

## 10. Critics of Enlightenment (as debates)

- Hamann (27 Aug 1730 – 21 June 1788), Königsberg, "the Magus of the North"; never held a university post; introduced Kant to Hume and Rousseau (WP); his unpublished "Metacritique of the Purism of Reason" answers Kant's first *Critique*: a proper view of language makes the alleged "purity" of a priori reason untenable; faith over autonomous reason. Letter of 27 July 1759 (Zweig's trans.): "I look upon the finest logical demonstration the way a sensible girl regards a love letter". [sources: SEP-Ham §4.1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Georg_Hamann]
- Herder: student of Kant; *Treatise on the Origin of Language* (prize 1771, published 1772); *This Too a Philosophy of History for the Formation of Humanity* (1774); emphasis on individuality and the particular "well advanced" the supplanting of Enlightenment by Romanticism and historicism (SEP-E). [sources: SEP-Her; SEP-E §3.3]
- Isaiah Berlin's essay "The Counter-Enlightenment" (1973; in *Against the Current*, 1981) popularised the term, tracing it to Hamann; McMahon, *Enemies of the Enlightenment* (2001), extends it to French Catholic writers; Burke's *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790) (cross-ref); Burke on Rousseau (1791): "entertained no principle … but vanity". [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-Enlightenment ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau]
- Horkheimer and Adorno, *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (*Dialektik der Aufklärung*), 1947 (Querido, Amsterdam per standard bibliography; WP infobox 1947); two theses: "Myth is already enlightenment, and enlightenment reverts to mythology" (Jephcott trans., Stanford 2002); SEP-E: they "interpret Nazi death camps as the result of 'the dialectic of the Enlightenment'", of the supremacy of instrumental reason. Cross-ref Critical Theory chapter. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectic_of_Enlightenment ; SEP-E §2.1]

## Debates
- One Enlightenment or many: Robertson (unity) vs Pocock (Enlightenments) vs Israel (radical vs moderate); Israel's critics (La Vopa, Lilti, Moyn, Edelstein, Chisick, Verbeek) reject Spinoza as single source and the monism–radicalism link. [sources: §1]
- Rousseau: Enlightenment insider or founder of the Counter-Enlightenment (Berlin vs Everdell, Melzer, Garrard). [source: WP Counter-Enlightenment]
- Status of the state of nature: historical claim vs philosophical device (Neuhouser 2014 vs Kelly 2006); Ferguson's empirical objection. [SEP-R; SEP-S18]
- *Amour-propre*: wholly negative (Charvet) vs ambivalent/redeemable (Dent, Neuhouser; McLendon dissents). [SEP-R]
- General will: democratic vs transcendent (Bertram), three general wills (Canon); totalitarian reading (Talmon, Berlin, Crocker, Popper) vs liberal/democratic readings (Leigh, Cranston, Cohen, Rawls, Neuhouser); Maloy calls the totalitarian thesis "discredited" as historical attribution. [SEP-R; WP]
- Rousseau's misogyny: Fermon (1997), Rousselière (2021) critical; Rosenblatt (2002) qualifies (as listed in SEP-R); IEP stresses he did not regard men as simply superior. [SEP-R §3.4; IEP-R §5]
- Smith and commerce: endorsement (Berry, McCloskey) vs Rousseauian worry (Dwyer) vs critique of Rousseau (Hanley). [SEP-Sm]
- Hume's footnote: polygenist (Popkin, Zack) or not (Valls). Kant on race: changed his mind (Kleingeld) or consistent inegalitarian (Lu-Adler); critics Eze, Mills, Bernasconi. [SEP-Race]
- Salons: Goodman's "Republic of Letters" thesis vs Pekacz. [WP Salon]
- Enlightenment and colonialism: Michèle Duchet argued Enlightenment anti-colonialism was largely a myth, aimed at preserving European rule (WP Age of Enlightenment); against this, SEP-D on Diderot's abolitionism and Forster on Herder. Buck-Morss on Hegel and Haiti.
- Dialectic of Enlightenment vs defenders of Enlightenment universalism (SEP-E §2.1).

## Surprises / commonly believed but contradicted
- Voltaire's "defend to the death" line is Hall's (1906).
- *Letters on the English* appeared first in **English (1733)**, a year before the French *Lettres philosophiques* (1734).
- Rousseau did **not** win the Dijon prize with the Discourse on Inequality — only with the First Discourse.
- Montesquieu, the critic of slavery, also wrote that in very hot climates slavery is "more reconcilable to reason" — his position is mixed, and his famous anti-slavery chapter is ironic.
- The "totalitarian Rousseau" thesis is now widely regarded as discredited as a claim of historical influence (Maloy), though the interpretive tension remains.
- Hume's footnote was a later addition (1753–54), revised (1776/77) to narrow — not soften — it to Black people.
