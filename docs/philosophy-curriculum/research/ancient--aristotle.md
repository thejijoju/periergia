## 1. Life (384–322 BCE)
- Born 384 BCE in Stagira, a small city in the Macedonian region of north-eastern Greece (hence "the Stagirite"); died 322 BCE. [source: SEP-Ar §1] Stagira is in Chalcidice, about 55 km east of modern Thessaloniki. [source: WP-Aristotle] The dates (first half of Olympiad year 384/383; 322 shortly before Demosthenes' death) were established by August Boeckh. [source: WP-Aristotle, note citing Düring 1957]
- Father: Nicomachus, personal physician to King Amyntas III of Macedon; mother Phaestis, from a family of Chalcis on Euboea. Both parents died while he was young and he was brought up by a guardian. [source: WP-Aristotle; SEP-Bio §1 for father/mother] The medical family background is "likely" (not certainly) the source of his interest in biology. [source: WP-Aristotle]
- Went to Athens at about 17 to study in Plato's Academy and stayed until Plato's death in 347, i.e. about twenty years. [source: SEP-Ar §1; SEP-Bio §1 "for 20 years"]. WP-Aristotle says "seventeen or eighteen" and that he left in 348/47. IEP-Bio notes Diogenes Laertius says he left before Plato's death.
- Why he left is uncertain: the traditional story is disappointment when Plato's nephew Speusippus took over the Academy; anti-Macedonian feeling in Athens may also have played a part. [source: WP-Aristotle]
- Went to Assos (north-west coast of present-day Turkey) at the invitation of Hermias (Hermeias), a former Academic and ruler of Assos/Atarneus; stayed about three years; then, around Hermias' death, moved to the nearby island of Lesbos for about two years, working with Theophrastus, a native of Lesbos. [source: SEP-Ar §1] Sources disagree on the date of Hermias' death (345, 342, or arrest in 341, per the scholars cited). [source: WP-Aristotle, note]
- Married Pythias, Hermias' niece (WP: "adoptive daughter and niece"), and had a daughter also named Pythias. [source: SEP-Ar §1; WP-Aristotle]
- In 343 (WP: 343/42) Philip II of Macedon summoned him to Pella to tutor his thirteen-year-old son Alexander. [source: SEP-Ar §1; WP-Aristotle] CAVEAT: "little concrete is known about their interaction"; SEP judges that some tuition took place "but that it lasted only two or three years", from Alexander's 13th to 15th year, since by 15 (WP: about 16) Alexander was already acting for his father; some scholars suppose up to eight years. [source: SEP-Ar §1; WP-Aristotle] Teaching reportedly took place at Mieza, near Pella. [source: WP-Aristotle]
- Little is known of 341–335; he returned to Athens in 335 and set up his own school in a public exercise area sacred to Apollo Lykeios, hence "the Lyceum". [source: SEP-Ar §1] As a metic (resident foreigner) he could not own property in Athens. [source: WP-Aristotle]
- "Peripatetics": the name probably comes from a covered walkway (peripatos) on the school's property. [source: SEP-Ar §1] (The popular story that it comes from Aristotle walking while lecturing is not what the sources here say.)
- The Lyceum researched botany, biology, logic, music, mathematics, astronomy, medicine, cosmology, physics, history of philosophy, metaphysics, psychology, ethics, theology, rhetoric, political history, government and the arts, and collected manuscripts, by some ancient accounts forming the first great library of antiquity. [source: SEP-Ar §1] His students collected the constitutional histories of 158 cities, of which the Constitution of Athens survives. [source: IEP-Pol §2]
- After Pythias died he lived with Herpyllis, perhaps also from Stagira; whether she was slave, freedwoman or wife is disputed. They had a son, Nicomachus, after whom the Nicomachean Ethics is "presumably" named. [source: SEP-Ar §1]
- After Alexander died in Babylon in 323, anti-Macedonian feeling in Athens boiled over and Aristotle left for Chalcis on Euboea (his mother's family's estate), reportedly saying he would not let Athens "sin twice against philosophy" (an "oft-repeated ancient tale", alluding to Socrates' execution). He died there of natural causes in 322. [source: SEP-Ar §1; WP-Aristotle] Date discrepancy: SEP-Ar puts his departure in 323; WP-Aristotle says he was denounced for impiety (by Demophilus and the hierophant Eurymedon) in 322 and died "later that same year". Safe wording: "left Athens after Alexander's death in 323 and died in Chalcis in 322."
- His will named Antipater executor and asked to be buried beside his wife; it reportedly freed his slaves. [source: WP-Aristotle; IEP-Pol §7d]
- Theophrastus succeeded him as head of the Lyceum. [source: WP-Aristotle]
- All biography rests on "insecure, mainly late sources" (chiefly Diogenes Laertius); two opposed ancient portraits of his character exist (arrogant ingrate vs. devoted researcher). [source: SEP-Ar n.3; WP-Aristotle]

## 2. The corpus
- He may have written as many as 200 treatises; about 31 survive in the Corpus Aristotelicum judged authentic. [source: SEP-Ar intro and §2] WP-Aristotle: "only around a third" survives. Diogenes Laertius lists 156 titles. [source: WP-Works_of_Aristotle]
- The surviving works read like "lecture notes, drafts first written and then reworked ... in-house compilations intended not for a general audience". Aristotle mentions polished "exoteric" works for a wider audience. [source: SEP-Ar §2]
- He wrote dialogues, presumably while in the Academy, which are lost except for fragments (e.g. On Philosophy, Eudemus, On Justice, Protrepticus). Cicero called his prose "a flowing river of gold" (Academica 2.38.119), so Cicero must have been reading works we no longer have. [source: SEP-Ar §2; WP-Works_of_Aristotle; WP-Bekker_numbering]
- Transmission story (from Strabo and Plutarch): the papers passed to Theophrastus, then Neleus of Scepsis, whose heirs hid them in a cellar; the collector Apellicon bought them (c. 100 BCE); Sulla took the library to Rome after taking Athens in 86 BCE; there Andronicus of Rhodes (fl. c. 60 BCE; 11th head of the Peripatetic school) produced the edition on which our texts are based. [source: WP-Works_of_Aristotle; WP-Andronicus_of_Rhodes] Treat the cellar story as an ancient report; other copies of major works were already circulating. [source: WP-Aristotle]
- Several titles are not Aristotle's own (e.g. Categories, Metaphysics); some treatises may have been put together by later editors. [source: SEP-Ar §2 and n.4]
- Aristotle divided the sciences into theoretical (first philosophy/metaphysics, mathematics, physics), practical (ethics, politics) and productive (crafts, poetry, rhetoric). [source: SEP-Ar §2]
- Bekker numbers (e.g. 1094a1, the opening of the Nicomachean Ethics) refer to page, column and line of the Prussian Academy edition edited by August Immanuel Bekker (Berlin, 1831–1837). The Constitution of the Athenians lacks Bekker numbers because it was recovered from papyrus after Bekker's edition (WP-Bekker_numbering: papyri acquired 1890, first edited 1891; WP-Works_of_Aristotle says "discovered in 1879" — sources differ). [source: WP-Bekker_numbering]
- The logical treatises were later grouped as the Organon ("instrument"): Categories, On Interpretation, Prior Analytics, Posterior Analytics, Topics, Sophistical Refutations. Aristotle never used the word; the title takes a side in a later dispute over whether logic is a part of philosophy (Stoics) or its tool (later Peripatetics). [source: SEP-Log §2]

## 3. Logic: the syllogistic
- Aristotle's logical works "contain the earliest formal study of logic that we have". [source: SEP-Log §1] "Logic as a fully systematic discipline begins with Aristotle." [source: SEP-AncLog]
- Definition of a deduction (sullogismos): "A deduction is speech (logos) in which, certain things having been supposed, something different from those supposed results of necessity because of their being so." (Prior Analytics I.2, 24b18–20; Smith's translation as given in SEP) [source: SEP-Log §3]
- The four categorical forms (later lettered A, E, I, O): Every S is P / No S is P / Some S is P / Some S is not P (Aristotle's own wording of O is "Not every S is P"). [source: SEP-Log §4.3; SEP-Sq §2.2]
- A syllogism has two premises and a conclusion with exactly three terms; the shared "middle term" disappears from the conclusion. Aristotle sorted premise pairs into three "figures" by the position of the middle term. [source: SEP-Log §5; SEP-AncLog §2.4]
- He went through the 58 possible premise combinations and showed that 14 yield a conclusion of necessity; he treated first-figure syllogisms as "complete" (self-evident) and proved the others by reducing them to the first figure (conversion, reductio, "ecthesis"). All can be reduced to Barbara and Celarent. [source: SEP-AncLog §2.4; SEP-Log §5.4]
- He used letters in place of terms — schematic letters or term variables — an innovation. [source: SEP-AncLog §2.4]
- Mnemonic names (Barbara = AAA, Celarent = EAE, etc.) are medieval. [source: WP-Syllogism]
- Valid example (Barbara, first figure; illustrative wording, ours): Every animal is mortal; every human is an animal; therefore every human is mortal. The form is valid whatever terms are substituted. [form from SEP-Log §5; example constructed]
- The famous "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal" is NOT Aristotle's example: WP traces it to J. S. Mill's System of Logic (3rd ed. 1851); and Aristotle's syllogistic discusses only universal and particular sentences — singular sentences ("Socrates is...") "seem to be excluded". [source: WP-Syllogism; SEP-AncLog §2.4]
- Square of opposition (from On Interpretation; diagram first appears in the 2nd century CE, later via Boethius): A and O are contradictories (exactly one true), E and I are contradictories; A and E are contraries (cannot both be true, can both be false); I and O are subcontraries (cannot both be false); I is subaltern to A, O to E (the universal implies the particular). [source: SEP-Sq §1, §2.1; IEP-Log]
- Modern logic (since Boole/Frege) reads "Every S is P" as true when there are no S, so contrariety and subalternation fail for empty terms; the traditional square is defended if the O form is read "Not every S is P" (Aristotle's wording). [source: SEP-Sq §1, §2.2]
- Modal syllogistic (necessity/possibility) is far more problematic; e.g. the "two Barbaras" problem (he accepts Barbara with a necessary major premise and assertoric minor yielding a necessary conclusion, but rejects the reverse), disputed since antiquity. [source: SEP-Log §5.6]
- On Interpretation 9 ("sea-battle tomorrow") makes an apparent exception to bivalence for future contingents; interpretation debated. [source: SEP-Log §4, §12; SEP-AncLog]
- WHAT IT COULD NOT DO: Aristotle's syllogistic "covers only a small part of all arguments" that are valid. [source: SEP-AncLog §2.4] It is a term logic built on subject–predicate form; it cannot treat relations and multiple generality as Frege's logic does: e.g. in Aristotelian logic the inference from "John loves Mary" to "Something loves Mary" and the one to "John loves something" "have nothing in common", whereas Frege's quantifier logic treats both by one rule. [source: SEP-Frege §2.2.3] Since Frege and Russell "very few would try to maintain that it is adequate as a basis for understanding science, mathematics, or even everyday reasoning". [source: SEP-Log §1] Propositional logic (if/or/and) was developed separately by Diodorus Cronus, Philo and above all the Stoic Chrysippus (mid-3rd c. BCE), whom many in antiquity regarded as the greatest logician. [source: SEP-AncLog intro]
- Reputation: Kant held that nothing significant had been added to Aristotle's logic in two millennia (SEP-Log §1; WP-Syllogism places it in Kant's Logic, 1800). Aristotle's logic "was taught by and large without rival from the 4th to the 19th centuries CE." [source: SEP-AncLog §2] Jonathan Lear: "Aristotle shares with modern logicians a fundamental interest in metatheory." [source: SEP-Log §1]
- First predicate logic: Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879). [source: WP-Term_logic]

## 4. Categories and substance
- The Categories gives a list of ten categories (1b25–2a4): substance, quantity, quality, relation, where, when, being-in-a-position, having, doing, being-affected; IEP's examples: "horse/man" (substance), "four feet long" (quantity), "white" (quality), "double" (relation), "in the market-place" (where), "last year" (when), "sitting" (position), "has shoes on" (having), "cutting" (doing), "being burned" (being affected). Other works give shorter lists; whether ten is meant to be complete is an open question. [source: IEP-Log; SEP-Cat §1]
- Four-fold division of beings by "said of" and "present in": primary substances are neither said of nor present in a subject (2a11–13); his favourite examples are an individual man and an individual horse. Species and genera ("man", "animal") are secondary substances. [source: SEP-Cat; SEP-Met §2]
- "Were primary substances not to exist then no other entity would exist" (2b6): in the Categories concrete individuals like Socrates are the most real things — "firmly anti-Platonic". [source: SEP-Cat]
- In Metaphysics Zeta he reopens "What is substance?" (1028b4) and points toward form as substance: "by form I mean the essence of each thing and the primary substance" (1032b1). How this squares with the Categories (individuals as primary substances), and whether forms are universal or particular, are long-running scholarly controversies. [source: SEP-Met §§2, 8–9]

## 5. Hylomorphism, the four causes, potentiality and actuality, the unmoved mover
- Hylomorphism: ordinary objects (statues, houses, horses, humans) are composites of matter (hulê) and form (eidos/morphê). It was devised to answer Parmenides' and Zeno's puzzles about change: in every change something persists (matter/substrate) and something is gained or lost (form). Argument in SEP's reconstruction: (1) change requires matter and form; (2) there is change; so (3) there are matter and form. Socrates getting a tan: Socrates persists, pallor lost, tan gained. [source: SEP-Ar §8]
- Four causes (aitia), Physics II.3, 194b23–35: material ("that out of which", the bronze of a statue), formal ("the form or the pattern", e.g. the ratio 2:1 of the octave), efficient ("the primary source of the change", e.g. the sculptor; "the father is the cause of the child"), final ("that for the sake of which", e.g. health is the cause of walking about). [source: SEP-Ar §7]
- Not every thing has all four: coincidences lack a final cause (SEP's example: a debtor and creditor meeting by chance at the market); mathematical abstractions lack a material cause. Some scholars prefer "becauses"/explanations to "causes" for aitia. [source: SEP-Ar §7]
- Potentiality (dunamis) vs actuality (energeia/entelecheia), Metaphysics Theta: activity is to potentiality as "what is awake is in relation to what is asleep, and what is seeing is in relation to what has its eyes closed but has sight, and what has been shaped out of the matter is in relation to the matter" (1048b1–3, Reeve). Wood is potentially a table or a bowl. Potentiality is not mere possibility: bronze is potentially a statue but not potentially butter (SEP-Ar §8). [source: SEP-Met §7; SEP-Ar §8]
- Actuality is prior to potentiality in account, in substance and even in a sense in time: an acorn comes before its oak but is preceded by the adult oak that produced it. [source: SEP-Met §7]
- Motion is defined as the actuality of a potentiality of the thing undergoing motion, in so far as it is potential (Physics). [source: SEP-Nat §5]
- The unmoved mover: Physics VIII argues there must be a first unmoved mover; Metaphysics Lambda: the eternally circling heaven is moved by something "that moves without being moved, being eternal, substance, and activity", moving things as "the object of desire and the intelligible object move things" (1072a21–27, Reeve). It is pure activity of understanding; its life is "best and eternal" (1072b18–28); it thinks itself: "active understanding of active understanding" (hê noêsis noêseôs noêsis, 1074b34–35). He counts the celestial movers at forty-nine (Λ.8, 1074a16). [source: SEP-Met §11; SEP-Ar §2; SEP-Nat §5]
- Teleology: nothing potential brings itself into actuality; "no pile of bricks ... spontaneously organizes itself into a house". Final causation is the most controversial of the four. [source: SEP-Ar §9]

## 6. The soul as the form of the body
- De Anima: the soul is "a first actuality of a natural body which has life in potentiality" (II.1, 412a27–8); "substance as form of a natural body which has life in potentiality" (412a20–1); "first actuality of a natural organic body" (412b5–6). This applies to plants, animals and humans alike. [source: SEP-Psy §2]
- "It is not necessary to ask whether soul and body are one, just as it is not necessary to ask whether the wax and its shape are one" (412b6–9). [source: SEP-Psy §3]
- Hence the soul is generally not separable from the body (413a3–5), but he adds that some parts may be separable because they are not the actuality of any bodily part (413a6–7) — the root of the long dispute over the "active mind" of De Anima III.5. [source: SEP-Psy §3, Supplement G]
- Hierarchy of soul-capacities: nutritive (all living things; plants have only this), perceptual (animals), intellectual (among natural organisms only humans). [source: SEP-Psy; SEP-Eth §2]

## 7. Biology
- Aristotle and Theophrastus "originated the science of biology", Aristotle for animals, Theophrastus for plants (Enquiry into Plants). [source: SEP-Bio §1; WP-Aristotle's_biology]
- On Lesbos he spent about two years studying its zoology, especially the lagoon of Pyrrha in the centre of the island, now the Gulf (Bay) of Kalloni. [source: WP-Aristotle's_biology citing Leroi 2014 p.14; WP-Kalloni]
- Biological works include History of Animals, Parts of Animals, Generation of Animals, Movement of Animals. Data came from his own observation, from specialists (fishermen, beekeepers) and from less reliable travellers' reports. [source: WP-Aristotle's_biology]
- He examined over 500 species (IEP-Bio §8); his accounts of about 35 animals are detailed enough to show he dissected them, and he mentions the internal anatomy of about 110. [source: WP-Aristotle's_biology citing Leroi]
- Good observations: ruminants' four-chambered stomach; the dogfish (smooth hound, Mustelus) whose embryo is attached by a placenta-like cord (in fact a yolk sac) — reinvestigated by Johannes Müller in 1840; the octopus changing colour; opening hens' eggs at intervals and seeing the heart early (supporting epigenesis against preformation). [source: WP-Aristotle's_biology; IEP-Bio §8]
- The hectocotylus: Aristotle described the specialised arm of male cephalopods and reported fishermen's claim that it is used in mating. IMPORTANT NUANCE: he himself allowed it served "only for the sake of attachment" and rejected that it was useful for generation, since "it is outside the passage and indeed outside the body" (GA 720b; HA 541b9–12, 544a6–14). Nineteenth-century biologists found the reported function correct; Cuvier, finding a detached one in a female argonaut, took it for a parasitic worm and named it Hectocotylus (1829). [source: WP-Aristotle's_biology; WP-Hectocotylus] IEP-Bio says the cephalopod sperm-transfer process "was not fully verified by moderns until 1959" — sources disagree on the date of full confirmation; say "confirmed by modern zoology (from the nineteenth century on)".
- Errors: thought thinking happens in the region of the heart and the brain is a cooling organ (PA 652b21–25); thought men hotter than women; overweighted the male contribution to reproduction (male supplies form, female only matter); wrong count of women's teeth; believed in spontaneous generation (e.g. flies from dung). [source: IEP-Bio §8] William Harvey's De motu cordis (1628) showed the blood circulates and the heart is a pump, against Aristotle. [source: WP-Aristotle]
- Later reputation: Peter Medawar (1984) called the zoology "a strange and generally speaking rather tiresome farrago of hearsay, imperfect observation, wishful thinking and credulity amounting to downright gullibility", while modern observation has confirmed many surprising claims (octopus camouflage; elephants snorkelling). [source: WP-Aristotle's_biology citing Leroi]

## 8. Ethics: Nicomachean Ethics
- Two ethical treatises, Nicomachean and Eudemian; the titles are later, perhaps after the editors (son Nicomachus, friend Eudemus). [source: SEP-Eth §1]
- The highest good is desirable for itself, not for anything else, and everything else is desired for its sake; all agree to call it eudaimonia ("happiness", "flourishing") = "living well" (eu zên). [source: SEP-Eth §2]

### The function (ergon) argument, NE I.7 (1097b22–1098a20) — step by step [sources: SEP-Eth §2; IEP-Ar §practical philosophy; WP-Nicomachean_Ethics]
1. Happiness is agreed to be the highest good, but we need to say what it consists in.
2. For anything that has a function (a flautist, a sculptor, an eye, a hand), its good and "doing well" lie in performing that function, and doing it well.
3. A human being has a function.
4. The function must be peculiar to humans: not mere life/nutrition (shared with plants) nor perception (shared with animals), but the active life of the part of the soul that has reason.
5. Doing a function well requires the relevant excellence (aretê, "virtue" in a broad sense).
6. Therefore the human good is "activity of soul exhibiting excellence, and if there are more than one excellence, in accordance with the best and most complete" — and "in a complete life", for "one swallow does not make a summer" (Ross).
- Objection 1: Aristotle assumes without argument that humans have a characteristic function at all (it does not follow from flautists and eyes having one). Reply (Angier 2010, per IEP): the key is the craft analogy — as a master craftsman's specialised activity needs a range of subordinate skills, so acting well as a human needs an inclusive set of capacities serving reason. [source: IEP-Ar]
- Objection 2: even granted, the argument is too general to show that the traditional virtues (justice, courage, temperance) are what flourishing needs. Replies: (a) Aristotle addresses only those already well brought up (1095b4–6), not Plato's sceptic; or (b) Book I is explicitly an "outline" (1098a20–22) whose content is filled in by the later accounts of the particular virtues. [source: SEP-Eth §3.1]
- Further objections in the secondary literature (why must the good be what is distinctive? does having a function give a reason to perform it?) are noted in discussions surfaced by search, e.g. https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/GUAWII — lower-grade sources; use only in general terms. [source: WebSearch]
- He insists happiness is activity, not a state; and it needs external goods (friends, some wealth, power; extreme misfortune — e.g. losing children — mars it, 1099a31–b6). [source: SEP-Ar §12; SEP-Eth §2]

### Virtue, habit, the mean, practical wisdom
- Two kinds of virtue (1103a1–10): intellectual and ethical (of character). Intellectual virtue comes by teaching; virtue of character comes about by habituation. [source: SEP-Eth §4; WP-Nicomachean_Ethics]
- Character virtue is a hexis (stable disposition) to have appropriate feelings (1105b25–6) — rejecting the Socratic thesis that virtue is just knowledge. [source: SEP-Eth §5.1]
- Doctrine of the mean (1106a26–b28): each ethical virtue lies between a vice of excess and one of deficiency — courage between cowardice and rashness. The mean is "relative to us", not arithmetic: if 10 lb of food is too much and 2 too little, it does not follow I should eat 6 (SEP's example of an athlete's diet). Sometimes great anger is the mean. [source: SEP-Eth §5.1]
- Ethical virtue is fully developed only together with practical wisdom (phronêsis) (1144b14–17); practical wisdom cannot be acquired solely by learning general rules; ethics gives no decision procedure. [source: SEP-Eth §§4, 5.2, intro]
- Sachs (IEP) warns against reducing Aristotle's ethics to "habit": character (êthos) is not the same as habit (ethos) because it involves choice. [source: IEP-Eth; WP-Nicomachean_Ethics]

### Akrasia (weakness of will) — against Socrates
- NE VII: akrasia ("lack of mastery") vs enkrateia (continence). Two kinds: weakness (deliberates, then acts against the choice) and impetuosity (acts on passion without deliberating). [source: SEP-Eth §7]
- Socrates held there is no akrasia, which Aristotle says "clearly conflicts with the appearances" (1145b25–8); yet his analysis concludes "in a way Socrates was right after all" (1147b13–17): the akratic has knowledge only as the drunk, asleep or the actor reciting lines have it. His agreement is partial: he insists emotions can rival, weaken or bypass reason. [source: SEP-Eth §7]

### Friendship
- Books VIII–IX. Philia is wider than English "friendship" (includes family ties). Three bases: the good, the useful, the pleasant; friendship of the good is complete/perfect, the others imperfect and unstable. A friend wishes good to the other for the other's sake (eunoia, goodwill), reciprocated and recognised. [source: SEP-Eth §9]

### Contemplation (Book X)
- X.7–8: the happiest life is that of theoria (contemplation/study) by one who already has theoretical wisdom, compared to the life of a god; the political life of practical virtue is happy "in a secondary way". Some scholars think X.7–8 clashes with the rest of the Ethics; Kraut (SEP) argues they are continuous (one needs the ethical virtues to live the philosophic life). This is the "inclusivist vs intellectualist/dominant-end" debate. [source: SEP-Eth §10; Kraut 1979 "Two Conceptions of Happiness" cited in SEP-Ar bibliography]
- Aquinas objected that identifying the ultimate good with an earthly activity makes it something other than God. [source: SEP-Ren on NE I.7]

## 9. Politics
- "Man is by nature a political animal" (Politics I.2, 1252b30–1253a3, as cited by IEP in Carnes Lord's translation; the phrase is at 1253a2–3). [source: IEP-Pol §7b] Reason: nature does nothing in vain and gave humans alone speech (logos), which reveals the advantageous and harmful, the just and unjust (1253a7–18). [source: IEP-Pol §7c; SEP-Pol supplement 3]
- The polis "comes to be for the sake of life, and exists for the sake of the good life" (I.2.1252b27–30, Miller's rendering; Shields: "comes into being for the sake of living, but it remains in existence for the sake of living well"). It grows naturally from household (male–female; master–slave) to village to city. [source: SEP-Pol supplement 3; SEP-Ar §12]
- The city is prior by nature to the individual (1253a18–29): "if the whole [body] is destroyed there will not be a foot or a hand" (1253a20, Lord via IEP). One who cannot live in a city is "either a beast or a god". "Just as, when perfected, a human is the best of animals, so also when separated from law and justice, he is the worst of all" (1253a31–3). [source: SEP-Pol supplement 2/3; IEP-Ar; IEP-Pol]
- Debate: how can the city be both natural and founded by a lawgiver ("the one who first constituted [a city] is responsible for the greatest of goods", 1253a29–31)? Problem posed by David Keyt; solutions by Ernest Barker, Fred Miller, Trevor Saunders, Adriel Trott. [source: SEP-Pol supplement 3]
- Citizen = one entitled to share in deliberative or judicial office (1275b18–21). [source: SEP-Pol §3]
- Six constitutions (Politics III.7, adapted from Plato's Statesman 302c–d): correct forms aim at the common advantage — kingship (one), aristocracy (few), polity (many); deviant forms aim at the rulers' advantage — tyranny, oligarchy, democracy (1279a17–21). Oligarchy is really rule of the rich and democracy of the poor; polity is later a mixed constitution resting on the middle class (IV.11). [source: SEP-Pol §3]
- Natural slavery (Politics I.4–7/8): a natural slave is one "who participates in reason only to the extent of perceiving it, but does not have it" (1254b16–23, Lord via IEP); slaves are "living tools" (I.4); he even calls war to capture people "naturally suited to be ruled but unwilling" just (1256b25). He also argues that many actually enslaved (war captives, slaves' children) are not slaves by nature (I.6, 1255b3), and that nature fails to make the difference visible — so his theory undermines the two main actual sources of slaves. [source: IEP-Pol §7d] Miller: he asserts "without evidence" that natural slaves lack a deliberative faculty, and gives no argument why despotic rule should serve the master rather than the slave. [source: SEP-Pol §3]
- Women: "the male is by nature more capable of leadership than the female, unless he is constituted in some way contrary to nature" (I.12.1259a39–b4); "The slave is wholly lacking the deliberative element; the female has it but it lacks authority [akuron]; the child has it but it is incomplete" (I.13.1260a12–14). Marital rule is "political" yet the husband always rules. Women's virtues differ: "a ruling and a serving courage" (1260a19 as cited by IEP). [source: SEP-Pol §3; IEP-Pol §7e]
- Debate on "lacks authority": either women's reason cannot command men (because of temperament or Greek culture), or reason lacks authority over women's own emotions; Clayton (IEP) notes the vicious circle of exclusion. Some commentators think the arguments about slaves and women are so weak they are ironic; Miller rejects that. [source: IEP-Pol §7e; SEP-Pol §3] SEP-Pol's bibliography (section "Women, Children, and Slaves") lists debate literature: Fortenbaugh, Modrak, Deslauriers, Mayhew (The Female in Aristotle's Biology), Garnsey (Ideas of Slavery from Aristotle to Augustine), Pellegrin ("Natural Slavery"). [source: SEP-Pol bibliography]
- In biology he held that the female contributes only matter and the male form (IEP-Bio §8) — one root of these views.

## 10. Poetics and catharsis
- Tragedy (Poetics 6, 1449b24–28, Butcher): "an imitation of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude; in language embellished with each kind of artistic ornament ... in the form of action, not of narrative; through pity and fear effecting the proper catharsis of these emotions." [source: WP-Poetics_(Aristotle)]
- Katharsis appears only once in the Poetics, in this definition (IEP-Poet); it is also used of music in the Politics (WP quotes the passage on melodies serving "both of education and of purgation"), with a promise to explain it "more explicitly" in the poetry discussion — an explanation not in our text. [source: IEP-Poet §2; WP-Catharsis]
- Interpretive debate: purgation (medical sense; F. L. Lucas; Jakob Bernays 1857, whose work influenced Breuer and Freud's "cathartic method"); purification/moral balancing (Lessing, 1769: tragedy brings excessive or deficient pity and fear back to a mean); intellectual clarification (Leon Golden, Stephen Halliwell); Gerald Else argued "purgation" presupposes spectators as patients, with no support in the text; Elizabeth Belfiore proposed an allopathic reading. [source: WP-Catharsis; IEP-Poet §3]
- The best plot turns on a good person's hamartia ("mistake", later romanticised as "tragic flaw"). [source: WP-Poetics_(Aristotle)]
- A second book on comedy is lost (central to Eco's The Name of the Rose, 1980). [source: WP-Poetics_(Aristotle)]
- Learning through imitation is natural and a delight to humans (Poet. 1448b4–24). [source: SEP-Ar §13]

## 11. Afterlife (reception)
### Antiquity
- After his death his works seem to have fallen out of circulation for a century and reappear in the 1st century BCE; there followed some seven centuries of commentary tradition (Alexander of Aphrodisias, holder of an imperial chair in Aristotelian philosophy created by Marcus Aurelius; Neoplatonist commentators such as Porphyry, whose Isagoge introduced the Categories, and Simplicius). [source: SEP-Ar §14; WP-Aristotle]
- In the Latin West Aristotle was practically unknown c. 600–1100 except Boethius' Latin Organon. [source: WP-Aristotle]

### Islamic world
- Under the ʿAbbasids (750–1258), especially in Baghdad, Greek philosophy and science were translated into Arabic, much via Syriac (the Graeco-Arabic translation movement; Gutas' subtitle dates it 2nd–4th/8th–10th centuries). [source: SEP-ArGr §§1–2; WP-Graeco-Arabic_translation_movement]
- The "circle of al-Kindī" (the "first faylasūf") translated Aristotle's Metaphysics and De Caelo; the so-called "Theology of Aristotle" was actually an adapted translation of Plotinus, Enneads IV–VI — a confusion that coloured how Aristotle was read. [source: SEP-ArGr §3]
- Hunayn ibn Ishaq (d. 873), a Nestorian Christian physician, his son Ishaq ibn Hunayn (d. 911) and associates made available the complete Aristotelian corpus; later Abu Bishr Matta and Yahya ibn ʿAdi (the Baghdad Aristotelians). [source: SEP-ArGr §§4–5]
- Aristotle was called "the First Teacher" (al-muʿallim al-awwal). [source: SEP-Ar §14; SEP-IbnSina]
- Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā, c. 970–1037, born near Bukhara), the "Preeminent Master". [source: SEP-IbnSina]
- Averroes (Ibn Rushd, 1126 Córdoba – 1198 Marrakesh) wrote three kinds of commentary on Aristotle: short (epitome), middle (paraphrase) and long (literal). Latin translations began with Michael Scot c. 1220; by the 1230s he was known in Latin Europe simply as "the Commentator"; a papal decree of 1341 told the Paris arts faculty to follow "the science of Aristotle and his commentator Averroes ... except in cases that run counter to the faith". [source: SEP-IbnRushd intro, §1, §9.3]
- The Poetics reached the medieval West through a Latin translation of Averroes' Arabic (middle commentary). [source: WP-Poetics_(Aristotle)]

### Jewish philosophy
- Maimonides (Moses ben Maimon, 1138 Córdoba – 1204 Fustat; birth often given as 1135 but research favours 1138) wrote the Guide of the Perplexed (completed 1190, in Judeo-Arabic), "heavily influenced by the Neo-Platonized Aristotelianism" of Islamic circles but stressing the limits of human knowledge. [source: SEP-Maim and notes; WP-Maimonides]
- On creation he holds (like Aquinas) that neither creation nor eternity can be demonstrated; he contrasts Moses (creation ex nihilo), Plato (creation from pre-existing matter) and Aristotle (eternal world) and prefers Moses. [source: SEP-Maim §5]
- He told Samuel ibn Tibbon that Aristotle's works suffice and are superior to earlier philosophers'. [source: WP-Aristotle]

### Latin Christendom
- 12th–13th-century Latin translations from Arabic (Gerard of Cremona) and Greek (James of Venice, William of Moerbeke). [source: WP-Aristotle]
- Albert the Great and above all Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274) sought to reconcile Aristotle with Christian thought; Aquinas wrote commentaries on Aristotle and calls him "the Philosopher". [source: SEP-Ar §14; SEP-Aq; WP-Aristotle] Aquinas: "if anything is found in the words of the philosophers that is contrary to the faith, this is not philosophy but rather an abuse of philosophy, due to a failure of reason" (Comm. on Boethius' De trinitate 2.3c). He argued (On the Eternity of the World, 1271) that no demonstration is possible either way. [source: SEP-Aq]
- The Paris arts curriculum "consisted largely in the works of Aristotle". [source: SEP-Aq]
- 1210: a provincial synod at Sens forbade reading Aristotle's natural philosophy and its commentaries at Paris. [source: WP-Condemnations_of_1210–1277]
- 7 March 1277: Bishop Stephen Tempier of Paris prohibited teaching of 219 theses discussed in the arts faculty; motives, targets (e.g. Siger of Brabant, Boethius of Dacia; possibly Aquinas's views) and effects are disputed. Pierre Duhem held 1277 marked "the birth of modern science" (by asserting God's absolute power, e.g. that several worlds or a rectilinear motion of the heavens are possible); "no one in the field any longer endorses" that view, though Edward Grant comes closest. [source: SEP-1277 intro, §5; WP-Condemnations]
- Dante calls Aristotle "the master of those who know" (Inferno IV.131). [source: WP-Aristotle]
- Peter John Olivi complained Aristotle "without reason ... is believed, as the god of this age". [source: SEP-IbnRushd §9.3]

### The Scientific Revolution's break
- Galileo's mathematical-experimental approach "implicitly challenged both Aristotelian physics and cosmology"; in the 1632 Dialogue Aristotelianism is represented by Simplicio. Harvey (1628) refuted the heart-centred physiology. Bacon (1623) complained Aristotle sought final causes in physics. [source: SEP-Ren; WP-Aristotle's_biology; WP-Aristotle]
- Yet universities kept teaching Aristotle long after Bacon and Descartes, and Descartes' break with Aristotle "was perhaps less radical and momentous than [he] claimed". [source: SEP-Ren]

### Modern revival
- Virtue ethics "re-emerged in Anglo-American philosophy in the late 1950s", heralded by G. E. M. Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy" (Philosophy 33, no. 124, January 1958, pp. 1–19), which argued that the concepts of moral obligation and the moral "ought" should be jettisoned and that moral philosophy should wait for an adequate philosophy of psychology; it also coined "consequentialism". [source: SEP-VE §1; WP-Modern_Moral_Philosophy]
- Developed by Alasdair MacIntyre (After Virtue, 1981), Philippa Foot, Rosalind Hursthouse; recent work turns also to Confucian, Buddhist and Hindu traditions; in the East virtue ethics goes back to Confucius and Mencius. [source: SEP-VE §1 and §4; WP-After_Virtue]

## Debates
- Aristotle and Alexander: how much influence? Tuition probably 2–3 years; some argue for up to eight; little concrete is known. [source: SEP-Ar §1]
- Categories vs Metaphysics Zeta on what is primary substance (individual vs form), and whether forms are universal or particular. [source: SEP-Met; SEP-Cat]
- Causes or "becauses"? Whether aitia are causes or explanations. [source: SEP-Ar §7]
- The active mind (De Anima III.5): separable, divine, personal? Basis of the later Averroist "unicity of the intellect" controversy. [source: SEP-Psy Suppl. G; SEP-IbnRushd §9.3]
- NE X.7–8 contemplation vs the life of practical virtue: inclusive vs dominant-end readings (Kraut and others). [source: SEP-Eth §10]
- Whether the function argument assumes what it needs (see §8). [source: IEP-Ar; SEP-Eth §3.1]
- City as natural vs made by a lawgiver (Keyt; Barker/Miller/Saunders; Trott). [source: SEP-Pol suppl. 3]
- Natural slavery: does the theory justify Greek slavery, or (as Clayton stresses) undercut the actual sources of slaves (war captives, slaves' children)? Miller: asserted "without evidence". Some read the arguments as ironic; Miller and others reject that. [source: IEP-Pol §7d; SEP-Pol §3]
- Women's deliberative capacity "without authority" (akuron): what it means is debated. [source: IEP-Pol §7e]
- Catharsis: purgation vs purification vs intellectual clarification (Bernays, Lessing, Lucas, Else, Golden, Halliwell, Belfiore). [source: WP-Catharsis; IEP-Poet]
- Origin of falsafa: most scholars credit the Greek translation movement; some locate it in Qurʾanic ḥikma. [source: SEP-ArGr intro]
- 1277: anti-Averroist/anti-"radical Aristotelian" measure (Mandonnet, van Steenberghen) vs attempt to curb philosophy as an autonomous path to happiness (de Libera, Flasch, Bianchi); Duhem's "birth of modern science" thesis now rejected. [source: SEP-1277 §5]
- Aquinas's synthesis: brilliant synthesis vs "bastardizing Aristotle" vs "pandering to pagan philosophy". [source: SEP-Ar §14]
- Was the scientific revolution's break with Aristotle as sharp as claimed? SEP-Ren: Descartes' break "perhaps less radical and momentous than [he] claimed"; universities kept teaching Aristotle. [source: SEP-Ren]

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use
- "Amicus Plato, sed magis amica veritas" as Aristotle's words: MISATTRIBUTED in that form. It is a later Latin proverb loosely paraphrasing NE I.6, 1096a11–17 (and echoing Plato's Phaedo 91b–c, where Socrates says to care little for Socrates and much more for truth). Medieval forms: Berengar of Poitiers (c. 1150) attributes "Amicus est Socrates, sed magis amica est veritas" to Aristotle; Roger Bacon (Opus Majus I.5) attributes a "Socrates" version to Plato; a version occurs in a medieval Life of Aristotle (Vita Marciana); Aquinas's NE commentary (I, lect. 6) cites "amicus quidem Socrates sed magis amica veritas" as Plato's. The exact "Amicus Plato..." wording is popularised by Cervantes, Don Quixote II.51; Newton's 1661 notebook has "Amicus Plato amicus Aristoteles magis amica veritas". [source: WP-Amicus_Plato,_sed_magis_amica_veritas, citing Guerlac 1978 and Tarán 1984]
- "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal" as Aristotle's example — not his (Mill 1851 per WP-Syllogism; Aristotle's syllogistic excludes singular terms per SEP-AncLog).
- "We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit" — not checked in any source this session (often said to be a modern paraphrase); do not attribute to Aristotle without verification.
- Plato calling Aristotle "the mind (nous) of the school" / "the reader" — reported in WP-Aristotle from a secondary source (Hazel 2013); late anecdote, not verified in SEP/IEP.
- Aristotle advising Alexander to be "a leader to the Greeks and a despot to the barbarians" — WP-Aristotle via Green 1991; ancient source (Plutarch) not checked.
- Story that "Peripatetic" comes from Aristotle lecturing while walking — SEP-Ar derives it "probably" from the peripatos (walkway) on the property.
- Exact Ross wording "we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts" (NE II.1): WebSearch indicates it is Ross's wording, but the exact Bekker line (c. 1103a34–b2) was not confirmed; use only if checked.

- The "House of Wisdom" (Bayt al-Ḥikma) as a translation academy — WP presents it so, but whether it was a translation academy or mainly a library is (to my knowledge) disputed; not verified this session. Avoid or hedge.
