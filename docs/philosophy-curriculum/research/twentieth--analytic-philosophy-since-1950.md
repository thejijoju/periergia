## The shape of the period (framing)
- IEP-AP divides analytic philosophy into five phases: 1900–10 (Moore/Russell realism), 1910–30 (logical atomism, ideal-language analysis), 1930–45 (logical positivism), **1945–65 (ordinary-language analysis: Wittgenstein and John Wisdom at Cambridge; Ryle, Austin, Strawson, Grice at Oxford)**, and from the mid-1960s a fifth phase of "eclecticism or pluralism" with no common doctrine, loosely united by a style emphasising "precision and thoroughness about a narrow topic" [source: IEP-AP §1].
- IEP-AP: "During the 1960s, criticism from within and without caused the analytic movement to abandon its linguistic form. Linguistic philosophy gave way to the philosophy of language, the philosophy of language gave way to metaphysics, and this gave way to a variety of philosophical sub-disciplines" [source: IEP-AP §1].
- IEP-AP §6b ("The Renaissance in Metaphysics"): metaphysics is now "one of its three most important sub-disciplines" (with epistemology and philosophy of language), "noteworthy given analytic philosophy's traditional anti-metaphysical orientation". Causes it names: the collapse of the theories of meaning that had banned metaphysics; Strawson's "descriptive metaphysics" (1960s, in IEP's phrasing — the book is 1959); Quine's holism; Davidson's Tarskian semantics leading to Lewis's possible-worlds semantics; and in the 1970s direct-reference theories "developed independently by Saul Kripke and Ruth Barcan Marcus" — "tags" (Marcus's term) or "rigid designators" (Kripke's term) [source: IEP-AP §6b].
- IEP-AP §6c also records a "renaissance in history": from the 1970s analytic philosophers began to take the history of philosophy (and later of analytic philosophy itself) seriously; a sign reading "just say no to the history of ideas" reportedly once hung on a door in Princeton's philosophy building (Grafton 2004) [source: IEP-AP §6c].
- The SEP's Uebel (cited in the published *Logical Positivism* chapter) and IEP-AP agree metaphysics returned, "though not of the kind the Circle combated" — consistent framing.

## Ordinary language philosophy at Oxford
- IEP-OL: "Ordinary Language philosophy is generally associated with the (later) views of Ludwig Wittgenstein, and with the work done by the philosophers of Oxford University between approximately 1945-1970"; its origins go back to Cambridge, "usually marked as beginning in 1929 with the return of Wittgenstein"; Moore was an influence "though not an Ordinary Language philosopher himself"; early figures John Wisdom, Norman Malcolm, Alice Ambrose, Morris Lazerowitz; later phase Ryle, Austin, Strawson [source: IEP-OL intro]. Dates disagree slightly: IEP-OL says c. 1945–1970, IEP-AP says 1945–1965 (the published *Wittgenstein* chapter uses "about 1945 and 1970").
- Wittgenstein "never accept[ed] any label for his work, let alone 'Ordinary Language' philosophy" [source: IEP-OL §1].
- The Oxford philosophers were "much more critical" of Wittgenstein than the Cambridge Wittgensteinians, "much less dogmatically" linguistic and "more interested in applying their views to real, current, philosophical problems" [source: IEP-OL §4].
- Critics called the therapeutic method "therapeutic positivism" (Farrell 1946) and charged it with "quietism"; Benson Mates (1958) asked how claims about "what we say" could be verified; Stanley Cavell (1958) replied that such claims are normative, not empirical [source: IEP-OL §2–3].
- Outside critics: Ernest Gellner's *Words and Things* (1959), which "had a large, international impact", and W. C. K. Mundle's *Critique of Linguistic Philosophy* (1970); T. P. Uschanov's phrase "the strange death of ordinary language philosophy" [source: IEP-AP §6a].
- IEP-OL: "It was ultimately the re-introduction of the possibility of a systematic theory of meaning by Grice, later at Oxford ... that finally spelled the end for Ordinary Language philosophy" [source: IEP-OL §3/§5].

### Ryle
- Gilbert Ryle: born Brighton, 19 August 1900; Queen's College, Oxford, from 1919; first in PPE 1924; lecturer then tutor at Christ Church; Waynflete Professor of Metaphysical Philosophy from 1945 (at Magdalen); retired 1968; Welsh Guards intelligence officer in WWII, rising to Major; editor of *Mind* 1947–1971 (after Moore); died 6 October 1976 at Whitby after a day's walking on the moors [source: SEP-RY §1]. (WP Gilbert_Ryle adds a "triple first": Mods 1921, Greats 1923, PPE 1924.)
- He "befriended Wittgenstein whose work ... he greatly admired" [source: SEP-RY §1].
- *The Concept of Mind*, 1949 [source: SEP-RY; WP-CM]. Earlier: "Knowing How and Knowing That", *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 46 (1946), "ostensibly reworked as the second chapter" of the book [source: SEP-RY §7 and bibliography].
- Ryle calls the Cartesian picture "the Official Doctrine"; IEP-OL: "what Ryle calls the 'Dogma of the Ghost in the Machine' (1949, pp. 15)" [source: SEP-RY §6; IEP-OL §4a]. WP-CM quotes Ryle on the doctrine: "I hope to prove that it is entirely false, and false not in detail but in principle. It is not merely an assemblage of particular mistakes. It is one big mistake and a mistake of a special kind. It is, namely, a category-mistake." [source: WP-CM].
- Ryle "consistently hyphenates 'category-mistake'"; Ofra Magidor believes this is the first use of the label [source: WP-CAT].
- **The university example** (first example, chapter 1, *Concept of Mind* p. 16 in the University of Chicago Press edition): a visitor to Oxford is shown the colleges, libraries, playing fields, etc., and then asks "But where is the University?" — mistakenly allocating the University to the same category as the institutions that make it up. Ryle's further examples: a child watching a division march past asks when the division will come after seeing battalions, batteries and squadrons; a foreigner watching cricket asks who contributes the "team-spirit" [source: WP-CAT; WS summary of commentators]. The exact full wording of the passage could NOT be read in a primary text; quote only "But where is the University?" (see NOT VERIFIED).
- SEP-RY: Ryle's target "was not merely the ghostliness of the mental processes hypothesized by the Cartesian; it was their essential hidden-ness" — his objections apply equally to any theory making mental terms name hidden inner processes, "whether irreducibly mental or at bottom physical" [source: SEP-RY §6].
- SEP-RY on Ryle's own label: "Ryle is often described as an ordinary language philosopher ... In a certain sense this is apt; in another it is not" — philosophy studies the "stock use" of expressions, technical or vernacular [source: SEP-RY §5].
- Ryle's legacy in philosophy of mind: credited with tying mental predicates to behaviour, but "Place, Armstrong, Putnam, and Fodor" thought he misconstrued the connection, replacing his conceptual links with causal ones [source: SEP-RY §6]. He is commonly called a "logical behaviourist" (WP Gilbert_Ryle: "Some of Ryle's ideas ... have been called logical behaviourist").

### Austin
- John Langshaw Austin: born Lancaster, 26 March 1911; Balliol (Classics) 1929; First in Lit. Hum. 1933; Fellow of All Souls 1933; Magdalen tutor 1935; British Intelligence Corps in WWII ("he more than anybody was responsible for the life-saving accuracy of the D-Day intelligence", reported in Warnock 1963); left the army 1945 as lieutenant colonel; White's Professor of Moral Philosophy 1952; died in Oxford, 8 February 1960 [source: SEP-AU §1].
- He "wrote little and published less"; instituted the "Saturday Morning" discussion sessions (on Aristotle's *Ethics*, Frege's *Grundlagen*, Wittgenstein's *Investigations*, Merleau-Ponty's *Phenomenology of Perception*, Chomsky's *Syntactic Structures*) [source: SEP-AU §1].
- He gave the **William James Lectures at Harvard in 1955**; a version was published posthumously as *How to Do Things with Words* (1962; 2nd edn ed. J. O. Urmson and Marina Sbisà, 1975) [source: SEP-AU §1 and bibliography]. *Sense and Sensibilia* (1962) was "reconstructed from the manuscript notes by G. J. Warnock" [source: SEP-AU bibliography]; its title puns on Jane Austen's *Sense and Sensibility*; it attacks the sense-datum theory (e.g. the stick that "looks bent" in water) [source: IEP-OL §4b].
- **Performatives vs constatives.** Austin's opening examples: "I take ... to be my lawfully wedded ..." (marriage), "I name this ship the Queen Elizabeth" (bottle against the stern), "I give and bequeath my watch to my brother" (a will), "I bet you sixpence it will rain tomorrow" [source: SEP-AU §2.3, citing 1962b: 5]. He writes that to utter such a sentence "is not to describe my doing ... or to state that I am doing it. None of the utterances cited is either true or false: I assert this as obvious and do not argue it" (1962b: 6) [source: SEP-AU §2.3].
- SEP-AU notes the irony: that assertion uses the performative form "I assert …", and a footnote warns "Everything said in these sections is provisional, and subject to revision in the light of later sections" (1962b: 4 fn. 1) [source: SEP-AU §2.3].
- **Felicity.** Performatives are assessed for "happiness and unhappiness, or felicity and infelicity". Two main infelicities: **misfires** (the act is not achieved: wrong formula, already married, "it is the purser and not the captain who is conducting the ceremony") and **abuses** (performed but insincerely) (1962b: 15–16) [source: SEP-AU §2.3].
- "From the wreckage of the initial distinction" Austin builds a new model: the **locutionary** act (saying something with a content), the **illocutionary** act (with a force: stating, warning, promising) and the **perlocutionary** act (effects on the audience: persuading, encouraging) (1962b: 94–101). The need for this distinction "is now very widely accepted and probably amounts to Austin's central contribution" [source: SEP-AU §2.3].
- Austin's distinction between serious and non-serious ("parasitic") uses was challenged by Derrida (1977) and defended by Searle (1977) — a recurring point of analytic–continental contact [source: SEP-AU §2.3]. (Cross-ref: *Structuralism and After* covers the Searle–Derrida exchange.)
- Austin's remark: "Certainly ordinary language has no claim to be the last word, if there is such a thing. It embodies, indeed, something better than the metaphysics of the Stone Age, namely… the inherited experience and acumen of many generations of men" [source: IEP-OL §4, quoting Austin; the source paper is "A Plea for Excuses" (1956/57), per IEP-OL bibliography — the attribution to that paper is standard but I only saw IEP's citation].
- Austin called his method "linguistic phenomenology" (1956, p. 182) [source: IEP-OL §4b].

### Strawson
- Peter Frederick Strawson: born London, 23 November 1919; PPE at St John's, Oxford, 1937–40, tutored by H. P. Grice; war service; Fellow of University College 1948; "On Referring" (*Mind*, 1950) and his debate with Austin about truth made him internationally famous; from 1968 Ryle's successor as Waynflete Professor; British Academy 1960; knighted 1977; Library of Living Philosophers volume 1998; died Oxford, 13 February 2006 [source: SEP-ST §1].
- SEP-ST: he "succeeded in redirecting Oxford philosophy away from the limitations which had to some extent been accepted under the influence of Austin, towards a re-engagement with some traditional abstract philosophical issues" [source: SEP-ST §1].
- *Individuals* (1959) coined "descriptive metaphysics", opposed to "revisionary metaphysics" [source: SEP-ST §5]. Strawson: "there is a massive central core of human thinking which has no history ... there are categories and concepts which, in their most fundamental character, change not at all" (1959, 10) — "a recognisably Kantian thesis" [source: SEP-ST §5].
- Strawson on the limits of ordinary-language method: "Up to a point, the reliance upon a close examination of the actual use of words is the best, and indeed the only sure, way in philosophy. But the discriminations we can make ... are not general enough and not far-reaching enough to meet the full metaphysical demand for understanding" (1959, pp. 9–10) [source: IEP-OL §4c].
- *The Bounds of Sense* (1966), on Kant's first *Critique* [source: SEP-ST §6]. (Cross-ref *Kant* chapter, which lists Strawson among two-world readers.)

### Grice
- Herbert Paul Grice: born Birmingham, 13 March 1913; Corpus Christi, Oxford; taught at Oxford (with nearly five years in the Royal Navy) until 1967, then the University of California, Berkeley; died Berkeley, 28 August 1988. His **William James Lectures at Harvard, early 1967**, "circulated widely in unauthorized manuscript form" until published in *Studies in the Way of Words* (1989, posthumous) [source: SEP-GR §1]. "Logic and Conversation" appeared in Davidson and Harman (eds.), *The Logic of Grammar* (1975), pp. 64–75 [source: SEP-GR bibliography].
- **Cooperative Principle**: "Make your conversational contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged" (Grice 1989, 26). Maxims of Quantity, Quality ("Try to make your contribution one that is true"), Relation ("Be relevant") and Manner ("Be perspicuous") [source: SEP-GR].
- Examples: "I'm low on gas" / "There is a station around the corner" implicates that the station is open; implicatures are cancellable ("but I don't know whether it is open") [source: SEP-GR]. The testimonial: "Dear Sir, Mr. X's command of English is excellent, and his attendance at tutorials has been regular. Yours, etc." — implicating that X is a poor candidate [source: SEP-IMP, citing Grice 1975: 33].
- **Why this ended OLP** (IEP-OL's account): Grice distinguished what words mean (semantics) from what speakers communicate (pragmatics/implicature); the ordinary-language philosophers' appeals to the oddness of saying something (e.g. Malcolm on "know") confused meaning with use; Grice reopened "the pursuit of a systematic theory of language". Grice: "My primary aim is… to determine how any such distinction between meaning and use is to be drawn ... Any serious attempt to achieve this aim will, I think, involve a search for a systematic theory of language…" (1989, p. 4). Note Grice "remained more or less in agreement with the general view that 'ordinary language is correct'" [source: IEP-OL §5].
- Grice and Strawson, "In Defense of a Dogma" (1956), replying to Quine [source: IEP-MM §6; cross-ref *Logical Positivism and Its Critics*, which already covers it].

## The Oxford "quartet": Anscombe, Foot, Murdoch, Midgley
- All four were at Oxford around the war. SEP-FO: "These four philosophers, recently dubbed 'the Quartet,' formed lasting intellectual relationships during their Oxford years" (citing Lipscomb and Mac Cumhaill & Wiseman) [source: SEP-FO §1]. SEP-AN: at Oxford Anscombe met "Iris Murdoch, Mary Midgley, and, above all, Philippa Foot, with whom she developed a deep friendship her entire life" [source: SEP-AN §1].
- Midgley's own explanation (autobiography *The Owl of Minerva*, 2005, p. 123): with many men away at war, "I think myself that this experience has something to do with the fact that Elizabeth [Anscombe] and I and Iris [Murdoch] and Philippa Foot and Mary Warnock have all made our names in philosophy... I do think that in normal times a lot of good female thinking is wasted because it simply doesn't get heard." [source: WP-MM, citing the page].
- Two recent books: Benjamin J. B. Lipscomb, *The Women Are Up to Something: How Elizabeth Anscombe, Philippa Foot, Mary Midgley, and Iris Murdoch Revolutionized Ethics* (Oxford University Press; dated **2021** by SEP-MU and WP-MM, **2022** by SEP-FO — say "2021"; ISBN 978-0197541074); Clare Mac Cumhaill and Rachael Wiseman, *Metaphysical Animals: How Four Women Brought Philosophy Back to Life* (2022; Doubleday in New York; WP lists London too) [source: SEP-MU bib; SEP-FO bib; SEP-AN bib; WP-MM; WP-GEMA].

### Anscombe
- G. E. M. (Elizabeth) Anscombe: born Limerick, 18 March 1919; converted to Catholicism in her early teens; St Hugh's, Oxford (1937); First in Lit. Hum. 1941; Newnham, Cambridge, 1942, where she met Wittgenstein; research fellow at Somerville 1946 (teaching fellow 1964); in 1970 took the Cambridge chair "formerly occupied by Wittgenstein"; retired 1986; married Peter Geach 1941 (seven children); Wittgenstein's chief translator after his death in 1951; died Cambridge, 5 January 2001 [source: SEP-AN §1].
- 1948 Socratic Club paper attacking C. S. Lewis's argument (in *Miracles*, ch. 3) that naturalism is self-refuting; "accounts vary" on whether Lewis was crushed, but he "significantly rewrote the relevant chapter" [source: SEP-AN §1].
- **Truman protest**: "In 1956 she publicly opposed Oxford University's decision to award an honorary degree to Harry Truman, whom she considered a murderer for intentionally killing the civilians of Hiroshima and Nagasaki" [source: SEP-AN §1]. Her pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree" (privately produced) is dated 1958 by SEP-AN §2 — protest 1956, pamphlet 1958. Key line: "choosing to kill the innocent as a means to your ends is always murder" (MTD, 66, as reprinted in *Ethics, Religion and Politics*) [source: SEP-AN §2].
- *Intention* (Oxford: Blackwell, 1957; 2nd edn 1963) — "one of the classics of 20th century" philosophy; she wrote it after being perplexed by Truman's defenders [source: SEP-AN §4 and bibliography].
- **"Modern Moral Philosophy"**, *Philosophy* 33 (1958): 1–19. Three theses: (1) moral philosophy is useless until we have an adequate philosophy of psychology; (2) we should abandon the concepts of moral obligation, moral duty and the moral "ought", which make sense only in a law-based ethics with a divine legislator; (3) there are no important differences among English moral philosophers from Sidgwick onwards [source: SEP-AN §2].
- She coined **"consequentialism"** ("a term she invented") — originally a doctrine about responsibility (equal responsibility for intended and merely foreseen consequences), traced to Sidgwick; on her usage Mill was *not* a consequentialist and W. D. Ross was [source: SEP-AN §2; WP-MMP].
- She held that talk of moral obligation without a legislator was not false (Mackie) but nonsense — "like castaways talking about whether their clothing accords with the new company policy" (SEP's analogy summarising her) [source: SEP-AN §2].
- She "flouted gender conventions": did not take her husband's name, wore men's clothes, smoked cigars [source: SEP-AN §1].

### Foot
- Philippa Foot (née Bosanquet): born 3 October 1920, Owston Ferry, Lincolnshire; granddaughter of U.S. President Grover Cleveland; no formal schooling; Somerville (PPE); Somerville fellow 1949; married M. R. D. Foot (ended 1960); resigned fellowship 1969; UCLA from 1976 to 1991; died on her 90th birthday, 3 October 2010 [source: SEP-FO §1]. Credits Anscombe with introducing her to Wittgenstein [source: SEP-FO §1].
- "It is sometimes said that Foot was a founding member of Oxfam; however, the earliest records of her involvement date from 1948, six years after the founding" [source: SEP-FO §1] — see NOT VERIFIED.
- **Trolley problem**: "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect" (*Oxford Review* no. 5, 1967; repr. *Virtues and Vices*, 1978) [source: SEP-FO §4; WP-TP]. Foot's original agent is "the driver of a runaway tram, which he can only steer from one narrow track on to another; five men are working on one track and one man on the other", set beside a judge who can stop a riot only by framing an innocent [source: WP-TP, quoting Foot]. Her explanation: negative rights (not to be harmed) outweigh positive rights (to aid); the driver faces negative rights on both sides, so should minimise their violation [source: SEP-FO §4]. Thomson named "the trolley problem" (*The Monist*, 1976) and moved the choice to a bystander (*Yale Law Journal*, 1985) [source: WP-TP] — consistent with the published *What Is Philosophy?* chapter.
- **Natural Goodness** (Oxford University Press, 2001): late ethical naturalism built on "natural normativity", drawing on her student Michael Thompson — to grasp something as an organism is to evaluate it against its life form [source: SEP-FO §6].
- "Morality as a System of Hypothetical Imperatives" (1972) [source: SEP-FO §3].

### Murdoch
- Iris Murdoch (1919–1999): her first book was *Sartre: Romantic Rationalist* (1953); she helped bring Sartre, Beauvoir and Camus to English readers; left her Oxford post in 1963; twenty-six novels from 1954 [source: SEP-MU §2]. She combined analytic training with Continental, Christian, Hindu and Buddhist influences [source: SEP-MU intro].
- *The Sovereignty of Good* (London: Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1970): three 1960s essays ("The Idea of Perfection", "On 'God' and 'Good'", "The Sovereignty of Good over Other Concepts") [source: SEP-MU §2; WP-SG]. *Metaphysics as a Guide to Morals* (1992) [source: SEP-MU §2].

## Rawls and the revival of political philosophy
- John Rawls (1921–2002): born and raised in Baltimore; studied at Princeton and Cornell (influenced by Norman Malcolm) and at Oxford (H. L. A. Hart, Isaiah Berlin, Stuart Hampshire); lost his Christian faith as an infantryman in WWII; opposed the Vietnam draft as discriminating against black and poor Americans; Harvard from 1962 for over thirty years [source: SEP-RA §1].
- *A Theory of Justice* (Belknap/Harvard, 1971; revised edition 1999); *Political Liberalism* (Columbia, 1993); *The Law of Peoples* (1999); *Justice as Fairness: A Restatement* (2001) [source: SEP-RA §1; WP-TJ; WP-PL].
- "the first virtue of social institutions" (Rawls 1971, p. 3; 1999, p. 3) [source: SEP-JU intro]. (Full line "Justice is the first virtue of social institutions, as truth is of systems of thought" — only the quoted fragment was seen.)
- Rawls's aim, from the Preface: a "workable and systematic moral conception to oppose" utilitarianism (TJ xvii) [source: IEP-RA §2b].
- Reviewers: Marshall Cohen in the *New York Times Book Review* (1972) called it "magisterial" [source: WP-TJ].

### The argument from the original position, step by step (for the chapter's reconstruction)
1. Question: what are fair terms of cooperation for free and equal citizens? Rawls translates it into: "What terms of cooperation would free and equal citizens agree to under fair conditions?" — placing him in the contract tradition of Locke, Rousseau and Kant [source: SEP-RA §4.6]. The OP is "frankly and completely hypothetical" [source: IEP-RA §2c].
2. Negative thesis: citizens do not deserve to be born rich or poor, more or less gifted, male or female, of a given race; these facts are "morally arbitrary" [source: SEP-RA §4.2].
3. **Veil of ignorance**: parties do not know race, ethnicity, gender, age, income, wealth, natural endowments, comprehensive doctrine, or generation of those they represent, nor their society's political system or level of development; they do know general facts of social science, that conditions are of "moderate scarcity", and that people have different conceptions of the good [source: SEP-RA §4.6]. Parties want more rather than fewer **primary goods** (rights, liberties, opportunities, income and wealth, the social bases of self-respect) and are "mutually disinterested" (TJ 12, 123) [source: IEP-RA §2c].
4. Choice is between Rawls's principles and utilitarianism (average and classical) [source: SEP-RA §4.7; SEP-OP §6.2].
5. **Maximin**: it is rational to maximise the minimum share. Rawls's three conditions (TJ 154–55 / 134 rev.): no reliable basis for probabilities; the maximin choice gives an acceptable minimum one "cares relatively little" to exceed; the alternatives risk outcomes "we could not accept and live with". Later Rawls said the first plays a minor role and the second and third are crucial (JF 99) [source: SEP-OP §6.2.2].
6. Under average utility, the liberties of a weak minority could be sacrificed for majority gain; no party would gamble with "the political standing and deepest commitments" of those it represents [source: SEP-RA §4.7].
7. Conclusion — **the two principles** (1971 wording as quoted by SEP-OP): First: "Each person has an equal right to the most extensive total system of equal basic liberties compatible with a similar system of liberty for all" (TJ 266 rev.; revised in 1982/PL to "a fully adequate scheme"). Second: "Social and economic inequalities are to satisfy two conditions. First they must attach to offices and positions open to all under conditions of fair equality of opportunity; and second they must be to the greatest advantage of the least advantaged members of society [the **difference principle**]" (PL 281, JF 42–43, TJ 301/266 rev.) [source: SEP-OP §6.2.1]. Lexical priority: liberty first; fair equality of opportunity before the difference principle [source: SEP-RA §4.3; SEP-OP §6.1].
8. Also the "General Conception": "All social goods ... are to be distributed equally unless an unequal distribution of any or all of these goods is to the advantage of the least favored" (TJ 1971, 302) [source: SEP-OP §6.2.1].
- **Main objection (Harsanyi, 1953/1975)**: rational choice under uncertainty assigns equal probability to each position and so selects **average utility**; maximin is an irrational, "risk-averse" rule. **Reply**: the parties are not psychologically risk-averse; it is rational to choose conservatively when fundamental interests are at stake and there are no "do-overs", as with buying insurance [source: SEP-OP §6.2.2; IEP-RA §2c].
- Method: **reflective equilibrium** (narrow vs wide) [source: SEP-RA §2.4].

### Objections and later developments
- **Nozick**, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (New York: Basic Books, 1974); National Book Award (Philosophy and Religion) 1975 [source: SEP-NO intro; WP-ASU]. Nozick (Brooklyn 1938 – 2002) argued only a minimal state protecting life, liberty, property and contract is legitimate; historical entitlement theory vs "end-state" and "patterned" principles; taxation is "on a par with forced labor" (169) [source: SEP-NO].
  - **Wilt Chamberlain** ("How Liberty Upsets Patterns"): start from any favoured just pattern P1; a million fans each pay Chamberlain 25 cents to see him play, giving him $250,000 and a new pattern P2. "If the people were entitled to dispose of the resources to which they were entitled (under D1), didn't this include their being entitled to give it to, or exchange it with Wilt Chamberlain?" (161). "No end-state principle or distributional patterned principle of justice can be continually realized without continuous interference with people's lives" (163) [source: SEP-NO §4.2]. Hillel Steiner's quip: patterned theories "create rights to interfere with the rights that they have created" [source: SEP-NO §4.2]. SEP-NO stresses this is an internal problem for patterned principles, not a question-begging appeal to liberty.
- **Communitarians**: MacIntyre, Sandel, Taylor, Walzer, drawing on Aristotle and Hegel; they "never did identify themselves with the communitarian movement (the communitarian label was pinned on them by others, usually critics)" [source: SEP-CO intro]. Sandel and Taylor argued Rawls relies on "an overly individualistic conception of the self", neglecting selves constituted by communal attachments [source: SEP-CO §2]. Sandel, *Liberalism and the Limits of Justice* (Cambridge UP, 1982; 2nd edn 1998): the "unencumbered" self of the original position [source: WP-LLJ]. Rawls's *Political Liberalism* argues "in a communitarian vein" that his conception of the person describes liberal-democratic political culture [source: SEP-CO §1].
- **Okin**, *Justice, Gender, and the Family* (New York: Basic Books, 1989): theories of Rawls, Nozick, MacIntyre and Walzer each rest on a faulty assumption about gender or the family; they wrongly assume the family is just, though children learn their values there [source: WP-OK; SEP-FP bib]. Okin (Auckland, 19 July 1946 – 3 March 2004); she also *used* Rawls to extend liberal theory to women [source: WP-OK; SEP-FP §2].
- **Mills**, *The Racial Contract* (Cornell University Press, 1997): the social contract theories of Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau and Kant, presented as race-neutral, were in fact a "racial contract" [source: WP-RC]. Mills (3 January 1951 – 20 September 2021), Jamaican-born [source: WP Charles_W._Mills]. Later Mills feared Rawls's ideal theory could "whitewash" non-white oppression (2013) and proposed a non-ideal contract whose parties know their society's history of racial exploitation and would choose reparations; Tommie Shelby replied that ideal theory remains indispensable (2013) [source: SEP-RACE]. 25th-anniversary edition 2022 with foreword by Shelby; over 50,000 copies sold [source: WP-RC].
- **Political Liberalism** (1993): the problem of stability under reasonable pluralism; an **overlapping consensus** in which citizens holding different reasonable comprehensive doctrines each endorse the same political conception for their own reasons [source: SEP-RA §3.5].

## Kripke, naming and necessity
- Saul Kripke (13 November 1940, Bay Shore NY/raised Omaha – 15 September 2022): published a completeness theorem in modal logic at 18 ("A Completeness Theorem in Modal Logic", 1959); Harvard BA mathematics 1962; Rockefeller 1968–76; Princeton 1978; later CUNY Graduate Center; Schock Prize 2001 [source: WP Saul_Kripke; IEP-MM bib]. (Birthplace not checked in detail — say "raised in Omaha".)
- *Naming and Necessity*: three lectures at Princeton, **20, 22 and 29 January 1970**; transcript published 1972 in Davidson and Harman (eds.), *Semantics of Natural Language* (Dordrecht: Reidel), pp. 253–355, 763–69; book, Harvard University Press, 1980 [source: WP-NN; IEP-MM bib]. (Consistent with the published *Birth of Analytic Philosophy* chapter's "1972/1980".)
- Kripke attributes descriptivism (a name means, or has its reference fixed by, a description or cluster of descriptions) to Frege, Russell, Wittgenstein and Searle [source: WP-NN].

### The case against descriptivism, step by step
- **Descriptivism**: "Socrates" means (or refers via) "the teacher of Plato"; Searle (1958) weakened it to a cluster theory: the referent satisfies most of a cluster of descriptions [source: SEP-NA §2.6; WP-NN].
- **Modal argument**: "Socrates might not have taught Plato" is true, but "the teacher of Plato might not have taught Plato" (on the natural reading) is false. So names are **rigid designators** — they pick out the same object in every possible world where it exists — and most descriptions are not (WP's example: "Richard Nixon" vs "the person who won the 1968 U.S. presidential election") [source: SEP-REF §2.1; WP-NN].
- **Epistemic argument**: if "Socrates" meant "the teacher of Plato", it would be a priori that Socrates taught Plato; but it is not — documents could show otherwise (Kripke 1980: 65) [source: SEP-NA §2.3].
- **Semantic argument**: (a) speakers often lack uniquely identifying descriptions — ordinary people know only that Feynman was "a physicist", yet refer to Feynman [source: SEP-REF §2.1]; (b) even a uniquely identifying description can pick out the wrong person: the **Gödel/Schmidt** case — if the incompleteness proof was really by an unknown Schmidt, "Gödel" would still refer to Gödel (Kripke 1980, p. 71 area) [source: WP-NN; SEP-XP quoting the vignette].
- **Alternative — the causal picture** (Kripke refused to call it a theory): reference is fixed by an initial "dubbing" or "baptism" and passed on through chains of communication; borrowers need not be able to identify their lenders [source: SEP-REF §2.2; WP-CTR]. Geach (1969) and Donnellan (1970) adumbrated similar views [source: SEP-REF §2.2].
- **Objection — Evans's Madagascar** ("The Causal Theory of Names", *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society Supp. Vol.* 47 (1973): 187–208): Marco Polo took "Madagascar", a native name for part of the African mainland, to name the island; the name's reference changed, which a pure chain-from-baptism theory cannot explain [source: WP-CTR; SEP-NA bib].
- **Replies**: Michael Devitt — reference can be "grounded" repeatedly in an object (multiple groundings), allowing change [source: WP-CTR]; Evans's own "name-using practice" theory, in which the dominant source of information matters [source: SEP-NA §2.7]. Descriptivist replies: "rigidified" descriptions ("the *actual* teacher of Plato") [source: SEP-REF §2.1]; Strawson (1959) lets speakers rely on others/experts for the descriptive content — handles Feynman "but at an intuitive cost" [source: SEP-REF §2.1]. (Published *Birth of Analytic Philosophy* chapter already lists rigidified descriptions, reference-fixing descriptions, community clusters and Chalmers–Jackson apriorism.)
- **Experimental twist**: Machery, Mallon, Nichols and Stich (2004) gave Kripke's Gödel case to students at Rutgers and the University of Hong Kong: 56.5% of Westerners vs 31.5% of East Asians gave the causal-historical answer (31 Westerners, 41 East Asians); direct replications are mixed, but a clarified version replicates [source: SEP-XP].

### Necessity, the a priori and essence
- Kripke separated three notions often conflated: analyticity (semantic), necessity (metaphysical), a priori (epistemic) [source: IEP-MM §6].
- **Necessary a posteriori**: "Hesperus is Phosphorus", "Cicero is Tully", "Water is H2O" — true identities between rigid designators are necessary, though discovered empirically. "Thus Kripke claims to have refuted the assumption ... that anything that is necessarily true will be known a priori (e.g., Immanuel Kant)" [source: WP-NN]. SEP-AP: "it is widely believed that some necessary truths are ... knowable only empirically (e.g., 'Water is H2O')" [source: SEP-AP].
- **Contingent a priori — the standard metre**: "the standard meter stick, S, in Paris is a meter long at t0" (Kripke 1972: 274–275). "One meter" rigidly designates the length L that S actually had at t0; S itself could have been longer or shorter, so the statement is contingent; yet one who fixed the reference this way knows it without empirical investigation [source: SEP-AP].
- "Jack the Ripper": a name whose reference is fixed by a description, yet the description is not its meaning — the Ripper might not have committed the murders [source: WP-NN].
- Kripke answered Quine's "mathematical cyclist" objection to de re modality; "Most philosophers are now convinced" Kripke (1972) and Plantinga (1974) answered it [source: SEP-ME §3].
- Anti-materialist argument (Lecture III): an identity like "pain = C-fibre firing" would have to be a necessary a posteriori identity, but pain without C-fibre firing seems possible; Chalmers later made similar arguments [source: WP-NN].
- Priority dispute: Quentin Smith (1995) claimed Ruth Barcan Marcus anticipated key ideas; Neale and Soames argued he was mistaken; "Smith's view is controversial" [source: WP-NN]. IEP-AP: direct reference "developed independently by Saul Kripke and Ruth Barcan Marcus".
- Kripke also gave the 1973 John Locke Lectures, *Reference and Existence* (published 2013) [source: WP-NN], and *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982) — covered in the *Wittgenstein* chapter.

### Putnam's Twin Earth
- Hilary Putnam (31 July 1926 – 13 March 2016) [source: WP Hilary_Putnam].
- "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975), in Putnam, *Philosophical Papers* vol. 2, *Mind, Language and Reality* (Cambridge UP) [source: SEP-CE bib]; Twin Earth first in "Meaning and Reference" (1973) [source: WP-TE].
- The thought experiment: Twin Earth duplicates Earth except that the watery stuff is not H2O but a different substance (Putnam's abbreviation XYZ); set in 1750, before chemistry; Oscar and Twin Oscar are molecule-for-molecule duplicates in identical psychological states, but Oscar's "water" refers to H2O and Twin Oscar's to XYZ. So meaning (reference, truth-conditions) is not fixed by what is in the head [source: SEP-CE §3.1; WP-TE].
- The quotation: "'meanings' just ain't in the head" (Putnam 1975/1985, p. 227) [source: WP-TE]. SEP-CE renders the full sentence "Cut the pie any way you like, meaning just ain't in the head" and calls it an "initially over-exuberant" formulation: the correct conclusion concerns how states are individuated, not where they are located (Davidson's sunburn analogy) [source: SEP-CE §3.1]. Safest wording: Cut the pie any way you like, "meanings" just ain't in the head — WS confirmed the line occurs in the paper but not the page.
- Burge's "arthritis" thought experiment (1979) extends externalism to the social environment [source: SEP-CE §3.1].

## The revival of metaphysics
- **Quine, "On What There Is"**, *Review of Metaphysics* 2(1) (1948): 21–38; repr. *From a Logical Point of View* (1953) [source: SEP-OC bib]. Criterion of ontological commitment: "A theory is committed to those and only those entities to which the bound variables of the theory must be capable of referring in order that the affirmations made in the theory be true" (Quine 1948: 33) [source: SEP-OC §1]. Quine's jibe at the Meinongian Wyman: "to give Wyman the word 'exist'. I'll try not to use it again; I still have 'is'" (1948: 23) [source: SEP-OC]. Quine's "desert landscape" is alluded to in SEP-OC. (Cross-ref: *Logical Positivism and Its Critics* covers Quine but does not discuss this paper.)
- Strawson's descriptive metaphysics (1959) — above.
- **David Lewis** (1941–2001): born Oberlin, Ohio; Swarthmore; heard Austin's last lectures and was tutored by Iris Murdoch during a year in Oxford; Harvard PhD under Quine (became *Convention*, 1969); UCLA 1966–70; Princeton 1970–2001; visited Australia almost every year 1979–99 [source: SEP-DL §1]. Books: *Convention* (1969), *Counterfactuals* (1973), *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), *Parts of Classes* (1991) [source: SEP-DL §1].
- **Modal realism**: this world is one among many like it; a proposition is possibly true iff true in some world; individuals exist in only one world and have **counterparts** elsewhere [source: SEP-DL §6]. Famous passage (*Counterfactuals*, 1973: 84): "I believe, and so do you, that things could have been different in countless ways ... I therefore believe in the existence of entities that might be called 'ways things could have been.' I prefer to call them 'possible worlds.'" [source: SEP-DL §6].
- *On the Plurality of Worlds* (Oxford: Blackwell, 1986) grew out of his 1984 John Locke lectures; ch. 1 "A Philosophers' Paradise" argues possible worlds earn their keep in analysing modality, counterfactuals, content and properties — an indispensability argument; worlds are concrete and spatiotemporally isolated [source: SEP-DL §6.1; IEP-MM bib].
- Objection, the **"incredulous stare"**: the ontology is wildly at variance with common sense; Lewis concedes it "does disagree, to an extreme extent, with firm common sense opinion about what there is" (1986, 133) but argues no rival explains so much so economically [source: SEP-PW §2; IEP-MM §2].
- Lewis's "Humean supervenience": "all there is to the world is a vast mosaic of local matters of particular fact, just one little thing and then another" (1986b: ix) [source: SEP-DL §5].
- **D. M. Armstrong** (8 July 1926 – 13 May 2014): *A Materialist Theory of the Mind* (1968), "an authoritative statement of Australian materialism" (Mumford); *Universals and Scientific Realism* (1978) defending immanent universals; *A World of States of Affairs* (1997); a combinatorial, naturalist account of possibility in which non-actual "heaps" of states of affairs are fictions [source: WP David_Malet_Armstrong; SEP-PR; IEP-MM §5].
- **Jaegwon Kim** (12 September 1934 – 27 November 2019) [source: WP Jaegwon_Kim]: the **exclusion problem** — if every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause, multiply realised mental properties seem "screened off" by their physical realisers and threatened with causal irrelevance (Kim 1998); it generalises to all "special science" properties [source: SEP-MC §6.2]. Kim also objected to Quine's naturalised epistemology (already in *Logical Positivism* chapter).
- **Metametaphysics debate**: *Metametaphysics: New Essays on the Foundations of Ontology*, ed. David Chalmers, David Manley and Ryan Wasserman (OUP, 2009) [source: SEP-GRD bib]. Positions: Fine (2001) — metaphysics as the theory of what grounds what; Schaffer (2010) — grounding between entities; Sider (2012) — metaphysics describes the world's fundamental structure; deflationists such as Amie Thomasson (2009, "Answerable and Unanswerable Questions") doubt many ontological disputes are substantive; Barnes (2017) and Mikkola (2017) argue Sider's criterion cannot accommodate the metaphysics of gender [source: SEP-ME §§4–5]. Carnap's internal/external distinction (1950) is the ancestor (cross-ref *Logical Positivism* chapter).

## Philosophy of mind after behaviourism
- **Identity theory**: U. T. Place, "Is Consciousness a Brain Process?", *British Journal of Psychology* 47 (1956): 44–50; Herbert Feigl, "The 'Mental' and the 'Physical'" (1958); J. J. C. Smart, "Sensations and Brain Processes", *Philosophical Review* 68 (1959): 141–156 [source: SEP-MI §1 and bib; WP Ullin_Place]. Place's paper "was written after discussions at the University of Adelaide with J.J.C. Smart and C.B. Martin"; Smart was then a Rylean behaviourist and, "being unable to refute Place", converted [source: SEP-MI §1]. (SEP-MI's author is Smart himself.) Place (24 Oct 1924 – 2 Jan 2000); Smart (16 Sep 1920 – 6 Oct 2012) [source: WP].
- **Multiple realizability / functionalism**: Putnam, "Psychological Predicates" (1967; later reprinted as "The Nature of Mental States"): pain is felt by humans, mammals, birds, reptiles, even molluscs such as octopuses — no single physical-chemical brain state is likely common to all, and Martians or robots could feel pain; so the brain-state identity theory is "certainly ambitious" and almost certainly false [source: SEP-MR §1.2; SEP-FU bib]. Canonical form: (1) some mental kinds are multiply realisable by distinct physical kinds; (2) if so, they cannot be identical to any one physical kind; (3) so some mental kinds are not identical to any specific physical kind [source: SEP-MR §1.2]. Functionalism: mental states are defined by causal/functional role; early versions modelled the mind on a Turing machine (Putnam 1960, 1967) [source: SEP-FU §2–3]. Objection: Block's inverted and absent qualia; "the economy of Bolivia" [source: SEP-FU §5.5].
- **Nagel**, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?", *Philosophical Review* 83(4) (October 1974): 435–450 [source: WP-BAT]. "an organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something that it is like to *be* that organism—something it is like *for* the organism" [source: WP-BAT, quoting Nagel]. Even complete objective knowledge of bat sonar would not tell us what it is like to perceive with it; Nagel "does not argue against physicalism, but rather claims that we presently have no understanding of how it can be true" [source: SEP-KA §1]. Thomas Nagel born 4 July 1937 [source: WP Thomas_Nagel].
- **Searle**, "Minds, Brains and Programs", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* (1980), published with 27 commentaries; John Searle (1932–2025; d. 17 September 2025) [source: SEP-CR §3 intro; WP John_Searle]. Popular restatement *Minds, Brains and Science* (1984); *Scientific American* exchange with the Churchlands, January 1990 [source: SEP-CR §3].
- **Jackson**, "Epiphenomenal Qualia", *Philosophical Quarterly* 32 (1982): 127–136; "What Mary Didn't Know", *Journal of Philosophy* 83 (1986) [source: SEP-KA bib]. Frank Jackson born 31 August 1943 [source: WP Frank_Cameron_Jackson].
- **Chalmers**: "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness", *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 2 (1995): 200–19; *The Conscious Mind* (OUP, 1996) [source: IEP-HP bib]. "Even when we have explained the performance of all the cognitive and behavioral functions in the vicinity of experience ... there may still remain a further unanswered question: Why is the performance of these functions accompanied by experience?" (1995, 202) [source: IEP-HP §1]. Zombies: creatures physically and functionally identical to us but lacking consciousness [source: IEP-HP §1]. David Chalmers born 20 April 1966 [source: WP David_Chalmers].
- **Eliminativism**: "the radical claim that our ordinary, common-sense understanding of the mind is deeply wrong and that some or all of the mental states posited by common-sense do not actually exist" [source: SEP-EM intro]. Precursors Feyerabend and Rorty ("Mind-Body Identity, Privacy, and Categories", 1965); Paul Churchland, "Eliminative Materialism and the Propositional Attitudes" (1981); Patricia Churchland, *Neurophilosophy* (1986); Stich, *From Folk Psychology to Cognitive Science* (1983) [source: SEP-EM §1].
- **Dennett** (28 March 1942 – 19 April 2024) [source: WP Daniel_Dennett]: "Quining Qualia" (1988) argues our qualia concepts are "fundamentally confused"; *Consciousness Explained* (1991) [source: SEP-EM §3; SEP-KA §4.2 (Dennett 1991)]; the intentional stance (1987): attitudes as real "abstracta" used in predicting rational agents [source: SEP-EM §4]. ### The knowledge argument, step by step (recommended reconstruction)
- Jackson's passage: "Mary is a brilliant scientist who is, for whatever reason, forced to investigate the world from a black and white room via a black and white television monitor. She specializes in the neurophysiology of vision and acquires, let us suppose, all the physical information there is to obtain about what goes on when we see ripe tomatoes, or the sky ... What will happen when Mary is released from her black and white room or is given a color television monitor? Will she learn anything or not? It seems just obvious that she will learn something about the world and our visual experience of it. But then is it inescapable that her previous knowledge was incomplete. But she had all the physical information. Ergo there is more to have than that, and Physicalism is false." [source: SEP-KA §2, quoting Jackson 1982].
- Argument (SEP-KA's explicit stronger version): P1 Mary has complete physical knowledge about human colour vision before release; C1 so she knows all the physical facts about it; P2 there is some knowledge about colour vision she lacks before release; C2 so there are facts about colour vision she does not know; C3 so there are non-physical facts about human colour vision [source: SEP-KA §3.1].
- Jackson's second case: Fred, who sees a colour unknown to normal perceivers [source: SEP-KA §2]. Precursors: Broad (1925), Nagel's bat (1974), Howard Robinson's deaf scientist (1982), and Kripke in unpublished 1979 lectures [source: SEP-KA §1].
- Objection 1 (deny P1→C1 / Mary could work it out): Dennett — Mary would not be fooled by a blue banana ("the blue banana trick") [source: SEP-KA §4.2]. The common reaction "is simply to doubt the claim".
- Objection 2 — **Ability Hypothesis** (Lewis 1983/1988, Nemirow): Mary gains no new facts, only abilities; Lewis: "knowing what it is like is the possession of abilities: abilities to recognize, abilities to imagine" (1983, 131); "It isn't knowing-that. It's knowing-how" (Lewis 1990, 516) [source: SEP-KA §4.3] — note the Rylean echo (knowing how vs knowing that).
- Objection 3 — **New Knowledge/Old Fact** (majority physicalist view; e.g. Loar's phenomenal concepts): Mary gains new knowledge of an old physical fact under a new concept, as one may learn "I am in Amsterdam" without learning a new fact [source: SEP-KA §3.1, §4.6].
- Reply/twist: Jackson himself "changed his mind": in 1995 he argued knowledge of qualia is impossible if qualia are epiphenomenal, so something must be wrong with the argument; in 2003 and 2007 he answered it with strong representationalism [source: SEP-KA §5].

### The Chinese room (alternative reconstruction)
- Searle in a room follows English rules to manipulate Chinese symbols passed under the door; outsiders think there is a Chinese speaker; he understands nothing [source: SEP-CR intro].
- Narrow argument (reductio of "Strong AI"): (1) If Strong AI is true, there is a program for Chinese such that any system running it understands Chinese; (2) I could run that program without understanding Chinese; (3) so Strong AI is false [source: SEP-CR §3]. Not aimed at "weak AI", nor at the claim that machines cannot think — "brains are machines, and brains think" [source: SEP-CR §3].
- 1984 version: programs are purely formal (syntactic); minds have mental contents (semantics); "Syntax by itself is neither constitutive of, nor sufficient for, semantic content"; therefore programs are not sufficient for minds [source: SEP-CR §5.1].
- **Systems Reply** ("perhaps the most common reply", associated with Yale): the man is only a CPU; the whole system understands (Block, Dennett, Hofstadter, Fodor, Rey, Kurzweil). **Searle's reply**: memorise the rules and do everything in your head — you are now the whole system and still do not know the meaning of the Chinese word for "hamburger" [source: SEP-CR §4.1]. **Robot Reply**: give the computer sensors and effectors so symbols are causally linked to the world (Boden, Dennett, Fodor, Harnad, Moravec) [source: SEP-CR §4.2]. SEP-CR's overview notes the debate's renewed relevance with Large Language Models from 2022 [source: SEP-CR §1].

## Davidson (brief)
- Donald Davidson: born Springfield, Massachusetts, 6 March 1917; died Berkeley, 30 August 2003 [source: SEP-DA §1].
- "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" (1963): reasons are causes [source: SEP-DA].
- **Anomalous monism** ("Mental Events", 1970): reconciles (i) mental events causally interact with physical ones; (ii) causally related events fall under strict laws; (iii) there are no strict psychophysical laws — every mental event is a physical event (token identity) but the mental is anomalous [source: SEP-DA].
- **Radical interpretation** (1973): interpreting a speaker "from scratch" requires solving for belief and meaning together, via the **principle of charity** — "optimise agreement", interpreting speakers as holding true beliefs "wherever it is plausible" — building on Quine's radical translation (*Word and Object*, 1960) and a Tarskian truth theory ("Truth and Meaning", 1967) [source: SEP-DA §3.3].

## Gettier and applied ethics
- Edmund Gettier, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?", *Analysis* 23(6) (1963): 121–123 [source: SEP-KN bib]. (Cross-ref *What Is Knowledge?* and *What Is Philosophy?*, which already tells it.)
- **Singer**, "Famine, Affluence, and Morality": written 1971, published *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 1(3) (Spring 1972): 229–243; prompted by the starvation of refugees from the Bangladesh Liberation War [source: WP-FAM]. Principle (p. 231): "if it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it" (WP prints "then we ought"; WS notes copies vary, and Singer also states a weaker "morally significant" version). The drowning-child analogy: muddy clothes are not of comparable importance to a child's death. "It makes no moral difference whether the person I can help is a neighbor's child ten yards from me or a Bengali whose name I shall never know, ten thousand miles away" (p. 231) [source: WP-FAM]. It influenced William MacAskill and effective altruism [source: WP-FAM].
- **Thomson**, "A Defense of Abortion", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 1(1) (1971): 47–66 [source: SEP-FO bib]. The violinist: you wake "back to back in bed with an unconscious violinist"; the Society of Music Lovers has kidnapped you because only your blood type can save him; unplugging kills him; "roughly nine months" [source: WP-DA; SEP-FO §4]. Thesis: even granting the fetus a right to life, it does not include the right to use another's body [source: WP-DA]. Foot's objection: unplugging the violinist allows an existing fatal sequence to run, while abortion initiates one (1984) [source: SEP-FO §4].
- Both journals' 1971–72 launch (*Philosophy & Public Affairs* vol. 1) frames the applied-ethics turn; the *Bentham, Mill and Utilitarianism* chapter already points to Singer on animals.

## Engagement with other traditions and the profession's self-critique
- **Comparative philosophy**: "sometimes called 'cross-cultural philosophy'"; the University of Hawaii sponsored the first East-West Philosophers' Conference in **1939**; journals *Philosophy East and West* and *Dao*; mainstream Western philosophy "has been slow to accept comparative philosophy"; Bryan Van Norden's "Open Letter to the APA" (November 1996) complained comparative work was segregated from mainstream journals [source: IEP-CP §1–2].
- SEP-ANA: colonialism "decimated the Indian analytic tradition"; "It was not until the 1970s, in the pioneering work of B. K. Matilal, that the Indian analytic tradition finally began to be recognized in the West" [source: SEP-ANA]. Matilal and J. Shaw (eds.), *Analytical Philosophy in Comparative Perspective* (1985) [source: IEP-CP bib]. (Cross-ref *Modern Chinese and Indian Philosophy* chapter on Matilal.)
- **Jonardon Ganeri** wrote his Oxford D.Phil under Matilal and now holds the Bimal K. Matilal Distinguished Professorship at Toronto; books include *The Concealed Art of the Soul* (2007), *The Lost Age of Reason: Philosophy in Early Modern India 1450–1700* (2011), *The Self: Naturalism, Consciousness, and the First-Person Stance* (2012), *Attention, Not Self* (2017) [source: WP-GA].
- **Jay Garfield**, *The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way: Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (OUP, **1995**; SEP-NG's bibliography lists an OUP 2005 printing, but its text cites "Garfield 1995") [source: SEP-NG]; *Engaging Buddhism: Why Does Buddhism Matter to Philosophy?* (OUP, 2015) [source: WP-JG]. With Van Norden, "If Philosophy Won't Diversify, Let's Call It What It Really Is", *New York Times* (The Stone), 11 May 2016 — it drew 797 comments in 12 hours [source: WP-JG].
- **Mark Siderits**: *Personal Identity and Buddhist Philosophy* (Aldershot: Ashgate, 2003); with Shōryū Katsura, *Nāgārjuna's Middle Way* (Wisdom, 2013) [source: SEP-NG bib].
- **Analytic–continental divide**: analytic philosophy defined itself partly by opposition to phenomenology, existentialism and "Continental" or "Postmodern" philosophy (Heidegger, Foucault, Derrida) [source: IEP-AP §1]. The **Royaumont colloquium** (April 1958, chaired by Jean Wahl; Austin, Ayer, Hare, Quine, Ryle, Strawson, Merleau-Ponty and others) is the emblematic non-meeting: asked by Merleau-Ponty "Aren't our programs the same?", Ryle answered "I hope not!" [source: WS, reporting Overgaard, "Royaumont Revisited" (BJHP); dates are often given wrongly as 1960/1961]. - Bridges: Murdoch (Sartre book 1953; continental influences) [source: SEP-MU]; Searle–Derrida on Austin [source: SEP-AU]; the published overview names Rorty, Taylor, Habermas as fluent in both idioms; the *Pragmatism* chapter has Rorty's *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979) and Brandom.
- **Experimental philosophy**: often dated to Weinberg, Nichols and Stich (2001); PhilPapers entries grew from under 100 (2007) to over 3,400 (January 2025) [source: SEP-XP]. **Knobe (2003)**, "Intentional Action and Side Effects in Ordinary Language", *Analysis* 63: 190–194: a chairman pursues profit knowing the programme will help/harm the environment; only 23% said he helped it intentionally, 82% that he harmed it intentionally — the "side-effect effect" or "Knobe effect", widely replicated [source: SEP-XP].
- **Demographic critique**: Sally Haslanger (b. 1955; MIT) — "Changing the Ideology and Culture of Philosophy: Not by Reason (Alone)", *Hypatia* 23(2) (2008): 210–223, with tables of women's share of faculty in top U.S. graduate programmes (reported range 4–36%) and of women authors in leading journals [source: WS; WP Sally_Haslanger]. Her ameliorative metaphysics: "Gender and Race: (What) Are They? (What) Do We Want Them to Be?", *Noûs* 34 (2000): 31–55 — roughly, to be a woman is to be subordinated on the basis of perceived sex; races are racialised groups marked for subordination or privilege [source: SEP-ME §3; SEP-RACE]; *Resisting Reality* (OUP, 2012) [source: WP Sally_Haslanger].
- **Kwame Anthony Appiah** (b. London, 8 May 1954; raised in Kumasi, Ghana; Clare College, Cambridge, PhD on probabilistic semantics): *In My Father's House* (1992); a "racial skeptic" (1995, 1996) — since biological races do not exist, "race" refers to nothing real — who later moved to a "racial nominalism" (2006) [source: WP Kwame_Anthony_Appiah; SEP-RACE].

## Debates
- **When and why OLP ended**: IEP-OL credits Grice's meaning/use distinction and revival of systematic semantics; IEP-AP adds Gellner's *Words and Things* (1959), internal divisions, and Quine's naturalism; end dated c. 1965 (IEP-AP) or c. 1970 (IEP-OL) [sources: IEP-OL; IEP-AP].
- **Was Ryle a behaviourist?** Often labelled a logical behaviourist; Tanney (SEP-RY) argues his target was the hidden-ness of the mental and that his arguments tell equally against modern functionalism and computational theories [source: SEP-RY §6].
- **Was Austin defending a performative/constative distinction?** SEP-AU: he is "sometimes read" so, but four features suggest he was setting it up to dismantle it [source: SEP-AU §2.3].
- **Maximin vs average utility** (Rawls vs Harsanyi) [source: SEP-OP §6.2.2; IEP-RA].
- **Ideal vs non-ideal theory on race** (Mills vs Shelby; Anderson; Lebron) [source: SEP-RACE].
- **Who originated direct reference?** Quentin Smith vs Neale and Soames on Marcus and Kripke [source: WP-NN].
- **Descriptivism's comeback**: rigidified descriptions; two-dimensionalism; whether Frege himself was a descriptivist (flagged in the published *Birth* chapter) [source: SEP-REF].
- **Knowledge argument**: ability hypothesis vs new-knowledge/old-fact vs dualism; Jackson's own reversal [source: SEP-KA].
- **Metametaphysics**: substantive (Sider, Fine, Schaffer) vs deflationary (Thomasson; Carnap's heritage) [source: SEP-ME].
- **Is philosophy "Western" by definition?** Garfield and Van Norden (2016) vs critics in *Aeon* and the *Weekly Standard* who held philosophy is by definition the tradition from Plato and Aristotle [source: WP-JG]. Cross-cultural x-phi (Machery et al. 2004) on whether Kripke's intuitions are universal; replications mixed [source: SEP-XP].
- **Demographic critique**: Haslanger (2008) vs Sesardic and De Clercq, who disputed that the figures "speak for themselves" [source: WS].
