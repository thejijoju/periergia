## 1. What "self" means: three questions, two kinds of self

- Olson: personal identity concerns questions "that arise about ourselves by virtue of our being people (or … persons)": "What am I? When did I begin? What will happen to me when I die?" [source: SEP-PI intro]
- Olson on the word "self": "sometimes synonymous with 'person', but often means something different: a sort of unchanging, immaterial subject of consciousness, for instance (as in the phrase 'the myth of the self'). It is often used without any clear meaning" — he avoids it. Useful opening point for the chapter: the Buddhist and Humean denials target the second sense. [source: SEP-PI intro]
- The **characterization question** ("Who am I?"): personal identity in the everyday sense = the properties I take to "define me as a person"; it is "contingent and temporary", can even be deluded ("if I become convinced that I am Napoleon…"). The name "characterization question" is Schechtman's (1996: 1). Distinguish it from the **persistence question** (what makes a past or future being me). [source: SEP-PI §1]
- Olson's list of answers to "What are we?": organisms (animalism); constituted things; temporal parts of animals (Lewis 1976); spatial parts such as brains; immaterial souls (Plato, Descartes, Leibniz) or soul+organism; "collections of mental states or events: 'bundles of perceptions', as Hume said (1978 [1739]: 252)"; and "There is nothing that we are: we don't really exist at all (Russell 1985: 50, Unger 1979, Sider 2013)". "There is no consensus or even a dominant view on this question." [source: SEP-PI §1]
- Peter Unger's paper is titled "I do not Exist" (1979, in G. F. MacDonald (ed.), *Perception and Identity*, London: Macmillan). [source: SEP-PI bibliography]
- **Minimal vs narrative self.** Shaun Gallagher, "Philosophical conceptions of the self: implications for cognitive science", *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 4(1): 14–21 (2000) [source: SEP-PSC bibliography]. Gallagher distinguishes the **minimal self** ("consciousness of oneself as an immediate subject of experience, unextended in time" — wording confirmed only via a secondary excerpt citing p. 15) with two aspects, a sense of ownership and a sense of agency, from the **narrative self**, which involves personal identity and continuity across time. [source: WS-8, summary of the paper and of secondary excerpts; treat the quoted wording as near-verified]
- Phenomenologists (Gallagher & Zahavi): basic, **prereflective self-consciousness** is present "whenever I am living through or undergoing an experience"; it is distinct from introspecting, mirror-recognition, using "I", or "constructs a self-narrative". [source: SEP-PSC intro]
- Flanagan's distinction (reported): low-level self-consciousness of experiencing my experiences as mine vs "the much stronger notion … when we are thinking about our own narrative self", which "presupposes both conceptual knowledge and narrative competence. It requires maturation and socialization". [source: SEP-PSC §1]
- Zahavi's **minimal self**: "one can define a (minimal) form of selfhood in processual and experiential terms as the very first-personal mode of experiencing (Zahavi 2005, 2014)"; Michel Henry: "self-consciousness is not something that the self has, but what the self is". [source: SEP-PSC §1]
- Phenomenologists side with Hume against a substantial self yet keep "for-me-ness": "This is not accounted for by a substantial self or a mental theater. On this point Hume was right. … What remains constant and consistent across these changes is the sense of for-me-ness (or perspectival ownership)". Critics (Bermúdez 2011, 2018; Dainton 2007) call the sense of ownership "an inflationary by-product of reflective or introspective processes". [source: SEP-PSC §§2–3]
- Gallagher has linked the minimal self to Avicenna: Gallagher, S., 2023, "The minimal self and the flying man argument", *Frontiers in Psychology* 14. [source: SEP-PSC bibliography] (Cross-ref *Falsafa* for the argument itself.)
- Shaun Gallagher born 1948, American; at the University of Memphis (Moss Chair) since 2011. Dan Zahavi born 1967 in Copenhagen; professor at the University of Copenhagen, director of the Center for Subjectivity Research from 2002. [source: WP-Shaun_Gallagher; WP-Dan_Zahavi]
- Descartes, Locke and Berkeley "can be interpreted as accepting the view that there is an inner perception of the self"; Hume "stands in stark contrast". [source: SEP-SC §1.2]
- Lichtenberg's "famous remark that one should not say 'I think' but rather, 'it thinks'". [source: SEP-SC §1.2]

## 2. Hume's bundle theory, step by step

- Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, Book 1 (1739), Part 4, Section 6 "Of Personal Identity"; Appendix published 1740 with Book 3 (the *Berkeley and Hume* chapter already says "Appendix of 1740"). [source: SEP-Hume §2.1; IEP-Hume §2e; WP-Personal_identity]
- SEP's summary: Hume applies the Copy Principle (every idea copied from an impression) to personal identity: "Unable to find any impression of an enduring and substantive self underlying our perceptions, Hume concludes that we have no such idea; he thus argues that the self—at least 'as it regards our thought or imagination' (T 1.4.6.5)—should be identified as a bundle (when considered synchronically) or succession (when considered diachronically) of associated perceptions." [source: SEP-Hume §2.1]
- IEP's three-part reading: (1) no simple impression of a self; (2) yet we have some idea of identity, produced by resemblance and causation among perceptions, with memory extending it (T 1.4.6.18 ff.); (3) the "abuse" is adding a soul or unchanging substance (T 1.4.6.6). [source: IEP-Hume §2e]
- **Reconstruction (for the chapter):**
  1. Every genuine idea is copied from an impression (Copy Principle).
  2. If we had an idea of a self that is simple and identical through a whole life, it would come from an impression that is constant and invariable through that life.
  3. Introspection finds no such impression: only "some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure" (T 1.4.6.3 — already block-quoted in *Berkeley and Hume*).
  4. So we have no such idea; the mind, so far as we can conceive it, is "nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions" (T 1.4.6.4).
  5. Our belief that we are one and the same is produced by the imagination gliding along resemblance and causation among the perceptions, extended by memory.
  [premise 1 and 3–5: SEP-Hume §2.1, IEP-Hume §2e; premise 2 is the standard gloss and is Hume's own (T 1.4.6.2), but its wording was NOT verified in the text — present it as a paraphrase]
- **Theatre image** (T 1.4.6.4, SBN 252–3): "The mind is a kind of theatre, where several perceptions successively make their appearance; pass, re-pass, glide away, and mingle in an infinite variety of postures and situations." Hume at once warns: "The comparison of the theatre must not mislead us. They are the successive perceptions only, that constitute the mind", and we have not "the most distant notion of the place where these scenes are represented". [source: WS-1 and WS-16 (Standard Ebooks text; 1740 printing at personal.lse.ac.uk; Hume Texts Online labels T 1.4.6.4); NOT read in the original — wording agreed across the snippets]
- **Commonwealth image** (T 1.4.6.19): "I cannot compare the soul more properly to any thing than to a republic or commonwealth, in which the several members are united by the reciprocal ties of government and subordination, and give rise to other persons, who propagate the same republic in the incessant changes of its parts. And as the same individual republic may not only change its members, but also its laws and constitutions; in like manner the same person may vary his character and disposition, as well as his impressions and ideas, without losing his identity." [source: WP-Personal_identity (quote field citing the Gutenberg text of T 1.4.6); paragraph number 1.4.6.19 from memory — cite as "Treatise 1.4.6"]
- Hume's word "heap": SEP-SC quotes Hume calling our idea of ourselves "a 'heap or collection of different perceptions'" and cites "bk.1, ch.4, §6". The "bundle or collection" phrase is T 1.4.6.4; "heap or collection" is, as far as these sources show, Hume's wording elsewhere (likely T 1.4.2.39). Use "bundle or collection" with the 1.4.6.4 citation. [source: SEP-SC §1.2; WP-Bundle_theory]
- Minor wording discrepancy: WP-Bundle_theory quotes "which succeed each other with inconceivable rapidity"; WP-Personal_identity and the published *Berkeley and Hume* chapter have "with an inconceivable rapidity" (the published version is the standard Norton text). Keep the published wording.
- **The Appendix.** "upon a more strict review of the section concerning personal identity, I find myself involv'd in such a labyrinth" (already quoted in *Berkeley and Hume*; IEP-Hume quotes the same passage). Later in the Appendix (T App 20; SBN 635–36): "all my hopes vanish, when I come to explain the principles, that unite our successive perceptions in our thought or consciousness." [source: IEP-Hume §2e for "labyrinth"; WS-2 for "all my hopes vanish" (opentextbooks.org.hk edition; Cottrell 2015 *Phil. Review*; Siakel 2018 *Hume Studies*)]
- The difficulty: Hume says he cannot render consistent (a) that all our distinct perceptions are distinct existences and (b) that the mind never perceives any real connection among distinct existences; "but these two principles are not inconsistent in themselves. … consensus has not been established on this point (Garrett 1981; Swain 1991; Baxter 1998; Ainslie 2008; McIntyre 2008; Cottrell 2015)." Daniel Siakel (2018) counts "over thirty incompatible interpretations" of the "Appendix problem". [source: SEP-Hume §2.1; WS-2 for Siakel's count]
- Galen Strawson's reading (*The Evident Connexion*, 2011): Hume is not a "Humean" about personal identity; he holds the bundle conception is the only *empirically legitimate* one but not a philosophically adequate account of the self. Strawson also wrote "All My Hopes Vanish: Hume's Appendix" (2012, in *The Continuum Companion to Hume*). [source: WP-Galen_Strawson]
- **Objection 1: who perceives the bundle?** "Critics of Hume state that in order for the various states and processes of the mind to seem unified, there must be something which perceives their unity, the existence of which would be no less mysterious than a personal identity." [source: WP-Personal_identity] Also the compresence objection: bundle theory does not say what ties the members together. [source: WP-Bundle_theory]
- **Objection 2: Reid.** Reid "affirms that humans have diachronic personal identity, and denies Hume's so-called 'bundle theory'". First principle: "our own personal identity and continued existence, as far back as we remember anything distinctly. This we know immediately, and not by reasoning" (EIP 6.5, 476). On the self: "the continued existence of that indivisible thing which I call myself. Whatever this self may be, it is something which thinks, and deliberates, and resolves, and acts, and suffers. I am not thought, I am not action, I am not feeling; I am something that thinks, and acts, and suffers. … that self or I, to which they belong, is permanent" (EIP 3.4, 264). SEP: "Here Reid echoes Descartes' operational definition of the self in Meditation 2"; Reid "refuses to speculate on the substance of the enduring self". [source: SEP-Reid §5.2] (*Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man*, 1785 — date in published *Locke* chapter.)
- **Objection 3: Kant.** Kant agrees with Hume that there is no inner intuition of the self "through which it is given as object" (B408), but argues that transcendental apperception "is required to account for the unity of conscious experience over time": "the 'I think' must be able to accompany all my representations" (B132). The "I think" is "purely formal, exhausted by its function in unifying experience". [source: SEP-SC §1.3] Cross-ref *Kant* (which already quotes B132).
- SEP-KM: the unity Kant has in mind is not met by representations "associated with one another in an appropriate way (the so-called bundle theory)"; representations "represent it to someone". [source: SEP-KM §(unity of consciousness)]
- Who sides with Hume today on not finding the self in introspection: Shoemaker (1986), Martin (1997), Howell (2010; 2023), Prinz (2012); against him: Chisholm (1976), Cassam (1995), G. Strawson (2009), Damasio (2010), Rosenthal (2012). [source: SEP-SC §3.1]
- Hume and Buddhism: the parallel "is explored at some length in Giles 1993, Tillemans 1996, and Kapstein 2001" (James Giles, "The No-Self Theory: Hume, Buddhism, and Personal Identity", *Philosophy East and West* 1993). Coseru: the Buddhist claims our sense of self is "imputed … just as Hume claims that a self is never apprehended in the series of perceptions". [source: SEP-MIB §1.1 and bibliography] (Gopnik's historical-contact suggestion is already in *Berkeley and Hume*.)

## 3. Buddhist no-self: the reductionist theory (beyond what *Early Buddhism* already gives)

- Canonical formula: regard any form of sensation and consciousness, "past, future, or present; internal or external; manifest or subtle … as it actually is …: 'This is not mine. This is not my self. This is not what I am'" (Majjhima Nikāya I 130, as quoted by Coseru). [source: SEP-MIB §1.1] IEP's rendering of M I 138–9: "this is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self." [source: IEP-Bud §3d]
- **Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta**: SN 22.59 (PTS S III 66), traditionally the second discourse, to the "group of five"; parallel SĀ 34. [source: WP-Anattalakkhaṇa_Sutta] Already reconstructed in *Early Buddhism*. If a second translation is wanted, IEP (Vélez de Cea's rendering of S III 66–7): "Monks, material form is non-self. If it were self, it would not lead to affliction. It would be possible [to say] with regard to material form: 'Let my material form be thus. Let my material form not be thus.' But precisely because it is non-self, it leads to affliction." And the catechism (M I 138 etc.): "Is what is impermanent, suffering, and subject to change, fit to be regarded as: 'this is mine, this I am, this is my self?' – 'No, venerable sir'." Vélez calls the second (control) argument "much less frequent". [source: IEP-Bud §3d]
- IEP also identifies a third pattern (A V 188): "whatever becomes, that is conditioned, volitionally formed, dependently arisen, that is impermanent. What is impermanent, that is suffering. What is suffering, that is [to be regarded thus]: 'this is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self.'" [source: IEP-Bud §3d]
- **Two truths applied to persons** (Siderits): conventionally real entities "are accepted as real by common sense, but … turn out on further analysis to be wholes compounded out of simpler entities and thus not strictly speaking real at all"; "The ultimate truth about sentient beings is just that there is a causal series of impermanent, impersonal psychophysical elements… It is thus conventionally true that there are persons… This is conventionally true because generally speaking there is more overall happiness and less overall pain and suffering when one part of such a series identifies with other parts of the same series. For instance, when the present set of psychophysical elements identifies with future elements, it is less likely to engage in behavior (such as smoking) that results in present pleasure but far greater future pain. The utility of this convention is, however, limited. Past a certain point—namely the point at which we take it too seriously, as more than just a useful fiction—it results in existential suffering." [source: SEP-Bud §2] Good for a step-by-step "argument for treating the person as a useful fiction".
- Siderits on "I": "since the word 'I' is a mere enumerative term like 'pair', there is nothing that it genuinely denotes"; the erroneous sense of "I" "stems from our employment of the useful fiction represented by the concept of the person". [source: SEP-Bud §3]
- Abhidharma: "There is nothing else, whether a being, or an entity, or a man or a person" (Pali commentarial excerpt, Dhs-a 155), aimed at the Pudgalavāda. [source: SEP-Abh]
- **Reductionism, not eliminativism** (Siderits's classification, reported by Coseru): "the Buddhist reductionist, as Siderits (2003, 13 and passim) has clearly argued, is not an eliminativist. Unlike the eliminative materialist … the Buddhist reductionist merely points to the conventional nature of persons as ordinarily conceived." Abhidharma rejects "not the existence of extra mental entities proper, but rather the existence of all partite entities such as chairs, pots, and tables". [source: SEP-MIB §6] (Chadha's opposing eliminativist reading already in *Early Buddhism*.)
- Siderits (2003: 89–96) reads the Pudgalavādins as proposing that persons **non-reductively supervene** on the aggregates. Their four arguments as Coseru summarises them: persons real like fire dependent on fuel; perceived; real without separate identity; ultimately existent because dependent on ultimately real aggregates. Direct accounts survive mainly in opponents' critiques and three short texts in Chinese. [source: SEP-MIB §5.6]
- **Vasubandhu's questions** (useful framing): "If there is no self, then what is the same about me as a child and as an adult? What is named by my name? What perceives perceptions …? What is memory, without a self? And crucially … what transmigrates … and reaps the karmic fruit …?" [source: SEP-Vas §(Refutation of the self)]
- Vasubandhu's test of reality: "everything that is real or substantial (dravya) is causally efficient… Everything that does not have such a causal basis is unreal, … merely a conceptual construct, a mere convention (prajñapti)." [source: SEP-Vas] (Dilemma itself already in *Identity*.)
- Experience without an experiencer: "any given awareness only appears to be made up of two parts, subject and object… In reality, any given moment of awareness consists in a full, constructed appearance of subject/object duality without there actually being any separate subject." [source: SEP-Vas]
- Against the Personalists' "ineffable" person: ineffability of something perceived "undermines the functionality of perception as a source of knowledge". Vasubandhu cites a scripture in which the Buddha says "whoever claims to remember past lives is only remembering past aggregates in his own continuum". [source: SEP-Vas]
- **Memory via seeds**: Vasubandhu explains the metaphor of seeds as "the capacity for recollection to arise originating from experiential knowledge", just as sprouts have the capacity "to produce a grain of rice from a previous grain of rice" (AKBh ad V 1d–2a). Seeds are a Sautrāntika notion, used "only in a conventional sense". [source: SEP-MIB §5.4]
- Vasubandhu's "Treatise on the Negation of the Person" (*Pudgala-pratiṣedha-prakaraṇa*) = chapter 9 of the *Abhidharmakośa(bhāṣya)*; targets both the Personalists and the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika self. Matthew Kapstein, "Vasubandhu and the Nyāya Philosophers on Personal Identity", in *Reason's Traces* (Wisdom, 2001), pp. 347–391. [source: SEP-MIB §1.1; SEP-Vas bibliography]
- Later Buddhist constructs to do the self's work without a self: "mind-stream, life-continuum mind, and repository consciousness (citta-santāna, bhavaṅga-citta, and ālaya-vijñāna, respectively) precisely in order to avoid the metaphysical implications of the traditional notion of self." [source: SEP-MIB §1.1]
- **Yogācāra** (brief; mostly already in *Mahāyāna*): the defiled mind (*kliṣṭamanas*) "is responsible for our mistaken belief in the self and our sense of personal identity", marked by four defilements: false view of self (*ātmadṛṣṭi*), delusion about self (*ātmamoha*), self-conceit (*ātmamāna*), self-love (*ātmasneha*). The ālayavijñāna "performs some of the traditional roles attributed to the self (ātman) … (e.g., ensures personal identity, karmic responsibility, undergoes rebirth)", hence the charge of "resuscitating" a self; Yogācāras replied it is like a river: "an uninterrupted series of transitory karmic seeds". The concept "largely developed from Yogācāra attempts to solve common Buddhist and Abhidharmic problems posed by the doctrine of no-self". First appearance perhaps in Asaṅga's *Yogācārabhūmi* (Schmithausen). [source: SEP-Yog §2; SEP-MIB §5.5]
- **Candrakīrti** (early 7th century per modern scholars; *Madhyamakāvatāra* survives only in Tibetan; *Prasannapadā* in Sanskrit and Tibetan). In Madhyamaka "I" and "self" are conventions: the Buddha "spoke according to the linguistic conventions of the people to whom he talked… It does not follow from grammatical correctness, however, that those words used in sentences have referents." [source: SEP-Madh §5]
- **Sevenfold analysis** (Madhyamakāvatāra 6.151, in the translation in *Ocean of Nectar*, p. 327): "[The self] is like a cart, which is not other than its parts, not non-other, and does not possess them. It is not within its parts, and its parts are not within it. It is not the mere collection, and it is not the shape." [source: WP-Bundle_theory (secondary quotation)] The passage runs MA 6.150–165 and extends a fivefold analysis in Nāgārjuna's MMK; Jones (2023, *Journal of Indian Philosophy*) reads it as a guide to meditation rather than a theory of self. [source: WS-10] Present briefly, flagged as quoted from a translation (Tsongkhapa's *Ocean of Nectar*, Wisdom; translator not verified).
- Modern minority readings of not-self (supplementing *Early Buddhism*): Caroline Rhys Davids (1924), Isaline Horner (1936: some passages support a self as "unchanging witness", *sakkhi*/*sākṣin*), Edward Conze (1962), Kamaleswar Bhattacharya (1973); Miri Albahari's "witness consciousness" (2006). "The prevailing view, however, is that such interpretations are tenuous" (Warder, Collins 1982, Kalupahana, Harvey 1995, De Silva). Collins's five points: the eternal self is a speculative error; body is not self; consciousness is not self; no self apart from experience; false sense of self from habitual use of "I" and "mine". [source: SEP-MIB §1.1]
- Miri Albahari, *Analytical Buddhism: The Two-Tiered Illusion of Self* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2006). [source: SEP-MIB bibliography]

## 4. The Nyāya replies (new material: cross-modal recognition)

- Already published: NS 1.1.10 ("Desire, aversion, volition (prayatna), pleasure, pain, and cognition are characteristics of the self", Chadha's wording) and the recollection argument. Do not re-quote at length.
- IEP's version of the standard argument: "Things like desire, cognition, experiences of pleasure and pain and volition are qualities. All qualities inhere in substances. Therefore, there is a substance to which desire and the rest belong. … None of the material elements like earth or water are the bearers of desire and the rest. Therefore, there must be a special, non-material substance, namely a self (see various commentaries on NS 1.1.10)." [source: IEP-Ny §3a]
- IEP on the Buddhist target, two ways: "(i) Diachronically: moment by moment, things are destroyed and new things arise… (ii) Synchronically: in a single moment, what we take to be wholes (including selves) are nothing more than heaps of micro-properties". [source: IEP-Ny §3a]
- **Synchronic argument (NEW to the curriculum):** "In defense of the synchronic identity of selves, Nyāya argues that cross-modal recognition ('that thing I see is the same thing I am touching') requires a single experiencer with the ability to synthesize data from various senses (NS 3.1.1–3)." [source: IEP-Ny §3a] Reconstruction for the chapter:
  1. I can judge "the thing I see is the thing I touch".
  2. That judgement compares the deliverances of sight and touch.
  3. A comparison needs one subject who has both deliverances (one cannot compare what one person sees with what another touches).
  4. Neither the eye nor the skin has both; each sense has only its own object.
  5. So there is one subject distinct from the senses: the self.
  Steps 2–4 are the natural unpacking of IEP's sentence; the Sanskrit sūtra wording was NOT verified (WS-14 found no translation). Attribute as "the *Nyāya-sūtra* (3.1.1–3), as the Internet Encyclopedia summarises it".
- **Diachronic argument** (already published): Uddyotakara, following Vātsyāyana: if I were a different self from yesterday's "me", I could not recollect what that "me" experienced, "since one self is unable to recollect the content of another's experience". [source: IEP-Ny §3a]
- **Buddhist reply** (to both): a causal series can do the work — the later moment arises "with the shape of my past aggregates imprinted upon it" (Vasubandhu, SEP-Vas); cross-modal integration needs only causal connection within one stream; the "single experiencer" is a constructed appearance (Vasubandhu's subject/object point above). **Nyāya counter**: causal connection is not enough — causally connected streams of two different people (teacher and pupil) do not let one remember the other's experiences (this counter is NOT verified in today's sources; Kapstein 2001 is the scholarly treatment — omit or present as the obvious rejoinder without attribution).
- Udayana's *Ātmatattvaviveka* ("Determining the Truth of the Self") is "the apex" of early Nyāya arguments for the self. Udayana c. 975–1050 CE; a dated work (*Lakṣaṇāvalī*) gives Śaka 906 = 984–985 CE. [source: IEP-Ny §3a; WP-Udayana]
- Uddyotakara (c. 6th century CE), *Nyāyavārttika*, written to defend Vātsyāyana's *Nyāyabhāṣya* against Dignāga. [source: WP-Udyotakara]
- The Nyāya self is not intrinsically conscious: consciousness is a quality it acquires; "Nyāya argues that a second cognition is required to illumine the first" (other-illumination, *paraprakāśa*), "a thesis more akin to contemporary higher order theories". [source: SEP-Śaṅ §3.2]
- Kumārila (Mīmāṃsā, 7th century): sided with Nyāya on an enduring self but held it is directly encountered in first-person awareness (already in *Classical Indian Schools*; no new source found today — keep to one clause).

## 5. Vedānta and other Indian self-theories (new angles only)

- Advaita's self: Brahman is "pure existence, pure consciousness and pure bliss… This Brahman is ever known to Itself and constitutes the reality in all individuals selves, while the appearance of our empirical individuality is credited to avidya (ignorance) and māyā (illusion)." Advaita "provisionally accepts the empirical reality of individual selves". [source: IEP-Adv §2a]
- Two classic images: "the space in a pot versus the space in the whole cosmos (undifferentiated in reality, though arbitrarily separated by the contingencies of the pot…)", and "the self versus the reflection of the self". The individual jīva functions as "a doer (karta) and enjoyer (bhokta) of a limited world". [source: IEP-Adv §2a]
- **Reflection theory (NEW):** Śaṅkara "develops a reflection or semblance (pratibimba or ābhāsa) theory of consciousness" to explain "individuals superimposing false identities on consciousness" (US ch. 18; BṛUBh 4.3.7; ChUBh 6.3.2). The mind is made of *sattva*, "akin to the reflective surface of a mirror"; "Just as light is pervasive but only becomes visible through a reflective medium …, so too is consciousness expressed experientially only in the reflective medium of the intellect." "Reflection is the seed of individuation and the I-notion"; the mind becomes "the reflexive center of self-identification, manifesting as knower-ship, doer-ship, and enjoyer-ship." Śaṅkara also uses a "limitation theory (avaccheda)". Dalal links this to the "decombination problem in cosmopsychism" (Albahari 2019). [source: SEP-Śaṅ §3.3]
- Śaṅkara's four functions of mind: intellect (*buddhi*), *manas*, the I-notion (*ahaṃkāra*, "literally the 'I-maker'"), and recollection (*citta*). [source: SEP-Śaṅ §3.3] The "I-maker" makes a good contrast with the Buddhist defiled mind.
- **Undeniability of consciousness:** "If one infers that this non-finding of consciousness is evidence of its non-existence, Śaṅkara counters that one cannot deny their own consciousness (BrSūBh 2.3.7)… any dismissal of consciousness presupposes its very existence." The witness "is elusive because it is unobjectifiable… One cannot isolate the witness by pulling it to one side and objects to another, for in drawing a line between the two, both become objects of consciousness." [source: SEP-Śaṅ §3.2] — a direct answer to Hume's "I never can catch myself": the self is not caught because it is the catcher.
- Witnessing consciousness "always accompanies cognition"; it is "intransitive and receptive, simply the passive witnessing itself". Albahari (2009) "Witness-Consciousness: Its Definition, Appearance and Reality" is cited. [source: SEP-Śaṅ §3.1]
- **Jain analogue of the flying man:** "in the writing of the eleventh century Jain writer Prabhācandra, there appears an argument very much like Avicenna's Flying Man argument for the possibility of self-awareness without awareness of the body (Ganeri 2012a: ch. 2), whereas various thinkers of the Advaita Vedānta school argue that there is no self-awareness without embodiment (Ram-Prasad 2013)." [source: SEP-SC §1.1] (Ram-Prasad, "Situating the Elusive Self of Advaita Vedānta", in Siderits, Thompson & Zahavi 2013: 217–238.)
- Jain jīva: already in *Jains, Ājīvikas and Materialists* (body-sized, many, eternal). IEP-Jain: basic constituents are souls (*jiva*), matter (*pudgala*), motion, rest, space, time; "Every living thing has a soul". [source: IEP-Jain §1] Cross-ref only. NB the Jain word for matter is *pudgala*, the same word the Pudgalavādins used for "person" — do not confuse.

## 6. Chinese traditions

- **Zhuangzi, ch. 2 ("Qi Wu Lun", "Equalizing Assessments of Things")**: Ziqi (Nanguo Ziqi), "Seemingly deep in some meditative state, … describes it as having 'lost myself'", then explains with the pipes of earth, humans and Tian. Wong translates the following lines (Ziporyn 2009, pp. 9–10): "Joy and anger, sorrow and happiness, plans and regrets, transformations and stagnations, unguarded abandonment and deliberate posturing—music flows out of hollows, mushrooms form from steam! Day and night they alternate before us, but no one knows from whence they sprout. … Without them there would be no 'I', and without an 'I' nothing chosen." Wong: "This Hume-like reflection about the source of the 'I' suggests it is not identified with the heart-mind." The text then asks of the body's "hundreds of bones, nine openings and six organs" whether any is more "I" than the others, and muses that there must be "some controller or true lord even if we cannot identify it." [source: SEP-CM §(Zhuangzi)] Translation: Brook Ziporyn, *Zhuangzi: The Essential Writings with Selections from Traditional Commentaries* (Hackett, 2009). [source: SEP-CM bibliography] NB Wong's quoted lines may be his own adaptation of Ziporyn ("trans. 2009"); attribute as "in Ziporyn's translation as quoted by David Wong".
- Chinese: 吾喪我 (*wu sang wo*), "I lost myself / I lost me"; the line "has been used to support both 'true self' and 'no self' interpretations" (Cambridge article on the Inner Chapters). [source: WS-6] Hansen's rendering: the narrator "said farewell to my wǒ (I:me)". [source: SEP-Zh]
- Hansen: "Zhuangzi postulates no homunculus exercising authority over the organs"; the "I:me (我 wǒ)" is an indexical "choosing point"; "Like Hume's self, without the naturally occurring grab-bag of emotional attitudes, it would not be there to play its choosing role. But it is the whole body, not just those attitudes, that chooses". "In a twist on Buddhism and Hume, without their role in our choosing, we would not have an indexed perspective, an 'I'." [source: SEP-Zh]
- Wong: the *Zhuangzi* "questions the very notion of a hierarchy with the heart-mind leading the rest of the person… This egalitarian conception of the parts of a person puts the heart-mind in conversation with the rest of the body rather than in command." [source: SEP-CM]
- Caution on texts: readers of translations of the received recension (Watson, Graham 1981, Mair 1998) should remember it "is not the original recension". [source: WS-6, citing SEP-Zh archive]
- **Confucian relational self (hedge as an interpretation):** Wong: ren and li "are the basis for characterizing Confucian ethics as a relational ethic, meaning that it is in part distinguished by its placement of relationships at the center of a well-lived life (see Ames, 2011, 2021)". "It is part of the Confucian vision … that it is a life of relationships marked by mutual care and respect, that one achieves fullest personhood that way"; "it can be part of one's very identity to be a particular person's son or daughter, mother or father". [source: SEP-CE §§1–2.3]
- Wong's caution: the contrast with individual autonomy "must be carefully described"; Confucianism in part accepts autonomy as living by one's own understanding of the right (Analects 4.14, 5.1; Mencius 1A3; Xunzi 29.2); Hall and Ames (1998) criticise "the rather common interpretation of Confucianism as prioritizing the group over the individual". Mencius 5A2: Shun married without telling his father, to preserve "an emotionally viable relationship". [source: SEP-CE §2.3]
- Roger T. Ames (born 12 Dec 1947, Toronto): *Confucian Role Ethics: A Vocabulary* (2011); *Human Becomings: Theorizing Persons for Confucian Role Ethics* (2020). Ames and Neville are "prominent and influential critics of the 'virtue' characterization of Confucian ethics"; Ames and Henry Rosemont Jr. suggested "exemplary person" for *junzi* (*The Analects of Confucius: A Philosophical Translation*, 1998). [source: WP-Roger_T._Ames; SEP-CE §1]
- Herbert Fingarette (1921–2018), *Confucius: The Secular as Sacred* (Harper & Row, 1972): "construes ritual performance as an end in itself, as beautiful and dignified, open and shared participation in ceremonies that celebrate human community". [source: SEP-CE §1; WP-Herbert_Fingarette] (Already named in *Confucianism*; the often-quoted Fingarette line about a person needing at least two people was NOT verified — do not use.)
- Cross-ref *African Philosophy* for the parallel communitarian debate (Menkiti's achieved personhood vs Gyekye) rather than repeating it.

## 7. Islamic traditions (cross-reference mostly)

- Avicenna's flying man: already fully in *Falsafa*. Ivry: Avicenna "attributes self-consciousness to the soul, an ego that has self-awareness and is not to be identified unilaterally with the rational faculty"; the soul "retains its individuality" after death. [source: SEP-AIM] Use only to set up the comparison with Prabhācandra and Gallagher 2023.
- Al-Ghazālī condemned as unbelief three Avicennan teachings, among them "(3) that after death the souls of humans will never again return into bodies" — i.e., he defended bodily resurrection against a soul-only afterlife. [source: SEP-Gh §(Incoherence)]
- Ash'arite occasionalism, which al-Ghazālī combined into his view: "A person's thoughts, for instance, are considered accidents that inhere in the atoms of the person's brain… None of the accidents, however, can subsist from one moment (waqt) to the next… in each moment God assigns the accidents to bodies". [source: SEP-Gh] (Already in *Ghazālī and Averroes* and *Identity*; at most one sentence: a theistic cousin of momentariness, where God, not a causal stream, supplies continuity.)

## 8. Modern no-self and deflationary views

- **Thomas Metzinger** (born 12 Mar 1958; Mainz): *Being No One: The Self-Model Theory of Subjectivity* (MIT Press, 2003; paperback 2004). Argues "that the phenomenal self is a mental construct created by the brain". Key concept: the phenomenal self-model (PSM); three phenomenal properties: mineness, perspectivalness, selfhood. Earlier: *Subjekt und Selbstmodell* (1993). Popular version: *The Ego Tunnel* (2009). [source: WP-Thomas_Metzinger; WP-Self-model; SEP-SC bibliography for the 2003 title]
- Metzinger's headline claim, p. 1 of *Being No One*: "nobody ever was or had a self" (Zahavi 2005 quotes it so) / "No one ever was or had a self" (Harman) — word order and first word vary by source. [source: WS-11] Paraphrase rather than quote, or quote with "as Zahavi quotes him".
- **Daniel Dennett** (28 Mar 1942 – 19 Apr 2024; Tufts): "The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity", in F. Kessel, P. Cole & D. Johnson (eds.), *Self and Consciousness: Multiple Perspectives* (Hillsdale, NJ: Erlbaum, 1992); online text says "A center of gravity is just an abstractum". Page range conflicts between sources (103–115 vs other); chair vs lectern example differs between versions. [source: WS-5; WP-Daniel_Dennett for dates] *Consciousness Explained* (1991): no "Cartesian theater"; the brain is a "bundle of semi-independent agencies" (p. 260) whose content-fixations can lead to "the utterance of one of the sentences that make up the story in which the central character is one's 'self'" (WP's paraphrase). [source: WP-Consciousness_Explained]
- Reconstruction of Dennett's analogy (for the chapter, as an analogy not a quotation): a centre of gravity is real and useful for prediction but is not a physical part of the object; likewise the self is the abstract point around which a person's self-told stories are organised; so the self is real as an abstraction and nothing more. [source: WS-5 and WS-15 summaries]
- **Split brain**: callosotomy, cutting the corpus callosum ("approximately 200 million" fibres), mostly in the 1960s, as a last resort for severe epilepsy; in everyday life "their conscious life remains seemingly unified", but in the lab: flash "TAXABLE" with "TAX" to the left visual field and "ABLE" to the right; the patient says "ABLE" but writes "TAX" with the left hand. Whether split-brain subjects have two streams of consciousness "remains a highly debated question". Nagel (1971) and Parfit (1971, 1984) discussed the relevance to personal identity. [source: SEP-CU §3.1 and §1]
- Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga, "The Split Brain in Man", *Scientific American* (1967); Sperry won the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Gazzaniga born 12 Dec 1939. [source: WP-Split-brain; WP-Michael_Gazzaniga]
- **The interpreter**: the "left-brain interpreter" concept developed by Gazzaniga and Joseph LeDoux from split-brain work in the early 1970s; when the right hemisphere is given an instruction the left does not see, "the left-brain interpreter will nonetheless construct a contrived explanation for the action". Example (Gazzaniga 2000, *Brain* 123: 1293–1326): a patient shown a chicken foot (to one hemisphere) and a snowy scene (to the other) picks a chicken and a shovel, then explains "the shovel is for cleaning out the chicken coop". WP calls the interpreter "the glue that attempts to hold the story together". [source: WP-Left-brain_interpreter; WP-Split-brain] Connects naturally to Dennett and to the narrative self.

## 9. Narrative selves: the debate step by step

- **MacIntyre**, *After Virtue* (University of Notre Dame Press, 1981; 2nd ed. 1984 with postscript; 3rd ed. 2007). Alasdair MacIntyre, 12 Jan 1929 (Glasgow) – 21 May 2025 (South Bend). [source: WP-After_Virtue; WP-Alasdair_MacIntyre] Chapter 15, "The Virtues, the Unity of a Human Life and the Concept of a Tradition": the self's unity "resides in the unity of a narrative which links birth to life to death as narrative beginning to middle to end"; a life's "unity is the unity of a narrative embodied in a single life"; "the unity of a human life is the unity of a narrative quest". [source: WS-9 (excerpts quoted in secondary sources; page numbers NOT verified)]
- **Schechtman**, *The Constitution of Selves* (Ithaca: Cornell University Press, 1996). The narrative criterion: "what makes an action, experience, or a psychological characteristic the one of a particular individual is its correct incorporation into the self-told story of his or her life (Taylor 1989; Schechtman 1996; DeGrazia 2005; Goldie 2012; Schroer & Schroer 2014)." A mere subject to whom experiences happen is unified only passively; "for that subject to be a person, a genuine moral agent, those experiences must be actively unified, must be gathered together into the life of one narrative ego by virtue of a story the subject tells that weaves them together (Schechtman 1996: 136–162)." [source: SEP-PIE §10] Schechtman answers the characterization question rather than the reidentification question. [source: SEP-PI §1; SEP-PIE §10] Self-narration organises experience "according to an implicit narrative" (review summary); her later work (*Staying Alive*, 2014) gives narrative "a more circumscribed role". [source: WS-12; SEP-PIE bibliography for *Staying Alive*]
- **Galen Strawson** (born 1952; British): "Against Narrativity", *Ratio* 17 (2004): 428–52 (journal issue: Ratio new series XVII, 4 December 2004). Also "A Fallacy of our Age", *TLS* 15 Oct 2004; "Episodic ethics" (2005). [source: WP-Galen_Strawson; WS-3] Strawson attacks two theses: the **psychological Narrativity thesis** (a descriptive claim that people typically experience their lives as a story) and the **ethical Narrativity thesis** (that a richly narrative outlook is essential to a well-lived life and to true or full personhood). He distinguishes **Diachronic** self-experience (one figures oneself, considered as a self, as something that was there in the past and will be there in the future) from **Episodic** (one does not), says the two distinctions are not the same as Narrative/non-Narrative though they correlate, and counts himself "relatively Episodic". [source: WS-3, WS-4 (summaries; exact wording NOT verified)]
- **Reconstruction (for the chapter):**
  - *Narrativist argument*: (1) To be a person (an agent answerable for her life) one's experiences and actions must be one's own in a rich sense. (2) Mere succession of experiences in one body does not make them one's own in that sense (Schechtman's passive "subject of experiences"). (3) What makes them one's own is their place in a self-told life story (Schechtman; MacIntyre's narrative unity). (4) So persons are constituted by self-narration, and a well-lived life is a narratively unified one. [SEP-PIE §10; WS-9]
  - *Strawson's objection*: premise (3) and the ethical conclusion fail for Episodics; there are people who have no sense of their lives as a story and do not figure themselves as persisting, yet live fully moral lives; so narrativity is neither universal nor ethically required, and the claim that it is may be harmful to such people. [WS-3, WS-4]
  - *Reply*: Schechtman's narratives can be implicit, not told; Strawson's Episodics still act on long-term plans and keep promises, which needs a minimal narrative; Korsgaard (below) argues that agency itself demands diachronic unity. Critics (Mackenzie & Poltera 2010, *Hypatia*) argue Schechtman's constraints are too restrictive; Battersby (2006, *Narrative*) argues Strawson shows broad narrative claims fail but offers no adequate alternative and that his dichotomy is too sharp. [SEP-PIE §10; WS-12; WS-3]
- **Korsgaard**, "Personal Identity and the Unity of Agency: A Kantian Response to Parfit", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 18(2) (1989): 101–132. "Viewing ourselves as agents practically requires us to view ourselves as diachronically unified, irrespective of the actual strength of the various psychological connections… We have to view ourselves as unified across time because an agent's deliberations, acts and rational life plans extend through time"; and at a time, "due to the need of eliminating conflict among our various desires… We only have one body to act with after all (Korsgaard 1989)." SEP-PIE: "A similar line of reasoning motivates narrative accounts". Christine Korsgaard born 9 Apr 1952; Harvard emerita. [source: SEP-PIE §10 and bibliography; WP-Christine_Korsgaard]

## 10. Parfit: what matters and the ethics of no-self (beyond *Identity*)

- Derek Parfit, 11 Dec 1942 (Chengdu, China) – 2 Jan 2017 (London); Oxford, All Souls. *Reasons and Persons* (Oxford: Clarendon, 1984); Part III on personal identity. [source: WP-Derek_Parfit; SEP-PIE bibliography]
- Fission case as Dufner tells it (Parfit 1984: 254f): my two hemispheres, functional duplicates, transplanted into my two triplet sisters; both are psychologically continuous with me; both cannot be me; picking one is arbitrary; so neither is, yet "I care about their future as if it were mine"; so "what matters for prudential concern—and arguably also for moral questions—is psychological continuity, not … numerical identity as such." Options: what matters is (a) identity, (b) continuity, (c) connectedness, (d) both continuity and connectedness (Parfit 1984: 215, 313). [source: SEP-PIE §3] (Fission's logic already in *Identity*; use this for the "what matters" step.)
- Olson's survival scenario: surgeons will put your brain in my head; "which of us would have a reason to pay" to spare the resulting person pain? Parfit (1971, 1984: 215, 1995) and Martin (1998) argue identity itself has no practical importance; Sosa (1990) and Merricks (2022) argue it does. [source: SEP-PI §1]
- **Degrees of responsibility**: since connections "can be a matter of degree… responsibility may decline in corresponding degrees whenever these psychological connections … are weaker (Parfit 1984: 325f)." The **Russian nobleman**: a young socialist who will inherit estates signs a document giving land to the peasants, revocable only with his wife's consent, and makes her promise not to revoke it if he later changes; years later he does; is she still bound? Continuity says yes; connectedness says perhaps not. [source: SEP-PIE §6]
- Dementia and responsibility; accountability vs attributability (Wolf 1986; Watson 1996; D. Shoemaker 2012, 2015). [source: SEP-PIE §6]
- **Separateness of persons**: Rawls (1971: 27) — utilitarianism "does not take seriously the distinction between persons". Parfit replies that on his view separateness is "less pronounced than we usually think", which "weakens the objection against the utilitarian principle (e.g., Parfit 1973; 1984: sec. 111−118; 1986)"; he favours prioritarianism. Parfit 1973 = "Later Selves and Moral Principles". Rawls (1974) later took separateness as a normative ideal. [source: SEP-PIE §§9–10]
- The rest of the glass-tunnel passage (continuation of the lines quoted in *Identity*): "There is still a difference between my life and the lives of other people. But the difference is less. Other people are closer. I am less concerned about the rest of my own life, and more concerned about the lives of others." [source: WP-Derek_Parfit (citing NYT obituary and Fearn 2007)] Use this continuation; do not repeat the first three sentences.
- **Parfit and the Buddha**: §92 "Wittgenstein and Buddha" begins at p. 273, where Parfit writes "Buddha would have agreed"; **Appendix J** ("Buddha's View", pp. 501–502) quotes passages relied on from the *Milindapañha*, Vasubandhu's *Abhidharmakośabhāṣya* and Buddhaghosa's *Visuddhimagga*, in Stcherbatsky's 1919 translation (also cited in Parfit 1987, 1995, 1999). Samarasinghe (UBC thesis) argues Parfit takes Buddhism out of context. [source: WS-7 (Coseru, Davis and other papers' statements); the appendix's exact title and quoted passages NOT verified]
- WP: Parfit's view is "similar to David Hume's bundle theory, and also to the view of the self in Buddhism's Skandha", but "besides being reductive, Parfit's view is also deflationary". [source: WP-Reasons_and_Persons]
- Siderits's programme: *Personal Identity and Buddhist Philosophy: Empty Persons* (Burlington, VT: Ashgate, 2003) reads Abhidharma as reductionism in Parfit's sense and argues the Personalists propose non-reductive supervenience (pp. 89–96). [source: IEP Madhyamaka bibliography (https://iep.utm.edu/madhyamaka-buddhist-philosophy/); SEP-MIB §§5.6, 6]

## Debates
- **What did Hume's Appendix worry about?** No consensus; Garrett 1981, Swain 1991, Baxter 1998, Ainslie 2008, McIntyre 2008, Cottrell 2015; Siakel counts 30+ interpretations; Galen Strawson reads Hume as never a "Humean" bundle theorist. [SEP-Hume §2.1; WS-2; WP-Galen_Strawson]
- **Is there an introspectible self?** Pro-Hume: Shoemaker, Martin, Howell, Prinz; anti: Chisholm, Cassam, G. Strawson, Damasio, Rosenthal; phenomenologists: no self-object, but pervasive for-me-ness; critics (Dainton, Bermúdez): for-me-ness is a reflective by-product. Advaita: the witness is never found because it is the finder (Śaṅkara, BrSūBh 2.3.7). [SEP-SC §3.1; SEP-PSC §2; SEP-Śaṅ §3.2]
- **Does Buddhism reduce or eliminate persons?** Siderits (reductionist; conventional truth; "not an eliminativist") vs Chadha (eliminativist) vs Pudgalavāda (non-reductive supervenience, Siderits's gloss) vs minority "witness"/two-tier readings (Horner, Bhattacharya, Albahari) vs prevailing rejection of them (Collins, Harvey and others). [SEP-MIB §§1.1, 5.6, 6; SEP-Bud §2; *Early Buddhism*]
- **Is the ālaya a self in disguise?** Critics said yes; Yogācāra: a river-like series of seeds, mistakenly taken as self by the defiled mind. [SEP-Yog §2]
- **Nyāya vs Buddhists on synthesis**: cross-modal recognition and memory need one subject (NS 3.1.1–3; Uddyotakara) vs causal streams suffice (Vasubandhu); Kapstein 2001 is the scholarly study. [IEP-Ny; SEP-Vas]
- **Advaita vs Nyāya on self-illumination**: self-luminous witness vs cognitions revealed by further cognitions (Nyāya's "higher-order" view); Śaṅkara also rejects Buddhist self-revealing cognitions (knife analogy). [SEP-Śaṅ §3.2]
- **Narrative selves**: MacIntyre and Schechtman (narrative constitution) vs Strawson (Episodic lives; against both psychological and ethical Narrativity) with critics on each side (Mackenzie & Poltera; Battersby). [SEP-PIE §10; WS-3, WS-9, WS-12]
- **Does identity matter?** Parfit, Martin (no) vs Sosa, Merricks (yes); Korsgaard: agency requires treating oneself as unified whatever the metaphysics; Rawls vs Parfit on separateness of persons. [SEP-PI §1; SEP-PIE §§3, 9, 10]
- **Is the Confucian self "relational"?** Ames (role ethics, against "virtue" framing) vs Wong (virtue language with qualifiers captures it; Confucians partly accept autonomy); Hall and Ames reject the "group over individual" caricature. [SEP-CE]
- **Split brains: one stream or two?** Debated; normal everyday unity vs lab dissociation. [SEP-CU §3.1]
- **Zhuangzi's "lost myself": true self or no self?** Both readings exist. [WS-6; SEP-CM; SEP-Zh]
