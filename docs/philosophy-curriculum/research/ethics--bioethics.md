## 1. The field: origins and names
- The word "bioethics" is usually traced to Van Rensselaer Potter, "Bioethics, the Science of Survival" (1970), and the founding of the Kennedy Institute (1971); IEP says this "oft-repeated story" is incorrect: the German theologian Fritz Jahr used "Bio-Ethik" in articles of 1927, 1928 and 1934, with a "bioethical imperative": "Respect every living being, in principle, as an end in itself and treat it accordingly wherever it is possible" (1927). [source: IEP-Bio §2a]
- Institutions: Institute of Society, Ethics, and the Life Sciences (1969, later the Hastings Center); Joseph and Rose Kennedy Center for the Study of Human Reproduction and Bioethics (1971, now the Kennedy Institute of Ethics, Georgetown). [source: IEP-Bio §2b]
- Catalysing events listed by IEP: Harvard definition of brain death (1968), Roe v. Wade (1973), the Karen Ann Quinlan case (1975), Baby Doe (1982); Henry Beecher's "Ethics and Clinical Research", *NEJM* 274 (1966). [source: IEP-Bio §2b and refs]
- Surprise: individual informed consent to experimentation was first a **German/Prussian** bureaucratic rule: Prussian regulations of 1900/01 after the Albert Neisser case (1896), and a Reich Interior Ministry circular of 28 Feb 1931 after 75 children died in a tuberculosis-vaccine episode; Robert Baker: informed consent "was originally a regulatory innovation created by Prussian bureaucrats". [source: IEP-Bio §2b] The Nuremberg Code article confirms Weimar-era "Guidelines for New Therapy and Human Experimentation" requiring consent, which Hitler's regime ignored. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg_Code]

## 2. Beauchamp and Childress's four principles (principlism)
- Tom L. Beauchamp and James F. Childress, *Principles of Biomedical Ethics*, Oxford University Press, first edition 1979; 8th edition 2019. [source: SEP-BMR bibliography]
- Four principles: respect for autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice — presented as *prima facie* principles, not lexically ordered. [source: SEP-BMR §1.2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principlism]
- Different starting points: Beauchamp a rule-utilitarian, Childress a Christian deontologist; the aim was a framework for a pluralist, secular society that does not presuppose a single moral theory. [source: SEP-BMR §1.2]
- Grounded in a "common morality" — "a universal morality to which all morally serious persons are committed"; historicist but not relativist. [source: SEP-BMR §1.2.1]
- Method: principles are made action-guiding by **specification** (adding content; idea from Henry Richardson 2000) and **balancing**, with coherence via reflective equilibrium; the authors call it a framework rather than a theory. [source: SEP-BMR §1.2.2 and critiques]
- Nicknames "Georgetown principles"/"Georgetown mantra" (both authors at Georgetown at the time). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principlism]
- Beauchamp also worked on the staff of the National Commission and co-wrote the Belmont Report. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Beauchamp]
- Editions caveat: SEP-BMR (Nov 2026) says the book is "now in its ninth edition"; WS-1 found a 9th edition (OUP, ISBN 978-0-19-783263-9) announced for December 2025 by one source and dated 2 February 2026 by a retailer listing. Beauchamp died in 2025 (WS-1, Bioethics Today tribute "1939–2025"). Say "nine editions, the first in 1979" and do not give an exact date for the 9th. [source: SEP-BMR; WS-1 https://bioethicstoday.org/blog/in-memoriam-a-tribute-to-tom-l-beauchamp-1939-2025/ ; https://www.schweitzer-online.de/en/buch/Beauchamp/Principles-Biomedical-Ethics-9e/9780197832639/A76327136/]
- Defender: Raanan Gillon argued the four principles provide a "transcultural, transnational, transreligious, and transphilosophical" framework and that autonomy should be "first among equals" (*JME* 2003). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principlism]

### Critiques of principlism
- The word "principlism" was coined by its critics K. Danner Clouser and Bernard Gert ("A Critique of Principlism", *Journal of Medicine and Philosophy* 15(2), 1990). Charge: the principles lack theoretical unity, come from conflicting theories, and with no ranking give no real guidance — "pick and mix". Gert, Culver and Clouser, *Bioethics: A Systematic Approach* (2nd ed. 2006) offers Gert's rule-based "common morality" alternative. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principlism; SEP-BMR §1.2 critiques and bib]
- Further criticism: lack of systematicity leaves too much to intuition (Gert et al. 2006); McMillan (2018): in practice newcomers just let autonomy trump the other three; Arras: unclear what extra justification the appeal to common morality adds. [source: SEP-BMR §1.2]
- Critics also say it is "American" (individualist) and neglects the virtues/agent (MacIntyre; Häyry). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principlism]

### Casuistry (Jonsen and Toulmin)
- Albert R. Jonsen and Stephen Toulmin, *The Abuse of Casuistry: A History of Moral Reasoning*, University of California Press. Date discrepancy: SEP-BMR cites 1989; Jonsen's Wikipedia page gives 1988. Use "1988" with caveat or "late 1980s". [source: SEP-BMR bib; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_R._Jonsen]
- Casuistry is "bottom up": reason by analogy from paradigm cases; we are more secure in particular judgments than in principles. Two versions: moderate (cases + established principles) and radical (principles are merely summaries of intuitions about cases). [source: SEP-BMR §1.3]
- Toulmin's evidence: on the US National Commission (1974–78) members agreed on hard cases despite disagreeing about moral theory. Jonsen was also a member of the Commission. [source: SEP-BMR §1.3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belmont_Report]
- Objections: what counts as "the case" is not neutral (O'Neill 1988), conservatism and inability to criticise received norms, analogies need principles to guide them, and a mishandled paradigm case propagates errors. [source: SEP-BMR §1.3]

## 3. Autonomy and informed consent
- Rationales for informed consent: protection; autonomy (dominant since the 1970s via Faden, Beauchamp, Childress and US commissions); preventing abuse; trust (O'Neill); self-ownership; non-domination; personal integrity. [source: SEP-IC contents and §2]
- Beauchamp & Childress on autonomy: "The autonomous individual acts freely in accordance with a self-chosen plan, analogous to the way an independent government manages its territories and establishes its policies" (2008 ed., 99–100). [source: SEP-IC §2.2]
- Example used by SEP: not transfusing a protesting, decisionally capable Jehovah's Witness even to save life — justified by autonomy, not health protection. [source: SEP-IC §2.2]
- Three disclosure standards: professional (what doctors customarily disclose), reasonable person, individual (subjective). The professional standard "has lost much of its traction with U.S., English, and Welsh courts". [source: SEP-IC §4]
- Onora O'Neill criticises a "fixation" with informed consent and the many senses of autonomy; Manson & O'Neill, *Rethinking Informed Consent in Bioethics* (2007). [source: SEP-IC]
- **Schloendorff v. Society of New York Hospital**, 105 N.E. 92 (N.Y. 1914): Benjamin Cardozo (then judge of the New York Court of Appeals): "Every human being of adult years and sound mind has a right to determine what shall be done with his own body; and a surgeon who performs an operation without his patient's consent commits an assault for which he is liable in damages." Facts: Mary Schloendorff consented to examination under ether, refused surgery; doctors removed a fibroid tumour. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schloendorff_v._Society_of_New_York_Hospital]
- The phrase "informed consent" in US law first recorded in *Salgo v. Leland Stanford Jr. University Board of Trustees* (1957). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Informed_consent]
- **Canterbury v. Spence**, 464 F.2d 772 (D.C. Cir. 1972): Jerry Canterbury (1939–2017), an FBI clerk, had a laminectomy by neurosurgeon William T. Spence (WP says the disk ruptured in 1958; operation year not given), fell from his hospital bed and was left partly paralysed; Spence had mentioned only "weakness", not paralysis. Judge Spottswood W. Robinson III held the disclosure standard is what a reasonable patient would want to know, and that expert testimony was not needed. Effect: shift from a "professional practice standard" to a "reasonable person standard". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canterbury_v._Spence]
- **Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board** [2015] UKSC 11, decided 11 March 2015 (UK Supreme Court; Lords Kerr and Reed gave the lead judgment; Lady Hale concurring). Nadine Montgomery, diabetic and of small stature, was not told of a 9–10% risk of shoulder dystocia; her baby was born severely disabled. The Court departed from *Sidaway* (1985) and the *Bolam* test for disclosure: "The test of materiality is whether, in the circumstances of the particular case, a reasonable person in the patient's position would be likely to attach significance to the risk, or the doctor is or should reasonably be aware that the particular patient would be likely to attach significance to it." (para 87). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montgomery_v_Lanarkshire_Health_Board] (The claimant's first name is not in the fetched text; omit it or check.)

## 4. Abortion
### 4.1 Framing
- SEP-Ab's basic anti-abortion argument (argument from a right to life): (1) The fetus is a person. (2) All persons have a right to life. (3) So the fetus has a right to life. (4) It is wrong to kill something that has a right to life. (5) Abortion kills the fetus. So abortion is wrong. [source: SEP-Ab §1]
- Equivocation objection: "person" = human being makes (1) obvious but (2) controversial; "person" = being with full moral status makes (2) obvious but (1) controversial. [source: SEP-Ab §1.1]
- Other responses: potentiality (Tooley's kitten-serum case, "Abortion and Infanticide", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 2(1), 1972, which also defended infanticide), self-consciousness requirement, consciousness requirement (Steinbock 1992), Harman's "Actual Future Principle" (1999), and the miscarriage objection (Berg 2017: miscarriage would be the greatest moral tragedy). [source: SEP-Ab §§1.4–1.6]
- Dworkin (*Life's Dominion*, 1993): both sides share belief in the intrinsic value of human life and differ in degree; opponents reply that many Catholics hold abortion to be murder (Thomson 1995). [source: SEP-Ab §4]
- Hursthouse (1991, virtue ethics): abortion "is always a matter of some seriousness". [source: SEP-Ab §4]
- Moral-risk argument (Moller 2011; Lockhart 2000): even if you reject anti-abortion arguments, the chance you are wrong gives a strong reason against abortion; replies: burdens on women may outweigh it; purely moral risk may not generate reasons (Weatherson 2019; Harman 2015). [source: SEP-Ab §5]

### 4.2 Thomson, "A Defense of Abortion" (1971) — ARGUMENT STEP BY STEP
- Judith Jarvis Thomson, "A Defense of Abortion", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 1(1): 47–66 (1971). Called perhaps "the most widely reprinted essay in all of contemporary philosophy" (William Parent, 1986). [source: SEP-Ab bib; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Defense_of_Abortion]
- Strategy: grant for the sake of argument that the fetus is a person with a right to life from conception, and show abortion can still be permissible. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Defense_of_Abortion; SEP-Ab §1.2]
- The case (Thomson 1971, 48–49, as quoted): "You wake up in the morning and find yourself back to back in bed with an unconscious violinist. A famous unconscious violinist. He has been found to have a fatal kidney ailment, and the Society of Music Lovers has canvassed all the available medical records and found that you alone have the right blood type to help. They have therefore kidnapped you, and last night the violinist's circulatory system was plugged into yours, so that your kidneys can be used to extract poisons from his blood as well as your own." He needs nine months. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Defense_of_Abortion]
- Reconstruction (SEP-Ab's positive version, which SEP notes is not made explicitly in Thomson 1971): (P1) If abortion is wrong, it is wrong to detach oneself from the violinist. (P2) It is not wrong to detach oneself from the violinist. (C) Abortion is not wrong. And the critical use: the violinist has a right to life yet may be killed by unplugging, so premise (4) of the right-to-life argument is false. [source: SEP-Ab §§1.2, 1.2.2]
- Thomson's diagnosis: the right to life is not a right to be given whatever one needs to live (Henry Fonda's touch example — SEP-Ab updates it to George Clooney; Thomson 1971 p. 55), nor simply a right not to be killed, but a right not to be killed *unjustly*. "[I]f you do allow him to go on using your kidneys, this is a kindness on your part, and not something he can claim from you as his due" (p. 55). IEP quotes: "You surely are not being unjust to him, for you gave him no right to use your kidneys, and no one else can have given him any such right." [source: SEP-Ab §1.3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Defense_of_Abortion; IEP-Ab §g] (SEP-Ab says the forehead-touch example is at Thomson 1971 p. 55 and uses Clooney; the original actor was Henry Fonda per the Wikipedia talk/common reports — NOT verified here; say "a film star".)
- Other Thomson cases: the tiny house with a rapidly growing child (self-defence against an innocent threat; "the mother owns the house", p. 53); "people-seeds" drifting through defective mesh window screens (contraception failure; p. 59). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Defense_of_Abortion]
- Limits Thomson sets: a late abortion "just to avoid the nuisance of postponing a trip abroad" would be "positively indecent" (pp. 65–66); she defends a right to stop being pregnant, not a right to secure the death of the child. She distinguishes being a Good Samaritan from a "Minimally Decent Samaritan" (term NOT verified this session; omit or check). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Defense_of_Abortion]
- **Main objections:** (a) Responsibility/tacit-consent objection: the violinist case only fits rape; a woman who had consensual sex is responsible for the fetus's need (Beckwith 1993; McMahan 2002 discuss). (b) Stranger vs offspring: the fetus is her child (Schwarz 1990; Beckwith 1993). (c) Killing vs letting die: abortion kills, unplugging lets die. (d) Intending vs foreseeing. (e) Natural vs artificial support: gestation, like breastfeeding, is ordinary parental provision, not a dialysis machine. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Defense_of_Abortion; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_aspects_of_the_abortion_debate]
- Peter Singer is an exception: on utilitarian grounds one is obliged to stay connected (Practical Ethics, 3rd ed. 2011, p. 134). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Defense_of_Abortion]
- **Reply to (a):** variant violinist case — you know the Society plans to kidnap you; you go about normal life with bodyguards; you are kidnapped anyway; surely you may still unplug, though your choice was a cause. A woman using contraception is no more responsible. SEP adds the open question whether abstaining from sex would be as onerous as staying home. Role-based duties of motherhood (Little 1999) presuppose a relationship. (Boonin 2003; Kamm 1992 defend Thomson.) [source: SEP-Ab §1.2.1]
- Third parties: if the fetus is an innocent threat, perhaps only the woman, not a doctor, may act (Davis 1984); English (1975) and SEP reply that doctors may act as her agents and that the reframing is about withdrawal of aid, not self-defence. [source: SEP-Ab §1.3]

### 4.3 Marquis, "Why Abortion Is Immoral" (1989) — ARGUMENT STEP BY STEP
- Don Marquis (1935–2022), University of Kansas (taught 1967–2016); "Why Abortion Is Immoral", *The Journal of Philosophy* 86(4): 183–202, April 1989; reprinted over 80 times. Marquis was an atheist (relevant: the argument is secular). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Marquis_(philosopher); SEP-Ab bib]
- Key move: avoid the personhood question entirely by asking what makes killing *us* wrong. Answer: the loss of one's future; "when I die, I am deprived of all of the value of my future" — the "experiences, activities, projects, and enjoyments" that would otherwise have constituted it. A being with a "future like ours" (FLO) is seriously wronged by being killed. Conclusion: "the overwhelming majority of deliberate abortions are seriously immoral", "in the same moral category as killing an innocent adult human being". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_aspects_of_the_abortion_debate (quoting Marquis)]
- SEP's form (argument from loss): (1) Abortion deprives the fetus of a valuable future. (2) Killing an adult human is wrong (unless justified) because it deprives them of a valuable future. (3) If what makes killing an adult wrong is present in another act, that act is also wrong (unless justified). So (C) abortion is wrong unless justified. [source: SEP-Ab §2]
- Claimed advantages (Marquis): explains why killing adults and infants is wrong without appeal to personhood; scope limits: fetuses so damaged they could never have conscious experience fall outside it; Marquis set aside hard cases such as rape. [source: SEP-Ab §2; WS-6 summaries of Marquis text, weaker]
- Consequence noted by WP: abortion is wrong whenever killing a child or adult with the same sort of future would be wrong — e.g. if euthanasia of someone facing a future of intense pain is acceptable, so is aborting a fetus with such a future. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_aspects_of_the_abortion_debate]
- **Objections:** (i) Moral-status objection: what makes killing wrong is loss of future *plus* moral status, so the argument smuggles back the personhood premise (SEP-Ab §2.1). (ii) Identity objection: if we are embodied minds/persons rather than organisms, the early fetus is not the same subject as the later person (McInerney 1990). (iii) Time-relative interests (McMahan 2002; DeGrazia 2007): the early fetus has little or no psychological connection to that future, so its loss matters little to it now. (iv) Contraception reductio (Norcross): if depriving of a FLO is wrong, so is contraception. [source: SEP-Ab §§2.1–2.2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_aspects_of_the_abortion_debate; WS-6]
- **Replies:** to (iv), Marquis and defenders: before conception there is no single identifiable subject who is deprived (sperm? ovum? both?); Blackshaw argues identifiability alone is not enough but that we are not identical with an ovum, so the reductio fails. To the interests objection, defenders cite the suicidal teenager who takes no interest in his future yet is wronged by being killed. [source: WS-6 https://philpapers.org/archive/BLACIN-2.pdf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_aspects_of_the_abortion_debate]
- Marquis and Thomson are compatible in structure: Marquis concludes abortion is wrong *unless justified*; Thomson grants a strong reason against abortion and argues it can be outweighed. Marquis 1989 does not cite Thomson. [source: SEP-Ab §2.3]

### 4.4 Warren's personhood criteria (1973)
- Mary Anne Warren (1946–2010), San Francisco State University; "On the Moral and Legal Status of Abortion", *The Monist* 57(1): 43–61 (1973); "Postscript on Infanticide" (1982). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Anne_Warren; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_aspects_of_the_abortion_debate]
- 1973 criteria (five): (1) consciousness, in particular the capacity to feel pain; (2) reasoning; (3) self-motivated activity; (4) the capacity to communicate messages of indefinitely many types; (5) the presence of self-concepts and self-awareness. A being need not have all; one with none is certainly not a person; the fetus has none, so it is not a person, and abortion is permissible. [source: IEP-Ab §c (from Warren 1984 reprint, 110–113); WS-9]
- DISCREPANCY: Wikipedia lists *six* criteria (sentience, emotionality, reason, communication, self-awareness, moral agency) — this matches Warren's later formulations (e.g. 1997 "Abortion" in *A Companion to Ethics*), not the 1973 paper. Use the five for 1973. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Anne_Warren vs IEP-Ab; WS-9]
- Objection: criteria seem to exclude newborns (hence her postscript on infanticide) and some cognitively disabled humans; Marquis: Warren's criteria are "plagued by difficulties concerning cases". Jane English (1975/1984) argued personhood is a cluster concept with a penumbra; even if the fetus is a person abortion may be justified, and if not, it may still be wrong. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Anne_Warren; IEP-Ab §c]

### 4.5 Religious perspectives
**Catholic**
- The *Catechism* (1992): "human life must be respected and protected absolutely from the moment of conception. From the first moment of his existence, a human being must be recognized as having the rights of a person – among which is the inviolable right of every innocent being to life". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_and_abortion]
- Condemnation of abortion from the 1st–2nd centuries (*Didache*, *Epistle of Barnabas*, Tertullian, Athenagoras). [source: same]
- History of delayed ensoulment: Aristotle's successive souls (vegetative, sensitive, rational) and 40 days (male)/90 days (female) informed medieval canon law; Augustine accepted the Septuagint's formed/unformed distinction and did not call abortion of an unformed fetus murder, while still condemning it. In 1869 (*Apostolicae Sedis*) Pius IX removed the distinction for excommunication. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ensoulment; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_and_abortion]
- Double effect: removing a cancerous pregnant uterus or a fallopian tube with an ectopic pregnancy is "indirect abortion" and permitted (Pius XII 1953 address); methotrexate/salpingostomy remain debated in Catholic medicine. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_and_abortion; SEP-DDE §1 "Abortion vs Hysterectomy"]
- Dissent within: Dombrowski and Deltete, *A Brief, Liberal, Catholic Defense of Abortion*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_and_abortion]

**Islamic (ensoulment at 120 days debate)**
- The Qur'an does not address abortion explicitly; verses against killing (5:32, 17:31) are applied. Hadith (Sahih al-Bukhari) places ensoulment after 120 days; many medieval jurists treated 120 days as the key line. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_abortion]
- Schools differ: Hanafi — 120 days (minority 40); some Hanafis permit abortion before 120 days, others call it *makruh* (disapproved); some Shafi'is 80 days; Maliki — most forbid abortion at any point (WP pages variously give Maliki ensoulment at conception or 40 days — inconsistent); Ibadi: conception; Zahiri (Ibn Hazm) ensoulment at 120 days but prohibition from conception; Shi'a: forbidden after implantation (Khomeini: forbidden "even at the earliest possible stage"); Iran's Shia scholars approved therapeutic abortion before 16 weeks in limited circumstances (2003). All schools put the mother's life first. Ibn Taymiyya: abortion haram by consensus. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_abortion; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ensoulment]
- The 40-vs-120 debate turns on readings of one hadith (stages of 40 days each vs. all within 40); Sachedina (*Islamic Biomedical Ethics*, 2009) notes Shi'i hadiths tying life to conception. [source: WS-12 https://contendingmodernities.nd.edu/field-notes/what-or-when-is-the-human-person/ ; https://www.zygonjournal.org/article/id/19008/ — weaker]
- Hadith on compensation (*ghurra*/diya) for causing miscarriage set at a slave, less than for a person. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_abortion]

**Jewish**
- No explicit Hebrew Bible text on intentional abortion; Exodus 21:22–23 (miscarriage caused in a fight): Rashi reads "harm" as harm to the mother, so only a fine for the fetus; Philo read it differently. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism_and_abortion]
- Mishnah (Ohalot 7:6): "If a woman is in hard travail, one cuts up the offspring in her womb ... because her life comes before the life of her foetus. But if the greater part has proceeded forth, one may not set aside one person for the sake of saving another." Rashi: the fetus is not a *nefesh* until born; Maimonides justified it instead by the *rodef* (pursuer) principle. Most movements discourage abortion except to save the woman's life, with authorities differing on other cases (e.g. Yair Bacharach's responsum). Sabbath may be broken to save a fetus. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism_and_abortion] (Tractate name "Ohalot 7:6" not in fetched text — WP says "the Mishnah"; NOT verified.)

**Buddhist**
- No single view; intentional killing is negative, many traditions date life from conception; monks can be expelled for helping procure an abortion; emphasis on intention and compassion; Thai discussion framed in terms of "benefit and harm" rather than rights (Peter Harvey); Tibetan Buddhists strongly negative but advise compassion and karmic remedies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism_and_abortion]
- Japan: **mizuko kuyō** ("water child" memorial service) for aborted or miscarried fetuses, with Jizō statues in red bibs; performed widely since the 1970s; criticised as commercial exploitation of guilt and fear of vengeful spirits (Helen Hardacre, *Marketing the Menacing Fetus in Japan*, 1997), defended by others. Taiwan has a similar *yingling gongyang* from the mid-1970s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mizuko_kuy%C5%8D; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism_and_abortion]
- William R. LaFleur, *Liquid Life: Abortion and Buddhism in Japan* (Princeton UP, 1992): abortion understood as sending a life back to the other world. [source: WS-11 https://press.princeton.edu/isbn/9780691029658 ; https://www.lrb.co.uk/v15/n19/thomas-crump/floating-it-away]
- Japan's Eugenic Protection Law (1948) made it one of the first Asian countries to legalise abortion; revised as the Maternal Health Protection Law (1996). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abortion_in_Japan]

### 4.6 Selective abortion and the disability critique
- Expressivist argument (Asch 1989; Parens and Asch 2000): aborting an otherwise wanted pregnancy because of disability expresses that disabled people should not exist; objections: burdens on the family, parents may legitimately aim at non-disabled children, no demonstrable harm. [source: SEP-Ab §6]
- Adrienne Asch (1946–2013), blind from infancy, supported legal abortion but opposed prenatal testing for disability: "My moral opposition to prenatal testing and selective abortion flows from the conviction that life with disability is worthwhile and the belief that a just society must appreciate and nurture the lives of all people, whatever the endowments they receive in the natural lottery" (*American Journal of Public Health*, 1999). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrienne_Asch]

## 5. Euthanasia and assisted dying
### 5.1 Terms and the case for
- Euthanasia: bringing about another's death for that person's benefit; voluntary (competent, enduring request), non-voluntary (no competent request), involuntary (against expressed wishes — "ought to be unlawful"). Physician-assisted suicide: patient self-administers. [source: SEP-Eu §1]
- Five conditions often proposed for candidacy: terminal illness; unlikely to benefit from a cure; intolerable pain or unacceptably burdensome life; enduring, voluntary, competent wish; unable to end life unaided. Many now regard the terminal-illness condition as too restrictive (Belgium, Netherlands, Canada). [source: SEP-Eu §2]
- Moral case: respect for autonomy plus well-being, "each necessary, neither sufficient" (Young; Sumner). [source: SEP-Eu §3]
- Inviolability view (Keown) and Velleman's Kantian argument (1999): one may not violate rational nature for the sake of well-being. [source: SEP-Eu §1]

### 5.2 Active vs passive: Rachels (1975)
- James Rachels, "Active and Passive Euthanasia", *New England Journal of Medicine* 292 (1975). Page range given as 78–86 in SEP-DA; commonly 78–80 — omit pages. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Rachels; SEP-DA bib]
- Smith and Jones: Smith drowns his young cousin in the bath to gain an inheritance; Jones plans to, finds the boy already slipped under, and watches him drown without helping. The cases differ only as killing vs letting die; Smith is no worse than Jones; so killing per se is not worse than letting die. If passive euthanasia is allowed, active should be, as it is more humane. (Inheritance motive: standard but not in fetched text — say "for gain".) [source: SEP-DA §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Rachels]
- Objections: Kagan (1988) — the "contrast strategy" assumes the Additive Assumption; Kamm (2007) — one could kill Smith but not Jones to bring the child back, revealing a difference; Hill (2018) — cases not equalised. [source: SEP-DA §1]
- Opponents (Finnis 1995; Keown) locate the difference in intention: withdrawing futile/burdensome treatment lets the disease kill; Young replies that terminal sedation plus withholding nutrition is often best explained as intending death. Even the Catholic Church accepts switching off a respirator at a competent patient's request. [source: SEP-Eu §4.4]

### 5.3 Double effect at the end of life (brief; cross-ref Deontology)
- Traditional DDE conditions as listed in SEP-Eu §4.3. Classic application: morphine to relieve pain, foreseeing hastened death, vs morphine to kill. [source: SEP-Eu §4.3; SEP-DDE §1]
- Factual correction: palliative-care specialists hold that properly titrated opioids rarely depress respiration or hasten death; "there is no debate among specialists in palliative care" (Fohr 1998). So the stock example rests on a myth, which can lead to under-treatment of pain. [source: SEP-DDE §5.1]
- Young: DDE is irrelevant where death is not a harm to the patient. [source: SEP-Eu §4.3]

### 5.4 Slippery slopes and the evidence (contested)
- Forms: logical, psychological, arbitrary-line. Young argues none is compelling; Keown (2018, 2022) argues the Dutch experience shows the slope is real. Nazi "euthanasia" was eugenic killing for society's benefit, not voluntary euthanasia (Burleigh 1994). [source: SEP-Eu §4.5]
- Dutch evidence as read by Young: national studies since the Remmelink Report (1991) "consistently" show no slippery slope; about 60% of assisted deaths were clearly voluntary; most of the rest were patients no longer competent, decided with family. Emanuel et al. (2016): little evidence of abuse of the vulnerable. Critics read the same "termination of life without explicit request" figures as evidence of a slope. [source: SEP-Eu §4.5; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euthanasia_and_assisted_suicide_in_the_Netherlands]
- Remmelink: in 1990, of ~130,000 Dutch deaths, ~2,300 voluntary euthanasia and ~400 PAS. [source: WP Netherlands]

### 5.5 Legal milestones (check dates carefully)
- **Netherlands:** 1973 Postma case; 1984 Schoonheim (Supreme Court, *force majeure*); Termination of Life on Request and Assisted Suicide (Review Procedures) Act passed lower house Nov 2000, Senate April 2001, in force **1 April 2002**; age 12+ (12–16 needs parental consent); Groningen Protocol for infants. Reported cases: 1,626 euthanasia (2003); 6,585 (2017); 9,068 (2023, 5.4% of deaths); 9,958 (2024). Psychiatric cases contested (e.g. Zoraya ter Beek, 29, May 2024). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euthanasia_and_assisted_suicide_in_the_Netherlands; SEP-Eu §1]
- **Belgium:** legalised 2002; extended to terminally ill minors (Senate vote Dec 2013; law 2014; first case Sept 2016); about 1,400 cases a year early on, record 1,807 in 2013. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legality_of_euthanasia; SEP-Eu]
- Luxembourg 2009; Spain 2021; Colombia via Constitutional Court from late 1990s. [source: SEP-Eu §1]
- **Oregon:** Death with Dignity Act = Ballot Measure 16, approved **8 November 1994** (51.3% to 48.7%); injunction lifted **27 October 1997**; repeal (Measure 51) rejected by ~60% in November 1997; *Washington v. Glucksberg* (1997): no federal constitutional right, states may legislate; *Gonzales v. Oregon* upheld the law. Six-month terminal prognosis; patient self-administers. 1998–2024: 4,881 prescriptions, 3,243 deaths; median age 73; concerns: loss of autonomy (90.1%), inability to enjoy life (89.4%), loss of dignity (69.4%). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994_Oregon_Ballot_Measure_16] Note: SEP-Eu says "legislation was introduced in 1997 after a referendum" — misleading; use 1994 vote / 1997 implementation.
- **Australia:** Northern Territory 1996 law overturned by federal Parliament 1997; Victoria 2017 (in force 2019); all states by 2023. [source: SEP-Eu §1]
- **Canada (MAID):** *Carter v Canada* (Supreme Court, February 2015); Quebec legislation 2014 (WP: legal in Quebec from June 2014; SEP: "came into effect in 2016" — sources differ, say 2014–16); federal Bill C-14 passed **17 June 2016** (death "reasonably foreseeable" required); *Truchon* (Quebec Superior Court, 11 Sept 2019) struck down that requirement; Bill C-7 passed **17 March 2021** creating "Track 2" for those whose death is not reasonably foreseeable (90-day assessment); inclusion of mental illness as sole condition postponed from 2023 to 2024 and then to **2027**. 2024: 16,499 MAID deaths (5.1% of deaths), median age 77.9; ~76,000 total since 2016 (as of late 2025). Canada uniquely allows nurse practitioners to administer. Alberta Bill 18 (March 2026) proposed barring MAID for sole mental illness (status unclear — hedge). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euthanasia_and_assisted_suicide_in_Canada]
- Critics of Canada: in 2021 UN Special Rapporteur on the rights of persons with disabilities (with the Independent Expert on older persons and the Special Rapporteur on extreme poverty) said C-7 risked violating disabled people's right to life and non-discrimination; concerns about poverty/housing (e.g. "Sophia", 2022, chemical sensitivity, unable to find suitable housing); panel member Ellen Cohen resigned. Supporters argue delays for mental illness are discriminatory, and studies show most people with mental illness retain decision-making capacity. [source: same]
- **UK (hedge — very recent):** Assisting suicide illegal under the Suicide Act 1961 (max 14 years). Kim Leadbeater's Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill (England and Wales) introduced 16 Oct 2024; Commons second reading passed 29 Nov 2024 (330–275), third reading 20 June 2025 (314–291); stalled in Lords committee and fell when the session ended (April 2026); reintroduced by Lauren Edwards (first reading 17 June 2026); **defeated at second reading 11 September 2026, 286–270**. Scotland: Liam McArthur's bill defeated 17 March 2026 (69–57 against). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminally_Ill_Adults_(End_of_Life)_Bill ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assisted_Dying_for_Terminally_Ill_Adults_(Scotland)_Bill ; WS-2 https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/11/uk-lawmakers-vote-against-bill-to-legalise-assisted-dying] Write as "as of late 2026".
- SEP-Eu lists US jurisdictions with assisted dying including Washington (2009), Vermont (2013), California, Colorado, Hawaii, Maine, New Jersey, New Mexico, DC, and also "Florida, Illinois, Iowa, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New York" — several of these not independently checked; do not list US states beyond Oregon, Washington, Vermont, Montana (courts 2008–09) without checking. [source: SEP-Eu §1]

## 6. Research ethics and its scandals
- **Nuremberg Code (1947):** set out in the verdict of *United States v. Karl Brandt et al.* ("Doctors' Trial", first of the 12 subsequent Nuremberg trials, US military tribunal); indictment 25 Oct 1946; trial 9 Dec 1946 – 20 Aug 1947; 23 defendants (20 physicians, 3 SS officials); 7 death sentences (hanged 2 June 1948 at Landsberg), 5 life, 4 terms of 10–20 years, 7 acquitted. Ten points; first: "The voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential." Drafted from memoranda by Leo Alexander and Andrew Ivy (authorship disputed: Ivy, Alexander, judge Harold Sebring). Not adopted as law by any nation; initially dismissed as "a code for barbarians" (Jay Katz). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg_Code ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctors%27_trial ; SEP-IC]
- **Tuskegee (1932–1972):** "Tuskegee Study of Untreated Syphilis in the Negro Male", US Public Health Service with Tuskegee Institute, Macon County, Alabama; 600 poor Black sharecroppers (399 with latent syphilis, 201 controls); never told the diagnosis ("bad blood"); not treated with penicillin even after it became standard (by 1947). Exposed by PHS investigator Peter Buxtun via AP reporter Jean Heller (story dated 26 July 1972, front page of the *New York Times*); ended 16 Nov 1972. By then 28 men had died of syphilis, 100 of complications, 40 wives infected, 19 children born with congenital syphilis. $10 million settlement (1974). Led to the National Research Act (1974) and Belmont Report. Clinton's apology 16 May 1997: "what the United States government did was shameful, and I am sorry." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study] Note: the study was not secret — results were published in medical journals from 1934/1936.
- **Guatemala (1946–48):** US researchers infected and enrolled Guatemalans without consent; SEP-LA gives "5500 Guatemalans" enrolled (the number deliberately infected was smaller — not verified). A key reason Latin American bioethicists reject "double standards" in international trials (e.g. proposed Surfaxin placebo trial in Bolivia, Ecuador, Peru, Mexico, early 2000s, blocked after Public Citizen protest). [source: SEP-LA §6.2]
- **Declaration of Helsinki:** World Medical Association, adopted June 1964 in Helsinki; eight revisions (latest October 2024) and two clarifications; 11 paragraphs (1964) to 37 (2024); not legally binding. 1975 revision: "concern for the interests of the subject must always prevail over the interests of science and society"; introduced independent committee review (→ IRBs/research ethics committees). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_Helsinki]
- **Belmont Report:** National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects (created by the National Research Act, 12 July 1974); four-day meeting at the Smithsonian's Belmont Conference Center, Feb 1976; report released 30 Sept 1978; published in the Federal Register 18 April 1979. Three principles: respect for persons, beneficence, justice; applications: informed consent, risk-benefit assessment, selection of subjects. Eleven members, incl. Albert Jonsen, Dorothy Height, Patricia King, Karen Lebacqz. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belmont_Report]
- **Henrietta Lacks** (born Loretta Pleasant, 1 Aug 1920 – 4 Oct 1951): cervical tumour cells taken at Johns Hopkins in 1951 without consent (then normal practice) and cultured by George Gey as HeLa, the first mass-produced immortal human cell line (L929 mouse line was earlier); used by Jonas Salk's polio vaccine work by 1954; family unaware until 1975; NIH–family agreement on genome data access August 2013; Thermo Fisher settled with the estate 31 July 2023 (undisclosed terms). Rebecca Skloot, *The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks* (2010). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Lacks]
- **Unit 731:** secret Imperial Japanese Army biological/chemical warfare unit (1933/1936–1945), main base Pingfang, Harbin (Manchukuo), led by Shirō Ishii; prisoners called "maruta" ("logs"), mostly Chinese but also Russians, Koreans and others including children; infections, vivisection, frostbite and pressure experiments; no documented survivors. Death estimates vary widely: infobox "over 3,000" in-facility (1940–45), lead text "an estimated 14,000"; biological attacks ~200,000 (estimate). Twelve members tried by the USSR at Khabarovsk (1949); Ishii and others granted US immunity in exchange for data. Tokyo District Court (28 Aug 2002) acknowledged the biological warfare but denied compensation; National Archives of Japan released 3,607 names (April 2018). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_731]
- Postwar Japan and research ethics: many former members returned to universities, research institutes and drug firms; Masaji Kitano and Ryōichi Naitō were associated with the Green Cross pharmaceutical company (Kitano as its head); the Japanese medical establishment long remained largely silent. [source: WS-10 https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masaji_Kitano ; https://japantimes.co.jp/news/1998/08/14/national/green-cross-founder-tied-to-unit-731-preservation — weaker]
- Japanese bioethics distrust: Juro Wada's heart transplant at Sapporo Medical University (8 Aug 1968; recipient died after 83 days) led to a manslaughter accusation over the donor's brain-death diagnosis (dropped) and lasting distrust; 1997 Organ Transplant Law allowed brain-dead donation with strict opt-in (donors 15+, family consent), relaxed in 2010. [source: WS-13 https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organ_transplantation_in_Japan — weaker]
- **He Jiankui (2018):** at SUSTech, Shenzhen, used CRISPR-Cas9 on embryos of couples with HIV-positive fathers to disable *CCR5*; twins "Lulu" and "Nana" born; story broken by *MIT Technology Review* (Antonio Regalado) 25 Nov 2018, He announced on YouTube the same day; presented at the Second International Summit on Human Genome Editing, Hong Kong, 28 Nov 2018; edits were mosaic and novel (not the natural CCR5-Δ32); consent forms "incomplete and inadequate"; a third child born ~2019. Sentenced 30 Dec 2019 to three years and a 3 million yuan fine for illegal medical practice; released April 2022; China's 2020 Civil Code and Criminal Law amendment prohibit such editing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/He_Jiankui_affair ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/He_Jiankui]

## 7. Genetic enhancement
- Treatment vs enhancement distinction contested (Buchanan 2011). Bioconservatives (Kass 2003; Fukuyama 2002; Habermas 2003; Sandel 2007) vs transhumanists/enthusiasts (Harris 2007 *Enhancing Evolution*; Savulescu 2009; Agar 2004 *Liberal Eugenics*). [source: SEP-Enh]
- **Sandel:** "The Case Against Perfection", *The Atlantic Monthly* (April 2004); book *The Case against Perfection: Ethics in the Age of Genetic Engineering* (Belknap/Harvard UP, 2007). Argument: enhancement expresses a drive to mastery that fails to acknowledge "the giftedness of life", treating children as "objects of our design or products of our will or instruments of our ambition" rather than accepting them as given (Sandel 2004, 56); concerns for humility, responsibility and solidarity. [source: SEP-SC; SEP-Enh bib; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Sandel]
- Kass on enhancement as passive: the subject "is not merely passive but who plays no role at all" (Kass 2003); reply: free, deliberate use of enhancements is not passive (Kamm 2005). [source: SEP-Enh §5.1]
- **Savulescu, procreative beneficence:** "Procreative Beneficence: Why We Should Select the Best Children", *Bioethics* 15(5–6): 413–26 (October 2001): couples selecting among possible children have a moral *obligation* (not mere permission) to select the child expected to have the best life, e.g. via PGD; later Savulescu & Kahane (2009). Critics: de Melo-Martín (2004); Rebecca Bennett (2014): no one is harmed by non-selection (non-identity), and the intuitions reflect non-moral preferences; Parens & Asch / disability critics: eugenic. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Savulescu]
- **Habermas:** *Die Zukunft der menschlichen Natur. Auf dem Weg zu einer liberalen Eugenik?* (Suhrkamp, 2001); English *The Future of Human Nature* (Polity, 2003). Distinguishes therapeutic ("negative") from enhancement ("positive") eugenics, opposing the latter: the designed person cannot be presumed to consent, and her sense of being the sole author of her life and the symmetry among free and equal persons are threatened; the "ethical self-understanding of the species" is at stake. [source: SEP-Enh §6; WS-7 https://web.stanford.edu/~mvr2j/habermas.html ; https://www.hrk.de/... catalogue]
- **Disability critique (Asch):** see §4.6; SEP-Enh: genotype-ranking ("jewels in the genome" vs "toxic waste") reduces persons to genotypes and undermines moral equality amid biological diversity (Asch and Block 2011); disability scholars note we are no better placed than early eugenicists to decide which traits to promote. [source: SEP-Enh §6.3]
- CRISPR: genome-editing tool used by He; WHO has issued three reports on human genome editing governance since 2019. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/He_Jiankui_affair]

## 8. Allocation of scarce care
- **QALY**: quality-adjusted life year; full health = 1, death = 0; e.g. a state valued 0.4 for 30 years = 12 QALYs; DALY works in reverse (0.6 × 30 = 18 DALYs). Values elicited by standard gamble, time trade-off, person trade-off, surveys. [source: SEP-DCR §1.1]
- Ethical problems: aggregation (Oregon Medicaid plan c. 1990–92 ranked tooth capping above appendectomy), priority to the worst off, the "rule of rescue", the democracy problem, and disability discrimination (Brock: ADA 1990 + Oregon 1992 sparked the debate). [source: SEP-DCR §1.2]
- **Harris:** *The Value of Life* (1985) set out a "fair innings" argument (those who have not had a fair innings get priority); "QALYfying the Value of Life", *Journal of Medical Ethics* 13(3): 117–123 — SEP-DCR dates it 1986, but vol. 13 is 1987 (WS-4 source cites "Harris, 1987" on QALYs' "double jeopardy" for the disabled/ill). Harris argued QALYs discriminate against the old and disabled; he later rejected fair innings for strict age-indifference. Alan Williams (1997, *Health Economics*) developed fair innings within health economics. [source: SEP-DCR; SEP-PH §2.2.4; WS-4 https://www.lse.ac.uk/lse-health/assets/documents/working-paper-series/LSEHWP14.pdf ; https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-03670001v1/file/Fair%20innings_english%20version.pdf]
- Persad, Wertheimer and Emanuel (2009): the "complete lives system" combining several principles. [source: SEP-PH §2.2.4]
- **Triage**: from French *trier* (to sort); modern triage from Napoleon's surgeons Dominique-Jean Larrey and Pierre-François Percy; Depage's *Ordre de Triage* (1914). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triage]
- **COVID-19 ventilator debates (2020):** Italy's SIAARTI recommendations (6 March 2020) used age, comorbidity and functional status, drawing criticism of age-based rationing; Emanuel, Persad, Upshur et al., "Fair Allocation of Scarce Medical Resources in the Time of Covid-19", *NEJM* (2020) — six recommendations, maximise benefits; US disability groups (Alabama Disabilities Advocacy Program and The Arc) filed a complaint on 24 March 2020 against Alabama's 2010 ventilator criteria excluding e.g. "profound mental retardation" and "moderate to severe dementia"; HHS Office for Civil Rights announced resolution 8 April 2020 after Alabama withdrew them. [source: WS-3 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8370519/ ; WS-10 https://www.hhs.gov/about/news/2020/04/08/ocr-reaches-early-case-resolution-alabama-after-it-removes-discriminatory-ventilator-triaging.html]

## Debates
- Principlism vs its critics (Clouser and Gert: no unifying theory; casuists Jonsen and Toulmin: start from cases; virtue and feminist critics). [source: SEP-BMR; WP Principlism]
- Abortion: right-to-life argument vs equivocation/potentiality objections; Thomson's bodily-rights argument vs responsibility/stranger/killing objections (Boonin, Kamm defend; Beckwith, Lee, Schwarz criticise); Marquis's FLO vs identity, time-relative interest and contraception objections; Dworkin's intrinsic-value reconciliation vs Thomson 1995. [source: SEP-Ab; WP]
- Euthanasia: Young/Sumner/Rachels (permissible, legalise) vs Keown/Finnis/Velleman (inviolability, intention, slippery slope); interpretation of Dutch and Canadian data contested. [source: SEP-Eu]
- Opioids and DDE: whether double effect is even needed at the end of life given the medical evidence. [source: SEP-DDE §5.1]
- Enhancement: Sandel/Kass/Habermas vs Savulescu/Harris/Agar; disability critics (Asch) vs procreative beneficence. [source: SEP-Enh]
- Allocation: QALY maximisation vs fair innings, priority to the worst off, and disability non-discrimination. [source: SEP-DCR; SEP-PH]
- Islamic ensoulment: 40 vs 120 days and its implications for early abortion. [source: WP Islam and abortion; WS-12]
