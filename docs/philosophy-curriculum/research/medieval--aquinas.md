## 1. Life
- Birth: "around the year 1225" (SEP-Aq: "ca. 1225"); IEP-Aq: "sometime between 1224 and 1226 in Roccasecca"; WP: "most likely born in the family castle of Roccasecca, near Aquino ... c. 1225". Recommended: "born about 1225 (some say 1224/25)". The Early Scholasticism notes use 1224/5, IEP-OA uses 1224. [sources: SEP-Aq §1.1; IEP-Aq §1a; WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- Place: near Aquino, "halfway between Rome and Naples"; a wealthy family "that presided over a prominent castle in Roccasecca" (SEP-Aq). WP: Roccasecca was then in the Kingdom of Sicily (present-day Lazio); father Landulf of Aquino, a *miles* in the service of Emperor Frederick II; mother Theodora (Countess of Teano). [sources: SEP-Aq §1.1; WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- Siblings: "the youngest of at least nine children" (SEP-Aq); "one of nine children ... the youngest of four boys" (IEP-Aq). [sources: SEP-Aq; IEP-Aq]
- Monte Cassino: educated there from about age 5 to 15; his parents probably hoped he would become abbot (his uncle Sinibald was abbot, per WP). In early 1239 fighting between Frederick II and Pope Gregory IX reached the abbey, and he was enrolled at the *studium generale* of Naples (IEP: began studies at Naples in autumn 1239). At Naples he probably first read Aristotle, perhaps introduced by Peter of Ireland. [sources: IEP-Aq §1a; WP-Thomas_Aquinas; SEP-Aq §1.1]
- Dominicans: "a newly founded order of priests devoted to preaching and learning"; he joined "at the age of nineteen" (SEP-Aq). IEP: received the Dominican habit in April 1244. [sources: SEP-Aq §1.1; IEP-Aq §1a]
- Family detention: his brothers, on their mother's orders, seized him on his way to Rome/Paris (WP: "as he was drinking from a spring") and held him at the family castles of Monte San Giovanni Campano and Roccasecca for about a year. SEP-Aq: "After confining him to Roccasecca for a year, his parents yielded"; IEP-Aq: "placed under house arrest" from late 1244 / early 1245 and returned to the Dominicans in autumn 1245. [sources: SEP-Aq; IEP-Aq; WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- The prostitute story (he drove her out with a burning brand and traced a cross on the door or wall; WP adds angels girding him with a girdle of chastity, citing the canonization records): IEP presents it as "a famous story"; SEP-Aq: many colourful stories "stem from testimony given during his canonization inquiry in 1319. The authenticity of these stories is unknowable." Present it as a legend. [sources: IEP-Aq; WP-Thomas_Aquinas; SEP-Aq §1.1]
- Paris and Cologne: three years in Paris (1245–48), then sent to Cologne in 1248 under Albert the Great (SEP-Aq; IEP-Aq). SEP-Alb: Albert went to Cologne to found a new Dominican *studium generale*, "accompanied by the young Italian student he had met in 1245 and taught in Paris, Thomas Aquinas." [sources: SEP-Aq; IEP-Aq; SEP-Alb]
- Albert's influence: "the leading figure in the newly prominent program of melding Christian theology with Greek and Arabic philosophy"; his conviction "that the Christian faith could only benefit from a profound engagement with philosophy and science" became Aquinas's own. [source: SEP-Aq §1.1] SEP-Alb: Aquinas would "never have reached the heights he did" without pioneers like Albert; Aquinas's greater influence is in part his master's. [source: SEP-Alb]
- "Dumb ox": WP reports that fellow students called him a "dumb ox" (*bos mutus*) and Albert replied that his bellowing would one day be heard throughout the world (citing Stump 2003, p. 3). Hagiographical; present as a story. [source: WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- 1252: Albert nominated him for the advanced theology degree at Paris "even though he was still two years younger than the minimum required age"; four years as bachelor lecturing on the Bible and Peter Lombard's *Sentences*; then master (SEP-Aq). IEP: bachelor of the *Sentences* 1252–56, master in 1256 at age 32. WP: appointed regent master in early 1256 by intervention of Pope Alexander IV, amid the dispute between secular masters and the mendicant orders. [sources: SEP-Aq; IEP-Aq; WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- First Paris regency: 1256–1259, the Dominican chair of theology. [source: SEP-Aq] Dominicans rotated masters through the chair. [source: SEP-Aq]
- Italy 1259–1268: SEP-Aq: "first in Orvieto (in Umbria) and then in Rome"; IEP-Aq: Naples 1259–1261, Orvieto 1261–1265, Rome 1265–1268. WP: general preacher at Naples (1260), conventual lector at Orvieto from September 1261, from 1265 at the Santa Sabina *studium* in Rome, where he began the *Summa theologiae*. [sources: SEP-Aq; IEP-Aq; WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- Second Paris regency: 1268–1272, "an unusual second term" (SEP-Aq); in it he wrote a short treatise on the eternity of the world and "a somewhat longer treatise against the view that all human beings share a single intellect", and worked on the *Summa* and commentaries on "all of Aristotle's principal works". [sources: SEP-Aq; IEP-Aq] WP: sent partly because of the rise of "Averroism"/radical Aristotelianism, and he disputed with Franciscans (Stump: *De aeternitate mundi* was persuasively argued to target John Pecham). [source: WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- 1272: to Naples to set up a Dominican *studium generale*. [sources: SEP-Aq; WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- The 1273 experience: IEP-Aq: "around December of 1273", while writing the treatise on the sacraments in the ST, he had "a particularly powerful religious experience" and afterwards refused to write despite urging from his confessor and assistant Reginald of Piperno. WP: 6 December 1273, during Mass. [sources: IEP-Aq; WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- The "straw" saying, versions:
  - SEP-Aq (Pasnau's translation): "All that I have written seems to me like straw compared with what has now been revealed to me."
  - WP (citing Davies 1993): "Reginald, I cannot, because all that I have written seems like straw to me."
  - WS-2: the source is the testimony of Bartholomew of Capua at the canonization inquiry (Naples, 1319); the Latin is reported as *Raynalde, non possum, quia omnia quae scripsi videntur mihi paleae* (Wikiquote); a second reply, "All that I have written seems to me as straw [paleae] in comparison to what I saw and what has been revealed to me"; some scholars prefer "chaff" for *paleae*.
  - WRITER: say "according to testimony given at his canonization inquiry", use SEP's wording, and note that *palea* can mean straw or chaff. [sources: SEP-Aq; WP-Thomas_Aquinas; WS-2]
- Death: on the way to the Second Council of Lyon (summoned by Gregory X; it opened 1 May 1274), at the Cistercian abbey of Fossanova, 7 March 1274. WP: he struck his head on a branch on the way, and a chronic subdural haematoma has been proposed as the cause of death (speculative). [sources: SEP-Aq; IEP-Aq; WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- Output: "more than eight million words (eight times more than has survived ... from Aristotle)"; "essentially everything he wrote has survived". [source: SEP-Aq §1.2]
- He did not know Greek; he worked from Latin translations of Aristotle. [source: IEP-Aq §1b]

## 2. Works
- SEP-Aq's five categories: encyclopedic theological treatises (ST, SCG); disputed questions (e.g. *On Truth*); short works (*opuscula*, e.g. *On Being and Essence*); commentaries on Aristotle and other philosophical texts; biblical commentaries. IEP-Aq lists nine genres, including polemical works (*On the Eternity of the World against Murmurers*) and expert-opinion letters (*On Kingship*). [sources: SEP-Aq §1.2; IEP-Aq §1b]
- Four theological syntheses: the *Sentences* commentary, the *Summa contra gentiles*, the *Compendium theologiae* and the *Summa theologiae*. [source: IEP-Aq §1b]
- ST structure: 1a (God, creation, human nature); 1a2ae (human action: passion, virtue, vice, law, grace); 2a2ae (virtues in detail); 3a (Christ and sacraments), complete only through q. 90, after which disciples compiled a "supplement". The second part is by far the longest. [sources: SEP-Aq §1.2; IEP-Aq §1b]
- ST aim, preface (Pasnau's translation): "our intent in this work is to develop those issues that concern the Christian religion in a way that suits the education of those who are just beginning." Today, Pasnau notes, "no one thinks of ST as a work for beginners". [source: SEP-Aq §1.2]
- Debate on the ST's purpose: once thought to be meant to replace Lombard's *Sentences* as a university textbook (which began to happen about 150 years after his death); recent scholarship suggests it was written for Dominican students preparing for priestly ministry. [source: IEP-Aq §1b]
- The article form: question; objections (usually against his own view); *sed contra* (an authority); body (*respondeo*); replies to objections. Based on classroom disputation, never a literal transcript. [sources: SEP-Aq §1.2; IEP-Aq §1b]
- English Dominican translation of the ST: 2,592 pages, unfinished. [source: IEP-Aq §1b]
- SCG: four books (God; creatures; providence; salvation); books I–III are natural theology, arguing from premises evident to reason, with Scripture cited only after the philosophical case. [source: IEP-Aq §1b] WP: written 1259–1265; Torrell identified the first 53 chapters of Book I as written in Paris; the tradition that Raymond of Peñafort commissioned it (1259) for Dominican missionaries to Muslims and Jews in Spain is questioned by modern scholars (no dedication; much of Book I finished by mid-1259; no specific engagement with Islamic tenets). Present as a debated tradition. [source: WP-Summa_contra_Gentiles]
- Aristotle commentaries: line-by-line paraphrase; whether paraphrase reflects his own view is disputed (Jenkins 1996; Elders 2009); the most revealing passages are where he breaks off to lecture on an issue. [source: SEP-Aq §1.2] He sometimes corrects Aristotle (e.g. *Physics* VIII commentary). [source: IEP-Aq]
- Neoplatonic commentaries: on Boethius' *De trinitate* and *De hebdomadibus*, pseudo-Dionysius' *Divine Names*, and the *Book of Causes*, which he correctly identified as derived from Proclus' *Elements of Theology* rather than Aristotle (consistent with the Neoplatonism notes: he used Moerbeke's 1268 Latin *Elements*). [sources: IEP-Aq §1b; medieval--neoplatonism.md]
- *De ente et essentia* (*On Being and Essence*): written for fellow Dominicans during the first Paris period (WP: c. 1252–56; the date is tagged "citation needed"); among his earliest works. [sources: WP-Thomas_Aquinas; WP-De_ente_et_essentia] (Note: WP-De_ente_et_essentia swaps the glosses, calling *esse* "whatness" and *essentia* "is-ness". Do not copy.)
- *De unitate intellectus* (*On the Unity of the Intellect*, against the Averroists): second Paris regency. The published *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes* chapter dates it 1270; SEP-Aq and WP place it in 1268–72 without a year. Title: SEP-Aq calls it "On the Unity of the Intellect against the Averroists", while the Ghazālī/Averroes research notes (SEP-IbnRushd) say "contra averroistas" is a later addition; it contains the first known use of *averroista*. [sources: SEP-Aq §6.2; WP-Thomas_Aquinas; medieval--ghazali-and-averroes.md]
- *De aeternitate mundi* (*On the Eternity of the World*), second Paris regency. [sources: SEP-Aq §1.1, §3; WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- Also by him: the liturgy for Corpus Christi (hymns *Pange lingua*, *Tantum ergo*, *Panis angelicus*; authorship confirmed per Torrell), the *Catena aurea*, *On Kingship*. [sources: IEP-Aq; WP-Thomas_Aquinas]

## 3. Faith and reason
- Two kinds of truths about God (SCG I.3–9): those reason can reach without revelation (that God exists, that God is one), which ST calls the "preambles of the faith", and those requiring grace, e.g. the Trinity ("articles of faith"). [source: IEP-Aq §2]
- Why reveal even the preambles? SCG gives three reasons (IEP summary): otherwise (1) few would know God (lack of ability, time or desire); (2) those few would know late in life; (3) with an admixture of error and uncertainty. [source: IEP-Aq §2] ST 1a 1.1 (Pasnau's translation): "it was necessary for the sake of human salvation that certain truths that surpass human reason be made known to us through divine revelation"; otherwise "the truth about God investigated by reason would be available only to a few people, after a long time, and with the admixture of many errors"; hence "beyond the philosophical disciplines investigated by human reason, it was necessary to have a sacred teaching through revelation". This sacred teaching (*sacra doctrina*) is theology. [source: SEP-Aq §2]
- No real conflict is possible between truths of faith and of reason; the theologian's task includes showing that apparent conflicts are not real (ST Ia q.1 a.8). [source: IEP-Aq §2] IEP contrasts his view with evidentialism, fideism and "separatism". [source: IEP-Aq §2]
- "if anything is found in the words of the philosophers that is contrary to the faith, this is not philosophy but rather an abuse of philosophy, due to a failure of reason" (Comm. on Boethius' *De trinitate* 2.3c, Pasnau's translation). [source: SEP-Aq §1.2] (Same quotation already used in the *Aristotle* notes.)
- Grace and nature, ST I q.1 a.8 ad 2: "Since therefore grace does not destroy nature but perfects it, natural reason should minister to faith as the natural bent of the will ministers to charity." [source: WP-Gratia_non_tollit_naturam,_sed_perficit; WS-11] (Medium confidence on exact wording: this is the Dominican wording as quoted by WP; not checked against a primary text.) IEP calls it a "medieval maxim". [source: IEP-Aq §2]
- Sacred doctrine as a science: Aquinas counts theology (and mathematics, music, philosophy) as a *scientia*. [source: IEP-Aq §4b] Content of ST I q.1 a.2 (WS-8): sacred doctrine is a science that takes its first principles from a higher science, the knowledge (*scientia*) of God and the blessed, as optics takes its principles from geometry. Exact Dominican wording not confirmed. That this "subalternation" reading is debated: SEP-Aq bibliography lists a 2004 paper "On the Alleged Subalternate Character of Sacra Doctrina in Aquinas". [sources: WS-8; SEP-Aq bibliography]
- Scientia in general: from Aristotle's *Posterior Analytics*: certain cognition of a thing through its cause, the result of a demonstrative syllogism from "true, primary, and immediate" premises; for Aquinas an aspirational ideal. Demonstration *propter quid* (from the cause) vs *quia* (only that a thing is so; the best available for proving God). [source: SEP-Aq §6.3]
- Non-demonstrative arguments for the Catholic faith he accepts: miracles, the transformation of fishermen into apostles, the spread of the faith despite persecution and "not ... by way of the sword" (IEP summary of SCG). Note the polemical contrast with Islam. [source: IEP-Aq §2]

## 4. ARGUMENT 1: the Five Ways, especially the Second Way
- Location: ST Ia q.2 a.3. First Way from motion (change); Second from efficient causation; Third from possibility and necessity (contingency); Fourth from degrees of perfection; Fifth from the governance of the world (final causes). [sources: SEP-Aq §2; IEP-Aq §6b; WP-Five_Ways_(Aquinas)]
- The first three are cosmological arguments "based on the impossibility of an infinite series of causes". [source: SEP-Aq §2] SEP-Cosmo: the argument from contingency was developed by Ibn Sīnā and "taken up by Thomas Aquinas" (ST I q.2 a.3; SCG I.13); WP calls the Third Way's first half a summary of Avicenna's "Proof of the Truthful", with Aquinas adding the regress step (WP-Five_Ways). [sources: SEP-Cosmo §1; WP-Five_Ways_(Aquinas)] Cross-ref *Falsafa* (Avicenna's proof).
- Each Way ends with a version of "this is what everyone calls God"; Aquinas "puts his claim cautiously" because a first mover "falls well short" of a being worthy of the name, "this first cause might itself be a body". [source: SEP-Aq §2]
- The Five Ways are abbreviated: the First Way is one paragraph in ST but 32 paragraphs in SCG I.13. [source: IEP-Aq §6b]
- The Ways do not assume a beginning of the world. SCG I.13 (Pegis's translation, via IEP): "the most efficacious way to prove that God exists is on the supposition that the world is eternal. Granted this supposition, that God exists is less manifest." [source: IEP-Aq §6b]
- Text of the Second Way, English Dominican translation as quoted by IEP: "The second way is from the nature of the efficient cause. In the world of sense we find there is an order of efficient causes. There is no case known (neither is it, indeed, possible) in which a thing is found to be the efficient cause of itself; for so it would be prior to itself, which is impossible. Now in efficient causes it is not possible to go on to infinity, because in all efficient causes following in order, the first is the cause of the intermediate cause, and the intermediate is the cause of the ultimate cause, whether the intermediate cause be several, or only one. Now to take away the cause is to take away the effect. Therefore, if there be no first cause among efficient causes, there will be no ultimate, nor any intermediate cause. But if in efficient causes it is possible to go on to infinity, there will be no first efficient cause, neither will there be an ultimate effect, nor any intermediate efficient causes; all of which is plainly false. Therefore it is necessary to admit a first efficient cause, to which everyone gives the name of God." [source: IEP-Aq §6b]
- IEP's step-by-step reconstruction (Brown), simplified for the writer:
  1. In the sensible world there is an order of efficient causes: some effect E exists at time t through causes that are themselves caused at t (IEP's example: an animal kept alive now by the warmth of the atmosphere, which is kept warm now by the sun).
  2. Any such order is either infinite, or contains something that causes itself, or has an absolutely first, uncaused cause.
  3. Nothing causes itself (it would be prior to itself).
  4. So the order is either infinite or has a first cause.
  5. In an ordered series the first causes the intermediate, which causes the last.
  6. To take away the cause is to take away the effect.
  7. So if there were no first cause, there would be no intermediate causes and no effect E.
  8. An infinite series has no first cause.
  9. So if the series were infinite, there would be no E. But E exists.
  10. So the series is not infinite; there is a first efficient cause (by disjunctive syllogism).
  11. A first uncaused efficient cause is "what everyone calls God". [source: IEP-Aq §6b]
- Key interpretive point: premise 6 is false for causal series stretched back in time (a sculpture outlives its sculptor; Rex the dog outlives his parents). So the argument must be about concurrent causes, "a singer's song exists only as long as the singer sings". [source: IEP-Aq §6b] SEP-Aq: "Commentators generally agree that what Aquinas means to exclude is an infinite series of simultaneous causes, a 'vertical' series as opposed to an infinite 'horizontal' series of causes going back in time, which he does not think can be proved to be impossible." [source: SEP-Aq §2]
- Per se (essentially ordered) vs per accidens (accidentally ordered) series: in an essentially ordered series each member has its causal power only from the prior member's present activity (Aquinas's stick moved by the hand moved by the arm, which moves the stone); such a series "is either infinite or terminates in a first, uncaused cause". Caleb Cohoe (2013): Aquinas's causality is "closer to contemporary theories of ontological dependence ... primarily a vertical relation, not a horizontal one". The phrase "essentially ordered" comes from Duns Scotus (WP). [sources: SEP-Cosmo §§2, 4; WP-Five_Ways_(Aquinas)]
- Objection (Oberle 2022): showing that removing the first cause of a finite essentially ordered series removes its causal power does not show that an infinite such series is impossible; Ross Cameron (2022) replies that without a first member the explanation is "never fulfilled", a vicious regress; Oberle (2024) answers that there is no vicious regress if the series is taken distributively. SEP-Cosmo: "the debate hinges in part on the distinction between per se and per accidens series ... and whether invoking this distinction begs the question". [source: SEP-Cosmo §4]
- Objection, Hume-style: Hume attacked the view of causation the argument presupposes and the Causal Principle (every contingent being has a cause) as non-evidential, since we can conceive an event without a cause (*Enquiry* 1748); also the "Hume-Edwards principle": explaining each part explains the whole (*Dialogues* 1779, part 9). Russell (1948 BBC debate with Copleston): the universe is "just there, and that's all", and inferring its contingency from its parts' contingency commits the fallacy of composition. [source: SEP-Cosmo §§1, 5.2–5.4]
- Objection, Kant: the cosmological argument covertly relies on the ontological argument, since only the concept of a "most real being" can fix what the necessary being is (*Critique of Pure Reason*, B632–35). Reply: the first part already establishes existence, and the argument needs only factual, not logical, necessity (Smart 1955 noted the first point). [source: SEP-Cosmo §5.5]
- Objection, "why one God / why God?" (the "gap" problem): SEP-Aq notes the conclusion falls short of God; IEP: the Five Ways are "simply five ways of beginning", with ST qq. 3–26 going on to argue that the first cause is simple, perfect, good, infinite, immutable, eternal, one (q.11), knowing, willing, loving. SEP-Cosmo: the identification of the first cause with God "seems to go beyond the causal reasoning that informs his argument". [sources: SEP-Aq §2; IEP-Aq §6b; SEP-Cosmo concluding section]
- Kenny: Anthony Kenny, *The Five Ways: St. Thomas Aquinas's Proofs of God's Existence* (London: Routledge, 1969). WS-3: Kenny argued none of the Ways is wholly valid; he attacked the First Way's dependence on Aristotelian physics (the principle that whatever is in motion is moved by another) and found the Third Way "vitiated by an elementary logical fallacy" and by incredible views about perishing; the book is credited with lasting influence on the consensus about the Ways' cogency; Oderberg and others defend them. [sources: SEP-Aq bibliography; SEP-Cosmo; WS-3] (Medium confidence: from search summaries of reviews, not Kenny's text.)
- Suárez rejected the First Way's principle "whatever is moved is moved by another" (*omne quod movetur ab alio movetur*), citing self-moving animals, and reformulated it as "whatever is made is made by another". [source: WP-Five_Ways_(Aquinas)]
- The Third Way, key sentence. SEP-Cosmo: "if everything can not-be, then at one time there was nothing in existence" (ST I q.2 a.3). The Dominican wording, seen only in search snippets (WS-1): "The third way is taken from possibility and necessity ... Therefore, if everything is possible not to be, then at one time there could have been nothing in existence. Now if this were true, even now there would be nothing in existence, because that which does not exist only begins to exist by something already existing." The Way then argues that some necessary beings have their necessity from another, which cannot go to infinity, so there is a being necessary of itself. Kenny: for Aquinas "necessary" here means unable to cease to exist (Kenny 1969: 48). [sources: SEP-Cosmo §5.2; WS-1; WP-Five_Ways_(Aquinas)]
- Objection to the Third Way: the inference from "each thing can fail to exist" to "at some time nothing existed" is a quantifier-shift fallacy / fallacy of composition (Plantinga 1967; Kenny 1969: 56–66; Rowe 1975 on the parallel in Samuel Clarke: every possible world could contain some contingent being). Reply (Haldane 1996): the argument is fallacious only on a temporal reading; Aquinas means an atemporal per se ordering. [sources: SEP-Cosmo §5.2; WP-Five_Ways_(Aquinas)] Rundle: matter/energy could be the necessary being. [source: SEP-Cosmo]
- The Fifth Way is not Paley's design argument: it starts from any regular end-directed behaviour of non-intelligent things, not from complexity. [source: WP-Five_Ways_(Aquinas)]
- Other parallels: Udayana's *Nyāyakusumāñjali* I.4 contains an abbreviated cosmological argument from dependence (global cross-ref for *God: Arguments For and Against*). [source: SEP-Cosmo §1]

## 5. Rejection of Anselm's argument
- Aquinas denies that Anselm's ontological argument has force as a proof (ST 1a 2.1 ad 2; SCG I.10–11). [source: SEP-Aq §2]
- ST Ia q.2 a.1 (English Dominican translation per IEP-OA's bibliography): "not everyone who hears this word 'God' understands it to signify something than which nothing greater can be thought, seeing that some have believed God to be a body"; and granting the concept, "it does not therefore follow that he understands what the word signifies exists actually, but only that it exists mentally." [source: IEP-OA §2c]
- Background distinction: God's existence is self-evident in itself but not to us; it can be demonstrated *quia* from effects, not *propter quid*. [source: WP-Five_Ways_(Aquinas); SEP-Aq §6.3] (IEP-OA's wording "Aquinas believed that God's existence is self-evident" is imprecise; do not copy.)
- Overlap: the *Early Scholasticism* notes (§5) already record this criticism. Keep it short here and cross-reference.

## 6. Eternity of the world
- Aristotle and the Arabic tradition held the world had always existed; many of Aquinas's contemporaries thought they could prove it had a beginning; the debate "was one of the most acrimonious issues in the early medieval university" (Dales 1990). In *On the Eternity of the World* Aquinas concluded "that no demonstrative argument is possible either way"; the beginning of the world is held by faith (ST Ia q.46 a.2). [sources: SEP-Aq §3; IEP-Aq §6b]
- Marenbon: Aquinas "rejects rather contemptuously the Philoponian arguments used by some of his contemporaries"; he argues it could have been that the world always existed and yet "is caused by God according to everything that is in it", while calling the view that something other than God could exist without being made by him an "abominable error". Marenbon notes that, as al-Ghazālī pointed out, it is unclear how an eternal world leaves room for God to make it by choosing it. [source: SEP-Med §3.1]
- Parallels: Maimonides likewise held neither creation nor eternity demonstrable (Jewish Philosophy notes; Aristotle notes). Bonaventure took the kalām-style argument from the impossibility of an infinite past into the Christian tradition. [sources: SEP-Cosmo §1; medieval--jewish-philosophy.md]
- Creation *ex nihilo* is God's alone; creatures need ongoing conservation: "a thing's existence cannot remain after the cessation of the action of the agent causing the effect" (ST 1a 104.1c, Pasnau). There is no best possible world (ST 1a 25.6). God's eternity is "being present to every time or instant of time" (SCG I.66.8). [source: SEP-Aq §3]

## 7. Essence and existence (*esse*); God as subsistent being
- In creatures essence and existence are distinct; God alone is not so composed; even angels are composed of essence and existence (Wippel 2000). [sources: IEP-Meta §2; SEP-Aq §4]
- Context in *De ente et essentia* ch. 4: against "universal hylomorphism" (that even angels have a kind of matter), which Aquinas attributes to Avicebron (Ibn Gabirol) and which Bonaventure held; Aquinas explains angels' potentiality instead by an essence in potency to a distinct act of existence. [source: IEP-Meta §2] (Cross-ref *Jewish Philosophy*: Ibn Gabirol.)
- The three stages of the *De ente* argument (IEP-Meta):
  1. The *intellectus essentiae* argument: whatever is not part of the understanding of an essence comes to it from outside; we can understand what a man or a phoenix is without knowing whether it exists; so essence and existence differ.
  2. If there were a being whose essence is its existence, there could be only one (it could not be multiplied by an added difference or by reception in matter); in everything else essence and existence differ.
  3. Whatever has existence distinct from essence must be caused to be by another (nothing causes its own existence); to avoid an infinite regress there must be a cause that is pure existence (*esse tantum*).
  Then: in creatures essence stands to existence as potency to act. [source: IEP-Meta §2]
- DEBATE over where (if anywhere) the *real* distinction is proved: stage 1 gives only a logical/conceptual distinction (Joseph Owens, John Wippel); stage 1 gives a real distinction (Walter Patt, Anthony Kenny, Steven Long, per IEP); Wippel: stage 2 is decisive; Owens: only stage 3, once a being whose essence is existence is proved, and Owens charges Wippel with an "ontological move". [source: IEP-Meta §2]
- Participation: essence/existence composites "participate" in existence, and each essence limits its act of existence. Fabro and Geiger brought out the Platonic sources of this. [source: IEP-Meta §3]
- Debt to Avicenna: Avicenna's "conceptual test" (we can know what a triangle is without knowing whether it exists) and his view that only God's essence is identical with existence; "Aquinas adopts it in *De ente et essentia* IV" (Falsafa notes, SEP-Infl/IEP-Avic). The Falsafa notes record a debate over whether Avicenna himself held a real distinction (SEP-Ess). [source: medieval--falsafa.md §8] Burrell (1986) argues the God–world distinction is best expressed through essence and existence, tracing Aquinas's use of Ibn Sīnā and Maimonides. [source: WS-10]
- God as *ipsum esse subsistens* (subsistent being itself) and *actus purus* (pure act): the phrase is quoted in SEP-DS (Dolezal 2011: "The consideration of God as ipsum esse subsistens and actus purus is crucial for any confession of God's absolute existence"). Divine simplicity: God is not composed even of essence and existence; "Deus est sua bonitas", "God is his goodness". [sources: SEP-DS; SEP-Aq §4; IEP-Aq §6c]
- Prime matter as "pure potentiality": "being in actuality is incompatible with matter's nature" (*Quodlibet* III.1.1, Pasnau's translation); "hardly anyone endorsed" this among later Aristotelians. [source: SEP-Aq §4]
- Matter individuates forms; "no commonness is found in Socrates" (*On Being and Essence*). [source: SEP-Aq §4]

## 8. Analogy and the limits of God-talk (cross-ref *Scotus, Ockham*)
- ST 1a q.3 prologue (Pasnau's translation): "because it is not possible for us to know what God is, but rather what God is not, we cannot consider how God exists, but rather how God does not exist." [source: SEP-Aq §2]
- Names said of God and creatures are neither univocal nor purely equivocal but analogical (ST 1a 13.5). ST 1a 13.2 (English Dominican translation, credited by IEP): "So when we say, God is good, the meaning is not God is the cause of goodness, or, God is not evil, but the meaning is, Whatever good we attribute to creatures, pre-exists in God, and in a more excellent and higher way." He rejects the purely negative reading he attributes to Maimonides ("God is good" = "God is not evil") and the purely causal reading. [sources: IEP-Aq §6d; SEP-Aq §2]
- Three ways to God: causation, negation (remotion), excellence (eminence). [source: IEP-Aq §6a]
- Aristotle's example: "healthy" said primarily of an animal and derivatively of urine (a sign) and exercise (a cause). [source: IEP-Aq §3]
- Ashworth/D'Ettore: "Despite the vast modern literature devoted to Aquinas's theory of analogy, he has very little to say about analogy as such." DEBATE: whether he moved from analogy of proportionality (*De veritate*) back to analogy of attribution grounded in causal likeness (SCG, ST), as Montagnes argued, or never abandoned *De veritate*'s doctrine (Hochschild). [source: SEP-Anal §6]
- Scotus: "being" is univocal, said in a single concept of God and creatures; he dropped the requirement that univocal terms be categorial and argued that without a unified concept of being neither metaphysics nor theology is possible. Cajetan (late fifteenth century) gave the most famous Thomist account of analogy. [sources: SEP-Anal §§7–8] SEP-Aq: Aquinas's "denial of the univocity of being was contentious among later medieval authors (most notably John Duns Scotus)". [source: SEP-Aq §4]

## 9. ARGUMENT 2: the soul, the intellect, and personal survival
- The soul is the substantial form of a living body, the "first principle of life" (following Aristotle's *De anima*); plants and animals have souls but are wholly material. [source: SEP-Aq §5]
- One substantial form ("unitarian") thesis. Questions on the Soul 11c (Pasnau's translation): "if Socrates were an animal and were rational in virtue of different forms, then these two, in order to be united simpliciter, would need something to make them one. Therefore, since nothing is available to do this, the result will be that a human being is one thing only as an aggregate, like a heap." (Also ST 1a 76.3–4.) Pluralists posited a "form of corporeity" (*forma corporeitatis*) plus the soul; their main argument was that it explains how a body persists after death; Aquinas held that only prime matter strictly persists through death. [source: SEP-Aq §5]
- Consequence: a corpse is "human" only equivocally; flesh and bone of a dead human are no longer flesh and bone except equivocally. [sources: IEP-Meta §4; WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- The intellect's immateriality. ST 1a 75.5c (Pasnau's translation): "If the intellective soul were composed of form and matter, then the forms of things would be received in it as individuals; then it would cognize only singular things, as happens in the sensory capacities, which receive the forms of things in a corporeal organ." Steps: (1) the intellect grasps universals (the concept of cat as such); (2) whatever receives forms in matter receives them as individual; (3) so the intellect does not operate through a bodily organ; (4) a thing acts as it is, so the human soul is "an incorporeal and subsistent principle" (ST 1a 75.2c); (5) a subsistent non-body has no natural way of ceasing to exist, so the soul is incorruptible (ST 1a 75.6). [source: SEP-Aq §§5, 6.2; WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- Objection (Pomponazzi, *On the Immortality of the Soul*, 1516): even on Aristotle's account intellectual thought cannot occur without mental images, which need a bodily organ, the brain, so the intellect depends on the body for a necessary condition of thinking. Pomponazzi nonetheless called Aquinas's argument for immortality "the best that can be made" (Marenbon's paraphrase). [source: SEP-Med §3.3]
- Against Averroes' single intellect: each human has their own agent and possible intellect. A shared agent intellect is "wrong but not incredible" (affinity with Augustinian illumination), but a separate possible intellect is "preposterous": "if I think about stone and you do likewise, then it would be necessary that you and I have one and the same intellectual operation" (*De unitate intellectus* 4.101–3, Pasnau's translation). Marenbon: Averroes' theory "makes humans into the objects of thought rather than thinkers"; Aquinas also rejected Avicenna's "dualist reading". [sources: SEP-Aq §6.2; SEP-Med §3.3] IEP: the single intellect was incompatible with personal immortality and moral responsibility "not to mention our experience of ourselves as individual knowers". [source: IEP-Aq §1a]
- Is he a dualist? Hard to call his general theory dualist, but in the human case, if a dualist holds that humans have a material body and an immaterial mind, "Aquinas clearly qualifies", though he insists on one substance (Bazán; Klima; Stump). [source: SEP-Aq §5]
- The separated soul: after death the soul exists without the body (ST 1a 89) until the resurrection (SCG IV.79–97). "Abraham's soul's having life would not suffice for Abraham's being alive.... The life of the whole compound is required, soul and body" (*Sentences* IV.43.1.1.1 ad 2, Pasnau's translation). DEBATE: "corruptionists" (Toner 2009; Van Dyke 2012; Nevitt 2014) read him as holding that the person does not survive as a separated soul; "survivalists" (Brown 2007; Brower 2014; Stump 2022) find that incredible and in tension with other texts and Christian doctrine. [source: SEP-Aq §5]
- "My soul is not I": Commentary on 1 Corinthians 15 (lect. 2 in some numberings; WS gives "XV, 1, 11"): *anima mea non est ego*. The soul is part of the human being, not the whole; used in the corruptionism debate (Toner; John O'Callaghan's 2019 paper "Anima Mea Non Est Ego"). [source: WS-6] (Medium confidence on the exact lecture number; the Latin phrase is well attested in the search results.)
- Cognition: the possible intellect starts as a blank tablet ("like a tablet on which nothing has been written", ST 1a 79.2c); the agent intellect abstracts universal "species" from sense images (phantasms); "the source of our cognition comes from the senses" (ST 1a 84.6 sc). Four internal senses following Avicenna: common sense, imagination, estimative/cogitative power, memory. The intellect does not directly grasp particulars (ST 1a 86.1). [source: SEP-Aq §§6.1–6.2]
- Will: "the will and free choice are not two powers, but one" (ST 1a 83.4c); DEBATE: compatibilist reading (Hause; Kenny 1993; Loughran; Pasnau 2002; Shanley) vs libertarian readings (Stump 2003; Hoffmann and Michon 2017). [source: SEP-Aq §7]

## 10. Ethics: happiness, natural law, virtue
- Teleology: "it is necessary that all agents act for the sake of an end" (ST 1a2ae 1.2c). After rejecting riches, honour, pleasure etc., "final and complete happiness can consist in nothing other than the vision of the divine essence" (ST 1a2ae 3.8c, Pasnau's translation). Imperfect happiness in this life consists in acting through the virtues (ST 1a2ae 5.5–6). [source: SEP-Aq §8.1]
- Two objections Pasnau records: the ultimate end should be love rather than intellectual contemplation; eudaimonism rests ethics on self-concern. Pasnau's reply: "happiness" just names the activity ultimately best for us, understanding God and creation. [source: SEP-Aq §8.1]
- Four laws (Treatise on Law, ST 1a2ae qq. 90–108): eternal law (God's plan of governance, q.93); natural law (rational creatures' participation in the eternal law, q.94); human law (qq.95–97); divine law (revealed, qq.98–108: Old Law and New Law). [source: SEP-Aq §8.2; SEP-NL §1.1] Law defined (ST 1a2ae 90.4) as a rule put in place by one who has care of the community. [source: SEP-NL]
- First precept (ST 1a2ae 94.2): Pasnau: "The good should be done and pursued, and the bad should be avoided." Finnis: "Good is to be done and pursued, and bad avoided"; Finnis (following Grisez 1965) warns that truncations like "Do good and avoid evil" are "exegetically and philosophically unsound": it is not an imperative nor a moral principle but rationally directive. Self-evident (*per se notum*) and undeduced, grasped by the power of *synderesis*. [sources: SEP-Aq §8.2; SEP-AqMP §2.3]
- "the order of the precepts of natural law accords with the order of natural inclinations" (ST 1a2ae 94.2c). Inclinations/basic goods: life; "marriage between man and woman and bringing up of children" (*coniunctio maris et feminae et educatio liberorum*); knowledge (including of God); living in society; practical reasonableness; Finnis stresses the lists are "explicitly open-ended". [sources: SEP-Aq §8.2; SEP-AqMP §2.5]
- The natural law is universally binding and knowable by nature (ST 1a2ae 94.4, 94.6), though its implications can be hard to work out and passion or bad habits can obscure it. Murphy: "If any moral theory is a theory of natural law, it is Aquinas's"; for Aquinas "the good is prior to the right". [source: SEP-NL §1]
- On Ten Commandments proem (Pasnau's translation): "the law of nature is nothing other than the light of intellect, placed within us by God, through which we grasp what is to be done and what is to be avoided". [source: SEP-Aq §8.2]
- DEBATE (is/ought): critics say natural law derives ought from is and makes false claims about universal inclinations (Massey 1999). Finnis, Grisez, Rhonheimer, Boyle ("new natural law"): Aquinas never deduces oughts from facts about nature; first practical principles are undeduced; explanatory priority goes to the basic goods. Others (e.g. McInerny; Lisska 1996) give primacy to natural inclinations/human nature. Pasnau defends the framework and notes it can be updated on gender and sexuality (Oliva 2015). [sources: SEP-AqMP §§2.4–2.6; SEP-Aq §8.2]
- Human law derives from natural law as conclusions from principles or as "determinations" (IEP's analogy with sciences, ST 1a2ae q.91 a.3, q.95). [source: IEP-Aq §9a]
- Virtues: dispositions (*habitus*) acquired by repeated acts; a true virtue cannot be the source of a bad act (*Questions on the Virtues in General* 2c). Four cardinal virtues: prudence (ST 2a2ae 47–51), justice ("to give to each ... what is due", q.58), courage (q.123), temperance (q.141); prudence underlies all, and the virtues are unified (ST 1a2ae 65.1). Three theological virtues, faith, hope, charity (1 Cor 13), infused by God; even infused cardinal virtues; grace is never merited. [source: SEP-Aq §8.3]
- About half of ST (the second part) is ethics; roughly a quarter (2a2ae) on virtues. [source: SEP-Aq §§8, 8.3]

## 11. Double effect (ST II-II q.64 a.7)
- SEP-DE: "Thomas Aquinas is credited with introducing the principle of double effect in his discussion of the permissibility of self-defense". Augustine had held private self-defence impermissible ("can only proceed from some degree of inordinate self-love"). [source: SEP-DE §1]
- Text as quoted by SEP-DE (English Dominican wording; translator not named by SEP): "Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention. … Accordingly, the act of self-defense may have two effects: one, the saving of one's life; the other, the slaying of the aggressor." "Therefore, this act, since one's intention is to save one's own life, is not unlawful, seeing that it is natural to everything to keep itself in being as far as possible." "And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful if it be out of proportion to the end. Wherefore, if a man in self-defense uses more than necessary violence, it will be unlawful, whereas, if he repel force with moderation, his defense will be lawful." [source: SEP-DE §1]
- Finnis: q.64 a.7 "is the locus classicus for what later became known, unhappily, as the 'principle' of double effect"; its core is that moral norms bear differently on what one intends and on side effects foreseen *praeter intentionem* ("outside the intention"). Public authority may intend to kill in war or punishment; private persons may not. [source: SEP-AqMP §3.4]
- Later four-condition formulation (Mangan 1949): act good or indifferent in itself; only the good effect intended; the good not produced by means of the bad; proportionately grave reason. [source: SEP-DE §1]
- DEBATE on origins (WS-4): Mangan, "An Historical Analysis of the Principle of Double Effect", *Theological Studies* 10 (1949): it "is not entirely clear that St. Thomas himself enunciates this principle"; no definite formulation before Aquinas. The classic modern formulation is usually credited to the Jesuit Jean-Pierre Gury (*Compendium*, 1850); Julia Fleming argues Gury relied far more on later theologians than on Aquinas. Present as "credited to Aquinas; whether he stated the principle as later formulated is disputed". [sources: SEP-DE; SEP-AqMP; WS-4]
- Anscombe's *Intention* and her work made Aquinas's double effect and theory of action influential in analytic philosophy (WP; flagged "citation needed"). [source: WP-Thomas_Aquinas]

## 12. Just war (brief)
- ST II-II q.40 a.1: war is not always sinful if three conditions hold: authority of the sovereign (legitimate public authority), just cause (those attacked deserve it "on account of some fault"), and right intention (to promote good or avoid evil; peace). [source: WS-9] (WP's version of the third condition, "peace must be a central motive", is loose.) Finnis: Aquinas admits defence and also just punishment and compensation as grounds; only public authority may wage war; feigned retreat can be right but lying to the enemy is wrong. [source: SEP-AqMP]
- Moseley: the "most systematic exposition in the Western tradition" up to then, building on Augustine; developed by Vitoria, Suárez, Grotius and others. [source: IEP-JW]

## 13. Women (handle accurately)
- ST I q.92 a.1 ad 1 (the "misbegotten" passage): as regards the individual nature, woman is "defective and misbegotten" (Latin *deficiens et occasionatus*), because the male seed's active power tends to produce a perfect likeness in the masculine sex; as regards human nature in general, woman is not misbegotten but is included in nature's intention as directed to the work of generation. Restated in ST I q.99 a.2 ad 1 ("misbegotten male"). The idea derives from Aristotle's biology (the female as a kind of deformed male; *Generation of Animals*, cf. the *Aristotle* notes: "overweighted the male contribution to reproduction"). *Occasionatus* means roughly "produced by an accidental cause", so "misbegotten" is a loose rendering (M. Nolan, "The Defective Male: What Aquinas Really Said", *New Blackfriars*). [sources: WS-5; ancient--aristotle.md] Confidence: substance high, exact Dominican wording medium (snippets only). WRITER: give the two-sided statement, not the single phrase.
- Christine de Pizan (1364–c. 1430), a philosopher writing in French, took Boethius "but also Aquinas as points of reference", "informed by a strong consciousness both of the restrictions placed on women and of their capabilities". Women were excluded from the medieval universities. [source: SEP-Med §2.9]
- Russell mocked Aquinas's case for the indissolubility of marriage: the father is useful in education "(a) because he is more rational than the mother, (b) because, being stronger, he is better able to inflict physical punishment." [source: WP-Thomas_Aquinas, quoting Russell's *History of Western Philosophy*; not checked in Russell]

## 14. The condemnations of 1277, canonization, 1325
- 7 March 1277: Bishop Stephen (Étienne) Tempier of Paris prohibited 219 philosophical and theological theses "discussed and disputed in the faculty of arts"; also condemned Andreas Capellanus' *De amore* and treatises on geomancy, necromancy and fortune-telling. Mandonnet's numbering: 179 philosophical, 40 theological. Themes include eternity of the world (80–89), unicity of intellect (117–133), free will (151–166). [source: SEP-1277 §§1, 3]
- Pope John XXI's letter of 18 January 1277 asked Tempier to investigate; the papal letter *Flumen aquae vivae* (28 April 1277) shows no awareness of the condemnation; Tempier may have been preparing it already. Sixteen masters of theology, including Henry of Ghent, assisted. [source: SEP-1277 §§1–2]
- Date: "three years, to the day, after his death" (Pasnau). [source: SEP-Aq §9]
- Did it touch Aquinas? Siger of Brabant and Boethius of Dacia are usually named as the main targets. Hissette (1977) found few propositions traceable to Aquinas; Wippel argued (against Hissette) that Aquinas's teaching was implied and some theses were taken from his writings. Pasnau: "a significant number ... seem to implicate Aquinas's teachings (Hissette 1977; Wippel 1995b)". WP-Thomas_Aquinas (citing Küng) says "twenty Thomistic propositions"; this precise count is not supported by SEP. [sources: SEP-1277 §4; SEP-Aq §9; WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- A separate process against Aquinas: begun after Giles of Rome's censure (51 propositions, before 28 March 1277), still before 28 March; according to Robert Wielockx it was halted on curial orders during the papal vacancy (20 May – 25 November 1277); "generally accepted", but questioned by Wippel and Thijssen. Pasnau (following Torrell): the authorities probably planned to censure his unitarian theory of substantial form and his prime matter as pure potentiality "until higher authorities cut this process short". [sources: SEP-1277 §6; SEP-Aq §9]
- Oxford: on 18 March 1277 Archbishop Robert Kilwardby (himself a Dominican) issued the Oxford Prohibitions of 30 articles on grammar, logic and natural philosophy, as primarily philosophical errors ("prohibitory rather than condemnatory"). Some articles were taken at the time as aimed at Aquinas's unity of substantial form (Peter of Conflans wrote to Kilwardby; Kilwardby's reply is perhaps the first explicit defence of the plurality of forms). Pasnau says the list "explicitly includes" unitarian form and pure-potency prime matter; WP-Kilwardby notes that Hissette challenged the anti-Aquinas reading. The Dominican General Chapter (Milan, 1278) sent a commission to punish those who disparaged Aquinas's writings. [sources: SEP-Kil; SEP-Aq §9; WP-Robert_Kilwardby]
- *Correctorium fratris Thomae* by the Franciscan William de la Mare; Dominicans replied with *Correctoria corruptorii*. [source: SEP-Aq §9]
- Canonization: 18 July 1323, by Pope John XXII at Avignon. [sources: SEP-Aq §9; WP-Thomas_Aquinas] (WP-Thomism wrongly says "canonization in 1325"; do not copy.)
- 1325: Bishop Stephen (Étienne) Bourret of Paris declared the 1277 condemnation not to apply to Aquinas's teaching "though the bishop made clear that this should not be taken as an endorsement" (SEP-Aq: "Two years after his canonization"). Date: 14 February 1325 (Maier; a Cambridge article), with one source giving 19 February. WP: partially annulled "insofar as the teachings of Thomas Aquinas would seem to be implied" (Grant 1974). [sources: SEP-Aq §9; WP-Condemnations_of_1210–1277; WP-Étienne_de_Bouret; WS-7]
- Effects of 1277 are debated (Duhem's "birth of modern science" thesis now rejected; Grant closest to it); after 1277 nondeterministic views of the will came to dominate scholasticism. [sources: SEP-1277 §5; SEP-Aq §7] Covered more fully in the *Aristotle* and *Early Scholasticism* notes.
- Earlier, 10 December 1270: Tempier condemned 13 propositions (one intellect for all, eternity of the world, etc.). [source: WP-Condemnations_of_1210–1277]

## 15. Later influence (cross-references)
- Dominicans were "statutorily required to promote his teaching". [source: SEP-Aq §9]
- John Capreolus (early fifteenth century) championed Thomism as a system; Cajetan (Thomas de Vio, 1469–1534) a century later. In Rome Cajetan "wrote the most influential commentary on the Summa theologiae". Cajetan met Luther (WP, image caption and section). [sources: SEP-Aq §9; SEP-Sal; WP-Thomas_Cajetan]
- Translations of Aquinas into Greek by Byzantine Latinophile scholars (thirteenth to fifteenth centuries) and into Hebrew (fourteenth to fifteenth centuries). [source: SEP-Med §1.3 (subsections "Latin to Greek", "Latin to Hebrew")]
- School of Salamanca: Francisco de Vitoria (1486–1546), who began teaching at Salamanca in 1524 and supplemented Lombard's *Sentences* with the ST as textbook; with Domingo de Soto and Melchor Cano applied Thomism to the evangelization of the indigenous peoples of the Americas and the legality of slavery; based themselves on Aquinas but "felt free to depart" from him. [source: SEP-Sal]
- 1567: Pope Pius V named him a Doctor of the Church (WP: 15 April 1567, bull *Mirabilis Deus*). Jesuits including Suárez were enjoined to follow especially Aquinas. [sources: SEP-Aq §9; IEP-Aq; WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- Critics: Luther ("soon no Thomist, Albertist, Scotist, or Ockhamist will be left in the world"); Hobbes, scorn for scholastics who "converse in questions of matters incomprehensible" (*Leviathan* viii.27). [source: SEP-Aq §9]
- 4 August 1879: Leo XIII's encyclical *Aeterni Patris* called for a revival of Aquinas, holding him up as the model Christian philosopher; one result was the Leonine critical edition; also a picture of later philosophy as "decline and decay". [sources: SEP-Aq §9; IEP-Aq; WP-Aeterni_Patris] 1914 *Postquam sanctissimus*: 24 Thomistic theses. [source: WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- Twentieth century: "Thomisms of all kinds": Maritain, Gilson, Anscombe, MacIntyre. [source: SEP-Aq §9] Analytical Thomism: term coined by John Haldane in the early 1990s; forerunners Anscombe, Geach, Kenny. [source: WP-Analytical_Thomism] Finnis (natural law in jurisprudence), Foot and MacIntyre (virtue ethics). [sources: WP-Analytical_Thomism; WP-Thomas_Aquinas]
- Pasnau's verdict: Aquinas today looks "not so much like the culmination of an era, but rather like the brilliant beginning of Europe's philosophical renaissance". [source: SEP-Aq §9]
- Busa's *Index Thomisticus* (computer concordance of Aquinas, planned 1946) is already covered by the published History chapter on digital history; possible cross-reference. [source: supabase/content/history--thematic-history--digital-history.sql]

## Debates
- Aquinas's synthesis: "brilliant synthesis" vs distortion of Aristotle (already in the *Aristotle* notes, SEP-Ar §14); Aquinas is as much Neoplatonic (Augustine, pseudo-Dionysius, Proclus, *Book of Causes*) as Aristotelian (IEP-Aq §1b; Fabro, Geiger on participation, IEP-Meta).
- Five Ways: per se vs per accidens regress and whether the distinction begs the question (SEP-Cosmo §4); temporal vs atemporal reading of the Third Way (Kenny, Plantinga, Rowe vs Haldane) (SEP-Cosmo §5.2); whether the conclusion reaches God (SEP-Aq; SEP-Cosmo).
- Real distinction of essence and existence: at which stage of *De ente* 4 it is proved (Owens vs Wippel vs Patt/Kenny/Long) (IEP-Meta §2); how far it is Avicenna's (Falsafa notes).
- Analogy: attribution vs proportionality (Montagnes vs Hochschild); Scotus's univocity (SEP-Anal).
- Corruptionism vs survivalism on the separated soul (SEP-Aq §5).
- Compatibilist vs libertarian free will (SEP-Aq §7).
- Natural law: inclination-based vs "new natural law" (Grisez, Finnis) readings; is/ought (SEP-AqMP; SEP-NL; SEP-Aq).
- Double effect: whether Aquinas formulated it (Mangan 1949; Fleming on Gury) (SEP-DE; WS-4).
- 1277: whether Aquinas was a target (Hissette vs Wippel); whether a separate process against him existed (Wielockx vs Wippel, Thijssen); Kilwardby's targets (Pasnau vs Hissette) (SEP-1277; SEP-Aq; WP-Robert_Kilwardby).
- ST's intended audience (university textbook vs Dominican pastoral training) (IEP-Aq §1b); SCG's purpose (missionaries to Muslims and Jews vs general apologetics) (WP-Summa_contra_Gentiles).
