## Framing: what "Latin American philosophy" covers
- SEP-LAP definition: philosophy produced in Latin America or by persons of Latin American ancestry outside it; usually excludes non-Iberian former colonies (occasional exception: French Caribbean). Alternative labels: Spanish American, Hispanic American, Iberoamerican, Latino/a philosophy. [source: SEP-LAP intro]
- It is "usually taken to have originated around 1550, when Spanish conquerors founded the first schools in Latin America"; recent historians try to include pre-Columbian thought, "although the pre-Columbian texts cited are often fragmentary and religious in tone". [source: SEP-LAP intro]
- SEP-LAP's five periods: Pre-Columbian (Amerindian religious cosmologies), Colonial (scholasticism), Independentist (Early Modern/Enlightenment), Nationalist (positivism), Contemporary (20th century on). [source: SEP-LAP §1]
- SEP-LAP: Latin American philosophy "has been both original and derivative"; comparatively more concerned with social issues than US philosophy; little read outside the region, partly for lack of English translations; but "the philosophy of liberation has had some impact both in North America and in developing countries in Africa". [source: SEP-LAP intro]
- IEP-LA: the term "Latin America" entered usage only in the 1850s, so applying it earlier is anachronistic; cultures named as having sophisticated systems of thought: Aztecs, Mayas, Incas, Tupi-Guarani. [source: IEP-LA intro]
- The question "is there a Latin American philosophy?" is "perhaps the most discussed subject matter within Latin American philosophy". [source: SEP-LAP §3.4]

## 1. Pre-Columbian thought (brief, with caution about the label)
- Whether it counts as philosophy "is a disputed matter" (SEP-LAP cites Nuccetelli 2001 ch. 3; Mignolo 2003). Conventional view: pre-Columbian reflection had "little to no impact" on the philosophy that followed the Conquest. [source: SEP-LAP §1]
- Hurtado: the first historians of Mexican philosophy did not count native cosmology as philosophy. Critics say calling it philosophy "retroactively attribute[s]" self-consciousness, rational reflection and critique; supporters say the Western concept must be decentred. [source: SEP-MX §2]
- Most texts and artefacts were "systematically burned or otherwise destroyed by European missionaries who considered them idolatrous". [source: IEP-LA §1]
- Sources for Nahua thought include codices (Mendoza, Borgia, Borbonicus), conquistador reports, missionary ethnographies, above all Bernardino de Sahagún's *Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España*; Sahagún (1499–1590) reported "philosophy and philosophers" among the Aztecs. [source: IEP-AZ §1b; IEP-LA §1; SEP-MX §2]
- Miguel León-Portilla (22 February 1926 – 1 October 2019), Mexican historian. Doctoral dissertation *La filosofía náhuatl estudiada en sus fuentes* (WP: 1956; SEP-MX: 1955 — see discrepancies). He argued the Mexica "in fact did philosophy", comparing them to the Greeks. [source: WP-Miguel_León-Portilla; SEP-MX §2] English: *Aztec Thought and Culture: A Study of the Ancient Nahuatl Mind*, tr. Jack Emory Davis, University of Oklahoma Press, Norman, 1963 (first English edition). [source: WS-7 (WorldCat, bookseller listing); IEP-AZ cites "Leon-Portilla 1963"] Also *Visión de los vencidos* (1959; English *The Broken Spears*). [source: SEP-MX §3]
- *tlamatinime* ("knowers of things", "sages", "philosophers"; sing. *tlamatini*). [source: IEP-AZ §1c]
- Nezahualcóyotl (1402–1472), king of Tetzcoco, the most prominent *tlamatini*: doubted the permanence of human life ("if gold and jade can splinter…"); meaning in *flor y canto* ("flower and song", art and beauty). Hurtado compares him to Heraclitus or Parmenides; caution that the texts are fragmentary and poetic. [source: SEP-MX §2; IEP-AZ §2g]
- Ometéotl, "god of duality", male-female, creator of all things. [source: SEP-MX §2; IEP-AZ §2f]
- James Maffie (IEP author; Univ. of Maryland): *Aztec Philosophy: Understanding a World in Motion*, University Press of Colorado, 2014 (Hurtado gives 2015). NDPR: its focus is metaphysics; León-Portilla's was the only earlier book-length English study; Maffie disagrees with him on several points. [source: WS-7 (NDPR review); SEP-MX §2]
- **Teotl** (Maffie): "a single, dynamic, vivifying, eternally self-generating and self-regenerating sacred power, energy or force"; "teotl is identical with everything and everything is identical with teotl" (pantheism); processive — Maffie suggests treating "teotl" as a verb ("teotlizing"). [source: IEP-AZ §2a]
- "Dialectical polar monism": the cosmos is identical with teotl, which presents itself as the "ceaseless, cyclical oscillation of polar yet complementary opposites" (being/not-being, life/death, masculine/feminine…), unlike Zoroastrian-style dualisms where one side wins. [source: IEP-AZ §2b]
- Defining problem: "How can humans maintain their balance upon the slippery earth?" (proverb "It is slippery, it is slick on the earth"). Nahua philosophy is "way-seeking" rather than "truth-seeking" (compare Daoism, Confucianism, pragmatism). Truth (*neltiliztli*) is "well-rootedness", not correspondence (León-Portilla 1963: 8: "'truth'… was to be identified with well-grounded stability"). [source: IEP-AZ §3a, §4b]
- Maffie's caution: views should strictly be attributed to "the upper elite of priests, scholars, and educated nobility", and Western divisions (metaphysics, epistemology…) are heuristic only. [source: IEP-AZ §1c]
- *Nepantla* ("in between") — Uranga reintroduced it in 1952; taken up by Gloria Anzaldúa. [source: SEP-MX §2]
- **Andean thought (thin sourcing — keep very short):** Francisco Miró Quesada (1978): "In a broad sense, philosophy has always existed in Latin America. The Maya and the Inca had thought that… may be considered of philosophical character" (Nuccetelli's translation). [source: SEP-LAM §1.2] Josef Estermann (b. 1956, Swiss philosopher-theologian; missionary in Cusco 1990–98) proposes a *Filosofía andina* (*Filosofía Andina: Estudio intercultural de la sabiduría autóctona*, 1998; WP says Quito, another source Lima-Cusco); he stresses relationality and *pacha* as the ordered space-time universe. [source: WP-Josef_Estermann; WS-8 (secondary summaries only)] Silvia Rivera Cusicanqui's *ch'ixi*: "the Aymara idea that things can be and not be at the same time". [source: SEP-FEM §2.4] Dussel notes colonial university philosophy took nothing from Nahua *tlamatinime* or Inca *amautas*. [source: WS-5 (French excerpt of *Philosophie de la libération*)]

## 2. Colonial scholasticism and universities (cross-ref Aquinas, Scotus/Ockham, Renaissance chapters)
- Royal and Pontifical University of Mexico founded by royal decree of Charles I (Charles V) signed at Valladolid, **21 September 1551**; closed 1865; UNAM (1910) claims to be its heir. [source: WP-Royal_and_Pontifical_University_of_Mexico]
- National University of San Marcos, Lima: established **12 May 1551** (royal privilege of Charles V); "first officially established" university in the Americas; began functioning officially 2 January 1553; grew out of Dominican studies begun 1 July 1548. [source: WP-National_University_of_San_Marcos]
- Mexico was the main philosophical centre early on, Peru gaining importance in the 17th century; teaching was by religious orders (Franciscans — Scotus; Dominicans — Aquinas; Jesuits more open to modern philosophy). [source: IEP-LA §2; SEP-MX §6]
- Only scholasticism was approved by colonial authorities. [source: SEP-MX §4]
- **Alonso de la Vera Cruz** (Alonso Gutiérrez; b. 1507 Caspueñas — SEP-MX "1507–1584", SEP-LAP "ca. 1504–84"; d. July 1584), Augustinian, pupil of Vitoria at Salamanca; landed at Veracruz 2 July 1536; professor at the new University of Mexico from 1553. Wrote the first philosophical texts printed in the Americas: *Recognitio summularum* (1554), *Dialectica resolutio* (1554), *Physica speculatio* (1557); also *Relectio de dominio infidelium et de justo bello*. Argued natives did not lose their lands by being pagans, that the Crown had taken them from lawful owners, and that sovereignty ultimately belongs to the people. [source: WP-Alonso_Gutiérrez; SEP-MX §4; SEP-LAP §1]
- Antonio Rubio (1548–1615), Jesuit, in New Spain 1576–1600: *Logica mexicana* (SEP-MX 1603; IEP 1605) adopted as a textbook at Alcalá; seven editions in Europe (IEP); Descartes studied Rubio's *Logica* at La Flèche (SEP-MX). [source: SEP-MX §4; IEP-LA §2; SEP-LAM §1.2]
- Tomás de Mercado (ca. 1530–1575): logic and the justice of commercial trade. [source: SEP-LAP §1; SEP-MX §4]
- Juan de Zumárraga (1468–1548): Erasmian humanist; founded the Colegio de Tlatelolco for natives; helped install the first press (1539). Vasco de Quiroga (d. 1565): hospital-towns in Michoacán inspired by More's *Utopia*. [source: SEP-MX §3]
- **Francisco de Vitoria** (c. 1483 Burgos – 12 August 1546; SEP-MX gives 1483, IEP c.1483, SEP-LAP 1492): Dominican, Salamanca. Published nothing; lectures survive in students' notes. Three *relectiones* on the Indies: *De temperantia* (1537), *De Indis* (1539, delivered summer 1539) and *De iure belli* (1539; WP: 18 or 19 June 1539). [source: WP-Francisco_de_Vitoria; SEP-SAL §7]
- *De Indis*: were the inhabitants *veri domini* (true masters) of their goods and public affairs? Yes, "even if they were unbelievers, sinners and irrational"; neither pope nor emperor had rightful claim; but the third part lists eight "just titles", e.g. violation of Spaniards' right to travel and preach, protection of innocents. Charles V reacted harshly to *De Indis*. James Brown Scott (1934) called Vitoria "the father of modern international law". [source: SEP-SAL §1, §7]
- Kohn (SEP-COL): Vitoria held all humans share rationality and natural rights, so the papal grant was illegitimate, but force was legitimate when Indians violated the Law of Nations (peaceful travel, trade, preaching): "Within Victoria's critique… was a rationalization for conquest, albeit a restrictive one." [source: SEP-COL §2]
- Paul III's bull *Sublimis Deus*, 2 June 1537, influenced by Las Casas, declared indigenous peoples fully human with souls and just title to possessions. [source: SEP-SAL §1]
- 18th century: Jesuits expelled from Spanish territories in 1767; Clavijero (1731–1787), *Storia antica del Messico* (1780); Díaz de Gamarra, *Elementa recentioris philosophiae* (1774). [source: SEP-MX §6]

## 3. The Valladolid debate, 1550–51 (core argument; cross-ref Aristotle's natural slavery)
- **Las Casas**: born Seville **11 November 1484** (the traditional 1474 was shown in the 1970s to be an error — Parish & Weidman 1976; SEP-SAL still prints 1474) – died Madrid 18 July 1566. Arrived in Hispaniola as a layman; held an encomienda; 1513 chaplain in the conquest of Cuba (campaigns at Bayamo and Camagüey and "the massacre of Hatuey", WP wording); 1514 conversion reading Sirach 34; Dominican novice 1522, vows 1523; first resident Bishop of Chiapas (consecrated 30 March 1544); first official "Protector of the Indians". [source: WP-Bartolomé_de_las_Casas]
- *Brevísima relación de la destrucción de las Indias* written 1542, published Seville 1552; *Historia de las Indias* finished 1561, published 1875; *Apologética historia sumaria*. New Laws of 1542 limited the encomienda; partly repealed 20 October 1545. [source: WP-Bartolomé_de_las_Casas] (Periergia History *The Conquest of the Americas*: Montesinos sermon 1511, Laws of Burgos 1512, New Laws 1542, *Short Account* written 1542 printed 1552 — consistent.)
- **Sepúlveda**: Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda (WP: 1490 – 17 November 1573, Pozoblanco; SEP-SAL: 1494–1573), humanist and Aristotle translator; never visited America. *Democrates alter* (*Democrates secundus sive de iustis belli causis*), refused publication by the theologians of Salamanca and Alcalá. [source: WP-Juan_Ginés_de_Sepúlveda; WP-Bartolomé_de_las_Casas; IEP-LA §2a]
- Setting: Charles I (Charles V) ordered in 1550 that further conquests cease until a *junta* of jurists and theologians judged the issue; held at the Colegio de San Gregorio, Valladolid. [source: WP-Valladolid_debate]
- Procedure: Sepúlveda read the conclusions of *Democrates alter*; Las Casas read his Latin *Apologia* (written late 1540s; IEP says *In Defense of the Indians*, written 1548–1550); Domingo de Soto summarised; Sepúlveda replied with twelve objections, Las Casas answered them. [source: WP-Bartolomé_de_las_Casas; WP-Valladolid_debate; IEP-LA §2a]
- **Sepúlveda's argument (four justifications for war):** (1) by their natural condition some Indians are unable to rule themselves (Aristotle's natural slaves, *Politics* I), so Spaniards should be their masters — "as children to parents, as women are to men"; (2) Spaniards may stop cannibalism as a crime against nature; (3) likewise human sacrifice of innocents; (4) conversion to Christianity requires prior pacification. Also: only Spain can protect weak Indians from stronger ones. [source: WP-Valladolid_debate; WP-Bartolomé_de_las_Casas; WP-Juan_Ginés_de_Sepúlveda]
- **Las Casas's replies:** (1) Aristotle's "barbarian"/natural slave does not apply: Indians are "fully capable of reason", with legitimate rulers and ordered societies (SEP-SAL after Hanke: there may be people simple-minded enough to be natural slaves, but they exist in Spain as much as in the Americas); (2) Scripture sanctions war only against certain Canaanite tribes, not all heathens; (3) Augustine and Chrysostom opposed forcing faith; peaceful persuasion is the only true way to convert; (4) human sacrifice is wrong, but war is worse: some weak Indians suffering at the hands of strong ones is preferable to all Indians suffering at the hands of Spaniards; (5) neither Spain nor the Church has jurisdiction over Indians who have not accepted Christ. [source: WP-Valladolid_debate; WP-Bartolomé_de_las_Casas; SEP-SAL §7; IEP-LA §2a]
- **Outcome:** judges "scattered without any definite decision"; most wrote separate opinions, now lost (except Dr Anaya's: conquest acceptable "for the good of the Indians and not for gold"); both sides claimed victory. Sepúlveda's works were not published again in Spain in his lifetime (Hanke), yet his arguments fed the "war by fire and blood" policy of the Third Mexican Provincial Council (1585, Chichimeca War). Conquest resumed (Valdivia in Chile in the 1550s); expansion formally allowed again May 1556. [source: WP-Valladolid_debate] SEP-LAP: results "inconclusive" — Sepúlveda's work suppressed for a time, but Las Casas's limits on force "never became officially adopted". [source: SEP-LAP §3.1]
- Soto, who summarised, rejected Aristotelian natural slavery: those incapable of guiding themselves should be guided for their own good, "not as living tools". [source: SEP-SAL §7]
- **Objection to Las Casas from within the debate's logic:** his position still presupposed the legitimacy of evangelisation and of the Crown's sovereignty (he sought peaceful, not no, conversion). Supported by: "Christianity should be presented to natives as a religious option" (WP); Dussel reads Las Casas as making appeals to reason "a theological and evangelical norm". [source: WP-Juan_Ginés_de_Sepúlveda; SEP-LIBN §1.1]
- **Las Casas and African slavery:** in his reform plans (1516 *Memorial*, 1517–18 plans) he suggested replacing Indian labour with imported African slaves; he later regretted it and included an apology in the *Historia de las Indias*. Debate on his responsibility: David Walker blamed him; John Fiske (1900) and Benjamin Keen denied he affected the trade, already in place; Sylvia Wynter argued the 1516 *Memorial* led directly to Charles V's 1518 licence for 4,000 African slaves. [source: WP-Bartolomé_de_las_Casas] A 19th-c. English rendering of *Historia* bk 3 says he gave the advice "not taking note of the injustice with which the Portuguese seize them", and later held Africans were enslaved unjustly on the same reasoning as the Indians; chapter numbering varies by edition (cited as bk 3 ch. 101 or ch. 129). [source: WS-13 (secondary; wording not verified)]
- Later Salamanca-trained Jesuits in America on African slavery: Diego de Avendaño (1594–1688, Lima) called contracts enslaving Africans a grave risk to conscience yet "somehow" justifiable, Africans being "vilissimi inter homines". [source: SEP-SAL §7]
- Nuccetelli: Las Casas's ethics is a counterexample to the "critical theory" — "original, authentic, and peculiar, though developed under extreme cultural and economic dependence". [source: SEP-LAM §2.2.3]
- Dussel's reading: Valladolid "marks the clear emergence of a liberation discourse and consciousness"; Sepúlveda articulated "the emergent imperial and colonizing modern consciousness of Europe". [source: SEP-LIBN §1.1]

## 4. Indigenous and mestizo writers of the colonial period
- **Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala** (c. 1535 [IEP: c.1550] – after 1616), Quechua noble of the non-Inca Yarowilca lineage; served as interpreter for Cristóbal de Albornoz's campaign against Taki Unquy (1560s–70s); assisted Martín de Murúa. *El primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno*: written c. 1600–1615, finished around 1615, addressed to Philip III (no evidence he saw it); 1,189 pages, mostly Spanish with Quechua, 398 full-page drawings (IEP: "nearly 800 pages of text… nearly 400 line drawings"); "the longest sustained critique of Spanish colonial rule produced by an Indigenous subject in the entire colonial period"; *Mapa Mundi* puts Cuzco at the centre. Kept in the Royal Danish Library, Copenhagen, since at least the early 1660s; rediscovered by Richard Pietschmann in 1908; facsimile by Paul Rivet, Paris 1936; critical edition Murra and Adorno 1980. [source: WP-Felipe_Guaman_Poma_de_Ayala; WP-El_primer_nueva_corónica; IEP-LA §2b] IEP: he built "a powerful case for maximum Indian autonomy", drawing among others on Las Casas; Mignolo treats him as an early decolonial thinker. [source: IEP-LA §2b, §4f] (Consistent with Periergia History *Colonial Latin America*: "nearly 1,200 pages… almost 400 drawings".)
- **Inca Garcilaso de la Vega** (born Gómez Suárez de Figueroa, Cuzco 12 April 1539 – Córdoba 23 April 1616), son of conquistador Sebastián Garcilaso de la Vega and the Inca *ñusta* Chimpu Ocllo; first language Quechua; went to Spain 1561, never returned. *La Florida del Inca* (Lisbon 1605); *Comentarios Reales de los Incas* (Lisbon 1609; second part on the conquest 1617). Banned from publication in Quechua/Lima by Charles III in the 1780s after Túpac Amaru II's rising. [source: WP-Inca_Garcilaso_de_la_Vega] (Periergia History has "another Rome" — not re-checked here.)
- Carlos de Sigüenza y Góngora (1645–1700): idealised Aztec society to articulate criollo identity yet despised contemporary Indians (1692 riot) — IEP's example of criollo ambivalence; *Libra astronómica y filosófica* (1690) against comets as omens. [source: IEP-LA §2c; SEP-MX §5]

## 5. Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz
- Born San Miguel Nepantla; birth 12 November 1648 (WP infobox) or 1651 (SEP-LAP, SEP-MX, IEP, SEP-FEM all use 1651); died Mexico City 17 April 1695 after nursing sisters in an epidemic. Hieronymite nun (Santa Paula, from 1669; briefly Discalced Carmelites 1667). Asked to attend the university disguised as a man; refused. [source: WP-Juana_Inés_de_la_Cruz; SEP-MX §5]
- November 1690: the Bishop of Puebla, Manuel Fernández de Santa Cruz, as "Sor Filotea", published without permission her critique of a 40-year-old sermon by the Jesuit António Vieira (*Carta atenagórica*) with a letter urging her to religious study; her reply *Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz* (1691) defends women's right to learn and for older women to teach younger ones. By 1693 she seemingly stopped writing; a 1694 penitential document is signed "Yo, la Peor de Todas"; she reportedly gave up a library of over 4,000 volumes. [source: WP-Juana_Inés_de_la_Cruz] (Consistent with published Periergia chapters.)
- IEP: the *Respuesta* "subtly but masterfully defends rational equality between men and women, makes a powerful case for women's right to education, and develops an understanding of wisdom as a form of self-realization". [source: IEP-LA §2d]
- SEP-LAP: "the first Latin American thinker to raise questions concerning the status of women"; retrospectively "the first Latin American feminist writer and philosopher". [source: SEP-LAP §1]
- Hurtado: among the first American philosophers because "the first to consider her triple condition as a human being, an American, and a woman"; *Primero sueño* (1692) is "an original exercise in philosophy": the soul's night journey toward knowledge, intellect rises through empirical and abstract knowledge, glimpses the Divinity, collapses, poet wakes at dawn; draws on Neoplatonic, Aristotelian, Scholastic, Hermetic, mystical sources; "Like Descartes… like Pascal" on science's value and limits. Hurtado concedes she "does not fully recognize the role of race, caste, or indigeneity". [source: SEP-MX §5]
- *Primero sueño*: 976 lines, silva form; published 1692 in the second volume of her works (Seville). [source: WP-Primero_sueño] (Earlier Periergia notes, via SEP-Rat: ends "yo despierta", feminine form.)
- Dussel/Mendieta include her in the first "discourse of emancipation" for "her blend of poetry, theological speculation, praise of Amerindian traditions, and nascent feminist awareness". [source: SEP-LIBN §1.1]
- English translations of the *Respuesta*: Electa Arenal and Amanda Powell, *The Answer/La Respuesta* (Feminist Press, 1994; 2nd ed. 2009); also Margaret Sayers Peden (*A Woman of Genius*, 1982), Edith Grossman, Alan S. Trueblood. Octavio Paz, *Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz o las trampas de la fe* (1982; English tr. Peden, *Sor Juana, or, The Traps of Faith*, 1988/89). [source: WP-Juana_Inés_de_la_Cruz]

## 6. Independence-era thought (brief)
- Bolívar's *Carta de Jamaica* ("Contestación de un Americano Meridional a un caballero de esta isla"), finished **6/7 September 1815** in Kingston (WP: 7 September), replying to merchant Henry Cullen; aimed at winning British support; ends with a call for the union of the American nations. ## 7. Positivism and its critics (summary only — covered in *Philosophy in an Age of Empire*)
- **Already published (do not duplicate):** Comte and "Order and Progress"; Brazil's flag (1889); Barreda's "Civic Oration" (1867) and Escuela Nacional Preparatoria (1868); Sierra and the *científicos*; Martí's "Nuestra América" (1891); Rodó's *Ariel* (1900); Ateneo de la Juventud (1909), Caso's lectures; Vasconcelos's *La raza cósmica* (1925) with the "eugenics of aesthetics" critique; Zea's *Positivism in Mexico*. Cross-reference, one or two sentences at most.
- SEP-LAP: positivism dominant from mid-19th c. to c. 1910, became official state philosophy of several nations, "used to justify dictatorial regimes, as in the case of Mexico"; by 1930 remaining positivists were "museum pieces". [source: SEP-LAP §1, §2]
- **New material — the "generation of founders"** (Romero's term; Miró Quesada's "patriarchs"): Alejandro Korn (Argentina), Alejandro Deústua (Peru), Vasconcelos and Caso (Mexico), Enrique Molina (Chile), Carlos Vaz Ferreira (Uruguay), Raimundo de Farias Brito (Brazil). Trained as positivists, they broke with it on philosophical grounds — chiefly that determinism, a corollary of positivism, leaves no room for freedom — drawing on Boutroux, Bergson, French vitalism; Ortega y Gasset's 1916 visit brought Scheler and Hartmann. SEP-LAP criticises them for accepting borrowed anti-positivist solutions "uncritically". [source: SEP-LAP §2.1, §3.3] (SEP-LAP oddly says the founders were "born around 1910" — an evident slip; see discrepancies.)
- **Alejandro Korn** (3 May 1860 – 9 October 1936), psychiatrist (directed the Melchor Romero asylum for 18 years) and philosopher; first university official in Latin America elected by student vote; *La libertad creadora* (1922), *Axiología* (1930). Fought to create the Facultad de Filosofía y Letras at Buenos Aires (Rabossi). SEP-LAP: his sophistication "planted the critical seeds" of the next period. [source: WP-Alejandro_Korn; SEP-LAM §2.1.1; SEP-LAP §2.1]
- **Alejandro Deústua** (22 March 1849 – 6 August 1945), Peru; briefly Prime Minister (9 Aug – 4 Nov 1902). Roig later called his work "a prime example of the uprooting and the alienation in Latin American thought denounced… by Augusto Salazar Bondy". [source: WP-Alejandro_Deustua; SEP-LAM §2.2.3]
- **Carlos Vaz Ferreira** (15 October 1872 – 3 January 1958), Uruguay; *Lógica viva* (1910), *Moral para intelectuales* (1908), *El pragmatismo* (1909); influenced by Mill, Spencer, then James and Bergson; defended university autonomy against Gabriel Terra's dictatorship. *Sobre feminismo* published 1933 (IEP, WP, SEP-FEM; SEP-LAP says lectures 1917, published 1935); IEP: lectures from 1914; 1933 also the year Uruguayan women won the vote. SEP-FEM: "compensatory feminism" — women are biologically disadvantaged but institutions can compensate. [source: WP-Carlos_Vaz_Ferreira; IEP-LA §4e; SEP-FEM; SEP-LAP §3.5]
- **Raimundo de Farias Brito** (24 July 1862 – 16 January 1917), Brazil; neo-Kantianism and spiritualism; *O mundo interior* (1914); chair of logic at Colégio Pedro II from 1909. [source: WP-Raimundo_de_Farias_Brito]
- **Antonio Caso** (19 December 1883 – 6 March 1946): *La existencia como economía, como desinterés y como caridad* (1919): three levels of existence — biological/economic, disinterested/aesthetic, charitable; charity the highest expression of human dignity; against Darwin, Spencer, Nietzsche's Übermensch and Stirner's egoism; drew on Bergson, Tolstoy, Christianity. Created UNAM's philosophy department; debated Lombardo Toledano against socialist education (1930s); *La persona humana y el estado totalitario* (1941). [source: SEP-MX §8.1; WP-Antonio_Caso_Andrade]
- SEP-LAP on philosophical anthropology: positivists explained humans psychologically/scientifically; vitalist antipositivists (Vaz Ferreira, Deústua, Caso, Molina, Korn) rejected this because it leaves no place for freedom. [source: SEP-LAP §3.3]

## 8. Vasconcelos (cross-ref; add later politics)
- (28 February 1882 – 30 June 1959.) Philosophical system: an "aesthetic a priori" and metaphysical categories based on music; "the universe is more like a symphony than a logical treatise"; *Estética* (1936) to *Lógica* (1945), sketched in *Pitágoras, una teoría del ritmo* (1916). His essays on Latin American culture proved more influential than his philosophy. [source: SEP-MX §8.1]
- SEP-LAP: in Vasconcelos indigenous peoples "were something to be assimilated along the way towards the formation of a 'cosmic' race". [source: SEP-LAP §3.1]
- Later politics: resigned as education secretary 1924 opposing Calles; ran for president 1929, lost to Pascual Ortiz Rubio in a controversial election, went abroad again; drifted to "a deeply Catholic political conservatism", wrote sympathetically of Franco, and in the magazine *Timón* (1940) praised Hitler ("Hitler represents, ultimately, an idea, the German idea…", *Timón* 16, 8 June 1940). Scholarship: Bar-Lewaw on *Timón* and Nazi collaboration; Héctor Orestes Aguilar, "Ese olvidado nazi de nombre José Vasconcelos" (2007). [source: WP-José_Vasconcelos]
- In 1950 he attacked Mexican existentialism as "as sterile as the nothingness from which it takes its point of departure". [source: SEP-MEX-EX §3]

## 9. José Carlos Mariátegui
- 14 June 1894 Moquegua – 16 April 1930 Lima. In Italy 1919–23 (Livorno congress, 1921, founding of PCI); leg amputated 1924; founded the journal *Amauta* (1926); broke with Haya de la Torre/APRA 1928; founded the Peruvian Socialist Party 7 October 1928 (renamed Communist Party after his death) and the CGTP (1929). [source: WP-José_Carlos_Mariátegui]
- *Siete ensayos de interpretación de la realidad peruana* (Lima, 1928): economic evolution, the problem of the Indian, the problem of the land, public education, the religious factor, regionalism and centralism, literature. [source: WP-Seven_Interpretive_Essays] English tr. by Marjory Urquidi (University of Texas Press, 1971) — **translator not verified this session**; WP lists a 1997 UT Press edition.
- **Argument on "the problem of the Indian"** (WP + IEP): (1) Indians are some four-fifths of Peru's agricultural population; (2) their misery is not a racial, administrative, legal, educational or ecclesiastical problem but an economic one; (3) its root is land tenure — *gamonalismo*/*latifundismo*, a semi-feudal regime inherited from the colony and untouched by independence, which criollos led; (4) so humanitarian, educational or legal remedies "will necessarily fail" while that structure survives; (5) Andean communities preserve elements of agrarian collectivism (Inca "socialist type" economy), a basis for socialism; therefore Peru should pass to socialism even though bourgeois capitalism never fully developed. [source: WP-Seven_Interpretive_Essays; IEP-LA §4b; SEP-LAP §2.3, §3.1]
- Heterodox: no essential conflict between religious thought and Marxism; Marx's European stages do not apply to Peru; aesthetics and "myth"/spirituality fuel revolution; "Indo-America" his preferred name. [source: SEP-LAP §2.3; IEP-LA §4b]
- Socialism "without tracing or copying" (Spanish "calco y copia… creación heroica"), editorial "Aniversario y balance", *Amauta* no. 17, September 1928. [source: WP-José_Carlos_Mariátegui (English phrase); WS-6 (editorial and issue confirmed; exact Spanish sentence only via a reprint)] (Consistent with Periergia History *Intellectual History*.)
- Mariátegui 1925, "¿Existe un pensamiento hispano-americano?" (*Repertorio Americano*): "Hispanic-American thought is generally only a rhapsody composed from the motifs and elements of European thought" (Nuccetelli's translation). [source: SEP-LAM §2.2.3]
- Critics: Víctor Andrés Belaúnde, *La realidad nacional* (Catholic reply); Grijalva on his "Inka utopianism". Shining Path and MRTA both claimed him. [source: WP-Seven_Interpretive_Essays; WP-José_Carlos_Mariátegui]

## 10. Gaos and the Spanish exiles; Ramos; the Hyperion group
- Spanish Civil War exiles to Mexico: Joaquín Xirau, Gallegos Rocafull, Wenceslao Roces, José Gaos, Recaséns Siches, Eugenio Ímaz, Eduardo Nicol; García Bacca and María Zambrano partly; young arrivals Adolfo Sánchez Vázquez, Ramón Xirau, Carmen Rovira. Gaos's word for them: *transterrados*. Abellán counts some fifty. [source: SEP-MX §8.3; IEP-LA §4c; SEP-LAP §2.2]
- **José Gaos** (26 December 1900 Gijón – 10 June 1969 Mexico City), pupil of Ortega; exiled 1938; first Spanish translator of *Being and Time*; radical perspectivism: philosophy is "a personal confession"; teacher of Zea and the Hyperion group. Gaos later criticised them: the "Mexican being" they studied does not exist, their method "arbitrary" (1952). [source: WP-José_Gaos; SEP-MX §8.3; SEP-MEX-EX §1, §3]
- **Samuel Ramos** (1897 Zitácuaro – 20 June 1959): *El perfil del hombre y la cultura en México* (1934): Mexicans suffer an "inferiority complex" (Adler) from imitating more developed nations; the *pelado* as "the most elemental and clearly defined expression of national character". English: *Profile of Man and Culture in Mexico*, tr. Peter G. Earle, University of Texas Press, 1962. Ramos nonetheless held Mexican philosophy should serve universal values and reason; *Hacia un nuevo humanismo* (1940). It prompted Octavio Paz's *El laberinto de la soledad* (1950). [source: WP-Samuel_Ramos; SEP-MX §8.2, §8.4]
- **Hyperion group (Hiperión)**: young UNAM philosophers — Zea, Emilio Uranga (1921–1988), Jorge Portilla (1918–1963), Ricardo Guerra, Luis Villoro, also Joaquín Sánchez McGregor, Salvador Reyes Nevares, Fausto Vega; named after a Titan; used existentialist categories to analyse *lo mexicano*. Dates disputed (see discrepancies). Portilla: "we Mexicans are existentialist from birth" (1949). [source: SEP-MX §8.4; SEP-MEX-EX §1; WP-Emilio_Uranga; IEP-LA §4d]
- **Uranga, *Análisis del ser del mexicano* (1952):** the Mexican's mode of being is "accidentality" (scholastic accident vs substance): "The accident is fragility: oscillation between being and nothingness" (Uranga 1952 [2021: 116], tr. in Sánchez & Sanchez); but since human being as such is accidental, Mexicans are closer to the human essence than Europeans, who must learn from them. Related notions: *zozobra*, *nepantla*. [source: SEP-MX §8.4; SEP-MEX-EX §2.2]
- Portilla, *Fenomenología del relajo* (posthumous, 1966): *relajo* as the group suspension of a value through mockery; the opposite of seriousness. [source: SEP-MX §8.4]
- Critiques: Gaos (1952; non-existent object); Abelardo Villegas (1979; confused history with being); Roger Bartra, *The Cage of Melancholy* (1987; rooted in "the disdain of the dominant classes"); Ana Santos Ruiz (2015; a state ideology erasing ethnic and class differences). The movement faded after the 1960s. [source: SEP-MEX-EX §3; SEP-MX §8.4] (Cross-ref published *Existentialism* chapter: Hiperión "took up existentialist themes before abandoning them" — consistent.)

## 11. The "is there a Latin American philosophy?" debate (core argument)
- Precursors: Alberdi, "Ideas…" (Montevideo, 1840) proposed a philosophy course instructing youth in "the principles that reside in the conscience of our societies" and said theoretical philosophy was for Europe; Ardao and Roig argue its influence was created a century later by Gaos. [source: SEP-LAM §2.2.3]
- **Zea** (30 June 1912 – 8 June 2004): "En torno a una filosofía americana" (SEP-LAM: 1942, *Cuadernos Americanos*; SEP-MX: 1945); "La filosofía como compromiso" (1948); *América en la historia* (1957); *La filosofía americana como filosofía sin más* (Siglo XXI, 1969); *Discurso desde la marginación y la barbarie* (1988). English: *The Latin American Mind* (1963). Founded UNAM's Latin American Studies centre (1966/1978). [source: WP-Leopoldo_Zea_Aguilar; SEP-MX §8.5; IEP-LA §4d]
- **Salazar Bondy** (Peruvian, 1925–1974): *¿Existe una filosofía de nuestra América?* (Mexico City: Siglo XXI, 1968). Called these "the philosophy of philosophy" and sorted the questions as descriptive, prescriptive and metaphysical. [source: SEP-LAM §2, bibliography] Died 1974. [source: SEP-LAP; SEP-LIBN]
- Frondizi (b. 20 November 1910 Posadas – d. 23 February 1983, Waco, Texas; brother of President Arturo Frondizi): "Is There an Ibero-American Philosophy?" (*Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 9, 1949): universalist; only about 10% of Latin American philosophy original. [source: WP-Risieri_Frondizi; SEP-LAM §2.2.1–2]
- SEP-LAP's four approaches: **universalist** (philosophy like science; Frondizi — largely a failure), **culturalist** (truth perspectival; Zea), **critical** (philosophy depends on social conditions; Salazar Bondy — inauthentic while dependent), **ethnic** (Gracia — the philosophy of the Latin American ethnos). [source: SEP-LAP §3.4]
- **Salazar Bondy's argument (critical theory), reconstructed:**
  1. Authentic philosophy is a cultural product "that… has integrity and is devoid of falsity or pretense" (Salazar 1968: 100–101; distinguish *peculiarity*, mere regional colouring, "the cheapest" feature).
  2. A culture's philosophy is shaped by its socio-economic conditions.
  3. Latin America lives in dependence and underdevelopment relative to Europe and the US, producing a "defective culture" in which imitation is mistaken for creation.
  4. Hence Latin American philosophy has been, from colonial times, "a thought of the upper class or of a refined oligarchical elite… In all these cases underdevelopment and domination are influential" (Salazar 1969: 241, Nuccetelli's translation).
  5. Therefore authentic Latin American philosophy requires prior liberation from domination; meanwhile philosophy must be a philosophy of liberation. (Corollary: the philosophy of the dominant countries is also inauthentic insofar as it depends on domination.)
  [source: SEP-LAM §2.2.3; IEP-LA §4d; SEP-LIBN §1.2; WS-12]
- **Zea's reply (circumstantialism), reconstructed (SEP-LAM's version):** (1) All philosophical works are authentic in that they show the cultural circumstance of their production; (2) Latin American works show theirs; (3) so they are authentic. "Even though being, God, etc., are issues appropriate for every man, the solution to them will be given from a Latin American standpoint" (Zea 1948: 226). This reconciles universal problems with situated answers. Further (IEP): Zea calls for critically assimilating the past, not waiting for economic liberation; Hurtado: "Zea believed that the originality or authenticity of Latin American philosophy did not require complete liberation from European colonialism." WS-12 (secondary): Zea treats philosophy as a common human task and asks why the question arises only for Latin America (philosophy "sin más", without qualifiers). [source: SEP-LAM §2.2.3; IEP-LA §4d; SEP-MX §8.5; WS-12]
- **Objections:**
  - To Salazar Bondy: reductionist — counterexample Las Casas (original, authentic and peculiar under dependence); ad hominem 1: the critical theorist usually belongs to the upper class, so by its own lights the theory is inauthentic; ad hominem 2: it borrows from Marxism, existentialism, structuralism, the Frankfurt School — borrowed traditions, so again inauthentic (Gracia 2003, Hurtado 2007). [source: SEP-LAM §2.2.3]
  - To Zea: premise 1 is very strong; it is "not obvious what is Greek about Aristotle's theory of the syllogism or German about Kant's categorical imperative"; at most it yields peculiarity, not authenticity; and no one can say what counts as *the* Latin American circumstance given cultural diversity (Gracia 2003). IEP: Salazar attacked Zea's "ungrounded idealism". [source: SEP-LAM §2.2.3; IEP-LA §4d]
  - To Frondizi: originality is too demanding a criterion — non-academic philosophers (Hume, Bentham, Mill, Marx) count elsewhere (Nuccetelli 2003); no other ethnic philosophy is required to be original (Gracia 2003). [source: SEP-LAM §2.2.2]
- **Replies / middle ways:** Gracia's ethnic-philosophy account (family resemblance, no necessary and sufficient conditions); Moulines (2010) — universal arguments by Latin Americans with international uptake (paraconsistent logic of da Costa, Alchourrón–Bulygin deontic logic, AGM belief revision, Bunge); Nuccetelli's applied-philosophy account (original arguments + sensitivity to Latin American context). "New skeptics" (Rabossi, Hurtado, Pereda): problem is "invisibility" — no internal or external dialogue; Rabossi's "periphery philosopher" as *guacho* (orphan). [source: SEP-LAM §2.1.2, §2.3]
- SEP-LIBN: the philosophy of liberation "arguably, began in the late sixties" with this Zea–Salazar Bondy debate; at the 1973 San Miguel *jornada* Salazar Bondy gave "Filosofía de la dominación y filosofía de la liberación", Zea "La filosofía latinoamericana como filosofía de la liberación". [source: SEP-LIBN §1.1–1.2] Cerutti: Zea's 1969 book only began the controversy. [source: WS-12]

## 12. Philosophy of liberation and Enrique Dussel
- Movement: emerged in Argentina late 1960s; explicitly labelled at the Second Argentine National Congress of Philosophy, Córdoba, 1971; group (Ardiles, Parisini, Scannone, de Zan, Fornari) shaped at *jornadas* at the Universidad del Salvador, San Miguel (SEP prints "Universidad of San Salvador"); 1975 First Mexican National Congress of Philosophy, Morelia; journal *Revista de Filosofía Latinoamericana* (1974). Persecution after the March 1976 coup; exile to Mexico, Ecuador, Venezuela, Peru, Canada; AFYL founded Bogotá 1980. [source: SEP-LIBN §1.2]
- Founders (SEP-LAP/IEP): Arturo Andrés Roig (1922–2012; exile Ecuador), Horacio Cerutti-Guldberg (b. 1950; Mexico), Dussel (Mexico); also Rodolfo Kusch, Juan Carlos Scannone. Ignacio Ellacuría (b. 1930, Vizcaya, Spain; Jesuit, student of Rahner and Zubiri; rector of the Universidad Centroamericana, San Salvador; assassinated 1989 — SEP-LIBN says he became rector in 1969, unchecked; IEP: killed by a military death squad). [source: SEP-LAP §2.3; IEP-LA §4e; SEP-LIBN §3.4]
- Sources ("matrixes", Beorlegui): dependency theory (dos Santos, Cardoso, Faletto, Furtado, Quijano); liberation theology; Freire's pedagogy; the literary Boom and muralists; Fals Borda's sociology of liberation. [source: SEP-LIBN §2]
- Cerutti's four currents: ontologicist (Kusch, Casalla, Cullen, Podetti: the *estar* of the people; anti-historicist populism), analectical (Dussel, Scannone), historicist (Roig, Ardao, Zea, Cerutti), problematizing (Cerutti, Severino Croatto, Assmann, Salazar Bondy). Cerutti urges speaking of "philosophies of liberation". [source: SEP-LIBN §3; SEP-LAP §2.3]
- **Dussel**: born 24 December 1934, La Paz, Mendoza (Argentina); died 5 November 2023 (aged 88). Studied at Cuyo, Madrid (doctorate), Sorbonne (history doctorate), Paris and Münster (theology); 1959–61 manual work in Israel with Paul Gauthier; returned 1969, influenced by dependency theory and Levinas; house bombed, sacked; exile to Mexico 1975 (UAM-Iztapalapa, UNAM); interim rector of UACM 2013–14. More than 50 books. [source: WP-Enrique_Dussel]
- Works: *Filosofía de la liberación* (Mexico: Edicol, 1977); English *Philosophy of Liberation*, tr. Aquilina Martinez and Christine Morkovsky (Maryknoll: Orbis, 1985; reissued Wipf & Stock 2003); *1492: El encubrimiento del Otro* (1992) / *The Invention of the Americas: Eclipse of "the Other" and the Myth of Modernity* (1995); *The Underside of Modernity* (1996, ed. tr. Mendieta); *Ética de la liberación en la edad de la globalización y de la exclusión* (Trotta, 1998; English 2013, ed. Vallega). [source: SEP-LIBN bibliography; WS-11; WP-Enrique_Dussel; SEP-MX §8.7]
- Core thesis (IEP): European philosophy's progress "has come at the expense of the vast majority of humanity, whose massive poverty has only rarely appeared as a fundamental philosophical theme"; method "analectical" (from *ana*, beyond), influenced by Levinas, against "the totalizing tendencies of the Hegelian dialectic". [source: IEP-LA §4e; SEP-LIBN §3.2]
- Analectic/exteriority (SEP-LIBN): a "metaphysical critique of the thinking of the totality"; all Western philosophy "from Aristotle and Plato, to Hegel, Marx and Habermas" is subsumed under self-referential ontological totality; "the authentic people is what is always outside the totality"; the philosopher listens to the "interpellations" of the people. Dussel later moved to a "more dialectical" position after engaging Apel and Habermas (linguistic-pragmatic turn), and joined the "decolonial turn". [source: SEP-LIBN §3.2]
- **"Ego conquiro" (wording provisional):** in *Philosophy of Liberation* Dussel writes that before the Cartesian *ego cogito* there is an *ego conquiro*, and "I conquer" is the practical foundation of "I think". [source: WS-5 (search summary of the 1985 Orbis text; not read directly)] The conquest of America (1492) as the birth of modernity and of "Europe" as a category. [source: SEP-LAP §3.1; WP-Coloniality_of_power]
- **Argument (reconstruction for the chapter, drawing on IEP/SEP-LIBN/SEP-LAP):** (1) Modernity began not with Descartes but with 1492: Europe constituted itself as centre by conquering a periphery; (2) so the modern subject's "I think" presupposed the practical "I conquer"; (3) a philosophy that thinks only from the centre's totality cannot see the victims it excludes (the poor, the colonised, women, the Indigenous); (4) ethics begins from the face/interpellation of the Other outside the system (Levinas's exteriority, made historical and geopolitical); (5) therefore philosophy must start from the periphery and serve its liberation. **Objections:** Schutte (1993) — his early "erotics" was a "machista" ontologisation of Catholic gender roles, hostile to non-binary sexual difference; Alcoff (2000) — insufficient attention to identity categories; Vargas (2005) — Eurocentric despite itself (built on Levinas, Hegel, Marx); Cerutti (1983) — populist/ontologising of "el pueblo"; analytic philosophers dismissed it as unrigorous. **Reply:** Dussel in the 1994 epilogue to *Liberación de la mujer y erótica latinoamericana* rejected his earlier views and their "homophobic" dimension; SEP-LIBN: liberation philosophy is "critical of European philosophy… from within, immanently". [source: SEP-LAP §2.3; SEP-LIBN §1.2, §4]
- Dussel's 1977 *Filosofía ética latinoamericana* III developed an "erotics" with woman as man's "Other"; republished 1980 as *Liberación de la mujer y erótica latinoamericana*. [source: SEP-LIBN §4]
- Transmodernity: WP associates the "cultural movement of transmodernism" with Dussel (term first in Rosa María Rodríguez Magda, 1989) — thin sourcing; use only as "Dussel's term transmodernity, beyond both modernity and postmodernity" with care. [source: WP-Transmodernity; WP-Enrique_Dussel ("a critic of postmodernity, preferring instead the term 'transmodernity'")]
- "Pluriversality" instead of "universality"; "South-South dialogue" (Dussel 2015). [source: SEP-LIBN intro]
- Dialogue partners: Apel, Ricoeur, Habermas, Rorty, Taylor, Vattimo, Alcoff, Mendieta. [source: SEP-LAP §2.3; WP-Enrique_Dussel] (Consistent with Periergia *Critical Theory*.)

## 14. Paulo Freire
- Born Recife, **19 September 1921**; died **2 May 1997** (heart failure). Father died 1934; childhood hunger; law at Recife 1943–47; SESI 1947–57; doctorate 1959. [source: WP-Paulo_Freire; IEP-FR §2–4]
- Literacy: by 1962 (WP) 300 sugar-cane workers taught to read in 45 days; "culture circles" (coordinators and participants, not teachers and pupils); generative words (e.g. *tijolo*, brick); plan for 20,000 circles. Literacy was a condition for voting. [source: WP-Paulo_Freire; IEP-FR §4, §6c]
- 1964 coup (1 April 1964; IEP "supported by the CIA"): imprisoned **70 days** (WP) / **75 days** (IEP); exile 1964–1980 (~16 years): Bolivia briefly, Chile Nov 1964 – Apr 1969 (agrarian reform, FAO/UNESCO), Harvard 1969–70, Geneva (World Council of Churches) 1970–80; Guinea-Bissau literacy campaign 1975. Returned 1980; co-founded the Workers' Party (PT); São Paulo Secretary of Education 1989–May 1991. [source: WP-Paulo_Freire; IEP-FR §7–8; WP-Pedagogy_of_the_Oppressed]
- *Educação como prática da liberdade* (1967). *Pedagogia do oprimido*: written in Portuguese 1967–68, first published in Spanish 1968; English 1970 (tr. Myra Bergman Ramos, New York: Herder & Herder/Continuum); Portuguese original published 1972 (Portugal) and 1974 Brazil (IEP: 1975). Epigraph "To the oppressed, and to those who suffer with them and fight at their side". Banned under apartheid South Africa; removed from Tucson classrooms after Arizona HB 2281 (2010). Sold over 750,000 copies by 2000. [source: WP-Pedagogy_of_the_Oppressed; IEP-FR §6–7; WP-Paulo_Freire]
- **Banking vs problem-posing argument (reconstruction):** (1) In "banking" education the teacher deposits knowledge into students treated as empty receptacles; the relation is vertical; (2) this treats students as objects, not subjects, and trains them to adapt to the world rather than question it — it "transforms students into receiving objects [and] attempts to control thinking and action" (Freire 1971: 64, as quoted by WP); (3) it mirrors and reproduces oppression (as the coloniser treats the colonised's culture as worthless); (4) the oppressed have moreover "internalized" the oppressor (the peasants who could not guard their captured landlord; the peasant who said animals were freer); (5) liberation cannot be given — "freedom is acquired by conquest" by the oppressed themselves; (6) so education must be dialogical, "problem-posing", teacher-student and student-teachers investigating "generative themes" and "limit situations" — *conscientização*, becoming aware of social contradictions and acting against them; method and goal must match. [source: IEP-FR §5–6; WP-Paulo_Freire; WP-Pedagogy_of_the_Oppressed]
- Influences: Hegel's master–slave dialectic, Marx (false consciousness), existentialism, phenomenology, Fromm (biophilia/necrophilia), Memmi, Fanon, Gramsci (organic intellectuals), Anísio Teixeira and Dewey, Christianity/liberation theology. [source: IEP-FR §3]
- **Critiques:** verbose style; sexist language (male pronouns) — revised in later editions (WP: 1995 edition); non-transferability to rich countries; neither fully Marxist, feminist nor Catholic, and no ready-made method; leftists found dialogue too slow; Friedenberg (early review): "pedantic style", underestimates the opposition; Sol Stern (2006, 2009): ignores curriculum and testing, licenses political teaching; Ford/Lewis: the Leninist chapter 4 is neglected. [source: IEP-FR §10; WP-Pedagogy_of_the_Oppressed]
- Legacy: critical pedagogy (Giroux, McLaren, bell hooks…); Boal's Theatre of the Oppressed; Steve Biko's Black Consciousness Movement. [source: IEP-FR §11; WP-Pedagogy_of_the_Oppressed; WP-Paulo_Freire]

## 15. The decolonial turn
- **Aníbal Quijano** (17 November 1930 – 31 May 2018), Peruvian sociologist; CEPAL 1966–71 (dependency theory); later Binghamton. Concepts: "coloniality of power" and "coloniality of knowledge". Key texts: "Colonialidad y modernidad/racionalidad" (WP list: 1991; often cited 1992 — not settled here); "Coloniality of Power, Eurocentrism, and Latin America", *Nepantla: Views from the South* 1(3), 2000, 533–580. [source: WP-Aníbal_Quijano; WP-Coloniality_of_power]
- Coloniality of power: colonial hierarchies — above all the invention of "race" as naturalised difference — and a racial division of labour outlived formal colonialism; Eurocentric control of knowledge: "Europe's hegemony over the new model of global power concentrated all forms of the control of subjectivity, culture, and especially knowledge" (Quijano 2000, as quoted by WP). Modernity/coloniality group: Mignolo, Grosfoguel, Wynter, Maldonado-Torres, Castro-Gómez, Walsh, Lugones. [source: WP-Coloniality_of_power]
- **Walter Mignolo** (b. 1 May 1941, Argentina; Duke): *The Idea of Latin America* (2005) — the idea "still rests upon colonial foundations"; "border thinking", "pluriversality". [source: IEP-LA §4f; earlier Periergia notes (WP-Walter_Mignolo)]
- **María Lugones** (WP: 26 January 1944 – 14 July 2020; IEP: b. 1948), Argentine-born; Carleton, Binghamton. "Heterosexualism and the Colonial/Modern Gender System", *Hypatia* 22(1) (2007): 186–219; "Toward a Decolonial Feminism" (2010). She "was the first scholar to articulate the concept of the coloniality of gender": the modern dimorphic, heteronormative sex/gender system was a colonial imposition, and gender was integral to the colonial classification that Quijano overlooks. Earlier: "Playfulness, 'World'-Travelling, and Loving Perception" (in *Pilgrimages/Peregrinajes*, 2003). [source: SEP-FEM §3; WP-Maria_Lugones]
- Postcolonial vs decolonial: IEP — debate over whether postcolonial theory (from former English/French colonies) fits Latin America; Latin American feminists do not generally see themselves as postcolonial feminists (Schutte and Femenías 2010). [source: IEP-LA §4f]
- Dussel says the philosophy of liberation is part of the decolonial turn. [source: SEP-LIBN §3.2]

## 16. Latin American feminist philosophy
- **Ofelia Schutte** (b. 1945, Cuba), Yale PhD 1978 (Nietzsche); Florida, South Florida. *Cultural Identity and Social Liberation in Latin American Thought* (SUNY, 1993): critique of liberation philosophy; method of Latin American feminist theory: critical conception of knowledge, theory–practice link, freedom and liberation, transformative politics of culture; "feminism is too new there to be able to effectively transform centuries of masculine intellectual dominance in philosophy" (Schutte 2011: 784). [source: WP-Ofelia_Schutte; SEP-FEM §3; SEP-LAP §3.5]
- **Graciela Hierro** (1928–2003), Mexico, student of Vera Yamuni; introduced feminist philosophy into UNAM's curriculum from the 1970s, first feminism panel at a Mexican philosophy congress 1979; founded UNAM's Centro de Estudios de Género; *Ética y feminismo* (1985), *De la domesticación a la educación de las mexicanas* (1989), *Ética de la libertad* (1990); a hedonist/utilitarian feminist ethics of pleasure against the "double sexual morality". [source: IEP-LA §4e; SEP-MX §8.7; SEP-FEM §3]
- Others: Paula Gómez Alonso, first UNAM philosophy graduate, thesis *La cultura femenina* (1933); Rosario Castellanos, *Sobre cultura femenina* (1950); María Luisa Femenías, María Pía Lara; Encuentros Feministas Latinoamericanos y del Caribe from 1981 (Bogotá). Latin American feminism has given more weight to family, class and ethnicity than US feminism (Schutte and Femenías 2010). [source: SEP-MX §8.7; IEP-LA §4e; SEP-LAP §3.5]

## Debates
- **Is pre-Columbian thought philosophy?** León-Portilla, Maffie, Miró Quesada: yes; critics: anachronism; Nuccetelli: "proto-philosophy"; Gracia defers. [SEP-MX §2; SEP-LAM §1.2]
- **Las Casas**: hero of human rights vs limited reformer within evangelisation; responsibility for the African slave trade (Walker, Wynter vs Fiske, Keen). [WP-Bartolomé_de_las_Casas]
- **Vitoria**: father of international law (Scott) vs restrictive rationalisation of conquest (Kohn/Williams). [SEP-SAL §7; SEP-COL §2]
- **Authenticity debate**: universalist (Frondizi), culturalist/circumstantialist (Zea), critical (Salazar Bondy), ethnic (Gracia), Moulines, Nuccetelli; new skeptics (Rabossi, Hurtado, Pereda). [SEP-LAP §3.4; SEP-LAM]
- **Philosophy of Mexicanness**: authentic philosophy vs nationalist state ideology (Bartra, Santos Ruiz); method (Gaos, Villegas). [SEP-MEX-EX §3]
- **Dussel**: Schutte (gender), Alcoff (identity), Vargas (Eurocentrism), Cerutti (populism/ontologising "el pueblo"), analytic dismissal. [SEP-LAP §2.3; SEP-LIBN]
- **Freire**: style, sexism (revised), transferability, insufficiently revolutionary vs too political (Stern). [IEP-FR §10; WP-Pedagogy_of_the_Oppressed]
- **Vasconcelos**: anti-racist intent vs assimilationist and later pro-fascist (cross-ref Age of Empire). [WP-José_Vasconcelos; SEP-LAP §3.1]
- **Mariátegui**: creative Marxist vs "Inka utopianism" (Grijalva); Catholic critique (Belaúnde). [WP-Seven_Interpretive_Essays]
- **Postcolonial vs decolonial frameworks for Latin America**. [IEP-LA §4f]
