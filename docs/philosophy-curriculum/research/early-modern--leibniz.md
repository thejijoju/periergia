## 1. Life

### Leipzig, education
- Born **1 July 1646** in Leipzig, two years before the end of the Thirty Years War; Lutheran family. Father Friedrich Leibniz, jurist and professor of moral philosophy at Leipzig (d. 1652); mother Catharina Schmuck, daughter of a law professor. Had his father's library from a young age (ancient history, Church Fathers). [source: SEP-L §1; IEP-Ov §1]
- University of Leipzig from 1661 (IEP: 1661–63, brief stay at Jena 1663 under Erhard Weigel). Teacher Jakob Thomasius supervised his first philosophical treatise, *On the Principle of Individuation*; education chiefly Scholastic plus Renaissance humanism. Lifelong aim: reconcile modern philosophy with Aristotle, Plato, the Scholastics. [source: SEP-L §1; IEP-Ov §1]
- Rosental anecdote: in a 1714 letter to Nicolas Remond he recalled walking in the Rosental near Leipzig "at the age of fifteen" deliberating whether to keep substantial forms; "Mechanism finally prevailed and led me to apply myself to mathematics" (G III 606/L 654–55). SEP: "some reason to doubt whether Leibniz was really fifteen". [source: SEP-L §2; IEP-Ov §1]
- *Dissertation on the Art of Combinations* (*De arte combinatoria*), **1666**, sketching a "universal characteristic" and logical calculus. [source: SEP-L §1; WP-GWL]
- Doctorate in law: **Altdorf, November 1666** (Leipzig had refused him, probably because of his youth; WP); IEP also says Altdorf 1666. SEP says "upon the completion of his Doctorate of Law in 1667" he was offered a faculty post — CONFLICT: use **1666 (Altdorf)**, which IEP and WP agree on. He declined an academic post at Altdorf. [source: WP-GWL; IEP-Ov §1; SEP-L §1]

### Mainz 1668–72
- First major employment in the court of the Prince-Archbishop (Elector) of Mainz, Johann Philipp von Schönborn, 1668 (IEP), via Baron Johann Christian von Boineburg; legal reform; *Catholic Demonstrations* (1668–70, never completed) aimed at church reconciliation; *New Physical Hypothesis* (1671), parts dedicated to the Paris Académie and the Royal Society. Wrote an unanswered letter to Hobbes in this period. [source: SEP-L §1; IEP-Ov §1]

### Paris 1672–76
- 1672 sent to Paris on a diplomatic mission; stayed four years, with trips to London (SEP mentions 1673; IEP "twice visited London, in 1673 and 1676"). Met Arnauld, Malebranche and above all Christiaan Huygens, who tutored him; read unpublished manuscripts of Descartes and Pascal; said reading Pascal's mathematical manuscripts started him toward the differential calculus. [source: SEP-L §1; IEP-Ov §1]
- Calculating machine (four operations) designed; demonstrated to the Royal Society on 1 February 1673 (WP-Stepped_reckoner). WP-GWL says the machine "was the basis of his election to the Royal Society in 1673" (one adjacent sentence tagged "citation needed"). Treat "elected to the Royal Society in 1673" as WP-only. [source: SEP-L §1; WP-Stepped_reckoner; WP-GWL]
- IEP: "By 1675 he had developed the infinitesimal calculus, only three years after he started the serious study of contemporary mathematics." WP: notebooks record a breakthrough on **11 November 1675** (first use of integral calculus); the earliest use of differentials in his notebooks traces to 1675; he introduced the ∫ sign (from *summa*) and d (from *differentia*). [source: IEP-Ov §1; WP-GWL; WP-Leibniz–Newton]
- *Confessio philosophi* (1672–73?) = his first theodicy; *De summa rerum* papers (1675–76) with early responses to Spinoza. [source: IEP-Ov §1; SEP-Ev §2]
- Tschirnhaus told him much of the contents of Spinoza's *Ethics*. [source: IEP-Ov §1]

### Meeting Spinoza, November 1676
- On the way to Hanover he met Spinoza **18–21 November 1676**, about three months before Spinoza's death; by Leibniz's notes they discussed the unpublished *Ethics*, Cartesian physics and Leibniz's improved ontological argument (*That a Most Perfect Being Exists*, 1676). [source: SEP-L §1, §7.1.1]
- PLACE: IEP-Ov and WP-GWL say **The Hague** (where Spinoza lived; WP adds he also met van Leeuwenhoek there). SEP-L says he "stopped in Amsterdam to meet with Spinoza". Use **The Hague**; the Spinoza notes (WP-Baruch_Spinoza) also say The Hague. [source: IEP-Ov §1; WP-GWL; SEP-L §1]

### Hanover 1676–1716
- Took post as librarian to Duke Johann Friedrich of Brunswick in Hanover (end of 1676), also consulting on Harz mining engineering (IEP). Served Johann Friedrich (d. 1680), Ernst August (1680–98), and Georg Ludwig (from 1714 George I of Great Britain). Privy Counselor of Justice from 1677 (WP). Ernst August commissioned a history of the House of Brunswick, never finished. Librarian of the Herzog August Library, Wolfenbüttel, from 1691 (WP). [source: SEP-L §1; IEP-Ov §1; WP-GWL]
- Italy trip 1689 researching the Guelf ancestry (IEP; WP: travels 1687–90 through Germany, Austria, Italy). [source: IEP-Ov §1; WP-GWL]
- Correspondence with over 1,100 people (SEP); WP (Bodemann catalogue 1895): about 15,000 letters to more than 1,000 recipients and more than 40,000 other items. Akademie edition still incomplete (SEP 2013: philosophical writings only to 1690). [source: SEP-L §1–2; WP-GWL]
- Correspondents: Arnauld (from 1686), De Volder (1699–), Des Bosses (1706–), Clarke (1715–16), Johann Bernoulli, Christian Wolff, Sophie and Sophie Charlotte. [source: SEP-L §1.1; IEP-Ov §1]
- **Berlin Academy**: Electoral Brandenburg Society of Sciences founded in Berlin **11 July 1700** by Elector Frederick III on Leibniz's advice; Leibniz first president (for life, WP-GWL) and drafted its statutes. Sophie Charlotte "inspired the foundation" (WP). Paris Académie admitted him as a foreign member in 1700. [source: WP-Prussian_Academy; WP-GWL; WP-Sophia_Charlotte]
- 1712–14 in Vienna (Imperial Court Councillor). Monadology and Principles of Nature and Grace written there (WP-Monadology: last stay in Vienna 1712–Sept 1714). [source: WP-GWL; WP-Monadology]
- When Georg Ludwig became George I (1714), Leibniz was told to stay in Hanover (SEP: because of the acrimony in England; WP adds the King wanted the Brunswick history finished first). Mocked at court for his wig and old-fashioned clothes (SEP). Electress Sophie died 1714. Met Peter the Great at Bad Pyrmont 1716; adviser to the Tsar from 1711 (WP). [source: SEP-L §1; WP-GWL]
- **Died 14 November 1716** in Hanover (IEP: after gout and arthritis). [source: SEP-L §1; IEP-Ov §1]
- **"Died neglected" — verified (WP only):** buried in the Neustädter Kirche; neither George I (who was near Hanover) nor any courtier except his personal secretary attended the funeral; neither the Royal Society nor the Berlin Academy honoured his death; grave unmarked for more than 50 years; Fontenelle gave a eulogy at the Paris Académie. Never married. [source: WP-GWL]
- Reputation recovered with the 1765 publication of the *Nouveaux Essais*; Dutens edition 1768. [source: WP-GWL]

### Sophie, Sophie Charlotte, Caroline
- Close to Electress Sophie of Hanover (1630–1714), wife of Ernst August, youngest sister of Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia (Descartes's correspondent), daughter of Elizabeth Stuart. Friendship 1676–1714 (WP-Sophia). [source: SEP-L §1; WP-Sophia_of_Hanover]
- Sophie Charlotte (30 Oct 1668 – 1 Feb 1705), Sophie's daughter, first Queen in Prussia (1701), "his avowed disciple" (WP). Conversations at the Prussian court with Sophie Charlotte and others about Bayle's *Dictionnaire* (2nd ed. 1702) were the direct impetus for the *Theodicy* (SEP-Ev, citing Antognazza 2009). [source: WP-Sophia_Charlotte; WP-GWL; SEP-Ev §3 intro]
- IEP: Sophie and Sophie Charlotte "encouraged, and in many ways made possible, Leibniz's philosophical pursuits while employed at the court." [source: IEP-Ov §1]
- Caroline of Ansbach (Princess of Wales) was also a correspondent and friend; the Clarke correspondence ran through her. [source: WP-GWL; WP-Leibniz–Clarke; SEP-Phys §1]
- Translation available: Lloyd Strickland (ed.), *Leibniz and the Two Sophies: The Philosophical Correspondence* (Iter Press, Toronto, 2011). [source: WP-GWL bibliography]

### Damaris Masham
- Damaris Cudworth Masham (1659–1708), daughter of Ralph Cudworth, friend of Locke. Corresponded with Leibniz "between 1704 and 1705–6", at his instigation, perhaps because he hoped for contact with Locke (SEP); Gerhardt's edition titles it "Briefwechsel zwischen Leibniz und Lady Masham. 1703–1705" (WP) — DATES DIFFER, say "c. 1703/4–1705". Leibniz wrote in French, she in English. [source: SEP-Masham §4; WP-Damaris_Cudworth_Masham]
- She knew his philosophy only indirectly (the 1695 *Journal des Sçavans* New System; Bayle's "Rorarius" article) and "takes issue with Leibniz on a number of points, including his theory of pre-established harmony, the nature of substance and free will"; they also discussed her father Cudworth's philosophy. [source: SEP-Masham §4]
- Scholarship: Pauline Phemister 2007 on the principle of uniformity in the Leibniz–Masham letters; Robert C. Sleigh 2005 "Reflections on the Masham–Leibniz correspondence". [source: SEP-Masham bibliography]
- Cross-ref: chapter 31 (Women Philosophers of the Early Modern Era).

### Calculus priority dispute
- Newton: fluxions from 1666 (October 1666 manuscript); *De analysi* 1669; fluxional notation not printed until 1693 (partial) and 1704. Leibniz began 1674, first paper **"Nova Methodus pro Maximis et Minimis" (1684)**, in the *Acta Eruditorum*. Leibniz used his notation in a 1677 letter to Newton. [source: WP-Leibniz–Newton; IEP-Ov §1 (Acta Eruditorum essays)]
- 1699: Nicolas Fatio de Duillier publicly accused Leibniz of plagiarism (had done so privately to Huygens in 1692). 1704: anonymous review (by Leibniz) of Newton's quadrature tract implied Newton borrowed from Leibniz. 1708: John Keill, in the Royal Society's journal, accused Leibniz of plagiarism. [source: WP-Leibniz–Newton; WP-GWL]
- Leibniz demanded a retraction; the Royal Society (Newton president) set up a committee that never asked for Leibniz's side; Newton was an unacknowledged participant and wrote the report. Committee reported **24 April 1712** (DNB via WS-6); printed as the *Commercium Epistolicum* — dated "1712" (EB1911, WP in one place) or "early in 1713" (WP elsewhere); say "the Society's 1712 report, printed as the *Commercium Epistolicum* (1712/13)". Leibniz saw it only in autumn 1714. Newton's anonymous "Account" of it appeared in the *Philosophical Transactions* (Jan–Feb 1714/15 Julian, i.e. 1715). [source: WP-Leibniz–Newton; WP-GWL; WS-6]
- Present consensus: independent invention, Newton first, Leibniz first to publish. SEP: "Most historians of mathematics now claim that Newton and Leibniz developed their ideas independently: Newton developing the ideas first with Leibniz the first to publish." IEP: "Leibniz has been cleared of the charges". Caveat: WP notes Leibniz on several occasions backdated and altered manuscripts, which hurt him in the dispute; he had copied extracts from a Newton manuscript at some point (timing uncertain). [source: SEP-L §1; IEP-Ov §1; WP-Leibniz–Newton; WP-GWL]
- Consistent with the published History chapter *Scientific Revolution* ("discovered independently, leading to a bitter dispute over priority").

## 2. Key texts (dates)
- *Meditations on Knowledge, Truth, and Ideas* 1684 (first mature publication); *Discourse on Metaphysics* **1686** (unpublished in his life; written "while it snowed in the Harz", "a little discourse on metaphysics"; he sent Arnauld a summary, starting their correspondence); *Primary Truths* (redated by the Akademie editors from 1686 to **1689** by watermark); *New System of Nature* **1695** (first public presentation of pre-established harmony, *Journal des Sçavans*); *Specimen Dynamicum* 1695; *On the Ultimate Origination of Things* 1697; *On Nature Itself* 1698; *New Essays* (begun 1703, finished **1704**, published **1765**; withheld after Locke's death in 1704); *Theodicy* **1710**; *Monadology* **1714**; *Principles of Nature and Grace* **1714**. [source: SEP-L §1.1, §2; IEP-Ov §1; WP-GWL; SEP-Masham §4 (Journal des Sçavans 1695)]
- *Monadology*: the title is not Leibniz's; German translation 1720 (*Lehrsätze über die Monadologie*, by Wolff's circle), Latin 1721 in *Acta Eruditorum*. *Principles of Nature and Grace* was intended for Prince Eugene of Savoy and appeared in French after his death. [source: WP-Monadology]
- *Theodicy*: full title *Essais de Théodicée sur la bonté de Dieu, la liberté de l'homme et l'origine du mal*; published by Isaac Troyel, Amsterdam, 1710; written in French for a wide audience; **published anonymously**, authorship claimed only in the 2nd edition (1712); Parts I–III plus "Preliminary Dissertation on the Conformity of Faith with Reason" and appendices (incl. on Hobbes and on King's *Origin of Evil*); Latin summary *Causa Dei* added to 2nd edition. Leibniz classed it as **exoteric** (popular). [source: SEP-Ev §3 intro; SEP-Exo intro]
- **"Only book published in his life?" — SOURCES DIFFER.** IEP-Ov: Theodicy is "the only book Leibniz published during his lifetime". SEP-Exo: "Only a small number of his essays and one philosophical book, the *Essais de théodicée*, were published during his lifetime." SEP-Ev: "the most substantial philosophical work that Leibniz published during his lifetime". WP-GWL: "only two philosophical books: *De Arte Combinatoria* and *Théodicée*" (plus many pamphlets; and he edited the *Novissima Sinica*, 1697). WRITER: say "the only full-length philosophical book he published in his lifetime (his youthful *Art of Combinations* of 1666 was a dissertation)". [sources as named]
- No magnum opus; his philosophy is pieced together from essays, letters and unpublished papers; he was "always very aware ... of the audience". [source: SEP-L §2; IEP-Ov §1]

## 3. Principles
- *Monadology* §§31–32: "Our reasonings are based on two great principles, that of contradiction ... [and] that of sufficient reason" (AG 217). [source: SEP-L §3]
- **Principle of contradiction (PC)**: "a proposition cannot be true and false at the same time, and that therefore A is A and cannot be not A" (G VI 355/AG 321). [source: SEP-L §3.3]
- **PSR** (*nihil est sine ratione*): "one of the greatest and most fruitful of all human knowledge" (L 227). Monadology §32 (IEP wording): "we can find no true or existent fact, no true assertion, without there being a sufficient reason why it is thus and not otherwise, although most of these reasons cannot be known to us". Leibniz derives PSR from the predicate-in-notion principle. [source: SEP-L §3.4; IEP-Ov §2]
- **Predicate-in-notion (PIN)**: to Arnauld: "in every true affirmative proposition ... the notion of the predicate is in some way included in that of the subject. *Praedicatum inest subjecto*; otherwise I do not know what truth is" (G II 56/L 337). [source: SEP-L §3.2]
- **Identity of indiscernibles (PII)**: "it is not true that two substances can resemble each other completely and differ only in number [*solo numero*]" (AG 42). Differences of place/time alone do not count; extrinsic differences must rest on intrinsic ones. In *Primary Truths* and the Clarke letters Leibniz derives PII from PC + PSR. Used against blank-slate minds, atomism and absolute space. "Leibniz's Law" (= PII + indiscernibility of identicals). [source: SEP-L §3.5]
  - PII argument (SEP-L): (1) suppose indiscernibles a, b in world W; (2) then there is a world W* with a and b switched; (3) God could have no reason to choose W over W*; (4) God must have a reason (PSR); (5) so there are no two indiscernibles.
- **Continuity**: "nature never makes leaps" (Preface, New Essays, RB 56). [source: SEP-L §3.6]
- **Principle of the Best**: God always acts for the best; in creation the PSR is "essentially the Principle of the Best" (SEP). [source: SEP-L §3.1]
- "Two labyrinths": freedom and the composition of the continuum, both arising "from the same source, infinity" (*On Contingency*, AG 95; Theodicy preface G VI 29/H 53). [source: SEP-Mod §4; SEP-L §3.6]

## 4. Substance and monads
- Complete individual concept (*Discourse* §8): the nature of an individual substance is "to have a notion so complete that it is sufficient to contain and to allow us to deduce from it all the predicates of the subject" (AG 41). Example: Alexander the Great; "from all time in Alexander's soul there are vestiges of everything that has happened to him and marks of everything that will happen to him and even traces of everything that happens in the universe, even though God alone could recognize them all" (AG 41). §9 consequences: PII; substances begin only by creation and end only by annihilation; indivisible; each "like a complete world and like a mirror of God or of the whole universe". [source: SEP-L §4.1]
- Unity: "what is not truly *one* being is not truly one *being* either" (to Arnauld, G II 97/AG 86). Bodies are aggregates; rainbow analogy; "well-founded phenomena". [source: SEP-L §4.2]
- Activity: "A Substance is a being capable of action" (PNG §1, AG 207). [source: SEP-L §4.3]
- Monads: the only true substances are mind-like simple substances with **perception** and **appetition** (the internal tendency to pass from perception to perception). Perception = "the representation in the simple of the compound, or of that which is outside" (PNG §2); Monadology §14 (IEP trans.): "the passing state which involves and represents a multitude in the unity". [source: SEP-L §4.3, §5.1; SEP-Mind §3; IEP-Mind §3]
- **Windowless**, *Monadology* §7 (AG trans.): "The monads have no windows through which something can enter or leave." Already in *Discourse* §26 (1686): "we have a bad habit of thinking of our soul as if it received certain species as messengers and as if it has doors and windows". [source: SEP-Mind; SEP-Caus §2]
- Hierarchy: bare monads, souls (distinct perception + memory: animals), minds (reason, apperception). PNG §4: perception vs apperception, "which is consciousness, or the reflective knowledge of this internal state, something not given to all souls, nor at all times to a given soul" (AG 208). Dog-and-stick example; humans act like animals "in three fourths of their actions". Anti-Cartesian: animals are not mere machines. [source: SEP-L §6.4]
- Panorganicism: Monadology §70 every living body has a dominant entelechy; "all of nature is full of life" (PNG §1). [source: SEP-L §5.2]
- **Petites perceptions**: "at every moment there is in us an infinity of perceptions, unaccompanied by awareness or reflection" (New Essays Preface, RB 53); roar of the sea made of each wave's noise (RB 54); the mind is never without perceptions, even in dreamless sleep — contrast with Descartes's transparent mind. [source: SEP-L §6.5]
- Innate ideas: marble with veins (New Essays Preface); "Does the soul have windows? Is it similar to writing-tablets, or like wax?" (RB 110). Allies himself with Plato against Locke's tabula rasa. [source: SEP-L §6.3]
- Debate: was the middle-period Leibniz (Discourse/Arnauld) a hylomorphist about corporeal substances, or always an idealist about monads? "This has been the subject of debate in the field" (Look 2010; Garber 2009). [source: SEP-L §4.2]

## 5. Pre-established harmony (vs interaction and occasionalism)
- Problem inherited from Descartes (how thinking and extended substance interact; pineal gland). Leibniz denies all real causal interaction between created substances. [source: SEP-L §4.4]
- **Clocks/pendula analogy**: Postscript of a letter to Basnage de Beauval (1696; G IV 498–500/AG 147–49): three ways two clocks (SEP: "two pendula hanging from a beam") could agree: (a) influence/causal link (influx), (b) a craftsman constantly adjusting them (occasionalism), (c) built so perfectly they agree by their own natures (pre-established harmony). IEP-Mind words it as "two pendulum clocks". [source: SEP-L §4.4; IEP-Mind §4]
- New System (1695) five steps (AG 143–44): (1) "[T]here is no real influence of one created substance on another"; (2) God created the soul so that "everything must arise for it from its own depths, through perfect spontaneity relative to itself, and yet with a perfect conformity relative to external things"; (3) each substance represents the universe "as if in a world apart, and as if there existed only God and itself"; (4) the body acts by the laws of the corporeal machine when the soul wills; (5) "this mutual relation, regulated in advance in each substance ... alone brings about the union of soul and body". [source: SEP-L §4.4]
- Against influx: accidents cannot "stroll about outside of substances, as the Scholastics' sensible species used to" (Monadology §7, WF trans.); called Suárez's "influx" a "barbaric expression" (1670, L 126). [source: SEP-Caus §2]
- Against occasionalism: it makes God perform a "perpetual miracle", unworthy of God (Explanation of Bayle's Difficulties §7, WF 205; Theodicy §61); and attributing all activity to God leads to Spinozism. Early Leibniz (1668–76) held a limited "physical occasionalism" (Henkel; Garber). [source: SEP-Caus §3; IEP-Mind §4]
- Monadology §§79, 81: "Souls act according to the laws of final causes ... Bodies act according to the laws of efficient causes ... these two kingdoms ... are in harmony"; "bodies act as if there were no souls ... souls act as if there were no bodies; and both act as if each influenced the other" (AG 223). Kingdom of nature / kingdom of grace (§87). [source: SEP-L §4.5]
- Wolff's endorsement of pre-established harmony was used against him: the Prussian king was allegedly told it excused deserters (their acts pre-established); 8 Nov 1723 edict expelled Wolff from Halle within 48 hours "on pain of hanging". [source: SEP-Wolff §1]

## 6. The mill argument (Monadology §17) — ARGUMENT
- Text (AG 215, as in SEP-Mind): "One is obliged to admit that perception and what depends upon it is inexplicable on mechanical principles, that is, by figures and motions. In imagining that there is a machine whose construction would enable it to think, to sense, and to have perception, one could conceive it enlarged while retaining the same proportions, so that one could enter into it, just like into a windmill. Supposing this, one should, when visiting within it, find only parts pushing one another, and never anything by which to explain a perception. Thus it is in the simple substance, and not in the composite or in the machine, that one must look for perception." [source: SEP-Mind §1]
- Reconstruction (SEP-Mind; IEP-Mind §3): (1) Materialism says matter can explain/give rise to perception. (2) A perception represents a variety of content in a true unity (a single "I"). (3) What is not a true unity cannot perceive. (4) Whatever is divisible is not a true unity. (5) Matter is infinitely divisible. (6) So matter cannot perceive; perception belongs to simple substances. Mirror example (IEP): a mirror represents my body only part by part, without unity. [source: SEP-Mind §1; IEP-Mind §3]
- Supporting texts: New System 1695 "a true unity which corresponds to what is called the *I* in us; such a thing could not occur in artificial machines" (WF 16); Reply to Bayle 1702 "the consciousness which is in us of this I" (WF 113). [source: SEP-Mind §1]
- Objection/assessment: "considerable disagreement among recent scholars about the correct interpretation" (IEP); the argument relies on Leibniz's views of perception and matter; IEP links it to today's "hard problem of consciousness"/explanatory gap and to the AI question. Lodge & Bobro 1998; Lodge 2014. [source: IEP-Mind §3; SEP-Mind §1]
- Cross-ref: Hobbes chapter (materialism), *Minds and Machines*, *Mind–Body Problem*; *What Is Philosophy?* notes list "Leibniz's mill" among standard thought experiments.

## 7. Complete concepts, contingency and freedom
- Threat: if every predicate (including future actions) is in my concept, my acts seem necessary. Leibniz's reply has three parts:
  1. **Contingent vs necessary truths**: necessary = contrary implies a contradiction, reducible to identities by finite analysis (e.g. 2+2=4); contingent = analysis "to infinity through reasons for reasons, so that one never has a complete demonstration ... understood completely by God, who alone traverses the infinite series in one stroke of mind" (*On Contingency*, AG 28). Analogy: commensurable vs incommensurable ratios (side and diagonal of a square) (AG 97). [source: SEP-Mod §2]
  2. **Hypothetical necessity / certain but not necessary**: *Discourse* §13 — the one whose contrary implies a contradiction "is absolutely necessary"; the other "is necessary only *ex hypothesi* and, so to speak, accidentally, but it is contingent in itself" (AG 45). Caesar crossing the Rubicon is "contingent, even though it is certain ... it is not its impossibility but its imperfection which causes [the alternative] to be rejected. And nothing is necessary whose contrary is possible" (AG 46). God "inclines our souls without necessitating them". [source: SEP-Mod §2, §4]
  3. Freedom = spontaneity (action flows from one's own nature) + choice of the apparent best; a distinctive compatibilism. [source: SEP-Mod §4]
- Possible worlds helped him escape Spinoza: "the consideration of possibles, which are not, were not, and will not be, brought me back from this precipice" (*On Freedom*, c. 1689, AG 94). [source: SEP-Mod §2]
- **Arnauld's objection** (letters, on the *Discourse* summaries): "Since it is impossible that I should not always have remained myself, whether I had married or lived in celibacy, the individual concept of myself contained neither of these two states" (G II 30). Leibniz's reply: speak not of a "vague Adam" but of possible Adams; had other events happened "he would not have been our Adam, but another Adam" (G II 41–42/AG 72–73). [source: SEP-Mod §3]
- Debate: superessentialism (every property essential; Look's reading) vs counterpart readings (other possible worlds contain counterparts of Adam/Caesar). "Just how strong this essentialism is has been the subject of debate". [source: SEP-Mod §3]
- Debate: did Leibniz anticipate possible-worlds semantics? SEP-Mod: he had "all the tools" but "did not, or, at least, ... not ... explicitly"; his account is "translatable" into it. [source: SEP-Mod §2]
- Kant cross-ref: Kant adopts Leibniz's definition of necessary (identity-reducible) truth as "analytic" but denies arithmetic is analytic; SEP: "the majority of jurors have ... returned a verdict in favor of Leibniz's classificatory scheme". [source: SEP-Mod §2]

## 8. God
- Ontological argument: Descartes's version is faulty because it must first be shown that a most perfect being is possible; perfections are simple, positive qualities, so cannot be shown incompatible (*Quod ens perfectissimum existit*, 1676); "If a necessary being is possible, it follows that it exists actually" — SEP likens this to the S5 axiom ◊□p → □p and to Gödel's argument. Shared with Spinoza in 1676. [source: SEP-L §7.1.1; IEP-Ov]
- Cosmological argument from PSR: the reason for the whole series of contingent things lies outside it (Monadology §§36–37); Theodicy §7 derives understanding, will, power, infinity and uniqueness. [source: SEP-L §7.1.2]
- "Why is there something rather than nothing" not checked here.

## 9. Best of all possible worlds and the problem of evil — ARGUMENT
- Reconstruction (SEP-L §7.2): (1) God is omnipotent, omniscient, benevolent, free creator. (2) There are other possible worlds. (3) Suppose this world is not the best. (4) Then God lacked power, or foreknowledge, or will to the best, or did not create, or there were no alternatives. (5) Each contradicts (1) or (2). (6) So this is the best possible world. SEP calls this "the Christian retort to the Epicurean argument against theism".
- Theodicy version (SEP-Ev §3.1): wisdom finds the best, goodness "morally necessitates" choosing it; "a lesser good is a kind of evil" and "if there were not the best (*optimum*) among all possible worlds, God would not have produced any" (H 131, 252–53). The world is the whole series ("I call 'World' the whole succession and the whole agglomeration of all existent things", H 131).
- Criteria of "best": "the happiness of minds is the principal aim of God" (AG 38); the most perfect world is "at the same time the simplest in hypotheses and the richest in phenomena" (AG 39). [source: SEP-L §7.2]
- Three kinds of evil (Theodicy): metaphysical (imperfection/limitation of creatures — "the root of evil is in nothingness, that is to say, in the privation, or limitation of creatures", Discourse, AG 62), physical (suffering), moral (sin). [source: SEP-Ev §3.1]
- Replies to "the world could be better": (a) everything is connected; to save other men God "must needs choose an altogether different sequence, seeing that all is connected in each sequence" (H 171); (b) evils contribute to the whole: "A little acid, sharpness or bitterness is often more pleasing than sugar; shadows enhance colors; and even a dissonance in the right place gives relief to harmony" (H 133) — Platonic/Augustinian/Thomist tradition (Aquinas ST I.25.6 ad 3, harp string); (c) we see only a small part (other planets may be peopled by happy beings, H 138); (d) good is unbounded, evil bounded: "God is infinite, and the Devil is finite" (H 381). [source: SEP-Ev §3.1]
- Author-of-sin problem: answered by antecedent vs consequent will, moral (not metaphysical) necessity, God's concurrence with the positive in action while sin is a privation (deficient cause). [source: SEP-Ev §3.2]
- Early (Confessio, 1672–73) vs mature theodicy: early Leibniz came close to saying evil follows necessarily from God's nature; he later saw this threatened contingency and freedom. [source: SEP-Ev §1–2]
- Objections: (i) maybe there is no best world, only ever-better ones (R. M. Adams 1972, "Must God Create the Best?"); Leibniz's reply: then God would not have created at all, yet the world exists. (ii) Necessitarianism: if God necessarily chooses the best, is this world necessary? Leibniz: moral not metaphysical necessity. (iii) Candide-style: this world is visibly not best — Leibniz never claimed everything is good locally. [source: SEP-Ev §3.1, §3.2.1; SEP-L §7.2]
- **Bayle**: Pierre Bayle's *Dictionnaire historique et critique* (1st ed. 1697 with "Rorarius" article on Leibniz; 2nd ed. 1702) prompted the Theodicy; Parts II–III are a running dialogue with Bayle; Leibniz defends conformity of faith and reason against Bayle's fideism/scepticism. [source: SEP-Ev §3 intro; IEP-Ov §1; SEP-Masham §4]
- **"Theodicy"** coined by Leibniz from Greek *theos* + *dikē*, "the doctrine of the right and justice of God"; first used in French and Latin forms in letters in the mid-1690s. [source: SEP-Ev §3 intro; WP-Theodicy]
- **Voltaire's *Candide, ou l'Optimisme*** (published January 1759, by c. 15 Jan, in several countries): Pangloss caricatures Leibnizian optimism ("all is for the best in the best of all possible worlds", *Tout est pour le mieux dans le meilleur des mondes possibles*); ends with "we must cultivate our garden"; Martin modelled on Bayle. [source: WP-Candide; SEP-Ev intro; SEP-L §7]
- **Lisbon earthquake: 1 November 1755** (All Saints' Day), c. 09:40; Lisbon deaths estimated 30,000–40,000 (infobox 40,000–50,000 total incl. Morocco). Voltaire attacked optimism in *Candide* and in his *Poème sur le désastre de Lisbonne*; Kant published three texts on the earthquake in 1756. Adorno: "The earthquake of Lisbon sufficed to cure Voltaire of the theodicy of Leibniz" (*Negative Dialectics*). Note: the optimism Voltaire attacked had passed through Wolff and Pope; WP-Candide says he "assaults Leibniz". [source: WP-1755_Lisbon_earthquake; WP-Candide]
- Voltaire's jibe: "Can you really believe that a drop of urine is an infinity of monads, and that each of these has ideas, however obscure, of the universe as a whole?" (*Oeuvres complètes* vol. 22, p. 434). [source: SEP-L §5.1]
- The Theodicy "pleased excellent theologians of all three main confessions" (Leibniz, per Antognazza 2009). Cautious, exoteric: did not air his most controversial metaphysics (Antognazza). [source: SEP-Ev intro, §3.1]

## 10. Leibniz–Clarke correspondence 1715–16
- Began when Caroline, Princess of Wales, passed to Samuel Clarke (1675–1729) a letter (late 1715) in which Leibniz decried the decline of natural religion in England under Newton's philosophy; set against the calculus dispute; Newton assumed to stand behind Clarke. Five letters from Leibniz, five replies from Clarke; ended by Leibniz's death in November 1716, Clarke "having the last word". Clarke published them in 1717 (with his translation from French). [source: SEP-Phys §1, §5; WP-Leibniz–Clarke]
- **God's intervention** (First Paper, Nov 1715, Clarke 1717 ed. via Newton Project): Newton's followers hold that "God Almighty wants to wind up his Watch from Time to Time: Otherwise it would cease to move", the machine being "so imperfect" that God must "clean it now and then ... and even to mend it, as a Clockmaker mends his Work". Clarke: Leibniz alludes to *Opticks* Query 31 (system "needs a reformation"). [source: WS-5 (newtonproject.ox.ac.uk THEM00226)] — exact wording beyond these fragments NOT checked.
- Space as God's sensorium: "if God stands in need of any organ to perceive things by, it will follow, that they do not depend altogether upon him" (First Paper §3, Alexander 11). [source: SEP-Phys §5.1]
- Clarke's absolute space/time: prior to bodies; bodies are in them; not really divisible; identified with God's immensity and eternity. [source: SEP-Phys §5.1]
- Leibniz's three attacks: (1) theological (space with parts would put parts in God's essence, Fifth Paper §43); (2) **PSR**: no reason for God to place the world in absolute space one way rather than shifted/rotated, or to create it at one time rather than another — "A mere will without any motive, is a fiction" (Fourth Paper §2); (3) **PII**: "To suppose two things indiscernible, is to suppose the same thing under two names" (Fourth Paper §6). [source: SEP-Phys §5.1]
- Relationalism: "I hold space to be something merely relative, as time is, that I hold it to be an order of coexistences, as time is an order of successions" (Third Paper §4; Alexander 25–26); family-tree analogy (Fifth Paper). Space and time ideal, "beings of reason". [source: SEP-Phys §5.2]
- Also at issue: free will, and whether God must create the best (WP); Clarke defended a God who can choose without a reason. [source: WP-Leibniz–Clarke]
- WP: the correspondence was valued because "Leibniz had published only one book on moral matters, the *Théodicée* (1710)". [source: WP-Leibniz–Clarke]
- Physics background: vis viva (mv²) vs Cartesian quantity of motion; *Specimen Dynamicum* 1695 written partly in response to Newton's *Principia*. [source: SEP-Phys §1; IEP-Ov §1]

## 11. Logic: characteristica universalis, calculus ratiocinator
- *De arte combinatoria* (1666) already planned a universal characteristic and logical calculus. Names he used: *characteristica universalis*, alphabet of human thoughts, *calculus philosophicus*, *calculus ratiocinator*, *spécieuse générale*; precursors Llull's *ars magna*; contrasted with Dalgarno (1661) and Wilkins (1668). [source: SEP-L §1; SEP-Log §4]
- April 1679 papers assign numbers to concepts (animal = 2, rational = 3, man = 6) to test syllogisms. Universal calculus developed c. 1679–1690; his algebra of concepts is "deductively equivalent to the Boolean algebra of sets" (Lenzen); *Generales Inquisitiones* (1686). [source: IEP-Log]
- Published almost nothing on logic; fragments hidden in Hanover until Couturat (*La logique de Leibniz*, 1901; *Opuscules et fragments inédits*, 1903). Russell, *A Critical Exposition of the Philosophy of Leibniz* (1900). Frege's *Begriffsschrift* (1879) claimed to be a *lingua characteristica* in Leibniz's sense, not a mere *calculus ratiocinator*. [source: IEP-Log; SEP-Log]
- "Let us calculate": "when there are disputes among persons, we can simply say: Let us calculate, without further ado, and see who is right" (*The Art of Discovery*, 1685; C 176/W 51, Wiener trans.). [source: SEP-Mind §6] (WP flags its own blockquote of this as possibly mis-typeset; the SEP citation is solid.)
- Frege on Leibniz: "in his writings, Leibniz threw out such a profusion of seeds of ideas that in this respect he is virtually in a class of his own". [source: SEP-L intro]
- WP-GWL (citing Lenzen 2004): Leibniz "has been noted as one of the most important logicians between the times of Aristotle and Gottlob Frege". [source: WP-GWL]

## 12. Binary arithmetic and the *Yijing* (I Ching) — handle carefully
- Leibniz worked out binary independently and before any Chinese contact: first dedicated work *On the Binary Progression* (**1679**), with conversion and the four operations; over a hundred manuscripts on binary, mostly unpublished. Thomas Harriot had earlier investigated binary (unpublished); Juan Caramuel published in 1700 (possibly first in Europe). [source: WP-Binary_number]
- He sent his binary method to the Jesuit **Joachim Bouvet** (1656–1730; in China from 1688 teaching the Kangxi Emperor mathematics; in France 1697–98, when he began corresponding with Leibniz). Bouvet replied (letter of **14 November 1701**, per Leibniz's own text) with the hexagram diagram ("Fuxi" order, going up to 64), recognising that the hexagrams correspond to binary 000000–111111. [source: WP-Binary_number; WP-Joachim_Bouvet; WP-GWL]
- "Explication de l'arithmétique binaire, qui se sert des seuls caractères 0 et 1, avec des remarques sur son utilité, et sur ce qu'elle donne le sens des anciennes figures chinoises de Fohy" — published **1703** (Paris Académie *Mémoires*). In it Leibniz claims the Chinese had "lost the meaning" of Fuxi's figures and that he and Bouvet had "deciphered the enigma of Fuxi". [source: WP-Binary_number (quoting Strickland's translation); WP-GWL timeline]
- The arrangement Bouvet sent derives from the Song scholar **Shao Yong** (1011–1077), "although he did not intend his arrangement to be used mathematically". [source: WP-Binary_number]
- Leibniz hoped binary (creation from 1 = God and 0 = nothing) could help convert the Chinese emperor. [source: WP-GWL]
- WRITER: correct framing = binary came first (1679), the *Yijing* parallel came via Bouvet (1701) and was published 1703. Do NOT say the *I Ching* inspired his binary.
- SEP-CompPhil: Leibniz "was greatly struck" by the *Book of Changes*' broken/unbroken lines, "which he believed to bear uncanny resemblance to his invention of binary arithmetic". [source: SEP-CompPhil]

## 13. China
- Leibniz "was perhaps the first major European intellectual to take a close interest in Chinese civilization" (WP); knew China through Jesuit missionaries; read *Confucius Sinarum Philosophus* (1687) in its first year; wondered whether Chinese characters were a form of his universal characteristic. [source: WP-GWL]
- European praise: Leibniz praised Confucius "for his discovery of universal natural laws through reason". [source: SEP-Conf]
- ***Novissima Sinica*** (*Latest News from China*), **1697**: a collection of missionary documents with his preface (some sources date the preface 1697/99). In the preface Leibniz judges China superior in practical philosophy/ethics and political organisation, Europe in theoretical sciences and mathematics, and proposes that, given Europe's moral decline, Chinese missionaries might be sent to Europe to teach natural theology/religion. [source: WS-2, WS-3 — summaries of scholarly articles; exact wording NOT VERIFIED]
- ***Discourse on the Natural Theology of the Chinese*** (1716): a letter to **Nicolas-François Rémond** (*Lettre sur la philosophie chinoise*), unfinished at his death; rebuts the anti-accommodationist treatises of the Jesuit **Niccolò Longobardi** (French ed. 1701) and the Franciscan **Antonio de Santa María Caballero** (1668), which Rémond sent him at the end of 1715; defends the Jesuit accommodation policy (Chinese Rites controversy). He argued against Longobardi that Chinese *li* (理, principle/pattern) is the "sovereign substance which we revere under the name of God" — i.e. that ancient Chinese thought contained a natural theology. English trans. Henry Rosemont Jr. & Daniel J. Cook (1977; *Writings on China*, Open Court, 1994). [source: WS-1, WS-4; WP-GWL bibliography]
- Debate: E. R. Hughes suggested Confucian influence on "simple substance" and pre-established harmony (WP); Joseph Needham wrote "Chu Hsi, Leibniz, and the Philosophy of Organism" (1956) (SEP-Zhu Xi bibliography). Whether Leibniz read *li* accurately (Zhu Xi's *li* is not a personal creator) is debated; Perkins (2004) is the standard study; Malebranche by contrast read *li* as material/atheistic (WS-4). [source: WP-GWL; https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/zhu-xi/ bibliography; WS-4]
- Wolff's 1721 Halle rectoral address praising Confucian ethics as compatible with virtue without revelation escalated the Pietist campaign that got him expelled (1723). [source: SEP-Wolff §1]
- Cross-ref: published *Chinese Buddhism and the Rise of Neo-Confucianism* uses *li* = "principle" or "pattern", Zhu Xi's "philosophy of pattern (*li*) and vital stuff (*qi*)" — consistent.

## 14. Ethics and law (brief)
- Doctor of law; justice defined as "the charity of the wise" (*caritas sapientis*). [source: SEP-Eth; IEP-Law]
- Exoteric vs esoteric (acroamatic) writings: Leibniz himself classified the *Theodicy* as exoteric; affects how much weight popular texts get. [source: SEP-Exo]

## 15. Legacy
- The "Leibnizian-Wolffian philosophy": a label "apparently unwittingly coined by his own student" and seized on by Pietist opponents; Wolff (1679–1754) rejected it, said his philosophy "begins only where Leibniz's ends", and gave little place to monads and pre-established harmony. Dominated German universities in the first half of the 18th century. Kant called Wolff "the greatest of all dogmatic philosophers" (CPR B xxxvi). [source: SEP-Wolff intro, §2]
- Wolff could know little of Leibniz's philosophy from published texts (Monadology 1720, New Essays 1765); the Clarke letters were a key public source. [source: SEP-Wolff §2]
- Russell: the *Monadology* "a kind of fantastic fairy tale, coherent perhaps, but wholly arbitrary" (Preface to his 1900 book). Diderot's *Encyclopédie* praise ("Perhaps never has a man read as much..."). [source: SEP-L §5.1, intro]
- Norbert Wiener (1961) suggested Leibniz as patron saint of cybernetics. [source: WP-GWL]
- Kant (cross-ref ch. 33): adopts/attacks Leibniz–Wolff rationalism; analytic/synthetic (see §7 above). Kant's specific *Critique* criticisms of Leibniz (Amphiboly) were NOT checked this session.
