## The Metaphysical Club and the name "pragmatism"
- The Metaphysical Club was a conversational philosophical club in Cambridge, Massachusetts, formed (per Menand 2001, p. 201) in January 1872 and dissolved in December 1872; members: Peirce, Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. (future Supreme Court Justice), William James, Chauncey Wright, John Fiske, Francis Ellingwood Abbot, Nicholas St. John Green, Joseph Bangs Warner [source: WP The_Metaphysical_Club]. Dating varies by source: SEP-P "around 1870"; IEP-PR "early 1870s", "a group of a dozen Harvard-educated men"; IEP-CSP says Peirce began the club "in 1872". Safest phrasing: early 1870s (1872).
- The name is known only from Peirce, in an unpublished paper over thirty years later (c. 1906, "Historical Affinities and Genesis", CP 5.11–13; first printed 1929 in *The Hound and Horn*); he said it was chosen "half-ironically, half-defiantly". There is "no record of a club of this name" in the writings of any other member, though Henry James mentioned in a letter that his brother had joined "a metaphysical club" (Menand 2001, 201–2) [source: WP The_Metaphysical_Club]
- Peirce said pragmatism was born in its discussions [source: WP The_Metaphysical_Club; SEP-P §2: "Pragmatism's key ideas originated in discussions at a so-called 'Metaphysical Club' that met in Harvard around 1870"]. Members included "philosophically inclined lawyers" [source: SEP-P]
- At Johns Hopkins (from 1879) Peirce founded a second club definitely named The Metaphysical Club, open to faculty and graduate students; John Dewey was a member [source: WP The_Metaphysical_Club]
- Nicholas St. John Green (30 March 1830 – 8 September 1876), lawyer, Harvard BA 1851, law lecturer at Harvard 1870, professor of law at Boston University 1873; known in law for the idea of multiple causes of an event [source: WP Nicholas_St._John_Green]. Peirce (c. 1906, CP 5.12): Green "often urged the importance of applying Bain's definition of belief, as 'that upon which a man is prepared to act.' From this definition, pragmatism is scarce more than a corollary; so that I am disposed to think of him as the grandfather of pragmatism." [source: WP Pragmatism; WP Nicholas_St._John_Green] (Alexander Bain, Scottish psychologist.)
- Chauncey Wright (born 1830 Northampton, Mass.; Harvard 1848–52; earned his living as a "computer" for the Nautical Almanac office; died of a stroke in Cambridge in 1875 aged 45) influenced Peirce, James and Holmes through conversation in Cambridge study groups; early American defender of Darwin; SEP calls him "sometimes cited as a founder of pragmatism" but "more appropriately remembered as an original thinker in the tradition of J. S. Mill's utilitarianism"; James: "Never in a human head was contemplation more separated from desire." [source: SEP-CW]. John Shook: Wright "demanded a phenomenalist and fallibilist empiricism as an alternative to rationalistic speculation" [source: WP Pragmatism]
- Louis Menand, *The Metaphysical Club: A Story of Ideas in America* (New York: Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2001) covers Holmes, James, Peirce and Dewey; won the 2002 Pulitzer Prize for History [source: WP Louis_Menand; WP The_Metaphysical_Club]. SEP-JA (Hamington) notes the Club "has taken on a mythical status among some in American philosophical circles (Menand 2001)" and argues that the intellectual and political impact of Hull House residents "dwarfs that of the Cambridge intellectuals" [source: SEP-JA]
- The word "pragmatism" first appeared in print in James's address "Philosophical Conceptions and Practical Results", delivered before the Philosophical Union of the University of California at Berkeley on 26 August 1898 (printed in the *University Chronicle*, September 1898). James credited Peirce: "the principle of practicalism or pragmatism, as he called it, when I first heard him enunciate it at Cambridge in the early '70s" (Wikipedia renders the date as "[1870s]") [source: WP Pragmatism; SEP-WJ chronology 1898; IEP-PR]. James "misremembered seeing the term 'pragmatism'" in "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (it is not there) [source: IEP-CSPP]
- Pragmatism "originated in the United States around 1870" and "now presents a growing third alternative to both analytic and 'Continental' philosophical traditions" [source: SEP-P §1.1]

## Peirce: life
- Charles Sanders Peirce: born 10 September 1839, Cambridge, Massachusetts; died 19 April 1914, Milford, Pennsylvania [source: SEP-CSP; IEP-CSP]. Death from cancer [source: IEP-CSP]
- Father Benjamin Peirce, Harvard professor of mathematics, a founder and for a while director of the U.S. Coast (and Geodetic) Survey [source: SEP-CSP]
- Harvard graduate 1859 (a poor student, bottom third/quarter of class); B.Sc. in chemistry 1863 summa cum laude (IEP: from the Lawrence Scientific School, "the first Bachelor of Science degree awarded Summa cum Laude") [source: SEP-CSP; IEP-CSP]
- Employed by the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey from 1859 until the last day of 1891 (32 years), doing geodesy, including gravity measurements with pendulums often of his own design [source: SEP-CSP]
- Taught logic at Johns Hopkins 1879–1884 — his only academic post. SEP-CSP: in the Department of Mathematics (headed by J. J. Sylvester); IEP-CSP: "teaching logic for the philosophy department" (sources differ). Students included Christine Ladd(-Franklin), Joseph Jastrow, Thorstein Veblen, O. H. Mitchell and John Dewey. SEP-CSP: the claim that James was Peirce's student "is erroneous" [source: SEP-CSP; IEP-CSP]
- Dismissal: connected with his separation (1876) from first wife Harriet Melusina (Zina) Fay and open cohabitation with Juliette (Froissy/Pourtalai), whom he married after his 1883 divorce. SEP-CSP: two days after the divorce; IEP-CSP: seven days after the decree. President Daniel Coit Gilman did not renew contracts in philosophy and then reinstated all but Peirce's [source: SEP-CSP; IEP-CSP]. SEP-CSP says Juliette "was Romani"; this identification is a claim of the SEP entry only and not settled here (see NOT VERIFIED).
- After 1891 he lived "on the edge of penury" on a Pennsylvania farm (near Milford) bought from an inheritance in 1888, writing Century Dictionary entries and *Nation* reviews, doing consulting; sometimes surviving through the charity of friends, notably William James [source: SEP-CSP; IEP-CSP]
- 1898: lecture series ("Reasoning and the Logic of Things", Cambridge Conferences) arranged by James and Royce was held in a private home because Harvard refused campus use; 1903 Harvard Lectures on Pragmatism were on campus; 1902 Carnegie Institution grant application failed [source: IEP-CSP; SEP-CSP; SEP-JR]
- Output: about 12,000 printed pages published; about 80,000 handwritten manuscript pages [source: SEP-CSP]. His widow sold the manuscripts to Harvard after his death [source: SEP-CSP; IEP-CSP]
- Editions: *Collected Papers* (CP), 8 vols, 1931–58 (Hartshorne & Weiss vols 1–6, 1931–35; Burks 7–8, 1958) — topically arranged and often splices texts from different decades; *Writings of Charles S. Peirce: A Chronological Edition* (W), Peirce Edition Project, Indiana UP, 1982– (8 volumes so far, to 1892); *The Essential Peirce* (EP), 2 vols, 1992 and 1998 [source: SEP-CSP; SEP-P bibliography; IEP-CSP]
- Peirce called himself (and is now widely called) the founder of pragmatism; IEP-CSP: "Long considered an eccentric figure whose contribution to pragmatism was to provide its name ... Peirce's significance in his own right is now largely accepted" [source: IEP-CSP]

## Peirce: "The Fixation of Belief" (1877) — argument step by step
- "The Fixation of Belief" and "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" are the first two of six articles, "Illustrations of the Logic of Science", in *Popular Science Monthly*, November 1877 – August 1878 [source: SEP-CSP]. "How to Make Our Ideas Clear": *Popular Science Monthly* 12 (January 1878): 286–302; reprinted W3 (1986) [source: SEP-TP bibliography]
- Premise 1 (doubt and belief): doubt is "an uneasy and dissatisfied state from which we struggle to free ourselves and pass into the state of belief"; belief is "a calm and satisfactory state which we do not wish to avoid"; the struggle Peirce calls "inquiry" ("The irritation of doubt causes a struggle to attain a state of belief" — wording confirmed in WebSearch snippets of the essay) [source: WP Charles_Sanders_Peirce; WebSearch: https://faculty.fiu.edu/~hauptli/Peirce'sTheFixationOfBelief.htm, https://www.unav.es/gep/VTheFixationOfBelieve.pdf]
- Premise 2: beliefs "guide our desires and shape our actions" (EP1: 114) — belief as habit [source: SEP-P §4.4]
- Thesis: "the sole object of inquiry is the settlement of opinion" (wording from WebSearch snippet of the essay text; Wikipedia paraphrases it as the struggle to reach "a secure belief, belief being that on which one is prepared to act"); inquiry is spurred by real doubt, not "mere verbal ... or hyperbolic doubt" [source: WP Charles_Sanders_Peirce; WebSearch https://www.unav.es/gep/VTheFixationOfBelieve.pdf]
- Four methods, least to most successful [source: WP Charles_Sanders_Peirce; SEP-P §4.2]:
  1. Tenacity — clinging to one's belief and refusing to consider contrary evidence; gives comfort and decisiveness but fails because of the "social impulse": one meets others whose opinions seem as good as one's own.
  2. Authority — an institution (state, church) dictates belief; overcomes disagreement, "sometimes brutally"; successes "majestic and long-lasting" but it cannot regulate all opinions, and people learn of other societies.
  3. The a priori method — beliefs adopted because "agreeable to reason"; makes opinion "something like tastes", follows intellectual fashion and goes in circles.
  4. Science — supposes the real is independent of particular opinion; uniquely, by its own account it can go wrong (fallibilism), so it tests and corrects itself.
- Conclusion (SEP-P): the method of science is "the only method of inquiry that can make sense of the fact that at least some of us are disturbed by inconsistent beliefs, and will subsequently reflect upon which methods of fixing belief are correct" [source: SEP-P §4.2]
- Objection/limits within Peirce: in practical affairs, "slow and stumbling ratiocination is often dangerously inferior to instinct and traditional sentiment"; science is best suited to theoretical research [source: WP Charles_Sanders_Peirce]. Interpretive point: John E. Smith: where "Peirce aimed at 'fixing' belief, Dewey aimed at 'fixing' the situation" (1978: 98) [source: SEP-P §4.2]
- Recent defenders of Peircean epistemology: Haack 1993, Cooke 2007 [source: SEP-P]

## Peirce: anti-Cartesianism and fallibilism
- "Some Consequences of Four Incapacities" (1868) attacks "Cartesianism": the teachings that (i) one can and should doubt everything at once, (ii) the test of certainty lies in individual consciousness, (iii) a single chain of inference should replace "multiform argumentation", (iv) everything must be explained [source: SEP-P §4.1, EP1: 28–30]
- Peirce urges us not to "pretend to doubt in philosophy what we do not doubt in our hearts" (EP1: 29); "to make single individuals absolute judges of truth is most pernicious"; philosophy's reasoning "should not form a chain which is no stronger than its weakest link, but a cable whose fibres may be ever so slender, provided they are sufficiently numerous and intimately connected" (EP1: 29) [source: SEP-P §4.1]
- Fallibilism: SEP-P calls the outcome being "contrite fallibilists", not sceptics; Peirce's "first principle of reason" is "Do not block the way of inquiry!" [source: SEP-P §4.1; SEP-CSP §4]
- Tychism (objective chance) and the idea that natural laws are evolved habits support his fallibilism; "Evolutionary Love" (1893) attacks social Darwinism as "The Gospel of Greed" [source: SEP-CSP §5; SEP-P §5.1]

## Peirce: "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878) — the pragmatic maxim
- Three grades of clarity: (1) familiar unreflective use; (2) verbal definition ("distinctness", from Descartes and Leibniz); (3) the pragmatic maxim [source: SEP-P §2.1; IEP-CSPP §2]
- The maxim — wording varies between editions (see Quotations); SEP-P (EP1: 132): "Consider what effects, which might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception of those effects is the whole of our conception of the object." [source: SEP-P §2.1]
- Examples: "hard" means "it will not be scratched by many other substances" [source: SEP-P]; Peirce used the maxim against transubstantiation: speaking of something having all the sensible characters of wine yet being really blood is "senseless jargon" (EP1: 131f) [source: SEP-P §2.1]
- Destroyed diamond: in 1878 Peirce said there is "absolutely no difference between a hard and a soft thing so long as they are not brought to the test"; later (realism about "would-bes", c. 1896/97) he held the untested diamond was really hard, reformulating meaning with subjunctive conditionals [source: IEP-CSPP §2b, §3]
- Later formulation (1905): "The entire intellectual purport of any symbol consists in the total of all general modes of rational conduct which, conditionally upon all the possible different circumstances and desires, would ensue upon the acceptance of the symbol." (EP2: 346) [source: SEP-P §2.1]
- Comparisons with verificationism are common, but Peirce aimed to make metaphysics scientific, not abolish it [source: IEP-CSPP §4; SEP-CSP]
- The maxim is a tool for clarifying meaning, not Peirce's whole theory of meaning, which lies in his semiotic [source: SEP-P §2.1]

## Peirce: truth and reality
- Final section of "How to Make Our Ideas Clear": the real is "that whose characters are independent of what anybody may think them to be"; then: "The opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate, is what we mean by the truth, and the object represented in this opinion is the real. That is the way I would explain reality." (EP1: 139; = CP 5.407; W3: 273) [source: SEP-P §3.1; SEP-TP §1.1; WP Pragmatic_theory_of_truth]
- "reality is independent, not necessarily of thought in general, but only of what you or I or any finite number of [persons] may think about it" (EP1: 139) [source: SEP-P §3.1]
- "End" of inquiry = goal/telos, not a finishing date [source: SEP-P §3.1]
- Objection "too realist" (convergence assumption): Russell: "Is this an empirical generalization from the history of research? Or is it an optimistic belief in the perfectibility of man?" ... "we seem committed to some very rash assertion" (1939: 146); Quine (1960: 23) doubts any unique best systematization. Reply: Peirce later treated convergence as a regulative hope; 1908 letter: "I do not say that it is infallibly true that there is any belief to which a person would come if he were to carry his inquiries far enough. I only say that that alone is what I call Truth" (cited Haack 1976: 246) [source: SEP-P §3.1]
- Objection "not realist enough" (lost facts, e.g. number of cakes at a long-ago party); reply: "it is unphilosophical to suppose that, with regard to any given question (which has any clear meaning), investigation would not bring forth a solution of it, if it were carried far enough… Who can be sure of what we shall not know in a few hundred years?" (EP1: 140) [source: SEP-P §3.1]
- Later: truth is "that concordance of an abstract statement with the ideal limit towards which endless investigation would tend to bring scientific belief" (1901, CP 5.565) [source: SEP-TP]
- Misak: "if we were to reach a stage where we could no longer improve upon a belief, there is no point in withholding the title 'true' from it" (Misak 2000: 101) [source: SEP-TP]
- Dewey (Logic 1938) called Peirce's the best definition of truth "from the logical standpoint" known to him [source: WP Pragmatic_theory_of_truth, quoting Dewey 1938, 343n]

## Peirce: abduction, deduction, induction
- From c. 1865 Peirce split probable inference into induction and abduction (also "hypothesis", "retroduction"), by permuting Barbara: deduction = Rule + Case → Result; induction = Case + Result → Rule (sample to population); abduction = Rule + Result → Case ("educated guess") — illustrated with balls drawn from an urn [source: SEP-CSP §3]
- Mature view: three stages of scientific method — abduction (forming an explanatory hypothesis to test), deduction (deriving what should be observed if it is true), induction (experimental testing) — plus the "economy of research" [source: SEP-CSP §4]. Abduction "is not always inference to the best explanation" but always inference to some explanation of something "surprising" [source: SEP-CSP]

## Peirce: semiotics
- Sign theory ("semeiotic") developed independently of Saussure; triadic rather than dyadic [source: SEP-P §4.4; SEP-CSP]
- Definition: "I define a sign as anything which is so determined by something else, called its Object, and so determines an effect upon a person, which effect I call its interpretant, that the later is thereby mediately determined by the former." (EP2: 478) [source: SEP-SEM §1]
- Sign (sign-vehicle/representamen), object, interpretant (roughly the understanding or "translation" of the sign; it is itself a sign, which suggests infinite semiosis — scholars disagree whether infinite semiosis belongs only to the early account: Short vs Liszka/Savan) [source: SEP-SEM §§1–2]
- Icon (shared quality: portrait, map), index (brute existential/causal connection: weathercock, smoke for fire, molehill for mole), symbol (conventional/general connection: words) — first set out in "On a New List of Categories" (1867; W2: 49–58) [source: SEP-SEM §2; SEP-P §4.4]
- Emotional, dynamic and logical interpretants [source: SEP-P §4.4]
- Peirce: "it has never been in my power to study anything, – mathematics, ethics, metaphysics, gravitation, thermodynamics, optics, chemistry, comparative anatomy, astronomy, psychology, phonetics, economics, the history of science, whist, men and women, wine, metrology, except as a study of semiotic" [source: SEP-SEM intro]

## Peirce: "pragmaticism" (1905)
- In "What Pragmatism Is", *The Monist* 15:2 (April 1905), 161–81 (CP 5.411–37; the passage at pp. 165–6 = CP 5.414; EP2: 355), Peirce, seeing "pragmatism" taken up by James and Schiller and then "abused" in "literary journals", announced "the birth of the word 'pragmaticism', which is ugly enough to be safe from kidnappers." [source: WP Pragmaticism; SEP-P §2]
- He conceded "So far all went happily" about James's and Schiller's use; in a 1906 manuscript he said he coined it after "James and Schiller made the word [pragmatism] imply 'the will to believe,' the mutability of truth, the soundness of Zeno's refutation of motion, and pluralism generally" (EP2: 457–8) [source: WP Pragmatism; WP Pragmaticism]
- Peirce thought James (and Dewey) too "nominalistic"; called "The Will to Believe" in a letter to James "a very exaggerated utterance, such as injures a serious man very much" (Misak 2013: 64) [source: IEP-CSP; SEP-P §5.1]

## James: life
- William James: born New York City, 11 January 1842; eldest child of Henry James Sr. and Mary Walsh; brother of novelist Henry James (b. 1843) and of Alice (b. 1848); died of heart failure 26 August 1910 at his summer home in Chocorua, New Hampshire [source: SEP-WJ chronology; IEP-WJ; WP William_James]
- Studied painting with William Morris Hunt in Newport, then entered Harvard's Lawrence Scientific School (1861) and Medical School (1864 per SEP-WJ; 1863 per IEP-WJ); joined Louis Agassiz's Amazon/Brazil expedition (1865); M.D. 1869, never practised [source: SEP-WJ; IEP-WJ]
- 1869–70 depression; spring 1870 reading Charles Renouvier on free will helped his recovery (resolve to believe in his own freedom) [source: IEP-WJ]
- 1872 hired by President Eliot to teach physiology at Harvard; mid-1870s taught psychology and set up the first American psychology laboratory; married Alice Howe Gibbens 1878; assistant professor of philosophy 1880 [source: SEP-WJ; IEP-WJ]
- *The Principles of Psychology* (Henry Holt, 1890), contracted 1878 and twelve years in the writing; James to Holt: "a loathsome, distended, tumefied, bloated, dropsical mass, testifying to nothing but two facts: 1st, that there is no such thing as a science of psychology, and 2nd, that W. J. is an incapable" (Letters, 1926, 393–4); *Psychology: Briefer Course* 1892 [source: SEP-WJ]
- *The Will to Believe and Other Essays in Popular Philosophy* (Longmans, Green, 1897), dedicated to Peirce [source: SEP-WJ chronology; IEP-WJ]
- 1898 Berkeley address launching pragmatism; joined the Anti-Imperialist League opposing U.S. policy in the Philippines [source: SEP-WJ; WP William_James]
- Gifford Lectures, Edinburgh, 1901–2, published as *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902) [source: SEP-WJ; WP The_Varieties_of_Religious_Experience]
- *Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of Thinking* (1907): lectures at the Lowell Institute, Boston, Nov–Dec 1906 and Columbia University, January 1907; resigned Harvard 1907 [source: SEP-WJ; IEP-WJ; WebSearch on the book's preface]
- *Pragmatism* is dedicated "To the memory of John Stuart Mill from whom I first learned the pragmatic openness of mind and whom my fancy likes to picture as our leader were he alive to-day." [source: WebSearch results citing the Gutenberg and Standard Ebooks texts: https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/william-james/pragmatism/text/dedication]
- *A Pluralistic Universe* (1909; Hibbert Lectures, Oxford, 1908); *The Meaning of Truth* (1909); posthumous *Some Problems of Philosophy* (1911), *Essays in Radical Empiricism* (1912) [source: SEP-WJ; IEP-WJ]
- Students at Harvard included W. E. B. Du Bois, Gertrude Stein, George Santayana, Theodore Roosevelt, Walter Lippmann, C. I. Lewis, Mary Whiton Calkins, Horace Kallen [source: WP William_James] (but see Alain Locke below)
- James on Royce: photograph at Chocorua, 1903: "Royce, you're being photographed! Look out! I say Damn the Absolute!" [source: WP William_James caption — anecdote only]

## James: psychology and the stream of consciousness
- *Principles* chapter IX "The Stream of Thought": "Consciousness, then, does not appear to itself chopped up in bits. Such words as 'chain' or 'train' do not describe it fitly ... It is nothing jointed; it flows. A 'river' or a 'stream' are the metaphors by which it is most naturally described. In talking of it hereafter let us call it the stream of thought, of consciousness, or of subjective life." (James 1890: 239) [source: WP Stream_of_consciousness_(psychology)]. James mostly says "stream of thought"; "stream of consciousness" appears only about nine times in the book [source: same, footnote]
- Thought has "flights and perchings" (PP 236); the baby's world as "one great blooming, buzzing confusion" (PP 462) [source: SEP-WJ]
- Five characteristics of thought: owned by a personal self; always changing; sensibly continuous; deals with objects independent of itself; selective (interest) [source: IEP-WJ §2a]
- Self: empirical "me" (material, social, spiritual) vs pure ego "I" [source: IEP-WJ]
- James–Lange theory of emotion: "we feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble" (PP 1065–6) [source: SEP-WJ]
- Habit: "the enormous fly-wheel of society, its most precious conservative agent" (PP 125) [source: SEP-WJ]
- Influence: on Husserl, Russell, Dewey, Wittgenstein [source: SEP-WJ]; the "stream of consciousness" metaphor influenced modernist literature (WP marks this citation needed)

## James: "The Will to Believe" (1896) — argument step by step
- Lecture to the Philosophical Clubs of Yale and Brown universities (occasion: see NOT VERIFIED); first published in *The New World* 5 (1896): 327–347; reprinted in the 1897 book [source: WP The_Will_to_Believe]
- Target: W. K. Clifford (1845–1879), "The Ethics of Belief", *Contemporary Review* 29 (1877): 289; the ship-owner who sends an unseaworthy emigrant ship to sea, sincerely believing it safe, is guilty because "he had no right to believe on such evidence as was before him"; conclusion: "it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence" [source: WP William_Kingdon_Clifford; SEP-P §5.1]
- Definitions (section I): a hypothesis is live or dead for a person ("measured by his willingness to act"); an option is a decision between two hypotheses; living (both live), forced ("no possibility of not choosing"), momentous (vs trivial: "when the opportunity is not unique, when the stake is insignificant, or when the decision is reversible"); a genuine option is "of the forced, living, and momentous kind" [source: WP The_Will_to_Believe]
- Sections II–III: largely agrees we cannot believe at will (against a crude Pascal's wager), but "it is only our already dead hypotheses that our willing nature is unable to bring to life again" [source: WP The_Will_to_Believe]
- Thesis (section IV): "Our passional nature not only lawfully may, but must, decide an option between propositions, whenever it is a genuine option that cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds; for to say under such circumstances, 'Do not decide, but leave the question open,' is itself a passional decision—just like deciding yes or not—and is attended with the same risk of losing truth." [source: WP The_Will_to_Believe]
- Premise (section V–VI): James is an empiricist fallibilist ("we may attain [truth], [but] we cannot infallibly know when"); dogmatists include the Cliffords, for whom Christianity is already "a dead hypothesis" [source: WP The_Will_to_Believe]
- Key premise (section VII): two duties, "We must know the truth; and we must avoid error"; "Believe truth! Shun error!" are "two materially different laws"; Clifford ranks avoiding error above gaining truth, which is itself a passional preference; "he who says, 'Better go without belief forever than believe a lie!' merely shows his own preponderant private horror of becoming a dupe"; Clifford is "like a general informing his soldiers that it is better to keep out of battle forever than to risk a single wound" [source: WP The_Will_to_Believe; SEP-P §4.1 (James 1897: 30)]
- Limit (section VIII): where the option is not forced or momentous, as in most scientific questions, wait for evidence: "wherever there is no forced option, the dispassionately judicial intellect with no pet hypothesis ... ought to be our ideal" [source: WP The_Will_to_Believe]
- Self-verifying faith (section IX): "Do you like me or not?" — faith in another's liking can help create it; "There are, then, cases where a fact cannot come at all unless a preliminary faith exists in its coming" [source: WP The_Will_to_Believe]
- Conclusion (section X): "In truths dependent on our personal action, then, faith based on desire is certainly a lawful and possibly an indispensable thing." The religious hypothesis is a genuine option (scepticism avoids error if religion is false but loses the good if it is true); "a rule of thinking which would absolutely prevent me from acknowledging certain kinds of truth if those kinds of truth were really there, would be an irrational rule" [source: WP The_Will_to_Believe; SEP-P §5.1]
- James renamed the doctrine over time: "the duty to believe", "the subjective method", "the will to believe", finally "the right to believe" [source: WP The_Will_to_Believe]
- Objections: (1) Peirce (1908, "A Neglected Argument for the Reality of God") deplored confusing "active willing (willing to control thought, to doubt, and to weigh reasons) with willing not to exert the will (willing to believe)" [source: WP The_Will_to_Believe; WP Pragmaticism]; Peirce's letter: "a very exaggerated utterance" [source: SEP-P]. (2) Walter Kaufmann (*Critique of Religion and Philosophy*, 1958, p. 83): James's appeal "depends entirely on blurring the distinction between those who hold out for 100 percent proof ... and those who refuse to indulge in a belief which is supported only by the argument that after all it could conceivably be true" [source: WP The_Will_to_Believe]. (3) Alfred Henry Lloyd, "The Will to Doubt" (1907) [source: WP The_Will_to_Believe]. The James–Clifford debate "continues today" (Bishop 2007; Aikin 2014) [source: SEP-P §5.1]
- Reply on James's behalf: options must be genuine and undecidable on intellectual grounds; in science he endorses suspension; and refusing to choose is itself a choice [source: WP The_Will_to_Believe; IEP-WJ §4a]

## James: Varieties, Pragmatism, truth, radical empiricism, pluralism
- *Varieties*: religion defined broadly as individual experience in relation to "whatever they may consider the divine"; "healthy-mindedness" vs the "sick soul", the "divided self", conversion, saintliness, mysticism; "over-beliefs"; the theologians' God: "the metaphysical monster which they offer to our worship is an absolutely worthless invention of the scholarly mind" (James 1902: 447) [source: IEP-WJ §4b; SEP-P §5.1]. Wittgenstein read *Varieties* [source: SEP-P §1.1]
- *Pragmatism* Lecture I: philosophy's history is "to a great extent that of a certain clash of human temperaments" — "tough-minded" vs "tender-minded"; pragmatism is the "mediating philosophy"; "a method for settling metaphysical disputes that might otherwise be interminable" (1907: 28) [source: SEP-P §2.2]
- Lecture II squirrel example: a man circling a tree never sees the squirrel, which keeps the trunk between them: "Does the man go round the squirrel or not?" Answer depends on what you "practically mean" by "going round" (north–east–south–west: yes; front–right–back–left: no) [source: SEP-P §2.2]
- "there is no one of them so fine as to consist in anything but a possible difference of practice" (1907: 29) [source: SEP-P]
- Cash-value: "You must bring out of each word its practical cash-value, set it at work within the stream of your experience" (Lecture II) [source: WP William_James]; truth's pragmatic "cash-value" (1907 [1975: 97]) [source: SEP-TP]
- Truth formulas (Harvard 1975 pages): "The true is the name of whatever proves itself to be good in the way of belief, and good, too, for definite assignable reasons" (42); "'The true', to put it very briefly, is only the expedient in the way of our thinking, just as 'the right' is only the expedient in the way of our behaving. Expedient in almost any fashion; and expedient in the long run and on the whole, of course" (106); ideas "become true just in so far as they help us to get into satisfactory relation with other parts of our experience" (34); "true instrumentally" (34); "Truth happens to an idea. It becomes true, is made true by events" (97); "it is useful because it is true" or "it is true because it is useful" — "Both these phrases mean exactly the same thing" (98); truth as "a collective name for verification-processes" (104) [source: SEP-P §3.2; SEP-TP §1.2]
- James credited the "instrumental" view of truth to Dewey and F. C. S. Schiller [source: SEP-TP]
- James accused critics of having "boggled at every word they could boggle at" (1909 [1975: 99]) [source: SEP-TP]
- Radical empiricism: "the relations between things, conjunctive as well as disjunctive, are just as much matters of direct particular experience as the things themselves"; establishing the pragmatist theory of truth was "a step of first-rate importance in making [it] prevail" (*Meaning of Truth*, 1909: 6f) [source: SEP-P §4.3]. Essays of 1904–5 ("Does 'Consciousness' Exist?", "A World of Pure Experience") collected in *Essays in Radical Empiricism* (1912) [source: SEP-WJ]
- Pluralism: *A Pluralistic Universe* (1909) against neo-Hegelian monism; James's finite God; "Battle of the Absolute" with Royce [source: IEP-WJ; SEP-JR]
- James "embraced utilitarian ethics as one of the branches of pragmatism (James 1907)" [source: SEP-P §5.2]
- "The Dilemma of Determinism" (1884) coined "hard" and "soft" determinism [source: WP William_James]

## Objections to James on truth (Russell, Moore) and replies
- Bertrand Russell, "Pragmatism", *Edinburgh Review* April 1909 (repr. *Philosophical Essays*, 1910): "when we say that a belief is true, the thought we wish to convey is not the same thought as when we say that the belief furthers our purposes; thus 'true' does not mean 'furthering our purposes'" (1910 [1994: 98]); and "it is so often harder to determine whether a belief is useful than whether it is true" (1910 [1994: 121]) [source: SEP-TP §4]
- Useful-but-false and true-but-useless beliefs (Russell 1910; Lovejoy 1908) [source: SEP-TP]
- Russell (*History of Western Philosophy*, 1946: 772): James's view commits him to the truth of "Santa Claus exists"; SEP-P calls this "unfair": James would call the belief "good for so much" but "wholly true" only if it did not "clash with other vital benefits" [source: SEP-P §3.2]
- G. E. Moore, "Professor James' 'Pragmatism'", *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 8 (1908): 33–77 (repr. *Philosophical Studies*, 1922) — Moore reconstructs James as holding (1) all and only true ideas are verifiable and (2) all and only true ideas are useful, and attacks both [source: SEP-TP bibliography; WebSearch summary of a Cambridge Companion chapter — not read directly]. Moore's detailed counterarguments: see NOT VERIFIED.
- Replies: Misak's "pragmatic elucidation": it is "pointless" to define truth; "we ought to attempt to get leverage on the concept ... by exploring its connections with practice" (2007a: 69); Haack (1976: 236): the pragmatists would have rejected a dichotomy between definitions and criteria [source: SEP-TP §4]. Charitable readings of James: Ayer 1968, Chisholm 1992, Putnam etc. [source: SEP-TP]

## Dewey: life
- John Dewey: born 20 October 1859, Burlington, Vermont (an elder brother also named John had died in infancy, January 1859); University of Vermont, graduated 1879 [source: SEP-JD; WP John_Dewey]
- Taught high school in Oil City, Pennsylvania (1879–81) and Charlotte, Vermont; graduate study at Johns Hopkins 1882–84 (logic with Peirce, which he found too "mathematical" and dropped; history of philosophy with George Sylvester Morris; experimental psychology with G. Stanley Hall); Ph.D. 1884 (dissertation "The Psychology of Kant", lost) [source: SEP-JD]
- Neo-Hegelian beginnings: Hegel "operated as an immense release, a liberation" (FAE, LW5: 153) [source: SEP-JD]
- Michigan (1884/1886–88, 1889–94), Minnesota (1888–89); married Alice Chipman 1886 (she died 1927); married Roberta Lowitz Grant 1946 [source: SEP-JD; WP John_Dewey]
- 1894 chair of philosophy (including psychology and pedagogy) at the University of Chicago; hired G. H. Mead; "Chicago School" (James's name) of functional psychology [source: SEP-JD]
- "The Reflex Arc Concept in Psychology", *Psychological Review*, 1896 [source: SEP-JD; WP John_Dewey]
- Laboratory School founded 1896 (Hyde Park, Chicago); Alice Chipman Dewey principal 1896–1904 [source: SEP-JD; SEP-P §5.4; WP University_of_Chicago_Laboratory_Schools]
- Resigned 1904 after conflicts over the Laboratory School; Columbia University 1904–1930 (philosophy department, with affiliation to Teachers College — WP calls him professor "at Teachers College") [source: SEP-JD; WP John_Dewey]
- President of the American Psychological Association 1899 (WP; SEP chronology misprints "1889–1900") and of the American Philosophical Association 1905–06 (SEP chronology says "American Philosophical Society" — evidently an error) [source: SEP-JD; WP John_Dewey]
- Helped found/lead the ACLU, AAUP, New School; wrote for the *New Republic* [source: SEP-JD]
- Paul Carus Lectures 1922 → *Experience and Nature* (1925); Gifford Lectures → *The Quest for Certainty* (1929) (SEP-JD dates the Giffords "1928"; see NOT VERIFIED); Terry Lectures 1933–34 → *A Common Faith* (1934) [source: SEP-JD]
- Died of pneumonia at home in New York City, 1 June 1952 [source: SEP-JD; WP John_Dewey]. IEP-JD gives 2 June 1952 (minority; WP: cremated "the next day").
- Output: more than 700 articles in 140 journals and about 40 books [source: WP John_Dewey]

## Dewey: ideas
- Reflex arc (1896): the stimulus–response model treats experience as "a patchwork of disjointed parts, a mechanical conjunction of unallied processes"; "The real beginning is with the act of seeing; it is looking, and not a sensation of light"; stimulus and response "mean distinctions of flexible function only, not of fixed existence" (EW5: 97, 102); child-and-candle example [source: SEP-JD §2.2]. Called the first major work of the (Chicago) functionalist school [source: WP John_Dewey]
- Instrumentalism (also "experimentalism"): the test of an idea "is its functional or instrumental use in effecting the transition from a relatively conflicting experience to a relatively integrated one" (*Studies in Logical Theory*, 1903, MW2: 359); knowledge measured by "the degree of its availability for conducting to a successful issue the activities of living beings" (MW4: 180); a "Copernican revolution" (QC, LW4: 232) [source: SEP-JD §4.1–4.2; SEP-JDP]
- Experience: "includes what men do and suffer, what they strive for, love, believe and endure, and also how men act and are acted upon ..." (LW1: 18) [source: SEP-P §4.3]
- *The Quest for Certainty* (1929): the quest to eliminate all error is "both doomed and destructive"; social diagnosis: fear and insecurity produced "priestly" knowledge separating theory from practice [source: SEP-P §4.1]
- *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry* (1938): logic as "inquiry into inquiry"; inquiry is "the controlled or directed transformation of an indeterminate situation into one that is so determinate in its constituent distinctions and relations as to convert the elements of the original situation into a unified whole" (ED2: 171); five phases: felt difficulty; formulating the problem; hypothesis; reasoning out implications; testing [source: SEP-P §4.2; SEP-JD §4.3]
- "Warranted assertibility" replaces "truth" and "knowledge" as the goal of inquiry (LTI 1938 [2008: 15–16]); the index of the 527-page *Logic* has one entry for "truth" plus "See also assertibility"; 1941 reply to Russell, "Propositions, Warranted Assertibility, and Truth"; correspondence in "the operational sense ... as a key answers to conditions imposed by a lock" (1941: 178); Quine: Dewey's strategy was "simply to avoid the truth predicate and limp along with warranted belief" [source: SEP-TP §1.3]. "There is no belief so settled as not to be exposed to further inquiry" (LW12: 16) [source: SEP-JD]
- *Democracy and Education* (Macmillan, 1916; subtitle *An Introduction to the Philosophy of Education*): Dewey later said it was the work in which "my philosophy, such as it is, was most fully expounded" (LW5: 156); philosophy "may even be defined as the general theory of education" (MW9: 338); democracy is "primarily a mode of associated living, of conjoint communicated experience" (MW9: 93) [source: SEP-JD §5, §7; WP Democracy_and_Education]
- Education: neither "traditionalist" (curriculum-centred) nor "romantic" (child-centred); school must reproduce "the typical conditions of social life" (EW5: 61–2); "My Pedagogic Creed" (1897), *The School and Society* (1899), *How We Think* (1910), *Experience and Education* (1938) [source: SEP-JD §5]
- Ethics: "growth" as the nearest thing to an ultimate value; "dramatic rehearsal" in deliberation; conduct "is always shared" (MW14: 16) [source: SEP-JD §6]
- *The Public and Its Problems* (1927): defence of participatory democracy against sceptics such as Walter Lippmann (*Public Opinion*, 1922; *The Phantom Public*, 1925), who denied citizens are "omnicompetent"; democracy "the idea of community life itself" (LW2: 328); worry about "inchoate" publics [source: SEP-JDP; SEP-JD §5.3, §7; WP The_Public_and_Its_Problems]. Dewey called *Public Opinion* "perhaps the most effective indictment of democracy as currently conceived ever penned" (in his 1922 review) [source: WP The_Public_and_Its_Problems]
- "Creative Democracy—The Task Before Us" (1939): "Democracy is the faith that the process of experience is more important than any special result attained" (LW14: 229) [source: SEP-JD]
- Political economy: "renascent liberalism"; *Individualism, Old and New* (1930), *Liberalism and Social Action* (1935), *Freedom and Culture* (1939) express "a form of liberal and democratic socialism" [source: SEP-JDP; SEP-JD §7]. His support for U.S. entry into the First World War "strained or broke many of his ties with the American left" [source: SEP-JDP]
- *Art as Experience* (1934): aesthetic experience continuous with "the live creature"'s everyday consummatory experiences [source: SEP-P §5.3]
- *A Common Faith* (1934): "religious" as a quality of experience vs supernatural "religion" [source: SEP-P §5.1; SEP-JD §9]
- Dewey's critics: Mumford, Hartz, Horkheimer, Marcuse etc. saw pragmatism as a philosophy for liberal technocrats; Russell feared its laxity about objectivity; Lippmann and Niebuhr thought Dewey's democracy "extravagantly optimistic" [source: SEP-JDP]

## Dewey abroad and in public life: China, Trotsky
- Lectured in Japan (1919) then China 1919–21: Dewey and Alice arrived in Shanghai 30 April 1919, days before the May Fourth demonstrations; stayed until July 1921; visited 11 provinces, gave nearly 200 lectures, interpreted by Hu Shih; called "Mr. Democracy" and "Mr. Science"; hailed as "the American Confucius"; a 1919 Peking University lecture was attended by the young Mao Zedong [source: WP John_Dewey; SEP-JD chronology]. His ideas were later denounced in Maoist China [source: WP John_Dewey]
- Hu Shih (17 December 1891 – 24 or 25 February 1962; WP infobox and text differ): Boxer Indemnity scholar at Cornell (from 1910), then Columbia under Dewey; leading advocate of written vernacular Chinese, New Culture/May Fourth figure; translated "pragmatism" as 實驗主義 ("experimentalism"); 1919 "problems and isms" debate with Li Dazhao; ambassador to the U.S. 1938–42; chancellor of Peking University 1946–48; president of Academia Sinica from 1957 [source: WP Hu_Shih]. Dewey's doctoral students also included Feng Youlan and Tao Xingzhi [source: WP John_Dewey]
- Dewey Commission ("Commission of Inquiry into the Charges Made against Leon Trotsky in the Moscow Trials"), initiated March 1937; Dewey, then 78, chaired; sub-commission held thirteen hearings at Trotsky's house in Coyoacán, Mexico, 10–17 April 1937; findings announced 21 September 1937; published as *Not Guilty*; "We therefore find the Moscow Trials to be frame-ups." [source: WP Dewey_Commission; SEP-JD chronology "1937 Chair of the Trotsky Commission, Mexico City"]. SEP-JDP says Sidney Hook persuaded him to take part in the "trial" of Trotsky "in Mexico in 1938" — the 1938 date is wrong (hearings April 1937). Historian Pierre Broué and J. Arch Getty note Trotsky withheld from the Commission his 1932 contacts with an opposition bloc in the USSR [source: WP Dewey_Commission]
- 1940: Dewey defended Bertrand Russell against removal from City College of New York [source: IEP-JD]

## Jane Addams, Hull House and the pragmatist link
- Laura Jane Addams: born 6 September 1860, Cedarville, Illinois; died 1935; Rockford Seminary; inspired by Toynbee Hall in London [source: SEP-JA; IEP-JA]
- With Ellen Gates Starr opened Hull House in a poor immigrant neighbourhood of Chicago on 18 September 1889 [source: SEP-JA; SEP-P §5.4]
- Nobel Peace Prize 1931 [source: SEP-JA; SEP-P §1.1]. SEP-P says she "invented the profession of social work as an expression of pragmatist ideas" [source: SEP-P §1.1]
- Dozen books and over 500 articles; *Democracy and Social Ethics* (1902), *Twenty Years at Hull-House* (1910), *Newer Ideals of Peace*; "sympathetic knowledge"; "lateral progress" [source: SEP-JA; SEP-P bibliography]
- Dewey and Addams met in 1892; Dewey was elected to the Hull-House board of trustees in 1897; Dewey and his daughter Jane credited Addams with shaping his views of democracy, education and philosophy; Hull House influenced the Laboratory School [source: SEP-JA; SEP-JD; SEP-P §5.4]. Mead was also close (he lectured at Hull House; in 1916 he urged an honorary Chicago doctorate for her, overruled until 1931) [source: SEP-JA]
- Canon and gender: contemporaries cast Dewey, James and Mead as original thinkers and Addams as applier; "the dynamics of pragmatist canon formation largely ignored her philosophical work until the 1990s"; her "intellectual deference to Dewey is often overstated" [source: SEP-JA]
- Charlene Haddock Seigfried coined "pragmatist feminism" and took Addams as its quintessential example (*Pragmatism and Feminism: Reweaving the Social Fabric*, University of Chicago Press, 1996); she also edited the 2002 Illinois edition of *Democracy and Social Ethics* [source: IEP-JA; SEP-P bibliography]
- Mary Parker Follett (studied with Royce and James at Radcliffe/Harvard): "power-with" rather than "power-over" [source: SEP-P §1.1]

## W. E. B. Du Bois, Alain Locke and race
- Du Bois: born 23 February 1868, Great Barrington, Massachusetts; Fisk BA 1888; entered Harvard College 1888; philosophy courses with William James, George Santayana and F. G. Peabody (1889–90); Harvard BA cum laude in philosophy 1890; studied in Berlin; Harvard PhD in history 1895, "the first black to receive a PhD from Harvard"; *The Souls of Black Folk* 1903 (double consciousness); died in Accra, Ghana, 27 August 1963 [source: SEP-DB chronology]. IEP-DB adds Royce among his teachers; SEP-JR says Royce taught Du Bois [source: IEP-DB; SEP-JR]
- Du Bois on his turn from Schmoller and Weber: "I fell back upon my Royce and James ... This was the Jamesian pragmatism, applied not simply to ethics, but to all human action" ("My Evolving Program for Negro Freedom", 1944, 57–58) [source: SEP-DB §3]
- 1896: hired by the University of Pennsylvania for the Philadelphia Seventh Ward study [source: SEP-DB; SEP-P §5.4]
- Cornel West places Du Bois among the pragmatists, adding concern for "the wretched of the earth" [source: IEP-DB]
- Alain LeRoy Locke: born 13 September 1885, Philadelphia; Harvard 1904–07 (studied with Royce, Palmer, Münsterberg, Perry); first African-American Rhodes Scholar (1907, Hertford College, Oxford); Harvard PhD 1918; *The New Negro* (1925), "Father of the Harlem Renaissance"; Howard University; died 9 June 1954, New York [source: SEP-AL]. SEP-AL: "Though he was a member of the faculty at the time Locke never actually studied with William James" (contradicts WP's list of James's students — see Debates)
- Locke's value theory: "the gravest problem of contemporary philosophy is how to ground some normative principle or criterion of objective validity for values without resort to dogmatism and absolutism ..." (Locke 1935: 336); pluralist and culturally relativist axiology; "critical pragmatism" [source: SEP-P §5.2; SEP-AL]
- Du Bois and Locke "engaged in productive dialogue"; Locke called art-as-uplift "propaganda" [source: SEP-P §1.1; SEP-AL]

## Royce, Mead, C. I. Lewis
- Josiah Royce (born 20 November 1855, Grass Valley, California; died 1916): absolute idealist at Harvard (appointed 1882; professor of history of philosophy 1892); "Battle of the Absolute" with James; *The Philosophy of Loyalty* (1908: "loyalty to loyalty"); after studying Peirce's semiotics, *The Problem of Christianity* (1913) replaced the Absolute Knower with an infinite "community of interpretation" — "absolute pragmatism"; Peirce acknowledged him as a fellow pragmatist [source: SEP-JR; SEP-P §1.1]
- George Herbert Mead (born 27 February 1863, South Hadley, Mass.; died 1931): Oberlin, Harvard (studied with Royce; met but did not study with James; tutored James's son); Dewey's colleague at Michigan and Chicago; social self, "generalized other", "I" and "me"; *Mind, Self, and Society* (1934) compiled posthumously from student notes; father of "symbolic interactionism" (term not his); Dewey: "a seminal mind of the very first order" [source: SEP-GHM; SEP-P]
- C. I. Lewis (born 12 April 1883, Stoneham, Mass.; died 2 February 1964, Menlo Park, California): Harvard student of James and Royce; *A Survey of Symbolic Logic* (1918, strict implication/modal logic); "A Pragmatic Conception of the A Priori" (1923), *Mind and the World-Order* (1929): "conceptual pragmatism"; taught Quine, Goodman, Chisholm, Firth, Sellars at Harvard (supervised only some of them) [source: SEP-CIL; SEP-P §1.1]. Briefly worked on Peirce's papers [source: IEP-CSP]

## Later revival
- Decline: by the 1940s, after Dewey's *Logic*, pragmatism "had lost much of its momentum"; analytic philosophers found James and Dewey "infuriatingly vague" [source: IEP-PR]
- Frank Ramsey (Cambridge, 1920s) developed Peirce's ideas; Wittgenstein's later thought acquired "a pragmatist flavour" via Ramsey (Misak 2018) [source: SEP-P §1.1; IEP-CSP]
- Quine, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) drew on pragmatism; ends: Carnap and Lewis take a pragmatic stand on choosing language forms, but their pragmatism stops at the analytic–synthetic boundary; "In repudiating such a boundary I espouse a more thorough pragmatism" [source: IEP-PR; WebSearch (PhilPapers abstract of Sinclair) https://api.philpapers.org/rec/SINQAC-2]. "no statement is immune to revision" (1951, 43) [source: SEP-Q]. Quine later said he only extended Carnap's use of "pragmatic" [source: same WebSearch]
- Richard Rorty (born 4 October 1931, New York; died 8 June 2007): Chicago (BA 1949, MA 1952), Yale PhD; Wellesley; Princeton 1961–82; Virginia (Kenan Professor) 1982–98; Stanford 1998– [source: SEP-RR; IEP-RR]
- *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (Princeton UP, 1979) attacks knowledge as representation, the mind as "mirror of nature"; "what got philosophers talking about pragmatism again" [source: SEP-RR; IEP-PR]. "Neopragmatism" originated in PMN [source: WP Neopragmatism; SEP-P §1.2]. Truth as "what our peers will, ceteris paribus, let us get away with saying" (1979: 176; Rorty later admitted it was provocative); "an expression of commendation" (1991); "cautionary" use of "true" [source: SEP-TP §2; SEP-P §1.2]
- Rorty's Dewey is "good" vs "bad" Dewey; dismissive of Peirce; Rorty: Dewey "should have dropped the term 'experience' rather than redefining it" (1998: 297) [source: SEP-RR; SEP-P §4.3]
- Hilary Putnam (31 July 1926 – 13 March 2016): pragmatism's four characteristics: rejection of scepticism, fallibilism, rejection of dichotomies (fact/value etc.), "primacy of practice" (1993/1994); "pragmatist enlightenment"; internal realism, truth as idealized warranted assertibility (later "natural realism"); "my admiration for the classical pragmatists does not extend to any of the different theories of truth that Peirce, James, and Dewey advanced" [source: SEP-P §1.2; SEP-TP §2; WP Hilary_Putnam]
- Robert Brandom (born 13 March 1950; University of Pittsburgh; doctoral advisers Rorty and David Lewis): inferentialist semantics, "game of giving and asking for reasons", *Making It Explicit* (1994); critical of classical pragmatists; "experience" is "not one of his words" [source: SEP-P §1.2, §4.3; WP Robert_Brandom]
- Habermas drew on Mead and Peirce's community of inquiry [source: SEP-P §1.2; IEP-CSP]
- "New pragmatists" (Haack, Hookway, Misak) object to Rorty's dismissal of truth and seek to rehabilitate objectivity [source: SEP-P §1.2]. Haack finds Rorty's "pragmatism" "literally unworthy of the name"; Misak: when Rorty abandons objectivity he "dispenses with the capacity for normative critique" — "a pragmatism of subjectivity in the tradition of William James" (Misak 2013) [source: SEP-RR §4.2]
- Cheryl Misak (born 16 July 1961; University of Toronto): *Truth and the End of Inquiry: A Peircean Account of Truth* (OUP; first ed. 1991, expanded 2004), *Truth, Politics, Morality* (2000), *The American Pragmatists* (2013), *Cambridge Pragmatism* (2016), *Frank Ramsey* (2020) [source: WP Cheryl_Misak]
- Cornel West (born 2 June 1953, Tulsa): Harvard BA 1973; Princeton PhD (influenced by Rorty); *The American Evasion of Philosophy: A Genealogy of Pragmatism* (1989) traces pragmatism from Emerson, "evading" Cartesian epistemology; final chapter argues for "prophetic pragmatism" drawing on Christian and Marxian thought and showcasing Du Bois [source: SEP-P §1.2; WP Cornel_West; IEP-DB; WebSearch (Philosophy and Literature review, ebsco summary) https://muse.jhu.edu/article/413436/summary]
- Other liberatory pragmatisms: feminism (Seigfried 1996), whiteness studies (Sullivan 2006), Native American philosophy (Pratt 2002), Latin American (Pappas 1998); pragmatist networks in South America, Scandinavia, central Europe and China [source: SEP-P §1.2]
- Pragmatism also had early followers in Oxford (Schiller), France and especially Italy (Papini) [source: SEP-P §1.1; IEP-PR]

## Debates
- **Who founded pragmatism, and was the Metaphysical Club its cradle?** Peirce's late recollection is the only evidence for the Club's name; Menand made it famous; Hamington (SEP-JA) argues Hull House's impact "dwarfs" the Cambridge group's and that Addams belongs among the founders; Fischer: including all relevant figures "turns pragmatism's early tree from a sapling into a sequoia"; Pratt (2002) argues for Native American roots; West (1989) starts with Emerson [source: WP The_Metaphysical_Club; SEP-JA; SEP-P]
- **Peirce vs James on pragmatism and truth**: SEP-P frames the split as Peircean monism about truth (scientific, convergence at the end of inquiry) vs Jamesian/Deweyan "alethic pluralism"; Peirce called James "nominalistic"; Peirce held the maxim was a logical rule, not a theory of truth, while James made it a theory of truth [source: SEP-P §2, §3; IEP-CSP]
- **Is Peirce a unified system-builder?** Goudge (1950): two conflicting strands; Murphey (1961): four failed attempts at a system; Hookway (1985), Anderson (1995), Houser: one architectonic system — now the orthodox view [source: IEP-CSP §3]
- **Pragmatic maxim and verificationism**: often compared with the logical positivists' verification principle; Atkin: the similarity strains once Peirce's realism about "would-bes" and his aim to reform rather than abolish metaphysics are seen [source: IEP-CSPP §4; SEP-CSP]
- **Was James's theory of truth as crude as Russell and Moore said?** Critics: truth ≠ utility (Russell, Moore, Lovejoy); defenders: Russell's Santa Claus objection is "unfair" (SEP-P); Misak's "pragmatic elucidation"; charitable readers Ayer, Chisholm, Putnam [source: SEP-P §3.2; SEP-TP §4]
- **The Will to Believe**: evidentialists (Clifford, Kaufmann, Russell) vs defenders (Bishop 2007); Peirce's own objection [source: SEP-P §5.1; WP The_Will_to_Believe]
- **Addams and Dewey**: was Addams Dewey's applier or his teacher? Dewey and his daughter credited her; Seigfried, Deegan, Hamington, Fischer argue for her originality [source: SEP-JA; IEP-JA]
- **The "Lippmann–Dewey debate"**: textbook framing (from James Carey, 1980s–90s) of Lippmann as anti-democratic elitist vs Dewey; Michael Schudson (2008, *International Journal of Communication* 2: 1031–42) argues it was not really a debate (Lippmann may never have seen himself in dialogue with Dewey) and that Lippmann was not anti-democratic [source: WebSearch https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/download/343/229; SEP-JDP gives the standard contrast]
- **Rorty's right to the name "pragmatist"**: Haack, Misak and Dewey scholars (Sleeper, Lavine) reject Rorty's readings; Rorty separates "good" from "bad" Dewey; neopragmatists dropped "experience", which New Pragmatists (Koopman, Levine) contest [source: SEP-RR §4.2; SEP-P §4.3]
- **Is pragmatism a philosophy for technocrats?** Mumford, Hartz, Horkheimer, Marcuse, Wolin: yes; defenders read Dewey as a radical democrat; Talisse and Misak prefer Peirce's account of inquiry as a basis for democracy [source: SEP-JDP; SEP-P §5.2]
- **Was Alain Locke James's student?** WP lists him among James's students; SEP-AL says Locke "never actually studied with William James" [source: WP William_James; SEP-AL]
