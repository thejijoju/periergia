## 1. What a fallacy is (definitions and the problem of definition)
- Two conceptions: fallacies as false but popular *beliefs* ("belief conception", prevalent in popular discourse) and as deceptively bad *arguments* ("argument conception", preferred by scholars). Working definition in SEP: "a fallacy [is] an argument that seems to be better than it really is." [source: SEP-F intro, §1]
- Formal fallacies are instances of identifiable invalid forms (undistributed middle, denying the antecedent); "informal fallacy" names those whose weakness is better exposed without formal languages. [source: SEP-F intro]
- IEP-F: "A fallacy is a kind of error in reasoning." Its list "contains 231 names of the most common fallacies". "Fallacy" is ambiguous: an error in an argument; an error in reasoning generally; a false belief; or the cause of such errors. [source: IEP-F intro, §1]
- IEP-F: "A charge of fallacious reasoning always needs to be justified. The burden of proof is on your shoulders when you claim that someone's reasoning is fallacious." [source: IEP-F intro]
- IEP-F: "Reasoning validly is not a guarantee of avoiding a fallacy since begging the question is valid." Researchers are "deeply divided" over how to define "fallacy" and whether there are necessary and sufficient conditions separating fallacious from non-fallacious reasoning. [source: IEP-F §1, §4]
- "Standard definition of fallacy" (SDF, as Hamblin presented it): an argument "that seems to be valid but is not so" (Hamblin 1970, 12). Three conditions: argument, invalidity, appearance. All three have been questioned; Hansen (2002) argues SDF was never as widely held as Hamblin implied. [source: SEP-F §3.1]
- SDF is too narrow (excludes begging the question, which is valid) and too wide (condemns good non-deductive arguments). [source: SEP-F §3.5]
- SEP's verdict: the traditional list "has grown as a hodgepodge collection of items, proposed at various time and from different perspectives"; "there is no single conception of good argument or argumentation to be discovered behind the core fallacies." [source: SEP-F §4.1]
- Two "fountainheads" of the textbook list: Aristotle's *Sophistical Refutations* and Locke's *Essay* (1690). They are "uneasy bedfellows": Aristotle's are logical mistakes with no proper use outside eristic; the Lockean *ad*-arguments are often legitimately used in dialogue; the appearance condition is Aristotelian; Aristotle's examples are logical/metaphysical puzzles while the *ad*-fallacies concern social and political topics. [source: SEP-F §1, §4.1]

## 2. History of the fallacy list
### Aristotle
- The history "begins with Aristotle's work, On Sophistical Refutations", an earlier work that "appears to be a continuation of the Topics" (his treatise on dialectical argument). He also discusses fallacies in *Prior Analytics* and *Rhetoric*. [source: SEP-F §2.1]
- 34 chapters; tactics for the questioner (chs 3–8, 12–15) and for the answerer (16–32). [source: WP-Sophistical_Refutations]
- Aristotle discusses **thirteen** ways refutations can be sophistical, in two groups: dependent on language (*in dictione*, ch. 4) and not dependent on language (*extra dictionem*, ch. 5). Chs 19–30 advise answerers. [source: SEP-F §2.1.4]
  - Six language-dependent: equivocation, amphiboly, combination of words, division of words, accent, form of expression. [source: SEP-F §2.1.4]
  - Seven not language-dependent: accident, *secundum quid*, consequent, non-cause, begging the question, *ignoratio elenchi*, many questions. [source: SEP-F §2.1.4]
  - Caveat: on one reading *ignoratio elenchi* is the thirteenth fallacy; on another (which Aristotle "seems to favour") it is not a separate fallacy but an alternative way of classifying the other twelve. Hence WP says "twelve or thirteen". [source: SEP-F §2.1.4; WP-Sophistical_Refutations]
  - CAUTION: WP-Sophistical_Refutations labels the language-dependent group "formal fallacies" and the others "informal". This does not match the modern usage; do not repeat it.
- Aristotle's composition/division differ from the modern ones: they concern combining/dividing *words*, e.g. "walk while sitting"; division example: 5 is 2 and 3, 2 is even and 3 odd, so 5 is even and odd. [source: SEP-F §2.1.4]
- Fallacy of consequent: "if A is, B necessarily is, men also fancy that, if B is, A necessarily is"; example: not every man who is hot has a fever because a man in a fever is hot; sometimes claimed as an early statement of affirming the consequent. [source: SEP-F §2.1.4]
- Appearance: "reasoning and refutation are sometimes real and sometimes not, but appear to be real owing to men's inexperience; for the inexperienced are like those who view things from a distance" (SE 1, 164b25); the most prolific source of illusion is that "there are more things than names", so names are ambiguous (SE 1, 165a6). [source: SEP-F §2.1.3]
- Interpretive debate: Hamblin (1970, ch. 2) holds that a dialectical framework is indispensable to understanding Aristotle's fallacies (followed by Walton, van Eemeren & Grootendorst; Hintikka calls it "an outright mistake" to treat them primarily as mistaken inferences); Woods and Hansen (1997, 2001) give a non-dialogical reading in which the unity lies in the concepts of deduction and proof; Schreiber (2003) stresses Aristotelian epistemology and metaphysics. [source: SEP-F §2.1.5]
- "Begging the question" descends from Aristotle's *to ex archēs aitein* / *to en archēi aiteisthai*, "asking for the initial thing" in a *Topics*-style question-and-answer debate; discussed in SE and *Prior Analytics* II.16 (64b28–65a26). The Latin *petitio principii* (literally "assuming the original point") was translated into English in the 16th century. [source: WP-Begging_the_question]
- Aristotle's advice (SE 27) on begging the question: refuse to concede the original point if you notice it is being asked for. [source: WP-Begging_the_question, quoting a secondary source]

### Medieval Latin and Islamicate traditions
- Medieval logic textbooks trained students for disputation, "with chapters on fallacies, consequence ... obligationes". Latin authors largely did not distinguish *logica* from *dialectica*. [source: SEP-ARG Historical Supplement §2.1]
- The seventh tract of Peter of Spain's *Tractatus* (later called *Summulae logicales*; 13th century, author's exact identity unknown) is on fallacies, "written in the tradition of the Fallacie maiores (Major fallacies)". From the second half of the 12th century there was "a growing interest in the linguistic elements ... at the basis of ambiguity and fallacious reasoning" (cf. L. M. de Rijk, *Logica Modernorum* vol. I, "On the Twelfth Century Theories of Fallacies", 1962). [source: SEP-PS §3 and bibliography]
- IEP: after Aristotle "very few advances were made for many centuries"; fallacies were again studied systematically in medieval Europe, "This is why so many fallacies have Latin names." (IEP uses the phrase "Dark Ages"; avoid it.) [source: IEP-F §1]
- Islamicate: *jadal* ("dialectic" or "disputation theory"); earliest full theories arose in theology around the turn of the 9th–10th centuries (Miller 2020). [source: SEP-ARG Historical Supplement §2.2]
- Cross-tradition: SEP-ARG says the opposition between rational and irrational argument marked "the Indian, Chinese, and Islamicate traditions" too. [source: SEP-ARG §2.5]

### Early modern
- Francis Bacon, *New Organon* (1620), aphorisms 40–44: four "idols" (tribe, cave, theatre, market place); they stand to the interpretation of nature as fallacies stand to logic. Market place idol: "words plainly force and overrule the understanding" (aph. 43). [source: SEP-F §2.2]
- Arnauld and Nicole, *Logic, or the Art of Thinking* (Port-Royal Logic, 1662): includes 8 of Aristotle's 13; adds *post hoc, ergo propter hoc* ("This happened following a certain thing, hence that thing must be its cause", III.xix.3), imperfect enumeration, and faulty induction (hasty generalisation), marking "the entry of inductive fallacies"; also the sophism of authority (deference to age, experience, noble birth) and of "manner" ("grace, fluency, seriousness, moderation and gentleness" are not marks of truth). [source: SEP-F §2.3]
- **Locke**, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690), Book IV, ch. xvii ("Of Reason"), §§19–22: three kinds of argument, *ad verecundiam*, *ad ignorantiam*, *ad hominem*, contrasted with *ad judicium*, arguments from "the foundations of knowledge and probability". Locke described them as arguments "that men, in their reasoning with others, do ordinarily make use of to prevail on their assent; or at least so to awe them as to silence their opposition." [source: SEP-F §2.4]
  - Locke is "credited with intentionally creating a class of ad-arguments, and inadvertently giving birth to the class of ad-fallacies"; he "never used the term 'fallacy' in connection with them". The passage comes almost as an afterthought at the end of a chapter criticising syllogistic logic. [source: SEP-F §2.4]
  - *Ad hominem* for Locke: "to press a man with consequences drawn from his own principles or concessions", i.e. showing an opponent's inconsistency with his own commitments; "not a fallacy but an acceptable mode of argumentation". None of the modern abusive/circumstantial/tu quoque forms "is what Locke described". [source: SEP-F §2.4]
  - *Ad verecundiam*: literally "appeal to modesty"; Locke's language ("eminency", "dignity", "breach of modesty") suggests social deference rather than expertise (Goodwin 1998). It is the *locus classicus* of appeal-to-authority arguments. [source: SEP-F §2.4]
  - *Ad ignorantiam*: requiring "the adversary to admit what they allege as a proof, or to assign a better"; the ignorance is comparative. [source: SEP-F §2.4]
  - Antecedents: Hamblin and Nuchelmans trace *ad hominem* to Aristotle; Finocchiaro (1974) finds the term and argument in Galileo more than half a century before Locke. [source: SEP-F §2.4]
  - Edition: SEP-F cites "Locke 1690 ... Many editions" and quotes in modernised spelling; the Nidditch edition (Oxford 1975) keeps capitals ("Men"). Check final wording against Nidditch.
- Isaac Watts, *Logick* (1724) adds *ad fidem*, *ad passiones*, *ad populum*; introduces "false cause"; and his description of disputers who make "images of straw" may be the origin of "straw man". [source: SEP-F §2.5; WP-Straw_man]
- Bentham, *Handbook of Political Fallacies* (1824; this is the title of Larrabee's edition; WP-Fallacy calls the 1824 work the *Book of Fallacies*): about thirty fallacies of authority, danger, delay and confusion, used to defeat reform in Parliament; "vituperative fallacies" try "to draw aside attention from the measure to the man". Grootendorst (1997) sees him as a precursor of dialogical theories. [source: SEP-F §2.6; WP-Fallacy]
- **Whately**, *Elements of Logic* (1826), Book III: a fallacy is "any unsound mode of arguing, which appears to demand our conviction, and to be decisive of the question at hand, when in fairness it is not". Logical fallacies (purely logical; semi-logical, e.g. ambiguity, composition/division) vs material fallacies (premises "unduly assumed", e.g. begging the question; irrelevant conclusion, *ignoratio elenchi*, under which he files the *ad*-arguments, "fallacies only when they are used unfairly"). [source: SEP-F §2.7] (WP-Fallacy gives a different wording of the definition; prefer SEP's, which cites the 9th ed., 1875.) Whately lived 1 Feb 1787 – 8 Oct 1863 and was Church of Ireland Archbishop of Dublin from 1831. [source: WP-Richard_Whately]
- Whately quoted in IEP: "A very long discussion is one of the most effective veils of Fallacy; ...a Fallacy, which when stated barely...would not deceive a child, may deceive half the world if diluted in a quarto volume." [source: IEP-F §1] (IEP glosses "quarto volume" as "an eight-page booklet"; do not repeat that gloss: a quarto volume is a large book.)
- **J. S. Mill**, *A System of Logic* (1843), Book V: the first extensive treatment of inductive fallacies; "A catalogue of the varieties of evidence which are not real evidence ... is an enumeration of fallacies" (V.iii.1); distinguishes moral causes (bias, indifference to truth) from intellectual errors; "the most extensive work on fallacies since Aristotle". [source: SEP-F §2.8]
- Alfred Sidgwick, *Fallacies: A View of Logic from the Practical Side* (1884): argument evaluation as a "sieve". [source: SEP-F §2.9]
- Irving Copi, *Introduction to Logic* (1961 ed. cited): 18 informal fallacies, eleven Aristotelian, seven from the post-Lockean *ad*-tradition; a fallacy is "a form of argument that seems to be correct but which proves, upon examination, not to be so". [source: SEP-F §1, §2.10]

### India (Nyāya and Buddhist debate theory) — cross-ref *The Classical Indian Schools*, *Logic across Traditions*
- *Nyāya-sūtra*, attributed to Gautama/Akṣapāda: SEP-LI dates it c. 2nd century CE, final redaction perhaps 3rd; IEP-N c. 200 CE; SEP-ARG supplement "widely available by 150 CE (exact dates ... uncertain)"; WP-Fallacy gives a range from the 6th century BCE to the 2nd century CE. Use "around the second century CE". [sources as named]
- Much of the text concerns debate: all of Book 5, all of Book 1 ch. 2, and several sūtras of Book 1 ch. 1; the aphorisms are often little more than lists of terms. [source: SEP-LI §3.1.3]
- Sixteen topics of NS 1.1.1, including discussion (*vāda*), sophistic disputation (*jalpa*), cavil (*vitaṇḍā*), fallacies/pseudo-reasons (*hetvābhāsa*), quibbles (*chala*), futile rejoinders (*jāti*) and points of defeat (*nigrahasthāna*). [source: WP-Nyāya_Sūtras; WP-Nyaya; IEP-N §1]
- Three kinds of debate: *vāda* (aims at truth, between teacher and student or equals); *jalpa* and *vitaṇḍā* (aim at victory). In *vitaṇḍā* no counter-thesis is established; the debater only attacks. Jalpa permits quibbling, illegitimate rejoinders and "clinchers"; the text lists 3 kinds of quibble, 24 illegitimate rejoinders, 22 clinchers. [source: WP-Debates_in_ancient_India, citing Matilal, *The Character of Logic in India*, 1998, pp. 45–51]
- **hetvābhāsa** ("pseudo-provers"/"specious reasons"): named at NS 1.2.4–9 (and VS 3.1.10–11), but per SEP "neither examples nor explanations" are given; WP says the text identifies five kinds at NS 1.2.4 and discusses each in following sūtras. [source: SEP-LI §3.3; WP-Nyāya_Sūtras]
- IEP's five common kinds with examples: (i) deviation (the mark is not reliably correlated with the target: "my mother must be visiting, since there is a Mazda parked outside"); (ii) contradiction (the reason proves the opposite); (iii) unestablished (the reason does not belong to the subject); (iv) rebutted (overridden by stronger evidence, e.g. perception); (v) counterbalanced (an equally strong counter-argument). [source: IEP-N §1b.iii]
- Sanskrit names as given in WP-Fallacy: *asiddha* (unproved), *savyabhicāra* (irregular), *satpratipakṣa* (counterbalanced; example "Sound is eternal, because it is audible" vs "Sound is non-eternal, because it is produced"), *bādhita* (contradicted by another proof; "Fire is cold because it is a substance"), *viruddha* (proves the opposite; "Sound is eternal because it is produced"). WP-Nyāya_Sūtras calls NS 1.2.9's kind "the untimely" (sublated). [source: WP-Fallacy; WP-Nyāya_Sūtras]
- Nyāya does not stress validity vs soundness: formal flaws and false premises are both *hetvābhāsa*. [source: IEP-N §1b] (Matches the published *Classical Indian Schools* chapter.)
- NS 5.1 lists 24 fallacious rejoinders (*jāti*); NS 5.2 sets out 22 grounds for defeat (*nigraha-sthāna*), ranging "from making fallacious arguments to infringing the rules of debate procedure". The medical *Caraka-saṃhitā* (CS 3.8.50–65) lists 14 cases of defeat and identifies three defective arguments (CS 3.8.57); Buddhist works (*Vāda-vidhi*, *Rúshí lùn*, Asaṅga's *Hetu-vidyā*) treat the same topics; the Chinese *Fāng biàn xīn lùn* lists 20 rejoinders and, "surprisingly", treats them as good ones. [source: SEP-LI §3.3]
- *Caraka-saṃhitā* divides debate into peaceful (*anuloma sambhāṣā*) and hostile (*vigṛhya sambhāṣā*). [source: WP-Debates_in_ancient_India]
- Indian manuals helped novices "identify and rebut" tricks; Indian theories of fallacies "served purposes similar to Aristotle's Sophistical Refutations" (Ganeri 2001). [source: SEP-ARG Historical Supplement §1]
- Nāgārjuna's *Vaidalya-prakaraṇa* attacks each term of NS 1.1.1, judging counter-arguments fallacious as circular or regressive. [source: SEP-LI §3.1.3]
- Hamblin's *Fallacies* (1970) has a chapter on the Indian tradition. [source: SEP-F §3.1]

### China (later Mohists) — cross-ref *Mozi, the Daoists and the Legalists*, *Logic across Traditions*
- The later Mohist "Lesser Selection" (*Xiǎo Qǔ*) names four analogical techniques (analogy, "paralleling" *móu*, "pulling", "pushing") and warns that each can mislead: "Things have respects in which they are similar, yet it doesn't follow that they are completely similar. Parallels between expressions are correct only up to a point ... expressions in analogies, paralleling, 'pulling,' and 'pushing' become different as they proceed, become dangerous as they change direction, fail when taken too far, and leave their roots as they flow, and so one cannot fail to be cautious and cannot invariably use them." (Fraser's translation) [source: SEP-MO §7.3]
- Example of a misleading parallel: "white horses are horses, riding white horses is riding horses" is acceptable ("this and so"), but "carts are wood, riding carts is riding wood" is not ("this but not so"). The Mohists used the distinction to defend punishing robbers against a charge of inconsistency: "robbers are people, but killing robbers is not killing people". [source: SEP-MO §7.3] (A good cross-cultural example of an alleged fallacy of form and its defence.)

## 3. Modern fallacy theory
- **Charles Leonard Hamblin** (20 Nov 1922 – 14 May 1985), Australian philosopher, logician and computer pioneer (introduced Reverse Polish Notation), professor at the NSW University of Technology (now UNSW). *Fallacies*, London: Methuen, 1970; reissued by Vale Press. [source: WP-Charles_Leonard_Hamblin; SEP-F bibliography]
- Hamblin on "the standard treatment": "as debased, worn-out and dogmatic a treatment as could be imagined—incredibly tradition bound, yet lacking in logic and in historical sense alike, and almost without connection to anything else in modern Logic at all." (1970, 12). He criticised Copi among others; his book was "the first book-length work devoted to fallacies in modern times" and divides "traditional approaches" from "new, contemporary approaches". His positive proposals: formal dialectic, the concept of argument, equivocation. [source: SEP-F §2.10, §3.1]
- Hamblin's diagnosis that rules of dialogue can be misconceived and so "conceal and facilitate dialectical malpractice" (1970, 254) led to dialogue-based theories. [source: SEP-F §3.6]
- Hitchcock (2023), studying six widely used textbooks (each 10+ editions), finds "little improvement" by Hamblin's standard. [source: SEP-F §4.3]
- Finocchiaro (1981): textbooks contain few real examples; he doubts that "types of logically incorrect arguments" are common, and prefers "fallacious arguments" to "fallacies". [source: SEP-F §3.2]
- Gerald Massey (1981), "The fallacy behind fallacies": no theory of invalidity; an argument is not proved invalid by instantiating an invalid form, since valid arguments also instantiate invalid forms (asymmetry thesis). "a theory of fallacy presupposes a theory of invalidity (…) there is no method whatsoever of establishing invalidity that has theoretical legitimacy" (Massey 1981, 494). [source: SEP-F §3.2; SEP-ARG §2.5]
- **Informal logic**: Johnson and Blair, *Logical Self-Defense* (1977): a cogent argument has premises that are acceptable, relevant and sufficient; a fallacy is "any argument that violates one of the criteria of good argument … and is committed frequently in argumentative discourse" (1993, 317–18); Johnson (1987) replaced the appearance condition by a frequency condition. New entries: dubious assumption, two wrongs, slippery slope, faulty analogy. [source: SEP-F §3.3]
- **Woods and Walton**: the textbook fallacies "are a kind of caricature of their associated improprieties, which lie deeply imbedded in human practice" (Woods 1992, 25); "methodological pluralism" matches each fallacy with a fitting logic (inductive logic for hasty generalisation and post hoc, plausible reasoning for *ad verecundiam*, dialectical games for begging the question). Woods and Walton, *Fallacies: Selected Papers, 1972–1982* (Foris, 1989). Woods (2013) later reads many fallacies as reasonable heuristics. [source: SEP-F §3.4]
- **Epistemic approach** (Biro and Siegel 1992): the function of argument is to move from known or justified beliefs to unknown ones; begging the question is a fallacy because the premises cannot be known independently of the conclusion (Biro's "old Etonians" example: All committee members are old Etonians; Fortesque is a committee member; so Fortesque is an old Etonian — question-begging unless there is an independent way to know the major premise, such as a bylaw). [source: SEP-F §3.5]
- **Pragma-dialectics** (Frans van Eemeren and Rob Grootendorst, University of Amsterdam; 1984 onward): argument as a critical discussion aimed at resolving a difference of opinion, with four stages (confrontation, opening, argumentation, concluding) and ten rules; "every violation of any of the rules ... for conducting a critical discussion" is a fallacy (2004, 175). The definition drops all three SDF conditions; it admits "new fallacies" such as declaring a standpoint sacrosanct and evading the burden of proof. Ad baculum violates the rule against preventing the other party from expressing a view; equivocation violates the clarity rule; post hoc violates the rule that argument schemes be correctly applied. [source: SEP-F §3.6; WP-Pragma-dialectics]
- The ten rules (wording from WP-Pragma-dialectics): (1) Freedom: parties must not prevent each other from advancing or doubting standpoints; (2) Burden of proof: whoever advances a standpoint must defend it if asked; (3) Standpoint: an attack must relate to the standpoint actually advanced; (4) Relevance: defend a standpoint only with argumentation relating to it; (5) Unexpressed premise: do not deny your own implicit premise or falsely attribute one; (6) Starting point: do not falsely present a premise as accepted, nor deny an accepted one; (7) Argument scheme: a defence is conclusive only via an appropriate, correctly applied scheme; (8) Validity: arguments must be valid or made valid by explicit unexpressed premises; (9) Closure: a failed defence means retracting the standpoint, a successful one means retracting the doubt; (10) Usage: no unclear or ambiguous formulations, and interpret the other side carefully. [source: WP-Pragma-dialectics] (Rule 3 is the natural home of the straw man; that mapping is an inference, not a quotation.)
- Strategic manoeuvring (van Eemeren 2010): "All derailments of strategic maneuvering are fallacies"; each fallacy has "sound counterparts", which is why it "may pass unnoticed". [source: SEP-F §3.6]
- Key book: van Eemeren and Grootendorst, *A Systematic Theory of Argumentation: The Pragma-Dialectical Approach* (Cambridge UP, 2004). [source: SEP-F bibliography; WP-Pragma-dialectics]
- **Douglas N. Walton** (2 June 1942 – 3 Jan 2020), Canadian; PhD Toronto 1972; later at the University of Windsor (CRRAR). He "has written more about fallacies and fallacy theory than anyone else": *Begging the Question* (1991), *Slippery Slope Arguments* (1992), *A Pragmatic Theory of Fallacy* (1995), *Ad Hominem Arguments* (1998). [source: SEP-F §3.6; WP-Douglas_N._Walton]
- Walton's definition (1995, 255): a fallacy is (1) an argument (or something purporting to be one) that (2) falls short of some standard of correctness, (3) is used in a context of dialogue, (4) has a semblance of correctness, and (5) poses a serious problem to the goal of the dialogue. Short version: "a deceptively bad argument that impedes the progress of a dialogue" (1995, 256). He recognises several dialogue types (persuasion, inquiry, negotiation...); paralogisms vs sophisms; unintentional slips are "blunders" (1995, 235). [source: SEP-F §3.6]
- **Argumentation schemes and critical questions**: schemes are patterns of defeasible reasoning (e.g. appeal to expert opinion, ad hominem) that are "sometimes used fairly, and, other times, fallaciously"; critical questions decide which. Answering yes creates a presumption, not a guarantee. [source: SEP-F §3.6] Books: Walton, *Argumentation Schemes for Presumptive Reasoning* (1996); Walton, Reed and Macagno, *Argumentation Schemes* (Cambridge UP, 2008). [source: WP-Argumentation_scheme]
- Six critical questions for argument from expert opinion (Walton): expertise (how credible is E as an expert?), field (is E an expert in the field of A?), opinion (what did E assert that implies A?), trustworthiness (is E personally reliable?), consistency (is A consistent with what other experts say?), backup evidence (is E's assertion based on evidence?). [source: WP-Argumentation_scheme]
- Critical questions for argument from ignorance: how far has the search for evidence gone? Which side has the burden of proof? How strong must the proof be? [source: WP-Argumentation_scheme]
- Virtue argumentation theory (e.g. Cohen 2009): fallacies arise from arguers' vices. Akin and Casey treat straw man, "bothsiderism" and free-speech fallacies as meta-argumentation fallacies (*Straw Man Arguments*, Bloomsbury 2022). [source: SEP-F §3.6]
- **Bayesian approach**: Hahn and Oaksford, "The Rationality of Informal Argumentation: A Bayesian Approach to Reasoning Fallacies", *Psychological Review* 114 (2007): 704–732. Many so-called fallacies can be good arguments that "considerably raise the probability of the conclusion"; even affirming the consequent can give support, depending on content and prior probabilities, not form. [source: SEP-ARG §5.3 and bibliography] (Cross-ref *Induction, Probability and Inference to the Best Explanation*.)
- **Teaching debate**: some argue teaching fallacies makes students "overly critical" and see fallacies where there are none, so teach positive criteria instead (Hitchcock 1995); Blair (1995) replies that non-perfunctory teaching works; Blair (2023) now thinks the field too complex for introductory courses. [source: SEP-F §4.3] Hitchcock (1995, 324): teaching reasoning by fallacies is "like saying that the best way to teach somebody to play tennis without making the common mistakes is to demonstrate these faults in action and get him to label and respond to them". [source: SEP-IL §4.2] Schwartz (1981): "Some appeals to authority are fallacious; most are not ... the hard work comes in judging and showing that this condition holds, and that is where the fallacy-label-texts leave off." [source: IEP-F §5]
- **Fallacies with and without legitimate cousins**: "Equivocation, post hoc ergo propter hoc, non sequitor and hasty generalization are ... forms of argument that seem inherently mistaken. In contrast, traditional fallacies like ad hominem, two wrongs reasoning, guilt by association, and appeal to pity define patterns of reasoning which can play a legitimate logical role." [source: SEP-IL §4.2]
- **Fallacies and biases**: Mill saw biases as predisposing causes of fallacies; Correia (2011) links particular biases to particular fallacies; Thagard (2011) argues motivated inference is unconscious, so "Overcoming people's motivated inferences ... is therefore more akin to psychotherapy than informal logic." SEP's reply: biases explain why people commit fallacies but not what the mistake is, which is a normative question. [source: SEP-F §4.4]

## 4. The individual fallacies (definition, example, when it is not a fallacy)
### Ad hominem (abusive, circumstantial, tu quoque)
- Definition: bringing negative aspects of an arguer or their situation to bear on their view. Abusive: rejecting a view because of an unfavourable property (SEP example: rejecting Thompson's wetlands proposal because she was arrested for hunting without a licence). Circumstantial: alleging the position serves self-interest (industry pollution studies). Tu quoque: rejecting advice because the adviser does not follow it (a neighbour who does not exercise). [source: SEP-F §1.13]
- IEP: "an irrelevant attack on the person"; example: dismissing a fifteen-year-old's reasoning about Kepler because of her age. "The major difficulty ... is deciding whether the personal attack is relevant or irrelevant": sexual conduct is irrelevant to her astronomy but relevant to promotion to church leadership. [source: IEP-F "Ad Hominem"]
- Legitimate uses: Walton holds circumstantial ad hominem can be non-fallacious when the person's character is relevant, e.g. a trial witness caught lying and cheating (Walton 2001, 213). An attorney who shows a witness was convicted of lying commits no fallacy by concluding the witness should not be trusted, but does if concluding the witness is lying now. [source: WP-Ad_hominem] SEP-IL's example: a Danish TV debater's charge of conflict of interest "raises legitimate questions". [source: SEP-IL §4.2]
- Tu quoque: IEP: "Discovering that a speaker is a hypocrite is a reason to be suspicious of the speaker's reasoning, but it is not a sufficient reason to discount it." [source: IEP-F "Tu Quoque"] Example: a father who smoked telling his daughter not to smoke. [source: WP-Ad_hominem, citing Walton 2001, 211]
- Older, non-fallacious sense (argument *ex concessis*, "from commitment"): using an opponent's own beliefs against them; found in Sextus Empiricus, Galileo, Locke; Whately: arguments "addressed to the peculiar circumstances, character, avowed opinions, or past conduct of the individual". [source: WP-Ad_hominem; SEP-F §2.4, §2.7]
- Madison, *Federalist* No. 40: "The prudent enquiry in all cases, ought surely to be not so much *from whom* the advice comes, as whether the advice be *good*." [source: WP-Ad_hominem, linking founders.archives.gov]

### Straw man
- A variant of *ignoratio elenchi*: an opponent's view is distorted to make it easier to refute. SEP example: replacing "industrialization causes global warming" with "all ills are due to industrialization"; two errors: the proponent does not hold it, and its falsity would not refute the original. [source: SEP-F §1.8]
- Port-Royal already extended *ignoratio elenchi* to "attribut[ing] to our adversaries something remote from their views". Watts (1724): disputers attack "images of straw of their own making". [source: SEP-F §2.3, §2.5; WP-Straw_man]
- Walton found the first textbook inclusion in Stuart Chase, *Guides to Straight Thinking* (1956); Hamblin's *Fallacies* (1970) does not mention it at all. [source: WP-Straw_man]
- Variants: Talisse and Aikin (2006) distinguish representative and selection forms; Aikin and Casey (2010) name them straw man, "weak man" and "hollow man" (wholly invented opponent: "some say..."). "Steelmanning" is the opposite, the principle of charity. [source: WP-Straw_man]
- Not every simplification is a straw man; the fault is inaccurate and unfavourable distortion (Akin and Casey). [source: SEP-F §3.6]

### Begging the question (petitio principii) and circularity
- Whately's example: "to allow every man an unbounded freedom of speech must always be, on the whole, advantageous to the State; for it is highly conducive to the interest of the Community, that each individual should enjoy a liberty perfectly unlimited, of expressing his sentiments." Premise and conclusion are the same proposition in different words. [source: SEP-F §1.5]
- Contextual version: "Capital punishment requires an act of murdering human beings. So, capital punishment is wrong" begs the question where it is disputed that execution is murder. [source: SEP-F §1.5]
- Not invalid; best treated as epistemic rather than logical. Circular reasoning: Descartes' example of believing the Bible because it is God's word and God because the Bible says so. [source: SEP-F §1.5]
- The circularity debate: every valid argument's conclusion is in some sense "contained" in its premises; logicians disagree why some valid arguments beg the question and others do not (psychological novelty; how the premises were justified; whether a premise could be defended independently). [source: IEP-F "Begging the Question"] Biro and Siegel's epistemic answer (§3 above).
- Usage: in modern everyday English "begs the question" often means "raises the question"; this usage "is criticized for diluting the original meaning". [source: WP-Begging_the_question]
- Cross-ref: published chapters already use the charge, e.g. Crispin Wright's "transmission failure" against Moore's proof (*Birth of Analytic Philosophy*) and Broad against Kant's First Antinomy (*Kant*).

### False dilemma
- A premise that wrongly limits the options, typically treating two contraries (both possibly false) as contradictories (one must be true). The fault is a false premise, not invalid form. [source: WP-False_dilemma]
- IEP example: "Will you contribute $20 to our environmental fund, or are you on the side of environmental destruction?" Not a fallacy: "Will you contribute $20 or won't you?" "everything is either black or not black, but not everything is either black or white." [source: IEP-F "Black-or-White"]

### Slippery slope
- Form: from a starting point, a series of steps leads to an unwanted end, so reject the start. Steps may be causal (SEP's "not going to college" chain) or rest on vagueness (beard, heap). [source: SEP-F §1.15]
- IEP: "Arguments of this form may or may not be fallacious depending on the probabilities involved in each step"; with three steps at 80% each, the chance of reaching the end is "about 50%" (0.8³ = 0.512). [source: IEP-F "Slippery Slope"]
- Types: causal vs judgmental (conceptual, decisional). Walton requires "a gray zone ... where the agent loses control". Metaphor goes back to Cicero, *Laelius de Amicitia* XII.41; Sidgwick (1910) on the "thin end of a wedge". [source: WP-Slippery_slope]
- Eugene Volokh, "The Mechanisms of the Slippery Slope", *Harvard Law Review* 116 (2003): 1026–1137: a slippery slope is any case "where decision A, which you might find appealing, ends up materially increasing the probability that others will bring about decision B, which you oppose". [source: WP-Slippery_slope]
- Reasonable example: Churchill's argument against the Munich Agreement (that it would embolden Germany) "cannot be summarily dismissed as fallacious". [source: SEP-IL §4.2] CAUTION: SEP-IL says "Early in World War II", but Munich was September 1938, before the war; say "in 1938".

### Appeal to authority (ad verecundiam)
- Fallacy: accepting as evidence the word of someone who is not really, or not relevantly, an authority (celebrity endorsements), or relying on some authorities where experts are divided. [source: SEP-F §1.9]
- IEP: "Most reasoning of this kind is not fallacious, and much of our knowledge properly comes from listening to authorities"; fallacious when the authority is not an authority on this subject, cannot be trusted, authorities disagree, or is misquoted. [source: IEP-F "Appeal to Authority"]
- Walton's scheme: E is an expert in S; E asserts p on the basis of knowledge of S; so p. Defeasible; the critical questions (see §3). [source: SEP-F §3.6]
- Cautionary case: Theophilus Painter's 1923 count of 24 human chromosome pairs was repeated on his authority until 1956 (correct number 23). [source: WP-Argument_from_authority]
- Bentham gave four conditions for reliable appeals to authority. [source: SEP-F §2.6]

### Appeal to ignorance (ad ignorantiam) and burden of proof
- IEP: taking not knowing that a statement is true as proof it is false, or vice versa; it is "proper reasoning only if the proof attempts were quite thorough, and ... if the being or object were to exist, then there would be a discoverable proof". [source: IEP-F "Appeal to Ignorance"]
- Legitimate example (Walton): a train timetable that does not list Schiphol licenses inferring the train does not stop there. [source: WP-Argumentation_scheme]
- Legal presumption of innocence: "It has not been proven that the defendant is guilty. Therefore, the defendant is not guilty." [source: WP-Argument_from_ignorance] Note: the verdict "not guilty" is a legal status fixed by the burden of proof, not a finding that the accused did not do it.
- Background: *Digest* 22.3.2 (Justinian, 6th century), attributed to Paulus: *Ei incumbit probatio qui dicit, non qui negat* ("Proof lies on him who asserts, not on him who denies"); UDHR Article 11; Lord Sankey's "golden thread" (*Woolmington v DPP*, 1935). [source: WP-Presumption_of_innocence]
- Carl Sagan, *The Demon-Haunted World*: "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence". [source: WP-Argument_from_ignorance]

### Post hoc ergo propter hoc
- Inferring causation from temporal succession alone (SEP: falling unemployment after a gasoline-tax cut). Related errors: reversing cause and effect, overlooking a common cause; "perhaps better understood as faults of explanation than faults of arguments". [source: SEP-F §1.7]
- IEP: "Correlations are often good evidence of causal connection, so the fallacy occurs only when the leap to the causal conclusion is done 'hastily.'" [source: IEP-F "Post Hoc"]
- Boudry et al.'s example: feeling sick after eating foraged mushrooms and blaming them is reasonable probabilistic reasoning, not a fallacy. [source: WS; WP-Fallacy]

### Hasty generalisation
- IEP: generalising from too small a sample; "I've met two people in Nicaragua ... So, all people I will meet in Nicaragua will be nice to me." Sample size depends on population homogeneity: "if we've seen one electron, we've seen them all." [source: IEP-F "Hasty Generalization", "Small Sample"]
- Entered the list via Port-Royal's "faulty induction" and Watts's "false induction". [source: SEP-F §2.3, §2.5]

### Equivocation
- "The end of life is death. Happiness is the end of life. So, death is happiness" ("end" as ceasing vs purpose). [source: SEP-F §1.1]
- Mill's "desirable"/"audible" step, as charged by Moore, is a classic alleged equivocation; consistent with the published *Utilitarianism* chapter.

### Composition and division
- Composition: from "every member of the team was an excellent researcher" to "it was an excellent team"; division is the reverse. [source: SEP-F §1.3–4] Everyday: "If someone stands up at a cricket match, they can see better. Therefore, if everyone stands up, they can all see better." [source: WP-Fallacy_of_composition]
- Not always fallacious: whether the property transfers depends on the property. (The published *Utilitarianism* chapter charges step 4 of Mill's proof with composition; consistent.)

### Genetic fallacy
- IEP: discrediting or supporting a claim "because of its origin (genesis) when such an appeal to origins is irrelevant". Not a fallacy where origin bears on reliability: asking for independent checks on a plan that came from the person who stands to profit. Hard case: Freud on belief in God. [source: IEP-F "Genetic"]
- Term's origin disputed: the *Oxford Companion to Philosophy* (1995) credits Cohen and Nagel, *Logic and Scientific Method* (1934), but Mortimer Adler wrote of "the fallacy of genetic interpretation" in 1926. [source: WP-Genetic_fallacy]
- CONSISTENCY: the published *Nietzsche* chapter defines it as "discrediting a claim by its origin when the origin is irrelevant (see *Informal Fallacies and Everyday Reasoning*)" and discusses whether the *Genealogy* commits it. Keep this chapter's definition the same.

### Red herring
- A diversion to an irrelevant issue (a fallacy of relevance); unlike the straw man it does not distort the opponent. The expression was popularised in 1807 by William Cobbett's story of using a smoked herring to divert hounds. [source: WP-Red_herring]

### Appeal to emotion, pity (ad misericordiam) and force (ad baculum)
- Ad baculum ("appeal to the stick"): threats give reasons to *act* (strikes, trade sanctions are not normally called fallacies), but are irrelevant evidence for *belief* ("believe X is the best candidate or you will be evicted"). Ad misericordiam likewise has "its natural home in practical reasoning". [source: SEP-F §1.11–12]
- IEP: "There is nothing wrong with using emotions when you argue, but it's a mistake to use emotions as the key premises or as tools to downplay relevant information." [source: IEP-F "Appeal to Emotions"] SEP-IL: appeals to pity "have ... a legitimate role to play in moral, political and aesthetic debate". [source: SEP-IL §4.2]

### Bandwagon (ad populum)
- Claiming something is true or right because many think so. [source: WP-Argumentum_ad_populum] IEP: the fallacy is suggesting this "too strongly", "because what most everyone believes is, for that reason, somewhat likely to be true"; the error is overestimating that support. [source: IEP-F "Appeal to the People"]

### Two wrongs; others
- Martin Luther King Jr.'s arguments for civil disobedience answer one wrong with another but "cannot be summarily dismissed as instances of the fallacy 'two wrongs make a right'". [source: SEP-IL §4.2]
- Complex (loaded) question: "Are you still a member of the Ku Klux Klan?"; some say it is not a fallacy because a question is not an argument. [source: SEP-F §1.6]
- Faulty analogy: "If a child gets a new toy he will want to play with it; so if a nation gets new weapons it will want to use them" (Churchill 1986, a textbook author, not Winston). [source: SEP-F §1.14]

### The fallacy fallacy (argument from fallacy)
- Inferring that a conclusion is false because an argument for it is fallacious; also called *argumentum ad logicam*. Example: Alice argues invalidly that Ginger is a cat; Bob concludes Ginger is *not* a cat. A form of denying the antecedent. [source: WP-Argument_from_fallacy]

## 5. Is "fallacy" a useful category? Argument set out step by step
**The Fallacy Fork** (Maarten Boudry, Fabio Paglieri and Massimo Pigliucci, "The Fake, the Flimsy, and the Fallacious: Demarcating Arguments in Real Life", *Argumentation* 29 (2015): 431–456; Boudry popularised it in "The Fallacy Fork: Why It's Time to Get Rid of Fallacy Theory", *Skeptical Inquirer* 41(5) (2017): 46–51). [source: WS (abstract); WP-Fallacy]
1. A named fallacy must be defined either strictly, as a demonstrably invalid form, or loosely, with qualifications about context and probability.
2. Defined strictly, it "has very limited applicability in real life": real arguments are rarely meant as deductions (Massey's asymmetry adds that invalidity is hard even to prove). [WS abstract; SEP-F §3.2]
3. Defined loosely, it can "no longer be held up as an effective tool for discriminating good and bad forms of reasoning", because "almost every known type of fallacy is a close neighbor to sound inferences or acceptable moves in a debate" (pragmatics, probabilistic reasoning, ecological rationality). [WS abstract]
4. Even approaches without schemes (pragma-dialectics) meet their own version of the fork. [WS abstract]
5. So fallacy-naming is not an effective way to tell good from bad reasoning; the work is done by context-specific assessment. (Commentators such as Paul Braterman suggest dropping the label for informal arguments.) [WS]
- **Objection 1 (Walton and schemes):** the loose horn is not empty: argumentation schemes with critical questions give principled, checkable criteria for when an ad hominem or expert appeal is fair or fallacious. [source: SEP-F §3.6]
- **Reply:** that supports the fork's moral: the evaluative work is done by the critical questions about the particular case, not by the fallacy label, as Schwartz (1981) said: the further a text goes in stating criteria, "the less it need to emphasize or even to use fallacy labels". [source: IEP-F §5]
- **Objection 2:** some fallacies really are inherently mistaken (equivocation, hasty generalisation), and names are useful mental packages (Pinker, quoted in IEP); Steven Novella (Neurologica blog, 2017) argues critical thinkers can still use fallacies to police arguments. [source: SEP-IL §4.2; IEP-F §3; WS]
- **Reply/middle position:** treat names as prompts for questions, not verdicts; and the charge of fallacy itself carries a burden of proof (IEP). [source: IEP-F intro]
- Older version of the same debate: Hamblin's critique of the standard treatment (1970), Finocchiaro (1981) and Massey (1981); the chief objection is "that one cannot have a self-standing theory of error, which is infinite in kinds (Hamblin 1970)". [source: SEP-ARG §2.5]

## 6. Everyday reasoning and the news
- **Base-rate neglect**: when a condition is rarer than a test's false-positive rate, most positives are false. WP's breathalyzer example: 1 in 1,000 drivers drunk; the test never misses a drunk driver; it falsely flags 50 of the 999 sober ones; so a positive result means about 1 chance in 51 (about 2%) of being drunk. Natural-frequency formats (counts rather than percentages) help people reason correctly (Gigerenzer; Cochrane recommends them). [source: WP-Base_rate_fallacy]
- Kahneman and Tversky explained base-rate neglect by the representativeness heuristic (Kahneman and Tversky, "On the psychology of prediction", *Psychological Review* 80 (1973): 237–251; Tversky and Kahneman, "Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases", *Science* 185 (1974)). Debate: heuristics-and-biases researchers see human probabilistic thinking as flawed; others (information-format researchers) say the conclusion is not generally warranted. [source: WP-Base_rate_fallacy]
- Conjunction fallacy (Linda problem, Tversky and Kahneman): most judge "bank teller and active feminist" more probable than "bank teller", which is impossible: P(A and B) ≤ P(A). Stephen Jay Gould: "a little homunculus in my head continues to jump up and down, shouting at me—'but she can't just be a bank teller; read the description.'" [source: WP-Conjunction_fallacy]
- **Confirmation bias**: tendency to search for, interpret, favour and recall information confirming prior beliefs. Peter Wason's 2-4-6 task (1960) and selection task (1968); Wason coined the term. Klayman and Ha (1987) reinterpret it as a "positive test strategy". Review: Nickerson, "Confirmation bias: A ubiquitous phenomenon in many guises", *Review of General Psychology* 2 (1998). [source: WP-Confirmation_bias] IEP: "people of opposing views can each find support for those views in the same piece of evidence". [source: IEP-F "Confirmation Bias"]
- Lord, Ross and Lepper (1979, *JPSP* 37), Stanford death-penalty study: people with opposite views read the same mixed evidence; 23% reported their views became more extreme ("biased assimilation", "attitude polarization"). Replication has been mixed. The "backfire effect" (Nyhan and Reifler 2010) has largely failed to replicate (a study of 10,100 participants on 52 issues). [source: WP-Confirmation_bias]
- **Motivated reasoning**: Ziva Kunda, "The Case for Motivated Reasoning", *Psychological Bulletin* (1990): accuracy goals vs directional goals; "several different kinds of biases have been shown to weaken in the presence of accuracy goals". [source: WP-Motivated_reasoning] Mercier and Sperber, *The Enigma of Reason* (Harvard, 2017): reasoning evolved for argument; we are better at evaluating others' arguments than producing good ones. [source: SEP-ARG §5.3]
- **Lateral reading**: Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew, "Lateral Reading: Reading Less and Learning More When Evaluating Digital Information", Stanford History Education Group Working Paper No. 2017-A1 (6 Oct 2017); published as "Lateral Reading and the Nature of Expertise: Reading Less and Learning More When Evaluating Digital Information", *Teachers College Record* 121 (2019). Participants: 10 PhD historians, 10 professional fact-checkers, 25 Stanford undergraduates. Historians and students read "vertically", staying on a site and being misled by surface cues; fact-checkers left the site quickly and opened new tabs to see what others said about the source, reaching better-warranted conclusions "in a fraction of the time". [source: WS]
- SHEG, "Evaluating Information: The Cornerstone of Civic Online Reasoning" (22 Nov 2016; Wineburg, McGrew, Breakstone, Ortega): 7,804 student responses from 12 states; the authors report 82% of middle-school students failed to identify an item labelled "sponsored content" as an advertisement (outlets round to "more than 80%"). [source: WS]
- SHEG became the independent nonprofit Digital Inquiry Group (DIG); sources disagree whether in 2023 or January 2024. [source: WS]
- Mike Caulfield's SIFT moves (introduced as "four moves" in early 2017, "SIFT" in 2019): Stop; Investigate the source; Find better coverage; Trace claims to their original context. Caulfield and Wineburg, *Verified* (University of Chicago Press, 2023). [source: WS]
- Bayesian thinking (brief; cross-ref the Induction chapter): update beliefs by combining prior probability (base rate) with how much more likely the evidence is if the claim is true than if false; the base-rate examples above are the everyday illustration. [source: WP-Base_rate_fallacy; SEP-ARG §5.3]

## Debates
- What fallacies are: logical (SDF), epistemic (Biro and Siegel), dialectical (pragma-dialectics; Walton's dialogue types), or a hodgepodge with no single theory (Hansen). [source: SEP-F §4.1]
- The appearance condition: kept by Walton and Powers ("an appearance, however quickly seen through, of being valid"); dropped by Finocchiaro, Biro and Siegel, Johnson (replaced by frequency) and pragma-dialecticians (too subjective, belongs to psychology). [source: SEP-F §4.2]
- Should fallacies be taught? Hitchcock and (now) Blair vs fallacy-based teaching; the Fallacy Fork (Boudry et al.) vs defenders such as Novella. [source: SEP-F §4.3; SEP-IL §4.2; WS]
- Reading Aristotle's fallacies: dialectical (Hamblin, Hintikka) vs deductive/non-dialogical (Woods and Hansen). [source: SEP-F §2.1.5]
- Is circularity always a fault? Which valid arguments beg the question (IEP's three positions; Biro and Siegel). [source: IEP-F; SEP-F §3.5]
- Base-rate neglect: robust flaw in human probability (heuristics and biases) vs artefact of information format (Gigerenzer and others). [source: WP-Base_rate_fallacy]
- Slippery slope: causal vs decisional/conceptual; whether "loss of control" is essential (Walton) or any probability-raising chain counts (Volokh). [source: WP-Slippery_slope]
- Origin of "genetic fallacy" (Cohen and Nagel 1934 vs Adler 1926). [source: WP-Genetic_fallacy]
