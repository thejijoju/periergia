## 1. Al-Ghazālī: life
- Full name Abū Ḥāmid Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad al-Ghazālī. Later medieval historians say he was born 1058 or 1059 in Ṭabarān-Ṭūs (near modern Mashhad, NE Iran); remarks about his age in his letters and autobiography point instead to 1055 or 1056 (Griffel 2009). [source: SEP-Gh §1] SEP-Gh, SEP-Occ and SEP-Caus give "c. 1056 (or c. 1055)–1111"; IEP-Gh's heading gives c. 1056–1111 but its text says "born c. 1058"; WP-Al-Ghazali and IEP-IR give c. 1058. WRITER: say "born in 1055/56 or 1058, the sources disagree" — do NOT print a bare "1058–1111".
- He studied with the leading Ashʿarite theologian al-Juwaynī (1028–85) at the Niẓāmiyya madrasa in Nishapur, which brought him close to the Seljuq sultan Malikshāh (r. 1071–92) and vizier Niẓām al-Mulk (1018–92). [source: SEP-Gh §1]
- In 1091 Niẓām al-Mulk appointed him to the Niẓāmiyya madrasa in Baghdad. [sources: SEP-Gh §1; IEP-Gh §1] IEP: he claims at one point to have had 300 students. [IEP-Gh §1]
- In 1095 he suddenly gave up his posts and left Baghdad; under the influence of Sufi literature he had begun changing his life two years earlier; he concluded that serving sultans, viziers and caliphs meant complicity in corrupt rule. He went to Damascus and Jerusalem and vowed at Abraham's tomb in Hebron never again to serve the authorities or teach at state schools; pilgrimage 1096; then home to Ṭūs, where he founded a small private school and Sufi convent (khānqāh). [source: SEP-Gh §1]
- IEP-Gh: the crisis had psychosomatic effects, preventing him from speaking and so teaching; he left his post and family in 1095 and travelled "for ten years" cultivating the Sufi life. [IEP-Gh §1] WP-Al-Ghazali says the crisis caused loss of appetite and inability to speak. WRITER: SEP's itinerary (back in Ṭūs after 1096–7) is more precise than "ten years of wandering".
- In 1106 he broke his vow and returned to teach at the state Niẓāmiyya in Nishapur, justifying it by public theological confusion and court pressure; he saw himself as the "renewer" (mujaddid) promised at the start of each century (1106 began the 6th Islamic century). [source: SEP-Gh §1]
- In Nishapur he was accused of falsafa influence in the *Revival*; summoned before the Seljuq governor Sanjar (d. 1157), who acquitted him. He died in Ṭūs in December 1111. [source: SEP-Gh §1] WP-Al-Ghazali gives 19 December 1111.
- Title "Proof of Islam" (Ḥujjat al-Islām). [sources: IEP-Gh §1; WP-Al-Ghazali]
- IEP-Gh: he "did not regard himself as a philosopher", since philosophy then meant the Avicennan Aristotelian tradition, but regarded himself as Sufi, theologian and jurist; nevertheless he plainly did philosophy (logic, ethics, epistemology, metaphysics). [IEP-Gh intro]

## 2. Works and the *Deliverance from Error* (reliability as autobiography)
- *Deliverance from Error* (al-Munqidh min al-ḍalāl): a spiritual and intellectual autobiography written late in life; English translation R. J. McCarthy. [sources: IEP-Gh §2; SEP-Gh bibliography] IEP notes it is "written in the style of" an intellectual autobiography (Menn 2003). [IEP-Gh §2]
- In the *Deliverance* he says he studied the philosophers' works for two years at the Baghdad Niẓāmiyya and wrote the *Incoherence* in a third year; IEP quotes: "God Most High gave me an insight into the farthest reaches of the philosophers' sciences in less than two years", and reflected "assiduously on it for nearly a year" (McCarthy, *Deliverance* 61). [sources: SEP-Gh §2; IEP-Gh §3]
- RELIABILITY: Griffel (SEP-Gh §2) calls this account "hardly credible" and "apologetic", meant to rebut critics who said he learned falsafa before his religious education was complete; he most probably met falsafa while studying with al-Juwaynī, whose works already show Avicenna's influence; the *Incoherence* "may have been decades in the making". Also his own remarks on his age conflict with the historians' birth date (above). WRITER: present the *Deliverance* as a carefully shaped narrative, not a diary.
- The *Deliverance*'s skeptical episode: doubts about the senses (the apparently motionless shadow) and a "dream doubt" against even the first truths of reason; the crisis lasted about two months and was ended not by proof but by "a light which God Most High cast into my breast. And that light is the key to most knowledge" (McCarthy trans., *Deliverance* 57). [source: IEP-Gh §2c]
- Descartes comparison: G. H. Lewes (1857) said it resembled Descartes' *Discours* so closely that "everyone would have cried out against the plagiarism" had a translation existed; but the *Deliverance* was not translated until 1842 (French), and the theory that Descartes knew it (e.g. via Golius orally) rests on circumstantial evidence only; also the doubts work differently (Ghazālī never doubts God; he is rescued by divine light, Descartes by the natural light). [source: IEP-Gh §2d] (Cross-ref: Descartes chapter.)
- *Revival of the Religious Sciences* (Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn), begun after he left Baghdad; forty books in four quarters (ritual practices; social customs; things leading to perdition; things leading to salvation); his "most widely read work". [source: SEP-Gh §1, §5]
- *Doctrines of the Philosophers* (Maqāṣid al-falāsifa): a loose Arabic adaptation of Avicenna's Persian *Dānishnāma-yi ʿAlāʾī*; translated into Latin in the third quarter of the 12th century at Toledo (Dominicus Gundisalvi with "Magister Iohannes"), who dropped the introduction saying it was an uncommitted report — so Latin readers took "Algazel" for a faithful Avicennan; Albert the Great and Aquinas used it. It was the only Ghazālī work translated into Latin in that period. [source: SEP-Gh §2] Griffel: the older view that it was a preparatory study for the *Incoherence* "can no longer be upheld". [SEP-Gh §2]
- *The Decisive Criterion for Distinguishing Islam from Clandestine Unbelief* (Fayṣal al-tafriqa): only three "fundamental doctrines" define unbelief — monotheism, Muhammad's prophecy, and the Qur'anic account of the afterlife; other errors, even "innovations", should be tolerated. [source: SEP-Gh §4]
- Other works named in sources: *Niche of Lights* (Mishkāt al-anwār); *Moderation in Belief* (al-Iqtiṣād fī l-iʿtiqād, "distinctly occasionalist"); *Scandals of the Esoterics* (Faḍāʾiḥ al-Bāṭiniyya, against the Ismāʿīlīs); his last work *Iljām al-ʿawāmm*, completed days before his death. [source: SEP-Gh §3, §7.4 and bibliography]
- About 70 works can be attributed to him. [source: WP-Al-Ghazali]

## 3. Not anti-philosophy simpliciter: logic and demonstration
- Griffel: the *Incoherence* "has long been misunderstood in Western literature as an anti-philosophical pamphlet from a religious zealot", largely because it was read through Averroes' reply; its philosophical character "has only been acknowledged in the 21st century", after Marmura's full English translation (1997). [source: SEP-Gh §3]
- Against Ismāʿīlī claims that all rational proofs are equally balanced, Ghazālī championed Aristotelian demonstration (burhān); in a letter: "A [valid] rational demonstration is never wrong" (Griffel 2015). [source: SEP-Gh §4]
- His rule of interpretation: if a demonstration contradicts the literal sense of revelation, read the text allegorically (taʾwīl) — e.g. God's "hand" or "throne"; where no demonstration disproves the literal sense, allegory is not allowed. Almost all later Muslim theologians accepted this rule; Averroes accepted it too but disagreed about what had been demonstrated; Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328) rejected it. [source: SEP-Gh §4]
- In the *Deliverance*, logic "does not conflict with religion", concerning only proofs, syllogisms and definitions; mathematics contains undeniable facts; natural science is objectionable only on certain points. [source: IEP-Gh §3] "The intelligent man, therefore, first knows the truth, then he considers what is actually said by someone. If it is true, he accepts it…" (McCarthy, *Deliverance* 68). [source: IEP-Gh §3]
- Logic manuals: *Miʿyār al-ʿilm fī fann al-manṭiq* ("Standard of Knowledge") and *Miḥakk al-naẓar* reportedly completed 488/1095, *al-Qisṭās al-mustaqīm* ("The Just Balance") c. 497/1103; Miʿyār closest to Greek terminology, Miḥakk adapted to jurisprudence, Qisṭās framed for (anti-)Ismāʿīlī/Sufi purposes. [source: WS-3, a journal abstract and an Indonesian study — weak; dates approximate] SEP-Gh's bibliography confirms *al-Qisṭās al-mustaqīm* as his work. WRITER: name *Miʿyār al-ʿilm* as his logic manual written alongside the *Incoherence*; avoid "first book on Muslim logic".
- Through Ghazālī "begins the successful introduction of Aristotelianism or rather Avicennism into Muslim theology"; his acceptance of demonstration led to "a flowering of Aristotelian logics and metaphysics". [source: SEP-Gh intro] He adopted Avicenna's psychology and account of prophecy almost wholesale; Richard Frank (1992) concluded that the Avicennan theses he rejected "are relatively tame and inconsequential compared to those in which he follows the philosopher". [source: SEP-Gh §5, §7]
- His ethics in the *Revival* is a virtue ethics of character traits acquired by habituation (via Miskawayh, d. 1030, and al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī), never acknowledging its philosophical source; he introduced public benefit (maṣlaḥa) and the "five necessities" (religion, life, intellect, offspring, property) into jurisprudence. [source: SEP-Gh §5]

## 4. The *Incoherence of the Philosophers* (Tahāfut al-falāsifa): structure and the three condemned doctrines
- Published around 1095 (SEP: "around 1095 a number of books"); WP: "1095 CE book". English: M. E. Marmura, *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*, parallel Arabic–English, Brigham Young University Press (1st ed. 1997; 2nd ed. 2000). [sources: SEP-Gh §2–3 and bibliography; WP-The_Incoherence_of_the_Philosophers]
- Twenty discussions: sixteen on the philosophers' metaphysics (ilāhiyyāt), four on their natural sciences (ṭabīʿiyyāt). The first (eternity of the world) is the longest; the 17th concerns causality. [source: SEP-Gh §3]
- Aim: to show that none of the arguments for these twenty theses meets the philosophers' own standard of demonstration; the philosophers merely repeat their founders (taqlīd). Some "refuted" positions he actually held to be true — he denies only that they are demonstrated. [source: SEP-Gh §3] IEP quoting Marmura: "I do not enter into [argument] objecting to them, except as one who demands and denies, not as one who claims [and] affirms" (*Incoherence* 7). [IEP-Gh §4]
- Target: the Muslim falāsifa, chiefly al-Fārābī (d. 950) and Avicenna (c. 980–1037). [sources: SEP-Gh §2; IEP-Gh §4]
- Against eternity he used arguments from the Christian John Philoponus (c. 490–c. 570) against an infinite past. [source: SEP-Gh §3]
- At the end he judges seventeen theses mere "innovations" (bidʿa) but three are unbelief: (1) the world has no beginning in time; (2) God knows only universals, not particulars; (3) after death souls never return to bodies (no bodily resurrection). He adds a brief fatwā: whoever teaches these publicly is an unbeliever (kāfir) and apostate "who can be killed" (Marmura trans., p. 226). [sources: SEP-Gh §3; IEP-Gh §4] Griffel: "Whoever publicly supports or teaches the three named positions indeed deserves to be killed" (Griffel 2007: 103, via IEP).
- Discussion numbers: eternity = 1st; God's knowledge of particulars = 13th; bodily resurrection = 20th. [source: IEP-Gh §4a–c]
- God's knowledge (13th): Avicenna's eclipse example — if the object known changes, knowledge and so the knower changes; "But change in God is impossible" (Marmura 135). Ghazālī replies that the changes are only relational, not intrinsic to God. [source: IEP-Gh §4b]
- Resurrection (20th): includes the philosophers' cannibal objection (Marmura 217); Ghazālī answers that identity is by the soul, so the resurrected body's matter does not matter (Marmura 219). [source: IEP-Gh §4c]
- Eternity (1st): the philosophers' "strongest" proof — an eternal, unchanging will with all conditions fulfilled cannot delay its effect; Ghazālī: an eternal will can choose a moment; the philosophers offer only "unlikelihood" and an analogy with human will, not demonstration (Marmura 15, 17). [source: IEP-Gh §4a]
- In the later *Decisive Criterion* the eternity of the world is no longer raised as a criterion of unbelief. [source: SEP-Gh §3]

## 5. ARGUMENT: the 17th discussion on causation (cotton and fire), step by step
Primary text: Marmura translation (al-Ghazālī 2000a, pp. 166–74); reconstruction from SEP-Gh §7, SEP-Caus §3.3, §4.2, IEP-Gh §4d.
- Purpose: not an attack on causality as such but to force the philosophers to admit that the Qur'an's miracles are possible. [source: SEP-Gh §7.2; SEP-Caus §3.3.4]
- Structure: three "positions" (maqām) of opponents; the reply to the Second Position (Avicenna's) has two "approaches" (maslak) — four parts in all, yielding "not one, but at least two coherent responses". [source: SEP-Gh §7.2]
- Thesis (Marmura 166, as quoted in SEP-Gh): "The connection (iqtirān) between what is habitually believed to be a cause and what is habitually believed to be an effect is not necessary (ḍarūrī), according to us. … Their connection is due to the prior decision (taqdīr) of God, who creates them side by side (ʿalā l-tasāwuq), not to its being necessary by itself, incapable of separation." Examples: quenching thirst and drinking, satiety and eating, burning and contact with fire, light and sunrise, death and decapitation. [sources: SEP-Gh §7.2; SEP-Caus §3.3.1]
- Step 1 (opponents' claims, per SEP-Caus): (i) natural substances such as fire are causes; (ii) causal connections are necessary; together implying (iii) affirming fire–cotton contact entails affirming the burning. Ghazālī: (iii) is false — "we allow the possibility of the occurrence of the contact without the burning, and … of the cotton's transformation into burnt ashes without contact with the fire" (Marmura 166–7). [source: SEP-Caus §3.3.1]
- Step 2 (against the First Position: fire alone is the agent, by nature): "The one who enacts the burning by creating blackness in the cotton, [causing] separation in its parts, and making it cinder or ashes is God, either through the mediation of His angels or without mediation. As for fire, which is inanimate, it has no action. For what proof is there that it is the agent? They have no proof other than observing the occurrence of the burning at the [juncture of] contact with the fire" (Marmura 167). [sources: SEP-Caus §4.2; IEP-Gh §4d]
- Step 3 (the epistemic point): "Observation (mushāhada) points towards a concomitant occurrence … but not to a combined occurrence" (Marmura 167, Griffel's rendering) — we observe that cotton burns when touching fire, not that fire burns it. [sources: SEP-Gh §7.2; SEP-Caus §4.2]
- Step 4 (the modal point): necessity means that the alternative is inconceivable; we can conceive fire touching cotton without burning, so the connection is contingent, even if it never in fact fails. Avicenna instead treats as necessary what always was, is and will be true (an Aristotelian "statistical" model of modality). [sources: SEP-Gh §7.3 (Kukkonen 2000; Dutton 2001); SEP-Caus §3.3.3]
- Step 5 (First Approach): no natures; God creates each event directly, following a "custom" (ʿāda); a miracle is God omitting His habit. Occasionalist. (Marmura 169–71) [source: SEP-Gh §7.4]
- Step 6 (Second Approach, Marmura's "second causal theory", 1981): grant natures and fixed connections; miracles may have natural causes unknown to us — Moses' staff might be rapidly transformed (wood → earth → plants → herbivores → serpent) by some unknown catalyst. (Marmura 171–4) [source: SEP-Gh §7.4]
- Objection (Ghazālī puts it in the philosophers' mouths): then anything goes — a book left at home might turn into "a beardless slave boy … or into an animal", ashes into musk, stone into gold (Marmura 170). Dutton (2001): this entails facing the world "with no expectations". [source: IEP-Gh §4d]
- Reply: God created a regular habitual order; "the continuous habit of their occurrence repeatedly, one time after another, fixes unshakably in our minds the belief in their occurrence according to past habit" (Marmura 170). [source: IEP-Gh §4d] SEP-Gh adds: revelation tells us God's habit will never change (Qur'an 33:62, 48:23: "You will not find any change in God's habit"), so an occasionalist universe "will always remain indistinguishable from one governed by secondary causality". [SEP-Gh §7.4]
- Target clarification: when he says the connection is not necessary he attacks Avicenna's necessitarian ontology (the world as a necessary effect of God's essence), not secondary causality; the dispute is over God's free choice between possible worlds. [source: SEP-Gh §7.3] Ghazālī's own example of necessity-free causation: "it is within divine power to create satiety without eating, to create death without a deep cut in the neck…" (Marmura 166). [SEP-Gh §7.3]

## 6. Averroes' reply on causation (Incoherence of the Incoherence), step by step
- Averroes accepts (i): natural substances such as fire are causes. [source: SEP-Caus §3.3.2]
- But he restates the philosophers' view: actions between things occur "through one relation out of an infinite number, and it happens often that one relation hinders another. Therefore it is not absolutely certain that fire acts when it is brought near a sensitive body … as is asserted of talc and other things. But one need not therefore deny fire its burning power so long as fire keeps its name and definition" (Van den Bergh trans., *Incoherence of the Incoherence* 318–19). [source: SEP-Caus §3.3.2]
- So his position: contact with fire + absence of impediments (e.g. talc coating) entails burning; Ghazālī misreported the philosophers. [source: SEP-Caus §3.3.2]
- Argument from natural science: we do have knowledge of nature, which just is knowledge of natural causes; the occasionalist "denies with his tongue what is present in his mind" (Van den Bergh 318). [source: SEP-Caus §4.3]
- Objection to Averroes: Ghazālī's modal criterion (conceivability) makes even the impediment-free connection non-necessary; and Ghazālī's skeptical argument may leave fire as a non-active cause, so Averroes' reply may miss its target (McGinnis 2006). The deepest disagreement is theological (miracles, divine omnipotence). [source: SEP-Caus §3.3.3, §4.3]
- Neither Ghazālī's argument nor Averroes' reply engages Avicenna's own defence of natural necessity from the principle of sufficient reason. [source: SEP-Caus §3.3.1–2]

## 7. Debates: was al-Ghazālī an occasionalist? (Marmura vs Frank vs Griffel); the Hume comparison
- See "Debates" below for the positions.
- Ashʿarite occasionalism background: al-Ashʿarī denied natures and causal connections among creatures; bodies are quality-less atoms bearing accidents that last only a moment; God re-creates the accidents every moment — "creating a new world every moment"; all Ashʿarites up to Ghazālī's generation, including al-Juwaynī, held this. [source: SEP-Gh §7.1]
- Ghazālī's influence west: Nicholas of Autrecourt (1300–after 1350), whose writings were burned in 1347, was apparently acquainted with Ghazālī's arguments (Wolfson 1969) and is often called "the fourteenth-century Hume" (Nadler 1996). [source: SEP-Occ §1.2] Steven Nadler's article "'No Necessary Connection': The Medieval Roots of the Occasionalist Roots of Hume" (*The Monist* 79, 1996). [sources: SEP-Occ and SEP-Caus bibliographies]

## 8. The "Ghazālī killed science/philosophy in Islam" myth
- The charge, e.g. Steven Weinberg (2007): "Alas, Islam turned against science in the twelfth century. The most influential figure was the philosopher Abu Hamid al-Ghazālī, who argued in The Incoherence of the Philosophers against the very idea of laws of nature…after al-Ghazālī, there was no more science worth mentioning in Islamic countries." [source: IEP-Gh §6]
- W. Montgomery Watt (1962: 91): it is "tempting to conclude that his attack on the philosophers had been so devastating that philosophy was killed off; but such a conclusion is not justified" — Avicenna, the last great eastern philosopher, had died in 1037, so any decline began earlier. [source: IEP-Gh §6] (Note: Watt still assumes a decline.)
- Griffel: "If al-Ghazālī tried to establish thought-police in Islam, he remained unsuccessful. There was simply no Inquisition in Islam" (Griffel 2007: 158, via IEP). [source: IEP-Gh §6]
- Griffel (SEP-Gh §2): the weak Arabic reception of the *Doctrines of the Philosophers* was once read as evidence of declining interest in falsafa after the 11th century; "In reality, the reverse was probably true": the *Doctrines* is the nucleus of a new genre, ḥikma, presenting and improving Avicenna's system — e.g. Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī's *al-Mulakhkhaṣ* and *al-Mabāḥith al-mashriqiyya* (12th c.), al-Abharī's *Hidāyat al-ḥikma* (d. 1265), al-Kātibī's *Ḥikmat al-ʿayn*. [source: SEP-Gh §2]
- Griffel, *The Formation of Post-Classical Philosophy in Islam* (OUP 2021): the post-classical period was long treated as "a sort of dark age"; the book traces the ḥikma genre, with reports of falsafa by theologians such as Ghazālī developing "step-by-step into critical assessments of philosophy that try to improve philosophical teachings", culminating in al-Rāzī. [sources: SEP-Gh bibliography; WS-8 (publisher description and a 2024 review in *Philosophy*)]
- Dimitri Gutas titled a 2002 paper "The Heritage of Avicenna: the Golden Age of Arabic Philosophy, 900–ca. 1350" — i.e. he places the golden age well after Ghazālī. [source: SEP-Razi bibliography] (Only the title is verified; say "Gutas has argued the golden age ran to about 1350".)
- George Saliba (*Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance*, 2007) calls the Ghazālī-caused-decline view the "classical narrative" and argues the post-Ghazālī 13th–16th centuries were the most creative period of Arabic astronomy (Maragha). A *Middle East Quarterly* review criticized him for exaggeration. [source: WS-11] Cross-ref: History chapter *The Scientific Revolution* (Ṭūsī couple, Maragha).
- Ghazālī's interpretive rule was accepted by almost all later theologians, "particularly those with rationalist tendencies". [source: SEP-Gh §4]

## 9. Kalām: Muʿtazila and Ashʿarites (brief)
- Mutakallimūn of the classical period generally held the world to consist of atoms and accidents, re-created by God at every moment, God being the immediate cause of every change. [source: SEP-Caus §1.1]
- Muʿtazila: flourished in Basra and Baghdad; traditional origin story: Wāṣil ibn ʿAṭāʾ (d. 748) "withdrew" from Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's circle over whether a grave sinner is a believer ("a position between two positions"); the story is traditional, and Nallino's alternative etymology (political neutrality) is noted. [source: WP-Mu'tazilism]
- Five principles (al-uṣūl al-khamsa): God's unity (tawḥīd); God's justice (ʿadl); the promise and the threat; the intermediate position of the grave sinner; commanding right and forbidding wrong. ʿAbd al-Jabbār (d. 1025) wrote *Sharḥ al-Uṣūl al-Khamsa*. [source: WP-Mu'tazilism]
- Free will: 8th-century Muʿtazilites held that humans produce their own actions — required by divine justice, since otherwise reward and punishment would be unjust; effects produced through "generation" (tawallud). [source: SEP-Caus §1.1]
- Created Qurʾān and the Miḥna: caliph al-Maʾmūn began the Miḥna ("inquisition") in 833, requiring scholars to affirm the createdness of the Qurʾān; Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal refused; al-Mutawakkil ended it in 851. [source: WP-Mu'tazilism]
- Ashʿarites: al-Ashʿarī (874–936 per WP and SEP-Caus; 873–935 per SEP-Gh), born in Basra, pupil of the Muʿtazilite al-Jubbāʾī; by tradition a Muʿtazilite until about 40, then broke with them after dreams of the Prophet (traditional account). [sources: WP-Abu_al-Hasan_al-Ash'ari; SEP-Caus §1.2]
- Ashʿarite doctrine: divine omnipotence requires God to produce all atoms and accidents; human "acquisition" (kasb) — God creates in me a momentary power for a specific act, but that power is not causally related to the act (Ghazālī's *On Power*); "Broadly speaking, Ashʿarite theologians seem to endorse occasionalism", though Frank (1966) thought al-Ashʿarī stops short of full-blown occasionalism. [source: SEP-Caus §1.2]
- Ghazālī belonged to the Ashʿarite school in theology and the Shāfiʿī school in law. [sources: SEP-Gh §7.1; WP-Al-Ghazali]
- Ashʿarite ethics: Ghazālī (in law) kept the Ashʿarite view that reason cannot tell good from bad; "good" acts are those rewarded in the afterlife. [source: SEP-Gh §5] Juwaynī's account of good and bad "in certain ways anticipates the moral theory of David Hume" (SEP-Rel's phrase). [source: SEP-Rel]

## 10. Averroes (Ibn Rushd): life
- Abū al-Walīd Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad Ibn Rushd, born Córdoba 1126 (WP: 14 April 1126); died 1198 (WP: 11 December 1198), according to one account in Marrakesh. [sources: SEP-IR §1.1; WP-Averroes]
- Called "the grandson" (al-ḥafīd) to distinguish him from his grandfather of the same name, a famous Mālikī judge and jurist. [source: SEP-IR §1.1]
- He wrote no autobiography; much of his life is obscure. [source: SEP-IR §1.1]
- SEP: he "was certainly not a disciple of either" Ibn Bājja or Ibn Ṭufayl, though he read their works. (IEP-IR says it is "generally believed" he was influenced by Ibn Bājja "and perhaps was once tutored by him" — contradicted by SEP; Ibn Bājja died 1139 when Ibn Rushd was about 13.) [sources: SEP-IR §1.1; IEP-IR §1; SEP-Bajja §1]
- Almohads came to power when he was 22; his family had been close to the Almoravids; he probably adopted Ibn Tūmart's Almohad creed at some point, later showing critical distance. [source: SEP-IR §1.1]
- Ibn Ṭufayl introduced him to the caliph Abū Yaʿqūb Yūsuf (r. 1163–84), who asked him to comment on Aristotle and make him accessible (WP: introduction in 1169; al-Marrākushī's anecdote that the caliph asked whether the heavens were eternal and Ibn Rushd at first kept silent from fear). [sources: SEP-IR §1.1; WP-Averroes; WP-Ibn_Tufayl]
- Judge (qāḍī) of Seville 1169; Córdoba 1171; Seville again 1179; in 1182 succeeded Ibn Ṭufayl as court physician and became chief qāḍī of Córdoba. [source: WP-Averroes] SEP: later chief physician to the caliph in Marrakesh. [SEP-IR §1.1]
- Fall: under Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb (al-Manṣūr), in 1195 a tribunal in Córdoba condemned his teachings, ordered his books burned and banished him to Lucena (about 60 km SE of Córdoba); reasons unclear; restored to favour after a few years; died soon after. [sources: WP-Averroes; IEP-IR §1; SEP-IR §1.1 ("for reasons that remain unclear")]
- Ibn ʿArabī (1165–1240) attended his reburial in Córdoba. [source: WP-Averroes]
- He was also a major jurist: *The Distinguished Jurist's Primer* (Bidāyat al-mujtahid, begun 1169, completed 1188); and physician: *al-Kulliyyāt fī al-ṭibb* (Latin *Colliget*). [sources: SEP-IR §6.2, bibliography; WP-Averroes]
- "Averroes" is the corrupted Latin form of his name; SEP calls it "often improperly" used. [source: SEP-IR intro] WRITER: either name is acceptable; Periergia's published chapters use "Averroes (Ibn Rushd)".

## 11. The *Decisive Treatise* (Faṣl al-maqāl, 1178/9)
- Full title in Butterworth's translation: *The Book of the Decisive Treatise Determining the Connection Between the Law and Wisdom* (BYU Press, 2001, Arabic–English, cited by section). [source: SEP-IR bibliography]
- A legal opinion (Butterworth calls it a fatwa) arguing that the Law requires philosophy and logic; its implicit target is Ghazālī's fatwa. [sources: SEP-IR §6.2; WS-4]
- Argument: the Qur'an commands reflection (IEP cites 59:2 "Reflect, you have a vision" and 3:191); Butterworth §2: "If the activity of philosophy is nothing more than reflection upon existing things and consideration of them insofar as they are an indication of the Artisan…", then the Law makes it obligatory or recommended. [sources: IEP-IR §3; WS-4 (Butterworth wording from search summary)]
- Obligatory for whom: the person with "natural intelligence" and "religious integrity" (IEP's terms) must study it; studying the ancients is obligatory for the qualified, whatever their religion; harm from philosophy is accidental, like choking on water. [sources: IEP-IR §3; IEP-Gh §4e]
- Three classes / three paths of assent: demonstrative, dialectical, rhetorical — philosophers, theologians, the masses; Averroes takes over al-Fārābī's tripartite division (Decisive Treatise 1–12, esp. 8). [sources: IEP-IR §3; SEP-Rel]
- Allegorical interpretation only for those capable of demonstration; teaching interpretations to the masses (as Ghazālī did in the *Incoherence*) harms their faith. [sources: IEP-IR §3; IEP-Gh §4e]
- The Qur'an has no passage unambiguously stating creation in time (Averroes, Decisive Treatise 16, Butterworth). [source: SEP-Gh §4]
- Unity of truth: "Since the law is true … we, the Muslim community, know firmly that demonstrative reasoning does not lead to disagreement with what the law sets down. For truth does not oppose truth, but rather agrees with and bears witness to it" (Decisive Treatise 12; SEP's quotation, section numbering as in Butterworth). [source: SEP-IR §6.2]
- SEP: he establishes no hierarchy between philosophy and true religion; in the Long Commentary on the Metaphysics he distinguishes a general sharīʿa and one "specific" to philosophers, but it is unclear he ever claims philosophy transcends the Law. [source: SEP-IR §6.2]
- Related works: *al-Kashf ʿan manāhij al-adilla* (Exposition of the Methods of Proof, 1179/80?) criticizing Ashʿarite methods as sophistical; *Ḍamīma* (on divine knowledge). [source: SEP-IR §1.2, §6.1, bibliography]

## 12. The *Incoherence of the Incoherence* (Tahāfut al-tahāfut, 1180/81)
- Direct response to Ghazālī, whose work had become influential in al-Andalus. [source: SEP-IR §1.2] English: Simon Van den Bergh, 2 vols, London: Luzac, 1954 (E. J. W. Gibb Memorial). [sources: SEP-IR bibliography; SEP-Caus bibliography]
- Latin translations 1328 and 1526 (the latter from Hebrew), both titled *Destructio destructionum*; Agostino Nifo wrote a Latin commentary on it. [source: SEP-Gh §4]
- On eternity: Averroes maintained Aristotle had demonstrated the world's pre-eternity, which by Ghazālī's own rule would make it a doctrine to accept. [source: SEP-Gh §4]
- On God's knowledge: God knows the world "in his own manner, neither in universal nor particular", as its cause; the divine mind's "thinking its own self is identical with its thinking all existence" (Incoherence I.11). [source: SEP-IR §6.1]
- On resurrection: he refuses, against Ghazālī, to endorse bodily resurrection (Incoherence II.4), and seems not to promise individual immortality (Taylor 1998; "scholarship is not unanimous"). [source: SEP-IR §8]

## 13. The commentaries and "the Commentator"
- Commentaries cover nearly the whole Aristotelian corpus, plus Galen, Plato's *Republic*, Avicenna and Ibn Bājja. [source: SEP-IR §1.2]
- Three kinds (a division going back to 19th-century scholarship): short/epitome (jawāmiʿ, early); middle/paraphrase (talkhīṣ, throughout his career); long/literal (sharḥ or tafsīr, later years). Recent scholars (al-ʿAlawī 1986; Druart 1994; Gutas 1993) doubt the neat threefold scheme. [source: SEP-IR §1.2] He wrote five long commentaries (one on the *Posterior Analytics*). [source: SEP-IR §2]
- On Aristotle: "no one who has come after him to this our time—and this is close to 1500 years later—has been able to add a word worthy of attention to what he said" (Long Commentary on the Physics, proem; Harvey 1985). [source: SEP-IR §1.2]
- Contrary to what is often said, most of his work survives in Arabic: of 49 works listed in SEP, 14 are not extant in Arabic. [source: SEP-IR bibliography] The *Republic* commentary and the middle commentary on the *Nicomachean Ethics* survive only in Hebrew. [same]
- Latin: translations from Arabic began with Michael Scot around 1220; by mid-century most Aristotle commentaries were translated; by the 1230s he is known as "the Commentator". A papal decree of 1341 tells the Paris arts faculty to follow "the science of Aristotle and his commentator Averroes … except in cases that run counter to the faith". The Junta editions of Aristotle with Averroes' commentaries appeared in 1552 and 1562. [source: SEP-IR §9.3]
- Hebrew: Maimonides acquired his commentaries perhaps around 1190; by 1340 virtually all were in Hebrew; Gersonides wrote supercommentaries in the 1320s. [source: SEP-IR §9.2]
- Dante placed "Averoìs, che 'l gran comento feo" ("Averroes, who made the great commentary") among the virtuous non-Christians in Limbo, *Inferno* canto IV. [source: WS-12 (Italian Wikisource text)]
- He fought "Neoplatonic" readings and Avicenna's "insufficiently Aristotelian" philosophy. [sources: SEP-IR §6.1; IEP-IR §2]

## 14. The unity of the intellect and Latin Averroism
- Final view (Long Commentary on the *De anima*, tr. Richard C. Taylor, Yale 2009): the material (receptive) intellect, like the agent intellect, is "a single, separate, eternal substance" shared by all humans; his views changed over time — he had once called it "repulsive". [source: SEP-IR §5]
- He knew it was new: "this claim came to me after long reflection and intense care, and I have not seen it in anyone else before me" (Commentary on Alexander, p. 30). [source: SEP-IR §5]
- ARGUMENT (SEP's reconstruction): (A) humans think abstractly; (B) abstract thought is impossible for corporeal beings; (C) humans are corporeal. Jointly inconsistent, each plausible. Solution: narrowly, a human is corporeal (C true, A false); extended to include the separate intellect fed by each person's imagination ("the soul never thinks without an image"), we think (A true, C false). Why one intellect: without shared concepts teachers could not convey knowledge; an intellect individuated by a body would receive individualized forms. SEP calls it "the first developed statement of the extended-mind thesis". [source: SEP-IR §5]
- Latin reaction: by the 1260s "Averroist" was pejorative, especially over the unicity of intellect, the eternity of the world and determinism; Aquinas' *De unitate intellectus* (On the Unicity of the Intellect) contains the first known use of averroista; "contra averroistas" in the title is a later addition. Condemnations at Paris in 1270 and 1277. [sources: SEP-IR §9.3; SEP-Infl]
- 7 March 1277: Bishop Stephen Tempier prohibited 219 theses; Siger of Brabant and Boethius of Dacia were among the main targets. [sources: SEP-Cond; SEP-BoeD]
- Three groups in Latin reception: radical "Averroists" (Siger, Boethius of Dacia, John of Jandun — Van Steenberghen prefers "radical Aristotelianism"); anti-Aristotelians (Bonaventure, Olivi); the majority, including Aquinas, who reject Averroes where he conflicts with faith but rely on "the Commentator". Later Padua (Pomponazzi, Nifo, Zabarella). [source: SEP-IR §9.3]

## 15. "Double truth": misattributed
- Tempier's prefatory letter (1277) accused some of saying things were true according to philosophy but not according to the Catholic faith, "as if there were two contrary truths, and as if against the truth of Sacred Scripture, there is truth in the sayings of the condemned pagans." [source: SEP-Cond]
- SEP-Cond: "Nowadays, scholars agree that there were no medieval authors who entertained the philosophically absurd theory that two contradictory propositions … can both be true at the same time"; Tempier's charge ridiculed the practice of evaluating a doctrine "philosophically speaking" and from faith. [source: SEP-Cond]
- Averroes himself held the unity of truth ("truth does not oppose truth"). [source: SEP-IR §6.2] IEP-IR: double truth "was not held by Ibn Rushd himself but was an innovation of the Averroists" — note SEP-Cond goes further: no one actually held it. [IEP-IR §8; SEP-Cond]
- Boethius of Dacia: scientifically the world cannot be "new", though according to faith it is; "faith is not knowledge/science". [source: SEP-BoeD] Fraenkel (2013) argues even the "Averroist" Del Medigo agreed with Averroes' "the truth does not contradict the truth". [source: SEP-DelM]
- Richard C. Dales, "The Origin of the Doctrine of the Double Truth", *Viator* 15 (1984). [source: SEP-Cond bibliography]

## 16. Averroes on women (Republic commentary) — verified
- Lacking Aristotle's *Politics* ("has not yet reached us on this peninsula", Paraphrase of the Ethics), he commented on Plato's *Republic*, dropping books I and X and the first half of II, recasting the rest in three treatises, and criticizing the Almohads. [source: SEP-IR §8]
- He holds that women share the same nature as men and are suited to the same activities, fit to be guardians, philosophers and rulers; but in actual cities "since women in these cities are not prepared with respect to any of the human virtues, they frequently resemble plants. Their being a burden upon the men in these cities is one of the causes of poverty" (Republic I.54; SEP's rendering; standard English translations: E. I. J. Rosenthal 1956, Ralph Lerner 1974, the latter cited by SEP as "improved"). [source: SEP-IR §8 and bibliography]
- Survives only in Hebrew. Whether he had Plato's text directly or worked from Galen's compendium is unclear (Mahdi). [source: SEP-IR §8, bibliography]

## 17. Ibn Ṭufayl and *Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān*; Ibn Bājja
- Ibn Ṭufayl (c. 1105–1185), born in Guadix near Granada; secretary to Almohad governors (Granada; Ceuta and Tangier), then vizier and physician to Abū Yaʿqūb Yūsuf; recommended Ibn Rushd and retired in 1182; died in Morocco 1185. [source: WP-Ibn_Tufayl] Latin name Abubacer. [WP-Ibn_Tufayl]
- Ibn Rushd's own account (via Nasr and Leaman 1996, quoted on WP): Ibn Ṭufayl told him the Commander of the Faithful complained of the "disjointedness of Aristotle's mode of expression"; "If you have the energy, … you do it". [source: WP-Ibn_Tufayl] (Translation of this anecdote is not named; paraphrase it.)
- *Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān* ("Alive, son of Awake"); Latin *Philosophus Autodidactus*, ed. Edward Pococke the Younger, 1671; first English translation direct from Arabic by Simon Ockley, 1708; English translation by Lenn E. Goodman (1972). [sources: WP-Hayy_ibn_Yaqdhan; WP-Ibn_Tufayl; SEP-Rel bibliography (Goodman trans.)]
- Story: a child on an equatorial island off India, either spontaneously generated or set adrift as an infant, raised by a gazelle/doe, reaches by unaided reason knowledge of nature and of God (his development structured in seven-year periods: by 35 he knows the Creator); about age 50 he meets Absāl, who came from a neighbouring island seeking solitude; they go to Absāl's society (ruled by Salamān), Hayy's teaching fails, and they return to the island. [sources: SEP-Rel ("raised by a deer on a desert island … subsequent encounter with a religious community … resigning himself to the fact that some people do not want to be intellectually challenged"); WP-Ibn_Tufayl (gazelle; Absal; names Ḥayy, Salamān and Absāl borrowed from Avicenna's tales); WS-5, WS-6 (spontaneous generation/basket; seven-year stages; Absāl's arrival at 50; return) — the detailed plot points rest on search summaries]
- SEP-Rel: the novel exemplifies al-Fārābī's view that truths of religion can be known by reason, while religious authority with its symbols is needed by most people. [source: SEP-Rel]
- Ibn Ṭufayl borrowed Avicenna's title and characters and drew on Avicenna's "floating man", turning sensory deprivation into social isolation (Goodman 2000). [source: WP-Hayy_ibn_Yaqdhan]
- Ibn Bājja (Avempace): born in Saragossa some years before 1085; died at Fez in May 1139 (several sources allege poisoning). His most representative works include the *Rule (Governance) of the Solitary* (Tadbīr al-mutawaḥḥid), presented as a regimen for spiritual health: in imperfect cities the philosopher who holds true opinions not shared by the city is one of the "weeds" (nawābit), a concept taken from al-Fārābī but evaluated differently. [source: SEP-Bajja §1, §7] Averroes criticized Ibn Bājja for judging the *Republic* the perfect treatise on the virtuous city. [source: SEP-IR §8]
- Ibn Ṭufayl wrote about Ibn Bājja (a contemporary) and al-Fārābī in the introduction to *Ḥayy*. [source: SEP-Bajja]

## 18. Maimonides (cross-reference only)
- Maimonides acquired most of Ibn Rushd's commentaries in Egypt, perhaps around 1190, and his praise launched four centuries of Jewish interest in Averroes. [source: SEP-IR §9.1–9.2] WRITER: cross-ref *Jewish Philosophy: from Saadia to Maimonides*.
- Ghazālī's *Doctrines of the Philosophers* was widely read in Hebrew (over 75 manuscripts); the *Incoherence* was translated into Hebrew in 1411. [source: SEP-Gh §2]

## 19. The later tradition after Averroes
- Arabic reception of Averroes: the "commonly heard claim that he had no influence on Islamic thought is false": 39 direct disciples (Ben Sharīfa 1999); used in the Maghreb (Ibn Ṭumlūs, Ibn Khaldūn's training); Ibn Taymiyya used his texts extensively; manuscripts copied in Safavid Iran in the 16th–17th centuries. But his Islamic influence "cannot compare" with Avicenna's. [source: SEP-IR §9.1]
- Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī (1149–1210), born in Rayy (near Tehran), died in Herat; Ashʿarite; "one of the most innovative and influential thinkers in the first stage of what is sometimes called 'post-classical' Islamic thought"; reacted critically to Avicenna on nearly every point; wrote one of history's most important Qur'an commentaries. [source: SEP-Razi §1]
- Suhrawardī (Shihāb al-Dīn, c. 1154–1191), "Master of Illumination" (shaykh al-ishrāq); trained in Avicennan Peripateticism; offered "an original Platonic critique" of it; theory of "presential" knowledge, ontology of lights, a "world of images"; completed *The Philosophy of Illumination* (Ḥikmat al-ishrāq) in 1186 in Aleppo; executed at the end of 1191 (or early 1192) after accusations of heresy, amid Aleppo politics under Saladin's son al-Ẓāhir. Never translated into Latin; revived in the West by Henry Corbin. [source: SEP-Suhr intro, §1.1]
- Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī (1201–1274): born Ṭūs, died Baghdad; c. 165 titles; served the Nizārī Ismāʿīlīs (Alamut) then the Mongol Hülegü; founded the Maragha centre; [source: IEP-Tusi] wrote a commentary on Avicenna's *Pointers and Reminders* (al-Ishārāt) answering al-Rāzī's criticisms. [sources: SEP-Caus §2.3; SEP-Razi bibliography (Mayer 2003, "Ṭūsī's Defence")] Maragha observatory founded 1259 (Periergia History chapter *Scientific Revolution*, not re-verified here).
- Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328), born Ḥarrān, family fled the Mongols to Damascus (1269); Ḥanbalī; inspiration of modern Salafism. *Refutation of the Logicians* (al-Radd ʿalā al-manṭiqiyyīn): definitions are linguistic conventions, not grasps of essences, and cannot yield new concepts (following al-Rāzī); the syllogism is formally valid ("there is no doubt that this combination yields [certain] knowledge that every A is C") but unnecessary: certainty can come in other ways, with more or fewer premises; the elaborate figures are "lean camel meat on a rugged mountain peak". He denied any real conflict of reason and revelation (*Darʾ taʿāruḍ*). SEP: some call him a philosopher (nominalism, empiricism, kinship with Averroes), others a literalist hater of reason; "certainly an apologist for the rationality of Islamic revelation". [source: SEP-Tay §1, §3.2, §4.2; SEP-Gh §4]
- Ibn Khaldūn (1332–1406), born Tunis, died Cairo; wrote the *Muqaddima* (1377, per Periergia History chapter *Global History*); studied philosophy and logic under al-Ābilī; his critique of philosophy is described as an Ashʿarite critique in the line of Ghazālī and al-Rāzī. [sources: WP-Ibn_Khaldun (the Ashʿarite characterization comes from a cited essay, weak); SEP-IR §9.1 (Averroes in his training)]
- Mullā Ṣadrā (ca. 1571–1636): "arguably the most significant Islamic philosopher after Avicenna"; revolutionized the doctrine of existence; "School of Isfahan" under Shah ʿAbbās I; taught at Shiraz; his thought dominant since the early 19th century. [source: SEP-Sadra intro] Cross-ref chapter 32 (*Beyond Europe: Mullā Ṣadrā…*). Consistent with Periergia's *What Is Philosophy?* (c. 1571–1636).

## Debates
- **Was Ghazālī an occasionalist?** (a) Marmura (1965, 1981, 1995): he never deviated from Ashʿarite occasionalism; his causal language is ambiguous, perhaps mocking philosophical parlance to lure falsafa followers; Marmura rejected the idea that he held two "cum-possible" views, while naming the Second Approach "al-Ghazālī's second causal theory". [source: SEP-Gh §7.4] IEP calls occasionalism "the standard reading" (Moad 2005). [IEP-Gh §4d] (b) Richard M. Frank (*Creation and the Cosmic System: Al-Ghazâlî & Avicenna*, 1992; *Al-Ghazālī and the Ashʿarite School*, 1994): Ghazālī's cosmos is essentially Avicennan — God acts through chains of secondary causes; Frank even read him as closer to Avicenna's necessitarian God than Griffel accepts ("contrary to Frank's (1992, 55–63) understanding of him", Griffel insists Ghazālī's God chooses freely). A Griffel article on Western reception summarizes: Marmura explained causal connections as direct actions of God, Frank as secondary causality. [sources: SEP-Gh §6–7; WS-2] (c) Griffel (2009; also Moad 2023): Ghazālī held occasionalism and secondary causality as two possible explanations and stayed undecided all his life; key evidence: 20th discussion, "Both these two views are possible for us" (Marmura 222), and his last work's verdict that the question "cannot be settled decisively". Rudolph (Perler/Rudolph 2000) showed that the 17th discussion's opening conditions fit non-occasionalist views too. [source: SEP-Gh §7.2, §7.4] SEP-Occ: the dispute turns on whether Ghazālī accepted that a genuine cause must necessitate its effect. [SEP-Occ §1.1] WRITER: Periergia's overview says he deployed "occasionalist arguments" — true of the First Approach, but say the label is debated.
- **The Hume comparison (handle with care).** Similarities: observation shows only concomitance, not necessary connection; belief in regularity arises from repeated habit (Marmura 170). IEP says he "anticipates Hume" but refers to Moad (2008) for the differences. [IEP-Gh §4d] Differences (from SEP-Gh and SEP-Caus): Ghazālī does not deny that connections exist, only that they are necessary; his argument is modal (alternatives are conceivable, so God could have chosen otherwise) and theological (to defend miracles), and the regularity is grounded in God's unchanging habit, not in mere psychology; for Hume, by contrast, there is no further ground and he used his analysis to doubt reports of miracles. [sources: SEP-Gh §7.2–7.4; SEP-Caus §3.3.3–4; WS-1 (purpose and grounding contrasts, from journal abstracts — weak)] Some scholars find the comparison "in some ways inapt" because Ghazālī does not wholly reject natures. [WS-1] Nadler (1996) traces "the occasionalist roots of Hume" back to medieval sources; Autrecourt, who knew Ghazālī's arguments, is the one usually called "the fourteenth-century Hume". [SEP-Occ] WRITER: say "strikingly similar on one point, differently motivated"; do not say Ghazālī influenced Hume.
- **Did Ghazālī kill philosophy/science?** Weinberg yes; Watt: decline began before him; Griffel, Gutas, Saliba: no decline after him — philosophy continued as ḥikma and in kalām, Avicennism flourished to c. 1350 and beyond, astronomy peaked at Maragha. [sources: IEP-Gh §6; SEP-Gh §2; SEP-Razi bibliography; WS-8; WS-11] Cf. Periergia History chapter *The Rise of Islam and the Caliphates*, which treats the "golden age then decline" narrative as partly a 19th-century orientalist creation (consistent).
- **"Theological" vs "philosophical" works of Averroes.** Since Renan (1852) scholars split his religious treatises (dialectical, popular) from the commentaries (his "true" demonstrative view); al-ʿAlawī (1986) defends the unity of the corpus. [source: SEP-IR §1.2] Related: whether he gave philosophy priority over religion (Taylor 2012 vs SEP's view that he sets no hierarchy). [SEP-IR §6.2]
- **Was the Latin "Averroism" Averroes'?** Van Steenberghen prefers "radical Aristotelianism"; doubts whether Averroes was the prime impetus. [source: SEP-IR §9.3] Double truth: attributed by Tempier, held by no one (SEP-Cond).
- **Ibn Taymiyya: philosopher or anti-rationalist?** See §19. [source: SEP-Tay]
- **"Post-classical" as a label.** Kaukua (2020), "Post-Classical Islamic Philosophy? A Contradiction in Terms?" [source: SEP-Razi bibliography] — the label itself is debated.

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use
- A bare "al-Ghazālī (1058–1111)": birth year disputed (1055/56 per Griffel from his own statements; 1058/59 per later historians). Use both.
- "Ghazālī wandered for ten years" as precise: SEP shows him back in Ṭūs c. 1097 and teaching in private schools; say "about a decade away from state teaching (1095–1106)".
- The robbers' anecdote (bandits seized his notebooks, so he memorized everything) — reported by IEP only as tradition; if used, mark "a later story".
- Averroes' "He who repudiates causes repudiates reason/the intellect" (often quoted from the *Incoherence of the Incoherence*): exact wording not found in a source read; use instead the verified "denies with his tongue what is present in his mind".
- Ghazālī's "whoever does not know logic, his knowledge is untrustworthy" (attributed to the *Mustaṣfā* introduction): not found in sources read.
- Exact title/wording of Ibn Khaldūn's Muqaddimah section "A refutation of philosophy. The corruption of the students of philosophy" (Rosenthal): not confirmed (WS-7 found no confirmation). Say only that he criticized philosophy (in the Ashʿarite line, weak source) while valuing logic as a craft.
- *Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān* as "the third most translated text from Arabic, after the Qur'an and the Thousand and One Nights" (WP citing a Guardian piece) — unsupported by a scholarly source.
- Claims that *Ḥayy* inspired Locke's tabula rasa, Defoe's *Robinson Crusoe*, Newton or Kant (WP, citing Samar Attar and Russell): speculative influence claims; if mentioned, say "some scholars have suggested" and name it as contested.
- "Ibn Ṭufayl was educated by Ibn Bājja" (WP, citing the 1911 Britannica): not confirmed by SEP; avoid.
- "Ibn Ṭufayl's novel was written as a response to Ghazālī's *Incoherence*" (WP, uncited): not verified.
- "Averroes was perhaps tutored by Ibn Bājja" (IEP-IR): contradicted by SEP-IR ("certainly not a disciple").
- "Interest in philosophy was waning in the Muslim world" after Ghazālī/Averroes (IEP-IR intro, §8): this is the decline narrative the newer scholarship rejects; do not repeat as fact.
- Al-Ghazālī's views on women: not researched in sources read — omit.
- Line number of Dante's verse (Inferno IV.144): canto confirmed, line not; cite "Inferno, canto IV".
- *Miʿyār al-ʿilm* as "the first book written specifically on Muslim logic" (a single journal abstract) — do not use.
- Periergia History chapter's Maragha date (1259) and Ṭūsī couple date (1247) were not re-verified here.

## Surprises (sources contradicting common belief)
- Al-Ghazālī was not simply anti-philosophy: he championed Aristotelian demonstration and logic and absorbed Avicenna's psychology; the *Incoherence* attacks claims to demonstration, and he accepted some theses he "refuted".
- He may not have been a committed occasionalist (Griffel): the 17th discussion gives two answers, one allowing natural causes.
- His birth year is probably 1055/56, not 1058.
- No medieval author is known to have held the "double truth"; it was Tempier's caricature.
- Most of Averroes' works survive in Arabic (35 of 49 in SEP's list), contrary to a common claim; and he did have Islamic successors and readers (39 disciples; Ibn Khaldūn; Safavid Iran).
- Averroes argued women share men's nature and could be guardians, philosophers and rulers.
