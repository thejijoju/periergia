## 1. Framing: two questions, and the terms

- SEP-Death splits "death" into **dying** (the event or process of losing life) and **being dead** (the state after it). Epicurus's argument is strongest about being dead, not about painful dying. [source: SEP-Death §3.1]
- **Harm thesis**: the claim that death is, at least sometimes, bad for the one who dies. If the harm thesis is true, the human condition has "a tragic side". [source: SEP-Death §3]
- Meaning **"in" life** (whether an individual's life is meaningful) is distinguished from the meaning **"of" life** (the point of humanity or of the universe as a whole). Most contemporary analytic work concerns meaning *in* life. [source: SEP-LM §1]
- There is near-consensus that meaningfulness is a final value. It comes in degrees, and it is conceptually distinct from happiness and from moral rightness. Examples: Nozick's "experience machine" (1974) could give a happy but not a meaningful life; Van Gogh shows that meaning can arrive after death. [source: SEP-LM §1]
- Talk of "the meaning of life" is recent. SEP-LM: such talk has "arisen only in the past 250 years or so" (citing Landau 1997). IEP-ME: "it was only early in the nineteenth century that writers began to write directly about 'the meaning of life'". IEP-ME also says Schopenhauer explicitly asked "What is the meaning of life at all?" (1860) and treats him as "the springboard" of modern Western inquiry. [source: SEP-LM intro; IEP-ME]
- SEP-LM says a distinct analytic field of the meaning of life dates only "since the 1980s or so", with real depth only in the last 25 years. The entry deliberately covers analytic work only and points to separate lists for non-Western traditions. [source: SEP-LM intro]

## 2. Epicurus: "death is nothing to us" (ARGUMENT, step by step)

(Biography and the tetrapharmakos are in the Hellenistic notes. Use dates 341–270 BCE as in SEP. IEP gives c. 341–271 and death "around 271 or 270".) [source: SEP-Death §3.1; IEP-Epi §1]

- Source texts: *Letter to Menoeceus* (LM) 124–5 and *Principal Doctrine* 2. The *Letter* is preserved in Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* book 10 (3rd century CE). [source: SEP-Epi §1; Hellenistic notes]
- **The passage, as quoted in SEP-Death:** "Make yourself familiar with the belief that death is nothing to us, since everything good and bad lies in sensation, and death is to be deprived of sensation. … So that most fearful of all bad things, death, is nothing to us, since when we are, death is not, and when death is present, then we are not." SEP-Death does not name its translator. Its bibliography lists Bailey, *Epicurus: The Extant Remains* (1926). [source: SEP-Death §3.1]
- **R. D. Hicks's translation** (public domain; the one most often reproduced online) of the second sentence: "Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not." [source: WP-Epicurus (cites the Internet Classics Archive text); WS confirms that the wording is Hicks's]
- Inwood & Gerson's rendering of the first sentence, as reported by a search summary: "Get used to believing that death is nothing to us. For all good and bad consists in sense-experience, and death is the privation of sense-experience." [source: WS — use only with a caveat, or prefer the SEP or Hicks wording]
- **IEP's reconstruction ("no subject of harm" argument)**, verbatim structure: (1) Death is annihilation. (2) The living have not yet been annihilated. (3) Death does not affect the living (from 1, 2). (4) So death is not bad for the living (3). (5) For something to be bad for somebody, that person has to exist, at least. (6) The dead do not exist (1). (7) Therefore death is not bad for the dead (5, 6). (8) Therefore death is bad for neither the living nor the dead (4, 7). Epicurus adds that if death causes no pain when you are dead, it is foolish to let the fear of it cause pain now. [source: IEP-Epi §5g(i)]
- **SEP's reconstruction (hedonist version)**: (1) Intrinsic hedonism: only pleasant and painful experiences are intrinsically good or bad for a person. (2) So something is intrinsically good or bad for a person only if it is an experience. (3) A person's death is not an experience. (4) So death is not intrinsically good or bad for her. (5) Extrinsic instrumentalism: something is extrinsically good or bad for a person only if it makes her have things that are intrinsically good or bad for her. (6) Death and existence do not overlap in time, so a person's death does not make her have any experiences. (7) So death is not extrinsically good or bad for her (2, 5, 6). (8) Something is good or bad for a person only if it is intrinsically or extrinsically so. (9) Therefore a person's death is not good or bad for her. SEP says this reconstruction fills in details Epicurus "did not supply". [source: SEP-Death §3.1]
- Physics behind premise 1: the soul is made of fine atoms distributed through the body. It cannot survive separation from the body, so there is no afterlife punishment and no regret for lost life. [source: SEP-Epi §3; IEP-Epi]
- Desire for immortality is an "empty" desire, in Epicurus's classification. [source: SEP-Epi §4]
- **Objections in SEP-Death:**
  - (a) Dying, as opposed to being dead, can be painful, so dying can be extrinsically bad.
  - (b) Intrinsic hedonism is questionable. On **preferentialism** (desire-fulfilment theories), a desire can be thwarted without any experience; SEP's example is "If I want my child to be happy, and she is, my desire is fulfilled" even if I never learn of it. This blocks both the step from 1 to 2 and the step to 7.
  - (c) Extrinsic instrumentalism overlooks **harm by preclusion**: Nagel's point that things can be bad for us by depriving us of goods. SEP's illustrations: an anaesthetic is good because it prevents suffering; unconsciousness during a joyous event one would have enjoyed is bad.
  [source: SEP-Death §3.1]

## 3. The deprivation account and its problems

- **Deprivationism / comparativism**: an event is overall bad for us if and only if, and to the extent that, it makes our lifetime welfare level lower than it otherwise would be. Death is bad when it deprives us of good life we would otherwise have had. SEP names as "early proponents" **Nagel 1970, Quinn 1984 and Feldman 1991**. [source: SEP-Death §3.2]
- SEP's worked example: Hilda dies painlessly at 25. Had she lived, she would have prospered for 25 years and suffered in the last five. Her death is overall bad for her. If instead she would have fared badly for 25 years, the same death would be overall good for her. So on this view death is **not** bad for everyone who dies. [source: SEP-Death §3.2]
- Thomas Nagel, "Death", *Noûs* 4(1) (1970): 73–80. Reprinted in Nagel, *Mortal Questions* (Cambridge UP, 1979) and in Fischer (ed.), *The Metaphysics of Death* (Stanford UP, 1993). [source: SEP-Death bibliography]
- Fred Feldman, "Some Puzzles About the Evil of Death", *Philosophical Review* 100 (1991): 205–27. Also Feldman, *Confrontations with the Reaper* (Oxford UP, 1992). [source: SEP-Death bibliography]
- Nagel's quotation (as in SEP and WP): "life is worth living even when the bad elements of experience are plentiful and the good ones too meager to outweigh the bad ones on their own. The additional positive weight is supplied by experience itself, rather than by any of its contents." (1970, p. 60 in Fischer's reprint.) WP quotes a variant from *Mortal Questions*: "...is supplied by experience itself, rather than by any of its consequences." [source: SEP-Death §7.1; WP-Thomas_Nagel] Wording differs slightly between the two printings; use SEP's.
- Nagel concludes that whenever death comes it would have been good to live longer: "if there is no limit to the amount of life that it would be good to have, then it may be that a bad end is in store for us all" (1970, p. 69). SEP objects that the claim that experience as such always outweighs evils is implausible; sedation before surgery shows we sometimes prefer not to experience. [source: SEP-Death §7.1]
- **The timing puzzle**: if death is bad for us, *when* is it bad for us? The "Epicurean presumption" is that what never makes us worse off at any time is not bad for us. The answers are listed below. [source: SEP-Death §4]
  - **Concurrentism**: harm while dying (Lamont 1998: death is an "ensuring event").
  - **Priorism**: harm before death, because death thwarts desires we have now (Pitcher 1984).
  - **Subsequentism**: harm after death, during the time we would have enjoyed goods (Feit 2002; Bradley 2004, 2009).
  - **Indefinitism**: harm at no determinate time (Nagel 1970; Silverstein 1980). Grey 1999 reads this as a stretch with blurry boundaries.
  - **Atemporalism**: harm, but at no time; the badness of death is a timeless truth (Feldman 1991; the term is from Johansson 2013).
- **The problem of the subject**: subsequentism must attribute a welfare level to someone who no longer exists. SEP links this directly to Epicurus's "when we are, death is not". One reply is metaphysical **eternalism**, on which past and future objects are ontologically on a par with present ones (Nagel 1970; Silverstein 1980). SEP adds a further worry: welfare concepts do not apply to corpses or the non-existent ("bags of concrete are not capable of having any welfare level, not even a level of 0"). [source: SEP-Death §4.3]
- **Posthumous harm** (desire-based, Pitcher 1984; Parfit) is controversial. Velleman (1991) holds that current well-being is "a fact intrinsic to the present". Partridge (1981) argues that a desire must still exist to be thwarted. [source: SEP-Death §6]

## 4. Lucretius's symmetry argument (ARGUMENT with objections and replies)

- Lucretius: *De rerum natura* (DRN), 6 books. The only external contemporary reference is Cicero's letter to his brother Quintus of February 54 BCE (Q.F. 2.10.3). Jerome gives his birth as 94 BCE and his death at 44 or 39 (the text is uncertain), with a story of a love-potion madness and suicide that SEP calls not credible and likely shaped by Christian hostility. Safe life span: "mid-90s to mid-50s BCE". [source: SEP-Luc §1]
- Book 3, lines 830–1094, aims to remove the fear of death after proving the soul is mortal. The key line is "Nil igitur mors est ad nos" ("Therefore death is nothing to us", 3.830). [source: SEP-Luc §4 (book 3); IEP-Luc (Latin and English)]
- The symmetry passages are DRN 3.832–42 and 3.972–75, per James Warren's analysis (Warren, "Lucretius, symmetry arguments and fearing death", *Phronesis* 46, 2001). [source: WS — line numbers via search summary of Warren; SEP-Luc credits the label "symmetry argument" to Warren 2004]
- Quotation as printed in SEP-Death (Rolfe Humphries tr., *The Way Things Are*, Indiana UP, 1968, p. 114): "Look back at time … before our birth. In this way Nature holds before our eyes the mirror of our future after death. Is this so grim, so gloomy?" [source: SEP-Death §5.2] (IEP-Epi also recommends Humphries's translation.)
- IEP's prose statement: anyone who fears death should consider the time before birth. Past infinite prenatal non-existence is like future infinite post-mortem non-existence ("as though nature has put up a mirror"). We do not think the first terrible, so we should not think the second evil. [source: IEP-Epi §5g(ii)]
- **Step by step (SEP's statement):** (1) We do not find it objectionable that we failed to exist before we were alive. (2) Our pre-vital and post-mortem non-existence are alike in all relevant respects. (3) Any objection to the one would apply to the other. (4) So it is irrational to object to death, assuming death ends our existence. [source: SEP-Death §5.2]
- **Objection 1 (asymmetry of birth and death):** read as a claim about dying, the argument is weak. Our complaint is not that death puts us in a ghastly state but that it **precludes more good life**. "Birth makes life possible … Death makes further life impossible; it brings a good thing to a close." Read as about *being dead*, there is a "kernel of truth": temporary gaps in existence would not upset us, but "temporary nonexistence is not the same as undergoing permanent nonexistence". [source: SEP-Death §5.2]
- **Objection/reply 2 — Nagel (1970):** it is reasonable to want to live longer but not to have been born earlier, because a person could not have come into existence significantly earlier than she did (her origin is essential), whereas she could have lived longer. Counter: Nagel himself, in footnote 3 of the *Mortal Questions* reprint, admits this rests on questionable assumptions about essential origins. SEP's example is a zygote frozen for a long time, which could have been frozen for a shorter time. [source: SEP-Death §5.2]
- **Reply 3 — Frederik Kaufman (2016, "Lucretius and the Fear of Death", in Cholbi (ed.) 2016, pp. 53–67):** the frozen-zygote case shows that an *organism* could have existed earlier, but not a *person*. "Persons (properly understood) cannot exist earlier than they do." This assumes persons have psychological persistence conditions (Parfit's view; animalists challenge it), so "when a particular consciousness emerges is essential to that person". [source: SEP-Death §5.2]
- **Reply 4 — Parfit's future bias** (*Reasons and Persons*, Oxford: Clarendon, 1984): we prefer goods to lie in the future and bad things in the past. Given this bias, it is rational to deplore death (which deprives us of future goods) more than prenatal non-existence. But Parfit thinks the bias is "unfortunate". If we became temporally neutral, we might be comforted at the end of life by the goods we have accumulated. [source: SEP-Death §5.2]
  - Parfit's thought experiment (waking in hospital, not knowing whether a painful operation was yesterday or will be today) is around p. 165 of *Reasons and Persons*, in §§62–64. [source: WS — details of the case come from secondary sources only; describe it loosely]
- **Reply 5 (SEP's own, after Kamm):** we have forward-looking projects and cannot plan for our past, so preferring life extended into the future is not irrational. Frances Kamm (1998, 2021) emphasises that we do not want our lives to be "all over". [source: SEP-Death §5.2]

## 5. Williams on immortality and tedium; replies

- Bernard Williams, "The Makropulos Case: Reflections on the Tedium of Immortality", in *Problems of the Self* (Cambridge UP, 1973), pp. 82–100. Reprinted in Fischer (ed.) 1993. [source: SEP-LM bibliography; SEP-Death bibliography]
- The title comes from Karel Čapek's play *Věc Makropulos* ("The Makropulos Thing"; English titles include *The Makropulos Affair*, *The Makropulos Case* and *The Makropulos Secret*, which was Čapek's preferred rendering). It premiered on 21 November 1922 at the Vinohrady Theatre, Prague, and Leoš Janáček based an opera on it. The singer Emilia Marty is really Elina Makropulos, a Cretan woman given an elixir of about 300 years of youth through Emperor Rudolf II's court alchemist. She has lived over 300 years and is cold and indifferent. At the end the formula is offered round and refused, and Kristina burns it over a candle. [source: WP-The_Makropulos_Affair]
- Williams's argument: **categorical desires** (desires not conditional on staying alive, such as a father's desire that his daughter have a good start) give us reason to live and help constitute our character. A premature death that thwarts them is bad. But if we lived long enough we would lose our categorical desires, and "oppressive boredom will set in". [source: SEP-Death §7.2]
- Williams's two conditions for desirable endless life: (1) "it should clearly be me who lives forever," and (2) "the state in which I survive should be one that, to me looking forward, will be adequately related, in the life it presents, to those aims I now have in wanting to survive at all" (1973, p. 83). Replacing one's categorical desires violates at least one of these. [source: SEP-Death §7.2]
- **Replies**: Nagel (1986, p. 224 n. 3), Glover (1977, p. 57) and Fischer (1994) argue that an endless life need not become dull. SEP's own reply is that gradual, continuous change of desires is survival, not annihilation, and many would welcome it. SEP stresses that Williams claims immortality cannot be made *desirable*, which is not identical to claiming it would inevitably be bad. [source: SEP-Death §7.2]
- John Martin Fischer, "Why Immortality Is Not So Bad", *International Journal of Philosophical Studies* 2 (1994): 257–70. Its key distinction is between **self-exhausting pleasures** and **repeatable pleasures** (examples given: fine food or wine, music, spiritual experiences), which can be enjoyed again when spaced out over an endless life. [source: WS — citation and gist via secondary sources; SEP-Death confirms Fischer 1994 as a reply]
- Further arguments that immortality would drain meaning, listed below. [source: SEP-LM §3.3]
  - **Repetition**: only finitely many actions are available (Scarre 2007; May 2009; Smuts 2011).
  - **Narrative**: an endless life would lack shape (May 2009: "With immortality, the novel never ends…"). Reply: life as a string of short stories (Fischer 2009, 2019); eternity could still be novel-like (Seachris 2011).
  - **Lost preciousness and urgency** (Nussbaum 1989; Kass 2002; Hägglund 2019).
  - **No courage in risking one's life** (Kass; Wielenberg 2005).
  - SEP-LM notes that the first two of these turn on the *belief* in immortality, not on immortality itself.
- Shelly Kagan, *Death* (Yale UP, 2012), also holds that living forever would be bad. [source: SEP-Death §7.2 and bibliography]

## 6. Ecclesiastes (Qoheleth): hevel (evenhanded treatment)

- One of the Ketuvim ("Writings") of the Hebrew Bible and wisdom literature of the Christian Old Testament. The Hebrew name is Qōheleṯ/Kohelet, translated in the Septuagint as Greek *Ekklēsiastēs* (from *ekklēsia*, "assembly"). The title means something like "one who convenes or addresses a *qahal*" (assembly). [source: WP-Ecclesiastes]
- **Hevel/hebel**, literally "vapor" or "breath", can figuratively mean "insubstantial", "vain", "futile" or "meaningless". KJV and most older versions render it "vanity" ("Vanity of vanities … all is vanity", 1:2). The NIV renders 1:2 "Meaningless! Meaningless!" Some versions use "meaningless" to avoid confusion with "vanity" as conceit. [source: WP-Ecclesiastes; WS (KJV/NIV renderings, Brown–Driver–Briggs "vapour, breath")]
- Count: a Baker commentary gives 38 occurrences of *hebel* in the book. Other sources give 33 or "nearly forty". Say "some three dozen times". [source: WS]
- Michael V. Fox (*Qohelet and His Contradictions*, 1989) argues that *hebel* is best understood as "absurd", drawing explicitly on Camus. C. L. Seow keeps "vanity" "for want of an adequate alternative". [source: WP-Ecclesiastes; WS (Seow)]
- SEP-LM cites Koheleth, the presumed author, describing life as "futility" (or "vanity") and akin to "the pursuit of wind". [source: SEP-LM intro]
- The key question: "What profit can we show for all our toil, toiling under the sun?" The phrase "under the sun" appears 29 times. Death levels the wise and the foolish. Kohelet's counsel is to enjoy eating, drinking and one's work as gifts "from the hand of God". [source: WP-Ecclesiastes (Fox 2004; Seow 2007)]
- Opposing judgments sit side by side: "the dead are better off than the living" (4:2) vs. "a living dog is better off than a dead lion" (9:4). One reading treats these as provisional stages of a quest. [source: WP-Ecclesiastes (Brown 2011)]
- **Authorship and date (a debate):**
  - Rabbinic tradition ascribes the book to King Solomon (reigned c. 970–931 BCE) in old age.
  - Persian loanwords (*pardes*, "orchard"; *pitgam*, "sentence") and Aramaisms point to no earlier than c. 450 BCE (Seow). The latest possible date is 180 BCE, when Ben Sira quotes it (Fox).
  - Scholars arguing for a Persian date (c. 450–330 BCE) see no Greek influence. Those arguing for a Hellenistic date (c. 330–180 BCE) see Greek thought and social setting; Martin Hengel saw a Hellenistic outlook.
  - Whether author and narrator are identical is unresolved.
  [source: WP-Ecclesiastes]
- **Epilogue**: most modern commentators regard 12:9–14 as a later scribal addition. Some see 12:13–14 ("Fear God and keep his commandments…") as an orthodox corrective. [source: WP-Ecclesiastes (Fox 2004; Seow 2007)]
- **Debate on the book's message**: pessimistic or life-affirming; coherent or not; orthodox or heterodox. The Talmud suggests the rabbis considered suppressing it over its contradictions. Augustine and Calvin raised no concerns about its canonicity. Shields (2006): "we do not know why or how this book found its way into such esteemed company". In Judaism it is read at Sukkot. [source: WP-Ecclesiastes]
- In SEP-LM, Ecclesiastes is cited as an early source of the argument that life without cosmic justice (an afterlife in which wrongs are righted) is senseless. SEP-LM's reply: a finite afterlife (1,000 years) could suffice for justice, and lives like Mandela's are meaningful precisely in fighting injustice. IEP-MA lists Qoheleth among historic supernaturalists. [source: SEP-LM §2.2; IEP-MA]
- **Evenhandedness note for the writer**: present Ecclesiastes as a text read in several ways: as faith-affirming wisdom (Jewish and Christian traditions, with readings at Sukkot and papal citations), as proto-absurdism (Fox), and as sceptical (Hecht 2003: Kohelet "doubted every aspect of religion…"). Do not adjudicate. [source: WP-Ecclesiastes]

## 7. Stoic preparation for death (verified passages only)

- Seneca (d. 65 CE) "rejects and criticizes … the idea that death is an evil". He works "to help people let go of their fear of death", especially in the *Consolations* and *Letters*. [source: IEP-Sen intro, §3]
- Seneca's death in 65 CE: Nero ordered him to commit suicide after the Pisonian conspiracy. He cut his veins, took hemlock, and finally suffocated in a steam bath (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.60 ff.). IEP notes that the death "seems to be modeled on that of Socrates in Plato's *Phaedo*" and that "Seneca seems to have crafted a philosophical death". [source: IEP-Sen §1]
- *De Brevitate Vitae* (*On the Shortness of Life*) is addressed to Paulinus, usually dated c. 49 CE (Griffin dissents). Chapter 1 argues that life is long enough if well managed. People waste time because they do not value it, and the wise use leisure (*otium*) properly. [source: WP-De_Brevitate_Vitae]
- Gummere's titles for two of Seneca's *Letters* are Ep. 24 "On Despising Death" and Ep. 26 "On Old Age and Death". Seneca's *Letters* "are full of injunctions to meditate on death". [source: WS (titles); WP-Memento_mori]
- **Epictetus**, *Handbook* (*Enchiridion*) ch. 5, tr. Nicholas P. White (Hackett, 1983): "What upsets people is not things themselves but their judgments about the things. For example, death is nothing dreadful (or else it would have appeared dreadful to Socrates)…" [source: WP-Enchiridion_of_Epictetus]
- Epictetus wrote nothing. The *Discourses* and *Handbook* were written by his student Arrian, perhaps c. 104–107 CE. Life, death and pain are "externals", not in our power (*aprohaireta*). [source: IEP-Epict]
- Epictetus (*Discourses* 3.24): when kissing a child, brother or friend, remind yourself that they are mortal, as do "those who stand behind men in their triumphs and remind them that they are mortal". [source: WP-Memento_mori]
- **Marcus Aurelius**, *Meditations* (Greek, *Ta eis heauton*, "things to himself"), 12 books, probably written on campaign in central Europe c. 171–175 CE. It was probably not intended for publication. Marcus was emperor 161–180 CE. [source: IEP-MAur §2; WP-Meditations]
- *Meditations* 4.17, George Long's translation (1862): "Do not act as if thou wert going to live ten thousand years. Death hangs over thee. While thou livest, while it is in thy power, be good." The modern "you were going to live" wording is a paraphrase. [source: WS (Lexundria text of Long)]
- *Meditations* 4.48.2: "consider how ephemeral and mean all mortal things are". The translator is not named in WP. [source: WP-Memento_mori]
- Background: Plato's *Phaedo* (64a) says the proper practice of philosophy is "about nothing else but dying and being dead". [source: WP-Memento_mori]

## 8. Buddhist impermanence and death

(The three marks, anātman and the four noble truths are in the early-Buddhism notes; cross-reference, do not repeat.)

- The first noble truth lists birth, ageing, illness and death among forms of suffering. The twelfth link of dependent origination is "old age and death". [source: early-buddhism notes (IEP-Bud §3c)]
- **Maraṇasati** ("mindfulness of death") is defined explicitly only twice in the earliest discourses, in AN 6.19 and AN 6.20. AN 6.19 (tr. Bhikkhu Sujato): monks who develop mindfulness of death "by wishing to live as long as it takes to breathe out after breathing in … live diligently". AN 6.20 compares the urgency to someone whose turban or head is on fire. [source: WP-Maraṇasati]
- Charnel-ground contemplations of a corpse in nine stages of decay appear in the *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (MN 10) and *Kāyagatāsati Sutta* (MN 119). Skeletons are displayed in some Thai monasteries' meditation halls (e.g. Wat Pah Nanachat). [source: WP-Maraṇasati]
- Pāli *maraṇa* ("death") is an Indo-European cognate of Latin *mori*. [source: WP-Memento_mori]
- **Kisā Gotamī and the mustard seed**: her only child dies, and she carries the body seeking medicine. The Buddha asks for (white) mustard seed from a house where no one has died. She finds none, realises that death is universal, becomes a nun and in time an arahant. [source: WP-Kisa_Gotami]
  - **Source and dating — important:** the mustard-seed story is **not** in the canonical *Therīgāthā* verses ascribed to her. It is told in the **commentaries**: the *Therīgāthā* commentary (ThigA X.1), the *Dhammapada* commentary (on verses 114/287; Dhp-a 8.13) and the *Aṅguttara* commentary. A translator notes that the canonical verses under her name tell a different story, one the commentary assigns to Paṭācārā. [source: WS (Access to Insight and ancient-buddhist-texts translations)]
  - The *Dhammapada* commentary (*Dhammapada-aṭṭhakathā*) is traditionally attributed to Buddhaghosa. [source: WP-Dhammapada infobox] A precise date for these commentaries was NOT verified (see the NOT VERIFIED section).
  - Dhammapada 114 is associated with her story (Buddharakkhita tr.): "Though one should live a hundred years without seeing the Deathless State, yet better indeed, is a single day's life of one who sees the Deathless State." [source: WP-Kisa_Gotami]
  - In the canonical *Gotami Sutta* (SN 5.3, tr. Thanissaro), the nun Kisā Gotamī declares: "I've gotten past the killing of [my] sons… I don't grieve, I don't weep… Having defeated the army of death, free of fermentations I dwell." [source: WP-Kisa_Gotami]
  - A parallel story is told of the Greek Cynic Demonax (Lucian): he offered to summon a dead son's shade if the father could name three people who had never mourned. [source: WP-Kisa_Gotami]
- The Buddha's own death is narrated in the *Mahāparinibbāna Sutta* (at Kuśinagara). [source: early-buddhism notes]

## 9. Tolstoy's crisis: *A Confession*

- Leo (Lev Nikolayevich) Tolstoy: born 9 September 1828 at Yasnaya Polyana; died 20 November 1910, aged 82 (Gregorian dates). [source: WP-Leo_Tolstoy]
- *Confession* (Russian *Ispoved'*) was written in 1879–80 (WP) when he was in his early fifties; IEP-ME says "written in Russian in 1879". The first attempt at publication was in *Russkaya Mysl'*, no. 5, **1882**, but Orthodox Church censorship removed it from virtually the whole print run. It was published in Geneva in 1884 and in Russia only in 1906. IEP-ME: "circulated in 1882, and translated and published in 1884". It was originally titled *An Introduction to a Criticism of Dogmatic Theology*, the first part of a four-part work that included *What I Believe*. **Safe wording: "written 1879–80, suppressed by the censor in 1882, published 1884".** [source: WP-Confession_(Leo_Tolstoy); IEP-ME §2d]
- The crisis came in the mid-to-late 1870s, after *War and Peace* (1869) and *Anna Karenina* (1878). Despite wealth, fame and family, Tolstoy suffered depression, "psychological paralysis, obsession with suicide". [source: IEP-ME §2d; WP-Leo_Tolstoy]
- His questions, as IEP lists them: "What is it for? What does it lead to? … What will come of my whole life? … Why do I live? … What is the meaning of my life?" IEP's summary of his problem: "since I will suffer, die, be forgotten, and make no difference (leave no trace) in the long run, how does my life, or anything I do, have any meaning?" [source: IEP-ME §2d]
- He sought answers in the sciences and in "Socrates, the Buddha, 'Solomon' (the author of Ecclesiastes), and Schopenhauer", and read all as negative. "Solomon" teaches that it is all "vanity". [source: IEP-ME §2d]
- **The Eastern fable** (in chapter IV of *Confession*): a traveller chased by a beast hides in a dry well and sees a dragon at the bottom. He clings to a branch that two mice, one black and one white (day and night), are gnawing. He licks drops of honey on the leaves. Tolstoy can no longer enjoy the honey, which stands for his family and his writing. WP calls it "the Oriental fable of the dragon in the well". [source: WP-Confession; WS (chapter IV; marxists.org Part 4)]
  - Origin: the "man in the well" parable is Indian. Versions appear in the *Mahābhārata* (Strī Parva, book 11, ch. 5, with honey and black and white mice), on 3rd-century CE Buddhist reliefs (Nāgārjunakoṇḍa, Amarāvatī; M. Zin), in *Kalīla wa Dimna*, and in the Barlaam and Josaphat legend. That legend is a Christianised life of the Buddha, which reached Old Russian as *Varlaam and Ioasaf*. Ya. Vassilkov (*Etnograficheskoe obozrenie* 2020, no. 4) traces Tolstoy's source. [source: WS — search summaries of Vassilkov, Zin, the Jewish Encyclopedia and C.-W. Lin; WP-Barlaam_and_Josaphat confirms the legend derives from a 2nd–4th century Sanskrit Buddhist text via a Manichaean version] Say only "an ancient Indian parable that reached Russia through the Barlaam and Josaphat tradition"; do not claim a single source text.
- **Four responses** that Tolstoy surveys: ignorance; "Epicureanism" (enjoy what one has, which he rejects as morally empty given others' poverty); suicide (the "logically consistent" response, which he is "too cowardly" for); and holding on despite absurdity. [source: WP-Confession — the quoted phrases are WP's rendering; check against a translation before quoting]
- **His answer**: he found it among the unlearned peasants. Meaning lies in "a kind of irrational knowledge called 'faith'". Faith is faith in God; meaning is found in the right relationship to "the Infinite". WP reports the formula "God is Life". He developed this in *What I Believe*, *On Life* and late fiction (*The Death of Ivan Ilych*, *Father Sergius*). To the end he held that faith in God, work, service, unselfishness and love are essential to meaning, and that wealth, status, power and fame contribute nothing. [source: IEP-ME §2d; WP-Confession]
- IEP-ME compares Tolstoy's solution to Kierkegaard's. William James discusses *Confession* at length in *The Varieties of Religious Experience*. [source: IEP-ME; WP-Confession]
- **Tolstoy in the analytic literature**: SEP-LM credits Tolstoy with the soul-centred argument that for life to be meaningful something must be worth doing, that something is worth doing only if it makes a **permanent difference**, and that a permanent difference requires immortality. Reply: helping people is worth doing even if all are mortal; Wielenberg 2005 even argues it is worthwhile *because* they are mortal. SEP-LM also says nihilism from "extreme supernaturalism + atheism" was "Tolstoy's prior to acquiring faith" (and Camus's). [source: SEP-LM §2.2, §4]
- The English translation cited by SEP-LM is by Louise and Aylmer Maude (*A Confession and What I Believe*, Oxford UP, 1921). [source: SEP-LM "Classic Texts"]

## 10. Camus: *The Myth of Sisyphus*

(Full treatment and the objections are in the existentialism notes; use only what the thematic argument needs.)

- *The Myth of Sisyphus* was published October 1942, in the "triptych of the absurd" with *The Stranger* and *Caligula*. [source: SEP-C §3; existentialism notes]
- Opening (Justin O'Brien tr., Knopf 1955; IEP-MA cites the Vintage 1983 reprint): "There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide." The final lines: "The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy." [source: SEP-C intro (quotes both)] Note: SEP-C elsewhere modifies the translation ("only one really serious philosophical question"); use the O'Brien wording.
- The absurd is the confrontation of the human demand for clarity with "the unreasonable silence of the world". The two rejected responses are suicide and hope. Since "the most obvious absurdity" is death, Camus urges us to "die unreconciled and not of one's own free will" (MS 55). [source: SEP-C §3.2–3.3]
- IEP-ME's summary: Camus held life absurd and meaningless, and that the best response is to live "with passion, using everything up, and with an attitude of revolt". [source: IEP-ME §1]
- SEP-LM classifies Camus's position as nihilism resting on extreme supernaturalism plus atheism. [source: SEP-LM §4]

## 11. Contemporary theories of meaning (SEP-LM, Metz)

### Supernaturalism
- **Extreme** supernaturalism: God or a soul is necessary for *any* meaning. **Moderate**: God or a soul is necessary only for great or ultimate meaning. This distinction cross-cuts the one between **God-centred** and **soul-centred** views. Supernaturalism (God *would* confer meaning) is logically distinct from theism (God exists). [source: SEP-LM §2]
- **God's-purpose view**: the oldest Western God-based account. Life is meaningful insofar as one fulfils a purpose God assigned. Supporting argument: only God could ground invariant moral rules or objective value (Craig; Cottingham 2005). Objections: the Euthyphro problem; the charge that a purpose assigned by God would **degrade** us. This objection "harks back to Jean-Paul Sartre"; Kurt Baier (1957) first articulated it in the analytic literature. [source: SEP-LM §2.1]
- **Nozick's regress** (*Philosophical Explanations*, 1981, pp. 594–618): a finite condition gets meaning from relation to something beyond it, and the regress ends only in something unlimited, God. Nozick's line: "Limited transcendence … does give our lives meaning—but a limited one. We may thirst for more" (1981, p. 618). Objection, which Nozick came to accept (1989): a finite thing can get meaning from relation to something valuable but not itself "meaningful". [source: SEP-LM §2.1]
- Counterexamples to extreme supernaturalism: Einstein, Mandela and Picasso seem to have meaningful lives even if there is no God. Hence the shift to moderate supernaturalism. [source: SEP-LM §2.1]
- **Soul-centred arguments**:
  1. The Tolstoy permanence argument (above).
  2. Justice requires an afterlife (Ecclesiastes; Craig).
  3. Kantian free will requires a non-physical self. Reply: compatibilism.
  4. Deep aspirations for eternal completion. Metz's reply is that these are parochial, rarer in "Confucian, Hindu, or traditional African" traditions.
  [source: SEP-LM §2.2]

### Naturalism
- **Subjectivism**: meaning depends on an individual's pro-attitudes. Harry Frankfurt: meaning lies in caring or loving (1988, 2004). It was dominant mid-20th century (Ayer, Hare, Taylor 1970, Williams 1976). Objection by counterexample: maintaining 3,732 hairs on one's head (Charles Taylor 1992), long-distance spitting or collecting a big ball of string (Wolf 2010), eating one's own excrement (Wielenberg 2005). [source: SEP-LM §3.1]
- **Objectivism**: meaning is at least partly mind-independent. "Morality (the good), enquiry (the true), and creativity (the beautiful)" are widely held to confer meaning, while "trimming toenails and eating snow" do not. [source: SEP-LM §3.2]
- **Susan Wolf's hybrid ("fitting fulfilment") view**: "Meaning arises when subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness." SEP-LM cites Wolf 2015, p. 112. IEP-MA cites Wolf 1997, p. 211 ("Happiness and Meaning: Two Aspects of the Good Life", *Social Philosophy and Policy* 14). The slogan has appeared in both places; attribute it to Wolf and cite the 1997 essay as its first appearance per IEP. On her view, no meaning accrues from caring about a worthless project, or from doing a worthwhile one one does not care about. [source: SEP-LM §3.2; IEP-MA §3d]
- Susan Rose Wolf (b. 1952) is Edna J. Koury Distinguished Professor at UNC Chapel Hill (from 2002; previously Johns Hopkins 1986–2002). *Meaning in Life and Why It Matters* (Princeton UP, 2010) comes from her Tanner Lectures, per standard bibliographies. The Tanner origin is NOT checked; omit it. [source: WP-Susan_R._Wolf; SEP-LM bibliography]
- **Metz's own theory**: SEP-LM lists among objectivist proposals "exercising rational nature in exceptional ways (… Metz 2013, 222–36 …)". That is Metz's theory in *Meaning in Life: An Analytic Study* (Oxford UP, 2013), known as the **"fundamentality theory"**. Metz also offers a pluralist "family resemblance" analysis of the concept. IEP-AF quotes it: to ask about meaning is to ask "which ends, besides one's own pleasure as such are most worth pursuing for their own sake; how to transcend one's animal nature; and what in life merits great esteem or admiration" (Metz 2013, p. 34). [source: SEP-LM §3.2; IEP-AF §1] The exact formula of the fundamentality theory (reason oriented towards "fundamental conditions of human existence") was not read in a source this session; see NOT VERIFIED.
- Thaddeus Metz (b. 1968), American-born, works mainly at South African universities: Distinguished Professor, Johannesburg, 2015–20; now University of Pretoria. Cornell PhD 1997. [source: WP-Thaddeus_Metz]
- **Holism and narrative**: a repetitive *Groundhog Day*-type life is less meaningful (Richard Taylor 1987). A life ending on an upswing seems more meaningful (Kamm 2013). Redemptive patterns matter (Velleman; Fischer; Kauppinen). Galen Strawson (2018) denies narrativity matters. Samuel Scheffler (2013) argues that little meaning would remain if humanity were to go extinct soon. [source: SEP-LM §3.2]
- **Extreme naturalism / "anti-theism"**: God or a soul would *reduce* meaning. Arguments: loss of independence and dignity (Baier 1957; Kahane 2011); loss of privacy before an omniscient God (Kahane; Lougheed 2020); and the immortality arguments in §5 above. [source: SEP-LM §3.3]

### The absurd and Sisyphus in analytic philosophy
- **Thomas Nagel, "The Absurd"**, *Journal of Philosophy* 68 (1971): 716–27. Absurdity arises from "the collision between the seriousness with which we take our lives and the perpetual possibility of regarding everything about which we are serious as arbitrary, or open to doubt" (p. 718). It ends: "If sub specie aeternitatis there is no reason to believe that anything matters, then that does not matter either, and we can approach our absurd lives with irony instead of heroism or despair." Nagel calls Camus's defiance "romantic and slightly self-pitying". [source: IEP-MA bibliography (1971 date); WS (wording of both quotations; "romantic…" via secondary sources)] SEP-LM's bibliography misdates it to 1970 (vol. 68 is 1971; the paper was prepared for an APA symposium on 29 December 1971).
- IEP-MA quotes Nagel 1971, p. 716: "… it does not matter now that in a million years nothing we do now will matter." [source: IEP-MA §4]
- **Richard Taylor, "The Meaning of Life"**, in *Good and Evil*. IEP-MA dates it 1967 (Macmillan); SEP-LM gives 1970 (Prometheus reprint 2000). Taylor uses Sisyphus as the paradigm of meaninglessness, then imagines the gods implanting in Sisyphus a desire to roll stones. His life then has meaning *for him*, though nothing external has changed. This is a classic subjectivist case. Joel Feinberg ("Absurd Self-Fulfillment") objects. [source: SEP-LM §1, §3.1; IEP-MA bibliography; WS (summary of the implant variation)]
- SEP-LM says most analytic philosophers deny meaning to the life of Sisyphus or of a person in the experience machine. [source: SEP-LM §1]

### Nihilism and pessimism
- **Nihilism**: what would make a life meaningful cannot obtain for any of us. Routes:
  1. Extreme supernaturalism plus atheism (Camus; Tolstoy pre-faith).
  2. Error theory (Mackie 1977: objective values would be "utterly different from anything else in the universe"; Ruse and Wilson 1986: we are "deceived by their genes").
  3. An infinite universe already contains infinite value, so we cannot add to it (Smith 2003).
  4. Benatar.
  5. Denial of self or free will.
  [source: SEP-LM §4]
- **David Benatar** (b. 1966), South African philosopher, emeritus professor at the University of Cape Town. *Better Never to Have Been: The Harm of Coming into Existence* (Oxford UP, 2006). His **asymmetry argument** for anti-natalism: (1) the presence of pain is bad; (2) the presence of pleasure is good; (3) the absence of pain is good, even if no one enjoys that good; (4) the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is someone for whom it is a deprivation. So coming into existence is always a harm relative to never existing. [source: WP-David_Benatar; WP-Benatar's_asymmetry_argument; SEP-LM §4]
- Benatar, *The Human Predicament* (Oxford UP, 2017). Drawing on Nagel's "view from nowhere" and Sidgwick's "point of view of the universe", he grants terrestrial meaning but denies **cosmic** meaning. Replies: lives could have cosmic significance (Kahane 2014; Wolf 2016); cosmic significance is irrelevant (Landau 2017); the concept is incoherent (Maitzen 2022). [source: SEP-LM §4]

## 12. Non-Western perspectives

### Zhuangzi on his wife's death
- Zhuangzi (Zhuang Zhou): IEP gives 369–298 BCE; WP says most historians place his birth around 369 BCE. Huizi (Hui Shi, IEP: 380–305 BCE) is his friend and foil. [source: IEP-Zhuangzi https://iep.utm.edu/zhuangzi-chuang-tzu-chinese-philosopher/; WP-Zhuangzi_(book)]
- The text comes in 33 chapters as arranged by Guo Xiang (252–312 CE), reduced from 52: inner chapters 1–7, outer chapters 8–22 and miscellaneous chapters 23–33. Traditionally only the **inner chapters** are ascribed to Zhuang Zhou himself. The wife story is in **chapter 18, "Zhi le" ("Ultimate Joy"), an OUTER chapter**, so it belongs to the Zhuangzi tradition rather than certainly to Zhuangzi himself. [source: WP-Zhuangzi_(book)]
- Ch. 18, Burton Watson tr. (1964, p. 115): Huizi finds Zhuangzi "sitting with his legs sprawled out, pounding on a tub and singing." Zhuangzi: "When she first died, do you think I didn't grieve like anyone else? But I looked back to her beginning and the time before she was born. … In the midst of the jumble of wonder and mystery a change took place and she had a spirit. Another change and she had a body. Another change and she was born. Now there's been another change and she's dead. It's just like the progression of the four seasons, spring, summer, fall, winter. … If I were to follow after her bawling and sobbing, it would show that I don't understand anything about fate. So I stopped." [source: WP-Zhuangzi_(book)] Note the structural parallel with Lucretius: looking back to before birth.
- Ch. 2 (inner chapter; Watson tr.): "How do I know that loving life is not a delusion? How do I know that in hating death I am not like a man who, having left home in his youth, has forgotten the way back?" [source: WP-Zhuangzi_(book), citing Nivison 1999]
- Ch. 32 (miscellaneous chapter): on his deathbed Zhuangzi refuses a lavish funeral: "I take heaven and earth as my inner and outer coffins…" When his disciples fear crows will eat him, he replies, "Above ground I'd be eaten by crows and kites, below ground … by mole crickets and ants. You rob the one and give to the other—how skewed would that be?" (Kern tr.) [source: WP-Zhuangzi_(book)]
- Interpretation: death is a natural transformation to be accepted (Mair 1994). [source: WP-Zhuangzi_(book)]

### African philosophical views on meaning
- IEP-AF (Attoe) identifies seven theories of meaning in African philosophical literature:
  1. the African God-purpose theory;
  2. the vital force theory;
  3. the communal normative (function) theory;
  4. the love theory;
  5. the Yoruba cluster theory;
  6. personhood and a meaningful life;
  7. the conversational theory.
  [source: IEP-AF contents]
- **Vital force**: a spiritual force "emanating from God and present in all created realities", strongest in humans and ancestors. On one account (Bikopo & van Bogaert 2010), to be dead is "to have a diminished life because of a reduced level of energy". [source: IEP-AF §3b]
- **Communal normative theory** (Attoe 2020) rests on communalism. Mbiti: "I am because we are and since we are, therefore I am" (Mbiti 1990, p. 106). The Ubuntu formula: "a person is a person through other persons". Meaning comes from "engaging harmoniously with others", acquiring personhood. Menkiti ties personhood itself to communal life. [source: IEP-AF §3c]
- Attoe (2021) adds subjectivity and **coherence** (a unifying narrative) to Metz's family-resemblance concept. [source: IEP-AF §1]
- SEP-LM lists Attoe (ed.), *African Perspectives to the Question of Life's Meaning* (Routledge, 2024) under non-Western books, and cites Gordon 2000 for Africana accounts. It notes that "deep" aspirations for eternal life are rarer in traditional African thought. [source: SEP-LM §2.2, §3.2, bibliography]

### Japanese: mono no aware and Kenkō
- *Mono no aware*, "the pathos of things", arises from their transience. It figures in the *Man'yōshū* (8th century) and in Murasaki Shikibu's *Tale of Genji* (early 11th century). Motoori Norinaga (18th century) made it central to literary theory through a study of *Genji*. The cherry blossom is the standard example: valued because it falls within about a week. [source: SEP-JA §2; WP-Mono_no_aware]
- Classical Japanese thought understands reality as impermanence (*mujō*); Dōgen (13th century) is its most forceful exponent. [source: SEP-JA §1]
- Yoshida Kenkō, *Essays in Idleness* (*Tsurezuregusa*, c. 1332), Donald Keene tr. (Columbia UP, 1967): "If man were never to fade away like the dews of Adashino, never to vanish like the smoke over Toribeyama, how things would lose their power to move us! The most precious thing in life is its uncertainty" (Keene, 7). Also: "While they live they do not rejoice in life, but, when faced with death, they fear it—what could be more illogical?" (Keene, 79). [source: SEP-JA §1–2] A useful foil to Williams: Kenkō argues that mortality is a condition of value.
- The *Hagakure* (samurai treatise) calls the Way of the Samurai "morning after morning, the practice of death". [source: WP-Memento_mori] Use cautiously: this is a martial text, not mainstream Buddhism.

## Argument for the chapter's step-by-step reconstruction (recommended)

**Epicurus's argument → deprivation objection → timing/subject problem → replies.**
1. Premises 1–9 as in SEP's reconstruction (§2 above), or IEP's eight-step version.
2. Best objection: the deprivation account (Nagel 1970; Feldman 1991). Death harms by precluding goods. Use the Hilda case.
3. Epicurean counter: the timing puzzle and the problem of the subject ("when we are, death is not").
4. Replies: atemporalism (Feldman; Johansson), eternalism (Nagel; Silverstein), subsequentism (Feit; Bradley).
5. Then Lucretius's symmetry argument as a second Epicurean attempt, with the replies of Nagel, Kaufman, Parfit and Kamm.

All of this is in SEP-Death §§3–5. A second, shorter reconstruction could be Williams's tedium argument with Fischer's repeatable pleasures (§5).

## Debates
- **Is death bad for the one who dies?** Epicureans (Epicurus; Lucretius; modern defenders such as Rosenbaum 1986, "How to Be Dead and Not Care") vs. deprivationists (Nagel 1970; Feldman 1991; Bradley). The sub-debate is *when* death harms: concurrentism, priorism, subsequentism, indefinitism or atemporalism. [source: SEP-Death §§3–4, bibliography]
- **Does the symmetry argument work?** Nagel (essential origins) vs. the frozen-zygote counterexample; Kaufman (persons vs. organisms); Parfit (future bias as rational given our attitudes, but regrettable); Kamm and Luper (forward-looking projects). Interpretive debate: is Lucretius addressing *dying* or *being dead*? Warren argues that only one version of the symmetry argument is in DRN 3.832–42 and 972–5, and that it is not aimed at a prospective fear of mortality. [source: SEP-Death §5.2; WS (Warren)]
- **Would immortality be good?** Williams 1973 and Kagan 2012 (no; tedium and loss of categorical desires) vs. Nagel, Glover and Fischer 1994 (repeatable pleasures; change of interests). Further debate over repetition, narrative and preciousness (May; Nussbaum; Hägglund). [source: SEP-Death §7; SEP-LM §3.3]
- **Supernaturalism vs. naturalism**, extreme vs. moderate; God-centred vs. soul-centred; and the "anti-theism" debate (would God reduce meaning?). [source: SEP-LM §§2–3]
- **Subjectivism vs. objectivism vs. hybrid** (Frankfurt and Richard Taylor vs. objectivists vs. Wolf). [source: SEP-LM §3]
- **Cosmic meaning**: Benatar (unavailable) vs. Kahane, Wolf, Landau and Maitzen. [source: SEP-LM §4]
- **How to read Ecclesiastes**: pessimist or life-affirming; Persian or Hellenistic date; Solomonic tradition vs. critical dating; whether the epilogue is original. [source: WP-Ecclesiastes]
- **Are the Zhuangzi outer chapters Zhuangzi's?** The traditional ascription is of the inner chapters only. Intertextual analysis does not show that the inner chapters are the earliest stratum (Klein 2010). [source: WP-Zhuangzi_(book)]
- **Camus's argument**: Jeanson (1947) calls absurdism "a contradiction in terms", and Aronson says Camus asserts more than he argues. Nagel says Camus's defiance is "romantic and slightly self-pitying". [source: existentialism notes (SEP-C); WS (Nagel)]

## Surprises (for the writer)
- The mustard-seed story of Kisā Gotamī, the most famous Buddhist story about grief, is **not in the canon**. It is in later commentaries, and her canonical verses tell a different story.
- Zhuangzi's drumming on a tub after his wife's death is in an **outer chapter** (18), not in the inner chapters traditionally ascribed to Zhuangzi himself.
- *A Confession* is usually dated 1882, but it was written in 1879–80 and **suppressed by the censor in 1882**. It first appeared in print in Geneva in 1884 and in Russia only in 1906.
- SEP-LM misdates Nagel's "The Absurd" to 1970 (it is 1971). SEP-LM and IEP disagree on Richard Taylor's "The Meaning of Life" (1970 vs. 1967), and the two SEP entries disagree on Fischer's book (2019 vs. 2020).
- SEP-Death (2026 revision) notes that deprivationism implies death is **good** for some people, those whose future would have been net bad (the Hilda case). The deprivation account does not show that death is always bad. Only Nagel's stronger "experience itself" claim does, and SEP rejects that claim.
- The man-in-the-well fable Tolstoy uses to express his despair has Indian (Mahābhārata and Buddhist) roots. In the Christian Barlaam and Josaphat legend it reached Russia as a parable from a Christianised life of the Buddha.
