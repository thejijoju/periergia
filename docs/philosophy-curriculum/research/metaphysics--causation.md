## 1. The problem and the map of theories
- Three projects: ontological (what causation is), semantic/conceptual (what "cause" means), and epistemological/methodological (how we find out); they are often pursued together. [source: IEP-C §1]
- Type vs token causation: "Drowsy driving causes crashes" (general) vs "The drought caused the famine" (particular); Gallow also distinguishes influence between variables ("How much I water my plant influences how tall it grows"). There is disagreement whether each relation exists and which is more fundamental. [source: SEP-Gal intro, §1]
- The main reductive families: regularities, counterfactuals, probabilities, processes, dispositions, mechanisms, and interventions (non-reductive). [source: SEP-Gal §1; IEP-C §1]
- Since Lewis (1973) criticised the regularity theory and proposed a counterfactual analysis, "counterfactual theories of causation have risen and regularity theories have more and more fallen into disuse". [source: SEP-Reg intro]
- Broadbent (IEP) calls disagreement about fundamentals "great news if you are a philosopher": "a field of engagement that has not settled into trench warfare". [source: IEP-C §1]

## 2. Aristotle's four causes (recap; cross-ref *Aristotle*)
- Account in *Physics* II 3 and *Metaphysics* V (Δ) 2, repeated "in almost the same words" in both. [source: SEP-AC §§2, 7]
- Knowledge requires grasping the cause: "we think we do not have knowledge of a thing until we have grasped its why, that is to say, its cause" (*Phys.* II 3, 194b17–20; cf. *APost.* I 2, 71b9–11). [source: SEP-AC §2]
- *Aitia* / *aition* = cause; "why" = *dia ti*, *dioti*. Because a why-question is a request for an explanation, "it can be useful to think of a cause as a certain type of explanation" (Falcon). Falcon also quotes Stein (2012a): causes "are not ways in which we explain things, except derivatively". [source: SEP-AC §2, §8 glossary]
- Falcon's question-glosses: material = "What is it made out of?" (not only physical stuff: letters A and B are the material cause of the syllable BA); formal = "What is it?" (essence); efficient = "Where does change (or motion) come from?"; final = "What is its good?" (that for the sake of which). [source: SEP-AC §2]
- Physics II 3 (194b23–35) as quoted in SEP-Ar (translator not named in the entry): "that out of which a thing comes to be and which persists, e.g. the bronze of the statue, the silver of the bowl"; "the form or the pattern … e.g. the cause of an octave is a ratio of 2:1"; "the primary source of the change and rest … the father is the cause of the child, and generally the maker is the cause of what is made"; "the end (telos) … that for the sake of which (hou heneka) a thing is done, e.g. health is the cause of walking about. 'Why is he walking about?' We say: 'To be healthy'". [source: SEP-Ar §7]
- Worked example (bronze statue): bronze = material (and the subject that undergoes change: melted and poured); shape = formal; efficient = for Aristotle, most precisely, *the art of bronze-casting*, not the individual artisan (*Phys.* II 3, 195a6–8, 195b21–25); final = the statue, for whose sake model, mould and pouring are done. [source: SEP-AC §2] (Shields's simpler version: sculptor = efficient; honouring the president = final. [source: SEP-Ar §7])
- Not everything has all four: coincidences have no final cause (debtor meets creditor at the market); mathematical abstractions lack matter. [source: SEP-Ar §7] A lunar eclipse has no final cause (*Metaphysics*). [source: SEP-AC §6]
- Aristotle is "committed to a form of causal pluralism" (Stein 2011): "four primary and irreducible kinds of causes" (*Metaph.* I 3; *APost.* II 11). [source: SEP-AC §1]
- Hankinson, *Cause and Explanation in Ancient Greek Thought* (Oxford, 1998) is listed in Falcon's general survey bibliography; Frede 1980 "The Original Notion of Cause" likewise. [source: SEP-AC bibliography]
- Avicenna re-defined the four causes for metaphysics (see §3). Modern theories of causation are mostly about Aristotle's *efficient* cause (implicit throughout SEP-Gal, SEP-Reg; state as a gloss, not a quotation).

## 3. Islamic philosophy: Avicenna, al-Ghazālī, Averroes (cross-ref *Falsafa*; *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes*)
### Avicenna (c. 980–1037) [dates: IEP-Av]
- In the *Metaphysics of the Healing* he defines the efficient cause or agent as "that which bestows existence to another" (MH 194): "Metaphysical philosophers do not mean by 'agent' only the principle of motion, as the natural philosophers mean, but the principle and giver of existence, as in the case of God with respect to the world." Natural efficient causes bestow only motion — an inferior way of bestowing existence. [source: SEP-AIC §2.2.2]
- Avicenna holds that efficient causes are sufficient for, necessitate, and **coexist** with their effects (cause and effect "exist together in time, in eternity, or whatever"; MH 127); causal priority is ontological, not temporal. Argued from the principle of sufficient reason (PSR): if, given A, B "hangs in the balance" between existence and non-existence, A has "no greater claim to be the cause than not to be the cause" (MH 127). [source: SEP-AIC §3.2.1]
- Natural necessity, Avicenna's example: fire necessitates burning in cotton "when it is in contact with (dry) cotton and any obstacles have been removed." [source: SEP-AIC §3.2.2]
- Avicenna himself noted the objection that sensation "leads only to concomitance" (MH I.1, p. 5), replying that empirical causal claims rest on two background claims: that causes exist and that regularities have a basis in the natural powers of bodies. [source: SEP-AIC §4.2]

### Al-Ghazālī (c. 1056–1111): the Seventeenth Discussion, step by step
- *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* (*Tahāfut al-falāsifa*), written (by his own account) in his third year at the Baghdad Niẓāmiyya; Periergia's chapter dates it "around 1095"; WP says 11th century. [sources: SEP-Gh §2; WP-The_Incoherence_of_the_Philosophers]
- Main target: Avicenna's view that natural substances are necessitating causes (Lizzini 2013); argument dubbed "no necessary connections" (Nadler 1996). [source: SEP-AIC §3.3.1]
- The thesis (Marmura trans., IP 166): see Quotations. Examples: quenching of thirst and drinking, satiety and eating, burning and contact with fire, light and the appearance of the sun, death and decapitation, "and so on to [include] all [that is] observable among connected things in medicine, astronomy, arts, and crafts." [source: SEP-AIC §3.3.1]
- **Argument A ("no necessary connections")**, reconstructed (Richardson):
  1. The philosophers hold (i) natural substances such as fire are causes and (ii) causal connections are necessary connections.
  2. (i) and (ii) imply (iii): the affirmation of contact between fire and cotton entails the affirmation of the burning.
  3. But "we allow the possibility of the occurrence of the contact without the burning, and we allow as possible the occurrence of the cotton's transformation into burnt ashes without contact with the fire" (IP 166–7).
  4. A connection is not necessary if it is conceivable that it be severed (al-Ghazālī's modal criterion: Kukkonen 2000; Dutton 2001).
  5. So (iii) is false, and the philosophers' account of causation in nature is mistaken. [source: SEP-AIC §3.3.1, §3.3.3]
- **Argument B (sceptical/epistemic)**: the philosophers' only evidence that fire is the agent is "observing the occurrence of the burning at the [juncture of] contact with the fire" (IP 167); "we observe not that fire burns cotton but rather that cotton burns when in contact with fire" (an Ashʿarite point); so repeated observation does not show fire is the agent, and is consistent with God's being the agent: "The one who enacts the burning by creating blackness in the cotton, [causing] separation in its parts, and making it cinder or ashes is God, either through the mediation of His angels or without mediation" (IP 167). Also: fire, "which is inanimate, … has no action". [source: SEP-AIC §4.2]
- The theological purpose (miracles possible) and the second, non-occasionalist answer in the same discussion: treat via cross-reference to *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes* (the published chapter covers it).
- Interpretive debate: Marmura held al-Ghazālī committed to Ashʿarite occasionalism (Marmura 2005); Griffel, Perler and Rudolph argue he leaves open genuine secondary causality of a non-falsafa type (Griffel 2007; Perler & Rudolph 2000). [source: SEP-Occ §2 (al-Ghazālī section)]

### Averroes (Ibn Rushd, 1126–1198), *The Incoherence of the Incoherence* (*Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*)
- A direct response to al-Ghazālī, whose work had become influential in al-Andalus. [source: SEP-IR §1]
- **Reply 1 (misreport + revised account):** acts proceed through "one relation out of an infinite number, and it happens often that one relation hinders another. Therefore it is not absolutely certain that fire acts when it is brought near a sensitive body … as is asserted of talc and other things. But one need not therefore deny fire its burning power so long as fire keeps its name and definition" (ITI 318–19). So: contact *plus no impediment* entails burning; the philosophers' account, properly understood, is not mistaken. [source: SEP-AIC §3.3.2]
- **Reply 2 (argument from natural science):** we do have knowledge of nature, and knowledge of nature just is knowledge of natural causes (ITI 318–19); the denier of causes "denies with his tongue what is present in his mind" (ITI 318). [source: SEP-AIC §4.3]
- **Objection to Averroes:** on al-Ghazālī's conceivability criterion even the impediment-free connection is not necessary, so Reply 1 begs the modal question; the real disagreement is over the nature of necessity (the philosophers do not define modality by conceivability). Reply 2 may miss al-Ghazālī's target, since he may allow fire as a non-active cause with God as active cause (McGinnis 2006). Neither al-Ghazālī nor Averroes engages Avicenna's PSR-based defence of natural necessity. [source: SEP-AIC §3.3.2–3.3.3, §4.3]

## 4. Occasionalism and Latin echoes (brief; cross-ref *Descartes*, *Leibniz*)
- Nicholas of Autrecourt (1300–d. after 1350): sources suggest he knew al-Ghazālī's arguments; held that the existence of one thing cannot be logically inferred from another; writings publicly burned 1347; often called "the fourteenth-century Hume" (Nadler 1996) — SEP judges him closer to a sceptic than an occasionalist. [source: SEP-Occ §2]
- Nicolas Malebranche (1638–1715); *The Search after Truth* 1674–75. [sources: WP-Nicolas_Malebranche; SEP-Mal §1]
- Malebranche: "there is only one true cause because there is only one true God; … all natural causes are not true causes but only occasional causes" (OCM II 312 / *Search* 448, trans. Lennon & Olscamp). [source: SEP-Occ intro]
- Malebranche's argument: "A true cause as I understand it is one such that the mind perceives a necessary connection between it and its effect. Now the mind perceives a necessary connection only between the will of an infinitely perfect being and its effects. Therefore, it is only God who is the true cause and who truly has the power to move bodies" (OCM II 316 / *Search* 450). [source: SEP-Occ §3.3; SEP-Mal]
- SEP-H notes Hume's account has "antecedents in Malebranche", though Hume is critical of crucial Malebranchean points. [source: SEP-H (line on Malebranche)]

## 5. Indian traditions: a worked contrast (cross-ref *Classical Indian Schools*)
- In Indian thought causality is typically "the origin of a thing", not a relation between events; the standard example is the potter making a pot from clay, the clay being "the cause par excellence". [source: IEP-S §4a]
- **Sāṃkhya *satkāryavāda*** ("existent effect theory"): the effect exists in its material cause before production, in latent, unmanifest form. Classical text: Īśvarakṛṣṇa's *Sāṅkhya-Kārikā* (ca. 350 CE, 72 verses). [sources: IEP-S §1, §4a; SEP-NI §2.4]
- Five considerations (IEP-S): (a) the non-existent cannot produce anything; (b) a specific effect needs a specific material cause (clay for pot, milk for curds); (c) otherwise anything would come from anything; (d) an agent produces only what it can (a potter cannot make jewellery); (e) the effect is essentially identical with its material cause (a pot is still clay). [source: IEP-S §4a] (The five reasons are SK 9 per the *Classical Indian Schools* notes; not re-verified here.)
- Chatterjee's fuller argument (SEP-NI §2.4): a cause produces an effect only by being related to it, but a relation needs two relata, so a non-existent effect cannot be related; cause and effect are identical (*tādātmya*); if unrelated causes could produce effects, "why does a pot not arise from threads?"; oil comes from oilseeds, not sand. Opponent: potency, not pre-existence, explains this. Sāṃkhya reply: where does the potency exist, and how can it be related to a non-existent effect? So "causal efficiency is nothing other than the existence of the effect in the material cause in a latent form." [source: SEP-NI §2.4]
- **Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika *asatkāryavāda* / *ārambhavāda*:** a cause is "an invariable, unconditional antecedent of an effect"; an effect is "the counter-correlative of a prior absence"; "Every effect is preceded by its prior absence, so each effect is a new production." Wholes are "new entities inhering in their own parts". [source: SEP-NI §2.1]
- Nyāya's three kinds of cause (cloth example): inherence cause (the threads), non-inherence cause (the contact/conjunction of the threads), instrumental/agential cause (weaver, loom); a "trigger cause" (striking a match). "Causally irrelevant" factors are excluded: the sculptor's hair colour invariably precedes her sculptures but is not a cause. [source: IEP-N §2h] Common causes include space, time, *adṛṣṭa*, God, prior absence. [source: SEP-NI §2.1]
- **Objection to satkāryavāda** (Ruzsa): "Understood literally, this is not tenable—if the cause existed, why was it not perceived prior to the point called its production?" Ruzsa's reading: sat-kārya means "the effect of existent [causes]", "a moderate form of determinism": "there is nothing absolutely new in the product". [source: IEP-S §4a] **Reply:** causal operation "enables a potent cause to manifest the latent effect". [source: SEP-NI §2.4]
- Sāṃkhya's stake: proving *prakṛti* as the ultimate material cause, and that evolution is genuine, "not merely illusory" (contra Vedānta). [source: SEP-NI §2.4]
- Comparison worth making: Sāṃkhya's "effect pre-exists in material cause" vs Aristotle's material/formal causes; Nyāya's "new whole" vs emergence. (Writer's gloss; no source draws it — present as the chapter's own comparison.)

## 6. Buddhist dependent origination and Nāgārjuna (cross-ref *Early Buddhism*, *Mahāyāna*)
- General principle of dependent arising (*pratītyasamutpāda*): "when this exists, that comes to be; with the arising of this, that arises. When this does not exist, that does not come to be; with the cessation of this, that ceases" (M.II.32; S.II.28) — IEP's rendering. [source: IEP-Bud §3]
- Nāgārjuna (ca. 150–250 CE): four ways things could be brought about: from themselves, from other things, from both, from neither. [source: SEP-Nag intro, §3.1]
- MMK 1.1, Dan Arnold's rendering in IEP: "There do not exist, anywhere at all, any existents whatsoever, arisen either from themselves or from something else, either from both or altogether without cause." The verse deploys the tetralemma (*catuṣkoṭi*). [source: IEP-Madh §5] (Translator: the IEP article is by Arnold and gives no other translator; matches Periergia's *Mahāyāna* chapter, which attributes it to Arnold.)
- Step by step (Westerhoff): premise to be refuted: cause and effect exist with *svabhāva* (as distinct, independent, mind-independent objects).
  1. Self-causation: identity fails (nothing is temporally prior to itself); containment fails — "amongst petrol, oxygen, a spark and so forth there is no explosion to be found."
  2. From other: substantially distinct things could not be dependent; effect depends existentially on cause, cause "at least notionally" on effect (nothing is called "cause" without an effect); with independent objects the labels "cause"/"effect" could be swapped.
  3. From both: a block of marble containing a statue as potentiality — fine as far as it goes, but then none of the items has its role "from their own side".
  4. From neither: denies causation; cannot explain knowledge (perception is causal) or the world's causal order.
  5. Conclusion: not that causation is impossible but that the shared premise (*svabhāva*) is false; cause and effect are mutually dependent (empty). [source: SEP-Nag §3.1]
- Note the parallel: option 1 resembles Sāṃkhya satkāryavāda, option 2 Nyāya asatkāryavāda (writer's gloss; Periergia's *Mahāyāna* chapter already gives the four horns, so recap briefly).

## 7. Chinese thought (brief, optional)
- Later Mohists: explanation (*shuō* 說) is giving reasons (*gù* 故) to support or explain a claim (NO11, B66); *gù* = "the grounds or criteria" for using expressions. [source: SEP-MC §5, §7]
- Wang Chong (27 – c. 97 CE), Eastern Han, *Lunheng*: argued that thunder must be caused by fire or heat, not a sign of heaven's displeasure, and that repeatable experience should be tried before adopting belief in divine will; mocked ghosts ("spirits should always appear naked"). [source: WP-Wang_Chong — weak; parts tagged "citation needed"/"which"]

## 8. Hume (recap; cross-ref *Berkeley and Hume*)
- Three components of our idea of cause: spatial contiguity, priority in time, and "most importantly, the necessary connection". Hume searches (Copy Principle) for the impression of necessary connection across single/multiple and external/internal cases; repetition "adds nothing new to the objects themselves" but produces an internal impression of reflection, a felt expectation, which the mind projects owing to its "great propensity … to spread itself on external objects" (T 1.3.14.25). [source: SEP-H §(metaphysics)]
- Billiard balls: Hume "challenges us to consider any one event and meditate on it; for instance, a billiard ball striking another", holding that only past experience lets us infer how the second ball will move. [source: IEP-HC §1] Sentence from EHU 4: "When I see, for instance, a Billiard-ball moving in a straight line towards another" … "Motion in the second billiard-ball is a quite distinct event from motion in the first; nor is there anything in the one to suggest the smallest hint of the other." [source: WS-5 — search snippets only]
- Two definitions, EHU 7.29 — exact wording in Quotations. The first identifies causation with constant conjunction (external impressions); the second with the mind's expectation (internal). They are "neither co-intensive … nor co-extensive"; Old Humeans prioritise the first; New Humeans deny either is a strict definition; Garrett (1997) argues they are co-extensive when construed correctly. [source: SEP-H]
- **The "in other words" problem:** Hume adds to the first definition "Or, in other words, where, if the first object had not been, the second never had existed." SEP-CF: "the first explicit definition of causation in terms of counterfactuals was, surprisingly enough, given by Hume"; "It is difficult to understand how Hume could have confused the first, regularity definition with the second, very different counterfactual definition (though see Buckle 2004: 212–13)"; Hume "never explored" the counterfactual approach. [source: SEP-CF §1]
- Old vs New Hume: Old Humeans (Winkler 1991; Millican 2009) — Hume denies objective necessary connection or its conceivability; New Humeans (Wright 1983; Strawson 1989; Kail 2007b) — such connections conceivable and possible though unknowable. [source: SEP-H] IEP-HC adds reductionist, sceptical (Armstrong 1983) and realist readings. [source: IEP-HC §§4–6]
- Broadbent's gloss: Hume turns "What is necessary connection?" into "How do we know about necessary connection?"; his own explanation makes necessary connection "nothing more than a feeling … but it is one that robs 'necessary' of anything resembling necessity." [source: IEP-C §2]
- Mary Shepherd, *Essay upon the Relation of Cause and Effect* (1824): everything that begins to exist must have a cause; similar causes have similar effects; cause and effect are synchronous (effects are newly formed objects arising from the union of objects). [source: SEP-MS §§2.1–2.3] (Periergia's *Berkeley and Hume* covers her; cross-ref.)
- Hume vs al-Ghazālī comparison: Periergia's *Ghazālī* chapter already says "On one point the likeness is real". Don't overstate influence (no source read shows Hume read al-Ghazālī; Autrecourt plausibly knew him).

## 9. Kant's reply (brief; cross-ref *Kant*)
- "I freely admit that it was the remembrance of David Hume which, many years ago, first interrupted my dogmatic slumber" (*Prolegomena*, 1783, 4:260). [source: SEP-KH §1]
- Second Analogy: "All alterations take place in accordance with the law of the connection of cause and effect" (B232) — a synthetic a priori principle. Majority 20th-century reading: Kant establishes only the general principle that every event has a cause; particular causal laws remain synthetic a posteriori, known by induction (so on that point Kant agrees with Hume). De Pierris and Friedman dispute that Kant thereby agrees with Hume about their status. [source: SEP-KH §1–2]

## 10. Regularity theories, step by step
- Humean Regularity Theory (HRT): c causes e iff (i) c is spatiotemporally contiguous to e, (ii) c precedes e, (iii) all events of type C are followed by an event of type E. "There is nothing more to causation according to HRT" — no necessary connection, production or powers. [source: SEP-Reg §1.1]
- Three problems: (1) sorting events into types; (2) singular causation (the dinosaur-killing meteor, a one-off); (3) regularity without causation: the cock's crow precedes sunrise, and joint effects of a common cause. [source: SEP-Reg §1.1]
- Mill (1843) refinement: the cause is the totality of present positive and absent negative factors sufficient for the effect; and the regularity must be a law, not an accidental regularity (cock and sunrise). [source: SEP-Reg §1.2]
- One effect, many sufficient clusters (noted by Venn 1889). [source: SEP-Reg §1.3]
- **Mackie's INUS (1965 "Causes and Conditions", *American Philosophical Quarterly* 2: 245–64; *The Cement of the Universe*, 1974)**. Worked example, the burnt house: one cluster = short circuit + oxygen + no sprinkler…; another = arsonist + petrol + oxygen…; another = burning tree falling on the house. Each cluster is sufficient, none necessary; each factor in a cluster is non-redundant. So the short circuit is an **I**nsufficient but **N**on-redundant part of an **U**nnecessary but **S**ufficient condition. A cause is "at least" an INUS condition, relative to a causal field ("a background against which the causing goes on", 1974: 63). [source: SEP-Reg §1.3; Mackie 1965: 245, 1974: 37–38 cited there]
- IEP's version of the acronym: "an insufficient but necessary part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition", illustrated with an assassin's shot. [source: IEP-C §3a] (Both "non-redundant" and "necessary part" are standard; SEP-Reg uses "non-redundant".)
- J. L. Mackie: born 25 Aug 1917, Killara, Sydney; died 12 Dec 1981, Oxford. [source: WP-J._L._Mackie]
- **Objection (common cause): Manchester hooters** (adapted from Russell 1921): Manchester factory hooters at five o'clock are regularly followed by London workers going home; on Mackie's theory the Manchester hooters come out as an INUS condition of the Londoners stopping work (Mackie 1974: 81–84 admits this). **Reply (Mackie):** the theory is incomplete; add causal priority — roughly, c is prior if an agent could prevent e by preventing c (1974: 190); preventing the Manchester hooters doesn't stop Londoners going home; his final notion: c was fixed at a time when e was unfixed. [source: SEP-Reg §1.3]
- **Barometer and storm** (Jeffrey 1969): a pressure drop causes both the barometer's fall and the storm; a simple regularity theory would say the mercury drop causes the storm. [source: SEP-Prob §1.1] IEP's reply on Mackie's behalf: the barometer's fall is not an INUS condition since "Storms happen even when no barometer is there". [source: IEP-C §3a.ii]
- **Overdetermination/pre-emption for INUS**: two assassins — both shots are INUS conditions, but only one is the cause. [source: IEP-C §3a.iii]
- Hempel and Russell link: Hempel (1965) suggested replacing "cause" with laws of succession and coexistence. [source: SEP-Reg §3]

## 11. Counterfactual theory (Lewis 1973), step by step
- David Lewis (28 Sep 1941 – 14 Oct 2001). "Causation", *Journal of Philosophy* 70 (1973): 556–67. [sources: WP-David_Lewis_(philosopher); SEP-CF and SEP-Reg bibliographies]
- Lewis: "We think of a cause as something that makes a difference, and the difference it makes must be a difference from what would have happened without it. Had it been absent, its effects – some of them, at least, and usually all – would have been absent as well" (1973b, 161, reprint pagination). [source: SEP-CF §1]
- Possible-worlds semantics: "If A were the case, C would be the case" is true iff some A-world where C holds is closer to actuality than any A-world where C fails. [source: SEP-CF §1.1]
- Causal dependence: for distinct actual events c and e, e depends on c iff, if c were not to occur, e would not occur. Distinctness rules out "Larry"/"rr". Backtracking counterfactuals are excluded (so joint effects of a common cause don't depend on each other). [source: SEP-CF §1.1]
- Causation = the **ancestral** of dependence: c causes e iff there is a chain c, d, … e of stepwise dependence. This makes causation transitive. [source: SEP-CF §1.3]
- Asymmetry: Lewis (1979) explains temporal direction by the "asymmetry of overdetermination" (Gretta cracks an egg at 8.00, Elga 2000). [source: SEP-CF §1.2]
- **Early pre-emption** (two assassins; B desists on seeing A fire): no dependence of the death on A's aiming (B would have fired), but a chain via A's bullet in mid-flight; so the ancestral handles it. [source: SEP-CF §1.3]
- **Late pre-emption (Hall 2004): Suzy and Billy**: "Billy and Suzy throw rocks at a bottle. Suzy throws first so that her rock arrives first and shatters the glass; Billy's rock sails through the air where the bottle had stood moments earlier." No dependence and no chain (the shattering doesn't depend on Suzy's rock in mid-flight either). Responses: fragile events (rejected by Lewis: eating dinner before poison would cause death); quasi-dependence (1986c); influence (2000/2004: alterations of Suzy's throw alter the shattering, alterations of Billy's don't). [source: SEP-CF §§2.3, 3]
  - NB Gallow's SEP entry uses a *different* Suzy/Billy story (Billy throws; Suzy, aggrieved, stays home as backup) to illustrate early pre-emption. Specify which version. [source: SEP-Gal §1.2]
- **Trumping** (Schaffer 2000a): major and sergeant simultaneously shout "Advance!"; soldiers obey the major. Quasi-dependence fails; influence handles it (vary the major's command and the response varies). [source: SEP-CF §3]
- Influence objections: Billy's throw has some influence; spurious causes (Bennett: December rain delays a forest fire). [source: SEP-CF §3]
- **Symmetric overdetermination**: firing squad — "We would probably want to say that it was the whole firing squad that was the cause"; Lewis said intuitions are hazy and set such cases aside. [sources: IEP-C §3b; SEP-CF §1.3]
- **Transitivity counterexamples**: Hall's boulder and hiker (double prevention; Hall's "short circuit"); Ehring's (1987) potassium salts/purple flame. [sources: SEP-CF §2.2; SEP-Gal §1.2.x]
- Pre-emption has proved "the biggest bugbear for Lewis's theory" (Paul and Hall 2013, ch. 3). [source: SEP-CF §2.3]
- Prevention and absences: Lewis later allowed causation by absence (gardener fails to water plants). [source: SEP-CF §1.1]

## 12. Probabilistic causation (cross-ref *Induction, Probability…*)
- Motivations: imperfect regularities (smoking causes lung cancer though not all smokers get it), indeterminism, irrelevance (hexed salt dissolves, Kyburg 1965), asymmetry, spurious regularities. Core idea: P(E|C) > P(E|~C). [source: SEP-Prob §1]
- Probability-raising is symmetric, and fails for common causes (barometer). [source: SEP-Prob §2.1]
- Reichenbach, *The Direction of Time* (posthumous, 1956): "screening off"; C screens A off from E if P(E|A&C) = P(E|C); first fully developed probabilistic theory (ideas from 1925). Atmospheric pressure screens the barometer off from the storm. [source: SEP-Prob §2.2]
- Suppes, *A Probabilistic Theory of Causality* (North-Holland, 1970): independently gave an equivalent definition. [source: SEP-Prob §2.2 and bibliography]
- Common Cause Principle (Reichenbach 1956): if A and B are correlated and neither causes the other, there is a common cause C screening them off (a "conjunctive fork"). [source: SEP-Prob §2.3]
- **Simpson's paradox**: SEP example — smoking correlated with country living; city pollution a stronger cause; smokers overall less likely to get cancer, yet within country and within city smoking raises it. Problem pointed out by Cartwright (1979, "Causal Laws and Effective Strategies", *Noûs*) and Skyrms (1980). [source: SEP-Prob §2.4]
- Real-data example: kidney-stone study (Charig et al., *BMJ*, 29 March 1986): Treatment A better for small stones (93% vs 87%) and large (73% vs 69%) but worse overall (78% vs 83%), because large-stone cases mostly got A. Edward H. Simpson described the phenomenon in 1951 (Yule 1903 and Pearson 1899 earlier). Also UC Berkeley admissions, fall 1973. [source: WP-Simpson's_paradox]
- Chance-lowering causes (birth-control pills). [source: IEP-C §3d]

## 13. Interventionist / manipulability theories, with a worked example
- Idea: "if C is genuinely a cause of E, then if I can manipulate C in the right way, this should be a way of manipulating or changing E." Philosophers: Collingwood (1940), Gasking (1955), von Wright (1971), Menzies and Price (1993), Woodward (2003). Cook & Campbell (1979): "manipulation of a cause will result in the manipulation of an effect". [source: SEP-Man §1]
- Standard objections: circularity and anthropocentrism (Hausman). Menzies and Price's agency theory tried to escape circularity through direct experience of "doing one thing and thence achieving another" (1993: 194–5). [source: SEP-Man §§1–2]
- Pearl: an intervention "lift[s] X_i from the influence of the old functional mechanism … and plac[es] it under the influence of a new mechanism that sets the value x_i while keeping all other mechanisms undisturbed" (Pearl 2009: 70); graphically it "breaks" arrows into X. do-operator: observing X = x vs setting do(X = x). [sources: SEP-Man §5; SEP-CM §4.7]
- **Worked example (barometer):** Z = pressure causes X = barometer reading and Y = storm. P(Y | X = low) ≠ P(Y), but P(Y | do(X = low)) = P(Y): intervening on the barometer breaks the Z→X arrow, so the correlation vanishes. If the correlation had persisted under intervention, X would be a cause of Y. [source: SEP-Man §§2, 5]
- Woodward/Hitchcock conditions on an intervention I on X with respect to Y (M1–M4): I is the only cause of X; I does not cause Y except through X (the **placebo** case: administering a drug may aid recovery by placebo even if the drug does nothing); I is not caused by anything that affects Y by another route; I leaves other causes of Y unchanged. [source: SEP-Man §§3, 5]
- Direct cause: X is a direct cause of Y iff, with all other variables fixed by interventions, some intervention on X changes Y. [source: SEP-Man §6]
- Non-reductive: interventions are themselves causal notions, so no reduction; Woodward argues this is not vicious, since characterising an intervention on X needs no information about the X–Y relation — which is how experiments teach us causes. [source: SEP-Man §6]
- Unmanipulable causes (race, gender, species) — some epidemiologists (Hernán and Taubman 2008) say such causal claims are defective; contrast the moon and the tides (manipulation conceptually clear, physically impossible). [source: SEP-Man §10]
- IEP's caution: interventionism "does not tell us precisely what causation is"; perhaps it offers "a language for talking about causation more precisely". [source: IEP-C §3c]
- Causal models: Spirtes, Glymour and Scheines (SGS), *Causation, Prediction and Search* (2nd ed. MIT 2000) and Pearl 2009 "particularly influential"; Causal Markov Condition; Faithfulness; do-calculus (Pearl 2009 ch. 3); back-door criterion. Roots in Sewall Wright (1921). [source: SEP-CM §§1, 4]
- Pearl: born 4 Sep 1936; Turing Award 2011. *Causality: Models, Reasoning, and Inference* (CUP, 2000; 2nd ed. 2009); 2001 Lakatos Award. [sources: WP-Judea_Pearl; WP-Causality_(book); SEP-CM bibliography]
- Woodward, *Making Things Happen: A Theory of Causal Explanation* (OUP, 2003). [source: SEP-Man bibliography]

## 14. Process theories
- Salmon, *Scientific Explanation and the Causal Structure of the World* (1984): causality belongs to continuous processes; mark criterion (from Reichenbach): a causal process can transmit a local modification ("mark"); pseudo-processes (a spot of light sweeping a wall, shadows) can exceed light speed without violating relativity. [source: SEP-Proc §§2–3]
- Conserved quantity theory: first detailed version Dowe (1992), anticipated by Skyrms (1980), Aronson (1971), Fair (1979). Dowe (1995): "CQ1. A causal interaction is an intersection of world lines which involves exchange of a conserved quantity. CQ2. A causal process is a world line of an object which possesses a conserved quantity." Salmon's version (1997) requires transmission of a conserved quantity. Dowe, *Physical Causation* (CUP, 2000). [source: SEP-Proc §5]
- Common-cause effects exchange nothing; pre-empted processes don't reach the effect. Objections: prevention (James Bond shoots down a missile heading for Blofeld's compound — no process links Bond to Blofeld's survival) and double prevention; absences as causes. Some (Aronson, Dowe, Beebee, Hall) deny prevention is genuine causation. [sources: SEP-Gal §2; IEP-C §5d]
- Wesley Salmon (1925–2001). [source: SEP-Sal]

## 15. Causation in science
- Russell, "On the Notion of Cause", *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 13 (1912–13): 1–26; delivered as presidential address on 4 November 1912 [date: WS-4]. Quotation in Quotations. [sources: SEP-Proc §1; SEP-Gal §1; SEP-Phys bibliography]
- Russell's targets: the "law of universal determinism" and causation as a relation between events, replaced in science by functional laws; "same cause, same effect" is trivial or false (a fully specified cause will never recur). [sources: SEP-Proc §1; SEP-Phys §2] In "advanced sciences such as gravitational astronomy, the word 'cause' never occurs" [WS-4 paraphrase].
- Later Russell (*Human Knowledge*, 1948) gave a positive theory of "causal lines". [source: SEP-Proc §1]
- Neo-Russellians: Huw Price, Hartry Field (2003), John Earman (2011), John Norton (2003). Five challenges (vagueness, etc.). Opposing "causal imperialists" hold scientific explanations are fundamentally causal. Cartwright (1979) argued causal laws are needed to distinguish effective strategies. [sources: SEP-Phys §§2, 6; SEP-Prob bibliography]
- Pearl/Mackenzie, *The Book of Why* (Basic Books, 2018). [source: SEP-CM bibliography]
- RCTs: first published medical RCT = MRC streptomycin trial for pulmonary tuberculosis, 1948; randomisation balances "both known and unknown prognostic factors"; precursors: van Helmont's proposal (1648), Lind's scurvy trial (1747, not randomised), Peirce & Jastrow (1880s), Neyman and Fisher (agriculture). [source: WP-Randomized_controlled_trial]
- Why randomisation = intervention: the experimenter sets the treatment "after consulting the output of some randomizing device", independent of confounders. [source: SEP-Man §2]
- Correlation and post hoc: cross-ref *Informal Fallacies* (post hoc is about inferring "hastily"; correlations are often good evidence).
- **Bradford Hill (1965)**, "The Environment and Disease: Association or Causation?", *Proc. R. Soc. Med.* 58: 295–300: nine "viewpoints" — strength, consistency, specificity, temporality, biological gradient, plausibility, coherence, experiment, analogy. Hill: "nine different viewpoints from all of which we should study association before we cry causation"; "none of my nine viewpoints can bring indisputable evidence for or against the cause-and-effect hypothesis and none can be required as a *sine qua non*." Often (WP says "incorrectly") called "criteria". [source: WP-Bradford_Hill_criteria]

[sources: IEP-C §3b; SEP-Gal §2; WS-2] Pages: SEP-CF says 225–76; SEP-Gal says 181–204 — discrepancy; omit page numbers.

- Other pluralists: Anscombe (1971 "Causality and Determination"), Cartwright; "thickism": basic causal concepts are kicks, pushes, etc. (Anscombe; Cartwright 1983). [source: IEP-C §§1, 7b]
- Mental causation: cross-ref *The Mind–Body Problem* (SEP-Gal bibliography lists Davidson "Mental Events" 1970 and Heil & Mele *Mental Causation*).

## Debates
- Aitia: "cause" or "explanation"/"because"? Falcon: think of causes as a type of explanation, but with a metaphysical component (Stein: causes explain "derivatively"). [source: SEP-AC §2]
- Was al-Ghazālī an occasionalist? Marmura (yes) vs Griffel, Perler & Rudolph (leaves secondary causality open). [source: SEP-Occ §2]
- Does Averroes answer al-Ghazālī? Richardson: the deeper dispute is about modality (conceivability vs Aristotelian necessity); McGinnis 2006: Averroes' natural-science reply may miss the target. [source: SEP-AIC §§3.3.3, 4.3]
- Old vs New Hume; whether Hume's two definitions are equivalent (Garrett 1997 yes; most say no). [source: SEP-H; IEP-HC]
- Does Kant disagree with Hume about particular causal laws? Majority reading: no; De Pierris & Friedman: yes, in a qualified way. [source: SEP-KH]
- Satkāryavāda literal vs Ruzsa's "moderate determinism" reading. [source: IEP-S §4a]
- Pre-emption and the counterfactual theory: ancestral, quasi-dependence, influence, fragility; Northcott (2021) argues pre-emption doesn't undermine the identification after all. [source: SEP-CF §§1.3, 2.3, 3]
- Is causation transitive? (Hall's boulder; Ehring; Paul's aspect causation.) [source: SEP-CF §2.2]
- Reductive vs non-reductive: interventionism admittedly circular — vicious or not? [source: SEP-Man §6]
- Is there causation in fundamental physics? Russell/neo-Russellians vs causal imperialists. [source: SEP-Phys §§2, 6]
- Is prevention/absence causation? Process theorists (Dowe, Beebee, Hall) say no; counterfactualists yes. [source: SEP-Gal §2]
