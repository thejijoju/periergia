## 1. Arrival of Buddhism in China (Han dynasty)
- Buddhism "first entered China in the latter part of the Han dynasty (206 BCE to 220 CE) along with growing trans-Eurasian trade"; the first Chinese term for Buddhism was "teaching of the images" (*xiangjiao*). [source: SEP-CH §2] SEP-HY: "brought to China by missionaries some time during the first century CE". [source: SEP-HY §1.2] **Write "by the first or second century CE, during the Han".**
- Legend (label as legend): Emperor Ming of Han (r. 58–75 CE) dreamed of a golden figure and sent envoys west; two Indian monks (Kāśyapa Mātaṅga and Dharmaratna) came to Luoyang on white horses, hence the White Horse Temple, and translated the *Sutra in Forty-two Sections*. WP calls the embassy "semi-legendary". [source: WP-White_Horse_Temple; WP-Silk_Road_transmission_of_Buddhism]
- First documented translator: An Shigao, a Parthian ("An" = Anxi, Parthia; tradition makes him a prince), fl. c. 148–180 CE in Luoyang, translating non-Mahāyāna texts on doctrine, meditation and abhidharma. Lokakṣema (a Kushan) first translated Mahāyāna scriptures (c. 167–186). [source: WP-An_Shigao; WP-Silk_Road_transmission] (The Mahāyāna Philosophy notes already record Lokakṣema's *Aṣṭasāhasrikā* c. 179.)
- Scale: by the early fourth century multi-week retreats of up to 5,000 people; by the sixth century "over two million people—roughly one out of every twenty-five people in China—were ordained as monks and nuns" (Hershock). [source: SEP-CH §2]
- Chinese Buddhism is eclectic because Indian texts arrived "piecemeal", and early Chinese adherents "did not perceive contributions from different Indian traditions as fundamentally inconsistent"; this produced *panjiao*, "doctrinal classification", which ranks systems as partial truths by how "rounded" (*yuan*) they are; Van Norden & Jones liken it to inference to the best explanation. [source: SEP-HY §1.2]
- The period c. 200–850 "marks the height of the influence of Buddhism on Chinese culture" (Berthrong); Buddhism introduced monastic communities "for both men and women". [source: IEP-NC §2c]
- Song emperor Xiaozong (r. 1162–89) compared the Three Teachings (Confucianism, Daoism, Buddhism) to the three legs of a bronze *ding* vessel. [source: SEP-CH §2]

## 2. Translation and "matching concepts" (geyi) — a contested story
- **Common textbook story:** early translators used *geyi*, "matching concepts/meanings", rendering Buddhist terms with Daoist vocabulary (e.g. *śūnyatā* by *wu* 無, "non-being"), until Kumārajīva's school overcame it. WP-Kumārajīva itself repeats this ("overcoming the previous *geyi* (concept-matching) system"). [source: WP-Kumārajīva; WP-Geyi]
- **Victor H. Mair's challenge** ("What Is *Geyi*, After All?", in Alan K. L. Chan & Yuet-Keung Lo (eds), *Philosophy and Religion in Early Medieval China*, SUNY 2010, pp. 227–264): no historical evidence supports the translation hypothesis; *ge* 格 never means "matching"; *geyi* occurs only ~23 times in the Buddhist canon and nowhere in the Daoist canon; it was "a highly ephemeral and not-very-successful attempt on the part of a small number of Chinese teachers to cope with the flood of numbered lists of categories" (Mair 2010: 228), i.e. explaining Buddhist numbered lists (five aggregates etc.) with comparable lists from Chinese classics. Mair calls the textbook version "pseudo-*geyi*", which "has spawned an entire industry of fake philosophizing". [source: WP-Geyi, quoting Mair]
- Earliest mention: the biography of Zhu Faya (fl. late 3rd–early 4th c.) in Huijiao's (497–554) *Memoirs of Eminent Monks* (*Gaoseng zhuan*); Dao'an (312–385) was the first recorded critic of the method; Sengrui (352–436) criticised it too; per Mair it lasted "no more than a generation". [source: WP-Geyi]
- **Writer:** present *geyi*-as-Daoist-translationese as a widespread claim that Mair has strongly contested; the broader point (Buddhist ideas were first understood through Daoist/xuanxue vocabulary) can still be made without hanging it on *geyi*. Berthrong: the post-Han *xuanxue* ("arcane/profound learning") Daoist revival "was ultimately eclipsed by the arrival of Buddhism". [source: IEP-NC §2c]

## 3. Kumārajīva and Sengzhao
- Kumārajīva: from Kucha (Tarim Basin, now Xinjiang); father an Indian monk; Mahāyānist who studied Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka. Captured when Fu Jian's general Lü Guang took Kucha; Lü set up his own state (386) and held him for years, during which he learned Chinese and was coerced into marrying the Kucha king's daughter. Later Qin ruler Yao Xing sent armies to bring him to Chang'an, where he settled c. 401 and headed a multi-ethnic translation team (amanuensis Sengrui). Translations include the *Diamond Sūtra* and *Lotus Sūtra*. [source: WP-Kumārajīva; SEP-CH §2]
- **Dates disagree:** 344–413 (WP; IEP-SZ "c. 344–413"); 334–413 (SEP-CH); 350–409 (IEP-Madhyamaka, per Mahāyāna notes). **Use "344–413 (some sources differ)".**
- Style: clarity and flowing readability, prioritising meaning over literal rendering (Nattier); his versions often stayed more popular than Xuanzang's more literal ones; Nattier rejects the view that he simply abbreviated. [source: WP-Kumārajīva]
- Translations of the *Middle Treatise* (MMK, *Zhong lun*), *Twelve Gate Treatise* and Āryadeva's *Hundred Treatise* became the basis of the Sanlun ("Three Treatise") school; but per Dippmann "no Mādhyamika 'school' is likely to have existed in China until Jizang (549–623 C.E.) projected such a lineage back to the time of Sengzhao". [source: IEP-SZ intro, §3b]
- Sengzhao: IEP gives "c. 378–413"; *Gaoseng zhuan* says he died in Chang'an in the 10th year of Yixi (c. 413–414) aged 31. A poor copyist who read Laozi and Zhuangzi, then converted on reading the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra*; followed Kumārajīva to Chang'an; Kumārajīva reportedly said, "My explanations are on par with yours, but your wording and expression is far better!" [source: IEP-SZ §1a] (Other common dating 384–414 not checked; give "early fifth century, died young".)
- *Zhaolun* (Treatises of Zhao), approximate composition order: *Prajñā Is Without Dichotomizing Knowledge* (c. 405), *Non-Absolute Emptiness* (c. 409), correspondence with Liu Yimin (c. 410), *Things Do Not Shift* (c. 410–411), *Nirvāṇa Is Without Conceptualization* (c. 412–413). Uses the tetralemma and Zhuangzi-like paradox; later Chan treated him as an unofficial patriarch; the *Baozang lun* attributed to him is regarded as spurious. [source: IEP-SZ §2]
- *Things Do Not Shift*: motion presupposes separation and distinction between things that does not ultimately obtain; from the standpoint of absolute truth, motion and rest are one. [source: IEP-SZ §4b]

## 4. Xuanzang and the Weishi (Yogācāra / Faxiang) school (brief)
- Xuanzang (602–664): became a monk at 13; journey to India 629–645 in defiance of a travel ban; studied at Nālandā under Śīlabhadra; returned to Chang'an in 645 with 657 texts (Watters's count) of which he translated only a portion (75 works, 1,335 fascicles); wrote the *Great Tang Records on the Western Regions*; inspired the Ming novel *Journey to the West*. Emperor Gaozong cancelled audiences for three days on his death. [source: IEP-XZ; WP-Xuanzang]
- Hershock: Xuanzang went partly to settle whether all beings have Buddha-nature and returned with "confirmation of the heterodox nature" of that idea, which "ironically seems to have buoyed the confidence" of Chinese Buddha-nature advocates. [source: SEP-CH §2]
- Faxiang/Weishi ("Consciousness-only") school initiated by Xuanzang and his disciple Kuiji (632–682); its central text, the *Cheng weishi lun* (*Vijñaptimātratāsiddhi*), is a compilation drawing on ten commentaries on Vasubandhu's *Thirty Verses*, chiefly Dharmapāla's (IEP-XZ gives 659 in one place and 648 in another — **internal inconsistency; say "650s"**). The school held "that not all beings possess pure seeds and, therefore, not all of them are capable of attaining Buddhahood". [source: IEP-XZ §4; WP-East_Asian_Yogācāra]
- Earlier "old translation" Yogācāra schools (Dilun, Shelun; Paramārtha) blended Yogācāra with Buddha-nature thought and were eclipsed by Xuanzang's school. [source: WP-East_Asian_Yogācāra] (Cross-ref: Mahāyāna Philosophy for Indian Yogācāra.)

## 5. Tiantai: Zhiyi and the three truths
- Tiantai = a mountain in Zhejiang, "platform of the sky". Representative figures: Tiantai Zhiyi (538–597), Jingxi Zhanran (711–782), Siming Zhili (960–1028). [source: SEP-TT intro]
- Zhiyi studied under Huisi (560–567); moved to Mount Tiantai in 575 (during Emperor Wu of Northern Zhou's persecution in the north); took the *Lotus Sūtra* as his main basis; his three great works: *Great Cessation-Contemplation* (*Mohe zhiguan*), *Profound Meaning of the Lotus Sūtra*, *Words and Phrases of the Lotus Sūtra*; tradition passed to Japan (Tendai) and Korea (Cheontae). [source: WP-Zhiyi]
- The Threefold Truth (emptiness *kong*, provisional positing *jia*, the centre/middle *zhong*) derives from Zhiyi's reading of MMK 24.18 (which WP renders "whatever is dependent arising, that is emptiness. That is dependent upon convention. That itself is the middle path"). [source: WP-Zhiyi; SEP-TT §2.3]
- Ziporyn's characterisation: thoroughgoing contextualism, holism, monism, immanence; any entity "intrinsically entails its self-overcoming", including what it seemed to exclude; "Good and evil, delusion and enlightenment, Buddhahood and deviltry, are all 'inherently entailed' in each and every event"; a canonical early Tiantai line: "The absolute totality encounters the absolute totality, and the result is the arising of the absolute totality" (法界對法界起法界). [source: SEP-TT intro, §1]
- "One moment of experience as three thousand worlds" (*yinian sanqian*): ten realms × ten realms × ten suchnesses × three worlds = 3,000, from the *Mohe zhiguan*; Zhiyi says any number would serve. [source: SEP-TT §5.2]
- Later doctrines: inherent evil is ineradicable even in Buddhahood; insentient beings have Buddha-nature (Zhanran). [source: SEP-TT §4.1 heading and text]

**ARGUMENT (Ziporyn's reconstruction of the three truths), SEP-TT §2.1, condensed:**
1. For anything to be what it is (X), it must be mutually exclusive with what it is not (determinacy).
2. To be determinate is to be finite and conditioned; a wholly unconditioned thing would be everywhere and always, hence indistinguishable — not determinate at all.
3. So every entity must be co-present with some otherness (things it excludes); all relation, causation and contrast are forms of this co-presence.
4. But any relation between two entities requires some overlap or common medium that includes both.
5. Genuinely distinct (mutually exclusive) entities, insofar as they are distinct, have nothing in common.
6. So genuinely mutually exclusive entities could not condition or relate to one another; hence there are none, and premise 1 must be revised.
7. Revision = the three truths: X is "non-different" from non-X (Emptiness); X is also "non-same" with non-X, or there would be no X to speak of (Provisional Positing); and these two are themselves neither the same nor different — two opposed senses of one referent (the Centre).
- Ziporyn's summary formulas: "Provisional Positing: Some X is always appearing. Emptiness: X is not (only) X. The Center: The simultaneity, inseparability, sameness-as-difference of X appearing and X not being only X." The "non-exclusive Centre" implies any two things are "intersubsumptive". [source: SEP-TT §2.1]
- Objection (Ziporyn's own framing): the conclusions "will initially strike many if not most readers as wildly outlandish and counterintuitive"; Tiantai's reply is that they are "inescapable entailments of everyday experience" — things appearing one way and turning out another. [source: SEP-TT §2] (No SEP-stated named critic; writer should present the objection generically, e.g. that step 5 equivocates on "distinct".) **The "equivocation" objection is the writer's/our suggestion, not in a source; label it as such or omit.**

## 6. Huayan: Dushun, Zhiyan, Fazang; the golden lion and Indra's net
- Huayan ("Flower Garland") takes its name from the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra* (*Huayan jing*); its other key text is the *Awakening of Faith in Mahāyāna* (One Mind with two aspects, suchness and saṃsāra; tathāgatagarbha, "Buddha-womb"). Known in Korea as Hwaeom, in Japan as Kegon. [source: SEP-HY intro, §1.2]
- Context: Emperor Wu of Northern Zhou's persecutions of 574 and 577 (after Wei Yuansong's 567 memorial) pushed monks to seek forms of Buddhism suited to China. [source: SEP-HY §1.2]
- Patriarchs: Dushun (557–640), first; Zhiyan (602–668), second; Fazang (643–712), third; Korean monk Ŭisang (625–702) took Huayan to Korea. Dushun's *Discernments of the Dharmadhātu* first used *li* (principle/pattern) for ultimate reality and *shi* (things/events) for phenomena, with "mutual non-obstruction between *li* and *shi*"; metaphor of water (li) and waves (shi), from the *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra*. [source: SEP-HY §§2–4]
- Fazang: ancestors from Sogdiana, born in Chang'an (SEP-HY) — IEP-FZ calls him "a native of Sogdiana" (**discrepancy; say "of Sogdian descent"**); at 16 burned a finger (IEP: "one of his fingers") as an offering; assisted Xuanzang in translation but differed with him doctrinally; disciple of Zhiyan c. 663; favoured by Empress Wu Zetian (r. 690–705), for whom he lectured on the *Avataṃsaka*, performed rain rituals and in 697 a ritual against the Khitans; helped prepare an 80-fascicle translation of the sūtra (695–699); supported the 705 coup that removed her. More than sixty works. [source: SEP-HY §4; IEP-FZ §§1, 3]
- Political reading: Stanley Weinstein (via IEP) — Huayan's ordered universe presided over by Vairocana was "analogous to the highly centralized imperial state that she ruled". Rothschild calls Fazang "a propagandist". [source: IEP-FZ §§2b, 3] (A debate-worthy point: metaphysics and imperial ideology.)
- Fazang's five-tier *panjiao*: Hīnayāna; Initial Mahāyāna; Final Mahāyāna; Sudden Teaching; Comprehensive ("round") Teaching of the One Vehicle = the *Avataṃsaka*. [source: IEP-FZ §2a] (Writer: "Hīnayāna" here is Fazang's polemical label, cf. Mahāyāna notes.)
- **Golden lion** (*Essay on the Golden Lion*, *Jin shizi zhang*, T 1881; one chapter; tradition says first demonstrated to Wu Zetian with a palace-gate lion around 700, "sources differ"): the gold = the underlying pattern/emptiness; the lion-shape = our perception of independent individuals; "There is really only gold; there is no lion. … The gold really does appear to be a lion" (Van Norden's gloss). [source: IEP-FZ §2d; WP-Fazang quoting Van Norden; SEP-HY §1.3]
- **Indra's net**: in pre-Buddhist myth, a net with a jewel at every intersection, each reflecting all others; metaphor for each thing's dependence for existence and identity on everything else. Fazang's hall of ten mirrors (eight in an octagon, one above, one below) around a Buddha image lit by a torch. [source: SEP-HY §1.3; IEP-FZ §2c]
- Fazang's eight theses (*Treatise on the Five Teachings*, ch. 10): each dharma includes, is included in, determines and is determined by all others and the totality; resting on two posits — every dharma is both existent and empty, and both powerful and powerless. [source: SEP-HY §4.3]
- Six characteristics (Rafter Dialogue): wholeness, particularity, identity, difference, integration, disintegration. [source: SEP-HY §4.3; WP-Fazang]
- Ethics: no individual self but a transpersonal One Mind; the root vice is selfishness; failing to feel compassion "is like ignoring a wound to my own limb because it is temporarily numb". [source: SEP-HY §1.3]

**ARGUMENT (the interpenetration argument, Fazang's Rafter Dialogue, as reconstructed by SEP-HY §1.3 and WP-Fazang):**
1. Whatever is conditioned depends for its existence and identity on something else (inherited from Madhyamaka).
2. A building is nothing more than its parts in their arrangement; remove one rafter and *this* building is not there. So each part is essential to the building's identity: "the building is the rafter".
3. What makes a piece of wood *a rafter* is its role in a building; outside the building "its identity could change to being a bench, or a teeter-totter, or simply kindling". So "the rafter is the building".
4. Generalise to the whole cosmos (the dharmadhātu): every phenomenon depends for its identity on every other and on the whole ("one is all"), and the whole on each ("all is one").
5. Yet each keeps a distinct function; "each part is identical … and they are identical because they are different" (Fazang as quoted in WP-Fazang; translator not named, probably Elstein 2014).
- **Objection (common-sense/mereological)**: a heap of rafters, planks and tiles after a storm is not a building, so a part's mere presence does not make the whole; and a plank is still the same plank when the building falls (so step 3 trades on a relational redescription). [source: WS-7, summarising a PhilArchive paper quoting Cook 1977 — second-hand]
- **Reply (Fazang's line, second-hand)**: a rafter "wholly creates" the building only *as a rafter*, i.e. together with the other conditions; a plank lying in a heap is not functioning as a rafter at all; and if any part only partly created the building, the whole would never come to be. [source: WS-7; SEP-HY §4.3 ("integration … disintegration")] **Exact wording of the dialogue not seen; paraphrase only.**
- Van Norden & Jones also present *panjiao* as inference to the best explanation (§1 above) and note Fazang's explanations are "notoriously dense". [source: SEP-HY §4.3]

## 7. Chan: Bodhidharma, Huineng, the Platform Sutra, sudden vs gradual
- "Chan" transliterates Sanskrit *dhyāna*, "meditation"; Japanese Zen, Korean Sŏn, Vietnamese Thiền. Chan coalesced c. 500–1000 CE. [source: SEP-CH intro, §2; IEP-HN §1]
- Traditional origin story: the Buddha held up a flower and Mahākāśyapa smiled; Bodhidharma, 28th Indian lineage holder, came to China; Huike (487–593) second patriarch. "Contemporary scholars now regard this account and much of what Chan eventually came to regard as the particulars of its own history as inventions of tradition (see, e.g., McRae 2003 and Faure 1991)". [source: SEP-CH §2] IEP-HN gives Bodhidharma "c. 470–553" and notes "a mysterious figure named Bodhidharma was indeed in southern China in the fifth century". [source: IEP-HN §1]
- Song-dynasty four-phrase self-description: "a special transmission outside the scriptures; not established upon words and letters; directly pointing to the human heartmind; seeing nature and becoming a Buddha" (Hershock's translation). [source: SEP-CH §1]
- Earliest strata attributed to Bodhidharma: two entries, by *li* (principle: all beings share one true nature) and by practice (*xing*). [source: SEP-CH §2]
- Hongren (600–674), Fifth Patriarch, at Dongshan ("East Mountain"). [source: IEP-HN §1]
- **Huineng (638–713)**: most scholars consider his life story "little more than pious fiction" with perhaps a kernel of truth; earliest mention an inscription dated 676 (lost); the only record from his lifetime lists him as Hongren's student. Tradition: illiterate woodcutter from the south; Hongren called him a "barbarian"; his reply: "Although my 'barbarian' body and yours differ, what difference is there in our buddha-nature?"; verse contest with head monk Shenxiu; secret transmission of the robe; died 713; title "Dhyāna Master Dajian" (816); a mummy said to be his is at Nanhua monastery, Caoxi. [source: IEP-HN §2]
- The *Platform Sutra* is "the only Chinese Buddhist discourse to be accorded sutra … status" (IEP-HN); Hershock: this "effectively proclaimed that Huineng was China's first 'homegrown' Buddha". [source: IEP-HN §2; SEP-CH §2]
- **Composition debate**: earliest copies found at Dunhuang (Mogao caves), dated c. 830–860 (WP) / "around 850" (IEP), both thought based on an edition of c. 780; attributed to the disciple Fahai, who appears nowhere else in Chan literature; some scholars think it was written c. 780 by a monk of another school, possibly the Niutou ("Oxhead") school; Schlütter: the Dunhuang text "is almost certainly a product of a long evolution with elements coming together from several different Chan groups with different agendas". The 1291 Yuan edition by Zongbao entered the Ming canon and is about a third longer. [source: IEP-HN §2; WP-Platform_Sutra]
- **Northern/Southern story as polemic (McRae)**: Shenhui (684–758), claiming to be Huineng's student, attacked Shenxiu (c. 606–706) and his heir Puji (651–739) — the imperially patronised line — as "gradualist", insisted Huineng was the real Sixth Patriarch and claimed to be Seventh; by the ninth century the "Southern" sudden school was the official line; ironically both direct lineages died out. Shenhui coined "Northern School" to deride Shenxiu's line (McRae 2003: 54). His campaign is dated from 731 (WP-Chan/Huineng citing McRae 2003: 68) and a public attack at Huatai in 734 (WP-Shenhui) — **say "in the 730s"**. Dumoulin called Shenhui "unscrupulous". McRae: Shenxiu's verse "does not in fact refer to gradual or progressive endeavor, but to a *constant* practice of cleaning the mirror". [source: IEP-HN §§3, 7; WP-Shenhui; WP-Platform_Sutra]
- Sudden vs gradual in the Platform Sutra itself (Yampolsky via IEP): "Good friends, in the dharma there is no sudden or gradual, but among people some are keen and others dull." (sec. 16). Thompson: *dun*, "sudden", might better be "poised/ready"; Zongmi (Guifeng, 780–841) proposed "sudden awakening followed by gradual cultivation". [source: IEP-HN §§4, 6b]
- After the Huichang purge, Linji Yixuan (d. 866) dismissed scriptures as "hitching posts for donkeys" and told students to kill "the Buddha" if met on the road (Hershock's paraphrase); rural Chan flourished: the "five houses" Linji, Caodong, Fayan, Yunmen, Guiyang; by the twelfth century most Song-court-supported public monasteries were Chan (Welter 2006). [source: SEP-CH §2]
- Mazu Daoyi (709–788), founder of the Hongzhou lineage. [source: SEP-CH §1]
- Chinul (1158–1210) in Korea reconciled meditation and doctrine and upheld "sudden enlightenment followed by gradual cultivation". [source: SEP-KB §3.1.2]

## 8. Pure Land (brief)
- Rebirth in Sukhāvatī, the pure land of Amitābha, chiefly through *nianfo* (reciting Amitābha's name; Japanese *nenbutsu*); three main sūtras (*Longer* and *Shorter Sukhāvatīvyūha*, *Contemplation Sūtra*). The "Lotus School" name derives from Pure Land lotus societies, the first founded by Huiyuan (334–416) on Mount Lu. [source: WP-Pure_Land_Buddhism]
- "Many modern scholars argue that no independent Pure Land 'school' … existed in China"; it was practised within Tiantai, Vinaya and Chan; the idea of a separate Chinese Pure Land patriarchate was shaped by Japanese sectarian scholarship (Sharf 2002). [source: WP-Pure_Land_Buddhism] (Cross-ref Japanese Hōnen/Shinran in History "Medieval Japan".)

## 9. The persecution of 841–845/846
- Emperor Wuzong of Tang (r. 840–846), Huichang era; peak in 845. Board of Worship figures: 4,600 monasteries, 40,000 hermitages, 260,500 monks and nuns returned to lay (tax-paying) life. Also targeted Zoroastrianism, Manichaeism and the Church of the East. Motives: war costs after the 843 Uyghur campaign, tax-exempt monastic wealth, foreignness; Wuzong was a zealous Daoist. [source: WP-Huichang_persecution]
- **Date ranges differ by source**: 842–846 (SEP-HY §8.1, "Great Anti-Buddhist Persecution"); Huichang era 841–846 with "more than a quarter million monks and nuns" laicised and "over 5,000 temples, monasteries and Buddhist libraries" destroyed (SEP-CH §2); 841–845 (IEP-HN; WP); "after 845" (IEP-NC). **Write "the Huichang persecution, culminating in 845".** Berthrong: "not a bloody event", but many schools "failed to revive fully". Reversed by the next emperor. [source: IEP-NC §3; SEP-HY §8.1]
- Text-based metropolitan schools were devastated; rural Chan flourished. [source: SEP-CH §2]

## 10. Late-Tang forerunners: Han Yu and Li Ao
- 755 An Lushan rebellion as the Tang's great divide. Earliest "Neo-Confucians" (Berthrong): Han Yu (768–824), Li Ao (ca. 772–836), Liu Zongyuan (773–819). [source: IEP-NC §3] (IEP-ZD gives Li Ao 772–836 and spells "Liu Zhongyuan".)
- Han Yu: *guwen* "ancient prose" movement; proposed a canon-within-the-canon of the *Mean*, *Great Learning*, *Analects*, *Mencius*. [source: IEP-NC §3] In 819 his *Memorial on the Bone-relic of the Buddha* to Emperor Xianzong called the finger-bone "a filthy object" to be destroyed "by water and fire"; condemned to death, saved by friends, exiled to Chaozhou; he called Buddhism of barbarian origin. "The Origin of the Dao" (*Yuandao*) attacked Buddhist and Daoist monasticism as economically unproductive. [source: WP-Han_Yu]
- Li Ao: "highly influential essay on human nature"; quoted (Barrett 1992: 102): "Therefore it is sincerity that the sage takes as his nature, absolutely still and without movement … It is returning to his true nature that the worthy man follows without ceasing." [source: IEP-NC §3] **Debate**: T. H. Barrett's book is titled *Li Ao: Buddhist, Taoist, or Neo-Confucian?* (Oxford, 1992). [source: IEP-NC references]

## 11. "Neo-Confucianism", daoxue, lixue: the names
- "Neo-Confucian" is an English label "of somewhat ambiguous scope": broad sense = any Tang-or-later reviver of Confucianism after Buddhism's rise; narrow sense = the 11th-century group around the Cheng brothers and their followers, who called their movement *Daoxue* 道學, "Learning of the Way", originally aimed at shifting education from literary attainment to the moral Way (Bol 2008). [source: SEP-SM §1]
- Other Chinese names: *lixue* ("learning of principle/pattern"; Cheng–Zhu), *xinxue* ("learning of heart-mind"; Lu–Wang). Chinese scholarship mostly calls it *lixue*. [source: SEP-SM §2.3; IEP-CHao]
- IEP-NC: the term has a "modern origin" and "is confusing and needs some careful revision"; "Confucianism" itself has no exact traditional equivalent (closest: *ru*, "scholar of ritual"). [source: IEP-NC §1]
- The 20th-century "New Confucianism" is a distinct later movement. [source: IEP-NC intro]

## 12. Northern Song founders
- Northern Song 960–1127 (SEP-SM; SEP-ZX and IEP give 960–1126). Founders: Zhou Dunyi (1017–1073), Zhang Zai (1020–1077), Cheng Hao (1032–1085), Cheng Yi (1033–1107), and on some accounts Shao Yong (1011–1077); Zhu Xi's anthology *Reflections on Things at Hand* (*Jinsilu*, compiled with Lü Zuqian, 622 passages) omitted Shao. Zhang, Cheng Hao and Cheng Yi opposed Wang Anshi's (1021–1086) reforms. [source: SEP-SM §2.1; IEP-ZD §1]
- **Zhou Dunyi**: from Daozhou (Hunan); never took the *jinshi*; held minor posts; retreat at Lianxi near Mount Lu; resigned 1071. Works: "Explanation of the Diagram of the Supreme Polarity" (*Taijitu shuo*), *Penetrating the Classic of Changes* (*Tongshu*, 40 short chapters), "On the Love of the Lotus". Only 12 of the 622 *Jinsilu* passages are his. [source: IEP-ZD §§1–2]
- The Explanation opens *wuji er taiji*: "Non-Polar(ity), and yet Supreme Polarity!" (John Thompson, IEP) / "Free from polarity, and yet the supreme polarity" (Kirill Thompson, SEP-ZX §5). *Taiji* "variously translated as the Great Ultimate, Supreme Polarity, or the Supreme Pivot" (SEP-SM). Sequence: wuji/taiji → yin and yang (alternating stillness and activity) → five phases → myriad things → humans with "the finest and most spiritually efficacious [qi]" → the sage. [source: IEP-ZD §2a; SEP-SM §2.1]
- Daoist roots: prevalent view that the diagram passed from the Daoist master Chen Tuan (d. 989) via Chong Fang (956–1015) and Mu Xiu (979–1032). [source: IEP-ZD]
- **Lineage construction**: Zhou's place as founder of the "orthodox" line is "due to efforts of Zhu Xi in the late 12th century"; the Lu brothers argued Zhou was too Daoist; Cheng Yi said Cheng Hao rediscovered the Way from the classics; the Chengs called Zhou by his personal name, not as teacher. [source: IEP-ZD legacy section] (Parallel with Chan lineages: IEP-HN compares Zhu Xi's lineage-building to Shenhui's, "with Confucius taking the place of Huineng and Master Zhu serving as the Confucian version of Shenhui". [source: IEP-HN §3])
- **Zhang Zai**: born 1020, died on the road 1077; studied Buddhism and Daoism before returning to Confucian texts; lectured on the *Changes* "on a tiger skin" in the capital c. 1056; *Correcting Ignorance* (*Zhengmeng*) completed 1076, which originally included the "Western Inscription". Everything is *qi*, condensing and dispersing; dispersed *qi* = the Great Vacuity (*taixu*), a term from the *Zhuangzi*; against Buddhists, change proves things real rather than unreal; *li* not central for him. Distinguished original nature from physical nature. [source: IEP-ZZ §§1–3] Western Inscription: "That which fills the universe I regard as my body"; Heaven and Earth are everyone's father and mother; "Even those who are tired, infirm, crippled, or sick … are all my brothers who are in distress" (translation as given in IEP-ZZ, translator not named); Cheng Yi defended it against the charge of Mohism. [source: IEP-ZZ §3; SEP-SM §2.1 calls it "perhaps the most famous inspirational essay in the Neo-Confucian tradition"]
- Relationship: IEP-ZZ says the Chengs were Zhang's "younger cousins"; WP-Cheng_Yi says Cheng Yi was Zhang's "nephew". **Conflict; avoid specifying.**
- **Cheng Hao** (1032–1085): "although I have learned much from others, the two words *tian li* [heavenly principle] I grasped on my own"; li as "life-giving activity"; humaneness explained by doctors who call numb limbs "not-*ren*": "a person of humanity will be in one body with ten thousand things" (*Yishu* 2a, trans. in IEP-CHao, Yong Huang). With Cheng Hao and Cheng Yi "li first becomes the central concept in a philosophical system". [source: IEP-CHao]
- Cheng Hao on complementarity: "The li of heaven, earth and the myriad things is that nothing exists in isolation; everything certainly has its opposite/complement … When I reflect on this truth late at night, I feel delighted as if my hands were waving and my feet were dancing." (based on Chan 1967, edited by K. Thompson) [source: SEP-ZX §5]
- **Cheng Yi** (1033–1107): born Luoyang; *jinshi* 1059; opposed Wang Anshi; imperial lecturer from 1086; teachings banned 1097, later pardoned. [source: WP-Cheng_Yi] (IEP-CYi contains date errors — "In 1109 he suffered a stroke … died in September of that year" and a revision "finished" in 1049 at age sixty — contradicting its own heading 1033–1107; **do not use IEP-CYi for dates**.)
- Cheng Yi: "the original nature is just [a matter of] li" (Chan 1963: 560, via SEP-ZX §3); Buddhists "based themselves on the heartmind" whereas Confucians "based themselves on Heaven" (*HNCSYS* 21b:274, via SEP-SM §3.3). Posited the *qi*-constituted "material nature" (*qizhi zhi xing*) beside the original nature to explain moral failure and species difference. [source: SEP-SM §3.2]
- Cheng Yi on *gewu*: *ge* = "arrive at", *wu* = "events/matters"; investigate one matter after another, "day after day", by reading, discussing historical figures, handling affairs; one need not exhaust all things — once a principle is exhausted in one case "for the rest one can infer by analogy", because "innumerable principles amount to one". [source: IEP-CYi §on investigating matters]
- Cheng Yi's tiger example of "genuine knowledge": the farmer once mauled by a tiger fears it as the other villagers do not (*HNCSYS* 2a:16; Graham 1992: 80). [source: SEP-SM §4.1]

## 13. Zhu Xi (1130–1200)
- Born October 1130 in Youqi, Fujian; father Zhu Song (1097–1143); educated by eclectic scholars; studied Chan with the monk Dao Qian, reputedly met Dahui Zonggao (1089–1163); passed the *jinshi* at 19, "drawing on Chan Buddhism in his answers"; formally became the student of Li Tong (1093–1163, of the Cheng Yi line via Yang Shi) in 1160; preferred temple guardianships to court office. [source: SEP-ZX §1; IEP-ZX §1] In 1179 revived the White Deer Grotto Academy as prefect of Nankang. [source: WP-Zhu_Xi]
- Works: *Classified Dialogues* (*Zhuzi yulei*); *Collected Works* (*Zhuzi wenji*); *Reflections on Things at Hand*; editions of the *Changes* and *Odes*; even an annotated edition of the Daoist alchemical *Cantong qi*. Worked on the *Great Learning* in his last days. [source: SEP-ZX §1]
- **Four Books** (*Sishu*): *Great Learning*, *Analects*, *Mencius*, *Doctrine of the Mean*, edited with commentary. Zhu's interpretation "became the basis of the civil service examination in 1313" (SEP-SM §2.2); exams based on it "from 1313 until their abolition in 1905" (WP-Zhu_Xi; WP-Imperial_examination; IEP-NC: abolished 1905). **SEP-ZX and IEP-ZX say "1908" — an error; use 1905.** IEP-ZX also dates the Yuan "1280–1341" (sic) — ignore.
- In his day Zhu "was just one among many prominent thinkers … who claimed the mantle of the Cheng brothers", but by the end of the Song he was seen as the most authoritative spokesman for Daoxue. [source: SEP-SM §2.2] He constructed the *daotong*, "transmission of the Way". [source: IEP-NC §4–5]
- Human nature: following Mencius, people are basically good, but each person's disposition is conditioned by their *qi* endowment (*qizhi*) and environment; Zhu preferred to say we have one nature described two ways rather than two natures (Angle & Tiwald 2017: 64–67). [source: SEP-ZX §2; SEP-SM §3.2]
- Cultivation: rejected both Li Tong's stillness (quiet-sitting) and Hu Hong/Zhang Shi's "introspection in action"; drawing on Zhou Dunyi's "interpenetration of stillness and activity" (Adler 2014), settled on a twofold method: reverence/mindfulness (*jing*; Kalton suggests "mindfulness") plus investigating things to discern their patterns (*li*). Later stressed first establishing resolve (*lizhi*). [source: SEP-ZX §2]
- *Ren* defined as "the essential character of mind" and "the essential pattern of love" (*Treatise on Humanity*, *Renshuo*). [source: SEP-ZX §3.2; IEP-ZX §3]
- Discretion (*quan*): Zhu allowed departures from ritual norms in emergencies (Mencius's drowning sister-in-law) and when more humane, e.g. "condoning the remarriage of a widow who would otherwise be destined to isolation and destitution" (Thompson). [source: SEP-ZX §3.1]
- *Li* metaphors: grain in wood, lines in jade, veins in a leaf; *li* "are manifested immanently rather than abstractly". [source: SEP-ZX §3.1]
- Observation: on Jade Mountain, Zhu found bamboo sprouts grew at the "same rate of growth day and night, exactly the opposite of the monk's claim" (SEP-ZX; IEP-ZX tells it of *bean* sprouts — **inconsistent; say "sprouts"**); fossil seashells on a mountain led him to infer a former seabed. [source: SEP-ZX §4.1; IEP-ZX §5]
- Wang Yangming's failed attempt to find the *li* of bamboo by staring at it (cross-ref later chapter): Qian Mu's diagnosis — Wang had no question or hypothesis in mind. [source: SEP-ZX §4.1; IEP-NC §5c]
- Zhu's critics among contemporaries: Lü Zuqian (1137–1181 per IEP-ZD; SEP-ZX/IEP-ZX "1134–1181" — **discrepancy**), Chen Liang (1143–1194; "utilitarian"; "I simply don't agree with [your] joining together principles and [complex] affairs … as if they were barrel hoops", Tillman 1994: 52), Ye Shi (1150–1223). [source: SEP-ZX §6; IEP-NC §5b; SEP-SM §2.5]
- Interpretations of *li*: Stoic natural law (J. P. Bruce, 1920s); Platonic Forms (Feng Youlan, from the 1930s); Aristotelian nature/essence (Carsun Chang, 1950s); Kantian critique (Mou Zongsan, 1960s: an a priori framework illicitly filled by a posteriori observation); Whiteheadian organic process (Joseph Needham, 1956); Hu Shi: *gewu* as "hypothesis and verification by evidence"; A. C. Graham: *li* is "pattern", never a premise in a syllogism. [source: IEP-ZX §5; SEP-ZX §4.1]

**ARGUMENT 1 (why li in addition to qi), SEP-SM §3.1 with SEP-ZX §§4.2, 6:**
1. All phenomenal things and events are constituted of *qi* (vital stuff).
2. But the world shows a valuable order and unity: plants and animals behave naturally, seasons cooperate to sustain life; humans can grasp and contribute to this order.
3. Some facts are not explained by the stuff alone: why boats travel only by water and carts only by land; why children are inclined to love their parents; why *qi* alternates between yin and yang phases.
4. And some of these facts are normative: it is not only that children tend to serve their parents but that it is *right* that they do.
5. So a further ground is needed: Pattern/Principle (*li*), whose content is "ceaseless life-generativity" (*sheng sheng bu xi*), shared by all things.
6. Li is "prior" to *qi* — not in time ("it is not that Pattern exists first and then qi arises"), but as what we infer back to as more fundamental (*ZZYL* 1:3) — and is the "master" (*zhu*, *zai*) of *qi*, setting rules which *qi* follows "of its own power" without li acting directly.
7. Applied to persons: human nature = li, hence good; moral failure = turbid *qi* obscuring it.
- **Objection (qi-monism)**: a minority — Zhang Zai, Wang Tingxiang, Wang Fuzhi (1619–92) and later Dai Zhen (1723–77) — held that the features of *qi* fully explain the order; on their view patterns are "a posteriori … realizations of qi interactions", which dissolves the dualism. Textual objection (Dai Zhen): Zhu reads his metaphysics into the classics; can Confucius' cry "Heaven has forsaken me" (*Analects* 11.9) mean that *li* had forsaken him? [source: SEP-SM §3.1; SEP-ZX §6; IEP-ZX §5]
- Korean version of the objection: "how could a dead rider (principle as a purely formal pattern) guide the living horse of vital force?" [source: IEP-NC §6a]
- **Reply** (Thompson, on Zhu's behalf): li is "implicated in the very make-up and possible configurations of qi", which is why regular a posteriori patterns can emerge; a qi-only view cannot account for "existence and the variegated yet systematic given world order"; li sets "possibilities of order", not determinations. Against Dai's textual charge: Zhu's actual commentary on 11.9 speaks of Confucius' grief and does not mention "pattern". Thompson also argues li and qi are complementary ("never exist in separation"), so Zhu's "dualism" is not crude. [source: SEP-ZX §§4.2, 6; IEP-ZX §5] (SEP-SM, by contrast, says Zhu made "more explicit than any predecessor a certain metaphysical dualism of qi … and li" — **interpretive disagreement between SEP-SM and SEP-ZX; name it.**)

**ARGUMENT 2 (the investigation of things, gewu, with Lu's objection), SEP-SM §§3.3, 4.1; IEP-NC §5b; IEP-CYi:**
1. Everyone has the moral pattern (li) innately, but it is obscured by *qi* and selfish desire.
2. Our intuitions are therefore "too unreliable": "we are too predisposed to rationalize or find clever ways of countenancing self-serving inclinations and judgments" (Zhu's worry, per Tiwald).
3. A check independent of our momentary heart-mind is needed; Cheng Yi had said Buddhists base themselves on the heart-mind, Confucians on Heaven.
4. Li is present in things and affairs, and in the classics: so investigate things — read the classics "with an open mind and reverential attention", study history, handle affairs, observe nature — "to discern their patterns", grasping also "the reasons why it is so" (*suoyi ran zhi gu*).
5. Because principle is one though its manifestations are many (*li yi fen shu*), accumulated cases allow inference by analogy and eventual understanding of the whole (Cheng Yi: one need not exhaust every thing).
6. Hence *gewu* (with *jing*, reverence) is the path to sagehood; Zhu distinguished the "heartmind of the Way" (*daoxin*), which tracks li, from the "human heartmind" (*renxin*), which imposes private desires.
- **Objection (Lu Xiangshan)** — the dialogue in Siu-chi Huang's translation (1977: 31), via IEP-NC: "Bomin asked: How is one to investigate things (gewu)? The Teacher (Lu Xiangshan) said: Investigate the principle of things. Bomin said: The ten thousand things under Heaven are extremely multitudinous; how, then, can we investigate all of them exhaustively? The teacher replied: The ten thousand things are already complete in us. It is only necessary to apprehend their principle." Also: book-learning is fragmentary and cannot be internalised wholeheartedly; since we have well-formed ethical capacities by nature, rely on deeply felt insight from daily life (wholeheartedness criterion). [source: IEP-NC §5b; SEP-SM §§3.3, 4.1]
- **Zhu's reply**: (a) Lu's method has no standard independent of subjective whim — it is "too Buddhist" (Zhu's frequent complaint, *ZZYL* 104, 124); (b) the infinite-task worry misreads the method: one does not exhaust all things, one extends by analogy from cases (Cheng Yi); (c) Zhu called Lu's and Li Tong's approach intuitionism, "inadequate for dealing effectively with the complex human affairs" (Thompson). [source: SEP-SM §3.3; SEP-ZX §3.1; IEP-CYi]
- **Lu's counter-reply** (Berthrong's reconstruction): not solipsism — "only by finding principle in the mind-heart could the person then effectively comprehend the rest of the world"; starting from external things, we "fall prey to the problems of self-delusion and partiality". [source: IEP-NC §5b]

## 14. Lu Xiangshan (Lu Jiuyuan) and the Goose Lake meeting
- Lu Jiuyuan, style Xiangshan: born 1139, Jiangxi; died 10 January 1193 (Britannica, via WS-2). WP's infobox has 1192 while its text has 1193 (the lunar year straddles); SEP, IEP: 1139–1193. **Use 1139–1193.** [source: WS-2; WP-Lu_Jiuyuan; SEP-SM §2.3]
- Lu "regarded the heartmind as a constituent of li" and spoke of a faculty of good knowledge; later historians grouped him with Wang Yangming as the Lu–Wang "Learning of Heartmind" (*xinxue*) against the Cheng–Zhu "Learning of Pattern" (*lixue*). [source: SEP-SM §2.3] (Slogan "the heart-mind is principle", *xin ji li* 心即理: stated in WS summaries and implied by SEP-SM/IEP-NC ("principle was to be found complete within the mind-heart"); the exact phrase was not seen in an SEP/IEP text.)
- "The universe is my mind, and my mind is the universe": WP-Lu_Jiuyuan gives this "in his own words" without a translator; other renderings: "The universe is my heart/mind. My heart/mind is the universe." [source: WP-Lu_Jiuyuan; WS-8] **Use only attributed as "a famous saying, variously translated".**
- Lu's school "attracted not many followers" after his death and was revived by Wang Yangming. [source: WP-Lu_Jiuyuan]
- **Goose Lake (Ehu Temple), 1175**: arranged by Lü Zuqian, bringing Zhu Xi together with Lu Jiuyuan and his elder brother Lu Jiuling, at Ehu Temple in Xinzhou, Jiangxi, to reconcile their views; Zhu stressed broad study and investigation of principle in things, Lu that the heart-mind is principle; they parted unreconciled. Britannica: the confrontation "further convinced him that Confucianism as Zhu Xi had shaped it was not Mencian". [source: WS-1, WS-2 — **second-hand summaries only; no SEP/IEP text mentions Goose Lake**] Details such as "ten days" or the poems exchanged: NOT VERIFIED.
- Zhu and Lu also disputed by letter over Zhou Dunyi's *wuji*, the Lu brothers arguing Zhou was too Daoist. [source: IEP-ZD legacy]

## 15. Buddhist influence vs anti-Buddhist polemic (debate)
- Neo-Confucian charges against Buddhism (SEP-SM §1): putting personal liberation first is selfish (*si*) and egoistic; it sacrifices family and social bonds; it relies on unsubstantiated afterlife theories; its meditation is a naive remedy; its "emptiness" (*kong*) makes things illusory and worthless. Tiwald: a charitable reading takes the selfishness charge as about ordinary practitioners' motives; Zhu Xi acknowledged subtler Buddhist views but thought the crude conception "was the operative one in most Buddhist practices". [source: SEP-SM §1]
- Influences (SEP-SM §1): (1) the strong reading of "human nature is good" as *well-formed* innate dispositions — Mencius "seems to have thought" nature is good because of a capacity to *become* good (6A6, 6A8); the strong version more likely comes from Buddha-nature/tathāgatagarbha thought; shared metaphors (pearl in muddy water, mirror covered with dust); (2) the technical term *li*, long used in Buddhist metaphysics; (3) interest in meditation (quiet-sitting); (4) elimination of desires as an aim. [source: SEP-SM §1]
- Van Norden & Jones: in the Four Books *li* occurs "precisely zero times" in the *Analects* and *Great Learning*, "only twice in the *Mean*, and a mere seven times in the *Mengzi*", never with deep metaphysical sense; Zhu gives it "a metaphysical meaning very reminiscent of Indra's net"; the stress on "selfish desires" (*siyu*) reflects Buddhist accounts of wrongdoing. Neo-Confucian thinkers reinterpreted their tradition "unconsciously" in Buddhist light. [source: SEP-HY §8.1]
- Thompson: Zhu's holistic synthesis "may have been inspired in part by Huayan Buddhism (Ivanhoe 2000: 47–49)"; traces of Huayan in Zhu's system (Makeham 2018, *The Buddhist Roots of Zhu Xi's Thought*). [source: SEP-ZX intro, §1]
- Counter-view (Berthrong): the "stimulus theory" has "a genuine element of truth", but "once prompted", the Neo-Confucians built "out of materials indigenous to the historical development of the Confucian Way", drawn "almost exclusively from the classical repertoire". [source: IEP-NC §4]
- Japan: Song Neo-Confucianism reached Japan from the 13th century via Zen monks and long stayed inside Zen monasteries. [source: SEP-JC §3]

## 16. Spread to Korea
- Neo-Confucianism became the official ideology of the Chosŏn dynasty (1392–1910); An Hyang (1243–1306) introduced it in late Koryŏ, bringing Zhu Xi's image and texts. [source: SEP-KC; IEP-NC §6a]
- Kwŏn Kŭn first linked the Four Beginnings (always good) with li and the Seven Feelings (possibly bad) with *qi*. Four Beginnings (Mencius 2A6): commiseration, shame and dislike, deference and compliance, right and wrong. Seven Feelings (*Book of Rites*): joy, anger, sadness, fear, love, dislike, liking. [source: SEP-KC]
- Yi Hwang (T'oegye, 1501–1570): *Ten Diagrams on Sage Learning* (1568); Yi I (Yulgok, 1536–1584 per SEP-KC and WP; IEP-NC "1536–1583" — **discrepancy**): *Essentials of Sage Learning* (1575). [source: SEP-KC §4; IEP-NC §6a]
- **Four–Seven debate**: letters between T'oegye and Kobong (Ki Taesŭng, 1527–1572), continued between Yulgok and Ugye (Sŏng Hon, 1536–1598). Michael Kalton: "the single most important intellectual controversy of the Yi [Chosŏn] dynasty". T'oegye first assigned the Four to li and the Seven to *ki*; Kobong objected that li and *ki* are inseparable in actual things; T'oegye's mature view of "alternate issuance" (*hobal*): the Four arise as li issues and *ki* follows, the Seven as *ki* issues and li rides it — li guiding *ki* "like a rider guiding a horse". Yulgok: *ki* activates both; li needs a locus; formulas *kibal isŭng* ("*ki* issues and *i* rides along") and *it'ong kiguk* ("*i* universally permeates while *ki* forms concrete configurations"); the Seven include the Four, not vice versa. [source: SEP-KC §4]
- Berthrong reads Yulgok's answer as "probably the most imaginative reinterpretation of Zhu's daoxue to be found in traditional East Asia" and warns against simply calling him a qi-monist. [source: IEP-NC §6a]

## 17. Spread to Japan (cross-ref only: "Beyond Europe … Tokugawa Confucianism")
- Introduced via Zen monks from the 13th century; Ashikaga Academy (early 15th c.) students were still Zen monks. [source: SEP-JC §3]
- Inoue Tetsujirō's three-school scheme (Zhu Xi school: Fujiwara Seika 1561–1617, Hayashi Razan 1583–1657, Yamazaki Ansai 1619–1682; Wang Yangming school; Ancient Learning) — Tucker says it "simply does not accurately represent developments of Japanese Confucianism". [source: SEP-JC §4] (WP gives Seika 1561–1619 — discrepancy.)
- Hayashi Razan: student of Fujiwara Seika; adviser to the second shogun Tokugawa Hidetada from 1607; rector of the Edo Confucian academy (Shōhei-kō); the title Daigaku-no-kami became hereditary in his family. [source: WP-Hayashi_Razan]
- Kaibara Ekken (1630–1714): naturalist; *Taigiroku* expresses doubts about Zhu Xi and opens by quoting Lu Xiangshan. [source: IEP-NC §6b; SEP-JC]

## 18. Women
- Buddhism brought monastic communities "for both men and women" to China. [source: IEP-NC §2c]
- *Biqiuni zhuan* (*Lives of the Nuns*), compiled by the monk Baochang (active early 6th c.; c. 516): sixty-five biographies of nuns of the 4th–6th centuries; English: Kathryn Ann Tsai (trans.), *Lives of the Nuns: Biographies of Chinese Buddhist Nuns from the Fourth to Sixth Centuries*, University of Hawai'i Press, 1994. [source: WS-3 — catalogue/review snippets]
- Zhu Jingjian (c. 292–361), widowed teacher in Luoyang, "referred to as the first nun in China", took vows c. 357 but without full vinaya ordination. [source: WP-Jingjian — a stub with "citation needed"; **use cautiously**]
- Empress Wu Zetian, "the only female emperor in Chinese history", was Fazang's patron and styled herself a cakravartin and bodhisattva. [source: IEP-FZ §1]
- Neo-Confucian gender norms: Cheng Yi said it would be improper to marry a widow; asked about impoverished widows: "To starve to death is a small matter, but to lose one's chastity is a great matter" (餓死事小，失節事大; WP translation, translator not named; WS-4 reports variants, e.g. "an extremely small matter … an extremely big one"). WP: Cheng is "widely believed to be responsible for the rise of the cult of widow chastity", but "in reality … only partly held this view". [source: WP-Cheng_Yi; WS-4] Counter-evidence: Rosenlee reports Cheng Yi "proudly spoke of how his father had housed and given his grand-niece in remarriage" (Rosenlee 2006: 133); Zhu allowed widow remarriage as discretion. [source: SEP-ZX §3.1]
- Lijuan Shen: by the Song, mainstream discourse "viewed both the ability and moral character of women as significantly inferior"; Zhu Xi blamed Wu Zetian's disregard of the three cardinal guides for later chaos and held the father "absolutely superior to the mother". [source: IEP-G]
- Rosenlee: the cult of widow chastity "reached its height during the Qing dynasty (1644 to 1912 CE)"; footbinding has "no textual basis connecting [it to] Confucian philosophy", and its exoticisation is "a clear case of 'orientalism'". [source: SEP-CG §§5.3, 6.1] (Note: the History chapter "Tang and Song" lists "the spread of foot-binding among elites" as a social consequence alongside Neo-Confucianism — the writer should not imply a doctrinal link.)
- Later counterpoint (cross-ref Wang Yangming chapter): Taizhou thinkers Luo Rufang (1515–1588) and Li Zhi (1527–1602) defended women's education; Luo held women more reliably virtuous. [source: SEP-SM §2.4]

## Debates
- **Was Huineng historical, and who wrote the Platform Sutra?** Fahai vs an Oxhead-school author c. 780 vs layered composition (Schlütter); McRae and Faure treat the lineage story as "invention of tradition". [IEP-HN; WP-Platform_Sutra; SEP-CH]
- **Sudden vs gradual, North vs South**: Shenhui's polemic (McRae: Shenxiu taught constant, not progressive, practice); Zongmi's "sudden awakening, gradual cultivation"; Thompson's reading of *dun* as "poised". [IEP-HN; WP-Shenhui]
- **What was *geyi*?** Textbook "matching concepts" translation method vs Mair's (2010) "ephemeral" exegetical technique for numbered lists. [WP-Geyi]
- **Was there a Pure Land "school" in China?** Many modern scholars say no (Sharf). [WP-Pure_Land]
- **Fazang: metaphysician or imperial propagandist?** Rothschild/Weinstein on Huayan cosmology and Wu Zetian's centralised rule. [IEP-FZ]
- **How much is Neo-Confucianism Buddhist?** Tiwald, Van Norden & Jones, Makeham (2018), Ivanhoe (strong influence: li, "good nature" as innate perfection, selfish desires, meditation) vs Berthrong (stimulus only; indigenous materials). [SEP-SM §1; SEP-HY §8.1; SEP-ZX; IEP-NC §4]
- **Zhu Xi: dualist or holist?** SEP-SM ("metaphysical dualism"), Feng Youlan (Platonic forms), Mou Zongsan (Kantian critique) vs Thompson, Graham, Needham (immanent pattern, complementarity, organicism). [SEP-SM §2.2; SEP-ZX §§4.2, 5, 6; IEP-ZX §5]
- **Zhu vs Lu on method** (study and investigation vs recovering the mind); Wang Yangming later sides with Lu. [SEP-SM §§3.3, 4.1; IEP-NC §5]
- **Was Zhu's gewu scientific?** Hu Shi, Needham, Yung Sik Kim (*The Natural Philosophy of Chu Hsi*, 2000) vs Thompson's view that his aim was "pragmatic rather than epistemic". [SEP-ZX §4.1]
- **Did Mencius hold the strong "innately good" view the Neo-Confucians read into him?** SEP-SM says not (6A6, 6A8). [SEP-SM §§1, 3.2]
- **Constructed lineages**: Chan patriarchs (McRae) and Zhu Xi's *daotong* placing Zhou Dunyi (IEP-ZD; IEP-HN's parallel).
- **Was Li Ao a Buddhist, a Daoist or a Neo-Confucian?** (Barrett 1992 title). [IEP-NC refs]
- **Four–Seven**: T'oegye's "alternate issuance" vs Kobong/Yulgok's inseparability; is Yulgok a qi-monist? (Berthrong: no). [SEP-KC §4; IEP-NC §6a]
- **Neo-Confucianism and women**: Cheng Yi as source of the widow-chastity cult (WP) vs evidence of family remarriages and Zhu's discretion (Rosenlee via SEP-ZX); Rosenlee against linking Confucian philosophy to footbinding; Shen on Song-era views of women's inferiority. [WP-Cheng_Yi; SEP-ZX; SEP-CG; IEP-G]
- **Japanese Confucian schools**: Inoue's three-school scheme vs Tucker's critique. [SEP-JC §4]

## Consistency with published Periergia texts
- *Confucius, Mencius and Xunzi*: says "fundamentally good" (*ben*) is Zhu Xi's gloss which entered the *Three Character Classic*, and that the "Learning of the Way" reread Mencius "with Buddhist-influenced concepts", and that Zhu (1130–1200) grouped the Four Books. **Consistent** with SEP-SM §§1, 3.2 (Mencius's view weaker than the Neo-Confucian reading) and with Zhu's dates/Four Books. Its remark that Raphals and Nylan find early texts "relatively positive about women compared with later Neo-Confucian writing" is consistent with IEP-G. The *Three Character Classic* point was not re-checked here.
- *Mozi, the Daoists and the Legalists*: points forward to this chapter for Daoism's meeting with Buddhism — consistent; this chapter should mention *xuanxue* and the Daoist vocabulary of early Buddhism (with the *geyi* caveat).
- *Mahāyāna Philosophy*: "China never translated Dharmakīrti, and its Madhyamaka and Yogācāra are the subject of [this chapter]" — consistent (IEP-Madh per those notes). Its notes give Kumārajīva 344–413 (WP) and 350–409 (IEP-Madh); SEP-CH adds 334–413 — **flag the range**. The Samye debate (Moheyan's "sudden" Chan) in that chapter links to the sudden/gradual theme here.
- *History of Philosophy* overview: says Confucianism was "later synthesized with Buddhist metaphysics as Neo-Confucianism (Zhu Xi, 12th c.; Wang Yangming, 15th–16th c.)". Dates fine (Wang 1472–1529). "Synthesized with Buddhist metaphysics" states one side of a live debate as fact and clashes with the Neo-Confucians' own anti-Buddhist self-understanding (SEP-SM §1; IEP-NC §4). **Minor tension; this chapter should present it as a debate.**
- History *Tang and Song China*: "in 845 the government launched a great persecution of Buddhism, closing thousands of monasteries" — consistent (peak 845; sources give 841/842–845/846). Xuanzang "returning from India in 645 with hundreds of texts" — consistent (657). Zhu Xi's commentaries the exam basis "From 1313 (the examinations resuming in 1315) … until 1905" — consistent with SEP-SM, WP; **contradicts SEP-ZX and IEP-ZX's "1908" (those entries are wrong)**; the 1315 resumption was not independently checked here. Its list including "the spread of foot-binding among elites" as a social consequence should not be echoed as a doctrinal consequence (SEP-CG).
- History *Medieval Japan*: Zen brought by Eisai and Dōgen; Pure Land of Hōnen and Shinran — consistent with SEP-JC that Neo-Confucianism came via Zen monks; nothing contradicts.
- **No outright contradictions requiring correction of published texts**; one interpretive overstatement in the overview.
