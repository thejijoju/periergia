## 1. Framing: "two rebels against Hegel"
- Both attacked Hegelian system-building, but differently. Schopenhauer called Fichte, Schelling and Hegel "humbugs" and "charlatans" and held that their systems rested on errors of the kind ruled out by his account of explanation (versions of the ontological argument). [source: SEP-S §2] Hegel in *Parerga* I: a "commonplace, inane, loathsome, repulsive, and ignorant charlatan" (Payne tr., 1974, p. 96). [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer, citing Payne]
- Kierkegaard: SEP-K says he "has often been understood as an arch opponent of Hegel, but scholarship in recent decades has challenged and complicated this view", suggesting some of his central ideas are "creative developments of Hegel's ideas" and that "the main target of his critique is certain Danish Hegelians … rather than Hegel himself (see especially Stewart 2003)". [source: SEP-K intro] **So "rebel against Hegel" must be qualified for Kierkegaard** (see Debates).
- Both were outsiders to academic philosophy: Schopenhauer "an atheist who stayed outside the academic profession" (IEP-S intro); Kierkegaard was never ordained and held no academic post (SEP-K §1). IEP-K opens: Kierkegaard "is an outsider in the history of philosophy".
- Contrast worth drawing: Schopenhauer was an atheist (IEP-S; SEP-S: "Within Schopenhauer's vision of the world as Will, there is no God"); Kierkegaard a Lutheran Christian whose aim (retrospectively) was "reintroducing Christianity into Christendom" (SEP-K §1).

## 2. Schopenhauer: life
- Born **22 February 1788, Danzig** (Gdańsk), exactly a month after Byron. Father **Heinrich Floris Schopenhauer** (1747–1805), merchant and shipowner of Dutch heritage, chose the name "Arthur" because it is spelled the same in German, French and English. Family moved to Hamburg in March 1793 after Prussia annexed Danzig. [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-S §1]
- Lived in France 1797–99 and England 1803 (Wimbledon boarding school, which he hated). [source: SEP-S §1]
- **Father's death:** Hamburg, 20 April 1805, "possibly by suicide" (SEP-S); "most likely as a result of suicide" (IEP-S); WP: drowned in a canal near the home; possibly accidental, but wife and son believed it suicide. Arthur was 17. [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-S §1; WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer] **Say "probably suicide", not as certain fact.**
- **Mother, Johanna Schopenhauer** (née Trosiener; 9 July 1766 – 17 April 1838), married Heinrich Floris at 18 (1785), twenty years younger than he. After his death she moved to **Weimar** with daughter **Adele** (1797–1849), ran a famous salon frequented by Goethe, Wieland, the Schlegels; became a well-known writer (biography of Fernow 1810; travelogues; novels *Gabriele* 1819, *Die Tante* 1823, *Sidonia* 1827, *Richard Wood* 1837); collected works 24 volumes. WP: "the first German woman to publish books without a pseudonym" and for a time "the most famous woman author in Germany". [source: SEP-S §1; WP-Johanna_Schopenhauer] (The "first … without a pseudonym" claim is WP only; use cautiously.)
- **The break:** constant friction (he accused her of extravagance, flirtation and disloyalty to his father's memory; she found him morose, tactless, argumentative). She found his dissertation incomprehensible. He left Weimar for Dresden in **May 1814**; "After 1814, mother and son never met again"; letters resumed only in 1831 and continued sporadically until her death in 1838; she made Adele her sole heir. [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer (Cartwright 2010); WP-Johanna_Schopenhauer (Safranski)] **CONFLICT:** IEP-S says the tensions "reached its peak when Arthur was 30 years old, at which time she requested that he never contact her again" (i.e. c. 1818). WP/Cartwright/Safranski put the final meeting in 1814. Safe wording: "they parted in 1814 and never met again."
- Studies: Göttingen 1809 (medicine, then philosophy under G. E. Schulze, who told him to read Plato and Kant); Berlin 1811–13 (heard Fichte and Schleiermacher; disillusioned with both). [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-S §1]
- **Dissertation:** *On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason* (*Über die vierfache Wurzel des Satzes vom zureichenden Grunde*), written in Rudolstadt 1813, doctorate awarded **in absentia by Jena, 1813**; revised 2nd ed. 1847. [source: SEP-S §§1–2, Bibliography]
- Dresden 1814–1818; *On Vision and Colours* (1816), in sympathy with Goethe's colour theory (relations with Goethe cooled after it). **The World as Will and Representation** completed March 1818, published **December 1818 with the date 1819**; he was about 30. It "made no impression on the public". [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-S §1; WP-WWR]
- **Berlin 1820 — verified:** he obtained the right to lecture at Berlin, where Hegel had held Fichte's chair since 1818, and in March 1820 "self-assuredly scheduled his class at a time that was simultaneous with Hegel's popular lectures, and few students chose to hear Schopenhauer" (SEP-S). IEP-S: "deliberately, and impudently" scheduled at the same hour; "Only a handful of students attended … while over 200 students attended the lectures of Hegel". WP (Cartwright): "Only five students turned up"; Hegel nevertheless voted to admit him; Schopenhauer had already quarrelled with Hegel at his test lecture in March 1820. [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-S §1; WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer] **Numbers differ (a handful / five; "over 200" for Hegel is IEP only)** — say "only a handful".
- He remained on the lecture list for years; a second attempt (1825) also failed. [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-S §1]
- Marguet lawsuit: shoving/assault quarrel with seamstress Caroline Luise Marguet (Aug 1821); he lost and had to pay her 60 thalers a year for life (IEP-S). [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-S §1] (Optional colour.)
- Left Berlin 1831 because of the cholera epidemic (which killed Hegel); settled permanently in **Frankfurt am Main, June 1833**, for 27 years; daily routine, flute, poodles, read the Upanishads before sleep. [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-S §1]
- Works: *On the Will in Nature* (1836); prize essay *On the Freedom of the Human Will* (1839, won the Royal Norwegian Society prize); ***On the Basis of Morality*** **(1840)**, the sole entry to the Royal Danish Society's competition, yet **denied the prize** (the Society said he had not answered the question and had disrespected eminent philosophers such as Fichte and Hegel); both published together as *The Two Fundamental Problems of Ethics* (1841). [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-S §1]
- **WWR second edition, two volumes, 1844** (vol. 2 = supplementary essays). [source: SEP-S §1 & Bibliography; WP-WWR] IEP-S says "1843" — **error; use 1844.** Third edition 1859. [source: SEP-S; WP-WWR]
- ***Parerga and Paralipomena*** **(1851)** ("appendices and omissions", Greek); published by Hayn of Berlin in 750 copies with only ten free copies as fee. Fame followed: John Oxenford's anonymous review of *Parerga* in the *Westminster Review* (1852, per WP) and his article "Iconoclasm in German Philosophy" (1853), translated into German. [source: SEP-S §1; IEP-S §1; WP-Parerga] (SEP-S dates only the 1853 article and notes the *Westminster Review* was then edited by George Eliot.)
- Died **21 September 1860**, Frankfurt, aged 72. Left his estate to a fund for disabled Prussian soldiers and families of soldiers killed suppressing the 1848 revolution. [source: SEP-S §1]

## 3. Schopenhauer: the main argument — from my body to the will as thing in itself
Background (Kant): Kant distinguished appearances from things in themselves, which we cannot know. Schopenhauer: "Kant's greatest merit was the distinction between appearance and the thing-in-itself" (Appendix to WWR I, as summarised by WP-WWR). The published *Kant* chapter says "Schopenhauer read the thing in itself as Will". [source: WP-WWR; published Kant chapter]

Reconstruction (from SEP-S §§3–4, IEP-S §2a.i, SEP-SA §5.2.5):
1. **The world as representation.** WWR opens "Die Welt ist meine Vorstellung" — "The world is my representation" (alt. "idea", "presentation"). Everything we know as object is an object for a subject, ordered by the principle of sufficient reason (space, time, causality); Schopenhauer reduces Kant's twelve categories to causality. [source: WP-WWR; SEP-S §3; IEP-S]
2. **Kant's route to the thing in itself is blocked.** Following Schulze (and Jacobi), Schopenhauer held that Kant could not infer the thing in itself as the *cause* of sensations, because causality applies only within experience. So if we reach the thing in itself, it must be "through another means altogether". [source: SEP-S §3]
3. **Science leaves an unexplained inside.** Causal explanation describes how forces operate but cannot say what the forces (gravity, electricity) are; they remain "occult qualities". [source: IEP-S §2a.i]
4. **Double knowledge of one's body.** One object only is given to each of us in two ways: as a physical object among objects (representation), and immediately from within, as we will, move and feel (will). Willing and the body's movement are not cause and effect but "a single act" seen from two sides — "the action of the body is nothing but the act of Will objectified". [source: SEP-S §4; IEP-S §2a.i]
5. **Analogy/extension.** Using "the double-knowledge of his own body as the key to the inner being of every other natural phenomenon", he regards every object as having an inner side like his own. [source: SEP-S §4]
6. **Conclusion:** the inner nature of every representation and of the world as a whole is will; "the will is the thing in itself" (IEP-S); every representation is an objectification of the will. Will is not conscious purpose: it is "a mindless, aimless, non-rational impulse" (SEP-S), "a blind, unconscious force" (IEP-S) that becomes conscious only in animals; he chose the word because will is how we experience it. Because plurality comes from space and time (the *principium individuationis*), the will in itself is one. [source: SEP-S §4; IEP-S; WP-WWR Book II]
- SEP-S: his originality lies not in calling the world will or act (Fichte had) but in "the conception of Will as being devoid of rationality or intellect". [source: SEP-S §4]
- Intermediate tier: the will objectifies itself first in timeless **Platonic Ideas** (grades of objectification, from natural forces to humanity), then in spatio-temporal individuals. [source: SEP-S §4; IEP-S §2a.ii]
- Sources of the double-aspect idea named by SEP-S: Spinoza (primary), Berkeley, and "a subsequent, but often highlighted inspiration" the Upanishads (Brahman/Ātman). [source: SEP-S §4]

**Objections and replies**
- *Objection 1 (Kantian): this is exactly the knowledge of things in themselves Kant forbade; it reifies the thing in itself.* IEP-S: "To have knowledge that is not conditioned by our forms of cognition would be an impossibility for Kant"; Schopenhauer claims he has "completed Kant's project because he has successfully identified the thing in itself". [source: IEP-S §2a.i–ii]
  - *Reply (Schopenhauer's own qualification):* in WWR II ch. 18 ("On the Possibility of Knowing the Thing-in-Itself", 1844, repeating notes of 1821) he says it is only "to us" that the thing in itself appears as will; it may have other modes of being incomprehensible to us. SEP-S concludes that readings of him as a Kantian who denies all knowledge of the thing in itself, and readings of him as a dogmatic metaphysician for whom it is "straightforwardly, wholly and unconditionally Will", both "stand in need of qualification". Julian Young (*Willing and Unwilling*, 1987) influentially argued the thing in itself "cannot be wholly identified with the will" (IEP-S bibliography note). [source: SEP-S §§6–7; IEP-S refs]
- *Objection 2 (epistemic): how could we know it? Inner experience is still experience.* SEP-SA: first-personal knowledge of willing is immediate, "shorn of all of the forms of the PSR … with one exception, the form of time"; so "absolutely direct experience of the will is impossible, because it will always be mediated by time", but in willing (and in music) the thing in itself is "no longer veiled by our other forms". [source: SEP-SA §5.2.5] (I.e. Schopenhauer concedes the knowledge is the nearest we can get, not perfect.)
- *Objection 3 (the analogy is weak / anthropomorphic):* extending the inner experience of one body to stones and gravity is an argument from a single case. SEP-S frames step 5 as Schopenhauer's answer to how to make the world "the most comprehensible", "as if he were trying to make the notion of universal empathy theoretically possible" — i.e. an inference to the best overall interpretation rather than a proof. [source: SEP-S §4] (Present as interpretation; SEP-S, not Schopenhauer, uses this framing.)
- *Objection 4 (internal tension):* if everything is will, where is the will-less state of the saint or the pure subject of aesthetic contemplation? SEP-S calls this a "puzzle" and says it "is difficult to accept Schopenhauer's mystical characterizations … and at the same time identify a consistent place for it" in his scheme; the "Will only to us" reply eases this but then makes it unclear why the world should be so violent. [source: SEP-S §§6–7]

## 4. Pessimism
Argument (IEP-S §3; SEP-S §§4–5; WP-WWR):
1. The essence of everything is will: aimless striving with no final goal. [IEP-S §3]
2. Striving springs from want or lack; want is suffering; satisfaction is unsustainable and is followed by new want. [IEP-S §3] WP-WWR: "pleasure is merely the sensation experienced at the instant one such pain is removed" — i.e. **satisfaction is negative**, the mere removal of a pain. [source: WP-WWR Book II summary]
3. When wants are satisfied and none replace them, we fall into **boredom**. SEP-S: moral consciousness delivers us from "the unremitting oscillation between pain and boredom". [source: SEP-S §5.2]
4. Individuated wills compete for matter: a war of all against all (Hobbes); image of the Australian bulldog ant whose cut halves fight each other. [SEP-S §4; IEP-S §3]
5. Intellect makes it worse in humans: it "brings home their suffering with greater clarity". [IEP-S §3]
6. Conclusion: existence is essentially suffering; daily life "is suffering" (WWR §56); "nonexistence is preferable to existence" (IEP-S's summary). [source: SEP-S §4; IEP-S §3] Images: Tantalus, the Danaids, Ixion's wheel. [SEP-S §4]
- **Not suicide:** suicide does not end suffering, since death "only terminates the form that one's suffering takes"; WP-WWR: suicide is "in some sense an affirmation of the will". The proper response is renunciation of desire. [source: IEP-S §3; WP-WWR Book IV]
- SEP-S: "Often considered to be a thoroughgoing pessimist, Schopenhauer in fact advocated ways – via artistic, moral and ascetic forms of awareness – to overcome" the painful human condition. [source: SEP-S intro]
**Objections and replies** (SEP/IEP give these only partly; label the rest as standard objections, not attributed to a named critic):
- *Objection: pleasure is not merely negative* (some pleasures, e.g. of a taste or a melody, arrive unannounced by any prior want). Not found stated in SEP/IEP; present as a natural objection, unattributed. Reply in Schopenhauer's spirit: such pleasures are brief and the overall balance remains negative (also unattributed).
- *Objection from Schopenhauer's own aesthetics:* his joyful description of music sits in tension with pessimism (SEP-SA cites Norman 2017 on this). [source: SEP-SA §5.2.5]
- *Objection that the pessimism is relative to a human standpoint:* SEP-S §7 argues that if will is the thing in itself only "to us", the pessimistic vision is "an expression of the human perspective" rather than an absolute truth — but then it is unclear why the world must be so violent. [source: SEP-S §7]
- Later reception: pessimism was a major German debate 1860–1900 (Beiser, *Weltschmerz*, 2008/2018 — WP gives 2008; SEP-S bibliography gives 2018). WP: Schopenhauer "remained the most influential German philosopher until the First World War" (citing Beiser). [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer; SEP-S bibliography] (Year discrepancy: OUP published *Weltschmerz* in 2016; neither 2008 nor 2018 is confirmed — do not give a year.)

## 5. Aesthetic contemplation, the Ideas and music (WWR Book III)
- In aesthetic perception we see through the individual object to its Platonic Idea; we "lose ourselves in the object", becoming a "pure will-less, painless, and timeless subject of knowledge" (WWR §34). The highest purpose of art (other than music) is to communicate the Ideas (WWR §50). Relief is temporary. [source: SEP-S §5.1; WP-WWR Book III]
- The **genius** has a "superfluity of intellect over will" and can sustain will-less contemplation; Schopenhauer held only men could be geniuses (IEP-S notes this). [source: IEP-S §2a.ii]
- Beautiful vs **sublime**: in the sublime we rise above an object hostile to our will. [source: WP-WWR; SEP-SA §4.3 heading]
- **Music** is unique: the other arts copy the Ideas, but music is "a copy of Will itself" (*Abbild des Willens selbst*, WWR §52), bypassing the Ideas; it expresses "joy, sorrow, pain, horror … as such in themselves, abstractly" (WWR I, 289, Payne). Analogies: bass notes ~ inorganic nature, melody ~ human life ("most secret story"). Schopenhauer admits the view cannot be demonstrated and asks readers to test it by listening (WWR I, 257). He valued "absolute" music without text. [source: SEP-S §5.1; SEP-SA §5.2.5]
- Influence on composers: Wagner (earliest famous adherent; *Tristan und Isolde*), Brahms, Mahler, Schoenberg and others. Schopenhauer did not return Wagner's admiration: "I remain faithful to Rossini and Mozart!" [source: SEP-S §8; WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer]

## 6. Ethics: compassion (*Mitleid*), *On the Basis of Morality* (1840)
- Attack on Kant: Kant's ethics begs the question (*petitio principii*) by assuming pure moral laws; the unconditioned "ought" is theological in origin; all imperatives are really hypothetical. Reason is instrumental (like Hume). [source: IEP-S §2b.ii]
- Three basic motives (egoism, malice, compassion); only compassion gives moral worth (WP-On_the_Basis_of_Morality — not read in detail; IEP-S supports egoism vs compassion). The supreme principle: "Harm no one and help others as much as you can" (IEP-S's English; Latin *Neminem laede, imo omnes, quantum potes, juva* — Latin form from memory, NOT verified). [source: IEP-S §2b.ii]
- Compassion is "felt knowledge" that individuation is merely phenomenal: the suffering of another is as real as one's own because the world in itself is one; the just person sees through individuation enough not to harm, the good person enough to help, and the highest point is self-sacrifice. [source: IEP-S §2b.ii; SEP-S §5.2]
- *Tat tvam asi* ("thou art that", Chāndogya Upaniṣad) is used to express this metaphysical identity (WWR §66 caption in WP). [source: WP-WWR; WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer]
- Compassion extends to animals (WP-WWR: he condemned religions he thought cruel to animals). Shapshay (2019) reconstructs his ethics around "hope, compassion and animal welfare". [source: WP-WWR; WS-11]
- Freedom: every act is necessitated by character plus motive (*On the Freedom of the Will*, 1839), but our "intelligible character" (a Kantian notion) is a timeless act of will; responsibility attaches to what we *are* (*operari sequitur esse* — Latin tag NOT verified). [source: SEP-S §5.2; IEP-S §2b.i]

## 7. Denial of the will; asceticism
- Seeing through individuation, the saint loses the desire to affirm life; result: renunciation, voluntary chastity and poverty, tranquillity. Prototypes: St Francis (WWR §68), Jesus (§70), and ascetics of every religion. [source: SEP-S §5.3]
- WWR I ends: to those in whom the will has turned and denied itself, "this very real world of ours, with all its suns and galaxies, is – nothing", with a note likening this to the Prajñāpāramitā of the Buddhists (WWR §71). [source: SEP-S §6; WP-WWR] (Translator not named by WP; SEP paraphrases "with all of its suns and galaxies 'is – nothing'".)
- Paradox noted by SEP-S: the denial of will is itself a fierce struggle of will. [source: SEP-S §5.3]
- Russell attacked him for praising asceticism without practising it; Kierkegaard made the same complaint in 1854 (see §9). [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer; WS-13]

## 8. Schopenhauer and Indian thought
- Introduced to Indian thought **late 1813** by the orientalist **Friedrich Majer** (1771–1818) at his mother's Weimar salon; library records: Bhagavad Gītā from December 1813 or soon after, **the Upanishads from March 1814**. (WP: met Majer winter 1813–14; serious study from summer 1814; WP-Anquetil: encountered the *Oupnek'hat* in spring 1814.) [source: SEP-S §4; WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer; WP-Anquetil-Duperron]
- In Dresden (1815–17) his neighbour **K. C. F. Krause**, who knew Sanskrit, introduced him to Indian material in the *Asiatisches Magazin*; WP says Schopenhauer learned meditation from Krause (WP, citing McCoy — weak). [source: SEP-S §4; WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer]
- **The *Oupnek'hat*** (*Oupnek'hat, id est, Secretum tegendum*), Anquetil-Duperron's two-volume Latin translation (with commentary) of the Persian *Sirr-i-Akbar* of 50 Upanishads made for Dārā Shikoh (c. 1657); published Strasbourg **1801–1802** (SEP-S says 1801). Schopenhauer read a Latin version of a Persian version, not the Sanskrit. [source: WP-Anquetil-Duperron; SEP-S §4; research note ancient--upanishads.md §10]
- His claims: WWR preface (1818): access to the Vedas through the Upanishads is "the greatest privilege which this still young century (1818) may claim" and every statement of the Upanishads could be deduced from his thought "though those deductions themselves are by no means to be found there" (WP's English; translator not named). [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer]
- "Solace" passage, *Parerga* II (1851): see Quotations.
- **Buddhism:** WWR II ch. 17 (1844): "If I wished to take the results of my philosophy as the standard of truth, I should have to concede to Buddhism pre-eminence over the others" and he claimed "in my philosophizing I have certainly not been under its influence", because up to 1818 few accounts of Buddhism existed in Europe (WP's English; translator not named, probably Payne). Correspondence with three of the Four Noble Truths (life is suffering, desire its cause, extinction of desire liberation) is noted by Janaway. [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer]
- Accuracy problems: Buddhist nirvāṇa is not equivalent to his denial of the will (WP, citing Holder); WP also notes Urs App's transcriptions of Schopenhauer's 1815–16 notes on Buddhism, which show earlier interest than he admitted. [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer]
- He sometimes read Chinese and Neo-Confucian sources too (Zhu Xi, in *On the Will in Nature*, 1836). [source: SEP-S §1]
- His admiration had limits and prejudice: *Parerga* passage holding that "the highest civilization and culture, apart from the ancient Hindus and Egyptians, are found exclusively among the white races". [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer, quoting Parerga II] (Use only if the chapter discusses his prejudices; translator not named.)

## 9. Schopenhauer: influence and reception (brief)
- Nietzsche's philosophical awakening came from reading WWR; essay "Schopenhauer as Educator" (*Untimely Meditations*). [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer; SEP-S §8]
- Wagner (above). Tolstoy: letter to Fet, 30 Aug [1869]: "Schopenhauer is the greatest genius among men". [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer] (Year truncated in WP text read; give as "around the time of *War and Peace*".)
- Wittgenstein: early transcendental idealism and traces in the *Tractatus*; later dismissive. [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer; IEP-S intro: his thought "informed the work of … Freud, Ludwig Wittgenstein and, most famously, Friedrich Nietzsche"]
- **Freud: hedge.** IEP-S and SEP-S §8 say his ideas on instinct "reappeared in Freud". Freud (1914, *On the History of the Psycho-Analytic Movement*) reportedly said he worked out repression independently, after Rank showed him a WWR passage (WS-12, second-hand). Say: "Freud acknowledged the resemblance but claimed independence."
- Literature: Hardy, Thomas Mann, Proust, Conrad, Borges, Beckett, Machado de Assis and many others (SEP-S §8 list). Physicists: Schrödinger, Einstein (WP). [source: SEP-S §8; WP]

## 10. "On Women" (1851) and misogyny
- Essay "On Women" (*Über die Weiber*), *Parerga and Paralipomena* II, ch. 27 (Cambridge edition lists it as ch. 27, beginning §363 — WS-8 snippet). Women are fit to be nurses and teachers of children because "they are themselves childish, frivolous and short-sighted … big children all their life long"; deficient in artistic faculty and sense of justice; "woman is by nature meant to obey"; he opposed monogamy and the "Teutonico-Christian" veneration of women. He allowed women more sober judgment than men and more sympathy for suffering. [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer] **Translator of the English wording not established** (it matches 19th-c. wording circulated with T. Bailey Saunders's *Studies in Pessimism*, but this could not be checked; WS-8).
- IEP-S notes his view that only men can be geniuses. [source: IEP-S §2a.ii]
- WP: his writings "continue to inspire sexist views today"; he was impressed late in life (1859) by the sculptor Elisabet Ney. [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer]

## 11. Kierkegaard: life
- Born **5 May 1813, Copenhagen**, youngest of seven children of **Michael Pedersen Kierkegaard** (1756–1838) and Ane (Anne) Sørensdatter Lund (1768–1834), who had been a maid in the household. Father: born poor in Jutland, summoned to Copenhagen aged 12 to work for an uncle in the cloth trade; became wealthy; devout, linked to the Moravian congregation; retired young to study theology and philosophy. [source: SEP-K §1; IEP-K §1a; WP-Søren_Kierkegaard]
- Father's "melancholy" and the story that he believed his family cursed because he had cursed God as a cold, hungry shepherd boy — **speculative**; WP: "based on a speculative interpretation" of journal fragments ("The Great Earthquake"); five of seven children died before the father, but Søren and Peter (later bishop of Aalborg) survived him. [source: SEP-K §1; WP-Søren_Kierkegaard]
- Mother never mentioned in his writings (IEP-K); Carlisle's 2019 biography gives her unusual attention (WS-11). [source: IEP-K §1a; WS-11]
- University of Copenhagen: theology degree (July 1840); **Magister dissertation *On the Concept of Irony with Continual Reference to Socrates*** — defended 29 Sept 1841 (7½ hours), degree 20 Oct 1841; written in Danish by royal permission (only the third such), with Latin theses; Magister later counted as a doctorate. Completed the pastoral seminary but **never ordained**. [source: SEP-K §1; IEP-K §1c; WP-Søren_Kierkegaard]
- **Regine Olsen:** met 8 May 1837; engaged **8 (or 10) September 1840** when she was 18 (WP: proposed 8 Sept); returned the ring 11 Aug 1841; final break 11 Oct 1841; then fled to Berlin, where he heard Schelling's lectures (IEP-K adds that "Karl Marx, Jacob Burckhardt and other luminaries" also audited them — not confirmed elsewhere; omit the names). Reasons unknown: his "melancholy", something "spectral" about him; tried to make her break it by posing as a scoundrel. She married Johan Frederik Schlegel (later Governor of the Danish West Indies). He left her his estate; her husband declined it except personal items. [source: SEP-K §1; WP-Søren_Kierkegaard; IEP-K §1b]
- Inheritance c. 31,000 rigsdaler funded his writing. [source: WP-Søren_Kierkegaard (Garff)]
- First book: *From the Papers of One Still Living* (1838), a review of H. C. Andersen's *Only a Fiddler*. [source: IEP-K §1c; SEP-K chronology]
- **Corsair affair 1845–46:** P. L. Møller's article (22 Dec 1845) on *Stages on Life's Way*; Kierkegaard, through the pseudonym Frater Taciturnus, attacked Møller and dared *The Corsair* (editor Meïr Aron Goldschmidt) to satirise him; it did, with caricatures of his uneven trouser legs and appearance. He became reclusive, gave up his "people baths" walks, gave up the idea of becoming a pastor, and began a "second authorship". [source: SEP-K §1; IEP-K §1e; WP-Søren_Kierkegaard]
- **Attack upon Christendom:** after Bishop J. P. Mynster's death (Jan 1854) H. L. Martensen called him "a witness to the truth"; Kierkegaard attacked the Danish state church in *Fædrelandet* (21 articles from Dec 1854) and his own broadsheet *Øjeblikket* (*The Moment/The Instant*), ten issues May–Sept 1855, in the name of "New Testament Christianity". [source: SEP-K §1 & chronology; IEP-K §1f]
- Collapsed in the street — IEP-K: "On September 28th 1855 Kierkegaard collapsed in the street. A few days later he was admitted to Frederiksberg Hospital" (the hospital was Frederiks Hospital per WP); died **11 November 1855**, aged 42. Cause uncertain: SEP-K "a tentative diagnosis … tuberculosis of the spine"; WP: possibly Pott disease. Told his friend Emil Boesen he still believed in Christ. At his funeral his nephew Henrik Lund protested against burial by the church he had attacked. [source: SEP-K §1; IEP-K §1f; WP-Søren_Kierkegaard]
- Kierkegaard read Schopenhauer in 1854 (journals NB29–NB32), found an "uncannily similar" voice but criticised him for not living the asceticism he praised (Patrick Stokes, "Kierkegaard's Uncanny Encounter with Schopenhauer, 1854"). [source: WS-13, second-hand — good bridge between the two halves of the chapter; verify before quoting]

## 13. Pseudonymity — why, and the attribution debate
- Kierkegaard asked that quotations from the pseudonymous works be attributed to the pseudonyms, not to him (CUP I 627, "A First and Last Explanation"). [source: SEP-K §1]
- Purpose: **indirect communication**. Ethical and religious truths cannot be passed on directly; the reader must appropriate ("reduplicate") them. Pseudonyms *embody* life-views, like characters in a novel. [source: SEP-K §1; IEP-K §3b]
- *The Point of View*: he claims he was "from first to last a religious author" — contested; critics (Fenger, Mackey, Garff) say it is retrospective self-presentation; Kierkegaard himself admits the unity was not planned from the start. [source: SEP-K §1]
- **Debate:** (a) Strong separation: Louis Mackey (1986) — "S. Kierkegaard" is "ultimately just another pseudonym" (SEP-K: "a minority view"); Roger Poole (*Kierkegaard: The Indirect Communication*, 1993) — reading the pseudonyms as Kierkegaard's views is a "blunt reading"; pseudonymous views are "undecidable" (WS-10; SEP-K classes Poole as a proto-deconstructive reader). (b) Moderate: SEP-K (Lippitt & Evans) — "rash to attribute all the things said by the pseudonyms to Kierkegaard", yet one can look for themes common to most pseudonyms (e.g. the spheres) and compare with signed works; Climacus seems closer to Kierkegaard than Judge William; Kierkegaard put his own name on the Climacus title pages as a "hint"; Anti-Climacus is often read as only "weakly" pseudonymous, a voice Kierkegaard endorsed but felt unworthy to claim. (c) IEP-K draws the same line: "Climacus, but not Kierkegaard, proclaims that 'truth is subjectivity'". [source: SEP-K §1; IEP-K §2d; WS-10]
- **Hannay's position: NOT verified** (see NOT VERIFIED).

## 14. The stages / spheres of existence
- Three "existence-spheres" — aesthetic, ethical, religious — with subdivisions (Religiousness A and B) and "boundary zones" (*confinia*): **irony** between aesthetic and ethical, **humour** between ethical and religious. SEP-K calls the three-stage picture "an over-simplification". [source: SEP-K §3; IEP-K §1d]
- "Stages" (*Stadier*, as in *Stages on Life's Way*) vs "spheres": not a developmental psychology; transitions are not automatic but require choice and transformed passion; the aesthetic is never wholly left behind; "spheres" better conveys that they are rival views of what makes life worth living. [source: SEP-K §3]
- **Aesthetic** (A in *Either/Or* I): life lived for "the interesting", fleeing boredom ("Crop Rotation"); a spectator's view of life; two seducers — Don Juan (immediate, sensuous, best expressed in music) and Johannes the Seducer (reflective, manipulative); ends in despair. [source: SEP-K §3.1]
- **Ethical** (Judge William): commitment, marriage as love preserved in time, social roles (Hegelian *Sittlichkeit*); "choose despair", "choose choice itself"; the self "chooses itself — or, more correctly, receives itself". [source: SEP-K §3.2]
- **Religious:** Religiousness A (immanent; "an absolute relation to the absolute", resignation, suffering, guilt; possible without revelation) vs Religiousness B (Christianity, resting on a transcendent revelation, the paradox of the God-man). [source: SEP-K §3.2–3.3.1; IEP-K §2d]
- MacIntyre's objection (*After Virtue*, 1981): the choice between aesthetic and ethical is "criterionless"; Kierkegaard scholars "almost unanimous" in rejecting it; debate over "narrative unity". [source: SEP-K §3.2]

## 15. Fear and Trembling: the argument, step by step
Text: Genesis 22 (the *akedah*). Author Johannes de silentio claims he cannot understand Abraham. [source: SEP-K §3.3.1]
Structure: "Attunement" (four "sub-Abrahams" who obey but fall short); "Preliminary Expectoration" (knight of infinite resignation vs knight of faith; the lad and the princess); three *Problemata*; Epilogue. [source: SEP-K §3.3.1; WP-Fear_and_Trembling]
Problema I — "Is there a teleological suspension of the ethical?":
1. "The ethical as such is the universal": it applies to everyone at all times and can be stated in public, shareable terms. [SEP-K; WP-FT]
2. Ethically, Abraham's willingness to kill Isaac is murder ("indistinguishable from murder", IEP-K). [IEP-K §2b]
3. The **tragic hero** — Agamemnon (Iphigenia), Jephthah (his daughter), Brutus (his sons) — also kills his child, but for a higher *ethical* end (state, vow, law) that everyone can understand; he stays within the universal and can be wept for. [SEP-K; WP-FT; IEP-K]
4. Abraham has no such end: his only "justification" is his private, absolute relation to God; he cannot explain himself (Problema III: his silence). His faith is "a purely personal virtue" (FT 52, Walsh tr.). [SEP-K §3.3.1]
5. So **either** the single individual can stand in an absolute relation to God higher than the universal (a teleological suspension of the ethical: the ethical suspended for a *telos* beyond it) — the "paradox" of faith — **or** Abraham is lost and not "the father of faith". Silentio: faith is this paradox. [SEP-K; WP-FT]
6. Faith is a *double movement*: infinite resignation (giving Isaac up) plus trust "by virtue of the absurd" that he will receive Isaac back **in this life**; the true measure of Abraham's faith is "less his willingness to sacrifice Isaac than his trust and hope that he will 'receive him back'". [source: SEP-K §3.3.1]
- What is "the ethical" here? Most scholars: Hegelian *Sittlichkeit*; others argue Kantian (Pojman, Hampson), Fichtean (Kosch) or Hegelian (Evans, Walsh, Westphal). [source: SEP-K §3.2]
**Objections**
- *Fanaticism / how to tell God's voice from delusion:* IEP-K: "how do we distinguish the voice of God from, say, a delusional hallucination? The answer, which induces fear and trembling, is that we can only do so by faith." [IEP-K §2b] **Kant** rejected the literal Abraham story (Wood 2020, cited by SEP-KR); the Kantian line: no divine command can contradict the moral law, so such a command cannot be from God (WP-FT summary). (Kant's own sentence in *Conflict of the Faculties* 1798 NOT verified — see below.) **Buber**, *Eclipse of God* (1952), worried about mistaking demonic impulses (Moloch) for God's voice (WP-FT; WS-7). **Sartre**, *Existentialism Is a Humanism* (1946): Abraham must still decide whether the voice is really an angel's. [source: WP-FT; WP-Søren_Kierkegaard]
- **Levinas**, "Existence and Ethics" (1963; in *Proper Names*, tr. Michael B. Smith, 1996): the suspension of the ethical is a form of violence; he reverses Kierkegaard, holding ethics is not impersonal universality but responsibility to the singular other. Quotation (WP; translator presumably Smith): "Kierkegaardian violence begins when existence is forced to abandon the ethical stage in order to embark on the religious stage …". [source: WP-FT; WP-Søren_Kierkegaard]
- *Irrationalism:* faith "by virtue of the absurd" cannot be told apart from delusion (MacIntyre-style criticism, Simmons per WP-FT, citation-needed there).
**Replies**
- Silentio does not *recommend* imitating Abraham: he says he cannot understand him; attributing his views to Kierkegaard is "a dangerous move" (SEP-K). The four sub-Abrahams warn against reading the book as simply about obedience. [SEP-K §3.3.1]
- The point may be about trust and receiving the finite back (faith as hope), not about licensing violence; the ram replaces Isaac. [SEP-K §3.3.1]
- "Absurd" / "contradiction" (*Modsigelse*) meant tension or incongruity, not logical contradiction (Evans; Lippitt) — answer to the irrationalist charge. [SEP-K §3.3.1; WP-FT]
- Biographical reading: knight of faith as Kierkegaard's hope of receiving Regine back — popular but disputed. [WP-FT]
- Derrida (*The Gift of Death*, 1992) generalises Abraham's situation: every responsible decision sacrifices other obligations. [WP-FT]

## 16. Anxiety, despair, subjectivity, the leap
- ***The Concept of Anxiety*** (1844, Vigilius Haufniensis), ostensibly on hereditary sin: anxiety is "an awareness of freedom", a "sympathetic antipathy and an antipathetic sympathy" (CA 42); "anxiety is the dizziness of freedom" (CA 61, Thomte tr.); sin comes by a "leap", never by necessity; learning "to be anxious in the right way" is learning "the ultimate" (CA 155). Kosch (2006) links this to Kant/Fichte/Schelling debates on freedom. [source: SEP-K §2]
- ***The Sickness unto Death*** (1849, Anti-Climacus): the self is not a substance but a task — a synthesis of finite/infinite, temporal/eternal, necessity/possibility, "a relation that relates itself to itself" (SEP-Ex, Hong 1849 [1989 tr.] p. 43 — note SEP-Ex cites a 1989 translation, i.e. Hannay's Penguin) and must rest "transparently" in the power that established it (God). **Despair** = failing to become a self: despair of possibility (lost in imagination) and of necessity (fatalism) — "Possibility is … like oxygen". Forms: not knowing one has a self, not willing to be oneself, willing defiantly to be oneself. Despair over forgiveness of sins is itself sin. [source: SEP-K §§2, 3.3.3; IEP-K §4b; SEP-Ex]
- **Truth is subjectivity** (*Postscript*, Climacus): objective reflection "makes the subject accidental" (1941 tr., p. 173); subjective truth is "the highest truth attainable" (p. 182) — Swenson–Lowrie 1941 translation, as cited by SEP-Ex. Definition (Swenson–Lowrie wording, WS-6): "An objective uncertainty held fast in an appropriation-process of the most passionate inwardness is the truth" (exact wording not checked; Hong wording differs). Climacus insists a "system of existence" is possible only for God (CUP I 118–125). Climacus revokes the book at the end. [source: SEP-Ex; SEP-K §4; IEP-K §2d; WS-6]
- *Philosophical Fragments*: Socratic recollection vs a divine teacher who supplies the condition; the incarnation as "the Absolute Paradox"; faith not based on historical evidence. [source: SEP-K §3.3.1; IEP-K §2c]
- **"Leap of faith" is not Kierkegaard's phrase.** He speaks often of a "leap" (*Spring*) and of transition (*Overgang*), but Alastair McKinnon's search of the *Samlede Værker* (and Mariele Nientied's) found no occurrence of the Danish equivalent of "leap of faith". [source: WS-1 (McKinnon, *Kierkegaardiana*; Nientied, *Ágora*); WP-Leap_of_faith: "he never used the term 'leap of faith', but instead referred to a 'qualitative leap'"] The leap appears in *Concept of Anxiety* (sin enters by a leap) and the *Postscript* (Lessing's "ugly broad ditch", Jacobi). [SEP-K §2; WP-Leap_of_faith] IEP-K itself uses the phrase "leap of faith" descriptively — fine as a modern label if flagged.
- Social critique: "the public" as a "monstrous abstraction" and "phantom" created by the press (TA 90); "levelling"; the "crowd". [source: SEP-K §2; IEP-K §3c]

## 17. Kierkegaard and Hegel
- Danish Hegelianism: **J. L. Heiberg** (credited with introducing Hegel to Denmark, though Steffens lectured earlier) and **H. L. Martensen** (theology professor, later Bishop) were the main local Hegelians; Kierkegaard's teachers F. C. Sibbern and Poul Martin Møller resisted Hegelianism. Heiberg's review of *Either/Or* turned Kierkegaard against him; *Prefaces* and the *Postscript* satirise Heiberg and Martensen. [source: IEP-K §§1c–d, 2c]
- IEP-K: "Hegel himself comes in for much less criticism, and much more positive endorsement, in Kierkegaard's work than is commonly assumed. It is the Christian Hegelianism of Danish intellectuals that is the main target." [source: IEP-K §1d]
- **Stewart's revisionist thesis** (*Kierkegaard's Relations to Hegel Reconsidered*, 2003 — "note the plural"): many targets were Danish Hegelians who misapplied Hegel; three periods (open to Hegel up to *Either/Or*; most polemical *Fear and Trembling*–*Postscript*; criticism waning after *Works of Love*, with positive Hegelian influence in *Sickness unto Death*'s method). It overturned **Niels Thulstrup**'s *Kierkegaard's Relation to Hegel* (1967), long thought to show Kierkegaard took little from Hegel. Critics say Stewart narrows the gap too much (philosophy vs religion; edification: Hegel "philosophy should beware of the wish to be edifying" vs *Either/Or*'s "Only the truth that edifies is truth for you"). [source: SEP-K §4]
- Also Hegelian-style elements: dialectical view of the self as "spirit" (SEP-K §2); SEP-RM: he "used Hegelian categories of thought himself" in the stages. [source: SEP-K §2; SEP-RM]

## 18. Kierkegaard: reception
- Largely unknown outside Denmark until Georg Brandes's lectures and book (1877/1879). [source: WP-Søren_Kierkegaard; WP-FT]
- "Father of existentialism" — label SEP-K says "obscures at least as much as it reveals". Influenced Heidegger (*das Man* echoes "the public"/"the crowd"), Sartre, Jaspers, Marcel, Levinas; SEP-Ex: existentialism's groundwork laid by Kierkegaard and Nietzsche. [source: SEP-K intro, §1; SEP-Ex; SEP-RM; IEP-K §3c]
- Theology: Karl Barth's *Epistle to the Romans* (1919/1922) — "infinite qualitative distinction"; dialectical theology circle (Barth, Brunner, Bultmann); Buber, Ebner, Rosenzweig. [source: WP-Søren_Kierkegaard]
- Wittgenstein: called him "by far the most profound thinker" of the 19th century and "a saint". [source: SEP-K intro]
- Psychiatry: Binswanger, Laing, Rollo May. Japan: translated into Japanese before English; influence on Nishida Kitarō. [source: SEP-K §1] — global cross-ref to Kyoto School chapter.
- Critics: Adorno, Levinas; Sartre accepted his analysis of anguish but not his God. [source: WP-Søren_Kierkegaard]
- Cross-refs: *Existentialism: Sartre, Beauvoir and Camus*; *Phenomenology: Husserl, Heidegger*; *The Kyoto School*; *God: Arguments For and Against*; *The Meaning of Life*; *German Idealism*; *Nietzsche* (Schopenhauer as educator).

## Debates
- **Is the will the thing in itself, or only how it appears "to us"?** Dogmatic-metaphysical reading vs Kantian reading vs SEP-S (Wicks): both need qualification; Young (1987): thing in itself not wholly identical with will. [source: SEP-S §§6–7; IEP-S refs]
- **How much did Indian thought shape Schopenhauer, and how well did he understand it?** (a) He himself claimed his system was independent of Buddhism (to 1818) yet in deep agreement; (b) **Urs App** (*Schopenhauer's Compass*, 2014; earlier articles) argues the Latin *Oupnek'hat* — itself a Persian, partly Sufi-coloured rendering — was crucial to the early formation of the metaphysics of will (reviewer: "convincing case"), while also arguing his understanding of Indian philosophy "was for the most part not accurate"; an App 1998 article called the idea of (Buddhist) influence a "misconception … carefully nourished by Schopenhauer himself" (WS-4 snippets; second-hand — attribute cautiously); App's transcriptions of 1815–16 notes show earlier interest in Buddhism than Schopenhauer admitted (WP); (c) **Douglas Berger** (*The Veil of Maya*, 2004) makes the strongest recent case for substantial Indian influence, based on the published works (WS-4); (d) **Stephen Cross** (2013) mainly analyses parallels (Advaita, Madhyamaka, Yogācāra) rather than influence (WS-5); Barua (ed. 2008) separates historical influence from philosophical comparison (WS-4). Also: Buddhist nirvāṇa ≠ his denial of the will (WP, Holder). Present as a debate between "influence", "confirmation" and "parallel" readings. [source: WS-4; WS-5; WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer; SEP-S §4]
- **Pseudonymity**: Mackey/Poole (strong separation; Mackey "minority view") vs SEP-K's moderate method vs readings taking *The Point of View* at its word (contested by Fenger, Garff). [source: SEP-K §1; WS-10]
- **Kierkegaard and Hegel**: Thulstrup (1967) vs Stewart (2003); critics of Stewart. [source: SEP-K §4]
- **The ethical in *Fear and Trembling***: Kantian / Fichtean / Hegelian. [SEP-K §3.2]
- **Fideist or irrationalist?** Shestov (applauds), Pojman (decries) vs Evans/Lippitt (paradox = incongruity, not logical contradiction). [SEP-K §3.3.1]
- **MacIntyre's "criterionless choice"** charge and the narrative-unity debate. [SEP-K §3.2, §4]
- **Christian vs secular readings**: Evans vs "Kierkegaard against Kierkegaard" (Adorno, Theunissen). [SEP-K §4]
- **Biographies**: Lowrie (1938, devoted) vs Garff (2000/2005, critical); Hannay, Backhouse, Carlisle in between. [SEP-K §1]
