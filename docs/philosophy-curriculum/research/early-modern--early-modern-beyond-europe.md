## 1. Framing
- "Early modern" Europe's contemporaries: Wang Yangming (26 Oct 1472 – 9 Jan 1529) [WP-Wang_Yangming; SEP-WYM gives 1472–1529]; Mullā Ṣadrā (c. 1571/2 – c. 1635/40) [WP-Mulla_Sadra; SEP-MS; IEP-MS]; Tokugawa shogunate 1603–1868 (SEP-JC uses "Tokugawa period (1600–1868)"; SEP-JP "Edo period (1600–1868)") [SEP-JC §4; SEP-JP §4.3]; Hatata traditionally dated 1667/1668 [WP-Hatata; WP-Ethiopian_philosophy].
- Tucker (SEP-JC) deliberately calls 17th–early-19th-century Japanese Confucianism "early modern", "emphasizing its role in establishing the foundations of modernity" rather than "late medieval" or "feudalistic". [SEP-JC §4]

## 2. Wang Yangming (1472–1529)

### Life
- Family name Wang, personal name Shouren, courtesy name Bo'an; "Yangming" from a nickname taken when living in the Yangming Grotto of Kuaiji Mountain. Born 1472 near Hangzhou in Zhejiang, son of a successful official. [SEP-WYM §1] WP: born Yuyao, Zhejiang; father Wang Hua placed first (*zhuangyuan*) in the 1481 examination. [WP-Wang_Yangming]
- Age 17 (1489): conversation with a Daoist priest; long torn between Daoism, Buddhism and Confucianism. [SEP-WYM §1]
- Passed the highest (*jinshi*) examination in **1499** (*juren* 1492 per WP). [SEP-WYM §1; WP-Wang_Yangming]
- **1506**: memorial protesting a eunuch's imprisonment of officials; Wang publicly beaten and exiled to a minor post in what is now Guizhou. (WP names the eunuch Liu Jin in connection with his father's dismissal; IEP: exiled 1507–1510.) [SEP-WYM §1; IEP-WYM intro; WP-Wang_Yangming]
- **1508**: "deep philosophical awakening" in Guizhou. [SEP-WYM §1] The place-name **Longchang** for this awakening appears only in a WebSearch summary ("his earlier breakthroughs in Longchang and Nanchang") [WS-6] — weak; safe to say "at his place of exile in Guizhou, in 1508".
- Poem on the awakening (Ivanhoe 2009, 181): "Everyone has within an unerring compass; / The root and source of the myriad transformations lies in the mind. / I laugh when I think that, earlier, I saw things the other way around; / Following branches and leaves, I searched outside!" [SEP-WYM §1]
- IEP-NC quotes Tu Weiming on the realization: "My own nature is, of course, sufficient for me to attain sagehood. And I have been mistaken in searching for the li [principle] i[n external things]…" (quote truncated in my extraction; use only the first sentence). [IEP-NC §5c]
- 1510 term in Guizhou ended; the eunuch executed; career resumed with high civil and military posts; accused of conspiring with a rebel he had himself put down. [SEP-WYM §1]
- Military: suppressed revolts in Jiangxi/Fujian/Guangdong 1517–18; defeated the **Prince of Ning rebellion (1519)**. Made an earl. [WP-Wang_Yangming] Last campaign against rebellion 1527; died soon after, **1529**. [SEP-WYM §1] Died at Nan'an, Jiangxi, 9 Jan 1529. [WP-Wang_Yangming]
- Deathbed words: "'This mind' is luminous and bright. What more is there to say?" [SEP-WYM §1]
- Posthumous: sacrifice offered to him in the Confucian Temple in 1584 ("the highest honour for a scholar"). [WP-Wang_Yangming]
- Works: the *Record for Practice* / *Chuanxi lu* 傳習錄 (Chan's title: *Instructions for Practical Living*), sayings/letters/dialogues compiled by disciples; "Inquiry on the Great Learning" (*Daxue wen*), recorded 1527 as a primer for new disciples. [SEP-WYM §1; IEP-WYM §1; WP-Wang_Yangming (Translations)]
- Translations: Henke 1916 (Chan thought it poor, per WP); **Wing-tsit Chan, *Instructions for Practical Living and Other Neo-Confucian Writings by Wang Yang-ming* (Columbia UP, 1963)** — full *Chuanxi lu* and *Daxue wen*; Julia Ching, *Philosophical Letters* (1972/1973); Ivanhoe 2009. [SEP-WYM Bibliography; IEP-WYM §6; WP-Wang_Yangming]

### The bamboo episode (verified; how it is told)
- Source: Wang's own recollection in the *Chuanxi lu*, quoted by SEP-WYM "Translation modified from Chan 1963, 249". In it Wang and **a friend named Qian** resolved to "investigate all the things in the world": Wang pointed to the bamboos in front of the pavilion; **Qian** investigated "day and night", fell ill on the **third** day; Wang then tried himself "from morning till night", was "unable to find the Pattern of the bamboos", and fell ill on the **seventh** day; "we sighed to each other and said that it was impossible to be a sage or a worthy". [SEP-WYM §1]
- Popular retellings (e.g. WP-Yangmingism; SEP-ZX §4) tell it as Wang alone staring for seven days — simplification; the friend Qian is in the text.
- Age/date: WP-Yangmingism places it after Wang (aged about 18) met the Cheng-Zhu scholar Lou Liang. Exact year NOT verified (often given as 1492) — avoid a year.
- Did Wang apply Zhu Xi's method correctly? SEP-WYM: "it is unclear"; Zhu stressed studying the classics as the best way to learn Pattern. [SEP-WYM §§1, 4] Qian Mu: Zhu's investigations were fruitful because he had questions/hypotheses in mind; Wang's bamboo-gazing was "bland looking" with no hypothesis. [SEP-ZX §4 (Qian 1986)] Cross-ref "Chinese Buddhism and the Rise of Neo-Confucianism" (its notes already cite this).

### Heart-mind is principle (xin ji li)
- "The mind is Pattern. Is there any affair outside the mind? Is there any Pattern outside the mind?" (TVN 264). [SEP-WYM §3] IEP: "the mind is principle" (*xin ji li*); li is not a searchable entity in the mind but "coextensive with the mind". [IEP-WYM §2]
- Wang revives Lu Xiangshan (1139–1193); Lu and Wang = "Lu–Wang school" / School of Mind / Learning of the Heartmind (*xinxue*), vs Cheng–Zhu "Learning of Pattern" (*lixue*). This two-school picture is partly a later historians' construction ("Later historians also noticed…"). [SEP-SMC §2.3; SEP-WYM §6]
- Context: Cheng-Zhu learning was the examination curriculum; under the Yongle reign (1403–24) Hu Guang (1370–1418) and others compiled the official *Great Compendia* of Zhu Xi's commentaries; Wang attacks "vulgar learning" (memorization, literary composition, philology) in "Pulling up the root and stopping up the source" in the *Chuanxi lu*. [IEP-WYM §1]
- Wang's populist ethos: "All the people filling the street are sages." [IEP-WYM §2 (Chan's wording presumably)]

### Liangzhi (pure / good / innate knowing)
- Term from *Mengzi* 7A15 (babes in arms "know to love their parents"; know "without pondering"); Lu had already used it. SEP translates "pure knowing"; SEP-SMC: "most literally … 'pure knowledge' or 'good knowledge' … more loosely 'innate knowledge' or 'conscience'". [SEP-WYM §2; SEP-SMC §2.3]
- Wang reads the *Great Learning*'s "extending knowledge" as extending the "pure knowing" of one's own mind (TVN 248). Nivison: Wang's extending "is more like extending one's arm than, say, extending one's vocabulary" (Nivison 1996a, 225). [SEP-WYM §4]
- Wang reinterprets *ge wu* as "rectifying things" (one's thoughts and their objects), against Zhu's "reaching things"/investigating their Pattern. [SEP-WYM §4]
- IEP's list: everyone possesses liangzhi; innate, not learned; never lost, only lost sight of; intuitive (mirror and balance images); obscured by selfish desires. [IEP-WYM §2]
- Critic Luo Qinshun: identifying mind with principle is "an infinitesimal mistake in the beginning leading to an infinite error at the end" (risk of subjectivism). Wang's reply resource: distinction between "mind in itself" (*xin zhi benti*) and "human mind" (*renxin*) clouded by selfish desire. [IEP-WYM §2]
- Metaphysics of "one body": the benevolent see Heaven, Earth and the myriad things as one body; argument from the child at the well → birds and beasts → plants → "tiles and stones broken" (TVN 242). Van Norden's three objections (psychopathy; not everyone cares about stones; evolutionary explanations). [SEP-WYM §5]

### ARGUMENT: the unity of knowing and acting (zhi xing he yi) — step by step
Sources: SEP-WYM §§3–4 (TVN translations, page numbers TVN 267–68); IEP-WYM §4 (Chan's translation via IEP).
1. Background (Zhu Xi): knowledge and action "always need each other … like how eyes cannot walk without feet, but feet cannot see without eyes … knowledge comes first. But … action is what is important" (TVN 180–81); Sincerity (*cheng*) bridges them. So, for Zhu, one can know the good and fail to do it (akrasia is possible). [SEP-WYM §4]
2. Wang's thesis: "There never have been people who know but do not act. Those who 'know' but do not act simply do not yet know" (TVN 267). IEP/Chan wording: "There have never been people who know but do not act. Those who are supposed to know but do not act simply do not yet know." [SEP-WYM §3; IEP-WYM §4]
3. Premise (verbal assent is not knowledge): "One cannot say he knows filial piety or brotherly respect simply because he knows how to say something filial or brotherly." Knowledge of pain, cold, hunger requires having experienced them. [SEP-WYM §3]
4. Premise (Great Learning analogy): loving the good is "like loving a lovely sight", hating evil "like hating a hateful odor". "Seeing a lovely sight is a case of knowing, while loving a lovely sight is a case of acting. As soon as one sees that lovely sight, one naturally loves it. It is not as if you first see it and only then, intentionally, you decide to love it. … Consider the case of a person with a stuffed–up nose. … the smell does not reach him, and so he does not hate it. This is simply not to know the hateful odor." (TVN 267) The phrase *hao hao se* has connotations of finding someone sexually attractive. [SEP-WYM §§3–4]
5. Conclusion: genuine (moral) knowing already includes the motivation/response that is acting; apparent cases of "knowing but not doing" are cases of not (yet) knowing.
- **The objection, as Wang's interlocutor put it** (IEP/Chan): "there are people who know that parents should be served with filial piety and elder brothers with respect but cannot put these things into practice. This shows that knowledge and action are clearly two different things." [IEP-WYM §4] (The modern version: the plagiarist who says "I knew it was wrong but gave in to temptation" — SEP's example.) [SEP-WYM §3]
- **Wang's reply** (IEP/Chan): "The knowledge and action you refer to are already separated by selfish desires and are no longer knowledge and action in their original state." [IEP-WYM §4]
- **Pragmatic reply** (SEP calls it perhaps Wang's "most compelling"): speaking of knowing and acting separately is legitimate only as remedy — for those who act "blindly and recklessly" stress knowing; for the "vague and irresolute" who "engage in speculation while suspended in a vacuum" stress acting (TVN 268). Followers of Zhu "separate knowing and acting into two distinct tasks … think that one must first know and only then can one act", becoming bookworms. "My current teaching regarding the unity of knowing and acting is a medicine directed precisely at this disease." [SEP-WYM §3]
- **Counter-objections (Van Norden in SEP):** (a) the pain example shows at most that one must once have experienced good/evil to understand the words, not that knowledge now motivates; (b) the odour/beauty examples show at most that knowing requires *some* motivation, which need not issue in action (finding someone attractive doesn't always lead to pursuit). [SEP-WYM §3]
- **Interpretive note (a debate):** SEP-WYM opening: the doctrine "can be interpreted as a denial of the possibility of weakness of will" (an interpretation, not Wang's term). IEP-WYM reads "knowledge" as knowing how to respond to a situation and "action" as all responses to a situation (including studying). IEP: "If you want to know bitterness, you have to eat a bitter melon yourself." [SEP-WYM intro; IEP-WYM §4]

### Four-Sentence Teaching (si ju jiao 四句教)
- Chinese (WP-Yangmingism): 無善無惡心之體，有善有惡意之動；知善知惡是良知，為善去惡是格物. [WP-Yangmingism]
- Tu Weiming's translation of the first three lines (via WS-6): "There is neither good nor evil in the mind-in-itself. There are both good and evil in the activation of intentions. Knowing good and evil is the [faculty] of liang-chih." Fourth line (my literal gloss of the Chinese, not a quotation): doing good and removing evil is *gewu* ("rectifying things"). [WS-6 (Tu Weiming, *Eastern Buddhist* article, otani.repo.nii.ac.jp); WP-Yangmingism]
- Date: **1527**, on the eve of the Guangxi campaign, night conversation with disciples **Qian Dehong** and **Wang Ji** (Longxi) at **Tianquan Bridge**; Qian read it as complete teaching for all; Wang Ji as a provisional teaching, pressing the "no good and no evil" line. [WS-6] WP-Yangmingism says 1528 — conflict; use 1527 (two independent web sources) but flag as WebSearch-only.

### Influence and critics
- Lu–Wang school one of two major wings of Neo-Confucianism; SEP-SMC: "After Zhu Xi, the second most influential Neo-Confucian philosopher". [SEP-WYM §6; SEP-SMC §2.3]
- Disciples' disputes; **Taizhou school** founded by Wang Gen (1483–1541), who studied with Wang: sagehood in "everyday practices of the common people"; Luo Rufang (1515–1588) and Li Zhi (1527–1602) (child/infant heartmind; defended women's education; Luo thought women more reliably virtuous). [SEP-SMC §2.4] Periergia's Ming–Qing chapter: Li Zhi "building on Wang Yangming" — consistent.
- **Japan (Yōmeigaku / Ōyōmeigaku):** Nakae Tōju (1608–1648) and Kumazawa Banzan (1619–1691) [SEP-WYM §6; SEP-JC §4]; Nakae, "the Sage of Ōmi", regarded as founder of Japanese Yōmeigaku; called conscience "the divine light of heaven" [WP-Nakae_Tōju] (WP's body text misprints 1846/1848 for 1646/1648 — ignore). Ōshio Heihachirō (1793–1837), Osaka *yoriki*, ran a Yōmeigaku academy (Senshindō) and led the 1837 Osaka rising [WP-Ōshio_Heihachirō] (Periergia's Tokugawa chapter calls him "a former official and Confucian scholar" — consistent; add Yōmeigaku). Wang "an inspiration for some of the leaders of the Meiji Restoration". [SEP-WYM §6] WP lists Saigō Takamori and Takasugi Shinsaku among Yōmeigaku-influenced; Admiral Tōgō's seal "One's whole life followed the example of Yangming". [WP-Wang_Yangming] (WP-only.)
- **Korea:** SEP-SMC: "tremendous influence of Zhu Xi and Wang Yangming in Korea, Japan, and Vietnam". [SEP-SMC §7] Specific Korean Yangming figures (Jeong Je-du / Hagok, Ganghwa school) NOT verified (WP page not retrieved; SEP-Korean Confucianism does not discuss them in my grep). Say only that Wang's teaching reached Korea, where Zhu Xi orthodoxy remained dominant (latter half NOT verified — avoid).
- **Critics / reaction:**
  - Luo Qinshun (above). [IEP-WYM §2]
  - Late-Ming/early-Qing thinkers c. 1640–1700: Gu Yanwu (1613–1682), leading scholar of "evidential studies" (*kaozheng*); Wang Fuzhi (1619–1692), reconceived Neo-Confucian metaphysics against li–qi dualism; Huang Zongxi (1610–1695), wrote the intellectual histories (*Records of Ming Scholars*) and radical political proposals. [SEP-SMC §2.5]
  - Qing evidential scholars "generally looked with disdain on 'the Song–Ming Confucians' (including both Zhu Xi and Wang Yangming), whom they accused of producing 'empty words'". [SEP-WYM §6] SEP-SMC: they saw Zhu and Wang "as too reliant on abstract speculation and personal introspection". [SEP-SMC §7]
  - Dai Zhen (1724–1777 per SEP-WYM; SEP-ZX gives 1723–77) criticised both schools: (1) treating subjective opinions as an infallible moral sense, (2) projecting Buddhist-inspired concepts onto the classics, (3) ignoring the value of ordinary desires. [SEP-WYM §6]
  - Wang Fuzhi's specific charge that Wang was more Buddhist than Confucian: WP-Yangmingism only (unsourced) — use cautiously or not at all. IEP-NC: after 1644 "a tremendous cultural backlash against the radical thinkers of the late Ming". [IEP-NC §5]
  - Chan's 1962 article "How Buddhistic Is Wang Yang-ming?" shows the Buddhism charge is a scholarly debate. [SEP-WYM Bibliography]
- Modern: Mou Zongsan (1909–1995) and many New Confucians hold Wang understood Confucianism more deeply than Zhu. [SEP-WYM §6] Mainland Marxist scholars interpreted Wang as "subjective idealism". [IEP-WYM §5]

## 3. Mullā Ṣadrā and the "School of Isfahan"

### Name, dates
- Ṣadr al-Dīn Muḥammad b. Ibrāhīm b. Yaḥyā Qawāmī Shīrāzī; title Ṣadr al-Mutaʾallihīn ("Master of the theosists" in Rizvi's rendering). [SEP-MS intro]
- **Birth**: Shiraz, "around 979 AH/1571–72", only child of a courtly family. [SEP-MS §1.1] IEP: c. 1572. WP: c. 1571/2.
- **Death**: in Basra on the way to his seventh pilgrimage to Mecca. Traditional date 1050 AH/1640–1, but "no clear evidence"; grandson ʿAlam al-Hudā reports 1045 AH/1635–6 with burial in Najaf; his last works dated 1044 AH/1635. [SEP-MS §1.1] IEP: once believed 1640 (buried Basra); "modern scholarship offers new evidence, though not conclusive evidence" for 1635–6 and Najaf. [IEP-MS §1] → **Write "c. 1571/2 – 1635/6 (traditionally 1640)" or "c. 1571–1636 / 1640"**. Periergia's Ghazālī–Averroes chapter and *What Is Philosophy?* give "c. 1571–1636" — consistent with SEP's preferred date; the Renaissance notes give the range — consistent.
- SEP: "arguably the most significant Islamic philosopher after Avicenna" (matches Periergia's Ghazālī–Averroes line). [SEP-MS intro] Leaman (via WP): perhaps "the single most important and influential philosopher in the Muslim world in the last four hundred years". [WP-Mulla_Sadra (Leaman 2013, 146)]

### Life
- Moved to Qazvin 1000 AH/1591, then Isfahan 1006 AH/1597 (successive Safavid capitals). [SEP-MS §1.1] (Periergia Safavid chapter: Abbas made Isfahan capital 1598 — near-consistent; SEP says Ṣadrā moved there 1597.)
- Teachers: **Mīr Dāmād** (Mir Muhammad Baqir Damad Astarabadi, d. 1040 AH/1631), philosophy and theology (Avicenna, Bahmanyar, the *Theology of Aristotle*, Suhrawardī); **Shaykh Bahāʾī** (Baha' al-Din ʿAmili, d. 1030 AH/1620–21), leading jurist of Isfahan under Shah ʿAbbās I, scriptural sciences. Mīr Dāmād called Ṣadrā his spiritual son; Bahāʾī called him "son Sadra". [SEP-MS §1.1] (IEP-MS gives Shaykh Bahāʾī "d. 1576" — an error; WP/SEP give 1620–21.)
- **Mīr Findiriskī** (d. 1050 AH/1640–41): SEP: "no evidence" Ṣadrā studied with him, "contrary to what Corbin and others have claimed"; the story that Findiriskī sent him to Mīr Dāmād is "apocryphal". IEP agrees there is "no historical evidence". [SEP-MS §1.1; IEP-MS §1]
- Returned to Shiraz (father died 1010 AH/1601–2); "failing to find an adequate patron and facing the opposition and criticism of a city that had forgotten the value of the study of philosophy", retreated to **Kahak**, a village near Qom; retreat (*khalvat*) "probably five years"; began the *Asfār* there in **1015 AH/1606**. [SEP-MS §§1.1–1.2] IEP: left Shiraz (which he denounced as hostile to philosophy, *Asfār* I 7) "for a life of solitude and contemplation". [IEP-MS §1]
- **"Exile" to Kahak — a debate/caveat:** WP says he "was both condemned and excommunicated by some Shiʿi ʿulamāʾ" and then retired to Kahak; SEP and IEP present it as a retreat after lack of patronage and hostility to philosophy. Write "retreat (some accounts say after condemnation by clerics)", not "exile". [WP-Mulla_Sadra; SEP-MS; IEP-MS]
- Students and sons-in-law: Muḥsin Fayḍ Kāshānī (d. 1090 AH/1680–1) and ʿAbd al-Razzāq Lāhījī (d. 1072 AH/1661–2). [SEP-MS §1.1]
- 1040 AH/1630–1 moved permanently to Shiraz at the request of governor Imāmqulī Khān, teaching at the **Madrasa-yi Khān** (completed 1024 AH/1615, founded by Imāmqulī's father Allāhvirdī Khān expressly for philosophy and science). Completed the *Asfār* in Shiraz **1038 AH/1628**; that year the English traveller Sir Thomas Herbert noted Shiraz's "colledge wherein is read Philosophy, Astrology, Physick, Chemistry and the Mathematicks". [SEP-MS §1.1]
- Works: over 45. Magnum opus *al-Ḥikma al-mutaʿāliya fī-l-asfār al-ʿaqliyya al-arbaʿa* (*Transcendent Wisdom of the Four Journeys of the Intellect*), "the Four Journeys"; also *al-Ḥikma al-ʿarshiyya* (*Wisdom of the Throne*), *al-Shawāhid al-rubūbiyya*, *al-Mashāʿir* (epitome of his doctrine of being), Qur'an commentaries, commentary on Kulaynī's *Uṣūl al-kāfī*. All in Arabic except *Risāla-yi Sih aṣl* (Persian). [SEP-MS §1.2; IEP-MS §1]
- The four journeys (SEP): from this world to God; in God with God; from God to the world; in the world with God. [SEP-MS §1.2] (IEP gives a different mapping of journeys to topics — minor.)
- Definition of philosophy (Rizvi's trans., *Asfār* I: 23): "Know that philosophy is the perfecting of the human soul, by the cognition of the true natures of existents, as they truly are, through judgements concerning them that are ascertained through demonstrations (*barahin*), and not understood through conjecture, or adherence to prior authority, insofar as is humanly possible." [SEP-MS §2.1]

### Metaphysics
- Three doctrines: (1) **primacy/fundamental reality of existence** (*aṣālat al-wujūd*); (2) **gradation/modulation of existence** (*tashkīk al-wujūd*): one single reality differing only "by perfection and deficiency … priority and posteriority … independence and dependence" (Rizvi trans., *Mashāʿir* 1964: 68–9); (3) **substantial motion** (*al-ḥaraka al-jawhariyya*). [SEP-MS §§3.1–3.2]
- IEP's image: reality is "a colorless light while essences are the colourful glasses"; sunlight and candlelight are the same reality in different grades. [IEP-MS §3]
- Ṣadrā's intellectual sources: Avicenna, Suhrawardī's Illuminationism, Ibn ʿArabī's "unity of being" (*waḥdat al-wujūd*); essences "have never smelt the fragrance of being" (citing Ibn ʿArabī). [SEP-MS §3.1; SEP-EE §6]
- Ṣadrā first followed his teacher's essentialism, then changed: "Influenced by his philosophy master, Mulla Sadra started as an advocate of essentialism but soon diverged". [IEP-MS §3] (His own first-person "conversion" sentence exists but wording/translator not verified — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- **Substantial motion:** change is not only in accidents but in substance itself; "The young Zayd is thus literally not the same existent as the old Zayd"; time is "a dimension of existence"; consequence: the world is created in time at every instant — a philosophical account of creation *ex nihilo* in time, against the Avicennan eternity of the world. [SEP-MS §3.2; IEP-MS §6b ("the fourth dimension of the bodily substance")]
- Objections SEP lists to substantial motion: (i) if everything is constantly in motion, what is the subject that undergoes change? (ii) motion would deny the fixity of species boundaries that define essences. Replies: (ii) essences are not fundamental, so their boundaries do not limit existence; (i) "existence is its own subject" — the matter of existence acquires forms as it changes. [SEP-MS §3.2]
- Proof of God, the "way of the veracious" (*manhaj/burhān al-ṣiddīqīn*), as summarized by Rizvi: there is existence; existence is a single simple reality; it is graded in intensity; the scale must have a limit point of greatest intensity; hence God (= pure existence) exists. Ṣadrā criticised Avicenna's version for reasoning from the concept, not the reality, of existence. Critique: Ṭabāṭabāʾī (d. 1981) glossed that it is assertoric, not a demonstration; Rizvi: it "seems to be tautological". [SEP-MS §3.3; IEP-MS §6] (Periergia Falsafa chapter: Ṣadrā objected that Avicenna's proof "begins with concepts" — consistent.)
- **Unity of intellect and intelligible** (*ittiḥād al-ʿāqil wa-l-maʿqūl*): Porphyrian doctrine that Avicenna rejected (soul knows by conjunction, *ittiṣāl*, not union); Ṣadrā defends union using primacy/modulation of existence and substantial motion. [SEP-MS §4.5]
- **Soul**: "corporeal in its origination and spiritual in its survival" (*jismāniyyat al-ḥudūth wa-rūḥāniyyat al-baqāʾ*). [SEP-MS §4.2; IEP-MS §5a]
- **Bodily resurrection**: Avicenna held it an article of faith but logically impossible; Ṣadrā, siding with al-Ghazālī that scepticism about it is unacceptable, reinterprets it: after death the soul, by its imaginative power, creates its own otherworldly (imaginal) body, which is still a body; scripture does not define the resurrected body; rejects metempsychosis. IEP: his picture "is not in complete conformity with that of theology". He quotes Jesus: "One who is not born twice will never reach the heavens of the afterlife." [SEP-MS §§5.1–5.2; IEP-MS §§5, 6c]

### ARGUMENT: primacy of existence over essence — step by step (with the Suhrawardian objection)
1. Every contingent thing is analysed (since Avicenna) into essence (what it is) and existence (that it is). [SEP-EE; IEP-MS §3]
2. If they are distinct, one of the two must be what is real and active in the composite. [SEP-MS §3.1]
3. Essence taken by itself is a universal in the mind with no external effects: "the real horse can give you a ride while the universal horse in the mind is incapable of that"; particularity and real effects come from existence. [IEP-MS §3]
4. An essence "waiting" for existence would have to exist before it exists — absurd ("an existence before existence"). [SEP-MS §3.1]
5. God has no essence; his causal link to the world "can only be existential". [SEP-MS §3.1]
6. Therefore existence is what is real; essences are "mental considerations"/delimitations; "the term 'existent' is applied to essence only with respect to its relation to being" (*Asfār* VI 163, as quoted in IEP). [IEP-MS §3; SEP-EE §6]
- **Objection (Suhrawardī and, following him, Mīr Dāmād — primacy of essence, *aṣālat al-māhiyya*):** existence is "only a mental construct" (IEP); for Suhrawardī existence is a "merely mental consideration" (*iʿtibār*) [IEP-MS §3; SEP-Suh §§4, 6]. A standard post-Avicennan argument (already in ʿUmar al-Khayyām): if existence were real outside the mind, existence itself would have to exist, requiring a second-order existence, and so on ad infinitum. [SEP-EE §5]
- **Reply (as reconstructed from SEP/IEP):** distinguish existence as a concept (a "secondary intelligible", attached to essences in the mind) from existence as a concrete reality; many metaphysical puzzles vanish once this distinction is kept; the reality of existence is simple, has no essence, and is graded, so it does not need a further existence added to it. [SEP-MS §3.1] (SEP-MS does not spell out the regress reply in one sentence; the writer should phrase it as "Ṣadrā's answer, roughly, is…".)
- Context: SEP-EE frames the later history as the debate between primacy of essence (al-Suhrawardī) and primacy of existence (Mullā Ṣadrā), and says Ṣadrā's doctrine "continues to define the ways of Islamic metaphysics in the modern period until our days". [SEP-EE §6]

### The "School of Isfahan" label and the debate
- SEP-MS: "A key figure of a group of thinkers whom **Nasr and Corbin** referred to as the 'School of Isfahan'". IEP: the network "that was later dubbed 'the School of Isfahan'". WP-School_of_Isfahan: name "coined by Seyyed Hossein Nasr and Henry Corbin". Encyclopaedia Iranica (via WS-7): "coined by Henry Corbin (1903–78) and Seyyed Hossein Nasr (1933–) to describe a philosophical and mystical movement patronized by the court of Shah ʿAbbās I". → **Not Corbin alone: Corbin and Nasr.** [SEP-MS intro; IEP-MS §1; WP-School_of_Isfahan; WS-7]
- Debate: Iranica (WS-7) says Corbin and Nasr's approach has been influential while "attracting the criticism of a number of detractors"; Nasr added perennialism, to which Corbin was averse; the label's success produced parallel labels "School of Shiraz" and "School of Tehran". A 2023 Cambridge review of Janis Esots, *Patterns of Wisdom in Safavid Iran*, calls the school "retroactively inaugurated" by Corbin and Nasr, a paradigm "not … met without criticism"; Esots narrows it to Mīr Dāmād, Ṣadrā and Rajab ʿAlī Tabrīzī. [WS-7] SEP-MS: Corbin's claim about Findiriskī is unsupported, and Ṣadrā actually taught mainly in Qom and Shiraz (not Isfahan) [SEP-MS §1.1] — itself a reason to treat "School of Isfahan" as a modern umbrella label.
- Corbin, Izutsu and Nasr introduced Ṣadrā to Western academia in the 1960s–70s "as part of a wider project of reviving 'perennial wisdom'"; Max Horten introduced him earlier (late 19th c.). [IEP-MS §8]
- Safavid context: philosophy patronized at first; growing antagonism to philosophy and mysticism under Shah ʿAbbās II (r. c. 1642–1666). [IEP-MS §1]

### Later influence
- Dominant paradigm in the Shiʿi seminary "since the early 19th century"; revival in Isfahan by ʿAlī Nūrī (d. 1251 AH/1836) and **Hādī Sabzavārī (d. 1289 AH/1873)**, "the 'last great Islamic philosopher'". [SEP-MS §6]
- India: his commentary on Abharī's *Hidāya* became a cornerstone of the rationalist curriculum of Indian madrasas from the 18th century. [SEP-MS §6; IEP-MS §8]
- 20th century: ʿAllāma Ṭabāṭabāʾī (d. 1981); Ayatollah Khomeini and Morteza Motahhari "profoundly influenced"; some call him "philosopher of the Revolution" "despite the distinct lack of an engagement with political philosophy in his work"; Javādī Āmulī's 20-volume commentary on the *Asfār*. [SEP-MS §6]
- Critics: his own son-in-law Lāhījī defended Avicennan essentialism; Ḥusayn and Jamāl Khwānsārī; Mahdī Narāqī (d. 1794); Shaykhī school of Shaykh Aḥmad al-Aḥsāʾī (d. 1826); the *maktab-i tafkīk* (separation school) accuses him of mixing faith with "Greek science". [SEP-MS §6]
- Rizvi's four types of modern readers: traditionalists, "analyticisers", "theosophers", "nativists". [SEP-MS §6]

## 4. Tokugawa Japan (1603–1868)

### Introduction and spread of Neo-Confucianism
- Song Neo-Confucianism reached Japan from the 13th century via Zen monks and long stayed inside Zen monasteries. [SEP-JC §3] Hideyoshi's Korean invasions brought Korean scholar-prisoners; "liberation of Neo-Confucianism from the domination of Zen learning only became possible in the late-sixteenth and early-seventeenth centuries, following" the invasions. [SEP-JC §4.9; SEP-JP §4.3.1]
- Fujiwara Seika (1561–1617 per SEP-JC; 1561–1619 per WP — conflict, give "1561–1619" or omit death), former Zen monk, pioneer. [SEP-JC §4; WP-Hayashi_Razan]

### Hayashi Razan and "orthodoxy"
- Hayashi Razan (1583–1657), student of Seika; adviser to the second shogun Tokugawa Hidetada from **1607**; rector of Edo's Confucian academy (Shōhei-kō, later Yushima Seidō); title *Daigaku-no-kami* hereditary in his family. [WP-Hayashi_Razan] (Matches Periergia's Chinese Buddhism chapter.)
- 1632: Tokugawa Yoshinao granted permission; the first hall built by Razan on his grounds at Shinobu-ga-oka (now Ueno); moved to Yushima by shogun Tsunayoshi in **1691**; became a state-sponsored academy in **1797** after the Kansei edict. [WP-Yushima_Seidō]
- Razan held Shinto a provisional, local form of Confucian ideas; privately argued Emperor Jimmu descended from the Chinese Wu Taibo (*Jimmu Tennō ron*). [WP-Hayashi_Razan]
- Razan on reading: "Read them horizontally! Read them vertically! … When you realize a mystical unity with the sages' writings, one in which the self and those texts are non-dual, you will have read them well!" (Tucker's translation). [SEP-JC §4.1]
- **Kansei edict (Kansei igaku no kin, "prohibition of heterodox learning"), 1790**: under chief councillor Matsudaira Sadanobu (rōjū from 1787), it enforced Zhu Xi Neo-Confucianism as official doctrine, especially in the curriculum of the Hayashi school; banned certain publications. Bodart-Bailey (2002, quoted by WP): "Scholars vary in their opinion on how far this heterodoxy was enforced and whether this first official insistence on heterodoxy [sic: orthodoxy] constituted the high point of Confucianism in government affairs or signalled its decline." [WP-Emperor_Kōkaku §Kansei Reforms (citing Nosco 1997, 20; Bodart-Bailey 2002); WP-Matsudaira_Sadanobu (Backus 1979, "The Kansei Prohibition of Heterodoxy")] → **1790 verified (WP only)**. Note the word "first official insistence": the edict itself is evidence that there was no strict orthodoxy before 1790.

### The orthodoxy debate (name it as a debate)
- Traditional picture: Inoue Tetsujirō (1855–1944) — three schools (Zhu Xi school: Seika, Razan, Ansai; Wang Yangming school: Nakae Tōju, Kumazawa Banzan; Ancient Learning: Yamaga Sokō, Itō Jinsai, Ogyū Sorai). Tucker: this "simply does not accurately represent developments of Japanese Confucianism". [SEP-JC §4]
- Maruyama Masao, *Studies in the Intellectual History of Tokugawa Japan* (written in the war years; Eng. trans. Mikiso Hane, Princeton 1974): Zhu Xi orthodoxy as static, dissolved by Sorai and others; Tucker: a "flawed interpretive framework that Hegel had provided", whose narrative "hardly transcended the war rhetoric of Japanese nationalism", though Maruyama later acknowledged errors and it is "a starting point". [SEP-JC §5]
- Revisionists: Herman Ooms, *Tokugawa Ideology: Early Constructs, 1570–1680* (Princeton, 1985): Seika and Razan were not the only teachers close to the early bakufu; Zen (Suzuki Shōsan) and Shinto (Yamazaki Ansai) were also used ideologically; the Hayashi promoted a "fiction" of shogun-backed orthodoxy; early orthodoxy at most a "negative standard"; historians misread Yamaga Sokō's exile as proof of bakufu concern with orthodoxy. Watanabe Hiroshi and Hori Isao (in Japanese) likewise showed Confucianism did not play the role traditionally assigned it in early Tokugawa Japan. [WS-8 (summaries of Ooms 1984/1986 articles, Ooms 1985 reviews, and a later study)] Tucker: Japanese Confucian philosophizing "was simply not always related to or an expression of the interests of the Tokugawa shoguns, nor did it always issue from … Edo". [SEP-JC §4] SEP-JP, by contrast, says the shogunate "believed Confucianism could support its aims" (secular, hierarchical harmony, urban academies). [SEP-JP §4.3.1]
- Ancient Japan never had Chinese-style civil-service examinations. [SEP-JC §3]
- Paramore (*Japanese Confucianism: A Cultural History*, 2016) claims Confucianism became "taboo" after 1945 (the "Maruyama rule"); Tucker disputes this. [SEP-JC §5]

### Yamazaki Ansai, Kaibara Ekken
- Yamazaki Ansai (1619–1682), born Kyoto; Zen monk (Myōshin-ji) turned Zhu Xi Confucian; *Heresies Refuted* (*Heikii*, 1647) against Buddhism; private school in Kyoto 1655; Kimon school; also Shinto (Suika Shinto — name NOT verified). [WP-Yamazaki_Ansai; SEP-JC §4] Maruyama later wrote on "Yamazaki Ansai's Learning and the School of Yamazaki Ansai" (1980). [SEP-JC §5]
- Kaibara Ekken (1630–1714): "to the end a professed Neo-Confucian"; *Taigiroku* (record of doubts about Zhu Xi) opens with Lu Xiangshan: "In learning, a person should be worried when he has no doubts. If he has doubts, then progress follows." Tucker: Neo-Confucianism in Japan was "Far from a rigid and unchanging orthodoxy". [SEP-JC §4.3] Naturalist: *Yamato honzō*; Siebold called him the "Aristotle of Japan"; *Onna daigaku* (*Greater Learning for Women*) attributed to him but "modern scholarship argues that it was actually prepared by other hands" (oldest copy 1733). [WP-Kaibara_Ekken; SEP-JC §4.8 ("attributed to")]

### Itō Jinsai (1627–1705) — Kogigaku
- Kyoto merchant's son; school called **Kogigaku** ("study of ancient meanings"); grouped by Inoue under *kogaku* (Ancient Learning) with Yamaga Sokō and Sorai, but "Jinsai never mentions Sokō", and Sorai attacked Jinsai. [WP-Itō_Jinsai]
- Magnum opus *Gomō jigi* (*Meanings of Words in the Analects and Mencius*); wanted to purge Buddhist-derived terms from Confucian discourse. [SEP-JC §4.1]
- Metaphysics: "ostensible monism of *ki*"; *ri* exists only within *ki*; *ri* a "dead word", *michi* (the way) a "living word"; human nature as each person's inborn disposition, varying; argued the *Great Learning* was not a Confucian text; Analects and Mencius sufficient. Tucker: calling Jinsai an anti-Neo-Confucian "ancient learning" thinker "is misleading". [SEP-JC §§4.2, 4.6, 4.8]
- Often compared with Dai Zhen's *Mengzi ziyi shuzheng*. [WP-Itō_Jinsai]

### Ogyū Sorai (1666–1728) — Kobunjigaku; the Way made by the sages
- Born Edo, 21 Mar 1666 – d. 28 Feb 1728; studied Zhu Xi under the Hayashi; father exiled 1679, 13 years of rural self-study; served Yanagisawa Yoshiyasu from 1696; on the 47 rōnin (1702–3) argued they must commit *seppuku* because the law must be impartial; school Ken'en-juku; *Bendō* (*Distinguishing the Way*, 1717) and *Benmei* (*Distinguishing Names*). [WP-Ogyū_Sorai; SEP-JC §4.1] Name of his method "kobunjigaku" (study of ancient words and phrases) NOT verified in sources read — see NOT VERIFIED.
- Standard: the Six Classics (older than the Analects); Confucius "had not been fully a sage" in the strict sense. [SEP-JC §4.8]
- **The Way as made**: for Sorai the sages were the early kings, whose sagehood "was a function of their having established the way of social and political organization"; "the way of the sages" consists in "the rites and music, as well as the laws and bureaucratic forms necessary for good government". [SEP-JC §4.7] Ogrizek (2017, citing Tucker's translation): "The way thus represents the rites, music, penal laws and administrative systems which the ancient kings established." [WS-9]
- Argument against Neo-Confucian self-cultivation: the mind cannot control itself without splitting into controlling and controlled minds; so external rites, music, penal law and administration are needed. Tucker: he passes over the mandate of heaven; his philosophy was "viewed as a heterodox alternative, one much akin to that of Xunzi". [SEP-JC §4.7]
- Maruyama read Sorai as a "vanquisher" of Zhu Xi thought. [SEP-JC §5]

### Kokugaku (nativism / "Native Japan Studies")
- Narrow sense: 18th–19th-c. effort "to discern a native Way distinct from Buddhism and Confucianism within Japan's most ancient writings" and resurrect it. Translations of the term vary ("Japan Studies", "National Learning", "nativism", "essentialism"). Not one movement but "a succession of schools—private academies". [SEP-KK intro, §1]
- Lineage: Keichū (1640–1701), Kada no Azumamaro (1669–1736 per SEP intro; 1666–1736 in the section heading — SEP inconsistent), **Kamo no Mabuchi (1697–1769)**, **Motoori Norinaga (1730–1801)**, Hirata Atsutane (1776–1843), "most effective popularizer". [SEP-KK]
- Mabuchi: *Man'yōshū* as a "natural arcadia"; ancient Japan governed "in accordance with the spirit of heaven and earth, and there was none of this petty sophistry", until Chinese theories brought "tremendous chaos" (*Kokui kō*). [SEP-KK §3.1]
- Norinaga: lived almost all his life in Matsusaka near the Ise Shrines; practising (paediatric) doctor for 40 years; met Mabuchi once, in 1763 ("the night in Matsusaka"); *Kojiki-den* (commentary on the 712 *Kojiki*, c. 35 years' work). [SEP-KK §3.2; WP-Motoori_Norinaga]
- **Mono no aware** ("the sadness/pity of things"): Norinaga applied it to *The Tale of Genji* and the *Shin Kokinshū*; *Genji* "is simply a tale of human life that leaves aside … the questions of good and bad and that dwells only on the goodness of those who are aware of the sorrow of human existence" (*Tama no ogushi*, SJT trans.). This overturned didactic Buddhist and Confucian readings. [SEP-KK §3.2] (Periergia's medieval Japan chapter says the same — consistent.)
- **Karagokoro** (the "Chinese heart") vs *mikunigokoro*/*magokoro* (true heart): the native heart is damaged by Chinese moralism; verse: "Though he may think himself / Rid of the Chinese heart / The heart of a man / Who reads Chinese / Is still Chinese" (adapted from Muraoka 1964: 148). [SEP-KK §3.2]
- Norinaga's definition of *kami* (anything "outside the ordinary, which possessed superior power or was awe-inspiring"; evil things too). [SEP-KK §3.2]
- Darker side: Norinaga held "Japan was the primal source of all the world's countries"; after 1868 Kokugaku notions of "racial identity and cultural superiority" were used for nationalist ideology. [SEP-KK intro, §3.2]
- SEP-JP: Norinaga "hoped to do for Shintō what Edo-period Confucians were doing for their tradition in going back to ancient texts". [SEP-JP §4.3.2] (Connection: Kokugaku borrowed the philological method of Jinsai/Sorai.)

### Other independent thinkers
- **Andō Shōeki** (1703 – 29 Nov 1762): rejected Buddhist and Confucian thought and feudal hierarchy; history since writing is a "World of Law" created by rulers, priests and scholars; ideal of everyone farming ("Right Cultivation") and equal status, the "Self-Acting World"; had few followers. [WP-Andō_Shōeki] (WP-only; fairly thin article.)
- **Tominaga Nakamoto** (1715–1746): Osaka merchant, educated at the merchants' Kaitokudō academy; *Shutsujō gogo* (textual criticism of Buddhist sutras; argued Mahāyāna sutras later than Hīnayāna, and most of both later than the Buddha); *Okina no fumi*; critical of Buddhism, Confucianism and Shinto alike; "hiding is the beginning of lying and stealing" (on Shinto secrecy). Michael Pye calls him an early example of religious studies; Jason Josephson disputes this. [WP-Tominaga_Nakamoto]

## 5. Ethiopia: the Hatata

### Historical context
- Emperor **Susenyos** (r. 1606/7–1632) converted to Catholicism (1622 per WP-Hatata) and tried to impose it with Portuguese Jesuits; his son **Fasilides** (r. 1632–1667) restored Orthodoxy and expelled the Jesuits (1633). [WP-Zera_Yacob; WP-Hatata; WP-Susenyos_I] (Susenyos reign: WP-Zera_Yacob says 1607–1632; WP short description of Susenyos says 1606–1632.)
- Earlier Ethiopian wisdom literature in Geʿez: *Physiologus*, *Life and Maxims of Skendes*, *Book of the Wise Philosophers* (Abba Mikael, c. 1510/22). [WP-Ethiopian_philosophy; WP-Zera_Yacob]

### What the text says about its author (if authentic)
- *Ḥatäta* (Geʿez ሐተታ) = "inquiry/investigation". Two texts: the *Hatata* of Zera Yacob (Zärʾa Yaʿəqob; in the text also called **Wärqe**) and the *Hatata* of his pupil **Walda Heywat** (Wäldä Ḥəywät), son of his patron. [WP-Hatata]
- Zera Yacob: born near Aksum to a poor farming family; traditional church education; denounced before Susenyos; fled ("with some gold and the Book of Psalms"), lived two years in a cave near the Tekezé river; afterwards settled at Emfraz under the patron Habta Egziabher (Habtu), married a maid of the family (Hirut), taught Habtu's sons; wrote at Walda Heywat's request. [WP-Zera_Yacob; WP-Hatata]
- Dates — sources differ: born **1599** (WP-Ethiopian_philosophy: "1599–1692") or **28 August 1600** (WP-Zera_Yacob; WP-Hatata "1600–1693"); died 1692 or **1693** (death recorded by Walda Heywat in an annotation). Hatata dated **1667** (WP-Ethiopian_philosophy; Periergia's Enlightenment chapter "1667/68") or **1668** (WP-Hatata "supposedly in 1668"; WP-Zera_Yacob). Flight c. 1630 (WP-Hatata). → Write "Zera Yacob (1599/1600–1692/3)", Hatata "1667/8", and note these dates come from the text itself, so they stand or fall with its authenticity.
- Walda Heywat: WP gives 1633–1710 (WP-only, unsourced); his treatise more practical/social: "God did not create me only for myself, but placed me in the midst of other created [men] who are equal to me" (translation unattributed). [WP-Walda_Heywat]

### Transmission (undisputed facts)
- The only known manuscripts are two copies sent by the Italian **Capuchin** missionary **Giusto da Urbino** (Giusto d'Urbino; 1814–1856; Geʿez name Abba Yostos; in Ethiopia from 1846; expelled by Tewodros II 1855; died at Khartoum) to the French collector **Antoine d'Abbadie** in the early 1850s (WP-Hatata: 1853–54; Wion's "Episode 1" covers 1852–1904). Giusto sent d'Abbadie 24 manuscripts in all, for which he was paid (BnF d'Abbadie 194–217 and 234). [WP-Giusto_da_Urbino; WP-Hatata; WS-1] (WP-Hatata wrongly calls Giusto a "Jesuit"/"Franciscan Jesuit" — he was a Capuchin.)
- Manuscripts to the Bibliothèque nationale in 1902; Boris Turayev drew attention to them 1903; **Enno Littmann** published the Geʿez text with Latin translation in **1904** (*Philosophi Abessini*, CSCO); German translation 1916. [WP-Hatata]
- Claude Sumner SJ (1919–2012), at Addis Ababa University from 1953, published the first complete English translation and a defence of authenticity: *Ethiopian Philosophy, vol. II: The Treatise of Zara Yaecob and Walda Hewat: Text and Authorship* (1976), vol. III *An Analysis* (1978), *Classical Ethiopian Philosophy* (1985). Sumner: "Zär'a Ya'eqob is a real philosopher in the strictest sense of the word." [WP-Claude_Sumner; WP-Hatata]
- New English translation: Ralph Lee, Mehari Worku and Wendy Laura Belcher (with Jeremy R. Brown), *The Hatata Inquiries: Two Texts of Seventeenth-Century African Philosophy from Ethiopia about Reason, the Creator, and Our Ethical Responsibilities* (De Gruyter; WP-Hatata: "late 2023"; WP-Giusto cites it as 2024), open access, preface by Dag Herbjørnsrud. [WP-Hatata; WP-Giusto_da_Urbino; WS-11]

### THE AUTHORSHIP DEBATE (present both sides)
Case for forgery / later composition:
- **Carlo Conti Rossini** (1872–1949), 1920: the texts were written by Giusto da Urbino. Arguments (per WP): extrinsic — the late date of the only manuscripts, both in Giusto's hand/possession; Giusto's knowledge of Geʿez and of Islamic sources; that he was the discoverer; hearsay via a monk Tekle Haymanot; d'Abbadie's collecting created demand for such texts. Wion (via WS-1): Conti Rossini built "a set of evidence rather than a decisive proof". [WP-Zera_Yacob; WP-Hatata; WS-1]
- **Eugen Mittwoch** (1934) added linguistic arguments (WP: that the texts could not have been written by an African — WP-Hatata's framing; WP-Zera_Yacob: "linguistic critiques"); Wion: his analysis was flawed because it relied on an indirect source. After this, scholarly interest largely lapsed. [WP-Hatata; WP-Zera_Yacob; WS-1]
- Political context (WP-Hatata, citing Maiocchi 2015): Conti Rossini publicly supported Italy's 1935 invasion of Ethiopia (Abyssinia "incapable of evolution and civil progress"). Use carefully: this concerns motive, not evidence.
- **Anaïs Wion** (French Ethiopianist), "The History of a Genuine Fake Philosophical Treatise (Ḥatatā Zarʾa Yāʿeqob and Ḥatatā Walda Ḥeywat)", in two "episodes" in *Afriques* (2021; Episode 1 "The Time of Discovery … (1852–1904)"; Episode 2 on the debunking, 1916–1950s): uses the manuscripts and the previously unpublished Giusto–d'Abbadie correspondence and "genetic criticism" to examine how Giusto may have composed the text; supports Giusto's authorship (title: a "genuine fake"). [WS-1; WP-Giusto_da_Urbino; WP-Hatata]
- John Marenbon (2024, in Cantor–Egid–Merawi): the works, if inauthentic, are "not forgeries but simply pseudonymous"; another view (cited in WP) is that Giusto compiled them from traditional material. [WP-Giusto_da_Urbino (citing Marenbon 2024, 110, 118)]
- Fasil Merawi (2023) argues the effort to make the Hatatas a foundation of Ethiopian philosophy is part of a politicised debate shaped by nationalism and decolonial aims. [WS-3]
Case for 17th-century Ethiopian authorship:
- **Sumner (1976)**: long rebuttal; used Giusto's letters in Rome; argued the ideas diverge from Giusto's known views and exceed his Geʿez ability ("dozens of pages" showing Giusto's skills weak, per Belcher); different biblical-citation patterns in the two treatises indicate two authors. [WP-Zera_Yacob; WP-Hatata]
- Ethiopian scholars Amsalu Aklilu and Alemayehu Moges: secular tone, syntax and Geʿez usage point to Ethiopian origin. Teodros Kiros (*Zara Yacob: Rationality of the Human Heart*, 2005) also defends. [WP-Zera_Yacob; WP-Hatata]
- **Getatchew Haile** (1931–2021), leading Geʿez philologist: per WP-Hatata he "had for a long time rejected the authenticity", but in a 2017 chapter ("The Discourse of Wärqe: Commonly Known as Ḥatäta zä-Zär'a Yaʿəqob", in *Ethiopian Studies in Honour of Amha Asfaw*) wrote he was "now firmly inclined to believe that the original Hatata is the work of an Ethiopian *debtera* who lived, as he claimed, during the era of the Catholics". Note: "the original" — leaves room for later reworking. [WP-Hatata; WP-Getatchew_Haile] (WebSearch could not independently confirm his earlier position — WP-only.)
- **Lee, Worku, Belcher (2023/24)**: Belcher's introduction devotes 37 pages to authorship; the team is "confident that two Ethiopians named Zara Yaqob and Walda Heywat composed these two texts. In this, we stand with dozens of other scholars, including the late Getatchew Haile." Belcher argues the texts' joyful tone refutes the "bitter" Giusto hypothesis, criticises Wion for not engaging Sumner's arguments, and concludes "Almost everyone who has argued against their Ethiopian authorship has not read them in Geʿez." [WP-Hatata (quoting Belcher)]
Status of the debate:
- Jonathan Egid's King's College London PhD (defended 14 June 2024), *In Search of Zärʾa Yaʿqob…*: notes that "over the last one hundred years, many scholars have concluded that these texts are in fact nineteenth-century forgeries", sets out those arguments and scrutinises them, tracing the debate's "intellectual and cultural-political underpinnings"; he argues resolving religious disagreement was the main motive of the text's thought. Lea Cantor, Jonathan Egid and Fasil Merawi (eds.), *In Search of Zär'a Ya'ǝqob: On the History, Philosophy, and Authorship of the Ḥatäta Zär'a Ya'ǝqob and the Ḥatäta Wäldä Ḥəywät* (De Gruyter, 2024, open access) — publisher: the "near-exclusive focus on authorship has obscured" the texts' philosophical interest. An Oxford intellectual-history page says the debate "still rages". [WS-2]
- WP-Zera_Yacob claims "the majority of scholars support Zara Yaqob as the authentic author" and the forgery hypothesis is "widely regarded as outdated" — but WP-Giusto_da_Urbino calls the works "inauthentic". **Wikipedia contradicts itself; the debate is live.** Chapter should say: the texts survive only in copies sent by Giusto; Conti Rossini (1920) and Mittwoch (1934) attributed them to Giusto; Sumner (1976), Getatchew Haile (2017) and the 2023 translators argue for Ethiopian authorship; Wion (2021) revived the forgery case; recent work (Cantor/Egid/Merawi 2024) treats it as open and urges reading the texts as philosophy either way.
- SEP-Afr (Outlaw) names "Zera Yacob and Walda Heyat, two seventeenth century philosophers (Sumner 1976–78)" without discussing authorship. [SEP-Afr]

### The Hatata's ideas (sources: WP summaries; translations unconfirmed)
- Method: rejects authority of tradition; accepts from Scripture and dogma only what survives rational inquiry; human reason can find truth if it searches. [WP-Ethiopian_philosophy]
- God: a first-cause argument (no infinite chain of parents/causes → an uncreated eternal creator); the creator who gave us reason and speech must have them. Creation is good because God is good → critique of ascetic morals and some Jewish, Christian and Islamic precepts (fasting, food laws, polygamy, monastic celibacy). [WP-Ethiopian_philosophy; WP-Zera_Yacob; WP-Hatata]
- Equality: chapter 6 opens (in an unattributed English translation) "All men are equal in the presence of God; and all are intelligent since they are his creatures…". [WP-Zera_Yacob]
- **Slavery — verified as a theme, wording unconfirmed:** Belcher's summary (quoted in WP-Hatata) says the texts "condemn slavery and warfare"; WP-Zera_Yacob quotes chapter 5: the "Mohammedans said that it is right to go and buy a man as if he were an animal. But with our intelligence, we understand that this Mohammedan law cannot come from the creator of man who made us equal, like brothers". [WP-Hatata; WP-Zera_Yacob] Slavery was widely practised in Ethiopia at the time (WP).
- **Women and marriage:** "husband and wife are equal in marriage" (quoted in WP-Hatata); against monastic superiority over marriage and against polygamy ("the law of creation orders one man to marry one woman"). [WP-Hatata; WP-Zera_Yacob] Belcher: the texts "advocate for the rights of women and of animals, plead for religious and cultural tolerance". [WP-Hatata]

### ARGUMENT (Hatata): the menstruation law (step by step; from Herbjørnsrud's summary in WP-Hatata)
1. The Creator in his wisdom made blood flow monthly from women's wombs so that they can bear children.
2. What the Creator ordains as part of his creation is good and cannot be impure.
3. The law of Moses declares menstruating women impure.
4. That law "impedes marriage and the entire life of a woman, and it spoils the law of mutual help, prevents the bringing up of children and destroys love" (quoted wording; translation unattributed).
5. A law that contradicts the Creator's order and harms love and mutual help cannot come from God.
6. So the Mosaic purity law is against nature and the Creator — a human, not divine, law. General principle: any religious law that contradicts what reason discovers in creation is human invention. [WP-Hatata; WP-Ethiopian_philosophy]
- Objection (from within the tradition): revelation can command what reason does not understand. Reply implied by the text: God gave reason to judge religious claims; a God who is good and created reason would not command what reason sees as harmful; competing faiths each claim revelation and cannot all be right, so revelation needs a rational test (WP-Ethiopian_philosophy: seeing contradictions between Christian interpretations, Judaism and Islam, he "refuse[s] the authority of … any tradition in general"). [WP-Ethiopian_philosophy] (Precise wording of a "they cannot all be right" passage NOT verified.)

## Debates
- **Unity of knowing and acting**: denial of weakness of will (SEP reading) vs knowing-how/responsive-action reading (IEP); Van Norden's objections. [SEP-WYM; IEP-WYM]
- **Bamboo episode**: did Wang misapply Zhu Xi? SEP-WYM "unclear"; Qian Mu says Wang lacked a hypothesis; SEP-ZX: Wang reacted "more to the scholastic attitudes fostered by the examination system than to Zhu Xi himself". [SEP-WYM; SEP-ZX]
- **How Buddhist was Wang?** Chan 1962; Qing critics; New Confucians (Mou Zongsan) think him more orthodox than Zhu. [SEP-WYM]
- **Mullā Ṣadrā's dates and the Kahak "exile"**; **mystic or philosopher?** Rizvi's four camps; SEP: he is "no mystical obscurantist". [SEP-MS]
- **"School of Isfahan"** — a modern label (Corbin & Nasr), criticised as retroactive and perennialist. [SEP-MS; IEP-MS; WS-7]
- **Was Tokugawa Confucianism state orthodoxy?** Inoue/Maruyama tradition vs Ooms, Watanabe Hiroshi, Hori Isao; Tucker's "early modern" reframing; Kansei edict 1790 as first formal orthodoxy and scholars disagree how far it was enforced. [SEP-JC; WS-8; WP-Emperor_Kōkaku]
- **Is Jinsai/Sorai "Ancient Learning" anti-Neo-Confucian?** Tucker: misleading for Jinsai. [SEP-JC]
- **Kokugaku**: philology and aesthetics vs nationalist/racial uses after 1868. [SEP-KK]
- **Authorship of the Hatata** (full above).
