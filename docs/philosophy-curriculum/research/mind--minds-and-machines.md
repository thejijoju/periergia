## 1. The question and early history

### Descartes's two tests (Discourse on the Method, Part V, 1637)
- Full passage in the **Haldane and Ross translation** (*The Philosophical Works of Descartes*, vol. 1, Cambridge 1911), as quoted in SEP-AI §1 (which says "Descartes proposed TT … long before Turing was born"): "If there were machines which bore a resemblance to our body and imitated our actions as far as it was morally possible to do so, we should always have two very certain tests by which to recognise that, for all that, they were not real men. The first is, that they could never use speech or other signs as we do when placing our thoughts on record for the benefit of others. For we can easily understand a machine's being constituted so that it can utter words, and even emit some responses to action on it of a corporeal kind, which brings about a change in its organs; for instance, if it is touched in a particular part it may ask what we wish to say to it; if in another part it may exclaim that it is being hurt, and so on. But it never happens that it arranges its speech in various ways, in order to reply appropriately to everything that may be said in its presence, as even the lowest type of man can do. And the second difference is, that although machines can perform certain things as well as or perhaps better than any of us can do, they infallibly fall short in others, by which means we may discover that they did not act from knowledge, but only for the disposition of their organs. For while reason is a universal instrument which can serve for all contingencies, these organs have need of some special adaptation for every particular action. From this it follows that it is morally impossible that there should be sufficient diversity in any machine to allow it to act in all the events of life in the same way as our reason causes us to act." [source: SEP-AI §1, citing Descartes 1637 in Haldane and Ross 1911, p. 116]
- The CSM rendering (Cottingham, Stoothoff, Murdoch, *Philosophical Writings of Descartes*, vol. 1) has "two very certain means of recognizing that they were not real men" at CSM 1:139–40, AT 6:56. [source: WS-5, quoting a scholarly article] Only these few CSM words are verified; quote the full passage from Haldane and Ross (or paraphrase).
- ARGUMENT (Descartes's two tests, step by step, from the passage):
  1. A machine could be built to look like us and imitate our actions, even to utter words in response to physical stimuli.
  2. Test 1 (language): no machine could "arrange its speech in various ways" to "reply appropriately to everything that may be said in its presence", as even the dullest human can.
  3. Test 2 (general adaptability): a machine acts "only for the disposition of their organs", each organ needing "special adaptation for every particular action", whereas reason is "a universal instrument which can serve for all contingencies"; so no machine could have enough diversity "to act in all the events of life" as reason does.
  4. So anything that passes both tests acts "from knowledge" (reason), not mere mechanism; anything that fails is a machine.
  - Descartes also applied the tests to animals, which he held fail both (WP-Animal_machine; Descartes "used parrots to argue that imitation of speech is not the same as language or understanding"). [source: WP-Animal_machine, citing Deborah Brown 2018]
  - IEP contrast: Turing "proposed his test as a qualifying test for human-level intelligence, not as a disqualifying test for intelligence per se (as Descartes had proposed)". [source: IEP-AI §2]
  - Objection (for the chapter): Descartes's tests are empirical bets about what mechanisms can do ("morally impossible" = practically impossible); today's language models produce appropriate replies across very many topics, so test 1 is now contested (see §6). Reply on Descartes's behalf: test 2 (general adaptability across "all the events of life") and the question whether fluent output comes "from knowledge" remain open. (This framing is the researcher's synthesis — present as such.)
  - A scholarly reading treats the tests as negative tests: failing shows behaviour is not reason-driven, but passing does not prove it is. [source: WS-5 summary of a scholarly article — weaker]
- Descartes compared animals to hydraulic automata such as the moving statues in the gardens at Saint-Germain-en-Laye; held only humans have immortal, immaterial souls. [source: WP-Animal_machine]
- Cross-ref: the published *Descartes* chapter covers animals as machines and the reflex quotation, but NOT the two tests — this chapter can introduce them.

### Hobbes: reasoning as computation
- *Leviathan* (1651) ch. 5 ("Of Reason and Science"): "REASON, in this sense, is nothing but Reckoning (that is, Adding and Subtracting) of the Consequences of generall names agreed upon, for the marking and signifying of our thoughts". [source: WS-6 (LitCharts, SparkNotes, Toronto Pressbooks 1651 text); 1651 spelling has "Substracting" per earlier Hobbes notes] The published *Hobbes* chapter quotes "nothing but reckoning (that is, adding and subtracting)" — consistent.
- *De Corpore* (1655) 1.2: "By reasoning I understand computation. And to compute is to collect the sum of many things added together at the same time, or to know the remainder when one thing has been taken from another. To reason therefore is the same as to add or to subtract." [source: SEP-Hob §2.4] (The *Hobbes* chapter already quotes this, trans. Martinich — don't repeat at length.)
- Leibniz endorsed the idea (1666): "Thomas Hobbes, everywhere a profound examiner of principles, rightly stated that everything done by our mind is a computation". [source: SEP-Hob §2.4]
- SEP: connections to the modern computational theory of mind "seem to amount to no more than" a shared slogan; "it's at least rather over-dramatic to say that Hobbes was 'prophetically launching Artificial Intelligence' (Haugeland 1985, 23)". [source: SEP-Hob §2.4] The *Hobbes* chapter already reports this verdict and points forward to this chapter.
- *Leviathan* Introduction asks why we may not say "that all Automata (Engines that move themselves by springs and wheeles as doth a watch) have an artificiall life" — already in the *Hobbes* chapter; mention only.

### Leibniz's mill (cross-ref *Leibniz*, *Consciousness*)
- SEP-CR §2.1 lists the mill as the first of "four important antecedents" of the Chinese Room, quoting *Monadology* §17 in **Robert Latta's translation**: "…supposing there were a machine, so constructed as to think, feel, and have perception, it might be conceived as increased in size, while keeping the same proportions, so that one might go into it as into a mill. That being so, we should, on examining its interior, find only parts which work one upon another, and never anything by which to explain a perception." SEP-CR: Leibniz's argument "is parallel to Searle's claim that no matter what syntactic processing there is, there would be no understanding of meaning". [source: SEP-CR §2.1]
- NB: the published *Leibniz* and *Consciousness* chapters use a DIFFERENT translation ("only parts pushing one another, and never anything by which to explain a perception", from SEP "Leibniz's Philosophy of Mind"). Use that one for consistency, or name Latta if quoting SEP-CR. The *Leibniz* chapter already sets the mill out step by step — just point to it.
- Leibniz also designed a calculating machine demonstrated to the Royal Society on 1 February 1673 (already in *Leibniz*). IEP-AI dates his Stepped Reckoner "circa 1671" and Pascal's Pascaline "circa 1642". [source: IEP-AI §3a.i]

### La Mettrie, *L'Homme machine*
- Julien Offray de La Mettrie (23 Nov 1709 – 11 Nov 1751), French physician, an early French materialist; *L'Homme machine* extended Descartes's animal-machine argument to humans; rejected Cartesian dualism; thought arises from bodily organisation. [source: WP-Julien_Offray_de_La_Mettrie; WP-Man_a_Machine; WP-Animal_machine]
- **Date: 1747 or 1748.** WP-Man_a_Machine and WP-Animal_machine say first published 1747; WP-La_Mettrie's own works list gives "*L'Homme Machine*. 1748 (anon.)" and its title-page image is "a 1748 French edition". Say "1747 (title pages often dated 1748)" or "1747–48". Hostility in the Netherlands forced him to Berlin in 1748, where Frederick the Great protected him. [source: WP-La_Mettrie]

### Ada Lovelace's objection (1843)
- Augusta Ada King, Countess of Lovelace (10 Dec 1815 – 27 Nov 1852). Menabrea's French account of Babbage's 1840 Turin lecture appeared in the *Bibliothèque universelle de Genève* in October 1842; Lovelace translated it and added seven notes, A–G, "about three times longer than the translation", published in Taylor's *Scientific Memoirs*, September 1843, signed "AAL". [source: WP-Ada_Lovelace]
- Note G wording: "The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to *originate* anything. It can do whatever *we know how to order it* to perform. It can follow analysis; but it has no power of anticipating any analytical relations or truths." (italics in the original; WP-Ada_Lovelace and WP-Computing_Machinery place the italics slightly differently: "can do *whatever we know how to order it*" vs "can do whatever *we know how to order it*"). [source: WP-Ada_Lovelace; WP-Computing_Machinery_and_Intelligence, citing *Scientific Memoirs* vol. 3, 1843]
- **Surprise:** Turing's paper (as quoted in SEP-TT §2.6) gives it WITHOUT "whatever": "The Analytical Engine has no pretensions to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform (cited by Hartree, p. 70)" — Turing quoted it second-hand via Hartree. Quote Lovelace's own text (with "whatever") and note Turing knew it via Hartree. [source: SEP-TT §2.6]
- The name "Lady Lovelace's Objection" is Turing's (1950). [source: WP-Ada_Lovelace] IEP-AI calls it "arguably the first and … certainly among the most frequently repeated objections to AI" (IEP dates it "Lovelace 1842" — the French original was 1842, the English notes 1843; use 1843). [source: IEP-AI §4.i]
- Lovelace also saw the Engine "might act upon other things besides *number*" (e.g. musical pitch relations). [source: WP-Ada_Lovelace]

## 2. Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (Mind 59 (236): 433–60, 1950)
- Alan Turing, 23 June 1912 – 7 June 1954. [source: WP-Alan_Turing] Moved to Manchester University in 1948. [source: SEP-Tur]
- Opening: "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'" [source: WP-Turing_test, citing Turing 1950 p. 433] The question is "too meaningless to deserve discussion" (p. 442). [source: SEP-TT intro] He will "replace the question by another, which is closely related to it and is expressed in relatively unambiguous words". [source: WP-Computing_Machinery; WP-Turing_test]
- ARGUMENT / SET-UP (imitation game, step by step):
  1. Original party game: a man (A), a woman (B), an interrogator (C) "who may be of either sex" in a separate room, communicating by teletype (to avoid clues from voice). C must say which is the man and which the woman; A tries to make C choose wrongly; B tries to help C. [source: IEP-AI §2 quoting Turing 1950 p. 433; SEP-TT §3.1]
  2. "We now ask the question, 'What will happen when a machine takes the part of A in this game?' Will the interrogator decide wrongly as often when the game is played like this as he does when the game is played between a man and a woman? These questions replace our original, 'Can machines think?'" [source: WP-Computing_Machinery; IEP-AI §2 (IEP has "when the game is being played like this")]
  3. Standard version: interrogator, a human and a machine, labelled X and Y; the machine tries to make the interrogator misidentify it, the human tries to help. [source: SEP-TT §1]
  4. Turing's claim: if a machine does as well as a human at this, we have as good reason to credit it with thinking as we have for other people (his reply to the consciousness objection; see below). [source: SEP-TT §2.4]
  - Interpretation debate: whether the machine must imitate a *woman* (Genova 1994, Traiger 2000; Sterrett 2000, 2020 dissents) or a human of unspecified gender (Copeland 2000, Piccinini 2000, Moor 2001 — SEP calls their case convincing). [source: SEP-TT §3.1]
  - Turing restricted attention to digital computers: "all digital computers are in a sense equivalent" (universality; cross-ref *Formal Logic* on Turing machines, 1936). [source: WP-Computing_Machinery; IEP-AI §3a.ii] He excluded "men born in the usual manner" from counting as machines (pp. 435–6). [source: SEP-TT §1]
  - SEP distinguishes "The Turing Test Claim" (the game is a good test of intelligence) from "The Thinking Machine Claim" (a programmed computer could pass it). [source: SEP-TT §3.3]
- **Prediction (p. 442):** "I believe that in about fifty years' time it will be possible to programme computers, with a storage capacity of about 10⁹, to make them play the imitation game so well that an average interrogator will not have more than 70 per cent chance of making the right identification after five minutes of questioning. … I believe that at the end of the century the use of words and general educated opinion will have altered so much that one will be able to speak of machines thinking without expecting to be contradicted." [source: SEP-TT §§1, 3.2 (SEP prints "70 percent"); WP-Turing_test (p. 442, "70 per cent"); WS-7 confirms 10⁹] Spelling: WP and IEP give "per cent" (British original); SEP "percent". IEP-AI paraphrases slightly differently ("will have no more than 70 per cent. chance of making the correct identification") — follow SEP/WP. Storage units were binary digits, so 10⁹ bits ≈ 125 megabytes (WS-7 blog says "100MB"; give "about a hundred megabytes" at most, or omit).
- Assessment of the prediction: "Most commentators contend that this claim has been shown to be mistaken"; Copeland (2000) replies that "about fifty years" is not "exactly fifty", and notes Turing elsewhere said "at least 100 years" before an unrestricted test would be passed; the second prediction (educated opinion) is arguably vindicated (Moor 2001). [source: SEP-TT §3.2] IEP: by 2000, Loebner Prize entrants were so poor "the average interrogator had 100 percent chance of making the correct identification" (Moor 2001). [source: IEP-AI §2]
- Turing on his own case: "no very convincing arguments of a positive nature to support my views. If I had I should not have taken such pains to point out the fallacies in contrary views." (p. 454) [source: SEP-TT §2.9]

### The nine objections (Turing's labels, SEP-TT §2)
(1) The Theological Objection; (2) The "Heads in the Sand" Objection; (3) The Mathematical Objection; (4) The Argument from Consciousness; (5) Arguments from Various Disabilities; (6) Lady Lovelace's Objection; (7) Argument from Continuity of the Nervous System; (8) The Argument from Informality of Behaviour; (9) The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception. [source: SEP-TT §2] (WP calls (1) the "Religious Objection"; use SEP's labels.)
- **Theological:** Turing: "In attempting to construct such machines we should not be irreverently usurping His power of creating souls, any more than we are in the procreation of children: rather we are, in either case, instruments of His will providing mansions for the souls that He creates." [source: WP-Computing_Machinery — quotation via WP only] SEP: if God can unite souls with human bodies, "it is hard to see what reason there is for thinking that God could not unite souls with digital computers". [source: SEP-TT §2.1]
- **Heads in the sand:** "The consequences of machines thinking would be too dreadful. Let us hope and believe that they cannot do so." [source: WP-Computing_Machinery] SEP: not an argument, only an expression of fears. [source: SEP-TT §2.2]
- **Consciousness:** Turing quotes Geoffrey Jefferson's Lister Oration (1949): "Not until a machine can write a sonnet or compose a concerto because of thoughts and emotions felt, and not by the chance fall of symbols, could we agree that machine equals brain—that is, not only write it but know that it had written it. No mechanism could feel (and not merely artificially signal, an easy contrivance) pleasure at its successes, grief when its valves fuse, be warmed by flattery, be made miserable by its mistakes, be charmed by sex, be angry or depressed when it cannot get what it wants." (pp. 445–6) Reply: taken strictly the objection leads to solipsism (one could be sure only by *being* the machine); Turing would be content if we had as much reason to think machines think as to think other people do; WP adds his "polite convention that everyone thinks" and: "I do not wish to give the impression that I think there is no mystery about consciousness … [b]ut I do not think these mysteries necessarily need to be solved before we can answer the question". [source: SEP-TT §2.4; WP-Computing_Machinery; WP-Chinese_room] This reply is now called the "other minds reply". [source: WP-Computing_Machinery]
- **Disabilities:** list — be kind, resourceful, beautiful, friendly, have initiative, have a sense of humour, tell right from wrong, make mistakes, fall in love, enjoy strawberries and cream, make someone fall in love with it, learn from experience, use words properly, be the subject of its own thought, have as much diversity of behaviour as a man, do something really new. Turing: such claims rest on induction from the machines we have met. [source: SEP-TT §2.5; WP-Computing_Machinery]
- **Lady Lovelace:** Turing reduces it to "machines can never take us by surprise" and replies that machines take him by surprise often; also, are *we* able to do anything "really new"? Bringsjord et al. (2001) call his reply "mysterious" at best and "incompetent" at worst; SEP (Oppy/Dowe) disagree. Turing's late "child machine" section returns to it: an idea "injected" into a sub-critical mind dies away, as a neutron in a sub-critical atomic pile; some minds are supercritical. [source: SEP-TT §2.6; WP-Computing_Machinery]
- **Mathematical:** see §5 (Lucas–Penrose). Turing's short reply: it is not shown that humans are free of such limits; but he added the argument cannot be dismissed "quite so lightly". [source: SEP-TT §2.3]
- **Continuity of the nervous system:** the brain may be a continuous-state machine; Turing replies a discrete-state machine can imitate it closely enough for the game. Dreyfus later pressed this as "the biological assumption" (1972). [source: SEP-TT §2.7; WP-Computing_Machinery]
- **Informality of behaviour:** no rules could describe what a person ought to do in every circumstance; Turing notes a slide between "ought" (rules of conduct) and "will" (laws of behaviour): "we certainly know of no circumstances under which we could say, 'we have searched enough. There are no such laws.'" [source: SEP-TT §2.8; WP-Computing_Machinery]
- **ESP:** Turing took the evidence for telepathy to be "overwhelming" (probably influenced by J. B. Rhine; WP suggests Samuel Soal's 1940s experiments) and proposed a "telepathy-proof room"; SEP: "no current statistical support for telepathy". [source: SEP-TT §2.9; WP-Computing_Machinery] Good worked example of how a great thinker can be wrong about evidence.
- Learning machines: simulate a child's mind and educate it; analogy to evolution (structure of child machine = hereditary material; changes = mutations; judgment of experimenter = natural selection); "provide the machine with the best sense organs that money can buy, and then teach it to understand and speak English". [source: WP-Computing_Machinery] Turing had written a "paper machine" chess program in "Intelligent Machinery" (1948, unpublished until 1968). [source: SEP-CR §2.2; WP-Computing_Machinery]

### Criticisms of the test
- **Blockhead (Block 1981, "Psychologism and Behaviorism", *Philosophical Review* 90: 5–43):** "a creature that looks just like a human being, but that is controlled by a 'game-of-life look-up tree,' i.e. by a tree that contains a programmed response for every discriminable input at each stage in the creature's life." ARGUMENT: (1) If passing the test (or any purely behavioural test) were logically sufficient for intelligence, anything that passes is intelligent. (2) Blockhead is logically possible and passes. (3) Blockhead is not intelligent (Block: it "has all of the intelligence of a toaster"). (4) So passing is not logically sufficient. Replies: deny Blockhead is possible (it is conceivable, but conceivability→possibility is contested — cross-ref *Possibility and Necessity*), or deny it lacks intelligence (it is at least an information processor). Many deny Blockhead is *physically* possible (the tree would be astronomically large; Tipler), so the test may still be a good *criterion* or *probabilistic* evidence. [source: SEP-TT §§4.2–4.4]
- **French (1990, "Subcognition and the Limits of the Turing Test", *Mind* 99: 53–65):** the test is "virtually useless" as a test of intelligence because nothing without a "human subcognitive substrate" could pass; associative-priming timing tests and "ratings games" ("Flugblogs" as a breakfast cereal name vs "Flugly" for a teddy bear; pens as weapons vs grand pianos as wheelbarrows) would unmask machines. So the test measures "culturally-oriented intelligence". Replies (Oppy/Dowe): timing data is excluded by the set-up; ordinary interrogators won't think of such tests; learning machines could acquire subcognitive competences. [source: SEP-TT §§1, 4.1, 5.1]
- **Chauvinism:** intelligent things might fail the test; reply: Turing claims only sufficiency, not necessity. [source: SEP-TT §1]
- **Too easy (ELIZA):** "quite unintelligent programs (e.g., ELIZA…) can seem to ordinary observers to be loci of intelligence"; reply: the test requires surviving interrogators *who know* one party is a machine, over many trials. [source: SEP-TT §1]
- Alternatives: Harnad's Total Turing Test (1989, 1991: full sensorimotor robot capacities); Bringsjord et al.'s Lovelace Test (2001: the designer cannot explain how the output arose); Schweizer's Truly Total Turing Test (1998). [source: SEP-TT §5.3]
- Competitions: Loebner Prize (first November 1991; final competition 2019, after Loebner's death in 2016). In 2014 Eugene Goostman (posing as a 13-year-old boy from Odesa with English as a second language) fooled 33% of judges and was claimed to have "passed"; SEP: such small trials were "not reliably projectible". [source: SEP-TT §1; WP-Turing_test; WP-Eugene_Goostman]

### ELIZA and the ELIZA effect
- ELIZA: developed 1964–67 at MIT by Joseph Weizenbaum; paper 1966 ("ELIZA—A Computer Program for the Study of Natural Language Communication Between Man and Machine", *Communications of the ACM* 9: 36–45); keyword matching and transformation rules from a "script"; the DOCTOR script imitated a Rogerian (non-directive) psychotherapist; named after Eliza Doolittle in Shaw's *Pygmalion*. [source: WP-ELIZA; SEP-TT bibliography]
- Worked example (DOCTOR): Human: "Well, my boyfriend made me come here." ELIZA: "Your boyfriend made you come here?" Human: "He says I'm depressed much of the time." ELIZA: "I am sorry to hear you are depressed." [source: WP-ELIZA_effect] Mechanism example: "You are very helpful" → decomposed → "What makes you think I am very helpful?" [source: WP-ELIZA]
- Weizenbaum's secretary reportedly asked him to leave the room so she could talk to ELIZA. Weizenbaum: "I had not realized … that extremely short exposures to a relatively simple computer program could induce powerful delusional thinking in quite normal people." (*Computer Power and Human Reason*, 1976, p. 7) [source: WP-ELIZA; WP-Joseph_Weizenbaum]
- ELIZA effect: "a claimed tendency to project human traits—such as experience, semantic comprehension or empathy—onto rudimentary computer programs." [source: WP-ELIZA_effect]
- In a 2025 study (below), ELIZA was judged human 23% of the time. [source: WP-Turing_test]

## 3. Searle's Chinese Room
- John Searle (31 July 1932 – 17 September 2025), Berkeley. [source: WP-John_Searle; SEP-CR "(1932–2025)"] Consistent with *Analytic Philosophy since 1950* ("John Searle (1932–2025)"). (Optional biographical fact: in June 2019 the University of California revoked his emeritus status after a finding that he had violated sexual-harassment policies — WP-John_Searle; include only if the house style covers such facts.)
- "Minds, Brains and Programs", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 3: 417–57 (1980), published with 27 commentaries and Searle's replies; target was the claim that programs like Roger Schank's story-understanding scripts (Schank and Abelson 1977) "literally understand". [source: SEP-CR §§2.2, 3]
- Strong vs weak AI (Searle): strong AI = "the appropriately programmed computer really is a mind in the sense that computers given the right programs can be literally said to understand and have other cognitive states"; weak AI = computers are useful tools for studying the mind. [source: IEP-CR intro] The argument "is not directed at weak AI, nor does it purport to show that no machine can think – Searle says that brains are machines, and brains think." [source: SEP-CR §3]
- Searle's own 1999 summary: "Imagine a native English speaker who knows no Chinese locked in a room full of boxes of Chinese symbols (a data base) together with a book of instructions for manipulating the symbols (the program). Imagine that people outside the room send in other Chinese symbols which, unknown to the person in the room, are questions in Chinese (the input). And imagine that by following the instructions in the program the man in the room is able to pass out Chinese symbols which are correct answers to the questions (the output). The program enables the person in the room to pass the Turing Test for understanding Chinese but he does not understand a word of Chinese." And: "if the man in the room does not understand Chinese on the basis of implementing the appropriate program for understanding Chinese then neither does any other digital computer solely on that basis because no computer, qua computer, has anything the man does not have." [source: SEP-CR §1]
- 1980 wording: "formal" means "I can identify the symbols entirely by their shapes"; "I have inputs and outputs that are indistinguishable from those of the native Chinese speaker, and I can have any formal program you like, but I still understand nothing." (1980a, p. 418) [source: IEP-CR §1; WP-Symbol_grounding_problem]
- ARGUMENT (narrow argument, SEP's reconstruction as a reductio): Let a "program for L" be a program for conversing fluently in natural language L; a computing system is any system, human or otherwise, that can run a program.
  1. If Strong AI is true, then there is a program for Chinese, C, such that if any computing system runs C, that system thereby comes to understand Chinese.
  2. I could run C without thereby coming to understand Chinese. (supported by the thought experiment)
  3. Therefore Strong AI is false. [source: SEP-CR §3]
- Searle 2010 restates the conclusion in terms of consciousness and intentionality: "the implementation of the computer program is not by itself sufficient for consciousness or intentionality … Computation is defined purely formally or syntactically, whereas minds have actual mental or semantic contents". SEP: this shift "is not directly supported by the original 1980 argument". [source: SEP-CR §1]
- Influence: Pat Hayes (by 1991) defined cognitive science as "the ongoing research project of refuting Searle's argument"; Pinker (1997) counted well over 100 articles by the mid-1990s; "probably … the most widely discussed philosophical argument in cognitive science to appear since the Turing Test". [source: SEP-CR §1]
- Antecedents (SEP): Leibniz's mill; Turing's "paper machine" (a human following a chess program need not know chess); Dreyfus; and "many-humans" computers — Anatoly Mickevich (pseudonym A. Dneprov), "The Game" (1961: 1,400 maths students in a stadium translate a Portuguese sentence into Russian without knowing it), Lawrence Davis (1974), and Ned Block's "Chinese Nation"/"China brain" ("Troubles with Functionalism", 1978). [source: SEP-CR §2] (China brain is set out in *The Mind–Body Problem* notes — point there.)

### Replies and Searle's responses (SEP-CR §4 classifies them by what they concede)
- **Systems Reply** ("perhaps the most common reply", which Searle associated with Yale): the man is only the CPU; the whole system (man + rule book + database + scratch paper) understands. Pressed by Block, Copeland, Dennett, Hofstadter, Fodor, Haugeland, Kurzweil, Rey. **Searle's response:** let the man memorise the rules and database and do everything in his head, even walk outdoors conversing in Chinese; he is now the whole system and still has no way to attach "any meaning to the formal symbols" — he would not know what the Chinese word for hamburger means. **Counter:** Cole (1984) and Block (1998) deny the memoriser *becomes* the system — a "virtual mind" could understand while he does not; Copeland: inferring the whole lacks understanding because the part does is a part–whole fallacy. [source: SEP-CR §4.1] (Already sketched in *Analytic Philosophy since 1950* — keep consistent: "Searle answered that he could memorise the rules and still understand nothing".)
- **Robot Reply** (Boden, Crane, Dennett, Fodor, Harnad, Moravec, Rey at various times): put the computer in a robot with cameras and arms so it can connect symbols to things, as a child learns what a hamburger is; appeals to "wide content"/externalist semantics (cf. Putnam's brain in a vat 1981 — cross-ref *Scepticism*). **Searle's response:** sensors only add more syntactic input — imagine digitised camera signals arriving on a ticker tape in the room; the man still understands nothing. Also, the reply "tacitly concedes that cognition is not solely a matter of formal symbol manipulation". [source: SEP-CR §4.2; IEP-CR §2]
- **Brain Simulator Reply** (the Churchlands): a program simulating every neuron firing of a native Chinese speaker. **Searle's response:** replace it with a man operating a vast system of water pipes and valves arranged like the speaker's neurons — "obvious" there is no understanding; simulation is not duplication. SEP notes this now relies on "our Leibnizian intuition that water-works don't understand". **Counter:** gradual-replacement ("cyborgization") cases — Pylyshyn (1980): replacing brain cells one by one with input–output-equivalent chips, "you would in all likelihood just keep right on speaking exactly as you are doing now except that you would eventually stop meaning anything by it" — is that plausible? (Chalmers 1996 "fading qualia"; WP-Artificial_consciousness). [source: SEP-CR §4.3]
- **Other Minds Reply:** we know others understand only by behaviour, so if a computer passes the same tests we must credit it. **Searle (1980):** "The problem in this discussion is not about how I know that other people have cognitive states, but rather what it is that I am attributing to them when I attribute cognitive states to them… It is no answer to this argument to feign anesthesia." **Counter:** presuppositions about our own species may be pragmatic and could extend to machines (Dennett's intentional stance); Searle ties understanding to consciousness, which raises issues about toddlers and animals. [source: SEP-CR §4.4]
- **Luminous Room** (Paul and Patricia Churchland, *Scientific American*, January 1990, "Could a Machine Think?", answering Searle's "Is the Brain's Mind a Computer Program?"): waving a magnet in a dark room produces no visible light, yet Maxwell's theory that light is electromagnetic waves is true — intuitions about such systems can mislead; "no neuron in my brain understands English, although my whole brain does". [source: SEP-CR §§3, 4.3]
- Hofstadter (1980): the argument is a "religious diatribe against AI, masquerading as a serious scientific argument". Dennett (2013): "clearly a fallacious and misleading argument". [source: IEP-CR §5; SEP-CR conclusion] "There is no consensus". [source: SEP-CR conclusion]

### Syntax and semantics (Searle 1984, 1989, 1990)
- Searle's "derivation from axioms" (1990 formulation, *Scientific American*, p. 27; first in *Minds, Brains and Science*, 1984): ARGUMENT
  - (A1) Programs are formal (syntactic).
  - (A2) Minds have mental contents (semantics).
  - (A3) Syntax by itself is neither constitutive of nor sufficient for semantics.
  - (C1) So programs are neither constitutive of nor sufficient for minds.
  - Added (p. 29): (A4) Brains cause minds → (C2) any other system capable of causing minds would need causal powers (at least) equivalent to those of brains. [source: IEP-CR §3; SEP-CR §5.1] The Chinese Room supports A3 (Churchlands: it is meant to shore up axiom 3). [source: IEP-CR §3]
  - Searle 1989: "Formal symbols by themselves can never be enough for mental contents, because the symbols, by definition, have no meaning (or interpretation, or semantics) except insofar as someone outside the system gives it to them". [source: SEP-CR §5.1]
  - **Objection** (Cole 1984, Dennett 1987, Boden 1988, Chalmers 1996): a running computer is not "syntax alone" but a complex physical causal system embedded in the world; a program on a shelf causes nothing, but neither does a recipe — Chalmers's parody: recipes are syntactic, syntax isn't sufficient for crumbliness, cakes are crumbly, so implementing a recipe isn't sufficient for making a cake. [source: SEP-CR §5.1] **Reply** (Searle, from the 1990s): computation, or syntax, is "observer-relative", not an intrinsic feature of reality — "…you can assign a computational interpretation to anything" (Searle 2002b, p. 17), even the molecules in the paint on the wall; so mind cannot be reduced to computation. [source: SEP-CR §5.1]
- Searle's positive view (biological naturalism): "Conscious states are caused by lower level neurobiological processes in the brain and are themselves higher level features of the brain" (2002). [source: SEP-CR §5.3]

### Harnad's symbol grounding problem
- Stevan Harnad, "The Symbol Grounding Problem", *Physica D* 42 (1–3): 335–346 (1990). How can the "semantic interpretation of a formal symbol system" be made "intrinsic to the system, rather than just parasitic on the meanings in our heads"? How can meanings of "meaningless symbol tokens, manipulated solely on the basis of their (arbitrary) shapes" be grounded "in anything but other meaningless symbols"? How do you "ever get off the symbol/symbol merry-go-round"? Harnad links it to the "problem of intrinsic meaning (or 'intentionality') in Searle's (1980) celebrated 'Chinese Room Argument'". [source: WP-Symbol_grounding_problem]
- Worked example (dictionary regress): looking up an unknown word in a dictionary of a language you don't know, you "cycle endlessly from one meaningless definition to another". [source: WP-Symbol_grounding_problem — this paragraph is flagged "citation needed" on WP; present as an illustration, not as Harnad's words]
- IEP lists the symbol-grounding problem (Searle 1980, Harnad 1990) among problems for classical cognitivism, with the frame problem and the common-sense problem; Harnad endorsed a version of the Robot Reply and proposed the Total Turing Test. [source: IEP-EC §1; SEP-CR §4.2; SEP-TT §5.3.1] The CRA "led Stevan Harnad and others on a quest for 'symbol grounding' in AI". [source: SEP-CR conclusion]

## 4. Functionalism, computation, embodiment
### Functionalism and the computational theory of mind (cross-ref *The Mind–Body Problem*, *Analytic Philosophy since 1950*)
- Functionalism: "what makes something a thought, desire, pain (or any other type of mental state) depends not on its internal constitution, but solely on its function, or the role it plays, in the cognitive system of which it is a part". Pain example: the state caused by bodily injury, producing the belief something is wrong and the desire to be out of it, anxiety, wincing or moaning; silicon Martians or androids could be in pain too — "multiply realized". [source: SEP-Fun §1]
- Machine-state functionalism (Putnam 1960 "Minds and Machines", 1967): any creature with a mind can be regarded as a Turing machine whose operation is fully specified by a machine table ("If the machine is in state Si, and receives input Ij, it will go into state Sk and produce output Ol"); Putnam 1967 preferred a *probabilistic* automaton. Putnam later doubted it (1988, *Representation and Reality*). [source: SEP-Fun §3.1; SEP-CTM §3.1]
- Title discrepancy: SEP-CTM's bibliography gives Putnam 1967 as "Psychophysical Predicates"; the *Mind–Body Problem* notes (via WP) and standard references give "Psychological Predicates" (reprinted as "The Nature of Mental States", 1975). Avoid the title or use "Psychological Predicates" with the reprint title. [source: SEP-CTM bibliography; docs/…/mind--mind-body-problem.md]
- Classical computational theory of mind (CCTM): "the mind is a computational system similar in important respects to a Turing machine"; not a metaphor — "CCTM holds that the mind literally is a computing system"; McCulloch and Pitts (1943) first suggested a Turing-machine-like model; "Hilary Putnam (1967) introduced CCTM into philosophy". [source: SEP-CTM §3, §3.1]
- Block and Fodor (1972) objections to machine functionalism: productivity (a finite machine table cannot pair with a potential infinity of thoughts) and systematicity (who can think *John loves Mary* can think *Mary loves John*). [source: SEP-CTM §3.1]
- **Fodor, *The Language of Thought* (1975):** thinking occurs in a mental language ("Mentalese") whose sentences have compositional meaning (worked example: Mentalese *whales are mammals* + *Moby Dick is a whale* → *Moby Dick is a mammal*). Fodor argued abductively that our best psychological theories posit it — CCTM+RTM is "the only game in town". LOTH has medieval roots: Ockham's *Summa Logicae* (c. 1323) gave "the first systematic treatment" (cross-ref *Scotus, Ockham*). [source: SEP-LOT intro; SEP-CTM §3.2]
- Logic Theorist (Newell and Simon 1956) proved 38 of the first 52 theorems of *Principia Mathematica*, once finding a simpler proof. [source: SEP-CTM §2]

### Connectionism vs classical AI (brief)
- Connectionism emerged in the 1980s as a rival: networks of nodes with weighted connections, learning by adjusting weights (backpropagation). [source: SEP-CTM §4]
- **Fodor and Pylyshyn (1988, "Connectionism and Cognitive Architecture: A Critical Analysis", *Cognition* 28: 3–71):** systematicity and productivity fail in connectionist models "except when the connectionist model implements a classical model"; so connectionism is at best an implementation-level story. Replies: networks can be systematic without classical architecture (Smolensky 1991; Chalmers 1990; van Gelder 1990; Horgan and Tienson 1996); systematicity is exaggerated (Johnson 2004). [source: SEP-CTM §4.3] The Churchlands defend a connectionist "vector transformer" view of the brain. [source: SEP-CR §4.3]
- Today's large language models are deep neural networks — heirs of connectionism (SEP-CTM §4 lists ChatGPT under deep neural network successes). The systematicity debate is thus live again (researcher's framing; SEP-CTM: "Debate on these fundamental issues seems poised to continue").

### Dreyfus and embodiment
- Hubert Dreyfus (15 Oct 1929 – 22 Apr 2017): RAND report "Alchemy and Artificial Intelligence" (1965, written while at MIT; ~100 pages); moved to Berkeley 1968; *What Computers Can't Do* (1972; revised 1979; *What Computers Still Can't Do* 1992); *Mind over Machine* (1986, with his brother Stuart). Drew on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty (he co-translated Merleau-Ponty's *Sense and Non-Sense*). [source: SEP-CR §2.2; WP-Hubert_Dreyfus; WP-Dreyfus_views] Consistent with *Phenomenology* and the twentieth-century overview, which already say he "turned Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty against classical artificial intelligence" — don't repeat; build on it.
- Dreyfus's four assumptions of AI (one of which AI needs): **biological** (the brain works by on/off switches), **psychological** (the mind operates on bits of information by formal rules), **epistemological** (all knowledge can be formalised), **ontological** (the world consists of independent facts representable by independent symbols). Each is "taken by workers in AI as an axiom … whereas it is, in fact, one hypothesis among others". [source: WP-Dreyfus_views, quoting Dreyfus 1979 p. 157]
- Core claim: human expertise rests on an unconscious, informal background and skilled "knowing-how" (cf. Heidegger's ready-to-hand), not explicit rules; experts "size up the situation". [source: WP-Dreyfus_views]
- Reception: met with "ridicule and outright hostility" in the 1960s; later vindicated in part — Crevier (1993): "time has proven the accuracy and perceptiveness of some of Dreyfus's comments"; Dreyfus in 2007: "I figure I won and it's over—they've given up." Simon had predicted (after the General Problem Solver) that within about a decade a computer would be chess champion and prove an important new theorem. [source: WP-Dreyfus_views] (Deep Blue beat Kasparov in 1997 — SEP-CTM §2.)
- **Embodied/enactive cognition (brief):** cognition depends on "the kinds of experience that come from having a body with various sensorimotor capacities" embedded in "a more encompassing biological, psychological and cultural context" (Varela, Thompson and Rosch, *The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience*, 1991, pp. 172–3); SEP-CTM calls it "an influential early statement" of embodied cognition. Enactivism: "cognition emerges from or is constituted by sensorimotor activity" (autopoietic, sensorimotor — O'Regan and Noë 2001, Noë 2004 — and radical strands). [source: WP-Embodied_cognition; SEP-CTM §7.5; SEP-EC §2.4] (*Phenomenology* already notes its "roots in Buddhism and phenomenology".)
- **Brooks:** Rodney Brooks, "Intelligence without Representation", *Artificial Intelligence* 47 (1991): 139–59. Against Shakey (SRI, 1966–72; sense–model–plan–act; "some tasks would take days"), Brooks's "Creatures" use a "subsumption architecture" of competing layers (robot Allen: Avoid, Wander, Explore) that connect "perception to action directly" and "use the world as its own model"; his ideas reached industry (Roomba). Objection: the layers still pass signals that "seem to have a semantics" ("go ahead", "stop!"), so representation may not be gone. [source: SEP-EC §4.1]
- Computationalist reply to embodiment: computational models can include sensory input and motor output; CTM "can incorporate any legitimate insights" (Clark 2014; Rupert 2009). [source: SEP-CTM §7.5]

## 5. The Lucas–Penrose (Gödelian) argument (cross-ref *Formal Logic*)
- J. R. Lucas, "Minds, Machines and Gödel", *Philosophy* 36 (137): 112–137 (**1961**): Gödel's theorem "proves that Mechanism is false, that is, that minds cannot be explained as machines"; "given any machine which is consistent and capable of doing simple arithmetic, there is a formula it is incapable of producing as being true … but which we can see to be true." [source: SEP-Göd §6.3] (IEP-AI dates it "Lucas 1965" with the same journal volume — an IEP slip; use 1961.)
- Roger Penrose, *The Emperor's New Mind: Concerning Computers, Minds and the Laws of Physics* (Oxford, published 8 Nov 1989), and *Shadows of the Mind* (1994); with Stuart Hameroff he proposed a quantum ("orchestrated objective reduction", microtubules) basis for non-computable mental processes. [source: SEP-Göd §6.3; WP-The_Emperor's_New_Mind; WP-Penrose–Lucas_argument] Penrose: "mathematical understanding … cannot be reduced to blind calculation!" [source: WP-Penrose–Lucas_argument]
- Antecedents: Turing considered and rejected such an argument in the late 1940s (and in 1950 as the "Mathematical Objection"); Nagel and Newman's *Gödel's Proof* (1958) drew an "unqualified anti-mechanist conclusion"; Gödel's own 1951 Gibbs lecture concluded only a disjunction: "either … the human mind … infinitely surpasses the power of any finite machine, or else there exist absolutely unsolvable diophantine problems" — he leaned to the first but admitted mechanism is consistent with his theorems. [source: SEP-Göd §§6.3–6.4]
- ARGUMENT (SEP-TT's reconstruction of the Mathematical Objection): (1) Let C be a digital computer. (2) C is subject to the Lucas–Penrose constraint, so there is an "unanswerable" question q for C. (3) If an entity is not subject to the constraint, no question is unanswerable for it. (4) The human intellect is not subject to the constraint. (5) So q is answerable by a human. (6) By asking q, a human could tell the computer from a human. (7) So C may fail the Turing test (and, by extension, is not a mind like ours). SEP: premise (3) should be challenged, and premise (4) is "merely asserted". [source: SEP-TT §2.3]
- **Standard objection (Putnam 1960; Boolos 1968; Shapiro 1998):** Gödel's theorem is conditional — the Gödel sentence is true *if the system is consistent*; so the anti-mechanist needs humans always to be able to see whether a given formal system is consistent, which "is highly implausible" (Davis 1990). "There is wide consensus that they fail." Detailed critiques of Penrose: Boolos 1990, Davis 1990/1993, Feferman 1995, Lindström 2001, Shapiro 2003. Lucas and Penrose replied (Lucas 1996; Penrose 1995, 1997); Searle (1997) partly defended Penrose. [source: SEP-Göd §6.3] WP: "The argument is rejected by most scholars." [source: WP-Penrose–Lucas_argument]
- Practical reply (Oppy/Dowe): Gödel questions for a given machine require detailed knowledge of its program, unavailable to interrogators. [source: SEP-TT §2.3]
- Consistency with *Formal Logic*: that chapter warns, quoting Raatikainen, against reading the first theorem as showing "there are truths that cannot be proved" (it concerns provability in a given formal system). Keep this chapter consistent: say "unprovable *in that system*". 

## 6. Today's systems: what they do and do not settle (all hedged, dated)
- Transformer: "Attention Is All You Need" (2017), eight Google authors (Ashish Vaswani, Noam Shazeer, Niki Parmar, Jakob Uszkoreit, Llion Jones, Aidan Gomez, Łukasz Kaiser, Illia Polosukhin; listed as "equal contributors", order randomised); aimed at machine translation; became the main architecture of large language models; cited more than 150,000 times as of 2026. [source: WP-Attention_Is_All_You_Need]
- An LLM is a machine-learning model (typically a neural network) "trained on a vast amount of text"; GPT-style models are "pre-trained to predict the next word" then fine-tuned (e.g. by reinforcement learning from human feedback) to follow instructions. [source: WP-Large_language_model]
- Milestones: GPT-3 (2020); ChatGPT (released November 2022 per WP-Turing_test; "2022" per SEP-CTM). [source: SEP-CTM §2; WP-Turing_test] SEP-TT (2021): GPT-3 is "not close to passing the Turing Test… it is, at best, highly contentious whether it is a site of understanding"; Chalmers (2020) speculated it "suggests a potential mindless path to artificial general intelligence". [source: SEP-TT §1] (Dated — the SEP-TT entry predates ChatGPT.)
- **Turing-test results (hedge heavily):** Nature news (Biever, 25 July 2023): "ChatGPT broke the Turing test". Jones and Bergen (arXiv March 2025; published *PNAS* 26 May 2026, "Large language models pass a standard three-party Turing test"): pre-registered three-party tests with five-minute conversations; GPT-4.5 prompted to adopt a humanlike persona was judged the human 73% of the time (significantly more often than the real humans); LLaMa-3.1-405B 56%; baselines ELIZA 23%, GPT-4o 21%. [source: WP-Turing_test] Note the parallel with Turing's own numbers (five minutes; 70%) — arguably his first prediction came true about 25 years late (researcher's framing; Copeland's "about fifty years" point in SEP-TT §3.2 is relevant). Critics (e.g. Gary Marcus) say such tests show "how easy it is to fool humans". [source: WP-Turing_test]
- **"Stochastic parrots":** Emily M. Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major and "Shmargaret Shmitchell" (pseudonym of Margaret Mitchell), "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big? 🦜", ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency (FAccT '21), March 2021. [source: WP-Stochastic_parrot; WS-1] Key sentence (as quoted, with ellipsis, by WP): LMs are "stitching together sequences of linguistic forms … observed in its vast training data, according to probabilistic information about how they combine, but without any reference to meaning". [source: WP-Stochastic_parrot] The paper also stressed environmental and financial costs and bias. Google asked for retraction or removal of Google authors' names; Gebru's departure from Google followed (2 Dec 2020). Bender is credited with coining the term (Quote Investigator). American Dialect Society nominated it 2023 AI-related word of the year. [source: WP-Stochastic_parrot; WS-1]
- **Bender and Koller (2020) octopus test:** "Climbing towards NLU: On Meaning, Form, and Understanding in the Age of Data", ACL 2020, pp. 5185–5198 (best thematic paper). Thesis: a system trained only on linguistic *form* has a priori no way to learn *meaning*. Thought experiment: two English speakers, A and B, stranded on separate islands, communicate by an undersea cable; a "hyperintelligent deep-sea octopus", O, taps the cable, learns the statistical patterns and starts impersonating B; it fails when A, attacked by a bear, asks how to defend herself with sticks, since O has no idea what bears or sticks are. [source: WS-2 (ACL Anthology paper, authors' slides, commentary)] Commentators treat it as an intuition pump; some argued GPT-3 answers such scenarios reasonably. [source: WS-2] (The exact wording of the bear episode is from summaries; paraphrase.)
- ARGUMENT (the form/meaning argument, synthesised from WS-2 and SEP-CR §5.1 — present as a reconstruction): (1) LLMs are trained only on text, i.e. on form. (2) Meaning is a relation between form and something outside language (communicative intent, the world). (3) Nothing in form alone fixes that relation. (4) So an LLM trained only on form cannot learn meaning. **Objections:** fine-tuning with human feedback goes beyond pure pre-training (Kelsey Piper); interpretability work finds internal "world models" — Othello-GPT (a small transformer trained on Othello moves developed an internal board representation that, when edited, changes its predictions correctly), and a Karel-programs model; Anthropic's 2025 interpretability study of its Claude 3.5 Haiku reported "internally generated plans" and "remarkably general abstractions"; models increasingly take images and act through tools (Robot Reply revived). **Reply:** internal structure and benchmark success show sophisticated form-processing, not that symbols are grounded or understood (Searle/Harnad). [source: WP-Stochastic_parrot; SEP-CR §4.6] (NB: Claude/Anthropic example — the chapter may prefer the neutral Othello-GPT case.)
- SEP-CR §4.6 (2024): LLMs "can generate paragraph after paragraph that may be all news to their coders"; Hossenfelder (2023) says chatbots understand in the sense we understand quantum mechanics; when asked in 2024, ChatGPT both claimed to "understand English words" and agreed Searle's argument applies to it — "paradoxically, the system appears to understand that it doesn't understand"; Cole concludes that if systems use language as proficiently as humans in all contexts yet are said not to understand, "'Really' understanding becomes epiphenomenal: undetectable, causally inert, and irrelevant." [source: SEP-CR §4.6] Good closing provocation, attributed to Cole/SEP.
- **LaMDA episode (2022, as illustration):** LaMDA ("Language Model for Dialogue Applications") announced at Google I/O, 18 May 2021. On 11 June 2022 the *Washington Post* reported that Google engineer Blake Lemoine had been placed on paid administrative leave after telling executives that LaMDA had become sentient; he later called it "a person" and an "alien intelligence of terrestrial origin" (Wired) and hired a lawyer on its behalf at its request; Google fired him on 22 July 2022, calling his claims "wholly unfounded". Experts (Gary Marcus, Yann LeCun and others) rejected the claim; Nick Bostrom noted the lack of agreed criteria warrants some uncertainty. [source: WP-LaMDA; WP-Artificial_consciousness] Use as an instance of the ELIZA effect writ large, and of the problem of evidence.
- **Chalmers, "Could a Large Language Model Be Conscious?"** — invited NeurIPS lecture, 28 November 2022; arXiv 2303.07103 (2023); *Boston Review*, 9 August 2023. Claims: given mainstream assumptions in consciousness science there are "significant obstacles to consciousness in current models: for example, their lack of recurrent processing, a global workspace, and unified agency", but "it is quite possible that these obstacles will be overcome in the next decade or so"; extended "LLM+" systems (vision, action, tools) might meet the criteria. His slides give "credence in current LLM sentience: <10%?" (tentative). [source: WS-3; WP-Artificial_consciousness] (No verified figure for future systems — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- **Butlin, Long et al., "Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness"** (arXiv 2308.08708, submitted 17 Aug 2023; 19 authors including Patrick Butlin, Robert Long, Yoshua Bengio, Jonathan Birch, Stephen Fleming, Chris Frith, Grace Lindsay, Liad Mudrik, Megan Peters, Eric Schwitzgebel). Method: derive "indicator properties" from scientific theories of consciousness (recurrent processing, global workspace, higher-order, predictive processing, attention schema), stated computationally, and assess AI systems against them. Conclusion: "no current AI systems are conscious, but also … there are no obvious technical barriers to building AI systems which satisfy these indicators." [source: WS-4; WP-Artificial_consciousness] Caveat: the method assumes a form of computational functionalism (widely reported framing; WS-4 could not confirm the phrase — say "assumes that implementing the right computations would suffice", hedged) and the theories themselves "remain incomplete and subject to ongoing debate". [source: WP-Artificial_consciousness]
- **Moral status (brief; cross-ref *Consciousness*, *Ethics of Technology and AI*):** moral agent vs moral patient; "The standard view is that AI and robotics systems have no moral status of any kind … but this view has come under pressure"; Gunkel (*Robot Rights*, 2018), Danaher (2020) urge taking robot rights seriously; Coeckelbergh's "relational turn"; Bryson against; "a trend in favour of the view that sentience is at least a necessary condition for moral status"; concern whether creating conscious AI would be ethical (Butlin et al. 2023); calls for a "moratorium on synthetic phenomenology" (Metzinger 2021 — WP: until 2050, against an "explosion of artificial suffering"). [source: SEP-EAI §§2.7.1–2.7.2; WP-Artificial_consciousness] *Consciousness* (published) already lists Birch, *The Edge of Sentience: Risk and Precaution in Humans, Other Animals, and AI* (2024) — cross-refer, don't duplicate.
- Chalmers's fading/dancing qualia (gradual silicon replacement) argue functional isomorphs have the same experience; critics say this begs the question (Van Heuveln et al.; Mogensen). [source: WP-Artificial_consciousness] (Probably belongs in *Consciousness*; mention only.)

## 7. Other traditions: "artificial persons" and where cognition belongs (brief, historical context)
- **Chinese — the Liezi's artificer Yan Shi:** the *Liezi* tells of King Mu of Zhou being shown a life-size human figure made by the artificer Yan Shi; it walked, sang in tune and danced; when it winked and "made advances to the ladies in attendance", the angry king would have executed Yan Shi, who took it apart to show it was "only a construction of leather, wood, glue and lacquer"; the king found all the internal organs artificial, and removing the heart stopped it speaking, the liver its seeing, the kidneys its walking; "The king was delighted." Translation: **Joseph Needham**, *Science and Civilisation in China*, vol. 2, p. 53 (WP quotes Needham; his word "robot" is his own modern rendering). [source: WP-King_Mu_of_Zhou; WP-Automaton] Philosophical hook (researcher's framing): the king tests the figure by taking out organs — a functional test of which part does what.
- **Dating the Liezi — conflict:** WP-King_Mu says "3rd century BC text"; WP-Automaton says "believed to have originated around 400 BCE and compiled around the fourth century CE"; WP-Liezi reports A. C. Graham's textual studies dating the compilation to c. 300 CE (the "guiding hand" probably Zhang Zhan's father or grandfather), a dating that "by now prevails also in the West"; T. H. Barrett: opinion "divided as to whether it is an ancient work with later interpolations or a forgery confected from ancient sources". Say: "the *Liezi*, a Daoist book attributed to Lie Yukou but probably compiled around 300 CE from older materials". King Mu: traditional reign c. 10th century BCE (WP-Liezi gives "r. 1023?–983? BCE"; WP-Automaton "1023–957 BCE" — legendary, don't give dates). [source: WP-Liezi; WP-Automaton; WP-King_Mu_of_Zhou] Graham's translation: *The Book of Lieh-tzǔ: A Classic of Tao* (1960; Columbia 1990). [source: WP-Liezi]
- **Islamic — al-Jazarī (1136–1206):** Badīʿ az-Zamān Abu l-ʿIzz ibn Ismāʿīl ibn ar-Razzāz al-Jazarī, engineer at the Artuqid court in Upper Mesopotamia; his *Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices* (*al-Jāmiʿ … fī ṣināʿat al-ḥiyal*), commissioned by Nasr al-Din Mahmud, first edition April 1206 (manuscript Ahmet III 3472, probably made at Amid/Diyarbakır). Automata include a drink-serving "waitress", a hand-washing automaton for the king's ritual ablutions (a jointed copper figure pouring water and then offering towel, comb and mirror), hydropowered peacocks, and musical automata; camshafts used to drive automata. Translated by Donald R. Hill (1973). Lamia Balafrej (2022) stresses the "automated slave" figure in the treatise. [source: WP-Ismail_al-Jazari; WP-Automaton] Hedge "first"-type claims (WP's "first … humanoid" style claims rest on Rosheim 1994; avoid).
- **Indian — yantras:** Bhoja's Sanskrit treatise *Samarāṅgaṇa Sūtradhāra* (11th century) has a chapter (ch. 31) on mechanical contrivances (*yantras*), describing mechanical bees and birds, fountains shaped like humans and animals, and male and female dolls that refilled oil lamps, danced, played instruments and re-enacted scenes from Hindu mythology. [source: WP-Samarangana_Sutradhara; WP-Automaton, citing Varadpande 1987 and Wujastyk 2003] (Descriptions, not evidence of built machines — hedge.)
- **Indian — Nyāya: cognition belongs to a self, not to matter** (cross-ref *Classical Indian Schools*, *The Mind–Body Problem*): ARGUMENT (IEP-Ny §2a; NS 1.1.10 and commentaries): (1) Desire, cognition, pleasure, pain and volition are qualities. (2) All qualities inhere in substances. (3) So there is a substance to which desire and the rest belong. (4) None of the material elements (earth, water etc.) are the bearers of desire and the rest. (5) So there is a special, non-material substance: a self (*ātman*). Further arguments aim to show "that the physical body, as a product of material elements cannot be the fundamental locus of conscious states." [source: IEP-Ny §2a] **Objection (Cārvāka/Lokāyata materialists):** "Our observation does not bring forth any instance of a disincarnate consciousness. For the manifestation of life and consciousness, body is an inalienable factor"; sensation and perception arise only as conditioned by "the bodily mechanism"; foods and drinks affect the intellect (quoting Mittal). [source: IEP-Lok §2b] Relevance: the Indian debate asks whether matter organised a certain way can be the bearer of cognition — the same question the AI debate asks about silicon (researcher's framing). **Buddhist reply** to Nyāya: no enduring self; continuity by causal connections in a stream. [source: IEP-Ny §2a]
- **Jewish — the Golem (hedged):** Hebrew *golem* occurs once in the Bible (Psalm 139, "my unformed substance"); in the Talmud (Sanhedrin 38b) Adam is first a golem; Sanhedrin 65b: Rava creates a man and sends him to Rav Zeira, who speaks to him; "he does not answer", so Zeira says, "You were created by the sages; return to your dust" — "early on, the main disability of the golem was its inability to speak". Earliest written instructions for making a golem: Eleazar of Worms (late 12th–early 13th c.), *Sode Raza*. The famous Prague golem of Rabbi Judah Loew (late 16th c.) is, in "the general view of historians and critics", "a German literary invention of the early 19th century" (first writers 1837–47, per John Neubauer); Yudel Rosenberg's *Nifl'os Maharal* (1909) presented a purported eyewitness account. Some tales: *emet* ("truth") on its forehead; erasing the aleph leaves *met* ("dead"). [source: WP-Golem] Nice parallel: the Talmudic test of a made man is speech — Descartes's first test.
- Greek myth: Hephaestus's self-moving golden tripods (*Iliad* 18). [source: WP-Automaton] (Optional.)

## Debates
- **Is the Turing test sufficient for intelligence?** Turing/Oppy–Dowe (good criterion or strong probabilistic evidence) vs Block (Blockhead: behaviour from brute look-up isn't intelligence), French (only tests culturally-oriented, subcognitive human intelligence), Searle (behaviour isn't understanding). [source: SEP-TT §4]
- **Did Turing mean the machine to imitate a woman?** Genova, Traiger, Sterrett vs Copeland, Piccinini, Moor (standard reading). [source: SEP-TT §3.1]
- **Was Turing's prediction falsified?** Most say yes for 2000; Copeland: "about fifty years"; recent studies (2023–2026) report LLMs passing five-minute tests. [source: SEP-TT §3.2; WP-Turing_test]
- **Does the Chinese Room work?** Searle, Harnad (against Systems Reply), Horgan vs Block, Dennett, Hofstadter, Copeland, Churchlands, Cole; "no consensus". [source: SEP-CR §§4–5]
- **Can semantics come from syntax/causal embedding?** Searle vs Cole, Dennett, Boden, Chalmers; externalists (Robot Reply) vs Searle's internalism. [source: SEP-CR §§4.2, 5.1]
- **Classical vs connectionist architecture.** Fodor and Pylyshyn 1988 vs Smolensky, Chalmers, van Gelder, Horgan and Tienson; Gallistel and King vs Dayan, Donahoe, Mole. [source: SEP-CTM §4.3]
- **Embodiment vs computation.** Dreyfus, Varela–Thompson–Rosch, Brooks, Chemero, Hutto and Myin vs computationalists (Clark, Rupert) who absorb embodiment. [source: SEP-EC §4.1; SEP-CTM §7.5]
- **Lucas–Penrose.** Lucas, Penrose (Searle partly) vs Putnam, Boolos, Davis, Feferman, Shapiro — "wide consensus that they fail". [source: SEP-Göd §6.3]
- **Do LLMs understand?** Bender, Gebru, Koller ("stochastic parrots", form ≠ meaning) vs critics citing fine-tuning, benchmarks, interpretability ("world models"); Hossenfelder's partial-understanding view; Cole's "epiphenomenal understanding" worry. [source: WP-Stochastic_parrot; SEP-CR §4.6; WS-2]
- **Could current or near-future AI be conscious, and would it matter morally?** Chalmers (current <10%?, obstacles may fall within a decade), Butlin et al. (none now, no obvious barriers) vs biological views (Searle; consciousness may need specific biological substrates — Mogensen, Seth per WP); moral-status debate: Gunkel, Danaher, Coeckelbergh vs Bryson; Metzinger's moratorium. [source: WS-3; WS-4; WP-Artificial_consciousness; SEP-EAI §2.7]
- **Is Hobbes a forerunner of AI?** Haugeland ("prophetically launching AI") vs SEP's Duncan ("at least rather over-dramatic"). [source: SEP-Hob §2.4]
- **Liezi's date.** Ancient core vs c. 300 CE compilation (Graham; prevailing). [source: WP-Liezi]
- **Golem of Prague's age.** Traditional attribution to the Maharal vs 19th-century literary invention (historians' general view). [source: WP-Golem]
