## 1. Is "aesthetics" a cross-cultural category? (framing)
- SEP-CPC opens: "Although there is no word such as 'aesthetics' in Chinese philosophy, if we understand it not in the restrictive sense of the science of beauty but in the broader sense of reflection on the theory of art, then aesthetics does indeed exist in Chinese philosophy." [source: SEP-CPC intro]
- The terms "philosophy" and "aesthetics" "belong to a modern vocabulary imported into China via Japan in the early 20th century" (Escande citing Ghilardi 2015; 2021); the term has given rise to controversies regarding the Chinese literary tradition (Egan 2006, Saussy 1993). [source: SEP-CPC intro]
- SEP-JA: although the Japanese produced great art and wrote about it for centuries, "the philosophical discipline in Japan corresponding to Western 'aesthetics' did not get underway until the nineteenth century"; its key ideas (mono no aware, wabi, sabi, yūgen, iki, kire) all arose "before aesthetics was formally established as a discipline". [source: SEP-JA intro]
- In Chinese theory "only artists may theorize" (unlike Europe, where serious writing on art is mainly by philosophers); rankings take the artist's morality into account; the focus is on the whole creative process and the relation between maker and viewer, not the artwork as object. [source: SEP-CPC §1.1]
- Court painters, academicians and professional artists "wrote close to nothing" about their work; sculpture, architecture, garden design were not considered "art" and theory on buildings starts only in the 12th c., on gardens in the 17th (Ji Cheng 1631–34). [source: SEP-CPC §1.1]
- Saito (SEP-Ev): in traditions such as Japanese and Chinese, "aesthetic practices permeate people's daily life"; "One of the findings of comparative aesthetics is that a greater emphasis is placed on the aesthetics of everyday life in many non-Western cultures than in the West (Higgins 2005)." Inuit and Navajo: aesthetic considerations integrated into making tools (Papanek 1995; Witherspoon 1996). [source: SEP-Ev §1]
- Japanese arts are called "ways" (*chadō*, way of tea; *shodō*, way of writing/calligraphy), reflecting Confucian self-cultivation; the Chinese scholar-official was expected to master the "Six Arts" (ritual, music, calligraphy, mathematics, archery, charioteering). [source: SEP-JA §1]
- Classical Japanese thought: the world of flux is "the only reality: there is no conception of some stable 'Platonic' realm above or behind it"; the arts reflect impermanence (*mujō*), "sometimes lamenting but more often celebrating it". [source: SEP-JA §1]

## 2. India: Bharata's Nāṭyaśāstra and the rasa sūtra
- *Nāṭyaśāstra*: Sanskrit treatise on the performing arts attributed to the sage Bharata; author actually unknown, possibly several authors (scholars disagree). Composition date unknown: estimates range from 500 BCE to 500 CE; most scholars put the first complete version between 200 BCE and 200 CE (Lidova 2014). [source: WP-Natya_Shastra]
- SEP-Th independently gives "somewhere between 200 BCE and 200 CE" (citing Ghosh's 1951 translation and Schwartz 2004). [source: SEP-Th §1]
- Structure: about 6,000 verses in 36 chapters in the most studied version (some manuscripts 37 or 38); tradition says originally 12,000 verses. Chapters 6–7 present rasa theory; it calls drama a "fifth Veda" (1.14–15). Revised in 9th-century Kashmir; some recensions interpolated. [source: WP-Natya_Shastra]
- *Rasa* literally "juice, essence or taste"; the word appears in the Rigveda (liquid, extract, flavour). The ideal spectator is the *sahṛdaya*, "one who has heart". [source: WP-Rasa_(aesthetics)] SEP-EmI: rasa "can mean juice, sap, essence, condiment or even flavour and refers to the different sentiments invoked by a work of art". [source: SEP-EmI intro]
- **Rasa sūtra** (Nāṭyaśāstra ch. 6): "*Rasa* is produced from a combination of Determinants (*vibhava*), Consequents (*anubhava*) and Transitory States (*vyabhicaribhava*)" — translator Daniel Meyer-Dinkgräfe as cited by WP (WP labels it 6.109; numbering varies by edition — in Ghosh's edition the sūtra is a prose passage after verse 31; NOT checked, so cite only "Nāṭyaśāstra ch. 6"). [source: WP-Rasa_(aesthetics); WS-1 confirms this is not Ghosh's wording]
  - Alternative renderings (WS-1, not primary): G. N. Devy (2002) "Now Rasas arise from a proper combination of the stimulants (Vibhav), the physical consequents (Anubhav), and the transient emotional states (Vyabhichari Bhav)".
- Glosses: *vibhāva* = causes (both the human object, *ālambana*, and excitants, *uddīpana*); *anubhāva* = ensuants/effects of emotion; *sañcārī/vyabhicārī bhāva* = passing feelings ancillary to a mood. [source: WP-Rasa_(aesthetics)]
- **Eight rasas** (Nāṭyaśāstra): śṛṅgāra (erotic/love), hāsya (comic), raudra (furious), karuṇa (compassion/pathos), bībhatsa (disgust), bhayānaka (terror), vīra (heroic), adbhuta (wonder). Each has a presiding deity and a colour (WP's two articles disagree on several deities/colours — don't use the deity/colour details). [source: WP-Rasa_(aesthetics); WP-Indian_aesthetics]
- **Eight sthāyibhāvas** (stable emotions) matching them: rati (love), hāsa (mirth), śoka (sorrow), krodha (anger), utsāha (energy), bhaya (fear), jugupsā (disgust), vismaya (astonishment). [source: WP-Rasa_(aesthetics)]
- **Eight sāttvika bhāvas** (involuntary physical signs) incl. stupefaction, sweating, horripilation, broken voice, trembling, pallor, tears, swoon. [source: WP-Rasa_(aesthetics)]
- **Ninth rasa, śānta (peace)**: added later; its acceptance "had to undergo much struggle between the sixth and tenth centuries" before "navarasa" (nine rasas) became established; Abhinavagupta argued for it, likening śānta to the string of a jewelled necklace on which the other eight are strung. [source: WP-Rasa_(aesthetics); WP-Indian_aesthetics] (WP-Indian_aesthetics' claim that Ānandavardhana "introduces" śānta is tagged citation-needed — do not use.)
- Rasa applies across Indian arts: dance (Bharatanatyam, Kathakali, Kathak, Odissi etc.), music (rāga), poetry, painting, sculpture; Satyajit Ray applied rasa method in the Apu Trilogy (1955–59). [source: WP-Rasa_(aesthetics)]

## 3. The commentators' debate (how does rasa "arise"?)
- Abhinavagupta's *Abhinavabhāratī* is the oldest extant commentary on the Nāṭyaśāstra; it reports earlier commentators now lost — Kīrtidhara, Bhāskara, Lollaṭa, Śaṅkuka, Nāyaka, Harṣa, Tauta. [source: WP-Abhinavabharati; WP-Natya_Shastra]
- Caveat: Lollaṭa's, Śaṅkuka's and Nāyaka's works on the rasa sūtra are lost; their views are known mainly through Abhinavagupta, who quotes and criticises them. [source: WS-2 (scholarly summaries); SEP-LN on Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka's "textual problems"]
- **Bhaṭṭa Lollaṭa** — "production" view (*utpatti*): rasa is the stable emotion (sthāyibhāva) intensified by the vibhāvas and anubhāvas; located primarily in the character portrayed (and in the actor by imitation). Common objection: it fails to explain the spectator's own response. [source: WS-2; WS-18] (Hedge: secondary summaries only.)
- **Śaṅkuka** — "inference" view (*anumiti*): the spectator infers the character's emotion, imitated by the actor; the cognition of the actor as the character is neither true nor false nor doubt nor resemblance, on the analogy of a painted horse (*citraturaga*). Objection (Abhinavagupta's teacher Bhaṭṭa Tauta, and Abhinava): inference is merely intellectual and cannot produce relish. [source: WS-2; WS-3 (jainqq copies of Kulkarni/Sambodhi scholarship)]
- **Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka** (c. 900 CE), *Mirror of the Heart* (*Hṛdayadarpaṇa*, lost): argues rasa is not a linguistic meaning but "an experiential event which is caused by sentences"; after primary meaning operates there is an "aesthetic text-event" (Pollock) that unifies the poem "through a generalizing power (*bhāvakatva*)"; the audience then enjoys ("experientializes") the result through an aesthetic capability (*bhojakatva*), "and the result is a transcendent, rapturous experience of rasa". [source: SEP-LN §3.3, citing Pollock 2016, Ollett 2020, De 1960]
- The generalisation Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka's *bhāvakatva* brings about is called *sādhāraṇīkaraṇa*; Abhinavagupta accepted that universalisation is essential to rasa but subsumed bhāvakatva and bhojakatva under manifestation/suggestion (*vyañjanā*), objecting to positing extra powers. [source: WS-18 (secondary)]
- **Abhinavagupta** (Kashmir, c. 950–1016; WP-Abhinavabharati says c. 950–1020 — give "c. 950–c. 1016/1020"): Kashmir Śaiva philosopher, mystic, aesthetician; works on art: *Abhinavabhāratī* (on the Nāṭyaśāstra) and *Locana* ("The Eye", on the *Dhvanyāloka*). [source: WP-Abhinavagupta; WP-Anandavardhana]
- Abhinava's view: rasa is manifested/suggested (*abhivyakti*), in line with Ānandavardhana's dhvani; aesthetic experience is "the manifestation of the innate dispositions of the self, such as love and sorrow"; the spectator "transcends the limitations of one's limited self because of the process of universalisation"; rasa is the *summum bonum* of literature. [source: WP-Abhinavabharati]
- Scholarly gloss: on Abhinava's account the rasa-experience is *alaukika* (sui generis, non-worldly), essentially pleasurable, and undergone rather than contemplated as something outside oneself. [source: WS-11 (secondary summary of a published paper)]
- Abhinava held that delight in art "is not limited to pleasant experiences but also includes painful ones"; he called those unable to appreciate good music *ahṛdaya* ("heartless"). [source: WP-Rasa_(aesthetics)]
- Tension noted in recent scholarship: Abhinava claims all rasas, including bībhatsa (disgust), are pleasurable for the sensitive appreciator, though elsewhere he treats disgust as incompatible with enjoying its object. [source: WS-11 (summary of a recent paper; author not confirmed)]

## 4. Ānandavardhana and dhvani
- Ānandavardhana (c. 820–890), Kashmiri court poet under King Avantivarman; author of the *Dhvanyāloka* ("Light on Suggestion"). [source: WP-Anandavardhana] SEP-LN: "ca 9th century CE"; "highly influential". [source: SEP-LN §1]
- Before him, Indian poetics was "primarily ... focused on taxonomies of figuration"; after him it "drew on philosophical work, in particular Mīmāṃsā, to theorize about meaning itself". [source: SEP-LN §1]
- He argues for a new linguistic capacity, suggestion (*dhvani* or *vyañjanā*), beyond primary and secondary (metaphorical) meaning; what is suggested divides into implied meanings, implied figures and aesthetic moods (rasa); rasa conveyed by suggestion = *rasa-dhvani*; "rasa is the proper aim of all poetry". [source: SEP-LN §3.1]
- For Ānandavardhana rasa "is found within the text, and not the reader"; after Abhinavagupta's commentary "the emphasis shifts onto the reader's emotive experience". [source: SEP-LN §3.1]
- Example: "The village is on the Ganges" — suggests "the purity of the village" (SEP wording) on the holy river's bank; suggestion "operates after both primary and secondary meaning". [source: SEP-LN §3.1]
- Dhvani is "roughly analogous to, but not co-extensive with" Gricean implicature, since Ānandavardhana denies that suggestion arises through inference. [source: SEP-LN §3.2]
- Abhinavagupta counts thirty-five types of suggestion. [source: SEP-LN §3.2]
- Opponents: Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka and Mahima Bhaṭṭa (c. 1025; *Analysis of "Manifestation"*, *Vyaktiviveka*) reduce suggestion to a non-linguistic process (Mahima: inference; characters are unreal, so their emotions are inferred/imputed); Mukula Bhaṭṭa and Prābhākara Mīmāṃsakas reduce it to existing linguistic capacities. The Nyāya philosopher Jayanta Bhaṭṭa (9th c.) has only a "stray remark" against it. [source: SEP-LN §§3, 3.3]
- Ānandavardhana's three grades of poetry: dhvani-kāvya (suggestion dominant, best), guṇībhūta-vyaṅgya (suggestion subordinate), citra-kāvya ("picture" poetry without suggestion, lowest) [source: WP-Anandavardhana — tagged citation-needed; use cautiously].
- Daniel H. H. Ingalls called Ānandavardhana "the most brilliant of all Sanskrit critics"; P. V. Kane compared the *Dhvanyāloka*'s place in poetics to Pāṇini's in grammar. [source: WP-Anandavardhana]

## 5. China: painting and calligraphy theory
- First theoretical texts on calligraphy and painting date from the 2nd–4th centuries CE. [source: SEP-CPC §1.1]
- **Zong Bing** (375–443): wrote the earliest text on landscape painting; WP quotes him: "Landscapes have a material existence, and yet reach also in a spiritual domain" (translator not given — paraphrase only). His idea of the "lying journey" (*woyou*, "travel while lying down") — wandering through landscapes in pictures — is linked by SEP-CPC with Zhuangzi's "carefree journey" (*xiaoyaoyou*). [source: WP-Zong_Bing; SEP-CPC §5] Bush, "Tsung Ping's Essay on Painting Landscape and 'Landscape Buddhism' of Mount Lu", in Bush & Murck eds., *Theories of the Arts in China* (Princeton 1983). [source: SEP-CPC bibliography]
- **Xie He** (fl. 6th century), painter and critic; the "Six Laws/Principles" (*liufa*) come from the preface to his *Record of the Classification of Old Painters* (*Guhua pinlu*). WP dates it "circa 550"; other scholars place it c. 532–549 or earlier — DATE UNSETTLED; write "first half of the sixth century" or "sixth century". [source: WP-Xie_He_(artist); WP-Six_principles_of_Chinese_painting; WS-6 could not confirm a precise date]
- The six (WP's renderings): (1) *qiyun shengdong* — "Spirit Resonance" / vitality; (2) *gufa yongbi* — "Bone Method", use of the brush; (3) *yingwu xiangxing* — correspondence to the object, depicting form; (4) *suilei fucai* — suitability to type, applying colour; (5) *jingying weizhi* — division and planning, composition; (6) *chuanyi moxie* — transmission by copying models. Xie He said without spirit resonance there was no need to look further. [source: WP-Six_principles_of_Chinese_painting]
- Translations vary widely: "each principle is stated in but four characters", and "each period of Chinese painting has its own special way of interpreting the six principles". [source: WP-Six_principles_of_Chinese_painting] Scholarly translators include William Acker (*Some T'ang and Pre-T'ang Texts on Chinese Painting*, Brill, 1954 and 1974), Alexander Soper ("The First Two Laws of Hsieh Ho", *Far Eastern Quarterly* 8, 1949) and Osvald Sirén; Paul Goldin, "Two Notes on Xie He's 'Six Criteria' (liufa)" (*T'oung Pao*, 2018); Victor Mair on their "Indian Parallels". [source: WS-6 (bibliographic only)]
- **Gu Kaizhi** (c. 345–406): Zhang Yanyuan said of him "His intention precedes execution ... and, once the work is completed, his intention remains; this is why it is entirely animated by spiritual breath" (Escande tr.). [source: SEP-CPC §2.1]
- **Wang Xizhi** (303–361), "considered the greatest calligrapher"; the expression "the intention precedes the brush/execution" (*yi zai bi qian*) is attributed to him (Afterword to Lady Wei's *Battle Plan of the Brush*): "the intention must precede the execution, then the character is realized" (Escande tr.). Son Wang Xianzhi (344–386). [source: SEP-CPC §2.1, §3.1] (Lantingji xu / Orchid Pavilion: WP-Lantingji_Xu fetched; not needed.)
- Calligraphy (*shufa*, "discipline of writing") is what "the Chinese consider to be the most accomplished art"; a brushstroke is described in terms of "bone" (structure), "flesh" (thickness), "sinew" (tension) and "blood" (ink); it must give "the impression of a living body". [source: SEP-CPC §1.3]
- Zhang Yanyuan (9th c.), *Lidai minghua ji* (847): the stroke defines painting; blowing colour onto silk "cannot be called painting (hua), because there are no brushstrokes involved". [source: SEP-CPC §1.3]
- **Sun Guoting** (646–691), Tang calligrapher; *Shu pu* (書譜, "A Narrative/Treatise on Calligraphy"), written 687, about 3,500 characters, autograph scroll in the National Palace Museum, Taipei; "the first important theoretical work on Chinese calligraphy"; whether it is the whole, part or preface of an unfinished work is debated. Eng. tr.: *Two Chinese Treatises on Calligraphy* (Yale UP, 1995). [source: WP-Sun_Guoting; WP-Shu_Pu]
- Yu Shinan (558–638) compares the calligrapher's brush to Zhuangzi's wheelwright Lunbian (ch. 13): "First, we must seize the movement with our heart before our hand may echo it. This cannot be expressed by words." (Escande tr.) [source: SEP-CPC §1.2]
- Artist as medium: the gesture lets the Dao act; "Shen Zongqian (c. 1734–before 1803)" (*Jiezhou xuehua bian*, 1781): paintings arise from "animated breaths" (*lingqi*) just as heaven and earth create living things — painting is not imitation of nature but analogous to cosmic creation. [source: SEP-CPC §1.2]
- Formal vs spiritual resemblance: Sikong Tu (837–908): "the resemblance is obtained by leaving the form behind" — *xingsi* (formal likeness) vs *shensi* (spiritual likeness). Jing Hao (10th c.), *Bifa ji*: "Formal resemblance means obtaining the apparent form (xing) but leaving out its breath (qi). Truth means that breath as well as substance are perfectly rendered" (Escande tr.). Escande: truth "goes beyond resemblance" but "does encompass it". [source: SEP-CPC §§4.1–4.2]
- **Guo Xi** (c. 1020–c. 1090), Northern Song court landscape painter; *Early Spring* (hanging scroll, dated 1072, National Palace Museum, Taipei); *Old Trees, Level Distance* (handscroll, c. 1080, Metropolitan Museum). The treatise *Linquan gaozhi* ("The Lofty Message of Forests and Streams") is "attributed to him"; compiled by his son Guo Si after his death. [source: WP-Guo_Xi; WP-Early_Spring_(painting); WP-Old_Trees,_Level_Distance; WS-8 for Guo Si]
- **Three distances** (*sanyuan*): high distance (*gaoyuan*: looking up from the foot of the mountain to the peak), deep distance (*shenyuan*: looking past the front of the mountain to what lies behind), level distance (*pingyuan*: looking from a near mountain to distant ones). Sources DIFFER on the exact definitions (WP-Old_Trees calls level distance "the foreground"); use the gloss above with "roughly". [source: WP-Old_Trees,_Level_Distance; WS-8]
- Guo Xi's seasons passage (translator not named by WP): "The mountains in spring are light and seductive as if smiling ... the mountains in winter are sad and tranquil as if sleeping." [source: WP-Guo_Xi] (Usable as paraphrase; translator unknown.)
- **Su Shi** (Su Dongpo, 8 Jan 1037 – 24 Aug 1101): Song literatus, official, poet, calligrapher (one of the Four Masters of Song calligraphy), painter of bamboo and weathered rocks, "championing the capture of spirit over outward likeness". [source: WP-Su_Shi]
- Su Shi's poem line 論畫以形似，見與兒童鄰 — literally "to judge painting by formal likeness is to see as a child does" (my literal gloss; the Chinese text is confirmed, a published translation is not). Susan Bush, *The Chinese Literati on Painting: Su Shih (1037–1101) to Tung Ch'i-ch'ang (1555–1636)* (Harvard-Yenching, 1971; HKU Press reprint 2012) treats Su Shi in ch. II. [source: WS-7]
- **Literati painting** (*wenrenhua*): from the Northern Song "a new class of scholar-artists" emerged; they disdained realistic depiction, colourful palettes and paid work, comparing professional painters to "butchers or tinkers in the marketplace" (WP blockquote from Barbieri-Low 2007). Ink painting became a gentlemanly occupation linked with poetry and calligraphy; Dong Qichang (Ming) later systematised a "Southern School" (literati) vs "Northern School" division. [source: WP-Chinese_painting; WP-Ink_wash_painting]
- 20th century: the literati spirit was blamed for China's decadence after 1911 and the May Fourth movement (1919); communist China rejected it, "fiercely at times"; renewed interest only from the early 1980s. Modern aestheticians Zhu Guangqian (1897–1986), Zong Baihua (1897–1987), Li Zehou (1930–2021; *The Path of Beauty*, 1981). [source: SEP-CPC §1.1]

## 6. China: music (Confucian) and Daoist themes
- **Xunzi** (3rd c. BCE) devotes a whole chapter (ch. 20, "Discourse on Music", *Yuelun*) to music; "ritual and music" (*liyue*) are two aspects of human artifice (*wei*) created by the sages: ritual for social cohesion, music for "the orderly expression of human emotions". [source: SEP-Xun §3; WP-Record_of_Music for title *Yuelun*]
- Xunzi wrote against the Mohist view that music is wasteful: Mozi's followers saw only material costs and missed "the psychological utility of music as an instrument of moral suasion" (Cook 1997; Graham 1989). [source: SEP-Xun §3]
- Quotation (as given in SEP-Xun; translator presumably Goldin): "When music is centered and balanced, the people are harmonious and not dissipated. When music is stern and grave, the people are uniform and not disorderly. When the people are harmonious and uniform, the army is firm and the citadels secure; enemy states dare not invade." (Xunzi 20.2) [source: SEP-Xun §3]
- The sages left the Odes (*Shi*) as compositions through which to channel our need for expression. [source: SEP-Xun §3]
- **Record of Music** (*Yueji*): the 19th chapter of the *Book of Rites* (*Liji*); basis for reconstructing the lost *Classic of Music*; authorship debated (attributed to Gongsun Nizi, or to Liu De, son of Han Emperor Jing); Scott Cook concludes Warring States authorship most probable; related material in Xunzi's *Yuelun*. [source: WP-Record_of_Music]
- **Zhuangzi**: exemplars are "butchers, musicians, cicada catchers, wheelmakers" who act "in a highly cultivated, precise, and smooth manner with ease and a sense of flow". [source: SEP-Zh §2] IEP-Zh: "any physical activity, whether butchering a carcass, making wooden wheels, or carving beautiful ceremonial bell stands, becomes a dao when it is performed in a spiritual state of heightened awareness"; Woodcarver Qing (ch. 19) "whose aesthetic skill reaches 'magical' heights". [source: IEP-Zh, ch. 3 section]
- *Ziran* ("self-so", naturalness/spontaneity) — owned by the Hundred Schools chapter; cross-refer. Chinese artists frequently invoke Zhuangzi ch. 2 ("I and all things are one"). [source: SEP-CPC §1.2]

## 7. Japan
- **Mono no aware** (already defined in Meaning-and-Death; here only Norinaga's theory): the idiom comes from Heian literature; Motoori Norinaga (1730–1801) made it central to literary theory through his study of *Genji*, arguing for a broader understanding of it as "a profound sensitivity to the emotional and affective dimensions of existence in general"; Murasaki's greatness lies in portraying characters with mono no aware so the reader can empathise. [source: SEP-JA §2; WP-Mono_no_aware]
- Ozu Yasujirō's films "are a series of exercises in conveying mono no aware", expressing feelings through "the faces of things rather than of actors". [source: SEP-JA §2]
- Heian contrast: *aware* in *Genji* vs *okashi* (detached amusement) in Sei Shōnagon's *Pillow Book*. [source: SEP-JP §4.1.2]
- Medieval shift: after the Heian court, new categories "suggestive of retreat or withdrawal": *wabi* (to the plainness of the rustic), *sabi* (to the lonely, neglected), *yūgen* (to the quiet, mysterious depths) — central to tea, poetry, nō and gardens. [source: SEP-JP §4.2.2]
- **Kenkō** (NEW quotation, not the published one): "Are we to look at cherry blossoms only in full bloom, at the moon only when it is cloudless?" ... "To long for the moon while looking on the rain, to lower the blinds and be unaware of the passing of the spring—these are even more deeply moving. Branches about to blossom or gardens strewn with faded flowers are worthier of our admiration." — *Tsurezuregusa* §137, tr. Donald Keene (1967), p. 115; SEP-JA presents it as an example of *wabi*. [source: SEP-JA §3] (Section number 137 is my identification, not stated in SEP — cite only "Keene, 115".)
- **Wabi** ("subdued, austere beauty"): first praised in poetry, most fully developed in the art of tea and Zen. [source: SEP-JA §3]
- **Sen no Rikyū** (1522 – 21 April 1591), born in Sakai; studied tea under Takeno Jōō; tea master to Oda Nobunaga (from 1579) and Toyotomi Hideyoshi (from 1582); ordered by Hideyoshi to commit seppuku (1591), reasons uncertain. Tiny rustic tea rooms (*sō-an*), e.g. the two-mat Tai-an at Myōki-an (National Treasure); Raku tea bowls with the tile-maker Chōjirō; preferred local, rustic wares to costly Chinese ones. [source: WP-Sen_no_Rikyū; SEP-JA §4]
- Lineage: Murata Shukō (1423–1502) → Takeno Jōō (1502–1555) → Rikyū; his style is *wabicha*. [source: SEP-JA §4]
- Rikyū's "equalizing" aesthetic: the *nijiriguchi* crawl-in entrance (about 60 × 60 cm) meant samurai left swords on a rack outside and all guests entered alike; Hirota: wabi as "non-hierarchical awareness of the real". [source: SEP-JA §4]
- *Nampōroku* (1690), "a record of sayings by the tea master Sen no Rikyū": "In the small [tea] room, it is desirable for every utensil to be less than adequate. There are those who dislike a piece when it is even slightly damaged; such an attitude shows a complete lack of comprehension" (Hirota tr., *Wind in the Pines*, 226). Repaired broken utensils valued more highly than intact ones. [source: SEP-JA §3] CAVEAT: authenticity disputed — its supposed author Nanbō Sōkei may not have existed, and some scholars attribute the text to Tachibana Jitsuzan (1655–1708), who claimed to have found it. [source: WP-Sen_no_Rikyū] Attribute as "the Nampōroku, a record of Rikyū's teachings that appeared a century after his death".
- *Zencharoku* (1828): "Wabi means that even in straitened circumstances no thought of hardship arises. Even amid insufficiency, one is moved by no feeling of want. ..." (Hirota, 275). [source: SEP-JA §3]
- **Sabi** ("rustic patina"): in the *Man'yōshū* connotes desolateness; later what has aged well/rusted (homophone *sabi*, rust); linked with Bashō and *sabishii* (lonely). Tanizaki, *In Praise of Shadows* (1933): "We do not dislike everything that shines, but we do prefer a pensive lustre to a shallow brilliance ... We love things that bear the marks of grime, soot, and weather" (Tanizaki, 11–12). Silver Pavilion vs gold-leafed Golden Pavilion (regilded 1987). [source: SEP-JA §5]
- **Wabi-sabi as a unified concept is largely modern**: Leonard Koren (b. 1948), *Wabi-Sabi for Artists, Designers, Poets & Philosophers* (1994) popularised it in the West; in *Wabi-Sabi: Further Thoughts* (2015) Koren himself says the 1994 book omitted how the two words "came to be conjoined" and that, though both terms are ancient, "they have not been used as a set phrase until very recent days". Paul S. Atkins (Univ. of Washington) argues the compound is "of recent, not ancient, vintage", is yoked together far more often outside Japan, and was re-imported to Japan; sabi comes chiefly out of poetry and wabi out of tea. [source: WP-Wabi-sabi; WS-10] SEP-JA itself treats wabi and sabi in separate sections. [source: SEP-JA §§3, 5]
- WP-Wabi-sabi's popular gloss "imperfect, impermanent, and incomplete" is Koren's. [source: WP-Wabi-sabi citing Koren]
- **Yūgen** ("mysterious grace/profundity"): the term first found in Chinese texts meaning "dark", "mysterious"; Kamo no Chōmei (*Hōjōki*, 1212) characterised it: "It is like an autumn evening under a colorless expanse of silent sky. Somehow, as if for some reason that we should be able to recall, tears well uncontrollably" (source Hume ed. 1995, 253–54). Yūgen concerns "the depth of the world we live in", not another world. [source: SEP-JA §6]
- **Zeami Motokiyo** (c. 1363 – c. 1443): Nō actor-playwright, son of Kan'ami; patronised by shogun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu (from 1374); 21 critical writings over about four decades — the first, *Fūshikaden* (*Kadensho*), is the first known Japanese treatise on drama; exiled to Sado Island in 1434 under Ashikaga Yoshinori. [source: WP-Zeami_Motokiyo; SEP-JA §6 gives 1363–1443]
- Zeami calls yūgen ("Grace") "the highest principle" (Rimer & Yamazaki tr., 92); "Cannot the beauty of Grace be compared to the image of a swan holding a flower in its bill, I wonder?" (Rimer, 73); "Notes on the Nine Levels": the highest level, "Peerless Charm", "surpasses any explanation in words and lies beyond the workings of consciousness ... 'in the dead of night, the sun shines brightly'" (Rimer, 120). [source: SEP-JA §6]
- Nō discipline: years of practising *kata* that go "counter to the movements of the body" until a breakthrough to "a 'higher naturalness'". [source: SEP-JA §6]
- **Iki** (Kuki Shūzō, *The Structure of "Iki"*, 1930): owned by the Kyoto School chapter; one-line mention only (SEP-JA §8 says published "shortly after returning to Japan in 1929"; Kyoto notes have 1930 — use 1930).
- **Kire** ("cutting"/cut-continuity): Hakuin (1686–1769); Nishitani Keiji's essay on ikebana — cut flowers shown "as absolutely rootless as every other being". [source: SEP-JA §9] (Optional.)
- **Everyday aesthetics** — Yuriko Saito (RISD), *Everyday Aesthetics* (OUP 2007), *Aesthetics of the Familiar* (2017), *Aesthetics of Care* (2022), the latter two "include extensive discussion of Japanese aesthetics"; first monograph titled *Everyday Aesthetics* was Katya Mandoki's (2007). [source: SEP-Ev §1 and bibliography]

## 8. Islamic aesthetics
- **Figural representation**: "The Quran itself does not prohibit visual representation of any living being"; it condemns idolatry. Prohibitions occur in the hadith (e.g. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, threatening on Judgment Day image-makers who are challenged to "breathe life" into their images); they condemn makers more strongly than owners and admit concessions (e.g. dolls). [source: WP-Aniconism_in_Islam]
- Sunni exegetes "from the 9th century onward" increasingly read them as categorical; practice varied by period, place, school; Shia and Sufi orders "less stringent". [source: WP-Aniconism_in_Islam]
- "In practice, the core of normative religion in Islam is consistently aniconic" — mosques and Qur'āns lack figurative images — but secular figural art "historically flourished in nearly all Islamic cultures": Umayyad palace frescoes and reliefs (Mshatta façade, now Berlin), Abbasid Samarra palaces, ceramics, metalwork, ivory; the human figure is central to Persian, Ottoman and Mughal painting; images of Muhammad (sometimes veiled, from about 1500 often with veiled face) occur in Persian and Indian manuscripts. [source: WP-Aniconism_in_Islam]
- Free-standing sculpture is very rare (Pisa Griffin; Alhambra lions). Album prefaces justified images by "comparing God to an artist". [source: WP-Aniconism_in_Islam]
- Dome of the Rock and Umayyad Mosque of Damascus: non-figural mosaics evoking paradise (plants, crowns, idyllic cityscape). [source: WP-Aniconism_in_Islam]
- Evenhandedness note: the article stresses differences by "epoch ..., country, religious orientation, political intent, popular beliefs"; avoid both "Islam bans images" and "there was never any restriction".
- **Calligraphy**: closely tied to the Qur'ān; its prominence "is not solely due to religious constraints on figurative imagery, but rather reflects the central role of writing and the written word in Islamic culture"; two main families, angular Kufic (late 7th c., disputed) and rounded Naskh; codification by Ibn Muqla (d. 939/940); Ibn al-Bawwāb (d. 1022); Yāqūt al-Mustaʿṣimī (13th c.). [source: WP-Islamic_calligraphy]
- **Arabesque**: rhythmic scrolling foliage of stylised half-palmettes; "probably invented in Baghdad around the 10th century"; distinctive in being apparently "infinitely extendable" beyond its frame. [source: WP-Arabesque]
- DEBATE: "Claims are often made regarding the theological significance of the arabesque ... these are without support from written historical sources", since the medieval Islamic world left no documentation of makers' intentions; links to Arabic geometry also debated. [source: WP-Arabesque] Contrast: Keith Critchlow and others argue Islamic patterns lead "the viewer to an understanding of the underlying reality, rather than being mere decoration". [source: WP-Islamic_geometric_patterns]
- **Geometric ornament**: girih tilings (10-point girih in the Jameh Mosque of Isfahan 1086; 9-, 11-, 13-point in Barsian 1098); the *Topkapı Scroll* (Timurid Iran, late 15th/early 16th c.) with 114 patterns. [source: WP-Islamic_geometric_patterns]
- **Persian miniature**: significant genre from the 13th century, with Chinese influence after the Mongol conquests; depth shown by placing distant figures higher; album (*muraqqaʿ*) pictures from the 16th c. [source: WP-Persian_miniature]
- **Behzād** (Kamāl al-Dīn Bihzād, c. 1455/1460–1535): head of the royal ateliers at Herat (appointed 1486 under Sultan Ḥusayn Bāyqarā) and from 1522 at Tabriz under Shah Ismāʿīl; "regarded as marking the highpoint" of Islamic miniature painting; *Būstān* of Saʿdī, Herat 1488 (Cairo). [source: WP-Kamāl_ud-Dīn_Behzād; WP-Persian_miniature]
- **Al-Ghazālī** (c. 1056–1111; birth 1055/56 per Griffel): *Revival of the Religious Sciences* (*Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn*) in forty books. [source: SEP-Gh §§1, 5; IEP-Gh]
- *Book of Love, Longing, Intimacy and Contentment* is the 36th book of the *Iḥyāʾ* (Eric Ormsby tr., Islamic Texts Society). His analysis: one cannot love what one does not know; beauty is among the causes of love; God alone is perfect beauty; inward qualities cause a stronger and more durable love than physical beauty. [source: WS-12 — publisher pages and a summary; NOT read in the text; state cautiously, e.g. "al-Ghazālī lists beauty among the causes of love and argues that inner beauty is loved more than outer"]
- *Samāʿ* (listening to music/poetry, esp. Sufi): Book 18 of the *Iḥyāʾ*, "On the Proper Conduct of Listening to Music and Ecstasy" (Anthony Johns tr., Islamic Texts Society, *Al-Ghazali on Proper Conduct when Listening to Music and the Experience of Ecstasy*); it weighs arguments for and against permissibility; al-Ghazālī's permission is conditional — the limits lie not in music itself but in external factors (the five impediments) and the right "time, place and company". [source: WS-12 (publisher descriptions and scholarly abstracts)]
- Samāʿ "may have been in use since the tenth century" for a form of dhikr; the Mevlevi (Rumi's order) whirling ceremony was proclaimed by UNESCO in 2005. [source: WP-Sama_(Sufism) — weak article; its "origination ... credited to Rumi" contradicts the tenth-century use, so don't repeat that]
- **Ibn al-Haytham** (c. 965–c. 1040), *Kitāb al-Manāẓir*: his account of the perception of beauty rests on "visible properties" — 22 of them per Ahmed Elkady's 1993 Penn dissertation (light, colour, distance, shape, proportion, etc.) — and he treats the beauty of light and colour as directly perceived, while beauty from proportion etc. needs discernment; a Turkish-language article argues he explains how beauty is perceived, not what it is. [source: WS-13 (secondary; hedge heavily)] Valérie Gonzalez, *Beauty and Islam: Aesthetics in Islamic Art and Architecture* (I.B. Tauris with the Institute of Ismaili Studies, 2001), first chapter "Beauty and the Aesthetic Experience in Classical Arabic Thought"; a review in *BCAI* lists Ibn Ḥazm, Ibn Sīnā, Ibn Rushd and Ibn al-Haytham among her sources and criticises her universalising method. [source: WS-16]
- Ibn Sīnā: the logic of the *Shifāʾ* includes a book on Aristotle's *Poetics*. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ibn-sina/ §2] (Minimal; don't build on it.)

## 10. Argument (step by step): how can we enjoy painful emotions in art? Abhinavagupta's answer
Frame: the Western "paradox of tragedy" — Hume (1757): "It seems an unaccountable pleasure, which the spectators of a well-written tragedy receive from sorrow, terror, anxiety, and other passions, that are in themselves disagreeable and uneasy." [source: SEP-PT intro] Rasa theory faces the same problem because karuṇa (pathos), bhayānaka (terror) and bībhatsa (disgust) are rasas, yet rasa is supposed to be relished.
1. P1. The emotions portrayed in drama include sorrow, fear and disgust, which are painful in ordinary life. [rasa list: WP-Rasa]
2. P2. Rasa, the aim of drama and poetry, is a relish (*āsvāda*) — something enjoyed. [WP-Rasa; SEP-LN: rasa "the proper aim of all poetry"]
3. P3 (rejected rivals). If rasa were the emotion located in the character (Lollaṭa), the spectator's own experience would be unexplained; if it were merely inferred (Śaṅkuka), inference is a cool intellectual act that yields no relish. [WS-2, WS-3]
4. P4 (Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka, adopted in part). Poetry has a generalising power: the vibhāvas etc. are presented not as this particular person's circumstances but in a generalised form (*sādhāraṇīkaraṇa*), freed from "mine" and "his". [SEP-LN §3.3; WS-18]
5. P5 (Abhinavagupta). In the generalised state the spectator's own latent dispositions (love, sorrow ...) are manifested, and the limited self is transcended. [WP-Abhinavabharati]
6. C. So what is relished is not the private, self-interested emotion that makes sorrow painful but a transformed, non-worldly (*alaukika*) state, which is essentially pleasurable — even when its material is sorrow. [WS-11; WP-Rasa: delight "includes painful ones"]
- **Objection** (a version SEP-PT raises against "distance" and "control" solutions): if the painful valence is removed, the spectator no longer feels sorrow at all — the theory "doesn't explain how we can take pleasure in a displeasing emotion, it just implicitly denies that we experience the displeasing emotion". [source: SEP-PT §3.2] A related internal problem: Abhinava says even disgust is pleasurable, yet elsewhere treats disgust as incompatible with enjoying its object. [WS-11]
- **Reply** (on Abhinava's behalf): he accepts the consequence — rasa is not the sthāyibhāva itself but derived from it; tears and grief can be present while the overall state is relish, as when we savour a bitter flavour (the rasa = "taste" metaphor). This places him close to what SEP-PT calls "conversionary" accounts (Hume: the passions are "converted into pleasure"). [source: SEP-PT §4; WP-Rasa "rasa may be said to be the original emotion transfigured by aesthetic delight" (Wallace Dace 1963 paraphrase)] Hedge: the bitter-flavour analogy is a gloss, not a verified quotation.
- **Comparison with catharsis**: on a traditional reading Aristotle's catharsis is a cleansing/release of pity and fear (SEP-PT: "the 'release' of the negative emotions of fear and pity that comes with the narrative resolution"; "The proper understanding and existence of catharsis is controversial (Shelley 2003)"); one comparative study argues catharsis works by weakening emotion's intensity, whereas Abhinava locates the solution in "heart-sharing through aesthetically transformed emotions themselves". [source: SEP-PT §4/§5 text at "catharsis"; WS-11 summary of a Project MUSE article — author not confirmed]
- Alternative argument available: "Did China have aesthetics?" — P: there was no word for aesthetics until the 20th c. (SEP-CPC); objection: the absence of a word doesn't show absence of theory; reply (Escande): take aesthetics as reflection on the theory of art and it plainly exists, though organised around process, artist and transmission rather than the art object. Cross-refer to What Is Art? for Kristeller.

## Debates
- **How rasa arises** (production / inference / enjoyment / manifestation): Lollaṭa, Śaṅkuka, Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka, Abhinavagupta; all but Abhinava known only through him. [SEP-LN; WS-2]
- **Where rasa lives**: in the text (Ānandavardhana) vs in the reader's experience (Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka, Abhinavagupta). [SEP-LN §3.1]
- **Is dhvani a distinct linguistic power?** Ānandavardhana/Abhinava yes; Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka, Mahima Bhaṭṭa (inference), Mukula Bhaṭṭa, Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā no. [SEP-LN §3.3]
- **Śānta as a ninth rasa**: contested sixth–tenth centuries; championed by Abhinava. [WP-Rasa]

- **Kuki and nationalism**: Pincus vs Parkes — owned by Kyoto chapter.
- **Islamic images**: the Qur'ān vs the hadith; categorical readings from the 9th century vs varied practice; modern Wahhabi/Taliban vs Shia devotional imagery. [WP-Aniconism_in_Islam]
