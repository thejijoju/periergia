## 1. Setting: "after Alexander"
- "Hellenistic philosophy" conventionally covers Greek philosophy from the death of Alexander the Great (323 BCE) to the battle of Actium (31 BCE); the dominant schools were the Stoics, Epicureans and Sceptics. [source: WP-Hellenistic_philosophy, citing Long 1996 p. 1]
- Diogenes the Cynic is often said to have died in 323 too, and Aristotle died in 322. [source: WP-Hellenistic_philosophy] (Diogenes' date is uncertain: see §5.)
- The idea that the insecurity and loss of autonomy of the era drove people to seek inner security through philosophy is presented as one author's view (A. C. Grayling 2019), not as settled fact. [source: WP-Hellenistic_philosophy] WRITER: present the "decline of the polis led to inward-turning philosophy" story as an interpretation.
- All the Hellenistic schools shared a eudaimonist framework: happiness (eudaimonia) is the end "for the sake of which everything is done, but which is not itself done for the sake of anything" (Stobaeus); schools were distinguished by their view of "the highest good" (summum bonum) and "the end" (telos), hence the title of Cicero's De Finibus. [source: SEP-Sto §4]
- By the end of the period philosophers had moved from Athens to Rome or Alexandria; Sulla's capture of Athens (WP: 87 BCE; the Aristotle notes give 86) led to destruction. [source: WP-Hellenistic_philosophy]
- Epicurus on philosophy as medicine: philosophy that does not heal the soul is no better than medicine that cannot cure the body (Usener 1887, fragment 221). [source: SEP-Epi §6] Wikipedia renders the same fragment "empty are the words of that philosopher who offers therapy for no human suffering" (citing Sellars 2018). [source: WP-Hellenistic_philosophy]
- Marcus Aurelius endowed four chairs of philosophy in Athens, one each for the Platonic, Aristotelian, Stoic and Epicurean traditions. [source: IEP-MA §1]

## 2. The Stoa: founders, dates, sources
- Name: from the Stoa Poikilê ("painted porch") in the Athenian Agora, decorated with mural paintings, where the first Stoics met and lectured. [source: SEP-Sto intro] The Stoa Poikile was a Doric colonnade built c. 460 BC (by Peisianax, brother-in-law of Kimon) on the north side of the Agora, with paintings by Polygnotus, Micon and perhaps Panaenus; the Cynic Crates spent time there, and Zeno taught there from about 300 BC until his death c. 262. [source: WP-Stoa_Poikile] Zeno's followers were first called "Zenonians". [source: WP-Zeno_of_Citium]
- Unlike the Epicureans, Zeno taught in a public space. [source: WP-Stoicism]
- Zeno of Citium (Cyprus): founded the school around 300 BCE. Dates: c. 334 – c. 262 BC [WP-Zeno_of_Citium]; 335–263 BCE [SEP-Log §5]. He began teaching in the Stoa Poikile in 301 BC per WP. He read Socratic dialogues, studied under the Cynic Crates and was influenced by Plato's Academy and the Megarians (Stilpo; the dialecticians Diodorus Cronus and Philo). [source: SEP-Sto §1.1; WP-Zeno_of_Citium; SEP-Log §5]
- Zeno's ancestry is described in sources as Phoenician or Greek; Citium was a Greek city whose population had been increased by Phoenician settlers. His funeral epitaph compared him to the Phoenician Cadmus, who brought letters to Greece. [source: WP-Zeno_of_Citium] WRITER: say "from Citium in Cyprus, a city with a large Phoenician population"; do not assert his ethnicity.
- Conversion story (Diogenes Laertius 7.2–3): after a shipwreck Zeno read Xenophon's Memorabilia in an Athenian bookshop, asked where such men as Socrates could be found, and the bookseller pointed to Crates passing by. [source: WP-Zeno_of_Citium; IEP-Cyn §5] IEP-Cyn: "too propitious to be historically accurate" but it records how Stoicism grew out of Cynicism.
- Zeno's most famous (lost) work was his Republic, written in imitation of or opposition to Plato's. No writing of Zeno survives except quotations. [source: WP-Zeno_of_Citium]
- Cleanthes of Assos: second head; c. 330 – c. 230 BC (Apollodorus: born 331/0, died 230/29); a former boxer who supported himself as a night-time water-carrier; head of the school for 32 years after Zeno's death c. 262. Largest surviving fragment: the Hymn to Zeus. [source: WP-Cleanthes] SEP-Log §5 gives 331–232.
- Zeno's end formula was "living in agreement" ("living in accordance with one concordant reason"); Cleanthes added "with nature" (Stobaeus); elsewhere the longer formula is ascribed to Zeno himself (Diogenes Laertius). [source: SEP-Sto §4.1]
- Chrysippus of Soli (Cilicia): c. 279 – c. 206 BC (died aged 73 in the 143rd Olympiad, 208–204 BC) [WP-Chrysippus]; c. 280–207 [SEP-Log §5; IEP-Chrys]; head of the school from around 230 until 206 [SEP-Sto §1.1]. "By all accounts its foremost theorist and systematizer." [source: SEP-Sto §1.1] Ancient saying: "without Chrysippus, there would have been no Stoa" (D.L. 7.183). [source: WP-Chrysippus; SEP-Carn §1]
- Chrysippus' output: more than 705 works (D.L. 7.180) [WP-Chrysippus; IEP-Chrys]; "over 300 books on logic" [SEP-Log §5]; SEP-Sto §1.3 says "over 150 works". Sources differ; safe wording: "hundreds of books, more than 700 by one ancient count". Only fragments survive; parts of Logical Questions and On Providence have been identified among the Herculaneum papyri. [source: WP-Chrysippus]
- No complete work by Zeno, Cleanthes or Chrysippus survives; the only complete Stoic works are by imperial-era writers (Seneca, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius, plus Cornutus, Cleomedes, Hierocles). Early Stoicism is reconstructed from doxographies (esp. Diogenes Laertius book 7) and largely hostile witnesses (Plutarch, Alexander of Aphrodisias, Galen, Sextus Empiricus); von Arnim's Stoicorum Veterum Fragmenta (1903–5) collected the fragments. [source: SEP-Sto §1.3]
- Later Greek Stoics: after Chrysippus came Diogenes of Babylon and Antipater of Tarsus (2nd c. BCE); Panaetius and his student Posidonius ("Middle Stoicism", a label whose accuracy is debated). [source: SEP-Sto §1.1]

## 3. Stoic system: logic, physics, ethics
- Three parts of philosophy: physics, logic, ethics. Images (D.L.): a living being (logic = bones and sinews, ethics = flesh, physics = soul); an egg (logic = shell, ethics = white, physics = yolk); a fertile field (logic = wall, ethics = fruit, physics = land/trees). [source: SEP-Sto §1.2] Scholars debate how far ethics depends on physics (Annas 1993: largely independent; Cooper: thoroughly intertwined). [source: SEP-Sto §1.2]
### 3a. Logic
- Stoic logic is a propositional logic (contrast Aristotle's term logic). Propositions (axiômata, "assertibles") are the only bearers of truth; unlike modern propositions they change truth-value over time ("it is day"). [source: SEP-Sto §3.3–3.4]
- Diodorus Cronus and his pupil Philo (the "Dialectical school") began a logic of propositions before the Stoics; Philo's conditional is false only when antecedent true and consequent false (resembles material implication). [source: SEP-Log §4]
- Chrysippus is "without doubt the second great logician in the history of logic" (after Aristotle); his main achievement was a propositional logic "crowned by a deductive system". It was said that if the gods used logic it would be Chrysippus' (D.L. 7.180). [source: SEP-Log intro and §5]
- THE FIVE INDEMONSTRABLES (D.L. 7.80–1; Sextus M 8.224–5), in SEP-Sto's abbreviated form (Stoics wrote "the first" / "the second" for p and q):
  1. If p then q; p; therefore q (modus ponens).
  2. If p then q; not-q; therefore not-p (modus tollens).
  3. Not both p and q; p; therefore not-q.
  4. Either p or q; p; therefore not-q (Stoic disjunction is exclusive).
  5. Either p or q; not-p; therefore q.
  [source: SEP-Sto §3.4; SEP-Log §5.4] Plus four inference rules (themata), of which only the first and third survive; a syllogism is any argument that is an indemonstrable or reducible to one by the themata. [source: SEP-Log §5.4; SEP-Sto §3.4] Example of a first indemonstrable from SEP-Log: "If it is day, it is light: but it is day: so it is light" (WP-Chrysippus) / "If five is a number, then either five is odd or five is even. But five is a number. Therefore either five is odd or five is even." (SEP-Log §5.4)
- Bobzien (SEP-Log): the common assumption that the Stoics represented the five indemonstrables by five schematic "modes" is false; they used meta-linguistic descriptions (e.g. "A first indemonstrable is an argument that concludes from a conditional and its antecedent the consequent"). Simplified p/q forms are fine for a general reader but should be called a modern simplification. [source: SEP-Log §5.4]
- Chrysippus held that dogs reason with disjunctive syllogism (choosing a path by scent), per Sextus, PH 1.69. [source: WP-Chrysippus] (Reported, not endorsed.)
- Chrysippus worked on the Liar and the Sorites. [source: SEP-Log §5]
- Similarities between Chrysippus' logic and Frege's are "especially striking" (SEP-Log); Bobzien (2021) has argued Frege may actually have derived some of his work from Stoic texts via Prantl's 1855 History of Western Logic. [source: SEP-Log intro; SEP-Sto §5.4] Stoic notation anticipated something like Łukasiewicz's Polish notation (connective first). [source: SEP-Log §5.2]
### 3b. Physics: a rational, living cosmos
- Corporealism: only bodies exist (only bodies act or are acted on); the soul and even wisdom are bodies; four incorporeals "subsist": time, place, void and lekta ("sayables"). [source: SEP-Sto §2.1]
- Two principles: an active principle (God/Zeus = reason, logos, designing fire, pneuma "breath") pervading a passive principle (matter); God is immanent, the cosmos a living thing, governed providentially; cosmos goes through endless cycles ending in conflagration (rejected by Panaetius). [source: SEP-Sto §2.3, 2.5, 2.7]
### 3c. Determinism with responsibility (ARGUMENT)
- Fate = "a certain natural everlasting ordering of the whole: one set of things follows on and succeeds another, and the interconnexion is inviolable" (Aulus Gellius). Every event is determined by preceding causes. [source: SEP-Sto §2.8]
- Chrysippus' argument from bivalence (Cicero, On Fate 20–21): if any motion exists without a cause, not every proposition will be true or false; but every proposition is either true or false; so there is no motion without a cause; so all things happen by fate. [source: WP-Chrysippus]
- Objection 1, the "Lazy Argument" (Cicero): if it is fated that I recover, I will recover whether or not I call the doctor, so action is pointless. Reply (Chrysippus): events are "co-fated" — recovery is fated together with calling the doctor; "having children is co-fated with being willing to lie with a woman". [source: SEP-Sto §2.8; WP-Chrysippus]
- Objection 2: if my actions come from prior causes they are not "in my power". Reply: distinguish kinds of antecedent cause; the external impression is only an "auxiliary and proximate" cause; the decisive cause is the agent's own nature and assent. THE CYLINDER (Cicero, On Fate 42–43 = LS 62C as cited): "[Chrysippus] resorts to his cylinder and cone: these cannot begin to move without a push; but once that has happened, he holds that it is thereafter through their own nature that the cylinder rolls and the cone spins. 'Hence', he says, 'just as the person who pushed the cylinder gave it its beginning of motion but not its capacity for rolling, likewise, although the impression encountered will print and, as it were, emblazon its appearance on the mind, assent will be in our power.'" [source: SEP-Sto §2.8] IEP-Chrys quotes the same passage with "cylinder and spinning-top". WRITER: "cylinder and spinning top (or cone)".
- SEP's gloss: same push, different motions because of the shapes; given the push and its nature each solid "could not do otherwise", so this is compatibilism, not freedom to do otherwise. Detailed study: Bobzien 1998. [source: SEP-Sto §2.8]
- DOG AND CART (Hippolytus, Refutation 1.21 = LS 62A): "They too [Zeno and Chrysippus] affirmed that everything is fated, with the following model: When a dog is tied to a cart, if it wants to follow it is pulled and follows ... but if it does not want to follow it will be compelled in any case. So it is with men too." Susanne Bobzien has argued the simile is from neither Zeno nor Chrysippus but from the Roman Stoa. [source: IEP-Chrys; WS-3 confirming Hippolytus 1.21 / LS 62A] NB IEP-Sto calls it a metaphor "used by Epictetus" — contradicted by IEP-Chrys and LS; do not attribute it to Epictetus.
- Stoic determinism-with-responsibility as a compatibilism is the standard description (SEP-Sto §2.8).
### 3d. Ethics: virtue the only good (ARGUMENT)
- Virtue is the perfected condition of reason; the virtues are forms of knowledge and inseparable (whoever has one has all); virtue is all-or-nothing. [source: SEP-Sto §4.2]
- The argument that only virtue is good (D.L. 7.102–3 = LS 58A as quoted in SEP): P1. What is good benefits and never harms ("just as heating, not chilling is the peculiar characteristic of what is hot, so too benefiting, not harming is the peculiar characteristic of what is good"). P2. Wealth and health "no more do benefit than they harm". C. "Therefore wealth and health are not something good." Life, health, pleasure, beauty, strength, wealth, reputation, noble birth and their opposites (death, disease, pain, poverty etc.) are "neither good nor bad". [source: SEP-Sto §4.3]
- Consequence: the virtuous person on the rack is "maximally happy" (Cicero, De Finibus 3.42), a bullet Aristotle refused to bite (NE 1.5); happiness does not come in degrees. [source: SEP-Sto §4.3]
- PREFERRED/DISPREFERRED INDIFFERENTS: Chrysippus rejected the view (held by some in Zeno's circle, e.g. Aristo) that all indifferents are equal; health, wealth, reputation are "promoted/preferred" (proêgmena), have value (axia), are "according to nature", and it is one's proper function (kathêkon) to select them in normal circumstances. [source: SEP-Sto §4.3]
- MAIN OBJECTION (ancient critics, Plutarch, Cicero): a dilemma — either preferred indifferents are really good (contradicting "only virtue is good") or pursuing them is irrational. [source: SEP-Sto §4.3]
- Passions (pathê) are excessive impulses resting on false judgments (that an indifferent is good or bad); the sage is passion-free (apatheia) but has "good feelings" (eupatheiai) and involuntary "pre-emotions" (propatheiai), so not the "unfeeling robot" of caricature. [source: SEP-Sto §4.7]
- OIKEIÔSIS ("appropriation", "affiliation"): from birth every creature seeks to preserve its own constitution (Seneca's turtle on its back rights itself); with adulthood "reason supervenes as the craftsman of impulse"; the social extension of concern underwrites justice. Scholars debate how descriptive claims yield norms and how self-preservation yields other-concern. [source: SEP-Sto §4.6]
- COSMOPOLITANISM: by shared rationality all humans, with Zeus, are citizens of one cosmic city (Marcus Aurelius 4.4; Arius Didymus); justice reaches even "the most distant Mysian"; argument: a city is a group administered by law, all humans are bound by the universal law of Zeus, so the cosmos is a kind of city. [source: SEP-Sto §4.5]

## 4. Epicurus and the Garden
- Epicurus, 341–270 BCE; son of Neocles and Chaerestrata, an Athenian citizen (deme Gargettus) raised in the Athenian colony on Samos; came to Athens at 18 (323); joined his father in Colophon (321), where he studied with the Democritean Nausiphanes; taught in Mytilene and Lampsacus; returned to Athens in 307/306, bought the property known as "the Garden" (kêpos), which gave the school its name; died 270 aged 70 or 71. [source: SEP-Epi §2] IEP-Epi: moved to Athens "around 306"; died of kidney stones "around 271 or 270". WP-Epicurus: died 270 aged 72 of a urinary blockage, remaining cheerful (Hermarchus via D.L.).
- The Garden lay roughly halfway between the Stoa and the Academy. First-generation members held assets in common, with no rigid teacher–student hierarchy; monthly feasts on the 20th. [source: WP-Epicurus; SEP-Epi §6 (rituals "may have originated after the founder's death")]
- WOMEN: The Garden "welcomed many female students during Epicurus's tenure", including Themista, Batis, Boidion, Demetria, Hedeia, Leontion, Mammarion and Nikidion (Dorandi 2020). [source: WP-Epicurus] Leontion wrote a (lost) work against Theophrastus mentioned by Cicero and Pliny; she was called a hetaira (courtesan), possibly as slander by rival schools; "it was very unusual for a school to accept women, non-citizens, slaves, and those without means". [source: WP-Leontion] An Epicurus work ("Neocles") was addressed to Themista. [source: WP-Epicurus]
- SLAVES: Diogenes Laertius (10.10, 10.21) names Mys as the most notable of Epicurus' slaves, who took part in philosophical discussion; Epicurus' will freed Mys, Nicias, Lycon and a woman Phaedrium. [source: WS-1 (Loeb/Hicks D.L. book 10 as summarised); supported by WP-Leontion] CAVEAT: from search summaries, not a text read directly.
- Diogenes Laertius records Epicurus' will (10.16–21), with provision for his friends' children and a successor (Hermarchus). Successors: Hermarchus, Polystratus, Dionysius, Basilides; much later Apollodorus "tyrant of the Garden". [source: SEP-Epi §2; WP-Epicurus]
- Sources: Diogenes Laertius book 10 preserves the Letter to Herodotus (physics), Letter to Menoeceus (ethics), Letter to Pythocles (astronomy; authorship sometimes doubted) and the 40 Principal Doctrines (Kuriai Doxai); the Vatican Sayings survive separately; parts of On Nature (37 rolls) were recovered from the villa library at Herculaneum (buried 79 CE), probably the collection of the Epicurean Philodemus, "from Syria" (Gadara); the 2nd-century CE inscription of Diogenes of Oenoanda (SW Turkey). [source: SEP-Epi §1]
- TETRAPHARMAKOS ("four-part cure"), as in Philodemus (PHerc. 1005, 5.9–14), Hutchinson's translation: "Don't fear god, / Don't worry about death; / What is good is easy to get, / What is terrible is easy to endure". The name cannot be traced back further than Cicero and Philodemus (Gordon 1996). [source: WP-Principal_Doctrines]
- Gods exist, are blessed and immortal, and do not concern themselves with human affairs (which would compromise their beatitude). [source: SEP-Epi §5–6]
### 4a. Atomism and the swerve
- Atoms and void only; atoms indivisible, below perception, infinitely many, of an inconceivably large but not infinite number of shapes; universe infinite. Argument for void: motion requires it ("counterwitnessing"); argument for infinity: a finite thing has an edge, conceived against something beyond it, but nothing is beyond the all. [source: SEP-Epi §3]
- Epicurus revised Democritus in response to Aristotle-type criticisms (atoms have minimal parts; all atoms move at equal speed). [source: SEP-Epi §3]
- THE SWERVE (clinamen): a minimal, random deviation "at no specific time or place" (Lucretius 2.216–93). Two jobs: (1) without it atoms falling in parallel "like drops of rain" would never collide; (2) Lucretius (2.251–93) also links it to voluntary action, lest the will be trapped in an infinite causal chain. [source: SEP-Luc §on Book 2; IEP-Epi §3.b.ii] CAVEAT: the swerve is "attributed to Epicurus in our sources but not attested in our surviving works"; Lucretius is "our sole Epicurean source"; the evidence is chiefly Lucretius and Cicero; in On Nature 25, where Epicurus argues for agency against determinism, the swerve seems absent, so how far he relied on it is debated (some think a swerve is in every voluntary action, others a more minimal role). [source: SEP-Luc; SEP-Epi §4]
### 4b. Pleasure
- Pleasure is the only intrinsic good, pain the only intrinsic evil ("intrinsic hedonism"). [source: SEP-Death §3.1] The goal is freedom from bodily pain (aponia) and mental disturbance (ataraxia) (LM 128). [source: SEP-Epi §4]
- Kinetic vs. katastematic ("static", Cicero's Latin) pleasure: the distinction is debated; katastematic pleasure is the pleasure of a state of well-being; the Cyrenaics and Cicero held that condition is neutral, not pleasant. [source: SEP-Epi §4]
- Desires: natural and necessary; natural but not necessary; empty (e.g. immortality, great wealth, fame) (LM 127). [source: SEP-Epi §4]
- Justice is a compact neither to harm nor be harmed, nothing in itself (KD 31–33); secret crimes are unwise because fear of detection destroys tranquillity (KD 34–35); one cannot live pleasantly without living prudently, honourably and justly (LM 132, KD 5). [source: SEP-Epi §5]
- Friendship: "friendship ... had its beginning as a result of utility, but is to be chosen for its own sake" (VS 23, translation as in SEP; manuscript reading gives "is a virtue"). [source: SEP-Epi §6]
- The popular "eat, drink and be merry" image is a later caricature: in the Middle Ages Epicurus appeared as "proprietor of the kitchen, the tavern, and the brothel"; Dante puts Epicureans in flaming tombs in the sixth circle for believing the soul dies with the body. [source: WP-Epicurus]

### 4c. "Death is nothing to us" (ARGUMENT, step by step)
- Texts: Letter to Menoeceus 124–5 and Principal Doctrine 2. [source: SEP-Epi §4; WP-Principal_Doctrines]
- IEP reconstruction ("no subject of harm" argument): (1) Death is annihilation. (2) The living have not yet been annihilated. (3) So death does not affect the living. (4) So death is not bad for the living. (5) For something to be bad for somebody, that person has to exist. (6) The dead do not exist (from 1). (7) So death is not bad for the dead. (8) So death is bad for neither the living nor the dead. Corollary: it is foolish to let fear of what will not pain you when present pain you now. [source: IEP-Epi §5.g.i]
- SEP-Death reconstruction (alternative): intrinsic hedonism; a person's death is not an experience, so not intrinsically bad; "extrinsic instrumentalism" — something is extrinsically bad only if it causes intrinsically bad things; death and the person never coexist, so death causes her no experiences; so death is neither intrinsically nor extrinsically bad. [source: SEP-Death §3.1]
- Physics behind it: the soul is atomic, too delicate to survive the body; at death soul atoms scatter and sensation ends; so no punishment after death. [source: SEP-Epi §4]
- Weakness 1: the argument is strongest about being dead, not about painful dying. [source: SEP-Death §3.1]
- MAIN OBJECTION — DEPRIVATION (Thomas Nagel, "Death", Noûs 4(1), 1970, 73–80; reprinted in Mortal Questions, 1979): things can be bad for us by precluding goods, not only by causing bad experiences (cf. being unconscious through a joyous occasion one would otherwise have enjoyed); comparativism: death harms by making a life as a whole worse than it would otherwise have been. Other early proponents: Quinn 1984, Feldman 1991. [source: SEP-Death §3.1–3.2] Preferentialist variant: desires can be thwarted without one's knowing. [source: SEP-Death §3.1]
- Epicurean reply / the "timing puzzle": when is the dead person harmed, and who is the subject? Answers include priorism, subsequentism, Nagel's "indefinitism" (harm at no determinate time) and eternalism. [source: SEP-Death §4]
- LUCRETIUS' SYMMETRY ARGUMENT (De Rerum Natura book 3, in its closing section 830–1094): "Look back at time … before our birth. In this way Nature holds before our eyes the mirror of our future after death. Is this so grim, so gloomy?" (trans. Rolfe Humphries, 1968, as quoted in SEP-Death §5.2). Argument: we do not find our pre-natal non-existence bad; post-mortem non-existence is relevantly the same; so it is not bad either. The name "symmetry argument" is modern (Warren 2004). [source: SEP-Death §5.2; SEP-Luc; IEP-Epi §5.g.ii]
- Replies to symmetry: death, unlike late birth, deprives us of future goods; Nagel: one cannot have come into existence much earlier and still be oneself, though one could have lived longer (Nagel himself admits, in a footnote to the Mortal Questions reprint, this rests on questionable assumptions about origins); Parfit: we are rationally biased towards future goods. [source: SEP-Death §5.2]
- Nagel vs. Bernard Williams ("The Makropulos Case", 1973): Nagel holds death is an evil whenever it comes; Williams that immortality would be bad for us. [source: SEP-Death §7]

## 5. The Cynics
- Name kunikos = "dog-like"; origin disputed (Antisthenes' Cynosarges gymnasium; Antisthenes' nickname Haplokuôn; or Diogenes "the Dog"). [source: IEP-Cyn §1]
- Diogenes Laertius makes Antisthenes (c. 446–366 BC, a companion of Socrates) the first Cynic, "controversially"; Plato and Xenophon never call him a dog, so the label may have been applied after Diogenes. [source: IEP-Cyn §1; WP-Diogenes] The lineage Antisthenes → Diogenes → Crates → Hipparchia and Zeno is a tradition whose historical authenticity is "suspect" but tracks a real intellectual transmission. [source: IEP-Cyn §2]
- Cynicism was a way of life more than a doctrine; askêsis (training) central; "a shortcut to virtue" (D.L. 6.104, 7.122); none of the many Cynic texts survive — the evidence is anecdotes and sayings. [source: IEP-Cyn intro]
- Diogenes of Sinope (on the Black Sea): IEP c. 404–323 BCE; WP c. 413/403 – 324/321 BC (story that he died the same night as Alexander is "likely legend"). [source: IEP-Diog; WP-Diogenes] Exiled after a scandal over "debasing the currency" — scholars divided whether historical or a fiction symbolising his aim to "revalue the currency" (nomisma means both coinage and custom). [source: WP-Diogenes]
- He lived in a large ceramic storage jar (pithos), not a "barrel" (a later Latin reinterpretation beginning with Seneca; barrels did not exist in his time). [source: WP-Diogenes]
- Famous anecdotes (all from Diogenes Laertius book 6; Hicks translation as altered by IEP): Alexander: "Ask of me any boon you like." Diogenes: "Stand out of my light" (IEP cites D.L. "Book 6, Chapter 28"; the passage is often cited as 6.38 — not checked; WRITER: cite as "Diogenes Laertius, book 6"); "I am Diogenes the Dog" (6.60); asked where he came from: "I am a citizen of the world [kosmopolitês]" (6.63) — possibly a neologism; the lamp in daylight "looking for a human being" (6.41). [source: IEP-Cyn §3–4; IEP-Diog] Plato called him "Socrates gone mad". [source: WP-Diogenes; IEP-Diog section title]
- Cynic cosmopolitanism: Diogenes does not say he is apolis (city-less) but claims allegiance to the kosmos; John Moles has discussed whether Cynic cosmopolitanism was merely negative. [source: IEP-Cyn §4]
- Hipparchia of Maroneia (Thrace), born c. 350 BC (fl. c. 325), sister of the Cynic Metrocles; married Crates against her parents' wishes (he stripped and said "Here is the bridegroom, and this is his property"); lived the Cynic life with him in public; "the first known woman Cynic philosopher" (IEP); the only woman with her own entry among the 82 philosophers in Diogenes Laertius. Her reply to Theodorus the Atheist (D.L. 6.98): would she be thought to have decided wrongly "if I devote that time to philosophy, which I otherwise should have spent at the loom?" The Suda says she wrote treatises and letters, all lost. [source: WP-Hipparchia_of_Maroneia; IEP-Cyn §2]
- Cynic influence on Stoicism: primacy of ethics, sufficiency of virtue, living according to nature, askêsis; Epictetus (Discourses 3.22) praises the Cynic but warns against taking it up lightly. [source: IEP-Cyn §5]

## 6. Pyrrho and Pyrrhonism
- Pyrrho of Elis, c. 365/360 – c. 275/270 BCE; wrote nothing; associated with Anaxarchus of Abdera, with whom he reportedly accompanied Alexander to India, meeting "naked wise men" (gymnosophistai). Diogenes Laertius (9.61) claims his philosophy developed from this meeting, "but it is not clear what basis, if any, he has for this assertion". [source: SEP-Pyr §1] Alexander's Indian campaign: 327–325 BCE. [source: WP-Pyrrho]
- His follower Timon of Phlius is the main source; the key text is the "Aristocles passage" (Aristocles of Messene, quoted by Eusebius, Praep. Ev. 14.18.1–5). Three questions: what are things like by nature? how should we be disposed? what will result? Things are adiaphora, astathmêta, anepikrita; so we should be without opinions, saying of each thing that it "no more is than is not"; the result is aphasia, then ataraxia. [source: SEP-Pyr §2–3]
- DEBATE (interpretation): "metaphysical" reading (things are themselves indeterminate; then Pyrrho is not a sceptic in the later sense) vs. "epistemological" reading (we cannot determine their nature; a forerunner of later Pyrrhonism). [source: SEP-Pyr §3]
- DEBATE (Indian influence): we do not know who the Indian sages were or what they thought; reports stress Indian impassivity, and Pyrrho's reported calm under surgery is "exceptional in the Greek context", possibly from Indian training; some scholars find detailed links with Indian thought, and Christopher Beckwith (Greek Buddha, 2015) finds in Pyrrho an authentic early Buddhist; against, some argue language barriers ruled out subtle exchange (Richard Bett, citing Onesicritus' report that conversing needed three interpreters), and Johannes Bronkhorst (How the Brahmins Won, 2016) denies any connection between early Buddhism and Pyrrho. Others (Barua 1921, Jayatilleke 1963, Flintoff 1980 "Pyrrho and India", Phronesis 25) have proposed Jain or Ajñāna (Indian sceptic) influence. SEP's own view: "at least room for debate about how far these similarities really go", and the "overly pessimistic" language argument may be wrong since the expedition lasted years. Greek antecedents (Democritus; Anaxarchus) are also plausible. [source: SEP-Pyr §7; WP-Pyrrho]
- Beckwith (2011) proposed emending aphasia to apatheia in the Aristocles passage; "attractive" but speculative. [source: SEP-Pyr §3]
- Anecdotes that Pyrrho ignored precipices, carts and dogs and had to be protected by friends (Antigonus of Carystus, D.L. 9.62) are "clearly polemical inventions"; Aenesidemus disputed them. They reflect the ancient dispute over whether one can live without belief. [source: SEP-Pyr §5]
- Pyrrho had little influence in antiquity; Cicero and Seneca call him a neglected figure without a following; a continuous "Pyrrhonist" tradition only began in the 1st century BCE with Aenesidemus, who left the Academy to revive a more radical scepticism. [source: SEP-Pyr §1, §8; SEP-Skep §4.2]
- Aenesidemus' scheme: oppose appearances and thoughts → equipollence (isostheneia, "equal weight") → suspension of judgement (epochê) → tranquillity, while living according to appearances (D.L. 9.62, 78, 106–7). [source: SEP-Skep §4.2]
- THE TEN MODES (Aenesidemus; Sextus PH 1.36–163; D.L. 9.78–88; Philo of Alexandria): differences between animals; between humans; between the senses; states (age, emotion, etc.); positions, distances and places; mixtures; quantity/composition of objects; relativity; frequency or rarity; ways of life, customs, laws, myths and dogmatic beliefs. Two readings: "conflicting appearances" (we are party to the dispute, so cannot judge) vs. "causal invariance" (if X were F by nature it would affect everyone as F). [source: SEP-Skep §4.2]
- THE FIVE MODES OF AGRIPPA (1st–2nd c. CE; PH 1.164–177): disagreement (diaphônia); infinite regress; relativity; hypothesis (unargued assumption); circularity (diallêlos). Among "the most famous arguments of ancient skepticism". [source: SEP-Skep §4.3]
- Sextus Empiricus: ca. 160–210 CE [SEP-Skep §4.4]; "probably in the second or third century CE" [SEP-Sext]. A doctor; "Empiricus" refers to the Empiricist medical school, though he says scepticism is closer to Methodism. Works: Outlines of Pyrrhonism (PH, 3 books; PH 1 is the only surviving general account of Pyrrhonism) and the works grouped as Against the Mathematicians/Learned (M). [source: SEP-Skep §4.4]
- Definition (PH 1.8, Annas & Barnes trans.): "Scepticism is an ability to set out oppositions among things which appear and are thought of in any way at all, an ability by which, because of the equipollence in the opposed objects and accounts, we come first to suspension of judgment and afterwards to tranquillity." [source: SEP-Sext]
- Apelles the painter (PH 1.28): unable to paint a horse's lather, he threw his sponge at the picture and it produced the effect — tranquillity comes unexpectedly; it follows suspension "as a shadow follows a body" (PH 1.29). [source: SEP-Sext §3.3]
- Sceptic's end: tranquillity in matters of opinion and "moderate affection" (metriopatheia) in things forced on us (PH 1.25–29). [source: SEP-Skep §4.4]
- Renaissance transmission: Latin translation of the Outlines by Henricus Stephanus (Henri Estienne), Geneva 1562; complete Latin Sextus by Gentian Hervet 1569; Greek text first printed 1621; profound effect on Montaigne, Hume and others. [source: WP-Sextus_Empiricus] (Consistent with the published overview's "Sextus in print by 1562".)

## 7. The Academy turns sceptical
- Arcesilaus (316/5 or 315/4 – 241/0 BCE), from Pitane in Aeolis; studied with Crantor, Polemo and Crates in the Academy; head after Crates' death (SEP-Skep: c. 268/266) for more than 25 years; wrote nothing; with him "Plato's Academy turns skeptical"; he revived Socrates the examiner; dialectical method; argued against the Stoic "cognitive impression" (phantasia katalêptikê) as criterion of truth, concluding in universal suspension. [source: SEP-Arc §1; SEP-Skep §3.1] Whether he claimed to know that nothing can be known (Seneca, Letter 88) is debated. [source: SEP-Skep §3.1]
- Carneades (214–129/8 BCE), born in Cyrene (a Greek city on the North African coast); head of the Academy; studied with the Stoic Diogenes of Babylon; "If Chrysippus had not been, I would not have been" (D.L. 4.62); on the embassy to Rome (SEP-Carn: 155 BCE; SEP-Skep: 156/5) with Diogenes and Critolaus, reportedly spoke for justice one day and against it the next — a story attested only in much later sources and doubted (Powell 2013). Wrote nothing. [source: SEP-Carn §1; SEP-Skep §3.2]
- Carneades' "persuasive" (pithanon; Cicero's probabile) criterion in three grades: persuasive; persuasive and undiverted; persuasive, undiverted and thoroughly explored (the rope/snake in a dark room, M 7.187). Whether this "approval" amounts to belief divided his pupils (Philo and Metrodorus: yes; Clitomachus, followed by Cicero: no). [source: SEP-Skep §3.2]
- Later Academy: Clitomachus (head 127–110 BCE); Philo of Larissa, who later at Rome allowed knowledge without Stoic cognition; with Philo the sceptical Academy ends. [source: SEP-Skep §3.3]
### 7a. The apraxia (inaction) objection and replies (ARGUMENT)
- Objection (Stoic): action requires impression, assent and impulse; there is no action without assent, i.e. without believing the action is to be done; the sceptic suspends assent; so the sceptic cannot act (or acts like a plant or animal: Arcesilaus is "robbing people of their minds", Cicero Acad. 2.37–9). [source: SEP-Skep §3.1]
- Reply 1 (Arcesilaus): one can act without assent, following "the reasonable" (eulogon) (Sextus M 7.150, 158); also, against Epicureans, perception without added belief is enough to use the door rather than walk into the wall. [source: SEP-Skep §3.1]
- Reply 2 (Carneades): act on the persuasive impression, tested more thoroughly the more is at stake. [source: SEP-Skep §3.2]
- Reply 3 (Sextus, PH 1.21–24): the sceptic follows appearances "without belief" through a fourfold observance: "By nature's guidance we are naturally capable of perceiving and thinking. By the necessitation of feelings, hunger conducts us to food and thirst to drink. By the handing down of customs and laws, we accept, from an everyday point of view, that piety is good and impiety bad. By teaching of kinds of expertise we are not inactive in those expertises which we accept." (PH 1.24, Annas & Barnes trans.) [source: SEP-Sext; SEP-Skep §4.4]
- DEBATE: does the Pyrrhonist have any beliefs at all? Michael Frede (1979): the sceptic can have ordinary, non-dogmatic beliefs; Myles Burnyeat (1980, "Can the Sceptic Live His Scepticism?") and Jonathan Barnes (1982): Sextus' scepticism rejects all belief, making it radically different from ordinary life. "In the past 40 years, scholars have paid attention to this question more than to any other interpretive issue." [source: SEP-Skep §4.4; SEP-Sext]

## 8. Roman transmitters: Cicero and Lucretius
- Cicero (106–43 BCE; IEP: born 3 January 106, murdered 7 December 43); never identified as a Stoic; his own position derived from his teacher Philo of Larissa and the New Academy; studied philosophy in Athens; sought to popularise philosophy at Rome. [source: SEP-Sto §1.1, §1.3; IEP-Cic §1] After his daughter Tullia's death and Caesar's dictatorship he wrote, in a short burst, works aiming to cover "the whole of philosophy" (On the Nature of the Gods 1.7): Academica, De Finibus, Tusculan Disputations, On the Nature of the Gods, On Fate, On Divination, On Duties (modelled on Panaetius) etc. [source: SEP-Cic §1, §6; SEP-Sto §1.1] He is a main (and critical) source for Stoic, Epicurean and Academic views; long valued mainly as a source, increasingly studied as a philosopher in his own right. [source: SEP-Cic intro; SEP-Epi §1] Augustine was turned to philosophy by Cicero's lost Hortensius. [source: IEP-Cic]
- Lucretius (Titus Lucretius Carus, mid-90s to mid-50s BCE): six-book Latin hexameter poem De Rerum Natura on Epicurean physics; our fullest source for Epicurean physics and sole Epicurean source for the swerve; addressed to Memmius (probably C. Memmius, praetor 58 BCE). Only external contemporary mention: Cicero's letter to his brother Quintus, February 54 BCE (Q.F. 2.10.3). Jerome's story that a love potion drove him mad and he wrote in "intervals of sanity" before suicide is "hard to take at face value". Epicurus is named only once in the poem. [source: SEP-Luc §1–2] Reproduces Epicurus "faithfully" (Sedley 1998; Clay allows more originality). [source: SEP-Epi §1]
- Rediscovered in 1417 by the papal secretary Poggio Bracciolini (story told in Greenblatt 2011, The Swerve); the text survives via two 9th-century Carolingian manuscripts in Leiden. [source: SEP-Luc]

## 9. The Roman Stoics
### Seneca
- Lucius Annaeus Seneca: born in Corduba (Córdoba), Spain; SEP c. 1 BCE, WP c. 4 BC; died 65 CE. Exiled to Corsica in 41 (charged with adultery with Caligula's sister, under Claudius); recalled 49 as Nero's tutor; Nero's adviser from 54 (with Burrus, 54–62); suffect consul 56; accused of complicity in the Pisonian conspiracy and compelled to commit suicide in 65 (probably innocent, no consensus). [source: SEP-Sen §1; WP-Seneca_the_Younger]
- Works: Moral Letters to Lucilius (124 letters; written in retirement 62–65), the "dialogues"/Moral Essays (incl. three consolations, On Anger, On the Shortness of Life, On Mercy addressed to Nero 55/56), Natural Questions, tragedies (Medea, Thyestes, Phaedra). [source: SEP-Sen §1; WP-Seneca_the_Younger] On the Shortness of Life (De Brevitate Vitae), to Paulinus, usually dated c. 49 CE; chapter 1 argues life is long enough if well managed. [source: WP-De_Brevitate_Vitae_(Seneca)]
- Hypocrisy charge: in 58 CE Publius Suillius Rufus (Tacitus Annals 13.42; Cassius Dio) accused him of amassing 300 million sesterces in four years of service to Nero. [source: WP-Seneca_the_Younger] J. M. Cooper criticises Seneca's advice to avoid logic as undercutting Stoic moral education. [source: IEP-Sen]
- Before Erasmus the philosopher and the tragedian were often thought to be two different Senecas. [source: SEP-Sen §1]
### Musonius Rufus (teacher of Epictetus)
- Gaius Musonius Rufus, born Volsinii, Etruria, c. 20–30 CE; exiled under Nero (65); his surviving lecture extracts include "That Women Too Should Study Philosophy" and "Should Daughters Receive the Same Education as Sons?" — he argued that men's and women's capacity for virtue is the same. [source: WP-Gaius_Musonius_Rufus] (Useful for "women where they actually were".)
### Epictetus
- Born in the 50s CE (WP: c. 50) in Hierapolis (Phrygia, Asia Minor); a slave of Epaphroditus, an administrator at Nero's court; studied with Musonius Rufus; freed; taught in Rome until Domitian expelled philosophers — SEP-Ept: "presumably by the edict of Domitian (in 89)"; SEP-Sto and WP: 93 CE — then founded a school at Nicopolis in Epirus (NW Greece), where he taught until his death around 135. Lame (arthritis or, per Celsus in Origen, abuse by his master). [source: SEP-Ept §1; WP-Epictetus; SEP-Sto §1.1] Safe wording: "expelled with other philosophers by Domitian, around 89–93 CE".
- He wrote nothing; the Discourses (4 of 8 or more books survive) and the Encheiridion (Handbook, an abridgment) were compiled by his pupil Arrian of Nicomedia. [source: SEP-Ept §1]
- Key ideas: prohairesis ("volition", Long's rendering), the correct use of impressions; what is "up to us" (eph' hêmin). Marcus Aurelius was never his pupil but read him (borrowed from Rusticus, Med. 1.7). The Encheiridion was translated into Latin by Poliziano in 1497. [source: SEP-Ept §1, 4.3, 6; IEP-MA §2]
- Admiral James Stockdale, Courage Under Fire: Testing Epictetus's Doctrines in a Laboratory of Human Behavior (Hoover Institution, 1993). [source: IEP-Ept bibliography]
### Marcus Aurelius
- Born 121 CE; adopted by Antoninus Pius in 138; emperor 161–180 (with Lucius Verus until 169); died 17 March 180 aged 58, at Vindobona (Vienna) or near Sirmium. Teachers acknowledged in Meditations Book 1 include the Stoics Rusticus, Apollonius and Sextus. [source: SEP-MA §1; IEP-MA §1; WP-Marcus_Aurelius]
- The Meditations: 12 books written in (Koine) Greek, a private notebook "to and for himself", probably partly on campaign in central Europe c. 171–175 CE; Greek title ta eis heauton ("to himself") first recorded c. 900 (Arethas); "Meditations" is a modern title (Casaubon); first printed edition Xylander, Zürich 1558/59 from a now-lost manuscript. [source: SEP-MA §1.1; IEP-MA §2; WP-Meditations] Earliest mention: IEP says Themistius in 364; WP says the earliest clear mention dates from c. 907 (Arethas). Nowhere does Marcus call himself a Stoic. [source: IEP-MA §3]
- Hadot (The Inner Citadel, 1992 French; 1998 English) reads the Meditations as spiritual exercises organised by Epictetus' three disciplines (desire, action, assent). [source: IEP-MA; IEP-Hadot §4e; IEP-Sto]

## 10. Hadot's "philosophy as a way of life" and its critics
- Pierre Hadot (Paris 1922 – April 2010): former Catholic priest; philologist of Plotinus, Marius Victorinus and Marcus Aurelius; early French writer on Wittgenstein; professor at the Collège de France from 1982 (chair of the History of Hellenistic and Roman Thought), honorary from 1991. Key books: Exercices spirituels et philosophie antique (1981; English Philosophy as a Way of Life, ed. Arnold Davidson, trans. Michael Chase, Blackwell 1995) and Qu'est-ce que la philosophie antique? (1995; English What Is Ancient Philosophy?, Harvard 2002). [source: IEP-Hadot §1]
- Thesis: since Socrates, the choice of a way of life "stands at the beginning" of ancient philosophy, not as an appendix; becoming a Stoic or Epicurean was more like a conversion; philosophical discourse served "spiritual exercises" (attention, meditation, memorising doctrines, premeditation of evils, examination of conscience). He did not deny the ancients' theoretical achievements. Influenced Foucault (History of Sexuality vols 2–3), though Hadot differed from Foucault. [source: IEP-Hadot §4a–4b, intro; WP-Pierre_Hadot]
- Hadot: "from 1970 on, I have felt very strongly that it was Epicureanism and Stoicism which could nourish the spiritual life of men and women of our times". [source: IEP-Hadot intro]
- Critics: Hadot's narrative "provoked both praise and criticism" [IEP-Hadot]; John M. Cooper, Pursuits of Wisdom: Six Ways of Life in Ancient Philosophy from Socrates to Plotinus (Princeton, 2012), accepts that ancient philosophy was a way of life but holds that reasoning and argument, not "spiritual exercises", were central, and challenges how strongly such exercises figured in Greek philosophy; Matthew Sharpe (Philosophy Today 2014) defends Hadot against Cooper using Epictetus. [source: WP-Pierre_Hadot (Cooper 2012 citation); WS-2 (NDPR review and Sharpe article as summarised) — CAVEAT: the NDPR text itself was not reachable]
- The published overview says Hadot's thesis "fits this era exactly"; the chapter should present it as an influential, contested thesis.

## 11. Modern revivals
- Neostoicism: Justus Lipsius (late 16th century), combining Stoicism and Christianity; du Vair's Traité de la Constance (1594). [source: SEP-Sto §5.3; WP-Stoicism]
- CBT: Aaron T. Beck et al., Cognitive Therapy of Depression (1979), p. 8: "The philosophical origins of cognitive therapy can be traced back to the Stoic philosophers". [source: WP-Stoicism; WP-Cognitive_behavioral_therapy] Albert Ellis acknowledged Epictetus (and Stoicism generally) as an influence on Rational Emotive (Behavior) Therapy, REBT being the major precursor of CBT; Enchiridion 5 was taught to REBT clients in first sessions (Robertson 2010). Ellis also credited Korzybski's general semantics, Adler, Horney and others. [source: WP-Stoicism; WP-Albert_Ellis; WP-Rational_emotive_behavior_therapy; WS-7] WRITER: "CBT's founders named the Stoics, especially Epictetus, as forerunners"; do not say CBT was derived from Stoicism (Wikipedia frames it as "precursors"; WP-CBT also names J. S. Mill's associationism as an influence).
- Modern Stoicism: philosophers Lawrence Becker (A New Stoicism, Princeton; IEP says 1997, WP 1998), William Irvine (2008; proposes a "trichotomy of control"), John Sellars; CBT therapist Donald Robertson (The Philosophy of Cognitive-Behavioural Therapy, 2010). [source: IEP-Sto §6] The "Modern Stoicism" project grew from a 2012 workshop at the University of Exeter (Christopher Gill); first Stoic Week November 2012; annual Stoicon events; Modern Stoicism reports nearly 40,000 Stoic Week sign-ups since 2012. [source: WS-6 (modernstoicism.com and John Sellars' site as summarised)]
- Epicureanism today: Emily A. Austin, Living for Pleasure: An Epicurean Guide to Life (OUP 2022); Greenblatt, The Swerve (2011). [source: WP-Epicurus; WP-Epicureanism]

## Debates
- Interpreting Pyrrho: metaphysical vs. epistemological readings of the Aristocles passage; how much is Pyrrho vs. Timon (Bett; Svavarsson; Green 2017). [source: SEP-Pyr §3]
- Indian influence on Pyrrho: Beckwith 2015 (Pyrrho as early Buddhist), Flintoff 1980, Kuzminski 2008 (Pyrrhonism "reinvented Buddhism") vs. Bett 2000 (language barrier) and Bronkhorst 2016 (no connection); SEP: "room for debate". Separately, McEvilley and others note parallels between Sextus and Nāgārjuna (2nd–3rd c. CE). [source: SEP-Pyr §7; WP-Pyrrho; WP-Pyrrhonism]
- Do Pyrrhonists have beliefs? Frede (ordinary beliefs allowed) vs. Burnyeat and Barnes (no beliefs at all). [source: SEP-Skep §4.4; SEP-Sext]
- Did Arcesilaus hold that nothing can be known, or argue only dialectically against the Stoics? [source: SEP-Skep §3.1; SEP-Arc]
- Did Carneades' "approval" involve belief? Philo/Metrodorus vs. Clitomachus/Cicero. [source: SEP-Skep §3.2]
- The swerve: Epicurus' own doctrine or emphasis of later sources? Every voluntary action, or minimal role? [source: SEP-Luc; SEP-Epi §4]
- Is death bad for the one who dies? Epicurus/Lucretius vs. deprivationists (Nagel 1970, Feldman 1991); the timing puzzle; Nagel vs. Williams on immortality. [source: SEP-Death]
- Stoic indifferents: coherent, or (Plutarch, Cicero) a self-contradiction? [source: SEP-Sto §4.3]
- Stoic determinism and responsibility: genuine compatibilism (Bobzien 1998); dog-and-cart simile's authorship (Bobzien: Roman Stoa). [source: SEP-Sto §2.8; IEP-Chrys]
- Unity of the Stoic system: ethics independent of physics (Annas 1993) vs. intertwined (Cooper). [source: SEP-Sto §1.2]
- Hadot vs. Cooper (2012) on whether "spiritual exercises" or reasoned argument define ancient philosophy as a way of life. [source: IEP-Hadot; WS-2]
- Was Seneca a hypocrite? Ancient charges (Suillius, 58 CE) and the debate over his wealth. [source: WP-Seneca_the_Younger]
- Was Cicero merely a transmitter? Increasingly studied as an original thinker (SEP-Cic; IEP-Cic calls the "derivative and unoriginal" verdict "incorrect"). [source: SEP-Cic; IEP-Cic]
- Was Diogenes' coin-debasing exile historical or a symbolic fiction? [source: WP-Diogenes]

## Consistency with published Periergia texts
- Overview (`philosophy--history-of-philosophy--overview.sql`, section IV): broadly consistent with sources. Points to watch: (a) "Hadot's ... thesis ... fits this era exactly" — sources present it as influential but contested (Cooper 2012); the chapter should name the debate. (b) "the walled Garden" — no source read here describes the Garden as walled; avoid. (c) "Stoic propositional logic anticipated the nineteenth century" — SEP-Log says its similarities to Frege's (late 19th c.) logic are striking; fine if phrased as "anticipated features of modern logic". (d) "Sextus Empiricus's compendium ... rediscovered in the sixteenth century" / "Sextus in print by 1562" — consistent (Stephanus 1562). (e) "the Skeptics ... for every argument an equal counter-argument, and in the suspension, tranquility" — consistent with Aenesidemus/Sextus (equipollence), less so with the Academics, whose aim was not stated as tranquillity in the sources read.
- Plato chapter: "later the Academy itself became a sceptical school" — consistent (Arcesilaus, from c. 268/266). Plato research notes: Academy as institution ended after Philo of Larissa (83 BCE) — consistent with SEP-Skep ("with Philo, the skeptical era ... comes to an end").
- Socrates chapter: "Almost every ancient school except the Epicureans claimed Socrates as founder or model" — consistent (Arcesilaus revived Socrates the examiner; Zeno inspired by Xenophon's Socrates; Cynics claimed Socratic lineage). Aristippus/Arete (Cyrenaics) — not checked here.
- Presocratics chapter quiz: "Diogenes the Cynic refuted [Zeno] by standing up and walking" is used as a wrong option; that anecdote was not checked in this session.
- What Is Philosophy? chapter: Lucretius' spear thought-experiment about the edge of the universe — not checked here (SEP-Epi gives Epicurus' related "edge" argument for infinity, LH 41–42).
- Aristotle research notes: Sulla took Athens in 86 BCE; WP-Hellenistic_philosophy says 87 BCE (the siege began in 87, the city fell in 86 per common accounts — not verified; use "in the 80s BCE" or "86").
- Internal contradictions among sources, recorded above: Epictetus' expulsion 89 (SEP-Ept) vs 93 (SEP-Sto, WP); Chrysippus' book count (150 / 300 on logic / 705); Seneca's birth c. 1 BCE (SEP) vs c. 4 BC (WP); Carneades' embassy 155 (SEP-Carn) vs 156/5 (SEP-Skep); first mention of the Meditations 364 (IEP, Themistius) vs c. 907 (WP, Arethas); IEP-Sto attributes the dog-and-cart image to Epictetus, contradicted by IEP-Chrys (Hippolytus: Zeno and Chrysippus); Becker's A New Stoicism 1997 (IEP) vs 1998 (WP).
