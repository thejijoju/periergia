## 1. What virtue ethics is (the theory's structure)
- Virtue ethics is "currently one of three major approaches in normative ethics", alongside deontology and consequentialism; it "emphasizes the virtues, or moral character". Illustration: someone in need should be helped — a utilitarian points to consequences, a deontologist to a rule ("Do unto others…"), a virtue ethicist to the fact that helping "would be charitable or benevolent". [source: SEP-VE intro]
- Each approach can make room for virtues, consequences and rules; what distinguishes virtue ethics is "the centrality of virtue within the theory": virtue ethicists resist defining virtue in terms of something more fundamental; virtues and vices are "foundational" and other normative notions "grounded in them". Consequentialists define virtues as traits yielding good consequences; deontologists as traits of those who reliably do their duty. [source: SEP-VE intro]
- "Virtue ethics" (the third approach) must now be distinguished from "virtue theory" (accounts of virtue inside the other approaches: Kantian virtue theory; consequentialist virtue theories of Driver 2001 and Hurka 2001). [source: SEP-VE §1]
- History in one line: "In the West, virtue ethics' founding fathers are Plato and Aristotle, and in the East it can be traced back to Mencius and Confucius." It was the dominant Western approach "until at least the Enlightenment", "suffered a momentary eclipse during the nineteenth century", and re-emerged in Anglo-American philosophy in the late 1950s, "heralded by" Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958). [source: SEP-VE §1]
- Three Greek concepts used by almost every modern version: aretê (excellence or virtue), phronesis (practical or moral wisdom), eudaimonia (happiness or flourishing). [source: SEP-VE §1]
- Virtue defined (SEP-VE): "an excellent trait of character … a disposition, well entrenched in its possessor—something that, as we say, goes all the way down, unlike a habit such as being a tea-drinker—to notice, expect, value, feel, desire, choose, act, and react in certain characteristic ways." It is a "multi-track disposition"; so it is "reckless" to attribute one from a single action (Sreenivasan 2002). Example: the honest person tells the truth because "To do otherwise would be dishonest", not because honesty is the best policy or from fear of being caught. [source: SEP-VE §1.1]
- Virtue comes in degrees; full or perfect virtue is rare. The fully virtuous do what they should without a struggle; the "continent" must overcome contrary desire. Foot (1978) on whether the continent are more admirable: it depends on what makes it hard — hard circumstances (a very poor person returning a full purse) make the act more admirable; a defect of character (temptation to keep what is not hers) does not. [source: SEP-VE §1.1]
- Practical wisdom: what "the virtuous morally mature adult has that nice children, including nice adolescents, lack"; good intentions plus knowledge of how to secure real benefits; it "characteristically comes only with experience of life" and includes the capacity to see which features of a situation matter most. Without it, "virtues" such as compassion or courage can lead to wrongdoing — Aristotle calls these "natural virtue", "a proto version of full virtue awaiting perfection by phronesis". [source: SEP-VE §1.2]
- Four contemporary forms (SEP-VE §2): (a) eudaimonist (virtues defined by their relation to flourishing; most neo-Aristotelians); (b) agent-based and exemplarist (Michael Slote; Linda Zagzebski — "We do not have criteria for goodness in advance of identifying the exemplars of goodness", Zagzebski 2004: 41); (c) target-centred (Christine Swanton 2003: a virtue "is a disposition to respond to, or acknowledge, items within its field or fields in an excellent or good enough way"); (d) Platonistic (Iris Murdoch, Timothy Chappell; Robert Merrihew Adams). Murdoch: "In the moral life the enemy is the fat relentless ego" (Murdoch 1971: 51). [source: SEP-VE §2]
- IEP-VE's division instead: three main directions since the revival — eudaimonism, agent-based theories, and the ethics of care. Its slogan for virtue-ethical advice: "Act as a virtuous person would act in your situation." [source: IEP-VE intro]
- Eudaimonia translation: "flourishing" is better than "happiness" because happiness in ordinary speech is subjective (if I think I am happy I am), whereas one can be wrong about whether one is flourishing; but plants and animals can "flourish" while eudaimonia is possible only for rational beings. It is a "moralized or value-laden concept" (Hursthouse 1999: 188–189). For Aristotle virtue is necessary but not sufficient for eudaimonia (external goods, luck, are also needed); for Plato and the Stoics it is necessary and sufficient (Annas 1993). [source: SEP-VE §2.1]

## 2. Aristotle: the structure (keep brief; Aristotle chapter covers it)
- The highest good is desirable for itself, not for the sake of anything else, and everything else is desired for its sake; all agree to call it eudaimonia, used by Aristotle as a substitute for eu zên ("living well"). Etymology eu + daimon ("well-favoured by a god") plays little role in his thinking. [source: SEP-ArEth §2]
- **Function argument (NE I.7, 1097b22–1098a20), stated step by step** [sources: SEP-ArEth §2; IEP-VE §3a; already reconstructed in the published Aristotle chapter]:
  1. The highest good is agreed to be eudaimonia; the question is what it consists in.
  2. Where a thing has a function (ergon), its good lies in performing that function well (IEP's example: a knife's function is to cut, and it is good when it cuts well).
  3. A human being has a function, and it must be what is peculiar to humans: not nutrition and growth (shared with plants) nor perception (shared with animals) but the activity of the rational part of the soul. SEP: "human beings are the only species that has not only these lower capacities but a rational soul as well."
  4. Doing anything well requires the relevant virtue or excellence.
  5. So the human good is activity of the rational soul in accordance with virtue, "over the course of a full life".
  - Objection (SEP-ArEth §3.1): too general to show that the traditional virtues are what flourishing requires. Replies: Aristotle addresses those already well brought up; Book I is an "outline" to be filled in later (1098a20–22).
  - Aristotle says happiness is virtuous *activity*, not virtue (a refinement of the traditional view identifying it with virtue, 1098b30–1); it also needs friends, wealth, power; being extremely ugly or losing children or friends mars it (1099a31–b6); virtuous activity is diminished without external goods (1153b17–19). [source: SEP-ArEth §2]
- Two kinds of virtue (1103a1–10): intellectual (of the reasoning part) and ethical/of character (of the part that can follow reason). [source: SEP-ArEth §4]
- **Habituation.** Development has two stages: in childhood proper habits; when reason is developed, practical wisdom. "Ethical virtue is fully developed only when it is combined with practical wisdom (1144b14–17). A low-grade form of ethical virtue emerges in us during childhood as we are repeatedly placed in situations that call for appropriate actions and emotions." [source: SEP-ArEth §4] IEP-VE: "Aristotle advises us to perform just acts because this way we become just … Virtue is not itself a habit. Habituation is merely an aid to the development of virtue, but true virtue requires choice, understanding, and knowledge." [source: IEP-VE §2b]
- NE II.1 wording "we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts" — second-hand only: WS summaries attribute this exact wording to Ross (and to Rackham 1934), at about 1103a34–b2. Use with "Ross" only as second-hand; the sense is confirmed by IEP-VE. [source: WS-4]
- **Definition of virtue (NE II.6, 1106b36–1107a2).** Ross's wording, second-hand via WebSearch summary of the Elpenor Ross text: "Virtue, then, is a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it." The first part through "rational principle" is in the WS summary; the final clause is confirmed in substance by SEP-ArEth, which quotes 1107a1–2 as the mean being "determined by logos ('reason', 'account') and in the way that the person of practical reason would determine it". IEP-VE paraphrases: Book II defines virtue "as a purposive disposition, lying in a mean and being determined by the right reason". [sources: WS-1 (Elpenor/Ross 1908, unreachable directly); SEP-ArEth §5.2; IEP-VE §2b]
- The mean is "relative to us": too little food for Milo (the wrestler) is too much for a beginner (NE II.6). [source: SEP-Comp §3] Sometimes great anger is the mean; the right amount is "proportionate to the seriousness of the situation", not a midpoint. [source: SEP-ArEth §5.1]
- Two doctrines of the mean: (1) each virtue lies between a vice of excess and one of deficiency; (2) the virtuous person aims at what is intermediate in action. Objection: (2) is inapplicable or unilluminating for non-quantitative choices (e.g. whether to attend a wedding). Reply (Kraut): a juror aims at a verdict reached by deliberation "neither overly credulous nor unduly skeptical"; aiming at the mean = giving each relevant consideration the right weight. A further difficulty: which emotions fall under the mean? The Stoics concluded anger and fear are always inappropriate. [source: SEP-ArEth §5.1]
- Not a decision procedure: "no ethical theory can do" this; the good person "sees the truth in each case, being as it were a standard and measure of them" (1113a32–3). But some emotions (spite, shamelessness, envy) and actions (adultery, theft, murder) are always wrong (1107a8–12). [source: SEP-ArEth §5.2]
- **Unity / reciprocity of the virtues.** Full ethical virtue requires practical wisdom (1144b14–17); WS (Ross text, second-hand): "it is not possible to be good in the strict sense without practical wisdom, nor practically wise without moral virtue", and "with the presence of the one quality, practical wisdom, will be given all the virtues" (NE VI.13; Bekker line not confirmed). The Stoics held a stronger unity thesis (§3). Dan Russell (2009) proposes "cardinality and a version of the unity thesis" to solve "the enumeration problem" (too many virtues); Adams (2006) gives up practical wisdom as the heart of all virtues to allow "frail and fragmentary" virtues — "a substantial sacrifice" (Russell 2009; Kamtekar 2010). [sources: WS-6 (second-hand); SEP-ArEth §4; SEP-VE §§3h, 4]

## 3. The Stoics: virtue the only good (brief; Hellenistic chapter covers it)
- To achieve the telos (living in agreement with nature) "only one thing is needed: virtue", "the perfected condition of human reason" (Seneca). [source: SEP-Stoa §4.2]
- Virtues are forms of knowledge (courage = knowledge of what should be endured and feared) and are inseparable: "whoever has one virtue has them all" (Long & Sedley 61B–H); since knowledge is all-or-nothing, so is virtue. [source: SEP-Stoa §4.2]
- Two "contentious" theses: happiness is fully in our power, and virtue is the only good. Argument (Diogenes Laertius, LS 58A), stated step by step:
  1. The good's peculiar characteristic is to benefit, never to harm (as heating, not chilling, is peculiar to the hot).
  2. Wealth and health "no more do benefit than they harm".
  3. Therefore wealth and health are not good; they are "indifferent" (adiaphora). Only virtue is good.
  - Objection (Academics, Peripatetics; Aristotle NE I.5 1095b33–1096a2 thinks the conclusion absurd): a tortured, impoverished, reviled virtuous person cannot be happy. Stoic reply: bite the bullet — the virtuous person on the rack is maximally happy (Cicero, De Finibus 3.42), equally happy as before; but "preferred" indifferents (health, wealth, reputation) have value and it is one's proper function (kathêkon) to select them in normal circumstances (Chrysippus, against marginal figures in Zeno's circle). [source: SEP-Stoa §4.3]
- Contrast for the chapter: Aristotle — virtue necessary, not sufficient; Stoics — necessary and sufficient. [source: SEP-VE §2.1; SEP-Stoa §4.3]

## 4. Confucian virtues and Mencius vs Xunzi (brief; Confucianism chapter covers it)
- Five behaviours/virtues of the gentleman most central to the Analects: benevolence (ren 仁), righteousness (yi 義), ritual propriety (li 禮), wisdom (zhi 智), trustworthiness (xin 信). "By the Han period" they "began to be considered as a complete set of human virtues, corresponding with other quintets of phenomena used to describe the natural world"; before that "it is unclear whether the possession of a particular virtue entailed having all the others", though ren was sometimes a general term for a combination of virtues. [source: SEP-Conf §4]
- Ren: Analects 12.22 "caring for others"; used both as one virtue among others and as comprehensive excellence; translations "benevolence" (D. C. Lau 1970), "Good/Goodness" (Waley 1938; Slingerland 2003); before Confucius it meant the strong, handsome appearance of an aristocrat; in the Analects a moral excellence anyone can potentially achieve; Confucius never claimed it for himself. [source: SEP-ChEth §2.1]
- Wisdom (zhi): appraising people and situations ("knowing others", 12.22); "How can a person be considered wise if that person does not dwell in benevolence?" (4.1); recognizing knowing as knowing and ignorance as ignorance (2.17). Trustworthiness (xin): "if one is trustworthy, others will give one responsibilities" (17.6); "If the people do not find the ruler trustworthy, the state will not stand" (12.7). Translations Csikszentmihalyi's own. [source: SEP-Conf §4]
- Junzi: originally "prince's son"; "gentleman" in early translations; Ames and Rosemont (1998) "exemplary person". Yu (2007): dao plays a role in ancient Chinese ethics "analogous to the role played by eudaimonia". [source: SEP-ChEth §2.1]
- Li: Analects 1.15 likens self-cultivation to cutting bone, carving horn, polishing jade. "While Aristotelian habituation generally corresponds to the Confucian cultivation of character, there is no comparable emphasis in Aristotle on the role of ritual performance." [source: SEP-ChEth §2.2]
- Mencius's four virtues: benevolence, righteousness, wisdom, propriety; wisdom and propriety secondary: "The core of benevolence is serving one's parents. The core of righteousness is obeying one's elder brother. The core of wisdom is knowing these two and not abandoning them. The core of ritual propriety is the adornment of these two" (4A27; Van Norden 2008, 101). "Mencian wisdom (somewhat like Aristotelian phronēsis) seems to be only partially constrained by rules"; sages act differently but "had [they] exchanged places, each would have done as the other" (4B31); ultimate standard "timeliness" (5B1). Rescuing a drowning sister-in-law overrides the ritual ban on touching (4A17). [source: SEP-Men §2]
- **Child at the well, 2A6 (trans. Bryan W. Van Norden 2008, p. 46):** "The reason why I say that all humans have hearts that are not unfeeling toward others is this. Suppose someone suddenly saw a child about to fall into a well: anyone in such a situation would have a feeling of alarm and compassion—not because one sought to get in good with the child's parents, not because one wanted fame among one's neighbors and friends, and not because one would dislike the sound of the child's cries. From this we can see that if one is without the feeling of compassion, one is not human." [source: SEP-Men §4]
- IEP's rendering (translator not named) continues: "A heart-mind that sympathizes is the sprout of co-humanity [ren]; a heart-mind that is aware of shame is the sprout of rightness [yi]; a heart-mind that defers to others is the sprout of ritual propriety [li]; a heart-mind that approves and condemns is the sprout of wisdom [zhi]…. If anyone having the four sprouts within himself knows how to develop them to the full, it is like fire catching alight, or a spring as it first bursts through." IEP stresses Mencius "says nothing about acting on" the response: "It is merely the feeling that counts." [source: IEP-Men §§6–7]
- **2A6 step by step** (built from SEP-Men §§2–4, IEP-Men §§6–7):
  1. Anyone who suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well feels alarm and compassion.
  2. The feeling is not explained by self-interest (favour with the parents, reputation, dislike of the noise) — this targets Yang Zhu's egoist picture of human nature.
  3. So all humans have a heart "not unfeeling toward others"; one without it "is not human".
  4. Likewise all have shame/disdain, deference, approval/disapproval.
  5. These four are the "sprouts" (duan) of ren, yi, li, zhi.
  6. Sprouts are "innate but incipient"; they must be "extended" or "filled out" (7B31; 1A7 the king who spared the ox), with material security, education and effort.
  7. So human nature is good (tends to virtue in a healthy environment).
  - Objection (Xunzi, ch. 23 "Human Nature Is Bad"; trans. Ivanhoe & Van Norden 2005, 299): "Mengzi says: When people engage in learning, this manifests the goodness of their nature. I say: This is not so … Those things in people that cannot be learned and cannot be worked at are called their 'nature.'" Reply/assessment: they may be at cross-purposes over "nature" (Graham 1967), yet a substantive disagreement remains: Xunzi denies innate incipient dispositions to virtue. [source: SEP-Men §5]
- **Warped wood** (Xunzi 23, trans. Ivanhoe & Van Norden 2005, 298): "crooked wood must await steaming and straightening on the shaping frame, and only then does it become straight. Blunt metal must await honing and grinding, and only then does it become sharp." For Mencius cultivation is like encouraging a sprout's innate tendency; for Xunzi like straightening wood. [source: SEP-Men §5]
- Xunzi 23.1a (trans. Paul R. Goldin): "Human xing is evil; what is good is artifice." Moral self-cultivation is to transform (huaxing 化性) nature; Xunzi located proper moral models outside the self, against the Mencian Four Beginnings (siduan) "lodged within the human heart (e.g., Mencius 2A.6)". The chapter is occasionally impugned as inauthentic (Zhou Chicheng 2015). [source: SEP-Xun §§2–3]
- Comparison point for this chapter: Aristotle's habituation + phronesis vs Mencius's growth model vs Xunzi's reshaping model. (SEP-Comp: Xunzi "is equally emphatic about the necessity of right and caring relationship to others for human flourishing, even though he denies … that human nature contains tendencies to engage in such relationships.") [source: SEP-Comp §3]

## 5. Is Confucianism a virtue ethics? Role ethics and comparative scholarship
- Wong (SEP): "For some scholars this will be an obvious, uncontroversial truth. For others, it is a misconstrual that imposes contentious Western assumptions." Uncontroversial in a broad sense (virtues = traits that are objects of aspiration, making up an ideal of the person). Controversial in narrower senses: Tiwald (2018) — virtue ethics as giving traits "explanatory priority over right action and promoting good consequences", a competitor to deontology and consequentialism; "There simply is not enough discussion in the Confucian texts … addressed to the kind of questions these Western theories seek to answer." Clearly wrong senses: virtues as atomistic (possessed in isolation from others) or as context-invariant. "Prominent and influential critics … Roger Ames (2011, 2021) and Robert Neville (2016) — seem to be supposing that the term is loaded with such controversial presuppositions." Wong's view: one can use virtue language "with the appropriate qualifiers" while accepting the critics' insights (virtues realized in relationships; being someone's son or mother can be part of one's identity). [source: SEP-ChEth §2.1]
- Rosemont and Ames (on the Classic of Filial Piety): "prescribed social roles are a defining characteristic of the 'Confucian tradition'", normative guides to conduct; they contrast this with "virtue ethics", which "they say requires rational calculation to determine moral conduct, while filial piety is simply a matter of meeting one's family obligations." [source: SEP-Conf §5]
- Ames & Rosemont, quoted (via WP-Role_ethics, citing Fraser, Robins & O'Leary 2011): "Confucian normativity is defined by living one's family roles to maximum effect." [source: WP-Role_ethics]
- Key texts: Roger T. Ames, *Confucian Role Ethics: A Vocabulary* (University of Hawai'i Press, 2011) [SEP-ChEth bibliography]; Ames, *Human Becomings: Theorizing Persons for Confucian Role Ethics* (SUNY, 2021) [SEP-ChEth]; Rosemont and Ames, *Confucian Role Ethics: A Moral Vision for the 21st Century?* (V&R Unipress / National Taiwan University Press, 2016) [SEP-Conf]; Ames & Rosemont, "Were the Early Confucians Virtuous?", in Chris Fraser, Dan Robins and Timothy O'Leary (eds), *Ethics in Early China: An Anthology* (Hong Kong UP, 2011), pp. 17–39 [WS-11, Project MUSE TOC; WP-Role_ethics cites pp. 17–35].
- Arguments of "Were the Early Confucians Virtuous?" (second-hand): early Confucianism "best understood as a distinctive type of role ethics grounded in … family relations"; much of the argument points out differences between Aristotle and Confucius; claims Western ethics presupposes a false view of the individual self. Van Norden's reply (review of *Ethics in Early China*): advocates of virtue-ethics readings "do not hold the misguided belief that Aristotle and Confucius were the same." [source: WS-10, WS-11 (philarchive VANFCD, COKCBA summaries) — second-hand]
- May Sim, "Why Confucius' Ethics is a Virtue Ethics", in Besser-Jones and Slote (eds) 2015, pp. 63–76 — named in SEP-VE bibliography; WS summary of a Jubilee Centre version: Sim treats it as disputed, citing skeptics Nuyen 2009, Rosemont and Ames 2009, Ames 2011, and argues a looser definition (dispositions, character, motivation) fits. [sources: SEP-VE bibliography; WS-10 second-hand]
- Virtue-ethics readings of Mencius began with Lee H. Yearley, *Mencius and Aquinas: Theories of Virtue and Conceptions of Courage* (1990). Similarities to Aristotle: wisdom as flexible responsiveness, cultivation over action-guiding rules, genuine virtues vs counterfeits (7B37). Differences: Aristotle values theoretical contemplation, which Confucians "typically regard as lacking intrinsic value"; Confucians see family life as intrinsic to living well, Aristotelians "(arguably)" as a means. New Confucian and virtue-ethics interpreters are both "criticized for projecting an alien vocabulary onto the text" (Yu 2008; Angle and Slote 2013); Van Norden: "any interpreter must bring to the text her own vocabulary"; the real issue is the most plausible explanation. [source: SEP-Men §5]
- Bryan W. Van Norden, *Virtue Ethics and Consequentialism in Early Chinese Philosophy* (Cambridge UP, 2007). Van Norden (2004, 2007): shame plays a crucial role in Mencius's moral development with no comparable role in Aristotle. Van Norden (2017): Confucianism has "a decided edge" over modern Western philosophy in theories of moral development. [sources: SEP-ChEth bibliography; SEP-Comp §3] Van Norden teaches at Vassar (James Monroe Taylor Chair); 2017–2020 at Yale-NUS. [source: WP-Bryan_W._Van_Norden]
- Jiyuan Yu, *The Ethics of Confucius and Aristotle: Mirrors of Virtue* (Routledge, 2007): eudaimonia (Aristotle) and dao (Confucius) are parallel starting points, and both then turn to virtue (aretê / de). Yu (1964–2016), professor at SUNY Buffalo from 1997, died of cancer 3 Nov 2016 aged 52. [sources: SEP-Comp §3; SEP-VE bibliography; WP-Jiyuan_Yu]
- May Sim, *Remastering Morals with Aristotle and Confucius* (Cambridge UP, 2007); Sim (b. 1962, Singapore-born), professor at College of the Holy Cross. [sources: SEP-Men bibliography; WP-May_Sim]
- Stephen Angle and Michael Slote (eds), *Virtue Ethics and Confucianism* (Routledge, 2013). [source: SEP-VE and SEP-Men bibliographies]
- SEP-Comp: "Confucianism is a perfectionist virtue ethic if such an ethic is distinguished by its central focus on … character traits identified as the virtues; the good and worthwhile life; and particularist modes of ethical reasoning"; differences: "There is no parallel in Greek virtue ethics for the centrality of family life in the Confucian conception of the good life"; ritual adds an aesthetic dimension (Kupperman; Cua; Fingarette 1972). [source: SEP-Comp §3]
- SEP-VE future directions: theorists "have turned their attention eastward, exploring Confucian, Buddhist, and Hindu traditions (Yu 2007; Slingerland 2011; … Angle and Slote 2013; … Sim 2015)"; it also lists Keshav Singh 2021, "Vice and Virtue in Sikh Ethics" (Monist). [source: SEP-VE §4 and bibliography]

## 6. The modern revival: Anscombe
- G. E. M. (Elizabeth) Anscombe: born Limerick, Ireland, 18 March 1919; died Cambridge, 5 January 2001. [source: SEP-Ans §1]
- "Modern Moral Philosophy", *Philosophy* 33, no. 124 (January 1958): 1–19. [source: WP-Modern_Moral_Philosophy; SEP-VE bibliography]
- Opening statement of three theses (verbatim, quoted in WP): "The first is that it is not profitable for us at present to do moral philosophy; that should be laid aside at any rate until we have an adequate philosophy of psychology, in which we are conspicuously lacking. The second is that the concepts of obligation, and duty—moral obligation and moral duty, that is to say—and of what is morally right and wrong, and of the moral sense of 'ought', ought to be jettisoned if this is psychologically possible; because they are survivals, or derivatives from survivals, from an earlier conception of ethics which no longer generally survives, and are only harmful without it. My third thesis is that the differences between the well-known English writers on moral philosophy from Sidgwick to the present day are of little importance." [source: WP-Modern_Moral_Philosophy; same three theses paraphrased in SEP-Ans §2]
- Anscombe coined the term "consequentialism" in this paper. Her sense differed from today's: she classed Mill as a non-consequentialist and W. D. Ross as a consequentialist. [source: SEP-Ans §2; WP-Modern_Moral_Philosophy]
- Third thesis explained: every best-known English moral philosopher since Sidgwick thinks killing the innocent is sometimes right if the consequences of not doing so are catastrophic; she traces this to Sidgwick's view that one intends every foreseen consequence (so one is equally responsible for intended and foreseen effects). [source: SEP-Ans §2]
- Second thesis explained: the moral "ought" makes sense only in a law-based ethics with a legislator (e.g. God); without it, such talk is like discussing what is "criminal" in a society with no criminal law or courts. She thought such talk nonsense, not false (contrast Mackie). Better to classify actions as truthful/untruthful, just/unjust. "And here we see the superiority of the term 'unjust' over the terms 'morally right' and 'morally wrong'. For in the context of English moral philosophy since Sidgwick it appears legitimate to discuss whether it might be 'morally right' in some circumstances to adopt that procedure [judicially punishing a man for what he is clearly understood not to have done]; but it cannot be argued that the procedure would in any circumstances be just." (MMP, 16) [source: SEP-Ans §2]
- **Surprise:** Anscombe "herself did not advocate" developing virtue ethics as a full alternative theory; her first thesis says moral philosophy should wait for an adequate philosophy of psychology. [source: SEP-Ans §2] The article nonetheless "has influenced the emergence of contemporary virtue ethics … especially through the work of Alasdair MacIntyre"; Onora O'Neill: "many exponents of virtue ethics take Anscombe's essay as a founding text". [source: WP-Modern_Moral_Philosophy]
- SEP-VE: action guidance comes from "Anscombe's hint that a great deal of specific action guidance could be found in rules employing the virtue and vice terms ('v-rules')". [source: SEP-VE §3a]
- Michael Thompson: a theory of "natural normativity" was "sketched in the concluding paragraphs" of MMP and developed by Hursthouse (On Virtue Ethics) and Foot (Natural Goodness). [source: WP-Modern_Moral_Philosophy]
- Critics: Roger Crisp (2004) doubts her main thesis about moral concepts; Thomas Pink criticises the claim that moral obligation needs a divine lawgiver. [source: WP-Modern_Moral_Philosophy]
- **Anscombe's argument step by step** (from SEP-Ans §2 and the opening theses):
  1. The concepts of moral obligation, moral duty and the moral "ought" have their point only within a law conception of ethics, i.e. with a legislator who commands.
  2. Modern secular moral philosophy no longer believes in such a legislator.
  3. A concept used outside the framework that gives it sense becomes empty (the "criminal" without criminal law).
  4. So these concepts should be dropped if psychologically possible, and actions described with thick concepts (unjust, untruthful, cowardly).
  5. But to do ethics with such concepts properly we need an adequate philosophy of psychology (action, intention, wanting, virtue, human flourishing), which we lack.
  - Objection: obligation need not presuppose a divine lawgiver (Pink); Crisp: applying her own strategy supports something nearer the consequentialism she attacked. Reply on her behalf: the shift to thick virtue concepts survives even if the historical genealogy is doubted; and Foot and Hursthouse supply a naturalistic (non-theistic) source of normativity in human nature. [sources: WP-Modern_Moral_Philosophy; SEP-VE §3f]
- IEP-VE adds Bernard Williams (morality vs the wider "ethics"; moral luck) as a third figure of the revival alongside Anscombe and MacIntyre. [source: IEP-VE §1b]

## 7. MacIntyre, After Virtue
- Alasdair Chalmers MacIntyre: born Glasgow, 12 January 1929; died 21 May 2025 at a care facility in South Bend, Indiana, aged 96. (IEP-Mac still lists him as living.) [source: WP-Alasdair_MacIntyre; IEP-Mac]
- *After Virtue: A Study in Moral Theory*: first published 1981 (University of Notre Dame Press per WP; Duckworth, London is the UK publisher of the 2nd ed. per SEP-VE bibliography); 2nd ed. 1984 adds a postscript replying to critics; 3rd ed. 2007 adds the prologue "After Virtue After a Quarter of a Century". Called "one of the most influential works of moral philosophy of the late 20th century". [sources: WP-After_Virtue; IEP-Mac]
- Opening allegory: a world where the sciences were destroyed and reassembled from fragments; "The hypothesis which I wish to advance … is that in the actual world which we inhabit the language of morality is in the same state of grave disorder as the language of natural science in the imaginary world which I described." (AV p. 2, 2nd ed.) [source: WP-After_Virtue]
- Diagnosis: a "culture of emotivism" — moral language used to manipulate; emotivism a false theory of meaning but a "cogent theory of use"; the Enlightenment project failed because it discarded teleology; Polynesian taboo analogy (Kamehameha II abolished taboos without resistance); ends with the choice "Nietzsche or Aristotle"; and "we are waiting not for a Godot, but for another—doubtless very different—St. Benedict." [sources: IEP-Mac §AV; WP-After_Virtue]
- Practices: "a complex social activity" with "goods internal to the practice", whose standards of excellence are "systematically extended" (AV p. 187); examples chess, medicine, architecture, football, politics; external goods = wealth, fame, prestige, power. Parable of the child paid in candy to play chess: while he plays only for candy he has reason to cheat; once he values the goods internal to chess, cheating defeats his own purpose. Institutions (clubs, hospitals, universities) sustain practices but tend to pursue external goods. [source: IEP-Mac §AV]
- Three-level definition of virtue: (1) "A virtue is an acquired human quality the possession and exercise of which tends to enable us to achieve those goods which are internal to practices and the lack of which effectively prevents us from achieving any such goods" (AV p. 191); (2) dispositions that also "sustain us in the relevant kind of quest for the good" across a whole life (AV p. 219) — the narrative unity of a life; (3) virtues sustain "those traditions which provide both practices and individual lives with their necessary historical context" (AV p. 223). [source: IEP-Mac §AV]
- "The unity of a human life is the unity of a narrative quest" — AV ch. 15, confirmed via WS summary of a Project MUSE excerpt (page not confirmed). [source: WS-8 second-hand]
- "I am born with a past, and to cut myself off from that past in the individualist mode, is to deform my present relationships" (AV p. 221). [source: IEP-Mac]
- MacIntyre dismissed Aristotle's "metaphysical biology" (AV pp. 162, 179); became a Thomist after AV (from 1984); denied belonging to either virtue ethics or communitarianism as schools. Sequels: *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?* (1988), *Three Rival Versions of Moral Enquiry* (1990), *Dependent Rational Animals* (1999). [source: IEP-Mac]
- MacIntyre and relativism: SEP-VE cites MacIntyre 1985 for the observation that "different cultures embody different virtues", source of the cultural-relativity objection. [source: SEP-VE §3c]

## 8. Foot and Hursthouse
- Philippa Foot (née Bosanquet): born 3 October 1920; died on her ninetieth birthday, 3 October 2010. Known also for introducing the Trolley Problem (1967). [source: SEP-Foot §1]
- *Natural Goodness* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 2001). Central concept: natural normativity, building on her student Michael Thompson: to identify something as an organism is already to judge it against its life form; e.g. a blue tit lacking a blue patch is not defective since this does not hinder its life (NG 30). Humans are evaluated the same way, but rationality brings a "sea change": "[W]hile [non-human] animals go for the good (thing) that they see, human beings go for what they see as good" (NG 56). Vices are defects in responsiveness to reasons — defective wills. "Virtue makes humans good in the same way that blight-free, lush green flesh in a certain shape makes a cactus good" (Hacker-Wright's summary). [source: SEP-Foot §NG]
- Foot's final essay "Rationality and Goodness" (2004): the Sudeten farm boy who chose death rather than serve in the SS: "We did not sign up for the SS, and so they condemned us to death. Both of us would rather die than stain our consciences with such deeds of horror." Foot argues his choice was rational. [source: SEP-Foot]
- Rosalind Hursthouse: born Bristol, 10 November 1943; grew up in Wellington, New Zealand; student of Anscombe and Foot; Open University 1975–c.2000; Professor at Auckland from 2002; FRSNZ 2016. *On Virtue Ethics* (Oxford UP, 1999): Crisp called it "the comprehensive statement modern virtue ethics has been awaiting for forty years". [source: WP-Rosalind_Hursthouse; SEP-VE bibliography]
- **Hursthouse's criterion of right action** (*On Virtue Ethics* 1999, p. 28): "An action is right iff it is what a virtuous agent would characteristically (i.e. acting in character) do in the circumstances." Wording confirmed only by secondary sources found via WebSearch (O'Leary, *Paideusis* 2006; Waikato paper; Cambridge Handbook of Virtue Ethics calls it "the qualified-agent account"). "Characteristically" excludes cases where a virtuous agent is acting out of character (exhausted, drugged — Kawall). Not stated in the current SEP-VE text. [source: WS-2, WS-12 second-hand]
- **v-rules**: "Do what is honest/charitable; do not do what is dishonest/uncharitable" (Hursthouse 1999). Our vocabulary of vice terms is "remarkably, and usefully, long" (irresponsible, feckless, lazy, inconsiderate … vindictive, calculating, ungrateful …). [source: SEP-VE §3a]
- Hursthouse's naturalism: virtues are traits that benefit their possessor and make her a good human being; humans are evaluated "qua specimens of their natural kind"; characteristically human = rational. [source: IEP-VE §3a; WP-Rosalind_Hursthouse] McDowell, Foot, MacIntyre and Hursthouse all outline "a third way" between grounding ethics in a scientific account of human nature and mere rationalization; humans are social animals "like elephants and wolves and unlike polar bears". [source: SEP-VE §3f]

## 9. Objections and replies
**(a) Action-guidance.** Objection: virtue ethics only says "Identify a moral exemplar and do what he would do" — as if a student choosing between music and engineering should ask "What would Socrates study…?" Replies: v-rules (Hursthouse); anti-codifiability — no code could be both a decision procedure and usable by the non-virtuous (McDowell 1979); bioethics in the 1960s–70s showed utilitarians and deontologists agreeing on rules yet disagreeing on cases, so they too need phronesis. [source: SEP-VE §3a] IEP reply: the exemplar and moral education; "Virtue ethics cannot give us an easy, instant answer. This is because these answers do not exist." [source: IEP-VE §4b]
**(b) Adequacy of right action** (virtue neither necessary nor sufficient for right action); replies: drop "right action" (Brewer 2009, following Anscombe and MacIntyre) or define only some senses of it. [source: SEP-VE §3b]
**(c) Cultural relativity.** Different cultures embody different virtues (MacIntyre 1985). Replies: tu quoque — rules and conceptions of welfare vary as much or more (Solomon 1988); bolder: the virtues themselves are not culture-relative, only local understandings of them (Nussbaum 1993, "Non-Relative Virtues"). [source: SEP-VE §3c]
**(d) Conflict** (charity vs justice in euthanasia; honesty vs kindness) — shared with deontology; the practically wise often see the conflict is only apparent. [source: SEP-VE §3d]
**(e) Self-effacement** (Stocker 1976; Keller 2007). [source: SEP-VE §3e]
**(f) Self-centredness / egoism.** IEP: "There seems to be something wrong with aiming to behave compassionately, kindly, and honestly merely because this will make oneself happier." Replies: the virtues are other-regarding; eudaimonism unifies morality and self-interest (IEP); the virtuous person acts for the reasons each virtue supplies (someone's suffering averted), not to get eudaimonia; virtue may require giving one's life (Foot 2001, 95); "self-regarding" vs "other-regarding" virtues is a false contrast for social animals (SEP). [sources: IEP-VE §4a; SEP-VE §3g]
**(g) Moral luck**: virtue depends on upbringing and friends; reply: virtue ethics embraces "the fragility of the good life". [source: IEP-VE §4c]

**(h) The situationist challenge.**
- Background: "person-situation debate" in psychology in the late 1960s–70s (Mischel); "By the 1980s, though, many psychologists parted ways with the situationist movement, and today very few would subscribe to the claims that were advanced at that time." [source: SEP-MCE §1]
- Philosophers: Gilbert Harman, "Moral Philosophy meets Social Psychology: Virtue Ethics and the Fundamental Attribution Error", *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 99 (1999): 315–331; John M. Doris, "Persons, Situations, and Virtue Ethics", *Noûs* 32 (1998): 504–530, and *Lack of Character: Personality and Moral Behavior* (Cambridge UP, 2002). [source: SEP-MCE bibliography] (SEP-VE's bibliography gives Harman as vol. "119: 316–31" — an error; vol. 99 is correct per SEP-MCE.)
- Doris's "global traits": consistency (across varied trait-relevant situations) and stability (over repeated similar situations) (Doris 2002: 22). [source: SEP-MCE §1.1]
- Quotations: Harman: "it may even be the case that there is no such thing as character" (Harman 1999: 328); Doris: "people typically lack character" (Doris 1998: 506; 2002: 2); Merritt, Doris and Harman: "Behavior is not typically ordered by robust traits" (2010: 358). Doris admitted there could be "a few virtuous and a few vicious people" (2002: 60, 65). [source: SEP-MCE §1.1]
- **Situationist argument step by step** (Miller's reconstruction, SEP-MCE §1.1.1, citing Miller 2014: 192; plus stage two):
  1. If most people possessed the traditional virtues and vices as global traits, appropriate psychology experiments would show most people behaving consistently virtuously (or viciously) across trait-relevant situations.
  2. The experiments do not show this: trivial situational factors (finding a dime, being in a hurry, a passive bystander, an authority's insistence) swing behaviour drastically.
  3. So most people do not possess the traditional virtues or vices (conclusion: "we are justified in believing … that most people do not possess the traditional virtues or vices such as compassion").
  4. Aristotelian virtue ethics "presupposes that there are character traits of the relevant sort" (Harman 1999: 319) and is "subject to damaging empirical criticism" when construed as descriptive psychology (Doris 1998: 520).
  5. So Aristotelian virtue ethics is in trouble — or (Doris 2002, ch. 6) practically irrelevant for most people.
  - Replies to stage one: the experiments do not test virtue (Miller 2003 on the dime study; Sreenivasan 2002; Annas; Adams 2006; Snow 2010); "competing virtues" (Milgram subjects' compassion outweighed by obedience/trust); mental states such as fear of embarrassment explain the results (Sabini and Silver 2005, *Ethics*); CAPS model (Snow, Russell). Naturalistic counter-evidence: Philpot et al. 2019 analysed 219 CCTV recordings of public aggression in the Netherlands, South Africa and the UK: "at least one bystander intervened in 90.9% of the situations" (mean 16.29 bystanders present). [source: SEP-MCE §1.2.1]
  - Replies to stage two: **rarity** — the "most widely used" reply: virtue ethics never claimed most people are virtuous; Aristotle thought virtue rare: "the many naturally obey fear, not shame; they avoid what is base because of the penalties, not because it is disgraceful" (NE 1179b11–16, as quoted in SEP-MCE); wrong conception of character — Kamtekar 2004: situationism attacks traits that "have very little to do with character as it is conceived of in traditional virtue ethics", which includes practical wisdom; or non-Aristotelian accounts (Merritt Humean; Wielenberg Kantian; Slingerland Confucian). Positive pictures: local traits (Doris), "mixed traits" neither virtues nor vices (Miller 2013, 2014). [source: SEP-MCE §1.2.2–1.2.3]
  - SEP-VE: the challenge "has left traditional virtue ethicists unmoved" but produced "healthy engagement" with psychology; reply that virtues are multi-track dispositions, and ascribing charity on the basis of mere conventional decency would itself be "a fundamental attribution error" (Sreenivasan 2002; Kamtekar 2004). [source: SEP-VE §3h]
- **The studies (with caveats):**
  - Isen & Levin 1972 ("Effect of Feeling Good on Helping: Cookies and Kindness", *JPSP* 21: 384–388): those who found a dime in a phone booth's coin return helped pick up dropped papers 88% of the time vs 4% of controls. SEP-MCE: "There were replication problems with this study, but there are many other studies on the effect of mood on helping which found a similar pattern." WS: Weyant and Clark 1976 ("Dimes and Helping: The Other Side of the Coin", *PSPB*) found no effect with a lost-letter measure; listed as a failed replication in the FORRT FLoRA Replication Atlas (linked to Levin & Isen 1975). [sources: SEP-MCE §1.1.1; WS-5 second-hand]
  - Darley & Batson 1973, "'From Jerusalem to Jericho': A Study of Situational and Dispositional Variables in Helping Behavior", *JPSP* 27(1): 100–108: Princeton Theological Seminary students sent to another building to give a talk (some on the Good Samaritan parable) passed a man slumped in apparent need; about 63% in the low-hurry condition helped vs 10% in the high-hurry condition (45% intermediate per one WS source); helping was not predicted by religious-personality measures; "being late for an appointment is the most influential factor … even if the appointment is to attend a lecture on the Good Samaritan" (SEP-ChEth wording, slightly loose — the students were to *give* a talk). Sample: 67 seminarians per Stanford SPARQ summary (one critique says 47 retained) — not confirmed from the paper. [sources: WP-Situationism_(psychology); SEP-ChEth §2.7; SEP-MCE; WS-3, WS-9 second-hand]
  - Latané & Rodin 1969 "Lady in Distress": 70% helped when alone, 7% when with an unresponsive stranger. [source: SEP-MCE §1.1.1]
  - Milgram (Yale; began August 1961; first report 1963, *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology* 67: 371–378; book *Obedience to Authority*, 1974). SEP-MCE (experiment five, Milgram 1974: 60): 80% went to at least 270 volts, 65% to the 450-volt maximum; when two authorities contradicted each other at 150 volts, participants stopped. WP (first version): every participant reached 300 volts, 65% the full 450. Caveats: Gina Perry (*Behind the Shock Machine*, 2012) argues Milgram's published accounts mismatched what happened and debriefing was inadequate; critics reply there were "well over a score" of replications in many countries; partial replication by Jerry Burger (2006, broadcast). [sources: SEP-MCE; WP-Milgram_experiment]
  - Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment: "Serious concerns have arisen", "with calls to remove it from psychology textbooks (Blum 2018)" — do not use as evidence. [source: SEP-MCE §1.2.1]
- **Confucian resources against situationism** (Wong, SEP-ChEth §2.7): Confucians appreciate the relational/situational nature of life; they describe "a long and arduous program of ethical training" (classics memorized until internalized; ritual giving "regularly recurring ways" to strengthen attitudes); exemplars and Haidt's "elevation"; Sarkissian 2010: one can oneself be a positive situational influence; Slingerland (2011, *Ethics* 121, "The Situationist Critique and Early Confucian Virtue Ethics") on the "high bar"; and Mencius 1A7: people cannot train in virtue without material security, so widespread lack of virtue is a social and political condition the experiments ignore (Hutton 2006). [source: SEP-ChEth §2.7]

## 10. Care ethics
- Origins: Milton Mayeroff's *On Caring* was an early work, but care ethics as a distinct theory is "most often attributed to" psychologist Carol Gilligan and philosopher Nel Noddings; Held identifies Sara Ruddick's 1980 article "Maternal Thinking" as the first articulation (book 1989). Influential later contributors: Annette Baier, Virginia Held, Eva Feder Kittay, Sara Ruddick, Joan Tronto. [source: IEP-Care intro, §1]
- Carol Gilligan (b. 28 November 1936), *In a Different Voice: Psychological Theory and Women's Development* (Harvard UP, 1982). [source: WP-Carol_Gilligan; SEP-Fem §2.2]
- **Kohlberg debate.** Kohlberg's six-stage theory (begun as a Chicago graduate student, 1958) holds that moral reasoning develops toward universal principles; he used dilemmas such as the Heinz dilemma. Kohlberg was Gilligan's mentor at Harvard; when girls were included in his studies they "scored significantly lower than boys". Gilligan argued the model was gender-biased and that Kohlberg "wrongly prioritizes a 'morality of rights' and independence from others as better than, rather than merely different from, a 'morality of responsibility'". Jake vs Amy on the Heinz dilemma: Jake treats it as "a math problem with people" (right to life beats property); Amy looks for ways to talk to the druggist, worrying Heinz in prison would leave his wife worse off. Gilligan characterised the difference "as one of theme … rather than of gender"; in Gilligan's studies women were as likely to express the justice perspective as the care perspective; she later called care "a distinctively 'human voice'" against a "patriarchal voice" (2023). [sources: IEP-Care §1a; SEP-Fem §2.2; WP-Lawrence_Kohlberg's_stages]
- Empirical criticism: narrow samples (elite college students, women considering abortion); "Reviews by Walker (2006) and Jaffee and Hyde (2001) found that Gilligan's theory was not supported by empirical studies"; neo-Kohlbergian Defining Issues Test studies find females score slightly higher, "generally not significantly so". [sources: IEP-Care §3b; WP-In_a_Different_Voice]
- Nel Noddings (19 January 1929 – 25 August 2022), *Caring: A Feminine Approach to Ethics and Moral Education* (1984): "one-caring" and "cared-for"; caring as "engrossment"; ethical action rooted in natural caring and the memory of being cared for; rejects universal principles; caring obligations strongest to proximate others capable of response, weakening in concentric circles — controversial (parochialism), later revised in a later book (IEP gives the title as *Starting From Home*; exact title not verified). Later she called it explicitly feminist (Noddings 2013). [sources: WP-Nel_Noddings; IEP-Care §1b; SEP-Fem §2.2]
- Virginia Held (28 October 1929 – 26 May 2026), *Feminist Morality* (1993) and *The Ethics of Care: Personal, Political, and Global* (Oxford UP, 2006): care "the most basic moral value"; care as "clusters" of practices and values; care is "more a characterization of a social relation than the description of an individual disposition"; with Kittay prefers to treat care as practice and value rather than virtue to avoid "losing sight of it as work" (Held 2006, 35). [sources: WP-Virginia_Held; IEP-Care §§1c, 2]
- Eva Feder Kittay (Stony Brook; mother of a multiply disabled daughter), *Love's Labor: Essays on Women, Equality, and Dependency* (Routledge, 1999): "dependency workers"; caregivers as "derivatively dependent"; a third principle for Rawls: "To each according to his or her need, from each to his or her capacity for care, and such support from social institutions as to make available resources and opportunities to those providing care" (p. 113); a public "Doula" principle (paid carers for carers; IEP's wording). Also *Learning from My Daughter* (2020). [sources: IEP-Care §1c; SEP-Fem §2.2; WP-Eva_Kittay]
- Joan Tronto & Bernice Fisher definition: care is "a species of activity that includes everything we do to maintain, contain, and repair our 'world' so that we can live in it as well as possible"; Tronto's four elements: attentiveness, responsibility, competence, responsiveness (1994, 126–136); "privileged irresponsibility". [source: IEP-Care §§1c, 2]
- **Relation to virtue ethics (debate).** IEP-VE lists care ethics as one of three branches of virtue ethics. IEP-Care: the most predominant comparison is with virtue ethics, "to the extent that care ethics is sometimes categorized as a form of virtue ethics, with care being a central virtue", but this "is not universally endorsed". Pro-assimilation: Rachels 1999, Slote (1998; *The Ethics of Care and Empathy* 2007, care as empathy), McLaren 2001 (virtue theory supplies a standard — the mean — and a framework — flourishing), Halwani 2003. Against: Held 2006, Sander-Staudt 2006 — assimilation dilutes care ethics' focus on power, relationship and dependency, and care is work. [sources: IEP-Care §§2, 3c, 5; IEP-VE §3c]
- Criticisms of care ethics: "slave morality" (Puka, Card 1990, Davion); empirically flawed; theoretically indistinct; parochial (favouritism); essentialist (Spelman); ambiguous. [source: IEP-Care §3]
- **Confucian–care comparison (debate).** Chenyang Li (1994): important similarities between ren and the care ethics of Gilligan and Noddings; both contextual rather than top-down; Li acknowledges Confucianism's patriarchal form. Li-Hsiang Lisa Rosenlee (2007, 2014) defends filiality (xiao) as root of caring and asks why women should cease caring rather than insist men share it. Kupperman (2000): Confucian roles and rituals would need reform to support women's capabilities. [source: SEP-ChEth §2.10] Similarities (Li 1994, 2000; Julia Lai Tao 2000; Rosemont 1997): relationship fundamental, no general principles, parent-child relation paramount, graded responses, emotions such as empathy. Objections: Ranjoo Seodu Herr (2003) — Confucian li vs feminist resistance to subjugation; Lijun Yuan; Daniel Star (2002, *Hypatia*) — Confucian ethics is a virtue ethics bound to tradition and role-based hierarchy, while care ethics rejects hierarchical role categories for contextual responses. [source: IEP-Care §5] SEP-Fem also notes affinities with Africana ethics (Harding 1987; Metz 2013) and Confucianism (Metz and Miller 2016). [source: SEP-Fem §2.2]

## Debates
- Is Confucianism a virtue ethics? Yes, in a qualified sense (Van Norden 2007; Yu 2007; Sim 2007/2015; Yearley 1990; Wong) vs. "role ethics" (Ames 2011, 2021; Rosemont & Ames 2011, 2016; Neville 2016; Nuyen 2009). [SEP-ChEth §2.1; SEP-Conf §5; SEP-Men §5; WS-10/11]
- Whether projecting virtue-ethics (or Kantian New Confucian) vocabulary onto Mencius distorts him (Yu 2008; Angle & Slote 2013; Van Norden's reply). [SEP-Men §5]
- Mencius vs Xunzi: real disagreement or different definitions of "nature" (Graham 1967; Van Norden). [SEP-Men §5]
- Did Anscombe found virtue ethics? She inspired it but did not herself advocate it (Wiland, SEP). [SEP-Ans §2]
- Can virtue ethics define right action (Hursthouse's qualified-agent criterion) or should it drop "right action" (Brewer 2009)? [SEP-VE §3b; WS-2]
- Situationism: Harman/Doris vs. Kamtekar, Annas, Snow, Russell, Miller, Sabini & Silver; replication worries about the classic studies. [SEP-MCE; SEP-VE §3h]
- Is care ethics a form of virtue ethics (Slote, McLaren, Halwani, Rachels) or distinct (Held, Kittay, Sander-Staudt)? [IEP-Care §5]
- Gilligan's empirical claims about gender (Walker; Jaffee & Hyde) and the essentialism charge; Gilligan's own "theme, not gender" clarification. [IEP-Care §3; WP-In_a_Different_Voice; SEP-Fem]
- Are Confucian and care ethics compatible (Li 1994; Rosenlee) or not (Herr 2003; Star 2002; Yuan)? [IEP-Care §5; SEP-ChEth §2.10]
- Aristotle vs Stoics: is virtue sufficient for happiness? [SEP-VE §2.1; SEP-Stoa §4.3]
- Doctrine of the mean: illuminating or empty for non-quantitative choices (Kraut's juror reply). [SEP-ArEth §5.1]
