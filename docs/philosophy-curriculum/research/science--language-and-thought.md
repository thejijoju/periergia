## 1. Language as action: the bridge from the later Wittgenstein

- The IEP's history of philosophy of language: the *Investigations* (1953) presented language as "a diverse, mercurial collection of 'language games'—goal-directed social activities for which words were just so many tools to get things done"; "This turn in Wittgenstein's philosophy ushered in a new concern for the 'pragmatic' dimensions of language usage." [source: IEP-PL §1b]
- IEP-PL: "Wittgenstein's later work sparked interest in the pragmatic dimensions of language use among some British philosophers working not long after his death, but a number grew exasperated with the more deflationary and 'ordinary language' approaches of Wittgenstein's acolytes, who saw almost no role for theoretical accounts... Some opted instead to pursue what has come to be known as speech act theory, led initially by the work of John Austin." Its example: "It is sunny outside" could be "a report, an admonition not to take an umbrella, a lie..., practicing English, a taunt". [source: IEP-PL §4c]
- Wittgenstein's own example (PI §27): "Water!" can be an exclamation, an order, a request, or an answer to a question. [source: WP-Language_game_(philosophy), citing PI §27]
- Influence on Austin is plausible but debated: Longworth lists C. I. Lewis and Wittgenstein as "plausible" further influences (citing Harris and Unnsteinsson 2018 on Wittgenstein). Longworth also doubts that "ordinary language philosophers" (Austin, Ryle, Strawson, Wittgenstein) form a real group: "it is far from clear what in addition to that the label is supposed to entail." [source: SEP-Aus §1, §2.1]
- Austin's method in one line: "Ordinary language is not the last word: in principle it can everywhere be supplemented and improved upon and superseded. Only remember, it is the first word." ("A Plea for Excuses", 1956, in *Philosophical Papers* 1961, p. 185). [source: IEP-Aus §1]

## 2. Austin: How to Do Things with Words

- John Langshaw Austin was born 26 March 1911 in Lancaster, England; trained as a classicist at Balliol College, Oxford; White's Professor of Moral Philosophy at Oxford from 1952 until his death in 1960. [source: IEP-Aus §1; SEP-Aus §1]
- War service: British Intelligence Corps; "he more than anybody was responsible for the life-saving accuracy of the D-Day intelligence" (reported in Warnock 1963); left the army in September 1945 as a lieutenant colonel; OBE, Croix de Guerre, US Legion of Merit. He also translated Frege's *Grundlagen* (1950) and invented a card game, CASE (1951). [source: SEP-Aus §1]
- Austin gave the William James Lectures at Harvard in 1955; *How to Do Things with Words* was published posthumously in 1962 (Clarendon Press / Harvard UP), edited from his notes by J. O. Urmson; 2nd edition, ed. Urmson and Marina Sbisà, 1975. Twelve lectures. Austin said the views were in place by 1939, first used in "Other Minds" (1946), and developed in Oxford lectures called "Words and Deeds" in the early 1950s. [source: WP-How_to_Do_Things_with_Words; SEP-Aus §1]
- Opening examples (1962: 5): "I take ... to be my lawfully wedded ..." (in a marriage ceremony); "I name this ship the Queen Elizabeth" (smashing a bottle against the stern); "I give and bequeath my watch to my brother" (in a will); "I bet you sixpence it will rain tomorrow". [source: SEP-Aus §4]
- Austin's warning footnote, two pages before his famous claim: "Everything said in these sections is provisional, and subject to revision in the light of later sections" (1962: 4 fn. 1). Longworth notes the famous claim ("I assert this as obvious") is itself made in the apparently performative form "I assert", which "appears... to falsify the generalisation that performatives lack truth-values". [source: SEP-Aus §4]
- **Felicity conditions** (IEP's numbering of Austin's rules). Misfires (act "null and void"): A.1 there must exist an accepted conventional procedure having a certain conventional effect, including the uttering of certain words by certain persons in certain circumstances; A.2 it must be invoked in adequate circumstances by appropriate persons; B.1 executed correctly; B.2 and completely. Abuses (act achieved but hollow): C.1 the procedure must be executed with appropriate thoughts, feelings or intentions; C.2 participants must subsequently conduct themselves accordingly. [source: IEP-Aus §3a]
- Austin's misfire examples: uttering the formula incorrectly, being "married already", or "it is the purser and not the captain who is conducting the ceremony" (1962: 15–16); an abuse example: an insincere marriage of convenience. [source: SEP-Aus §4]
- **Collapse of the distinction**: (a) constatives can be unhappy too: "France is hexagonal" can misfire if there is no France, and an assertion one does not believe is an abuse (1962: 47–52); (b) performatives answer to facts: "I warn you that the bull is about to charge" is mistaken if the bull is not about to charge (1962: 55); a verdict can be fair or unfair, advice good or bad. And "I state that..." meets all the formal marks of a performative yet is "surely the making of statements, and surely ... essentially true or false" (1962: 91). Conclusion: "to say something is always to do something". [source: SEP-Aus §4; IEP-Aus §3a]
- Interpretive debate: Austin "is sometimes read as seeking to defend" the performative/constative distinction; Longworth calls it "possible, but implausible" that Austin simply failed to draw a distinction he wanted, and "more plausible" that his purpose was to show, through the failed attempts, "that there is no such simple distinction". [source: SEP-Aus §4]
- **The three acts** (1962: 94–101): locutionary (producing an utterance with phonetic, grammatical and lexical features up to sentence meaning — the phatic act — and with content — the rhetic act); illocutionary (classified by force: stating, warning, promising); perlocutionary (classified by "consequential effects upon the feelings, thoughts, or actions of the audience"). Example: promising and later stating that I'll be home for dinner = same locutionary act, different illocutionary acts; warning that the ice is thin (illocution) may persuade someone to avoid it or encourage them to take a risk (perlocutions). Austin wanted "essentially to fasten on the second, illocutionary act" (1962: 103). The SEP judges the triad "probably amounts to Austin's central contribution to more recent work". [source: SEP-Aus §4]
- A test for the difference (SEP-SA): one can urge just by saying "I hereby urge you to shut the door", but "there are no circumstances in which I can persuade you just by saying, 'I hereby persuade you to shut the door.'" [source: SEP-SA §3.4]
- Austin on truth: "'true' and 'false', like 'free' and 'unfree', do not stand for anything simple at all; but only for a general dimension of being a right and proper thing to say as opposed to a wrong thing, in these circumstances, to this audience, for these purposes and with these intentions" (1962: 144–145). He spoke of "the true/false fetish" and "the value/fact fetish". [source: SEP-Aus §4]
- **Austin's five classes** (last lecture, tentative): verdictives (acquitting, reckoning, assessing, diagnosing), exercitives (appointing, voting, ordering, warning — exercising powers, rights or influence), commissives (promising, undertaking, betting), behabitives (apologising, congratulating, thanking — attitudes and social behaviour), expositives (affirming, denying, stating, describing — clarifying reasons and arguments). [source: IEP-Aus §3a; SEP-SA §3]
- Serious vs non-serious uses: Austin treats non-serious uses (e.g. mock assertions on stage or in fiction) as derivative (1962: 104); Derrida challenged this priority; Searle responded (Derrida 1977; Searle 1977). [source: SEP-Aus §4 intro]

## 3. Searle: systematising speech acts

- John Rogers Searle (31 July 1932, Denver – 17 September 2025); Rhodes Scholar at Oxford (all his degrees there); taught at UC Berkeley from 1959. In June 2019 his emeritus status was revoked after he was found to have engaged in sexual harassment and retaliation against a former student. [source: WP-John_Searle]
- *Speech Acts* (1969) analyses promising as the prototypical illocutionary act and offers semantic rules for "illocutionary force indicating devices". Searle treats speaking as rule-governed and distinguishes **constitutive rules** (which create an activity, as the rules of chess do) from merely regulative ones; IEP calls his view a "conventionalism of the means". [source: WP-John_Searle; IEP-Aus §4a]
- Searle (1975) criticised Austin's taxonomy as "unduly lexicographic" (it studies English verbs, though there could be acts no language names, or two verbs naming one act) and as lacking clear principles ("describe" appears as both verdictive and expositive; behabitives are heterogeneous). [source: SEP-SA §3]
- Searle's taxonomy: "A Taxonomy of Illocutionary Acts" (1975, in K. Gunderson ed., *Language, Mind, and Knowledge*, Minnesota Studies vol. 7, pp. 344–369; reprinted in *Expression and Meaning*, 1979). Five classes: assertives (also "representatives"), directives, commissives, expressives, declarations. Main dimensions: illocutionary point, direction of fit, expressed psychological state. [source: WebSearch results incl. Britannica and course summaries; SEP-SA cites "1975, p. 344" for the F(p) analysis] (secondary-source verification only for the bibliographic details)
- **Direction of fit** (example from Anscombe 1963): a husband's shopping list and a detective's list of what he buys may be identical; the husband's list has world-to-word fit (the cart must match the list), the detective's word-to-world (the list must match the cart). Assertions are word-to-world; commands world-to-word; thanking or "Hooray for Arsenal!" seem to have neither (Searle and Vanderveken 1985 call it "null"). [source: SEP-SA §3.1]
- Searle and Vanderveken (1985) define a force by seven components: illocutionary point; its degree of strength; mode of achievement (testifying = asserting in one's capacity as a witness); content conditions (I can only promise what is future and under my control); preparatory conditions (I cannot bequeath what I do not own); sincerity conditions (assertion expresses belief, apology regret, promise intention); degree of strength of sincerity. [source: SEP-SA §3.3]
- **Indirect speech acts** (Searle 1975, "Indirect speech acts", in Cole & Morgan eds., *Syntax and Semantics 3*): "you are standing on my foot" as a demand to move; "Can you pass the salt?" as a request; with "please" appended it cannot be a request for information. Searle proposed the force is worked out by Gricean reasoning. [source: SEP-SA §3.4; WP-Speech_act]
- Intentionalist rival: Kent Bach and Robert Harnish hold the link between sentence and illocution is "never semantic, or conventional, but rather inferential" (Speech Act Schema). Marina Sbisà's rival conventionalism: illocutions produce normative states (obligations, commitments, entitlements) that are defeasible. [source: IEP-Aus §4a]
- Relevance theorists object that the illocutionary level identified by speech-act theory "does not effectively play a role in the process of linguistic comprehension". [source: IEP-Aus §4a]

W. Moore 2000). [source: SEP-Aus §4 intro]

## 5. Grice: meaning and implicature

- Herbert Paul Grice, born 13 March 1913 in Birmingham, died 28 August 1988 in Berkeley; Corpus Christi College, Oxford; nearly five years in the Royal Navy; taught at Oxford until 1967, then UC Berkeley (retired officially 1979, taught on). His William James Lectures at Harvard in early 1967 "circulated widely in unauthorized manuscript form" before appearing in *Studies in the Way of Words* (1989, posthumous). "Logic and Conversation" was first printed in 1975 (in D. Davidson and G. Harman eds., *The Logic of Grammar*, pp. 64–75). He "played cricket, chess and piano, each at a very high level". [source: SEP-Gr §1, bibliography]
- **"Meaning" (1957)**: natural meaning ("Those clouds mean rain", "Those spots mean measles") vs non-natural meaning, now usually called speaker meaning ("In saying 'You make a better door than a window', George meant that you should move"). [source: SEP-SA §5.1]
- Grice's analysis, via SEP-Gr's example: a driver flashes her lights at night intending (1) that you believe your lights are not on, (2) that you recognise intention (1), and (3) that this recognition be part of your reason for believing it. Herod presenting Salome with the head of John the Baptist did *not* mean that John was dead, because her reason to believe it was seeing the head, not recognising Herod's intention. [source: SEP-Gr §4.1.1]
- **Cooperative Principle**: "Make your conversational contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged" (Grice 1989: 26). [source: SEP-Gr §3]
- **Maxims** (Grice says, "Echoing Kant, I call these categories Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner"): Quantity — (1) make your contribution as informative as is required, (2) not more informative than is required; Quality — supermaxim "Try to make your contribution one that is true": do not say what you believe false; do not say that for which you lack adequate evidence; Relation — "Be relevant"; Manner — "Be perspicuous": avoid obscurity, avoid ambiguity, be brief, be orderly. Grice's analogies: "if at a particular stage I need four screws, I expect you to hand me four, rather than two or six"; "If I need sugar... I do not expect you to hand me salt". [source: SEP-Gr §3; WP-Cooperative_principle quoting Grice 1989]
- Flouting vs violating: implicatures can arise by flouting a maxim — saying something so patently in breach of it that the hearer infers a different meaning; irony arises from flouting Quality (Grice 1975: 34). SEP-Imp's irony example: a truck driver in a blizzard, asked about the weather, says "The weather's lovely". [source: SEP-Imp §1, §7]
- **Conversational vs conventional implicature**: "The queen is English and therefore brave" implicates, via the meaning of "therefore", that bravery follows from being English; "The queen is English and brave" would not. Other triggers listed: but, even, too, still, yet, already, again, stop, start, know, regret. Conventional implicatures are determined by sentence meaning; conversational ones depend on context (Barb's "I have to work" implicates she is not going to Paul's party only because of Alan's question). [source: SEP-Imp §2, §1]
- **Cancellability**: "Some athletes smoke" conversationally implicates "not all do", but this can be cancelled ("...in fact, all do") without contradiction; logical entailments cannot. Scalar ("some → not all") implicatures are Quantity implicatures. [source: SEP-Imp §3; SEP-Gr §3; WP-Implicature]
- **Calculability**: conversational implicatures "must be capable of being worked out". Grice's general pattern (1975: 31): "He has said that q; there is no reason to suppose that he is not observing the maxims, or at least the Cooperative Principle; he could not be doing this unless he thought that p; he knows (and knows that I know that he knows) that I can see that the supposition that he thinks that p is required; he has done nothing to stop me thinking that p; he intends me to think, or is at least willing to allow me to think, that p; and so he has implicated that p." [source: SEP-Imp §7]
- **Grice's Razor**: other things equal, posit conversational implicatures rather than extra senses — "Senses are not to be multiplied beyond necessity" (alluding to Ockham). Often used to argue that exclusive "or" is an implicature, not a second meaning. [source: SEP-Imp §7]
- Grice's generalised vs particularised implicature: particularised ones arise "on a particular occasion in virtue of special features about the context" (the reference letter is one); generalised ones are normally carried by a form of words (Grice 1989: 37). [source: WP-Implicature]
- Implicature and lying: if Barb knew she was going to the party, she might be guilty of misleading but "not of lying or making a false statement"; though "In court, witnesses are typically required to answer questions directly. They cannot avoid perjury by implicating a falsehood". [source: SEP-Imp §1]
- Cross-cultural objection: Elinor Ochs Keenan (1976) argued Malagasy speakers routinely give less information than required (to avoid committing themselves and because information confers prestige); Harnish (1976) replied that Grice's maxims claim to hold only where the Cooperative Principle is in force. [source: WP-Cooperative_principle] (Keenan's first names not in the WP text; WP gives "Keenan (1976)" — writer should say "the linguist Elinor Ochs Keenan" only if checked; otherwise "Keenan")

### Argument: Grice's derivation of the testimonial-letter implicature (USE THIS, step by step)
- The case (Grice 1975: 33): "A is writing a testimonial about a pupil who is a candidate for a philosophy job, and his letter reads as follows: 'Dear Sir, Mr. X's command of English is excellent, and his attendance at tutorials has been regular. Yours, etc.'" [source: SEP-Imp §4; WP-Implicature]
- Grice's own gloss (via WP-Implicature quoting Grice 1989): "A cannot be opting out, since if he wished to be uncooperative, why write at all? He cannot be unable, through ignorance, to say more, since the man is his pupil; moreover, he knows that more information than this is wanted. He must, therefore, be wishing to impart information that he is reluctant to write down. This supposition is tenable only if he thinks Mr. X is no good at philosophy. This, then, is what he is implicating." Grice classes it as flouting the first maxim of Quantity. [source: WP-Implicature; WP-Cooperative_principle]
- Reconstruction for the writer: (1) A said only that X's English is excellent and attendance regular. (2) A is presumed to be observing the Cooperative Principle (he chose to write). (3) A knows more (X is his pupil) and knows more is wanted, so on its face (1) breaches Quantity-1. (4) The breach is explicable, consistent with (2), only if A thinks something he is unwilling to write. (5) The only plausible such thing is that X is poor at philosophy. (6) A knows the reader can work out (3)–(5) and does nothing to block it. (7) So A implicates that X is no good. Cancellability check: adding "and he is the best philosopher I have taught" removes the implicature without contradiction.
- Objection (Wayne Davis, SEP-Imp's author): such implicatures are better explained as conventional "modes of speech" — "damning with faint praise" is one of only two such modes with a common name — learned by precedent, not calculated afresh; Davis treats Calculability as an empirical hypothesis "that may turn out to be false". Reply (Gricean): the mode is itself explained by, and could be re-derived from, the maxims; many theorists "take Calculability to be true by definition". [source: SEP-Imp §4, §7]
- Second objection (relevance theory): Gricean maxims do not explain why someone would say the opposite of what they mean in irony; reply (Grice 1978) that ironists also pretend to assert what they say. [source: SEP-Imp §12]

### Relevance theory
- Dan Sperber and Deirdre Wilson, *Relevance: Communication and Cognition* (Harvard UP / Blackwell, 1986; 2nd ed. 1995). "all Grice's maxims can be replaced by a single principle of relevance" (Wilson & Sperber 1986: 381). Relevance in their technical sense rises with "positive cognitive effects" and falls with processing effort. Doctor example: "You have the flu" is more relevant than "You are ill" (more effects, same effort) and than "You have the flu or 29 is the square root of 843" (same effects, more effort). Communication is "ostensive-inferential". [source: SEP-Imp §12; WP-Relevance_theory]
- Davis's criticisms: maximal relevance neither implies nor subsumes Grice's maxims (it does not predict Barb's implicature); critics say many more informative propositions seem as easy to process. [source: SEP-Imp §12]

## 6. Speech acts and politics: silencing, subordination, hate speech, generics, slurs (treat evenhandedly)

- Catharine MacKinnon's thesis: pornography does not merely cause harm but *constitutes* women's subordination and silencing, violating their civil rights to equal status and free speech (MacKinnon 1984, 1987, 1992). Quotation: pornography "sexualizes rape, battery, sexual harassment, prostitution and child sexual abuse; it thereby celebrates, promotes, authorizes and legitimizes them" (MacKinnon 1987: 171–172). [source: SEP-Porn §4]
- MacKinnon and Andrea Dworkin drafted an anti-pornography ordinance for Minneapolis (1983); Indianapolis passed one in 1984 defining pornography as "the graphic sexually explicit subordination of women"; it sought civil remedies, not criminal bans. It was struck down in *American Booksellers Ass'n v. Hudnut*, 771 F.2d 323 (7th Cir. 1985), affirmed without opinion (aff'd mem.) by the US Supreme Court, 475 U.S. 1001 (1986), on First Amendment grounds. [source: WP-American_Booksellers_Ass'n_v._Hudnut; SEP-Porn §3] (Note: SEP-Porn loosely says the ordinance was "overturned on appeal by the U.S. Supreme Court"; the precise route is 7th Circuit, then summary affirmance.)
- Rae Langton, "Speech Acts and Unspeakable Acts", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 22(4): 293–330 (1993), defended MacKinnon's claims using Austin; developed with Jennifer Hornsby (Hornsby 1993, 1995; Hornsby & Langton 1998). [source: SEP-Porn bibliography; IEP-Aus §4b]
- Langton's three kinds of silencing: **locutionary** (preventing someone uttering the words at all, by violence, institutional norms or intimidation); **perlocutionary frustration** (the act is performed but fails to have its intended effect); **illocutionary disablement** (the communicative environment precludes uptake, so the act is not performed). Central example: where a woman's "No" is not recognised as a refusal, she is prevented from performing the illocutionary act of refusing. [source: IEP-Aus §4b; SEP-Porn §4]
- Langton born 14 February 1961 in Ludhiana, Punjab, India (to Australian lay-missionary parents); PhD Princeton 1995 (thesis *Kantian Humility*); in 2017 became Knightbridge Professor of Philosophy at Cambridge, the first woman to hold that chair; *Sexual Solipsism: Philosophical Essays on Pornography and Objectification* (OUP 2009). [source: WP-Rae_Langton]
- If pornography silences in this way, "there may be some reason to be skeptical that the solution preferred by many liberals... of countering the harms of pornography with more speech... will be effective, for pornography may make the relevant speech acts 'unspeakable' for women." [source: SEP-Porn §4]
- **Objection (Ronald Dworkin 1993)**: a right to free speech cannot include a right to "a sympathetic or even competent understanding of what one says"; that would license regulation "on a massive scale" and "tyranny". "Creationists, flat-earthers, and bigots, for example, are ridiculed..." but their free-speech rights are not violated. **Replies**: Hornsby and Langton (1998), West (2003): to be valuable, freedom of speech requires that there be no *systematic* barriers to audience uptake. **Further critics**: Jacobson (2001), Green (1998), Bird (2002), Anthony, De Gaynesford (2010), Bauer (2015). [source: SEP-Porn §4]
- Hate speech: drawing on Austin, philosophers distinguish **constitutive** harms (done *in* saying — illocutionary) from **consequential** harms (effects — perlocutionary) (Maitra and McGowan 2012). Mary Kate McGowan (2009, 2012) argues some hate speech enacts discriminatory norms via Austin's "exercitives", like a "Whites Only" sign, and could be restricted on that basis. Objection: Kukla (2014) and others find the illocution/perlocution line untenable, since illocutions need uptake; Gelber and McNamara (2016) say victims experience the harms cumulatively, so a neat split misrepresents them. Jeremy Waldron (2014) grounds restriction in dignity. Judith Butler also uses speech-act theory ("what hate speech does... is to constitute the subject in a subordinate position"). [source: SEP-Hate §2–§4]
- Counter-speech alternative: "supported counterspeech" — the state empowers targets to answer rather than banning speech. [source: SEP-Hate §5.3]
- **Slurs**: typically "conventionalized ways of demeaning and derogating individuals or groups" contrasted with a neutral counterpart (Jeshion 2013; Camp 2013): "Crackers make up about 80% of Congress" vs "White people make up about 80% of Congress". Lauren Ashwell (2016) argues gendered slurs ("bitch", "slut") lack neutral counterparts, which troubles both semantic and pragmatic accounts. Reclamation by targeted groups is noted. [source: SEP-Hate §3.1]
- **Generics**: "Ducks lay eggs" is true though only females lay; "Mosquitoes carry the West Nile virus" is true though under one per cent do, while "Books are paperbacks" is false though over eighty per cent are. Generics are neither universal nor "most" claims. [source: SEP-Gen §2]
- Sally Haslanger (2011): even if "women are submissive" is true given current social circumstances, uttering the generic suggests the property lies "in the very nature of women", rather than in "changeable social circumstances", and so can perpetuate oppression. Empirical work: hearing generics about a novel social group leads preschoolers and adults to essentialise it (Gelman, Ware and Kleinberg 2010; Rhodes, Leslie and Tworek 2012). [source: SEP-Gen §5.1]

## 7. Language and thought: from Humboldt to Sapir and Whorf

- Counter-Enlightenment roots: Hamann (1730–1788), Herder (1744–1803) and Wilhelm von Humboldt (1767–1835) "emphasized the diversity of languages and customs and their role in shaping human thought"; Hamann called language the "instrument and criterion of reason". [source: SEP-Rel §4]
- Humboldt (1820): "The diversity of languages is not a diversity of signs and sounds but a diversity of views of the world." (as quoted via Trabant). WP adds that Humboldt ranked inflectional languages such as German and English as "the most perfect" — a reminder that early linguistic relativity was entangled with hierarchies of peoples. [source: WP-Linguistic_relativity] (WP-sourced; treat the ranking claim cautiously)
- Franz Boas → Edward Sapir → Benjamin Lee Whorf: "Benjamin Whorf, inspired by his teacher Edward Sapir, who in turn was supervised by the social anthropologist Franz Boas, used ethnographic evidence from American Indian languages, such as Hopi, to argue that languages mold our views of the world". [source: SEP-Rel §4]
- Whorf (24 April 1897 – 26 July 1941, Hartford, Connecticut) graduated in chemical engineering from MIT (1918) and was a fire-prevention engineer for the Hartford Fire Insurance Company; he took Sapir's course at Yale but never attempted a degree; died of cancer at 44. [source: WP-Benjamin_Lee_Whorf]
- Whorf's "empty drums" anecdote: workers smoked near gasoline drums labelled "empty", which were full of explosive vapour and more dangerous than full ones; he argued habitual speech ("empty" = inert) shaped behaviour. [source: WP-Benjamin_Lee_Whorf]
- Whorf never stated a "hypothesis"; he spoke of a "principle of linguistic relativity". The term "Sapir–Whorf hypothesis" was coined by Harry Hoijer at a 1954 conference; Brown and Lenneberg (1953–54) turned it into a testable causal claim. [source: WP-Benjamin_Lee_Whorf]
- Strong vs weak versions: neo-Whorfians argue Whorf's famous passage is about the terms in which we *speak* ("thinking for speaking", Slobin), not the terms in which we can think; he held systems could be "calibrated". [source: WP-Benjamin_Lee_Whorf]
- Philosophical critics: Max Black (1959) called Whorf's metaphysics "amateurish crudity"; Donald Davidson (1973) attacked the idea of incommensurable conceptual schemes. John Leavitt notes Davidson attributes to Whorf the claim that English and Hopi time "cannot 'be calibrated'", but "calibrate" does not appear in the essay Davidson cites, and where Whorf uses the word he says the two *can* be calibrated. [source: WP-Benjamin_Lee_Whorf]

### Hopi time
- Whorf's 1936 paper "An American Indian Model of the Universe" (published posthumously in Carroll ed., 1956): "the Hopi language is seen to contain no words, grammatical forms, construction or expressions that refer directly to what we call 'time', or to past, present or future". He argued Hopi does not count durations like objects: not "three days" but something like "on the third day". [source: WP-Hopi_time_controversy]
- Ekkehart Malotki, *Hopi Time: A Linguistic Analysis of the Temporal Concepts in the Hopi Language* (1983): about 600 pages, after four years' research on Third Mesa; hundreds of examples of temporal words and constructions; Hopi has tense (future vs non-future), units such as days, weeks, months and seasons, and words for yesterday and tomorrow. [source: SEP-Rel §4; WP-Hopi_time_controversy]
- Debate: universalists (Pinker 1994; McWhorter) treat Malotki as final proof; relativists (John Lucy, Leanne Hinton, Penny Lee 1991, David Dinwoodie 2006) argue Malotki attacked a claim Whorf did not make — that Hopi *cannot express* time — rather than his claim that Hopi grammar structures time differently. Bernard Comrie's 1984 review suggested many of Malotki's "tense" examples mark modality. Cliff Goddard (2003): much confusion stems from "a lack of agreement about what exactly constitutes a 'fundamentally' different conceptualisation of time". [source: WP-Hopi_time_controversy; WP-Benjamin_Lee_Whorf]

### "Eskimo words for snow"
- Origin: Franz Boas, *Handbook of American Indian Languages* (1911), introduction, pp. 25–26, gave four words: *aput* (snow on the ground), *qana* (falling snow), *piqsirpoq* (drifting snow), *qimuqsuq* (a snowdrift) — as a point about how languages divide ideas (compare English lake/river/brook), not a count. Whorf (1940) said that for an "Eskimo" a single all-inclusive word for snow "would be almost unthinkable". [source: WP-Eskimo_words_for_snow]
- Inflation: by 1978 the figure quoted had reached fifty; an unsigned *New York Times* editorial of 9 February 1984 gave one hundred. Laura Martin, "Eskimo Words for Snow" (*American Anthropologist* 88(2), 1986) traced the claim; Geoffrey K. Pullum called it "the Great Eskimo Vocabulary Hoax" (book, 1991). [source: WP-Eskimo_words_for_snow]
- Linguistic point: Inuit and Yupik languages are polysynthetic, so a root can yield indefinitely many words; three snow roots are reconstructed for Proto-Eskimoan (*qaniɣ* falling snow, *aniɣu* fallen snow, *apun* snow on the ground). Counter-view: Igor Krupnik (2010) and others defend Boas and argue the languages do have many more roots for sea ice and frozen water; Sámi studies report 180+ snow-and-ice terms. [source: WP-Eskimo_words_for_snow]

### Colour, space, number: the experiments
- Brown and Lenneberg (1954) on Zuni colour terms found a slight effect; Berlin and Kay, *Basic Color Terms* (1969), found universal patterns (e.g. languages with three basic terms have black, white and red), widely taken to tell against relativity; Eleanor Rosch (1974) likewise. Lucy (1992) criticised Berlin and Kay for assuming colour terms encode only colour. [source: SEP-Rel §4; WP-Linguistic_relativity] (SEP misprints "Berlin and Key")
- **Russian blues**: Winawer, Witthoft, Frank, Wu, Wade and Boroditsky, "Russian blues reveal effects of language on color discrimination" (*PNAS*, 2007): Russian obligatorily distinguishes *goluboy* (light blue) and *siniy* (dark blue); Russian speakers discriminated blues across that boundary faster than within it, English speakers showed no such advantage, and the advantage disappeared under a verbal (but not a spatial) interference task. The authors read it as an online interaction of language and perception, not a permanent change; commentators note it does not settle causal direction. [source: WebSearch results (abstract via pure.york.ac.uk; Nature News via bioedonline.org)]
- **Absolute direction**: Guugu Yimithirr (Pama-Nyungan, Far North Queensland; most speakers at Hope Vale, near Cooktown) is "notable for its use of pure geographic directions (north, south, east, west) rather than egocentric directions (left, right...), though such 'purity' is disputed"; speakers are claimed to have a highly developed sense of direction (Haviland 1998). It gave English "kangaroo" (*gangurru*). As of June 2020 only about half of the roughly 1,400 Guugu Yimithirr people spoke it. [source: WP-Guugu_Yimithirr_language]
- Stephen Levinson "drawing on experimental evidence, has argued that the frame of reference that underlies any given language shapes our spatial experiences" (Gumperz & Levinson eds., *Rethinking Linguistic Relativity*, 1996, which "began a new period"); but SEP-Rel stresses neo-Whorfian claims "are much more modest than Whorf's original thesis". [source: SEP-Rel §4; WP-Linguistic_relativity]
- Debate on space: Peggy Li and Lila Gleitman, "Turning the tables" (*Cognition* 83, 2002, 265–294) argued spatial-reasoning differences reflect environment/culture and that everyone can use any frame; Levinson, Kita, Haun and Rasch replied in "Returning the tables: language affects spatial reasoning" (*Cognition* 84, 2002, 155–188), charging that Li and Gleitman confused absolute and intrinsic frames. [source: WebSearch results (MPI and Portsmouth repository listings; Majid 2002 commentary)]
- **Grammatical gender**: an unpublished 2003 study by Boroditsky et al. reported Spanish and German speakers describing objects in line with noun gender; "the experiment was criticised after the reported effects could not be replicated by independent trials." [source: WP-Linguistic_relativity]
- Boroditsky (born 1976 or 1977 in Belarus; English her fourth language; now at UC San Diego) argued for a weak relativity from English vs Mandarin conceptions of time (horizontal vs vertical metaphors), adding that speakers can be taught to think like speakers of other languages. [source: WP-Lera_Boroditsky]
- **Number and Pirahã**: Frank, Everett, Fedorenko and Gibson, "Number as a cognitive technology" (*Cognition* 108, 2008): Pirahã *hói* and *hoí* behaved like "few"/"more", not "one"/"two" (in a subtraction task one speaker used *hói* for six spools). Yet: "A total lack of exact quantity language did not prevent the Pirahã from accurately performing a task which relied on the exact numerical equivalence of large sets. This evidence argues against the strong Whorfian claim that language for number creates the concept of exact quantity"; number words instead help remember and compare quantities "across space, time, and changes in modality". [source: WP-Pirahã_language]
- **Chinese counterfactuals**: Alfred Bloom reported Chinese speakers had difficulty with counterfactual questions and linked it to how Chinese marks counterfactuality; "Other researchers attributed this result to Bloom's flawed translations." [source: WP-Linguistic_relativity] (WP does not name Terry Au; see NOT VERIFIED)
- Futureless languages: a 2013 study (Chen) reported speakers of languages without grammatical future marking save more and smoke less; a within-Chinese study found no effect of repeatedly using "will", suggesting culture may explain the correlation. [source: WP-Linguistic_relativity]

### Pinker's critique
- Steven Pinker, *The Language Instinct: How the Mind Creates Language* (1994) argues language is an innate capacity, not a cultural invention like writing; attacks "Whorf's radical position": "the more you examine Whorf's arguments, the less sense they make"; holds thought runs in "mentalese", following Fodor. Relativists (Casasanto 2008; Lucy 1992; Lakoff 1987) accuse Pinker of attacking a straw man. [source: WP-The_Language_Instinct; WP-Benjamin_Lee_Whorf; WP-Linguistic_relativity]

## 8. Chomsky: syntax, behaviourism and innateness

- Avram Noam Chomsky, born Philadelphia, 1928, to Jewish immigrant parents from Russia and Ukraine; University of Pennsylvania; four years in the Harvard Society of Fellows (attracted by Quine, whose behaviourism his work came to oppose). [source: IEP-Ch §1]
- *Syntactic Structures* (Mouton, The Hague, February 1957; vol. 4 of *Janua Linguarum*; about a hundred pages), based on his MIT lecture notes; introduced transformational generative grammar. Mouton took it after MIT's Technology Press had refused his *Logical Structure of Linguistic Theory*. [source: WP-Syntactic_Structures]
- "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" first appears in Chomsky's 1955 thesis *The Logical Structure of Linguistic Theory*, then "Three Models for the Description of Language" (1956) and *Syntactic Structures* (1957). Chomsky contrasted it with "*Furiously sleep ideas green colorless": neither had occurred in English discourse, so "in any statistical model... these sentences will be ruled out on identical grounds as equally 'remote' from English. Yet (1), though nonsensical, is grammatical, while (2) is not." [source: WP-Colorless_green_ideas_sleep_furiously]
- Reply (2000): Fernando Pereira fitted a simple statistical (Markov) model to newspaper text and found "Furiously sleep ideas green colorless" about 200,000 times less probable than "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" (*Phil. Trans. R. Soc.* 358, 2000). [source: WP-Colorless_green_ideas_sleep_furiously]
- B. F. Skinner, *Verbal Behavior* (1957); Chomsky, "A Review of B. F. Skinner's *Verbal Behavior*", *Language* 35(1): 26–58 (1959): children acquire language without the conditioning behaviourism requires, and reinforcement cannot explain how people produce and understand sentences never heard before. Newmeyer: the review "has come to be regarded as one of the foundational documents of the discipline of cognitive psychology". Reply: Kenneth MacCorquodale, "On Chomsky's Review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior*" (1970). [source: WP-Verbal_Behavior]
- Stimulus-freedom and creativity "goes back at least to Descartes"; Chomsky credits Descartes with the insight that language is productive. [source: IEP-Ch §2, §3e]
- *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965) introduced the competence/performance distinction; later sharpened into I-language (individual, internal, intensional) vs E-language. Principles and Parameters (1980s, *Lectures on Government and Binding*): "A language is not, then, a system of rules but a set of specifications for parameters in an invariant system of principles of Universal Grammar." Minimalist Program (mid-1990s): a single operation, Merge; recursion as the core of the narrow faculty of language (FLN). [source: IEP-Ch §3]
- Chomsky named the puzzle "Plato's Problem", after the slave-boy in the *Meno*; he also cites Leibniz and Descartes. [source: IEP-Ch §4b; WP-Poverty_of_the_stimulus] (sources disagree on date: IEP says *Aspects* 1965; WP says the first chapter of *Knowledge of Language*, 1986 — say "Chomsky later called this 'Plato's problem'" without a date)
- WP: Chomsky coined the phrase "poverty of the stimulus" in 1980 (in *Rules and Representations* era). SEP-Inn: the argument's fullest statements are in Chomsky 1968, 1980, 1986. [source: WP-Poverty_of_the_stimulus; SEP-Inn §2]

### Argument: the poverty of the stimulus, step by step (USE THIS)
- SEP-Inn's schema (Dupre 2026): (1) Children acquire the linguistic target T. (2) Children are exposed to primary linguistic data (PLD). (3) The child has some set of domain-general learning mechanisms LM. (4) T is not acquirable on the basis of PLD and LM. IC: So some factors F relevant to acquiring T must be innate. (5) F is language-specific. Conclusion: children possess language-specific factors in virtue of which they acquire their language. Responses can attack any premise: T misdescribed, PLD richer, LM more powerful, or F not language-specific. [source: SEP-Inn §2]
- Worked example (Chomsky 1975: 30–33): "The man is tall" → "Is the man tall?"; "The man who is tall is in the room" → "Is the man who is tall in the room?", never "*Is the man who tall is in the room?" Candidate rules: (i) raise any auxiliary, (ii) raise the first auxiliary, (iii) raise the main auxiliary. Only (iii) fits, and "main auxiliary" can only be defined structurally, not by linear order — the principle of **Structure Dependence**. Children do not try rule (ii), though simple data fit it. [source: SEP-Inn §2]
- IEP adds supporting considerations: children worldwide acquire language at roughly the same age regardless of circumstances; high-IQ children do not learn grammar faster; adult correction is rare and ineffective. Chomsky compares the language faculty to an organ that grows; even sympathisers call this "incautious", noting the critical period. [source: IEP-Ch §4b]
- **Objection 1 (Pullum and Scholz 2002)**: the stimulus is richer than claimed; in a child-directed corpus they found questions like "Where's the other dolly that was in here?", where the main auxiliary is raised over an embedded one. **Reply (Legate and Yang 2002)**: such sentences are about 0.05% of the corpus — rare enough that a child might never meet them, and a learner sensitive enough to generalise from them would also generalise from noise. **Reply (Lasnik and Lidz 2016)**: positive evidence that the main auxiliary *can* move does not show the embedded one *cannot*; Dupre notes this is a different POS argument from the original. [source: SEP-Inn §6.1]
- **Objection 2 (richer learning)**: statistical learning (Saffran, Aslin and Newport 1996) and Bayesian models (Perfors, Tenenbaum; Chater et al. 2015) show children can infer from the *absence* of forms that they are impossible. Constructionists such as Michael Tomasello reject UG. Fiona Cowie: the argument fails "on both empirical and conceptual grounds". [source: SEP-Inn §6.2–§7; WP-Poverty_of_the_stimulus]
- **Objection 3 (LLMs, as of 2026)**: LLMs have no language-specific innate biases yet behave like competent speakers, so are offered as a "possibility proof" that such knowledge can be extracted from data (Warstadt and Bowman 2022; Piantadosi, "Modern Language Models Refute Chomsky's Approach to Language", 2024). **Replies**: (a) data volume: children hear roughly 2–7 million words a year (Gilkerson et al. 2017), "tens or hundreds of millions" in total, whereas recent LLMs such as Meta's Llama train on "tens of trillions of tokens" — so they are "poor examples" against the POS; the BabyLM challenge (10 or 100 million tokens) gives results that are "still preliminary, and offer encouragement both to empiricists and to nativists"; (b) transformers may themselves encode hidden biases (though McCoy, Frank and Linzen 2020 found several networks lacked a hierarchical bias); (c) "impossible languages": Moro, Greco and Cappa (2023) argue LLMs learn humanly impossible languages as easily as possible ones, so cannot model human language; but Kallini, Papadimitriou, Futrell, Mahowald and Potts (2024, "Mission: Impossible Language Models") found LLMs do prefer human-like rules over, e.g., reversed strings. Dupre notes the tension: to refute nativism LLMs must lack language-specific biases, yet to vindicate them as models they must prefer human languages. Vong, Wang, Orhan and Lake (*Science* 383, 2024) trained a network on head-camera video and audio from one child over about 18 months and it learned word–object mappings comparably to CLIP on a test. [source: SEP-InnLLM A.1–A.4; SEP-Inn bibliography]
- Chomsky's own view of chatbots: guest essay "The False Promise of ChatGPT", *New York Times*, 8 March 2023, with Ian Roberts and Jeffrey Watumull: "The human mind is not, like ChatGPT and its ilk, a lumbering statistical engine for pattern matching"; the human mind "operates with small amounts of information" and seeks "not to infer brute correlations among data points but to create explanations"; such programs cannot separate possible from impossible languages. [source: WebSearch results (reprints/summaries; NYT original not reachable)] (quotation wording consistent across several reprints but not checked against the NYT page)

### The Everett–Pirahã dispute (evenhandedly)
- Pirahã: spoken by about 250–380 people along the Maici River, Amazonas, Brazil; the only surviving variety of Mura; community largely monolingual; very few linguists have field experience in it. [source: WP-Pirahã_language]
- Daniel Everett (born 26 July 1951) went to the Pirahã as an SIL missionary in 1977; later lost his faith. His paper "Cultural Constraints on Grammar and Cognition in Pirahã" (*Current Anthropology* 46(4): 621–646, 2005) claimed Pirahã lacks recursion (embedding), number words and colour terms, which he said falsifies Chomsky's UG; *Don't Sleep, There Are Snakes* (2008). [source: WP-Daniel_Everett; WP-Pirahã_language]
- Everett's claims: no recursive possessives ("John's brother's house" must be "John has a brother. This brother has a house."); hence a finite language. He reinterpreted his earlier analysis of *-sai* as embedding as parataxis. [source: WP-Pirahã_language]
- Critics point to Everett's own earlier publications analysing *-sai* as embedding; Sauerland (2010) reported a tonal distinction suggesting embedding; a nearly 100-page debate appeared in *Language* (June 2009). Chomsky called Everett a "charlatan" and said even if Pirahã had all the properties described it would have no implications for UG, which concerns a capacity that "may or may not manifest itself in any one particular language"; the Pirahã can learn Portuguese. Critics reply: "if a language without recursion would not be accepted as evidence against UG, it is difficult to imagine what can." [source: WP-Daniel_Everett; WP-Pirahã_language; IEP-Ch §4a]

## 9. Thought without (public) language: Fodor's language of thought

- The language of thought hypothesis (LOTH): thinking occurs in a mental language, "Mentalese", with words that combine into sentences whose meanings depend systematically on their parts. History: Augustine, Boethius, Aquinas, Scotus; William of Ockham gave "the first systematic treatment" in *Summa Logicae* (c. 1323); revived by Jerry Fodor, *The Language of Thought* (1975), who argued abductively: our best psychological theories posit Mentalese. [source: SEP-LOT intro]
- **Productivity**: "Mary gave the test tube to John's daughter's daughter's daughter..." — finite thinkers can entertain a potential infinity of thoughts; a finite base with recursive combination explains it (Fodor and Pylyshyn 1988). Worry: it rests on an idealised infinite competence. [source: SEP-LOT §4.2]
- **Systematicity**: anyone who can think that John loves Mary can think that Mary loves John; Mentalese explains this by recombinable constituents (Fodor 1987: 148–153). Unlike productivity it needs no infinitary idealisation. [source: SEP-LOT §4.3]
- Connectionist challenge (1980s; Rumelhart, McClelland and the PDP group 1986): neural networks without symbolic memory; classicists' famous reply is that networks cannot explain systematicity unless they implement a LOT. [source: SEP-LOT §5]
- Regress objection: if learning English means testing hypotheses framed in Mentalese, how is Mentalese learned? Fodor (1975: 65): it isn't — Mentalese is innate. Critics (P. S. Churchland 1986; Putnam 1988) found this "verging on the preposterous" (an innate concept CARBURETOR?); Fodor distinguished *acquiring* a concept (which needs experience) from *learning* it. [source: SEP-LOT §6.1]
- Relevance to Whorf: if thought is in Mentalese, natural language cannot determine thought — Pinker's position. [source: WP-Benjamin_Lee_Whorf]

## 10. Beyond Europe

### Bhartṛhari (India)
- Bhartṛhari (fl. c. 5th–6th century CE; often dated c. 450–510 or 485–540 CE), grammarian (Vyākaraṇa school) and philosopher of language; principal work *Vākyapadīya* ("Treatise on Sentences and Words"; also *Trikāṇḍī*, three books: Brahma-kāṇḍa, Vākya-kāṇḍa, Pada-kāṇḍa; about 635 verses). Modern scholars (K. A. Subramania Iyer, B. K. Matilal) usually distinguish him from the poet of the *Śatakatraya*. [source: WP-Bhartṛhari]
- His doctrine of *śabdādvaita* (linguistic non-dualism): ultimate reality (Brahman) is "of the nature of the word" (*śabda-tattva*) (VP 1.1). [source: WP-Bhartṛhari]
- On language and thought: "na so 'sti pratyayo loke yaḥ śabdānugamād ṛte" — rendered "There is no cognition in the world that is not permeated by the word" (WP) or, in a widely used translation, "There is no cognition in the world in which the word does not figure. All knowledge is, as it were, intertwined with the word." Usually numbered VP 1.123 (numbering varies between editions; some give 1.131). [source: WP-Bhartṛhari; WebSearch results] (a strong thesis: compare and contrast with Whorf — Bhartṛhari's claim is about all cognition being linguistically structured, not about differences between languages)
- *Sphoṭa*: the meaning-bearer that "bursts forth" (root *sphuṭ*) when sounds (*dhvani*) are heard in sequence; for Bhartṛhari the only real unit is the indivisible sentence (*akhaṇḍa-vākya-sphoṭa*); words and letters are analysts' abstractions; meaning is grasped in a flash (*pratibhā*), "analogous to seeing the whole image in a painting". Three levels of speech: *paśyantī* (undifferentiated, intuitive), *madhyamā* (internal, sequenced), *vaikharī* (uttered sound). [source: WP-Bhartṛhari]
- Earlier research notes (*Perception*) record the classical contrast: Dignāga/Dharmakīrti (genuine perception is non-conceptual) vs Nyāya/Mīmāṃsā (two stages) vs Bhartṛhari (all cognition linguistic). [source: docs/philosophy-curriculum/research/epistemology--perception.md]
- Conditions on sentence meaning (Mīmāṃsā, then adopted by other schools with modifications): *ākāṅkṣā* (mutual expectancy / syntactic need), *yogyatā* (semantic fitness: no contradiction among the related word-meanings), *sannidhi* or *āsatti* (proximity: words presented without interruption); some added *tātparya* (speaker's intention or the purport of the sentence), e.g. Nāgeśa (17th c.). Mīmāṃsakas read *ākāṅkṣā* psychologically, grammarians and logicians syntactically. [source: WebSearch results (wisdomlib.org studies; indianphilosophyblog.org 7 Feb 2018)] (secondary sources only; keep to one sentence; *tātparya* is a natural link to Grice's speaker meaning, but present that as the writer's comparison, not a source's)

### China
- Confucius, *Analects* 13.3: when names are not used properly, language does not flow, affairs fail, rites and music do not flourish, punishments miss the mark, and "the people will not know what to do with themselves... when the exemplary person puts a name to something, it can certainly be spoken, and when spoken it can certainly be acted upon" (tr. Ames and Rosemont 1998). *Zhengming*, traditionally "rectification of names", is increasingly rendered "ordering of names" (Hall and Ames). [source: SEP-ChLL §2]
- Xunzi, Book 22 "Correct Naming": the wise "instituted names to point out objects, in the first place in order to clarify noble and base, secondly to distinguish same and different... This is the purpose of having names" (tr. Graham 1989). [source: SEP-ChLL §2]
- Chad Hansen, *Language and Logic in Ancient China* (1983), argues early Chinese philosophers were not concerned with theories of truth but with "the pragmatic, behavior-guiding functions of language" — a view of language as action that the writer may set beside Austin (as a comparison). [source: SEP-ChLL §4]

## Debates
- **Did Austin abandon or never hold the performative/constative distinction?** Longworth (SEP): the book's structure suggests Austin set it up in order to show there is "no such simple distinction"; others read him as trying and failing to defend it. [source: SEP-Aus §4]
- **Convention vs intention in speech acts**: Austin, Searle (conventionalism of the means) and Sbisà (conventionalism of the effects) vs Strawson's Gricean turn and Bach & Harnish's inferential model; relevance theorists doubt the illocutionary level matters to comprehension. [source: IEP-Aus §4a]
- **Austin vs Derrida (and Searle)**: whether "non-serious" uses are parasitic (Austin, Searle) or an essential possibility of all signs (Derrida); real disagreement or misunderstanding? (cross-ref *Structuralism and After*). [source: SEP-Aus; WP-Limited_Inc]
- **Calculability of implicature**: Grice and most followers vs Davis (conventions of "modes of speech"); Gricean vs relevance theory. [source: SEP-Imp §7, §12]
- **Silencing and free speech**: MacKinnon, Langton, Hornsby, West, McGowan vs Dworkin, Jacobson, Green, Bird and others; hate-speech regulation (Waldron) vs counter-speech; whether the constitutive/consequential line can bear the weight (Kukla; Gelber & McNamara). [source: SEP-Porn; SEP-Hate]
- **How strong is linguistic relativity?** Universalists (Berlin & Kay, Chomsky, Pinker, Li & Gleitman) vs neo-Whorfians (Lucy, Levinson, Boroditsky); consensus at most for modest, domain-specific, often online effects; replication problems for some headline findings (gender). [source: SEP-Rel; WP-Linguistic_relativity]
- **Hopi time**: Malotki and universalists vs Lucy, Hinton, Lee, Dinwoodie. [source: WP-Hopi_time_controversy]
- **Snow words**: Martin and Pullum ("hoax") vs Krupnik and other Arctic specialists defending Boas. [source: WP-Eskimo_words_for_snow]
- **Poverty of the stimulus**: Chomsky, Crain, Lasnik & Lidz, Legate & Yang vs Pullum & Scholz, Cowie, Tomasello, Bayesian modellers; LLMs as possibility proofs (Piantadosi; Warstadt & Bowman) vs critics (Katzir 2023; Moro et al.; Chomsky 2023). [source: SEP-Inn; SEP-InnLLM]
- **Pirahã and recursion**: Everett vs Nevins, Pesetsky and Rodrigues and others (the 2009 *Language* exchange) and Chomsky; also whether a non-recursive language would even bear on UG. [source: WP-Daniel_Everett; WP-Pirahã_language; IEP-Ch §4a] (Nevins/Pesetsky/Rodrigues names from memory of the literature — see NOT VERIFIED; say "his critics in *Language* (2009)")
- **Language of thought**: Fodor vs connectionists; radical concept nativism vs Churchland and Putnam. [source: SEP-LOT]
