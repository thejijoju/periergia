## 1. Life and works
- Born 31 March 1596 in his maternal grandmother's house at La Haye, Touraine (town since renamed Descartes), 47 km south of Tours. Father Joachim, a lawyer, member of the Parlement of Brittany at Rennes. Mother Jeanne Brochard died when René was 13½ months old (SEP), i.e. in May 1597 after giving birth to a still-born child (WP). Raised by his grandmother with older brother Pierre and sister Jeanne. [source: SEP-D §1.1; WP-René_Descartes; IEP-D §1]
- He signed letters as "du Perron", after a small farm in Poitou inherited from his mother's family; introduced himself to Beeckman as "Poitevin". [source: SEP-D §1.1]
- A Roman Catholic, he entered the newly founded Jesuit College of La Flèche in 1606 or 1607 (IEP: Easter 1607; WP: 1607), leaving in 1614 or 1615 (IEP/WP: 1614). Curriculum: grammar school (Latin, Greek, classical poets, Cicero) then three years of Aristotelian philosophy (logic, morals, physics, metaphysics) plus mathematics. Galileo's discovery of Jupiter's moons (1610) was celebrated at La Flèche. As a sickly boy he was allowed to stay in bed until 10 a.m. (IEP). [source: SEP-D §1.1; IEP-D §1]
- *Discourse* (6:4): on leaving school "I found myself beset by so many doubts and errors that I came to think I had gained nothing from my attempts to become educated but increasing recognition of my ignorance." Yet in 1638 he wrote that "nowhere on earth is philosophy taught better than at La Flèche." [source: SEP-D §1.1]
- Law degree, Poitiers, 1616 (baccalaureate and licence in canon and civil law); never practised. [source: SEP-D §1.1; WP-René_Descartes; IEP-D §1]
- 1618: gentleman soldier/volunteer in the Protestant army of Maurice of Nassau at Breda; there met Isaac Beeckman, Dutch mathematician and natural philosopher (WP: principal of a Dordrecht school). They did "physico-mathematica" (mathematical physics) and shared a commitment to atoms; Descartes wrote the *Compendium of Music* for him (written 1618, published 1650). [source: SEP-D §1.2; WP-René_Descartes; IEP-D §1]
- 1619: joined the Catholic army of Maximilian I of Bavaria; attended the coronation of Emperor Ferdinand II; winter caught him at Ulm "or perhaps Neuburg" (SEP); WP (after Baillet) says Neuburg an der Donau, in a room heated by a stove. On the night of 10 November 1619 (WP: 10–11 Nov., St Martin's) he had **three dreams** that seemed to give him a mission: to reform all knowledge, beginning with philosophy. [source: SEP-D §1.2; WP-René_Descartes; IEP-D §1 says Ulm] WRITER: say "at Ulm or Neuburg in southern Germany"; the dreams are known through Baillet's 1691 biography (WP).
- Analytic geometry: "perhaps at this time, though certainly by 1628" he had the insight that lines can be described by equations relating lengths to coordinate lines; he did not require the lines to be perpendicular, did not foresee replacing geometrical construction with algebra, and "Cartesian coordinates" was a later name honouring the discovery. [source: SEP-D §1.2]
- Early 1620s poorly documented; Italy 1623–25; then Paris, in the circle of Marin Mersenne (1588–1648), his lifelong friend and correspondent; derived the sine law of refraction. Worked on the *Rules for the Direction of the Mind*, abandoned by the end of 1628 about half finished (published 1701). [source: SEP-D §1.2]
- Sine law: Descartes was the first to *publish* it (*Dioptrics* II, 1637), but it had been found before by Ibn Sahl (10th c.; SEP spells "Ibn Saul"), Thomas Harriot (c. 1600) and Willebrord Snell (c. 1620). [source: SEP-D §4] (Global point for the writer.)
- Moved to the Dutch Republic at the end of 1628 and stayed until 1649, changing address often; motto "who lives well hidden, lives well". Registered at Franeker, April 1629; later Leiden. WP lists some 14 addresses (Dordrecht, Franeker, Amsterdam, Leiden, Deventer, Utrecht, Egmond, Santpoort, Endegeest, Egmond-Binnen 1643–49). [source: SEP-D §1.2–1.3; WP-René_Descartes]
- 1629 "metaphysical turn": first nine months in the Netherlands on God and the soul, through which he claimed to have found "the foundation of physics"; wrote a little Latin metaphysical treatise, presumably an early version of the *Meditations*. [source: SEP-D §1.3]
- *The World* (*Le Monde*): conceived 1629 (letter to Mersenne 13/16 Nov. 1629 plans to explain "the whole of physics"); parts *Treatise on Light* and *Treatise on Man*; it advanced Copernicanism; he **suppressed it on learning of Galileo's condemnation (1633)**. Published posthumously 1664 (*Treatise on Man* first in an imperfect Latin translation, 1662). [source: SEP-D §1.3; SEP-Phys §1; SEP-Pin §2; WP-René_Descartes]
- He was offered a post in the medical faculty at Bologna in the early 1630s and declined. [source: SEP-D §1.3]
- Daughter Francine, born 1635 (WP: at Deventer, baptised Protestant), mother Helena Jans (van der Strom), his housekeeper/servant; Francine died in September 1640 (aged five; WP: scarlet fever, IEP: "a fever"); it "saddened him greatly" (SEP); in 1644 he contributed a dowry for Helena's marriage. [source: SEP-D §1.3; IEP-D §1; WP-René_Descartes]
- **1637**: *Discourse on the Method*, published anonymously in French at Leiden (Jan Maire), June 1637 (IEP), as a preface to three essays: *Dioptrics* (Optics), *Meteorology* and *Geometry*. It gave a first sketch of the metaphysics including mind–body dualism, but not the deep scepticism of the *Meditations*; he left the most radical sceptical arguments out because it was written in French for a wide audience, "including women". [source: SEP-D §1.3 and bibliography; IEP-D §1; SEP-Math intro]
- *Geometry* (*La Géométrie*, 1637) is "a short tract included with the anonymously published Discourse": algebraic techniques for geometrical problems, a "geometrical calculus". Fermat independently discovered coordinate methods but did not publish (WP); the pair of axes was introduced after van Schooten's Latin translation of 1649 (WP). [source: SEP-Math intro; WP-Cartesian_coordinate_system]
- The tree of philosophy (Preface to the French *Principles*, 1647): "Thus the whole of philosophy is like a tree. The roots are metaphysics, the trunk is physics, and the branches emerging from the trunk are all the other sciences, which may be reduced to three principal ones, namely medicine, mechanics and morals." (AT 9B:14; CSM I 186) [source: SEP-D §1.4; IEP-D §2b]
- **1641**: *Meditationes de prima philosophia*, Paris (Michel Soly), with Objections and Replies; Descartes and Mersenne had planned to show it to "the twenty or thirty most learned theologians". 2nd edition, Amsterdam (Elzevir) 1642, added the Seventh Objections (Bourdin) and the Letter to Father Dinet; its subtitle changed from promising to demonstrate "the immortality of the soul" to "the distinction of the human soul from the body". IEP: first edition had six sets of objections, the second added the seventh. French translation 1647 (duc de Luynes for the Meditations; Clerselier for the Objections and Replies). [source: SEP-D §1.4 and bibliography; IEP-D §1]
- The seven sets of objections, in order: (1) Johannes Caterus, Dutch priest/theologian; (2) theologians and philosophers gathered by Mersenne; (3) Thomas Hobbes; (4) Antoine Arnauld; (5) Pierre Gassendi; (6) a further miscellany gathered by Mersenne; (7) the Jesuit Pierre Bourdin. [source: WP-Meditations_on_First_Philosophy; SEP-D §1.4 names the same people]
- Descartes on Arnauld's set (letter to Mersenne): "I think they are the best of all the sets of objections" (CSMK III 175; AT III 331). Arnauld (1612–1694), then a young theologian at the Sorbonne, later of Port-Royal and the *Port-Royal Logic*. [source: IEP-Arn §1]
- Utrecht controversy: Henricus Regius (Henry le Roy, 1598–1679), professor of medicine at Utrecht, taught Descartes' system; the theologian Gisbertus Voetius (1589–1676), as rector, had the university senate condemn Descartes' (and Regius') philosophy in 1642; Descartes replied with the *Letter to Voetius* (1643), was summoned before the Utrecht council and threatened with expulsion and book-burning, and appealed to the Prince of Orange (IEP). Later he fell out with Regius (*Comments on a Certain Broadsheet*, 1648). [source: SEP-D §1.5; IEP-D §1]
- **1644**: *Principia philosophiae* (Amsterdam, Elzevir), dedicated to Princess Elisabeth; French translation (by Claude Picot) 1647. Four parts: metaphysics; general principles of physics (matter, laws of motion); astronomy; the earth. Planned parts on plants, animals and man were abandoned. He hoped it would be adopted in the schools, even Jesuit ones. [source: SEP-D §1.4; SEP-Eli §4.3; IEP-D §1; WP-René_Descartes]
- His works were placed on the Index of Prohibited Books in 1663. [source: SEP-D §1.4; WP-René_Descartes]
- **1649**: *The Passions of the Soul*, his last publication, written at Elisabeth's request (see §9). [source: SEP-Eli intro and §4.4; SEP-D §6]
- Queen Christina of Sweden: correspondence from late 1646 via the French diplomat Pierre Chanut (IEP); invitation 1649, accepted July 1649, arrived in Sweden September 1649 (IEP). At her request he composed statutes for a Swedish academy (SEP). From January 1650 he gave her philosophy lessons beginning at 5 a.m. (SEP-Works, citing Gaukroger: three days a week). Letter of 15 January 1650 (to Brégy): "men's thoughts are frozen here, like the water" (AT V 467; CSMK III 383). [source: IEP-D §1; SEP-D §1.5; SEP-Works §5]
- **Death**: fell ill in early February 1650 (SEP: on the day he presented the academy statutes); died **11 February 1650** in Stockholm, aged 53. Standard account: pneumonia/serious respiratory infection (IEP; SEP-Works; WP). [source: SEP-D §1.5; SEP-Works §5; IEP-D §1; WP-René_Descartes]
- Cause debated: WP notes "other causes have been proposed". The philosopher Theodor Ebert (Erlangen) argued that the French embassy chaplain (Viogué; first name given variously as François or Jacques) poisoned him with arsenic in a communion wafer, fearing he would hinder Christina's conversion; a minority, speculative hypothesis, received with caution; critics note he received communion from Viogué only twice and that the symptoms fit infection. [source: WP-René_Descartes; WS-5 (Ebert's book in French: *L'énigme de la mort de Descartes*, 2011)] WRITER: "usually said to have died of pneumonia; a poisoning theory exists but has little support."
- Burial and remains: buried in Stockholm (WP: among orphans, at what is now the Adolf Fredrik Church); remains moved to France 1666; reburied 1819 at Saint-Germain-des-Prés without his skull; a skull in the Musée de l'Homme "may be a forgery". His papers passed to Claude Clerselier, who edited his letters (1657–67). [source: WP-René_Descartes; SEP-D intro (*Letters* 1657–67)]

## 2. The project and the method of doubt
- Aim: to replace scholastic Aristotelian philosophy (all knowledge "from or through the senses"; substantial forms and real qualities) with a new metaphysics and a mechanistic physics; doubt was a tool to "withdraw the mind from the senses" (7:4, 12, 14) and reach innate ideas by pure intellect. Hatfield's reading: scepticism is a cognitive tool, and an answer to scepticism a side-effect; others (Popkin 1979, Curley 1978) think he took the sceptical threat seriously as such. [source: SEP-D §2, §3.1, §7]
- First Meditation opening (CSM): the need "to demolish everything completely and start again right from the foundations" (AT 7:17, CSM 2:12); "Reason now leads me to think that I should hold back my assent from opinions which are not completely certain and indubitable just as carefully as I do from those which are patently false. So, for the purpose of rejecting all my opinions, it will be enough if I find in each of them at least some reason for doubt." (AT 7:18, CSM 2:12) [source: SEP-Ep §2.2]
- Not doubt for its own sake: unlike "the sceptics, who doubt only for the sake of doubting," he aims "to reach certainty – to cast aside the loose earth and sand so as to come upon rock or clay" (*Discourse* 3, AT 6:29, CSM 1:125). [source: SEP-Ep §2.2]
- Apple-basket analogy (Seventh Replies, AT 7:481, CSM 2:324): tip all the apples out and put back only the sound ones. [source: SEP-Ep §2.2]
- Doubt is for theory, not life: "no sane person has ever seriously doubted" that there is a world and that humans have bodies (AT 7:16); the conduct of life is distinguished from "the contemplation of the truth" (Second Replies, AT 7:149, CSM 2:106); such doubt should be undertaken once in a lifetime (*semel in vita*; *Principles* I.1; 7:18; 3:695). [source: SEP-Ep §2.2; SEP-D §3.2; WP-Cogito (Principles opening line)]
- **Step 1, the senses**: the senses sometimes deceive (e.g. distant objects), and "it is not prudent to trust" what has deceived us even once; but this leaves beliefs like "I am here, sitting by the fire, wearing a winter dressing-gown, holding this piece of paper in my hands" (AT VII 18; CSM II 13). [source: IEP-D §3]
- **Step 2, dreaming** (CSM): "How often, asleep at night, am I convinced of just such familiar events – that I am here in my dressing-gown, sitting by the fire – when in fact I am lying undressed in bed! … As if I did not remember other occasions when I have been tricked by exactly similar thoughts while asleep!" (AT 7:19, CSM 2:13); "there are never any sure signs by means of which being awake can be distinguished from being asleep" (AT 7:19). Arithmetic and geometry seem to survive: "whether I am awake or asleep, two and three added together are five, and a square has no more than four sides" (AT 7:20, CSM 2:14). [source: SEP-Ep §3.1–3.2]
- Newman distinguishes a "Now Dreaming Doubt" (am I awake now?) from an "Always Dreaming Doubt" (perhaps waking experience is no more produced by external things than dreams are), the second "by no means the standard interpretation". Objection that dream arguments are self-refuting (we must distinguish dreams to compare them); Newman argues the Sixth Meditation formulation ("every sensory experience I have ever thought I was having while awake I can also think of myself as sometimes having while asleep", AT 7:77, CSM 2:53) avoids it. [source: SEP-Ep §3.1]
- **Step 3, the deceiving God / evil demon** (CSM): "firmly rooted in my mind is the long-standing opinion that there is an omnipotent God who made me the kind of creature that I am. How do I know that he has not brought it about that there is no earth, no sky, no extended thing, no shape, no size, no place … may I not similarly go wrong every time I add two and three or count the sides of a square…?" (AT 7:21, CSM 2:14); then: "I will suppose therefore that not God, who is supremely good and the source of truth, but rather some malicious demon of the utmost power and cunning has employed all his energies in order to deceive me." (AT 7:21, CSM 2:15) French *mauvais génie*; also called "evil genius" and *deus deceptor*. [source: SEP-Ep §3.2; WP-Evil_demon]
- One deceiver or two? Some (Gouhier 1937) see two (deceiving God and evil genius); Newman reads one; Kenny calls the two hypotheses epistemologically equivalent. Descartes adds that a less powerful origin makes deception *more* likely (AT 7:21; *Principles* I.5), so the doubt is really about whether our cognitive nature is flawed. [source: SEP-Ep §3.2; WP-Evil_demon]
- End of Meditation One: "there is not one of my former beliefs about which a doubt may not properly be raised" (AT 7:21). [source: SEP-Ep §3.2]
- Objections to the method: Gassendi (Fifth Objections, AT 7:257f, CSM 2:180) asked why not just a "simple and brief statement" that old beliefs are uncertain instead of imagining a deceiver; Descartes: "Is it really so easy to free ourselves from all the errors which we have soaked up since our infancy?" (AT 7:348). Peirce: "Let us not pretend to doubt in philosophy what we do not doubt in our hearts." Newman's reply: Descartes requires positive reasons for doubt and limits hyperbolic doubt to theory. [source: SEP-Ep §2.2]
- Possible source for the deceiver: Christia Mercer, "Descartes' debt to Teresa of Ávila, or why we should work on women in the history of philosophy" (*Philosophical Studies* 174, 2017), argues that the deceiver strategy follows the meditative steps of Teresa's *Interior Castle*; evidence is circumstantial (Descartes never names her; Mersenne admired her); disputed. [source: WS-6] WRITER: present as one scholar's proposal.

## 3. The cogito
- Second Meditation (CSM): "I have convinced myself that there is absolutely nothing in the world, no sky, no earth, no minds, no bodies. Does it now follow that I too do not exist? No: if I convinced myself of something then I certainly existed. But there is a deceiver of supreme power and cunning who is deliberately and constantly deceiving me. In that case I too undoubtedly exist, if he is deceiving me; and let him deceive me as much as he can, he will never bring it about that I am nothing so long as I think that I am something. So after considering everything very thoroughly, I must finally conclude that this proposition, I am, I exist, is necessarily true whenever it is put forward by me or conceived in my mind." (AT 7:25, CSM 2:16–17) Latin: "Ego sum, ego existo". [source: SEP-Ep §4.1; IEP-D §4a; WP-Cogito,_ergo_sum]
- The formula "cogito, ergo sum" does **not** appear in the *Meditations*. It first appears in French in the *Discourse* (1637), part 4: "je pense, donc je suis" (CSM renders it "I am thinking, therefore I exist"; WS-8), which he took "as the first principle of the philosophy I was seeking"; in Latin "ego cogito, ergo sum" in *Principles* I.7 (1644), as "the first and most certain of all to occur to anyone who philosophizes in an orderly way" (CSM 1:195); also in the Second Replies (AT 7:140). The posthumous *Search for Truth* has "dubito, ergo sum, vel, quod idem est, cogito, ergo sum". [source: WP-Cogito,_ergo_sum; SEP-Ep §4.1; SEP-D §3.1; WS-8]
- "Dubito, ergo cogito, ergo sum" is a later formula, from Antoine Léonard Thomas's 1765 éloge of Descartes. [source: WP-Cogito,_ergo_sum]
- Second Replies (CSM): "When someone says 'I am thinking, therefore I am, or I exist,' he does not deduce existence from thought by means of a syllogism, but recognizes it as something self-evident by a simple intuition of the mind." (AT 7:140, CSM 2:100) [source: SEP-Ep §4.1]
- Features (Newman): first-person ("Descartes thinks" is not certain for me); present tense ("it could be that were I totally to cease from thinking, I should totally cease to exist", AT 7:27); any mode of thinking works (doubting, willing, imagining, seeming to see), but not a bodily act ("I walk" fails; "it seems I'm walking" works; Fifth Replies, *Principles* I.9). [source: SEP-Ep §4.1]
- The cogito does not yet establish what the "I" is: "But I do not yet have a sufficient understanding of what this 'I' is, that now necessarily exists" (AT 7:25, CSM 2:17); and he explicitly leaves open whether the body he doubts might "in reality" be "identical with the 'I' of which I am aware" (AT 7:27, CSM 2:18). [source: SEP-Ep §4.1; SEP-D §3.4]
- "What am I?": "A thing that doubts, understands, affirms, denies, is willing, is unwilling, and also imagines and has sense perceptions" (AT VII 28; CSM II 19). [source: IEP-D §4b]
- Translation note: CSM often render *cogito* as "I am thinking" (progressive), since the certainty concerns present thinking. [source: WP-Cogito,_ergo_sum]

## 4. The wax and "mind better known than body"
- Second Meditation: a piece of wax fresh from the honeycomb: "It has not yet quite lost the taste of the honey; it retains some of the scent of the flowers from which it was gathered; its colour, shape and size are plain to see; it is hard, cold and can be handled without difficulty; if you rap it with your knuckle it makes a sound" (AT VII 30; CSM II 20). Near the fire: "the residual taste is eliminated, the smell goes away, the colour changes, the shape is lost, the size increases; it becomes liquid and hot" (ibid.). Yet it is the same wax. What persists is extension, grasped not by the senses or imagination but by "purely mental scrutiny" (AT 7:30f, CSM 2:20f). [source: IEP-D §4b; SEP-Ep §1.4]
- "If I look out of the window and see men crossing the square … Yet do I see any more than hats and coats which could conceal automatons? I judge that they are men." (AT 7:32, CSM 2:21) [source: SEP-Ep §9.1]
- Lessons: perception involves judgment; the mind is better known than body ("if I judge that the wax exists from the fact that I see it, clearly this same fact entails much more evidently that I myself also exist", AT 7:33, CSM 2:22); and it is an exercise in thinking without images. [source: IEP-D §4b; SEP-Ep §9.1]

## 5. The clear-and-distinct rule
- Third Meditation opening: "I now seem to be able to lay it down as a general rule that whatever I perceive very clearly and distinctly is true" (7:35). [source: SEP-D §3.2]
- Definitions (*Principles* I.45): clear = present and accessible to the attentive mind (like clear vision); distinct = clear and sharply separated from everything else. [source: IEP-MB §3]
- Gassendi (Fifth Objections, 7:318) asked how we can tell that a perception is clear and distinct, "since we are often deceived". Descartes' criterion: a perception is clear and distinct if, when we consider it, we cannot doubt it (7:145); "a great light in the intellect" is followed by "a great inclination of the will" (7:59). [source: SEP-D §3.2]
- Judgment involves intellect (perceives content) and will (affirms/denies); error arises when the will, which is in a sense unlimited, outruns the finite intellect (Fourth Meditation); restrict judgment to clear and distinct perceptions and error is impossible. [source: SEP-D §3.1, §3.5; IEP-D §6b]

## 6. The proofs of God
### 6a. Third Meditation causal ("trademark") argument, step by step (IEP-D §5a, SEP-D §3.2)
1. I have an idea of God, an infinite and supremely perfect substance.
2. Causal adequacy principle, "manifest by the natural light": "there must be at least as much reality in the efficient and total cause as in the effect of that cause" (AT VII 40; CSM II 28); something cannot come from nothing.
3. Applied to ideas: whatever is contained *objectively* in an idea (its representational content) must be contained *formally* (actually) or *eminently* (in a higher form) in its cause.
4. I am a finite substance; I could be the cause of ideas of finite things and of modes, but not of the objective reality of an infinite substance.
5. So the idea of God must be caused by something actually infinite: God exists.
- Supplementary, more cosmological version: what causes me, a finite thing having this idea? A regress of finite causes must end in God. [source: IEP-D §5a]
- Name: the idea of God is like "the mark of the craftsman stamped on his work" (WP renders it "the trademark, hallmark or stamp of their divine creator"). [source: WP-Trademark_argument] WRITER: use "trademark" as the scholars' label.
- God, being perfect, is no deceiver: "it is manifest by the natural light that all fraud and deception depend on some defect" (7:52). [source: SEP-D §3.2]
- Objections (as grouped by WP from the Objections): we have no clear idea of an infinite being (1st, 2nd, 5th); we could get the idea of God without God causing it (2nd, 3rd). [source: WP-Meditations_on_First_Philosophy]
- Objective/formal reality distinction draws on scholastic sources (Caterus pressed it; SEP-Ideas cites Wells 1990, "Objective Reality of Ideas in Descartes, Caterus, and Suárez"). [source: IEP-D §5a; SEP descartes-ideas bibliography]

### 6b. Fifth Meditation ontological argument (cross-ref *Early Scholasticism*, Anselm)
- Argument (IEP-D §5b): (1) I have a clear and distinct idea of God as a supremely perfect being, with all perfections; (2) existence (necessary existence) is a perfection; (3) so existence belongs to God's essence, as having angles equal to two right angles belongs to the essence of a triangle; (4) so God exists. Conceiving God without existence is like conceiving "a mountain without a valley" (SEP-OntD: more precisely, an up-slope without a down-slope). [source: IEP-D §5b; SEP-OntD §1]
- Descartes "claims not to be familiar with Anselm's version"; his argument rests on an innate idea and clear and distinct perception rather than a verbal definition. Aquinas's critique had made the argument die out "for several centuries", so contemporaries were surprised he revived it. Descartes agreed with Aquinas that God's existence is not self-evident *to everyone* (First Replies, AT 7:115; CSM 2:82), but held it can become self-evident (*per se notum*) to careful meditators (Second Replies, AT 7:164). [source: SEP-OntD intro, §1]
- Caterus (First Objections, CSM): "Even if it is granted that a supremely perfect being carries the implication of existence in virtue of its very title, it still does not follow that the existence in question is anything actual in the real world; all that follows is that the concept of existence is inseparably linked to the concept of a supreme being." (AT 7:99; CSM 2:72) Caterus also raised the "existing lion" parody; Descartes replied that only true and immutable natures, clearly and distinctly perceived, license such inferences, not invented concepts. [source: SEP-OntD §3]
- Later: Kant's "existence is not a real predicate" (hundred thalers), refined by Russell. [source: SEP-OntD §3] (Consistent with the published *Early Scholasticism* chapter.)
- Order: the *Principles* reverses the Meditations' order of the two proofs. [source: SEP-Works §4]

## 7. The Cartesian Circle (debate)
- Arnauld, Fourth Objections (CSM): "I have one further worry, namely how the author avoids reasoning in a circle when he says that we are sure that what we clearly and distinctly perceive is true only because God exists. But we can be sure that God exists only because we clearly and distinctly perceive this. Hence, before we can be sure that God exists, we ought to be able to be sure that whatever we perceive clearly and evidently is true." (7:214) IEP-Arn: Arnauld "was the first to argue" the circularity; the Second Objections (Mersenne's group) raised a related worry. [source: SEP-D §3.2; IEP-Arn §2a; WP-Meditations_on_First_Philosophy]
- Descartes' reply (7:246; Second Replies 7:146): the divine guarantee is needed for *remembered* conclusions, not for what is being clearly and distinctly perceived now. [source: SEP-D §3.2; IEP-D §6a]
- Readings, as a debate:
  - **Memory reading**: God guarantees memory of past clear perceptions (IEP-D §6a; WP-Cartesian_circle: "the Memory response"; Doney 1955). Bernard Williams' verdict: the trouble "is not that it is circular … The trouble is that the proofs of God are invalid and do not convince even when they are supposedly being intuited." [source: WP-Cartesian_circle]
  - Hatfield: Descartes "is not here challenging the reliability of memory (Frankfurt 1962)"; rather, the deceiver hypothesis can only arise when we are not attending to God's perfection; evident perception outweighs that doubt. [source: SEP-D §3.2]
  - **Coherence reading**: Frankfurt (1965; *Demons, Dreamers, and Madmen*, 1970): Descartes shows reason does not undermine itself, not that it matches a mind-independent world; Loeb (1992): psychological certainty. [source: SEP-D §3.2; WP-Cartesian_circle; SEP-Ep §6.4]
  - Natural light vindicated without God (Jacquette 1996). [source: SEP-D §3.2]
  - Newman's catalogue: "bounded doubt" readings (the cogito and the premises of the God proofs are immune to doubt: Broughton, Doney, Kenny, Wilson, Della Rocca …) vs "unbounded doubt" readings (all are vulnerable when not attended to, but assent is compelled while attending: Curley, Loeb, Sosa, Carriero, Newman & Nelson …); Newman & Nelson's "self-evident God" reading (by the end of Meditation V God's existence becomes self-evident, so the deceiver hypothesis becomes unthinkable; AT 7:69–70). [source: SEP-Ep §6.1–6.2]
- Lennon (2008): "it would be nothing less than astonishing if the central argument of the central text of the central figure of the central period in the history of philosophy were obviously circular." Newman: scholarship "generally resists the vicious circularity interpretation", though it is widely taught. [source: SEP-Ep §6.1]

## 8. The real distinction of mind and body (Sixth Meditation)
- *Discourse* version (6:32–3, CSM): "I saw that while I could pretend that I had no body and that there was no world and no place for me to be in, I could not for all that pretend that I did not exist … From this I knew I was a substance whose whole essence or nature is simply to think, and which does not require any place, or depend on any material thing, in order to exist." Hatfield: this argument from doubtability is fallacious ("conceivability based in ignorance"; his Batman/Bruce Wayne example); the *Meditations* changed the argument, refusing in Meditation II to infer distinctness and arguing in Meditation VI from clear and distinct perception of essences. [source: SEP-D §3.4]
- "Real distinction" = a distinction between substances, each able to exist without the other, at least by God's power (*Principles* I.60). [source: IEP-MB §1; IEP-D §7a]
- **Argument, step by step** (Sixth Meditation, AT VII 78, CSM II 54, as set out in IEP-Arn and IEP-MB):
  1. "I know that everything which I clearly and distinctly understand is capable of being created by God so as to correspond exactly with my understanding of it."
  2. "Hence the fact that I can clearly and distinctly understand one thing apart from another is enough to make me certain that the two things are distinct, since they are capable of being separated, at least by God."
  3. "On the one hand I have a clear and distinct idea of myself, in so far as I am simply a thinking, non-extended thing; and on the other hand I have a distinct idea of body, in so far as this is simply an extended, non-thinking thing."
  4. "And accordingly, it is certain that I am really distinct from my body, and can exist without it."
  [source: IEP-Arn §2a; IEP-MB §3]
- Second argument, from divisibility (AT VII 86–87, CSM II 59): "the body is by its very nature always divisible, while the mind is utterly indivisible. For when I consider the mind, or myself in so far as I am merely a thinking thing, I am unable to distinguish any parts within myself; I understand myself to be something quite single and complete." [source: IEP-MB §3]
- **Arnauld's objection** (Fourth Objections, CSM II 141–142; AT VII 201–202): someone may know that the triangle in a semicircle is right-angled yet doubt, or deny, that the square on the hypotenuse equals the squares on the other two sides; by Descartes' reasoning he would conclude that Pythagoras' property does not belong to the triangle's essence; "But how is my perception of the nature of my mind any clearer than his perception of the nature of a triangle?" (CSM II 142). [source: IEP-Arn §2a; WS-4]
- **Descartes' reply** (CSM II 158; AT VII 224–225): we can clearly understand the right triangle without being aware of the Pythagorean property, but we cannot clearly and distinctly understand it as *lacking* that property; with mind and body we clearly and distinctly perceive each *without* the other. Van Cleve's gloss: perceiving 'A' without perceiving 'B' differs from perceiving 'A without B'. Arnauld ultimately accepted the real distinction; the objection remains central to conceivability-to-possibility arguments (Yablo 1993). [source: IEP-Arn §2a]
- Other objections: we cannot conclude the thinking thing is not corporeal unless we know we know everything about the mind (2nd, 4th, 5th, 7th Objections). [source: WP-Meditations_on_First_Philosophy] Skirry: the main difficulty is that Descartes "has not adequately eliminated the possibility of minds being extended things like brains". [source: IEP-MB §3]
- Motives: religious (Letter to the Sorbonne: answering "irreligious people" on immortality; but the Synopsis claims only that bodily decay does not imply the mind's destruction) and scientific (expelling mind-like final causes and substantial forms from physics; stone "as if it had some knowledge of the centre", Sixth Replies AT VII 442). [source: IEP-MB §2]
- Union (CSM): "I am not merely present in my body as a sailor is present in a ship, but … I am very closely joined and, as it were, intermingled with it, so that I and the body form a unit." [source: WS-7 (CSM text in an MIT course handout); WP-René_Descartes gives an older "pilot in his ship" version — use the CSM wording]

## 9. Elisabeth of Bohemia and the interaction problem
- Elisabeth, Princess Palatine (born 26 December 1618; died 8 February 1680 at Herford per SEP; WP gives 11 February 1680 in its infobox, and "February 12th" in its text). Third of thirteen children, eldest daughter of Frederick V, Elector Palatine and briefly King of Bohemia (lost the throne 1620), and Elizabeth Stuart, daughter of James I. The family lived in exile in The Hague. Educated in Greek, Latin, French, English, German, logic, mathematics, philosophy, sciences; nicknamed "La Greque" by her siblings. [source: SEP-Eli §1; WP-Elisabeth_of_the_Palatinate] WRITER: give the date as "1680" or "February 1680".
- The correspondence began **at her initiative in 1643** and ran until Descartes' death in 1650 (WP: through Alfonso Pollot); her letters are almost all her extant philosophical writing; she refused Chanut's request to publish her side; her letters were first published by Foucher de Careil (1879) from early-18th-century copies found at Rosendael near Arnhem. [source: SEP-Eli §4, §4.1; WP-René_Descartes; WP-Elisabeth_of_the_Palatinate]
- **First letter, 6 May 1643** (some editions date it 16 May; probably Old vs New Style, not verified), trans. Lisa Shapiro: "tell me please how the soul of a human being (it being only a thinking substance) can determine the bodily spirits and so bring about voluntary actions". [source: WP-Elisabeth_of_the_Palatinate (citing Shapiro 1999); WS-1 (Shapiro 2007 p. 62; AT III 661)] She argues that all determination of motion comes from impulsion (contact) or from the shape of the surface of the mover (extension), and the soul has neither: in the translation found by search, "contact is required for the first two conditions, and extension for the third". [source: WS-3; WP-Elisabeth_of_the_Palatinate; IEP-D §7b]
- Shapiro's analysis: she asks about mind-to-body causation, not body-to-mind; she assumes a mechanist (efficient-causal) account of causation; she suggests Descartes must either give the account of causation proper to mind–body action or say more about the mind's nature. [source: SEP-Eli §4.2]
- **Descartes' reply, 21 May 1643: primitive notions** (CSMK): besides general notions (being, number, duration), "as regards body in particular, we have only the notion of extension, which entails the notions of shape and motion; and as regards the soul on its own, we have only the notion of thought, which includes the perceptions of the intellect and the inclinations of the will. Lastly, as regards the soul and the body together, we have only the notion of their union, on which depends our notion of the soul's power to move the body, and the body's power to act on the soul and cause its sensations and passions" (AT III 665; CSMK 218). He compared the union to the scholastic notion of heaviness acting on a body. [source: IEP-MB §5; IEP-D §7b; SEP-Eli §4.2]
- **Elisabeth's second letter, 20 June 1643** (Shapiro): she cannot understand how the soul, "nonextended and immaterial", moves the body by the model of heaviness; "And I admit that it would be easier for me to concede matter and extension to the mind than it would be for me to concede the capacity to move a body and be moved by one to an immaterial thing." [source: WP-Elisabeth_of_the_Palatinate, citing Shapiro 1999] Jaegwon Kim cites this as the first causal argument for physicalism (WP). She rejects the heaviness model because Descartes himself had shown it unintelligible. [source: SEP-Eli §4.2] (Search snippets give Bennett's chronology as 10 June; the 10/20 difference is probably Old/New Style; not verified.)
- **Descartes, 28 June 1643** (CSMK): the union "is known only obscurely by the intellect alone … but it is known very clearly by the senses"; "It does not seem to me that the human mind is capable of forming a very distinct conception of both the distinction between the soul and the body and their union; for to do this it is necessary to conceive them as a single thing and at the same time to conceive them as two things; and this is absurd" (AT III 693; CSMK 227). He advised spending only "a few hours a year" on pure intellect; "very harmful" to meditate often on metaphysics (AT III 695; CSMK 228). Hatfield: he "admitted to Elisabeth that he didn't have a good answer" (3:694). Elisabeth's letter of 1 July 1643 agrees the senses show that soul and body interact but says they do not teach how. [source: SEP-Pin §2.4 (quotations); SEP-D §3.4; WP-Elisabeth_of_the_Palatinate]
- Scholars on Descartes' replies: "evasive" (Shapiro); they raise whether the union is a third substance (Garber 1983), imply a contradiction in being both two and one (Mattern 1978), and jump between mind-to-body and body-to-mind (Rozemond 1999). [source: SEP-Eli §4.2]
- Gassendi raised the same contact problem in the Fifth Objections; Descartes replied the problem "arises simply from a supposition that is false and cannot in any way be proved, namely that, if the soul and the body are two substances whose nature is different, this prevents them from being able to act on each other" (AT VII 213: CSM II 275). [source: IEP-MB §5; IEP-D §7b]
- **Elisabeth as a philosopher in her own right** (Shapiro): she points to cases where bodily conditions ("the vapours") affect thought, suggesting mind might be extended; holds we control the order of our thoughts but that the capacity for thought depends on bodily condition, so questions whether a thinking thing is a substance properly speaking. Interpretations: Shapiro (1999, 2007) — open to revising dualism without clearly advocating an alternative; Janssen-Lauret (2018) — a "naturalistic dualism" (soul conscious and extended). She cited "the weakness of my sex" as a bodily condition; Descartes disregarded it. [source: SEP-Eli §4.2]
- Mathematics: in November 1643 Descartes set her Apollonius' problem of three circles; her (lost) solution used algebraic geometry, apparently learned from Stampioen's textbook, and Descartes praised its single-variable symmetry. Involved in Frans van Schooten's hiring at Leiden. [source: SEP-Eli §4.3]
- Physics: thanked him for the dedication of the *Principles* (1644) while criticising his accounts of magnetism and the heaviness of mercury; Shapiro (2019): in natural philosophy she should be understood as a Cartesian; Kambouchner (2021): not in practical/civil philosophy. [source: SEP-Eli §4.3]
- Ethics (1645–46): started from Seneca's *De vita beata*; she objected that his account of virtue (resolution to do what one judges best) makes virtue immune to luck while even reason is subject to luck, separates virtue from contentment (regret), and needs an "infinite science" (4:289). On 13 September 1645 she asked him to "define the passions, in order to know them better" (AT 4:289), which led to the draft treatise (she commented 25 April 1646) published as *The Passions of the Soul* (1649). Politics: she asked for his views on Machiavelli's *Prince* (exchanged September–October 1646). [source: SEP-Eli §4.4–4.5]
- Later life: involved in Westphalia negotiations and English Restoration efforts; entered the Lutheran convent at Herford 1660, abbess 1667; sheltered Labadists and Quakers (Penn, Barclay). Corresponded with van Schurman, Pell, Malebranche (briefly). [source: SEP-Eli §1, §3]
- Cross-ref: chapter 31 *Women Philosophers of the Early Modern Era* treats her fully.

## 10. The pineal gland
- He thought the pineal gland (*conarion*) "the principal seat of the soul, and the place in which all our thoughts are formed. The reason I believe this is that I cannot find any part of the brain, except this, which is not double" (letter of 29 January 1640, AT III:19–20, CSMK 143). Also discussed in the *Treatise of Man* and the *Passions*. [source: SEP-Pin §2.2]
- In the *Passions*, the soul is joined to the whole body but exercises its functions chiefly in the gland; the soul's volition and the animal spirits push the gland in different directions (AT XI:364, CSM I:345). [source: SEP-Pin §2.3; IEP-D §9]
- Contemporary anatomy: Steensen (1669) showed the gland is not suspended in the ventricles nor surrounded by arteries; Willis noted animals have large pineals. "Cartesian interactionism" provoked "either ridicule or disgust" (Spinoza, *Ethics*; SEP-Pin cites it as "Spinoza 1677, part 4, preface"; part number not checked), but "it is doubtful whether Descartes was a Cartesian interactionist himself". [source: SEP-Pin §3.1] WRITER: say "Spinoza mocked it in the *Ethics*" without the part number.
- Third law of motion caveat: Descartes wrote, "I am not here inquiring into the existence or nature of any power to move bodies which may be possessed by human minds" (AT VIII:65, CSM I:242). [source: SEP-Pin §2.3]

## 11. Animals as machines (bête-machine) — debate
- Only humans have souls; Descartes equated soul with mind. Animals and human bodies are machines ("machine[s] made of earth, which God forms", AT XI 120, CSM I 99); vegetative and sensitive functions explained mechanically through "animal spirits". [source: SEP-D §4; IEP-D §8d]
- Tests (*Discourse* V): a machine could mimic an animal, but not a human, because it could not use language (and could not act adaptively across situations). [source: IEP-D §8d; WP-Animal_machine]
- Fourth Replies (CSM): "When people take a fall, and stick out their hands so as to protect their head, it is not reason that instructs them to do this; it is simply that the sight of the impending fall reaches the brain and sends the animal spirits into the nerves … even without any mental volition, just as it would be produced in a machine." (7:229–30) [source: SEP-D §4, §7]
- Debate: Descartes' followers took him to deny animals all feeling; IEP-D presents that reading (a beaten dog "did not feel anything at all"). Cottingham ("A Brute to the Brutes?", 1978; "Descartes' Treatment of Animals", 1998) and Hatfield (2008) argue he did not deny all animal sensation; distinguishes *cogitatio* from *sensus*. Singer, *Animal Liberation* (1975), called Descartes' position the "absolute nadir". [source: SEP-D §4 and bibliography; IEP-D §8d; WP-Animal_machine]
- La Mettrie, *L'Homme machine* (1747), extended the machine to humans. [source: WP-Animal_machine]

## 12. Mechanical physics
- Matter's essence is extension in length, breadth and depth; space is not distinct from matter: "the extension in length, breadth, and depth which constitutes the space occupied by a body, is exactly the same as that which constitutes the body" (*Principles* II.10); hence **no void**: if God removed all matter from a vessel its sides would touch. [source: SEP-Phys §3; IEP-D §8b; SEP-D §3.3]
- Matter infinitely (or "indefinitely") divisible; so no atoms; one matter for heaven and earth; no substantial forms or real qualities; the earth, air, fire, water are just kinds distinguished by size, shape and motion of parts. [source: SEP-D §1.3, §4; IEP-D §8b–c]
- **Three laws of nature** (*Principles* II.37, 39, 40): (1) "each thing, as far as is in its power, always remains in the same state; and that consequently, when it is once moved, it always continues to move"; (2) "all movement is, of itself, along straight lines"; (3) "a body, upon coming in contact with a stronger one, loses none of its motion; but that, upon coming in contact with a weaker one, it loses as much as it transfers to that weaker body" (Miller & Miller translation). Laws 1–2 later incorporated into Newton's first law; but Descartes treated rest and motion as different states. Grounded in God's immutability; total "quantity of motion" (size × speed, a scalar) conserved (*Principles* II.36, 62); seven rules of impact. [source: SEP-Phys §4; IEP-D §8c gives CSM wording of the laws]
- **Vortices**: the plenum forms whirlpools of matter; planets are carried round the sun; gravity results from the pressure of the whirling ether; magnetism from screw-shaped particles. The vortex theory let him keep Copernicanism while saying the earth, at rest in its vortex, "properly speaking, is not moved" (*Principles* III.24–31). Influential until the mid-18th century; replaced by Newton's force-based account; Leibniz questioned his laws of impact. [source: SEP-D §4; SEP-Phys §7]
- The universe hypothesised to have begun as a chaos of particles from which suns and planets formed (*Principles* III.45; *Discourse* V). [source: SEP-D §4]
- Hypotheses: particular mechanisms are confirmed by their consequences (a hypothetico-deductive element), so sense experience matters in science. [source: SEP-D §2, §3.1]
- Optics/perception: accepted Kepler's retinal image; adapted Ibn al-Haytham's account of perceiving size by combining visual angle with distance; anticipated "size constancy". [source: SEP-D §5]

## 13. Passions and ethics (brief)
- *Passions* (1649): approached "as a physicist" (prefatory letters, 11:326); six primitive passions: wonder, love, hatred, desire, joy, sadness (II.69); the body often acts first (running from a beast) and the passion disposes the soul "to want the things for which they prepare the body" (I.40). [source: SEP-D §6]
- Provisional morality (*Discourse* III): obey laws and customs, holding to his religion; be firm and resolute (traveller lost in a forest); master himself rather than fortune; choose the best occupation. Later ethic of *générosité* (*Passions* III.153). [source: IEP-D §10]
- Damasio, *Descartes' Error* (1994): Hatfield notes such critics "espouse theories similar in many respects to Descartes' own". [source: SEP-D §7]

## 14. Influence
- Cartesians: Géraud de Cordemoy, Arnold Geulincx, Poulain de la Barre, Antoine Le Grand, Malebranche, Pierre-Sylvain Régis, Jacques Rohault; Henry More (admiration then hostility); Huet's critique (1689). Physics taught in the Netherlands, France, England, parts of Germany. [source: SEP-D §1.4, §7]
- Occasionalism: God is the only true cause (Malebranche: "there is only one true cause because there is only one true God; … all natural causes are not true causes but only occasional causes"). Cartesian occasionalists: Louis de La Forge (1632–66; possibly coined "occasional cause"), Cordemoy (1614–83), Clauberg (1622–55), Geulincx (1624–69); Malebranche the most famous. Whether Descartes himself was an occasionalist (about bodies) is debated. SEP-Occ: portraying occasionalism as an ad hoc fix for interaction is "a caricature at best"; it has deeper sources, including Islamic occasionalism (cross-ref *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes*). [source: SEP-Occ intro, §1.3; SEP-D §3.4]
- Spinoza's monism and Leibniz's pre-established harmony as alternative answers to the interaction problem (cross-ref chapters 26, 27). [source: SEP-D §3.4; IEP-MB §5]
- Women and equality: Poulain de la Barre used dualism to argue "the mind has no sex"; Mary Astell used Cartesian philosophy for women's education; Malebranche less favourable. [source: SEP-D §7]
- Anton Wilhelm Amo engaged Descartes (published *Enlightenment* History chapter) — not checked here.
- Epistemology's turn to the knower (Locke, Berkeley, Hume, Reid, Kant). [source: SEP-D §7]
- Ryle, *The Concept of Mind* (1949): Cartesian dualism as the "official doctrine". [source: SEP-Dual] (Ryle's "ghost in the machine" phrase not checked in a source.)

## 15. "Cartesian dualism": caricature vs scholarship (debate)
- Hatfield names three misreadings: (1) Descartes as a sense-data foundationalist (e.g. a reading in Williams 1986) — "has little to offer"; (2) that he thought we could naturally have sensations without a body — he held sensation and imagination need the brain; (3) Descartes as a straw man for a disembodied, over-rational "Western rationality" (e.g. Wheeler 2005), whereas he explained much behaviour mechanically without mind. Moriarty (2003): many absorb Descartes second-hand through Lacan and Foucault. [source: SEP-D §7]
- Dualism did not place mind "outside nature" for Descartes: mind–body laws belonged to natural philosophy; his followers spoke of psychophysical laws. [source: SEP-D §5]
- Interpretations of the mind–body relation (SEP-Pin §2.4): scholastic hylomorphism, the soul as substantial form (Hoffman 1986; Skirry 2003, 2005); Platonist "angelism" (Maritain: "an angel inhabiting a machine and directing it by means of the pineal gland"); **trialism**: three primitive notions, extension, thought and union (Cottingham 1985, "Cartesian Trialism", *Mind* 94; Cottingham 1986); dualistic interactionism. [source: SEP-Pin §2.4; WS-9 for the article title]
- IEP-MB (Skirry) defends the scholastic reading (Descartes calls the soul "the true substantial form of man", draft letter to Voetius, AT III 508; "substantially united", to Mesland 9 Feb 1645; body and soul "incomplete substances" forming an *ens per se*, to Regius Dec 1641, AT III 460) but says it "is a minority position"; Rozemond (*Descartes's Dualism*, 1998) argues against it. [source: IEP-MB §5]
- Hatfield's verdict: most philosophers today accept neither Descartes' method nor his dualism, but "a problem that Descartes made prominent has lived far beyond his proposed solution." [source: SEP-D §7]

## 16. Forerunners of the cogito (handle as the published chapters do)
- Augustine, *City of God* 11.26, "si fallor, sum"; Descartes' letter to Colvius, 1640 (CSMK): he read the passage in the town library; Augustine "does use it to prove the certainty of our existence. He goes on to show that there is a certain likeness of the Trinity in us… I, on the other hand, use the argument to show that this I which is thinking is an immaterial substance with no bodily element. These are two very different things. In itself it is such a simple and natural thing to infer that one exists from the fact that one is doubting that it could have occurred to any writer." Menn, *Descartes and Augustine* (1998). [source: WP-Cogito,_ergo_sum; SEP-Ep bibliography] Consistent with the published *Augustine* chapter.
- Avicenna's flying man: resemblance; whether deep or trivial disputed; **no evidence Descartes knew it**. Consistent with the published *Falsafa* chapter (keep that wording). [source: WP-Cogito,_ergo_sum lists it; research/medieval--falsafa.md]
- Al-Ghazālī's *Deliverance from Error*: comparison often made; translation only in 1842; any Descartes link circumstantial (per the Ghazālī notes, IEP). WRITER: one sentence at most.
- Gómez Pereira, *Antoniana Margarita* (1554): "nosco me aliquid noscere, & quidquid noscit, est, ergo ego sum". [source: WP-Cogito,_ergo_sum only — use with "Wikipedia notes" caution or omit]

## Debates
- Was the doubt serious scepticism or a meditative tool? Popkin/Curley vs Hatfield ("cognitive exercises", spiritual-exercise genre). [source: SEP-D §2, §7]
- Cogito: inference or intuition? Hintikka, "Cogito ergo sum: Inference or Performance?" (*Philosophical Review* 71, 1962): not a logical inference but a performance; "I do not exist" is not logically contradictory but "existentially incoherent" to think; the Second Replies "simple intuition" passage seems to support him; Margaret Wilson (1978): rejecting a *syllogism* is not rejecting *inference*; Kenny: "what is from one point of view intuited is from another point of view deduced"; Curley: Descartes "consistently blurs" the distinction. [source: SEP-Ep §4.1]
- Does "I" presuppose a self? Lichtenberg and Russell: he was entitled only to "there are thoughts"; Gassendi raised a version first (WP). Reply: subjectivity of experience is itself a datum (Newman). [source: SEP-Ep §4.1; WP-Cogito,_ergo_sum]
- Cartesian Circle: memory reading vs present-perception reading vs coherence (Frankfurt) vs psychological certainty (Loeb) vs bounded/unbounded doubt and self-evident God (Newman & Nelson). [source: SEP-D §3.2; SEP-Ep §6; IEP-D §6a; WP-Cartesian_circle]
- Real distinction: Arnauld's triangle vs Descartes' "A without B" reply (Van Cleve). [source: IEP-Arn §2a]
- Interaction: was Descartes an interactionist at all? interactionism vs trialism (Cottingham) vs scholastic hylomorphism (Hoffman, Skirry; opposed by Rozemond) vs occasionalist reading of bodies. [source: SEP-Pin §2.4; IEP-MB §5; SEP-Occ §1.3]
- Elisabeth's own position: open but noncommittal (Shapiro) vs naturalistic dualism (Janssen-Lauret); Cartesian in natural philosophy (Shapiro 2019) vs not in practical life (Kambouchner). [source: SEP-Eli §4.2–4.3]
- Animals: did Descartes deny all animal feeling? Traditional reading (IEP) vs Cottingham, Hatfield. [source: SEP-D §4; IEP-D §8d; WP-Animal_machine]
- Teresa of Ávila as source for the deceiver (Mercer 2017) vs scepticism. [source: WS-6]
- Cause of death: pneumonia vs Ebert's arsenic theory. [source: WP-René_Descartes; WS-5]

## Consistency with published Periergia texts
- *History of Philosophy* overview: "doubt everything dubitable — senses, body, even mathematics under the demon hypothesis … *cogito, ergo sum* … the mind–body problem (his substance dualism … at the price of their interaction, unexplained at the pineal gland ever since)". Broadly consistent, but the phrase "unexplained at the pineal gland ever since" leans toward the caricature: SEP-Pin says it is "doubtful whether Descartes was a Cartesian interactionist himself", and the Descartes chapter should present the interpretive debate. Also note *cogito, ergo sum* is not the *Meditations'* own wording. Not a factual contradiction.
- *Scientific Revolution* (History): "*Discourse on Method* (1637), began by doubting everything except his own thinking, and pictured nature as a machine of matter in motion". Mild tension: SEP-D says the *Discourse* "did not engage in the deep skepticism of the later Meditations" and presented the essence of matter as a hypothesis. Acceptable as a summary; no correction required.
- *Emotions* (History): *Passions* "grew out of his correspondence with Princess Elisabeth … at whose request he wrote it"; "only as a Physicist"; six primitive passions; animal spirits from the blood. All consistent with SEP-Eli (written at her request; she asked him on 13 Sept 1645 to define the passions) and SEP-D §6.
- *Augustine*: Colvius letter wording identical to WP-Cogito (CSMK); "similar to (and probably inspired)" claim is SEP-Aug's. Consistent.
- *Falsafa*: flying man resemblance disputed; no evidence Descartes knew it. Consistent; keep.
- *Early Scholasticism*: "Descartes gave a related proof in the Fifth Meditation (1641)"; the Descartes chapter can add that he claimed not to know Anselm's version (SEP-OntD). Consistent.
- *Al-Ghazālī and Averroes*: "Readers often compare it with Descartes (see *Descartes*)". Consistent if the chapter mentions it briefly without claiming influence.
- *Early Buddhism*: "not a dualism of two substances like Descartes's". Consistent.
- *Enlightenment* (History): Amo accepted Descartes's division of mind and body but argued the body senses (1734). Not checked here; no conflict with anything read.
- Mathematics content: no published mathematics or physics article mentions Descartes or Cartesian coordinates historically (grep found none).
