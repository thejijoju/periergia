## 1. Vedānta: name, canon and the Brahma-sūtra
- "Vedānta" means "end of the Veda", i.e. the Upaniṣads; IEP-Adv: Vedānta is "nominally a school of Indian philosophy, although in reality it is a label for any hermeneutics that attempts to provide a consistent interpretation of the philosophy of the Upaniṣads or, more formally, the canonical summary of the Upaniṣads, Bādarāyaṇa's Brahma Sūtra." [source: IEP-Adv intro; IEP-Rām §1]
- Perennial questions of Vedānta (IEP-Rām): the nature of Brahman, and the relationship of the multiplicity of individuals to it. Chief disagreements: the nature and ontological status of individual selves, objects and Brahman, and the importance of ethics/duty (dharma). [source: IEP-Rām §§1, 2a]
- **Prasthāna-trayī** ("three sources/starting points"): the principal Upaniṣads (śruti), the Bhagavad Gītā (smṛti) and the Brahma-sūtra (called the "nyāya" or "tarka" prasthāna). SEP-Ś calls these Advaita's "triple canon (prasthānatrayī)". In traditional Vedāntic hermeneutics a founding teacher (ācārya) is expected to comment on all three. [source: SEP-Ś §1; WP-Prasthanatrayi] Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja and Madhva each commented on the Upaniṣads (Rāmānuja: see caveat §6), the Gītā and the Brahma-sūtra. [source: WP-Madhvacharya (Madhva on all three); SEP-Ś §1 (Śaṅkara); IEP-Rām §1 (Rāmānuja: Śrī Bhāṣya on the Brahma-sūtra and Gītā Bhāṣya; IEP lists no separate Upaniṣad commentaries — his Vedārthasaṅgraha expounds the Upaniṣads)]
- **Brahma-sūtra** (also Vedānta-sūtra, Śārīraka-sūtra), attributed to Bādarāyaṇa (sometimes identified with Vyāsa). 555 sūtras in four chapters (adhyāya), each of four parts (pāda); about 189 topical sections (adhikaraṇa), counted slightly differently by different sub-schools. [source: WP-Brahma_Sutras]
- **Date (give as a range; disputed):** SEP-Ś: Bādarāyaṇa "ca. first century BCE". WP collects: Lochtefeld 500–200 BCE; Radhakrishnan and Dasgupta 2nd c. BCE; Deussen 200 BCE–400 CE; Jacobi 200–450 CE (because it seems to know Madhyamaka), disputed by Ingalls (1954), who held it "could not have been composed later than the start of the common era"; Nakamura: complete in present form 400–450 CE; Mayeda: multiple authors, c. 300 BCE to 400–450 CE; Isaeva: scholars "rather unanimous" on 2nd c. BCE–2nd c. CE. **Safe wording: "compiled over several centuries around the turn of the common era; its final form may date as late as the fifth century CE."** [source: SEP-Ś §1; WP-Brahma_Sutras]
- The sūtras are terse memory aids; chapter 1 rejects Sāṃkhya's inert first principle (pradhāna) as the cause of a purposive world; chapter 2 answers Sāṃkhya, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Yoga, Mīmāṃsā, Buddhists (2.2.18–32) and Jains (2.2.33–36). [source: WP-Brahma_Sutras]
- Mayeda: the text was possibly "written from a Bhedābheda Vedāntic viewpoint" (difference-and-non-difference) — i.e. not obviously Advaita. [source: WP-Brahma_Sutras] (Interpretive claim; name it as one scholar's view.)
- Śaṅkara's Brahma-sūtra commentary is "the oldest surviving"; it mentions older lost commentaries (e.g. by Dravida and Bhartṛprapañca). [source: WP-Adi_Shankara "Works"]; SEP-Ś: Śaṅkara composed "our earliest complete extant commentaries" on the Brahma-sūtra, Gītā and ten principal Upaniṣads. [source: SEP-Ś §1]
- Before Śaṅkara, Vedānta was not dominant: Nakamura (via WP) — until the 11th c. Vedānta "was a peripheral school of thought"; it became a major force when Hindu sects used it to ground their doctrines (Rāmānuja being the prime example). [source: WP-Adi_Shankara, "Vaishnavite Vedanta"] Bhedābheda (Bhāskara, Yādavaprakāśa) was a major pre- and post-Śaṅkara Vedānta. [source: IEP-Rām §2a; WP-Advaita_Vedanta]

## 2. Gauḍapāda and the antecedents of Advaita
- Advaita is not Śaṅkara's invention. SEP-Ś: "Śaṅkara was a systematizer of Advaita Vedānta, not a founder." His teacher was Govinda; by tradition Govinda's teacher was Gauḍapāda (SEP-Ś: "sixth century CE"), author of the *Gauḍapādakārikā* (*Māṇḍūkya-kārikā*) on the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad. "The historical record of Advaita Vedānta is obscure prior to Gauḍapāda." [source: SEP-Ś §1]
- IEP-Adv: the only two pre-Śaṅkara names with "some historical certainty" are Gauḍapāda and Govinda; "the first complete Advaitic work is considered to be the Mandukya Kārikā". [source: IEP-Adv §1]
- Gauḍapāda's dates: WP gives early 6th to 7th c. CE; the earlier estimate (c. 500 / first half of 6th c.) rests on citations of the Kārikā by the Buddhist Bhāviveka; he must postdate Asaṅga (4th c.) because he cites Nāgārjuna and Asaṅga. Potter: "teacher's teacher" (paramaguru) need not be literal. [source: WP-Gaudapada]
- The Kārikā has four chapters; the fourth uses Buddhist terminology; WP's sources: "doctrinally Gaudapada's work is Vedantic, and not Buddhist". Nakamura notes doctrinal contradictions between chapters; some scholars doubt single authorship. [source: WP-Gaudapada]
- Gauḍapāda's doctrine of **non-origination (ajātivāda)**: what does not exist in the beginning or end cannot be real in the present; origination is impossible from being, non-being, both or neither; bondage and liberation are only imagined. SEP-Ś also cites Śaṅkara's GK commentary on brahman as "that which has no birth (ajāti)". [source: WP-Gaudapada; SEP-Ś §2.3]
- Ramanuja and Madhva treated the first chapter of the Kārikā (with the Māṇḍūkya) as scripture (śruti) (Nakamura via WP). [source: WP-Gaudapada]

## 3. Śaṅkara: life, dates, legend, works
### Dates (record the debate)
- SEP-Ś: "There are several different dates ascribed to Śaṅkara. Early scholars placed him from 788–820 CE. Nakamura (1983: 48–88) argues for an earlier date of 700–750 CE … Potter (1981 …) places him from the late seventh to early eighth centuries CE due to the dating of a contemporaneous Advaitin named Maṇḍanamiśra." SEP-Ś's own summary: he "flourished during the eighth century CE". [source: SEP-Ś intro, §1]
- 788–820: proposed by late-19th/early-20th c. scholars following K. P. Tiele (based ultimately on a late traditional text), accepted by Max Müller, Deussen, Radhakrishnan and others; "recent scholarship has questioned" it. c. 700–750: late-20th/21st c. consensus; Koller: "the best recent scholarship argues that he was born in 700 and died in 750 CE." Reasoning: his works engage Buddhist and Mīmāṃsā authors of the 5th–7th c. (earliest limit c. 650) and Vācaspati Miśra commented on him in the first half of the 9th c. (latest limit c. 800). [source: WP-Adi_Shankara "Dating"]
- The Advaita monasteries (maṭhas) date him to the 5th c. BCE (e.g. 509 BCE Kāñcī; 483 BCE Śṛṅgeri). [source: WP-Adi_Shankara] (Mention only as tradition.)
- **Note:** IEP-Rām's and IEP-Madh's reference lists/text date Śaṅkara to the "9th century" — this follows the old 788–820 dating; do not use. The published Upanishads chapter says "Śaṅkara (c. 700 CE)" — consistent with Nakamura/Koller. [source: IEP-Rām refs; IEP-Madh §1; published text, §15]
- "There are no dependable historical records of Śaṅkara's life beyond his written works which provide few biographical clues." Religious identity unclear: Śaiva by tradition, but several scholars believe he was Vaiṣṇava. [source: SEP-Ś §1]
### Hagiography (digvijaya) — legend, not history
- The *Śaṅkaravijaya*s ("Conquests of Śaṅkara") "were composed several centuries after his death": born in Kālaḍi (Kerala), renouncer young, travelled the subcontinent debating, "supposedly died at the young age of thirty-two". [source: SEP-Ś §1]
- WP: at least fourteen hagiographies, written "many centuries to a thousand years after Shankara's death … filled with legends and fiction, often mutually contradictory"; they "are not historical accurate documents, but politically motivated hagiographies". The *Mādhavīya Śaṅkaradigvijaya* is traditionally attributed to Mādhava-Vidyāraṇya (14th c.), but attribution and dating are disputed — a 17th or even 18th c. date has been proposed (Bader 2000; Isayeva). The *Bṛhat-Śaṅkaravijaya* of Citsukha may never have existed (Sundaresan). [source: WP-Adi_Shankara; WP-Digvijaya]
- Common legend elements: crocodile seizes 8-year-old Śaṅkara until his mother consents to his renunciation; debates with Kumārila and Maṇḍana; founding of four maṭhas. Death places are contradictory (Kedarnath, Kāñcī, Kashmir; Tāranātha says he drowned himself in the Ganges). [source: WP-Adi_Shankara]
- The *digvijaya* genre ("conquest of the four quarters", originally royal) was applied to Madhva, Śaṅkara, Caitanya and Vallabha; the Śaṅkara versions may have been modelled on Madhva's (the *Madhvavijaya* of Nārāyaṇa Paṇḍitācārya, 14th c.), since one of the earliest Śaṅkara hagiographies postdates Madhva. [source: WP-Digvijaya]
- Vidyāraṇya (head of Śṛṅgeri 1380–1386, minister in the Vijayanagara empire) is credited by Hacker and others with turning Śaṅkara into a "divine folk-hero"; his doxography presented Advaita as the summit of all systems, which influenced early Indologists. [source: WP-Adi_Shankara "Vijayanagara"] (Popular WP synthesis; present as the view of Hacker and others.)
- Monasteries: SEP-Ś: Śaṅkara "purportedly established" the Daśanāmī renouncer orders and "four or five" principal maṭhas. Traditional four: Dvārakā (west), Puri (east), Śṛṅgeri (south), Badrikāśrama/Jyotirmaṭh (north). Hacker: the system may have been initiated by Vidyāraṇya in the 14th c. [source: SEP-Ś §1; WP-Adi_Shankara]
- Four traditional pupils: Padmapāda, Sureśvara, Hastāmalaka, Toṭaka. Padmapāda wrote the *Pañcapādikā*; Sureśvara the *Naiṣkarmyasiddhi*. [source: IEP-Adv §1]
- Influence in his own time was limited: Clark — "relatively unknown during his life-time, and probably for several centuries after"; Richard King: the claim that Śaṅkara was the most influential figure in Hindu thought "does not seem to be justified by the historical evidence"; until the 10th c. Maṇḍana Miśra was regarded as the main Advaitin. [source: WP-Adi_Shankara; WP-Maṇḍana_Miśra]
### Works and authenticity
- Secure core (SEP-Ś): commentaries on the Brahma-sūtra, Bhagavad Gītā and ten principal Upaniṣads (Īśā, Kena, Kaṭha, Praśna, Muṇḍaka, Māṇḍūkya [with the Kārikā], Aitareya, Taittirīya, Chāndogya, Bṛhadāraṇyaka) and the independent *Upadeśasāhasrī* ("A Thousand Teachings"). "There are hundreds of other texts attributed to Śaṅkara, but their authorship is questionable and most were likely composed in later centuries by monastic heads who held a 'Śaṅkarācārya' title." Paul Hacker devised criteria for authenticity (refined by Mayeda; critiqued by Sundaresan 2002). [source: SEP-Ś §1]
- WP: over 300 texts attributed; Flood accepts only the Bṛhadāraṇyaka and Taittirīya Upaniṣad commentaries as certain; Hacker and Mayeda add Chāndogya, Aitareya, Kena, Īśā, Kaṭha, Praśna; the Māṇḍūkya/Kārikā commentary has been questioned. The *Upadeśasāhasrī* is "his most important original philosophical work". [source: WP-Adi_Shankara "Works"]
- **Vivekacūḍāmaṇi** ("Crest-jewel of discrimination", 580 verses): traditionally Śaṅkara's but attribution "questioned and mostly rejected by modern scholarship" (Comans: "most probably erroneous"; Isayeva, Mayeda). Hacker thought it likely authentic; Ingalls rejected that on manuscript evidence; Grimes (translator, 2004) defends possible authenticity; Berger suggests corporate monastic authorship. *Ātmabodha* and *Aparokṣānubhūti* also not accepted as his. [source: WP-Vivekacūḍāmaṇi; WP-Adi_Shankara]
- Śaṅkara does not try to prove nonduality by independent reasoning: "nonduality is not subject to logical proofs … He accepts nonduality based on the Upaniṣad's authority alone. Nonetheless, he was an original philosopher who constructed novel arguments". [source: SEP-Ś §1] (Important caveat for the argument section.)

## 4. Śaṅkara's Advaita: doctrines
- *Advaita* "is often translated as 'non-dualism' though it literally means 'non-secondness'." [source: IEP-Adv intro]
- Core thesis (SEP-Ś): the Upaniṣads reveal a principle of nonduality, brahman, "the reality of all things"; the essential core of the self (ātman) is brahman; ātman "is pure non-intentional consciousness … one without a second, nondual, infinite existence, and numerically identical with brahman." [source: SEP-Ś intro]
- Brahman is pure existence, pure consciousness and pure bliss; consciousness is not a property of brahman but its very nature (contrast other Vedānta schools). Brahman without qualities = *nirguṇa*; with qualities, as personal God/Lord (īśvara) = *saguṇa*. [source: IEP-Adv §2a]
- **Criterion of the real** (SEP-Ś §2.1): "He defines the real as that which does not change its nature, whereas what is unreal does change." Clay-and-pot illustration: clay persists through lump, pot and plate, so clay is "more real" (persistence) and the pot depends on clay but not vice versa (asymmetrical dependence). Conclusions: the ground is more real than the effect; the effect is reducible to its ground; so the effect is not different from its ground. Objects are "name (nāma) and form (rūpa)". [source: SEP-Ś §2.1]
- Śaṅkara rejects (a) emergence from non-existence (which he attributes to Vaiśeṣika) and (b) the Madhyamaka conclusion that nothing has essential existence, which he finds "counter-intuitively nihilistic". [source: SEP-Ś §2.1]
- He is an epistemic realist at the empirical level: his commentary on BS 2.2.28–31 "affirms that we must assume perceptual realism and rejects Buddhist Yogācāra idealism". [source: SEP-Ś §2] (Consistent with published Mahāyāna chapter, which has Śaṅkara replying to Yogācāra.)
- **Māyā**: opponents "pejoratively labeled him a māyāvādin" ("one who argues the world is illusory"); SEP-Ś: "not exactly incorrect" but misleading, since "he infrequently uses the term 'māyā'" (Hacker 1995) and stresses moral virtue and acting for the good of the world. The world is "an objective but less-than-real appearance … akin to a magic trick"; "indeterminable (anirvacanīya)" as brahman or other than brahman; not like a hare's horn (non-existent) or square circle (self-contradictory). [source: SEP-Ś §2.2]
- Later Advaitins developed māyā as a creative material cause equated with beginningless ignorance; whether this can be read into Śaṅkara "is contested by academic and traditional scholars" (Comans 2000; Doherty 2005; Satchidanandendra). [source: SEP-Ś §2.2] **Debate to name.**
- Maṇḍana Miśra (not Śaṅkara) introduced *anirvacanīyatva* of māyā-avidyā as a technical doctrine: avidyā is neither the essence of brahman nor another thing, neither absolutely non-existent nor existent; "a common notion in Advaita which did not derive from Shankara". [source: WP-Maṇḍana_Miśra] (Partly conflicts with SEP-Ś's citation of BSBh 2.1.14 for indeterminability in Śaṅkara; present as "the doctrine was made technical by Maṇḍana, though Śaṅkara already uses the idea".)
- **Two levels of truth/reality** (SEP-Ś §2.3): (1) conventional intersubjective empirical reality (*vyāvahārika-sattā*); (2) ultimate reality (*pāramārthika-sattā*) of nondual existence. "A conscientious reader must understand this distinction and be mindful that Śaṅkara toggles his perspective between the two." From the ultimate standpoint the world "is never truly born". Ram-Prasad calls the result "non-realism". [source: SEP-Ś §2.3]
- IEP-Adv gives **three** planes in classical Advaita: absolute (*pāramārthika*), worldly (*vyāvahārika*, including heavens) and illusory (*prātibhāsika*, e.g. mirage objects); all different from the impossible, like a sky-lotus or a barren woman's son. [source: IEP-Adv §2b] (The threefold scheme is the later systematisation; SEP-Ś speaks of two.)
- Dream analogy: the dreamer cannot find the dream's ground from inside the dream; waking simultaneously falsifies the dream. Rope–snake: "Brahman never undergoes genuine transformation (pariṇāma) into the world, just as a rope mistaken to be a snake does not actually transform into a snake." [source: SEP-Ś §2.3] Later terminology: the world is a *vivarta* (appearance) not a real transformation. [source: IEP-Adv §2a]
- **Īśvara**: Śaṅkara "generally distinguishes īśvara as the lower (apara) brahman qualified with attributes … from the higher … brahman that is absolute nonduality (BrSūBh 4.3.14)"; īśvara is both material and intelligent cause of the universe (BrSūBh 1.1.2). [source: SEP-Ś §2.4] (SEP text has a typo, printing "apara" for both; the higher is *para*.)
- **Superimposition (adhyāsa)**: we "mutually superimpose consciousness on to our mind-body, and the mind-body on to consciousness (i.e., I am the mind-body, and the mind-body is I)"; examples: a scratch in a mirror taken to mar one's face; a clear crystal looking red from a flower behind it; rope taken for a snake. The mind-body is a "conditioning adjunct (upādhi)". Seminal text: the introduction to his Brahma-sūtra commentary, the *Adhyāsabhāṣya*. [source: SEP-Ś §3.4]
- Śaṅkara's two characterisations of illusion in the BSBh introduction (as given by IEP-Adv): "an appearance of something previously experienced—like memory—in something else (smṛtirūpaḥ paratra pūrvadṛṣṭaḥ avabhāsaḥ)"; and minimally "the appearance of one thing with the properties of another". Padmapāda: adhyāsa without a substratum "has never been experienced and is inconceivable". [source: IEP-Adv §3a]
- **Avidyā** (ignorance) causes mutual superimposition and is "the root cause of all existential suffering"; only direct knowledge of oneself as brahman removes it. Contested later questions: is the locus of ignorance the individual or brahman? Does it have the twofold power of covering and projecting? Cosmic root ignorance vs individual ignorance? Relationship to māyā? These drew critiques from Rāmānuja and Madhva. [source: SEP-Ś §3.4]
- IEP-Adv on māyā vs avidyā in classical Advaita: māyā has cosmic and individual aspects; the cosmic belongs to īśvara, the individual (avidyā) to the many jīvas; īśvara controls māyā, the jīva is overpowered by avidyā. Śaṅkara called avidyā beginningless and held that searching for its origin is itself founded on avidyā. [source: IEP-Adv §2a]
- **Witness consciousness (sākṣin)** and **self-luminosity (svaprakāśa)**: see Argument 1 below. [source: SEP-Ś §§3.1–3.2]
- Reflection theory (*pratibimba/ābhāsa*; intellect as mirror) and limitation theory (*avaccheda*; pot-space and space) explain individuation; later Advaita sub-schools elaborated both. [source: SEP-Ś §3.3]
- **Liberation is knowledge, not action**: "One becomes brahman simply by knowing brahman (MuU 3.2.9)"; action presupposes agency and yields only finite results; Śaṅkara rejects the combination of ritual works and knowledge (*jñāna-karma-samuccaya*); action's role is to purify the mind. Fourfold qualification of the student (BSBh 1.1.1): discrimination of eternal and transient; dispassion; disciplines such as mind- and sense-control; desire for liberation. [source: SEP-Ś §§4, 4.1; IEP-Adv §3a]
- "Tenth man" story (TaiUBh 2.1.1): ten children cross a river, each counts only nine; a passer-by says "you are the tenth!" — illustrating "accomplishing the accomplished". [source: SEP-Ś §4]
- Great sentences (*mahāvākya*): "I am Brahman" (BU 1.4.10, *ahaṃ brahmāsmi*), "You are that" (ChU 6.8.7, *tat tvam asi*); post-Śaṅkara Advaitins focused on four. Methods: continuity and discontinuity (*anvaya-vyatireka*), secondary implication (*lakṣaṇā*; "This is that Devadatta"), negation (*neti neti*, "not this, not this"). [source: SEP-Ś §4.2]
- Six means of knowledge accepted in Advaita: perception, inference, testimony, comparison, postulation (arthāpatti), non-apprehension (anupalabdhi); brahman known only through śruti, aided by reason (yukti) and experience (anubhava). [source: IEP-Adv §3]
- Ethics caveat: Śaṅkara held that Vedic ritual slaughter, being Veda-sanctioned, "cannot be evil" (BSBh 3.1.25), and (per Ranganathan) that dharma "for the seeker of liberation … is an evil, for it ensnares a person" (Gītā Bhāṣya 4.21). [source: IEP-Rām §6] (Ranganathan's reading; flag as interpretation if used.)

## 5. Maṇḍana Miśra, Ubhaya Bhāratī, and later Advaita
- **Maṇḍana Miśra**: Mīmāṃsaka, follower of Kumārila Bhaṭṭa, older contemporary of Śaṅkara (WP lead says "c. 9th century" but the same article and WP-Adi_Shankara call him an older contemporary — use "contemporary of Śaṅkara"; IEP-Adv "eighth century"). Author of the *Brahmasiddhi* (his largest work, the only one wholly on Vedānta), and of the *Vibhramaviveka* (on theories of error) and *Sphoṭasiddhi*. Version of Advaita influenced by Bhartṛhari's sphoṭa theory; gave joint weight to knowledge and works and to meditation (*prasaṃkhyāna*). Until the 10th c. he "seems to have been the most authoritative Advaitin"; the Brahmasiddhi's theory of error "became the normative" Advaita one. [source: WP-Maṇḍana_Miśra; WP-Adi_Shankara; IEP-Adv §1]
- **Identification with Sureśvara** (Maṇḍana defeated by Śaṅkara, became his disciple Sureśvara, first head of Śṛṅgeri): from the hagiographies, which "postdate Shankara by more than five centuries". Kuppuswami Sastri: identity unlikely; doctrinal differences (locus of avidyā: jīva for Maṇḍana, brahman for Sureśvara; mahāvākya alone liberating for Sureśvara, meditation needed for Maṇḍana). Balasubramanian (1962): no conclusive evidence either way. Śṛṅgeri officially identifies them. [source: WP-Maṇḍana_Miśra] **Name as debate.**
- **Ubhaya Bhāratī — legend, not evidence.** In the *Mādhavīya Śaṅkaradigvijaya*, Maṇḍana's wife Ubhaya Bhāratī, identified as an incarnation of the goddess Sarasvatī, is appointed judge of the debate between Śaṅkara and Maṇḍana; she garlands both, the loser's garland to wither first; Maṇḍana's withers and he takes renunciation. She then debates Śaṅkara herself and questions him on the science of love (kāmaśāstra), which the celibate cannot answer; he asks for a month, enters the corpse of King Amaruka by yogic power, learns, and returns; she then accepts him. [source: WS-7 (sringeri.net abridgement of the Mādhavīya; WP-Ubhay_Bharati)]
  - **Evidence:** the only sources are hagiographies composed many centuries later (14th–18th c.; see §3); WP-Ubhay_Bharati presents the story as biography and gives her as an 8th-c. scholar from Mithilā, but cites no independent evidence; no scholarly source found confirming she existed. The Maṇḍana–Sureśvara identity on which the story depends is itself doubted. **Chapter wording: "In the later legend, Maṇḍana's wife Ubhaya Bhāratī judges the debate… No evidence outside the hagiographies survives; the story says more about the tradition's image of a learned woman than about the eighth century."** [source: WP-Ubhay_Bharati; WP-Maṇḍana_Miśra; WP-Adi_Shankara; WS-7]
- **Vācaspati Miśra**: 9th or 10th c. (earliest dated text 840 CE, or 976 CE depending on whether the Śaka or Vikrama era is used); from Mithilā; wrote on nearly every school (called *sarva-tantra-sva-tantra*); his *Bhāmatī* (on Śaṅkara's BSBh, named, by legend, after his wife) founded the **Bhāmatī** sub-school; he harmonised Śaṅkara with Maṇḍana ("faithful follower of Maṇḍana"). [source: WP-Vāchaspati_Misra; IEP-Adv §1] (WP calls him Maṇḍana's "student": doubtful chronologically; say "follower".) Consistent with published Classical Indian Schools chapter ("whose date is disputed, the ninth or tenth century").
- **Vivaraṇa** sub-school: named after Prakāśātman's (10th c.) *Vivaraṇa* on Padmapāda's *Pañcapādikā*. Main difference: locus of avidyā — Bhāmatī: the individual jīva; Vivaraṇa: brahman. [source: IEP-Adv §§1, 2a] (IEP-Adv §1 contains a slip: "Vivarana … written by Vacaspati Mshra" — wrong; the Vivaraṇa is Prakāśātman's.)
- Later names with centuries (IEP-Adv): Sarvajñātman (10th), Śrīharṣa (12th), Citsukha (12th), Vidyāraṇya (14th), Sadānanda (15th, *Vedāntasāra*), Madhusūdana Sarasvatī (17th, *Advaitasiddhi*), Dharmarāja Adhvarīndra (17th, *Vedāntaparibhāṣā*), Appaya Dīkṣita (17th). [source: IEP-Adv §1]
- **Śrīharṣa**: 12th c.; court poet (father at the Gāhaḍavāla court); wrote the poem *Naiṣadhacarita* (1174) and the *Khaṇḍanakhaṇḍakhādya* ("Sweets of Refutation"), a sustained dialectical attack on Nyāya definitions (of the six categories and of knowledge): every philosophical argument is subject to "refutation-arguments" (khaṇḍana-yukti). He keeps only self-revealing consciousness. Reason cannot prove dualism or nondualism; nonduality is known from the Upaniṣads. Whether he is straightforwardly an Advaitin "remains controversial among modern scholars"; influential on both Nyāya (Navya-Nyāya's precision) and Advaita. [source: WP-Shriharsha] (Comparison with Nāgārjuna's refutations of Nyāya is a WP "see also", not a scholarly claim.)

## 6. Rāmānuja and Viśiṣṭādvaita
- **Dates:** tradition 1017–1137 (a 120-year life); modern scholarship (Carman 1974, p. 27) c. 1077–1157. Sydnor: reconstruction of his historical life "nearly impossible"; Carman's dates "well-reasoned" but "ultimately unverifiable". [source: IEP-Rām §1; WP-Ramanuja]
- **Contradiction to record:** the published Upanishads chapter says "Rāmānuja (c. 1000)". That fits the traditional birth date (1017) but not the modern dating (1077–1157). Recommend the new chapter say "eleventh–twelfth century (traditionally 1017–1137; Carman 1077–1157)". [source: published text; IEP-Rām; WP-Ramanuja]
- Born Śrīperumbūdur near Chennai, Tamil region; first teacher Yādavaprakāśa at Kāñcīpuram (a Bhedābheda/Advaita-leaning Vedāntin); hagiographic tales of an assassination plot by his teacher; intended successor of Yāmuna (head of the Śrī Vaiṣṇava community formed around the Tamil hymns of the twelve Āḻvārs); became a renouncer (title *yatirāja*, "king of ascetics"); reformed temple worship (Śrīraṅgam). These life stories come from tradition. [source: IEP-Rām §1]
- **Works**: tradition credits nine Sanskrit works. Three main: *Vedārthasaṅgraha* (likely first), *Śrī Bhāṣya* (Brahma-sūtra commentary, magnum opus), *Gītā Bhāṣya*. Authenticity of the rest (two short BS commentaries, three *Gadya*s, *Nityagrantha*) questioned "in recent times", a "minority position". [source: IEP-Rām §1]
- He left no writings on the Tamil Āḻvārs and never mentions them, though disciples report he lectured on them in Tamil. [source: IEP-Rām §1]
- "Viśiṣṭādvaita" is a label applied by later tradition; usually "Qualified Non-Dualism"; Ranganathan prefers "Non-duality of the qualified whole". [source: IEP-Rām §2a]
- His opponents in the texts are anonymous *pūrvapakṣin*s; probably Yādavaprakāśa among others; he targets Advaita and Bhedābheda, "not clearly distinguished during his day". [source: IEP-Rām §2a]
- **Body–soul (śarīra–śarīrin) model:** Brahman is the Self of all because "we, along with all individuals, constitute modes or qualities of the body of Brahman. Thus, Brahman stands to all others as the soul or mind stands to its body." Also *śeṣa–śeṣin* (dependent–that on which it depends). [source: IEP-Rām §2d.iii] Scriptural root: BU 3.7 (the Inner Controller "whose body is all beings"). [source: WP-Ramanuja]
- **Co-ordinate predication (sāmānādhikaraṇya)**: Vedārthasaṅgraha §24 (as quoted by IEP): "The signification of an identical entity by several terms which are applied to that entity on different grounds is co-ordinate predication." Applied to *tat tvam asi*: "that" and "thou" co-refer to one reality while expressing distinct qualities — "thou" emphasises that we are modes within the underlying unity. Ranganathan says it "strikingly anticipates the Fregean distinction between sense and reference" (interpretive comparison; attribute). [source: IEP-Rām §2d.ii]
- Theism: Brahman = Viṣṇu-Nārāyaṇa with Śrī (Lakṣmī); free of all faults, with innumerable auspicious qualities (kalyāṇa-guṇa). No creation ex nihilo: souls and basic qualities are eternal features of God's body. [source: IEP-Rām §3]
- **Bhakti**: theoretical knowledge does not suffice; *bhakti-yoga* combines duty done for its own sake (karma-yoga) with constant worship; Rāmānuja's "unique contribution to Indian epistemology is the view that bhakti, or devotion, is itself an epistemic state", a kind of direct perception of Brahman (Vedārthasaṅgraha §238). [source: IEP-Rām §§4, 5c]
- *Prapatti/śaraṇāgati* (surrender) and the later split: within two centuries Śrī Vaiṣṇavism divided into Northern (Vaḍagalai; Vedānta Deśika; grace conditional on effort) and Southern (Teṅgalai; Maṇavāḷamāmuni; grace freely given; one act of surrender suffices). Robert Lester (1966) argued surrender doctrine appears only in the disputed *Gadya*s; others (and the tradition) read the works holistically; Vedārthasaṅgraha §99 speaks of "no way of emancipation, other than surrender to the supreme Lord". [source: IEP-Rām §7] **Debate.**
- WP: prapatti was "open to all individuals regardless of caste, status, or gender", unlike bhakti-yoga restricted by Vedic eligibility. [source: WP-Ramanuja] (Popular claim, tradition-dependent; use cautiously.)
- Rāmānuja nonetheless defended Vedic animal sacrifice as not really violence ("a healing act like a physician's procedure"; Śrī Bhāṣya 3.1.25). [source: IEP-Rām §6a]
- Influence: Potter: Rāmānuja's tradition is "one of the main arteries through which philosophy reached down to the masses, and it may be that Viśiṣṭādvaita is today the most powerful philosophy in India in terms of numbers of adherents". Van Buitenen: he gave "bhakti an intellectual basis"; Doniger: "probably the single most influential thinker of devotional Hinduism"; scholars compare his importance to Aquinas's. [source: IEP-Rām §8; WP-Ramanuja]

## 7. Rāmānuja against Advaita: arguments
- **Self-refutation argument** (IEP-Rām §2b.ii): opponents hold that all means of cognition involve error; then the cognition "all cognition involves error" is itself erroneous and not knowledge; so some cognitions are not erroneous — which the opponent's conclusion denies (Śrī Bhāṣya I.1.1, "Great Siddhānta"). [source: IEP-Rām]
- **Intentionality of consciousness** (*dharmabhūtajñāna*): consciousness is always consciousness of an object distinguished by a characteristic; so there is no pure, objectless consciousness, and if consciousness exists, difference exists. [source: IEP-Rām §2c.i]
- **Consciousness is a property** of a self, not the self's whole being; "truth, knowledge, infinite" (TU 2.1.1) name Brahman's qualities. [source: IEP-Rām §2c.ii]
- **Individuals are real**: to be ignorant presupposes an individual who is ignorant; Brahman, being knowledge, cannot be ignorant; so ignorance cannot explain the existence of individuals — "their individuality is ontologically and logically prior to their ignorance" (Śrī Bhāṣya I.1.1). [source: IEP-Rām §2c.iii]
- **Hermeneutic**: the Vedas are one corpus; Advaitins privilege late, monistic passages and ignore those presupposing plurality and ritual duty. [source: IEP-Rām §2d.i; WP-Ramanuja]
- WP summary of four main objections: Brahman is differentiated, not undifferentiated, consciousness; nirguṇa Brahman is untenable; beginningless karma, not superimposition, causes ignorance; avidyā/māyā has seven flaws. [source: WP-Ramanuja "Criticism of Sankara"]
- **The seven "untenables" (saptavidha anupapatti)** against avidyā, Śrī Bhāṣya I.1.1 — names as given in second-hand sources (Grimes, *The Seven Great Untenables*, Motilal Banarsidass; publisher blurbs and study notes): [source: WS-1, WS-2]
  1. *Āśraya* (locus): where does avidyā reside? Not in Brahman (pure consciousness); not in the jīva (which avidyā is supposed to produce).
  2. *Tirodhāna* (concealment): how can self-luminous Brahman be concealed? To conceal it would be to destroy its nature.
  3. *Svarūpa* (nature): is avidyā real or unreal? If real, dualism; if unreal, what causes it?
  4. *Anirvacanīya* (indescribability): "neither real nor unreal" violates excluded middle.
  5. *Pramāṇa* (evidence): no means of knowledge establishes a positive ignorance.
  6. *Nivartaka* (remover): what removes it? Knowledge of attributeless Brahman is not available / scripture teaches a qualified Brahman.
  7. *Nivṛtti* (removal): can a positive, beginningless entity be removed? Some lists merge 6 and 7, giving six.
  - **Flag:** wording of each item is reconstructed from secondary summaries, not from a translation of the Śrī Bhāṣya read in this session; the writer should keep the list to the names and one-line gist, attributed to "the tradition's standard list (Grimes)".
- **Advaitin replies** (sources): on locus, the Bhāmatī says the jīva, the Vivaraṇa says Brahman (IEP-Adv §2a); Śaṅkara himself treats "Whose is avidyā?" (BSBh 4.1.3, Gītā Bh. 13.2) by denying any real connection between avidyā and the self while not affirming avidyā's reality — Ingalls (1953, *Philosophy East and West* 3(1): 69–72) argues Śaṅkara "avoids the dilemma" of real/unreal, whereas followers answered with a third status, *anirvacanīyatva* (SEP-Ś biblio; WS-3); on indescribability, Maṇḍana's argument that avidyā cannot be real (or it would not be ignorance) nor absolutely unreal (or it could not enter practical life, unlike a sky-flower) (WP-Maṇḍana_Miśra); on concealment, SEP-Ś's line that "both bondage and liberation are just figurative—no change occurs in reality" (SEP-Ś §4); the systematic Advaitin rejoinder is Madhusūdana Sarasvatī's *Advaitasiddhi* (17th c.) (WS-2, forum-level; IEP-Adv names the work). [sources as named]

## 8. Madhva and Dvaita
- **Dates:** IEP-Madh: 1238–1317. WP: "1199–1278 CE or 1238–1317 CE"; "Many sources date him to 1238–1317 period, but some place him about the 1199–1278 period." [source: IEP-Madh; WP-Madhvacharya] Published Upanishads chapter: "Madhva (c. 1200)" — compatible with the earlier dating only; recommend "thirteenth century (1238–1317 on the usual dating)". WP-Advaita_Vedanta calls Dvaita "14th-century" — wrong by either dating; do not use.
- Born at Pājaka near Udupi (coastal Karnataka); names Vāsudeva, Pūrṇaprajña, Ānandatīrtha; joined an Advaita monastery under Acyutaprekṣa, disagreed, left; credited with 37 works, terse style; chief work the *Anuvyākhyāna*; established the Kṛṣṇa maṭha at Udupi (WP: 1285, with an image from Dvārakā) and eight maṭhas there. He claimed to be an avatar of the wind-god Vāyu. [source: WP-Madhvacharya; IEP-Madh intro]
- His own name for his system: *tattvavāda* ("the realist viewpoint"/"arguments from a realist viewpoint"). [source: IEP-Madh; WP-Madhvacharya]
- **Two orders of reality**: *svatantra* (independent) — Brahman/Viṣṇu alone; *paratantra* (dependent) — souls (jīva) and insentient matter (jaḍa). Dependence itself makes them fundamentally different from God. [source: IEP-Madh intro, §3]
- **Five differences (pañcabheda)** (IEP-Madh list): between (1) Viṣṇu and souls, (2) Viṣṇu and matter, (3) soul and matter, (4) one soul and another, (5) one material thing and another. The Dvaita school calls the world *prapañca*, reading *pañca* ("five") in it (traditional folk-etymology). [source: IEP-Madh §3; WP-Dvaita_Vedanta]
- *Tāratamya* (gradation): no two souls alike; even in liberation bliss differs by soul. [source: WP-Madhvacharya]
- **Argument from perception** (IEP-Madh §3): our experience of separateness from God and of plurality is "an undeniable fact"; Advaita's denial of the intrinsic validity of perception undermines all knowledge, including knowledge of scripture, which reaches us only through perception and inference; so perception and inference must be valid, and scripture cannot teach non-difference, since that would contradict experience. [source: IEP-Madh §3]
- Three means of knowledge: perception, inference, testimony (āgama). [source: WP-Madhvacharya]
- **Tat tvam asi**: IEP-Madh: "By carrying over the 'a' from the preceding word, Madhva rendered the phrase atat tvam asi or 'you are not that.'" WP (citing B. N. K. Sharma) frames his reinterpretation as stressing difference, the "special immanence of the Deity". [source: IEP-Madh §4; WP-Madhvacharya] (The "atat" reading is IEP's description; consistent with published Upanishads chapter. Present it as "on one reading of the passage".)
- **Fierce critic of Advaita**: accused Śaṅkara and the Advaitins of "teaching Buddhism under the cover of Vedanta"; wrote four major anti-Advaita texts including *Upādhikhaṇḍana* and *Tattvoddyota*; argued against twenty-one earlier scholars. [source: WP-Madhvacharya]
- Later Dvaita: Jayatīrtha and Vyāsatīrtha/Vyāsarāya; centre at Udupi. IEP-Madh gives Jayatīrtha 1356–1388 and Vyāsarāya 1478–1589 — **the latter implies a 111-year life; not verified; do not use dates.** [source: IEP-Madh §4]
- **Predestination and eternal damnation (controversial — flag):** three classes of souls: *mukti-yogya* (fit for liberation), *nitya-saṃsārin* (eternally transmigrating), *tamo-yogya* (destined for eternal hell, Andhatamisra). Souls have innate natures and "were predestined to achieve certain ends"; karma "is merely the mechanism by which a given soul is propelled towards his or her destiny" — which put him "at odds with traditional Hindu views of the karma theory". Advaita and Viśiṣṭādvaita assume all souls can be liberated; Madhva held some are eternally damned. WP: this "differed significantly from traditional Hindu beliefs". [source: IEP-Madh §3; WP-Dvaita_Vedanta; WP-Madhvacharya; WP-Advaita_Vedanta citing Deepak Sarma in Bryant 2007, pp. 361–362]
  - Problem of evil: Sharma says the tripartite classification "makes it unnecessary to answer the problem of evil"; Buchta objects that an omnipotent God "could change the system, but chooses not to". [source: WP-Madhvacharya] (Attribute to these scholars.)
- **Christianity comparison (flag):** colonial-era writers (G. A. Grierson) claimed Madhva's ideas were "borrowed from Christianity"; S. C. Vasu translated him so as to identify him with Christ (Deepak Sarma, "Is Jesus a Hindu? S.C. Vasu and Multiple Madhva Misrepresentations", *Journal of Hindu-Christian Studies* 13, 2000). Parallels cited: one supreme God, God–soul distinction, devotion, Vāyu as intermediary "son of God", predestination, grace, miracle legends. **Modern scholarship rejects influence**: no evidence of a Christian settlement or contact where he lived (WP); IEP-Madh: "current scholarly consensus maintains that political and social changes in Madhva's region prompted a new approach"; von Glasenapp: monotheism derivable from Indian thought. [source: WP-Madhvacharya; IEP-Madh §4] **Chapter should say: the parallels (especially predestination and eternal hell) invite comparison with Christian, especially Augustinian/Calvinist, doctrine, but the influence theory is rejected.** (The specific "Calvinist" comparison was NOT found in a source read; see NOT VERIFIED.)

## 9. Theories of error (khyātivāda) compared
- All the theories ask what an erroneous cognition such as "this is silver" (of mother-of-pearl/shell) is *about* — the ontological status of the object of error. A traditional verse lists five: ātmakhyāti, asatkhyāti, akhyāti, anyathākhyāti, anirvacanīyakhyāti. [source: WP-Khyativada; WS-4]
- **Ātmakhyāti** (Yogācāra Buddhists): an inner cognition appears as if external; no objects outside consciousness. [source: WS-4 (Vidyasagar University paper)] — consistent with SEP-Epi on the Yogācāra argument from illusion: "A rope can be perceived as a snake, with no difference, from the perspective of the perceiver". [source: SEP-Epi §2]
- **Asatkhyāti** (attributed to Mādhyamikas/some Buddhists): something non-existent appears as existent. [source: WS-4] (Attribution varies between sources; keep general.)
- **Akhyāti** (Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā): there is no error proper, only a failure to distinguish a perception ("this") from a memory ("silver") — "non-apprehension of distinction" (vivekākhyāti). SEP-Epi: Prābhākara "no-illusion" or "omission" theory. [source: WP-Khyativada; SEP-Epi §2]
- **Anyathākhyāti** (Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika): the silver is real elsewhere; a memory of real silverness is "misplaced" onto the shell; Vācaspati: "one reality is mistaken for another". Kumārila's Bhāṭṭa variant is *viparītakhyāti*. [source: WP-Khyativada; SEP-Epi §2]
- **Anirvacanīyakhyāti** (Advaita): the illusory object is neither existent nor non-existent but indeterminable, a product of ignorance of the substratum; illusion always needs a real substratum (Padmapāda; Vācaspati: no illusion where the substratum is fully known or not known at all). Indeterminacy is the "illegitimate transference" of one order's qualities to another; perceptual illusion is the "bridge" to soteriology — liberation relates to everyday experience as veridical to delusive perception. [source: IEP-Adv §3a; WP-Khyativada]
- **Rāmānuja**: error is not apprehension of an unreal object. Per IEP-Rām §5d, erroneous experiences (e.g. dreams) are real events, but "the objects that the experience claims to be about are absent"; mistaking mother-of-pearl for silver is "the mistaken cognition that the object perceived is a piece of silver". Ranganathan likens this to Russell's "On Denoting" (interpretive). WP: "Knowledge is always of the real, even in dreams, and error is a disordered perception or faulty inference concerning what is really there." His school's name for its theory: *yathārthakhyāti* / *satkhyāti* ("apprehension of the real") — name reported second-hand (WS-5), not read in a scholarly source.
- **Madhva**: *abhinava-anyathākhyāti* ("new misapprehension"): the illusory silver is wholly non-existent (Jayatīrtha: *atyanta-asat*), yet it is cognised as existing in the real substratum (the shell); B. N. K. Sharma (*Philosophy of Śrī Madhvācārya*, p. 196) presents it as combining asatkhyāti and anyathākhyāti. Jayatīrtha rejects Prabhākara's fusion-of-perception-and-memory view. [source: WS-5 (second-hand: quotations of Sharma and Madhva's *Viṣṇutattvavinirṇaya* on mailing lists/blogs); WP-Khyativada (Jayatīrtha vs Prabhākara)] **Flag second-hand.**
- Why it matters: Advaita needs error to be a real appearance on a real ground (the rope), so that the world can be an appearance on brahman; the realists (Nyāya, Rāmānuja, Madhva) analyse error so as never to need an indeterminable third status. [synthesis of IEP-Adv §3a, IEP-Rām §5d, IEP-Madh §3]
- Sublation (bādha): Advaita's ultimate test of truth is non-sublatability; *Vedāntaparibhāṣā*: "that knowledge is valid which has for its object something that is nonsublated"; rope–snake is the stock example; "Only wrong cognitions can be sublated." Lesser tests: correspondence, coherence, practical efficacy; further criterion "foundationality" (anadhigatatva, "not known earlier"), which only self-knowledge passes. [source: IEP-Adv §3]

## 10. The "crypto-Buddhist" charge (pracchanna-bauddha)
- Richard King (1995, p. 183, via WP): "It is well-known that Sankara was criticized by later (rival) Vedantins as a crypto-Buddhist (pracchana bauddha)." [source: WP-Adi_Shankara note]
- Bhāskara (Bhedābheda Vedāntin, c. 800 / 9th c.) called Advaita "this despicable broken down Māyāvāda that has been chanted by the Mahāyāna Buddhists". [source: WP-Adi_Shankara; WP-Advaita_Vedanta] (Quoted second-hand from WP; translator unknown.)
- Rāmānuja "accused Adi Shankara of being a Prachanna Bauddha" (WP citing Biderman 1978 and Isaeva 1993); Madhva accused Advaitins of "teaching Buddhism under the cover of Vedanta" (WP-Madhvacharya). Dasgupta mentions such accusations "by Vijñāna Bhikṣu and others". [source: WP-Adi_Shankara; WP-Madhvacharya; WP-Advaita_Vedanta] (No primary passage was read; attribute as "according to modern scholars".)
- **The case for influence** (scholars): S. N. Dasgupta: "Shankara and his followers borrowed much of their dialectic form of criticism from the Buddhists. His Brahman was very much like the sunya of Nagarjuna … The debts of Shankara to the self-luminosity of the Vijnanavada Buddhism can hardly be overestimated … I am led to think that Shankara's philosophy is largely a compound of Vijnanavada and Sunyavada Buddhism with the Upanisad notion of the permanence of self superadded." Mudgal: the difference "may be a matter of emphasis, not of kind". Isaeva: Śaṅkara incorporated "a Buddhist notion of māyā which had not been minutely elaborated in the Upanishads". Gauḍapāda's fourth chapter uses Buddhist terminology. Shared monastic practice (Whaling). [source: WP-Adi_Shankara; WP-Advaita_Vedanta; WP-Gaudapada]
- **The case against / Advaita's reply**: the tradition "historically rejected accusations of crypto-Buddhism highlighting their respective views on Ātman, Anattā, and Brahman": Advaita posits a permanent self (substance ontology), Buddhism no-self and dependent origination (process ontology) (Bartley); Advaita accepts six pramāṇas incl. scripture, Buddhist epistemologists two. [source: WP-Advaita_Vedanta] Śaṅkara himself argues against Buddhist schools: he rejects Yogācāra idealism (BSBh 2.2.28–31), calls the Madhyamaka view nihilistic, and attacks the Buddhist self-illumination thesis (a cognition revealing itself) by the "knife cannot cut itself" reflexivity objection (BSBh 2.2.28). [source: SEP-Ś §§2, 2.1, 3.2]
- Suthren Hirst and others: Śaṅkara's Advaita "may stem from a Vaishnavite Advaita tradition". [source: WP-Adi_Shankara]
- **Chapter wording: present as a live debate — rivals' polemical label vs. modern scholars on borrowing vs. Advaitin replies about the self.** Link to published Mahāyāna chapter (Yogācāra self-awareness; Nāgārjuna's emptiness) and Early Buddhism chapter (no-self).

## 11. ARGUMENTS (step by step)
### Argument 1 — Śaṅkara: the self is nondual consciousness (from the witness and self-luminosity) [source: SEP-Ś §§2.1, 3.1–3.3, 4.2]
Caveat first: Śaṅkara holds nonduality is ultimately known from scripture, not proved; these are supporting arguments (SEP-Ś §1).
1. The real is what does not change its nature; what persists through change is more real than what does not, and what depends is less real than what it depends on (clay–pot). [§2.1]
2. Waking, dreaming and deep sleep exhaust human experience and exclude one another, but consciousness persists through all three (anvaya–vyatireka: present when each state is present, still present when it is absent). [§3.1, §4.2]
3. So consciousness is not any of these states or their contents, and it is the changeless element in experience. [§3.1]
4. Consciousness is self-luminous: it is known without a second cognition — if every cognition needed another to reveal it, there would be a vicious regress "and no perception could ever be known" (against Nyāya's other-illumination). [§3.2]
5. Consciousness cannot be objectified, and cannot be denied, since "any dismissal of consciousness presupposes its very existence" (BSBh 2.3.7). [§3.2]
6. Everything objective (body, senses, mind, the I-notion, even time and space) is an object of the witness and so not the self; the felt identity with mind-body is superimposition. [§3.3, §3.4]
7. Existence, sought in objects, is never found in any form (endless regress of parts and causes) yet never disappears; it is formless, partless and changeless — the same marks as pure consciousness. [§2.1, §3.3]
- C. "Non-reducible existence is numerically identical to pure non-intentional consciousness"; this is brahman, the single self of all beings; plurality is appearance. [§3.3]
- **Objection** (Nyāya; Rāmānuja): consciousness is intrinsically intentional and a property of a self; in deep sleep there is no consciousness (so premise 2 fails); and there is no "pure" consciousness without objects, so difference is real. [SEP-Ś §3.1; IEP-Rām §2c]
- **Reply** (SEP-Ś §3.1): the absence of intentional experience in deep sleep does not show absence of consciousness, since consciousness is not intrinsically intentional; one cannot perceive one's own unconsciousness, nor infer it inductively; in deep sleep there is "simply an absence of objects … akin to having one's eyes wide open and seeing but in a pitch-black room."
- **Counter-objection** (Buddhists: Vasubandhu, Dharmakīrti): a cognition can reveal itself and its object; no witness needed. Śaṅkara's reply: self-revealing cognition falls to the reflexivity fallacy ("Just as a knife cannot cut itself"), and we are aware of cognitions arising and perishing, which requires an illuminator distinct from them; the witness does not "objectify itself", so it escapes the same fallacy. [SEP-Ś §3.2] (Note: the published Early Buddhism chapter uses the same irreflexivity principle — "a knife cannot cut itself" — in Siderits's reconstruction of the Buddhist argument from control; a nice cross-link.)

### Argument 2 — The sublation argument for the world's lesser reality [source: IEP-Adv §§2a, 2b, 3; SEP-Ś §§2.2–2.3]
1. A cognition is true if its object is never sublated (bādhita); only false cognitions are sublated (*Vedāntaparibhāṣā*).
2. The snake seen in a rope is experienced (so not a hare's horn), yet sublated by "it is a rope" (so not real): it is neither real nor unreal — indeterminable (anirvacanīya).
3. Illusion always requires a real substratum (Padmapāda).
4. The world is experienced and intersubjectively agreed (so not absolutely unreal) but, on Upaniṣadic testimony, is sublated by knowledge of brahman (so not ultimately real).
- C. The world has the same indeterminable status as the snake: an appearance (vivarta) on brahman, its substratum; empirical reality is real for practical purposes (vyāvahārika), not ultimately (pāramārthika).
- **Objection** (Rāmānuja's svarūpa and anirvacanīya untenables; Madhva): "neither real nor unreal" violates the excluded middle; and denying perception's ultimate validity undermines the perception by which we learn scripture. [WS-1; IEP-Madh §3]
- **Reply**: Advaitins deny it is a contradiction — the two levels are asymmetrical ("This hierarchy of two orders and their asymmetrical relationship avoids their mutual contradiction"); the world is "not identical to brahman … but is not different either—it does not constitute a second reality". [SEP-Ś §§2.2, 2.3]

### Argument 3 — Rāmānuja's argument that ignorance presupposes individuals [source: IEP-Rām §2c.iii]
1. All Vedāntins agree Brahman is of the nature of knowledge.
2. Whatever is ignorant is a knower capable of error.
3. Brahman cannot be ignorant (contradiction with 1).
4. So the subject of ignorance is an individual other than Brahman.
5. But Advaita uses ignorance to explain why there seem to be individuals.
- C. The explanation is circular; individuals are real and prior to their ignorance.
- **Reply** (Advaita): Bhāmatī locates avidyā in the jīva and Vivaraṇa in brahman (IEP-Adv §2a); Śaṅkara treats the question "Whose is avidyā?" as itself arising within avidyā — beginningless, so its origin cannot be sought (IEP-Adv §2a; Ingalls 1953, WS-3).

## 13. Consistency with published Periergia texts
- **Upanishads chapter**: "Śaṅkara (c. 700 CE), Rāmānuja (c. 1000) and Madhva (c. 1200)". Śaṅkara: consistent. **Rāmānuja c. 1000: contradicts modern dating 1077–1157; only near the traditional 1017.** **Madhva c. 1200: matches only the minority 1199–1278 dating; the usual (IEP) dating is 1238–1317.** Recommend the Vedānta chapter give both and, if possible, flag for a correction pass on the Upanishads chapter. [source: published text; IEP-Rām; IEP-Madh; WP]
- Upanishads chapter: "The famous reading of tat tvam asi as declaring the self identical with brahman is Śaṅkara's, centuries later. Madhva's school even divided the words to read 'you are not that'." Consistent with IEP-Madh §4 and SEP-Ś §4.2. It also reports Brereton's grammatical argument ("in that way you are") — the Vedānta chapter can note that all three Vedāntins read the sentence within their own systems.
- Upanishads chapter says the single doctrine "came later" when the three Vedāntins wrote commentaries — consistent.
- Classical Indian Schools chapter: Vācaspati Miśra "whose date is disputed, the ninth or tenth century"; wrote on Nyāya, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta — consistent with WP-Vāchaspati_Misra. Kumārila "seventh century" — consistent with Maṇḍana as his follower and Śaṅkara's c. 700 date. Sāṃkhya holds "against Vedānta, that the world's evolution is real" — consistent (Brahma-sūtra 1 rejects pradhāna).
- Mahāyāna chapter: "The Nyāya commentator Vātsyāyana, and the Vedāntin Śaṅkara, replied that illusion is parasitic on veridical cognition" — consistent with SEP-Ś (Śaṅkara rejects Yogācāra, BSBh 2.2.28–31).
- Early Buddhism chapter: Siderits's irreflexivity principle ("a knife cannot cut itself") — Śaṅkara uses the same principle against Buddhist self-illumination (SEP-Ś §3.2). Not a contradiction; cross-link.
- No contradictions found on the Brahma-sūtra or Gauḍapāda (not covered in published texts).

## Debates
- **Śaṅkara's date**: 788–820 (19th-c. scholarship, Tiele/Müller/Radhakrishnan) vs c. 700–750 (Nakamura; Koller) vs late 7th–early 8th (Potter, from Maṇḍana's date); maṭha tradition 5th c. BCE. [source: SEP-Ś §1; WP-Adi_Shankara]
- **Authenticity of works** (Hacker's criteria; Mayeda; Sundaresan's critique; Vivekacūḍāmaṇi: Comans/Isayeva/Mayeda vs Grimes). [source: SEP-Ś §1; WP-Vivekacūḍāmaṇi]
- **Does māyā as cosmic material cause belong to Śaṅkara or to later Advaita?** (Hacker on his rare use of "māyā"; Comans; Doherty; Satchidanandendra Sarasvatī, who held that most post-Śaṅkara Advaita deviates from him — Potter: "there is good reason to think that it may well be true"). [source: SEP-Ś §2.2; WP-Adi_Shankara]
- **World-negating or world-affirming?** Pejorative *māyāvāda* vs SEP-Ś/Rambachan's reading of Śaṅkara as ambivalent and ethically engaged. [source: SEP-Ś §2.2; IEP-Adv §2 ("On some interpretations, Advaita Vedānta appears as a nihilistic philosophy")]
- **Locus of avidyā**: Bhāmatī (jīva) vs Vivaraṇa (brahman); Ingalls on "Whose is avidyā?". [source: IEP-Adv §2a; WS-3]
- **Crypto-Buddhism**: rivals' charge; Dasgupta/Mudgal/Isaeva on borrowing; tradition's denial. [source: §10]
- **Maṇḍana = Sureśvara?** Tradition yes; Kuppuswami Sastri, Hiriyanna no; Balasubramanian undecided. [source: WP-Maṇḍana_Miśra]
- **Who mattered more early on?** King, Clark, Roodurmun: Maṇḍana overshadowed Śaṅkara until the 10th c. [source: WP-Adi_Shankara]
- **Is the Brahma-sūtra Advaitic?** Mayeda: possibly Bhedābheda. [source: WP-Brahma_Sutras]
- **Rāmānuja and surrender (prapatti)**: Lester (Gadyas spurious) vs holistic/traditional reading; Vaḍagalai vs Teṅgalai. [source: IEP-Rām §7]
- **Śrīharṣa**: Advaitin or sceptic? [source: WP-Shriharsha]
- **Madhva and Christianity**: Grierson's influence thesis vs modern rejection. [source: WP-Madhvacharya; IEP-Madh]
- **Madhva's dates**: 1238–1317 vs 1199–1278. [source: WP-Madhvacharya]
