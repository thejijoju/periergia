## 1. The problem and the constraints on any answer
- A definition of art in analytic philosophy typically proposes necessary and sufficient conditions for something to fall under "art"; this is distinct from the ontological question of what kind of thing an artwork is. IEP opens with real puzzle cases: video games, a six-year-old's painting, a mall Christmas tree, "a banana taped to a wall" (Cattelan's *Comedian*). [source: IEP-Def intro, §6]
- IEP sorts definitions into six families: (1) classical (mimesis, expressivism, formalism); (2) functionalism (aesthetic function, a revived formalism); (3) scepticism; (4) relational (institutionalism, historicism); (5) determinable-determinate ("buck-passing", Lopes); (6) disjunctivism. [source: IEP-Def intro]
- IEP's criteria for a good definition: include (i) mediocre art, (ii) art by people who lack the concept of art, (iii) avant-garde, future or possible (e.g. extraterrestrial) art; exclude (iv) purely natural objects, (v) complete failures, (vi) non-art artefacts even with aesthetic functions (a nicely designed shoelace); and (vii) account for borderline cases (children's drawings, lullabies, animal paintings, Christmas trees, rituals, jokes). [source: IEP-Def §1]
- Dickie (1969) distinguished descriptive ("archaeologists found tools, clothes and artworks") from evaluative uses ("your couscous is a real artwork!"); definitions aim at the descriptive (classificatory) sense so as to include bad art. [source: IEP-Def §1a, §4a]
- SEP lists "uncontroversial facts" any definition must fit, including: artefacts intentionally given significant aesthetic interest "first appeared hundreds of thousands of years ago and exist in virtually every known human culture" (citing Davies 2012); they are "partially comprehensible to cultural outsiders"; they sometimes have ceremonial, religious or propagandistic functions; some cultures but not all have institutions for objects of aesthetic interest with no practical or religious use; such institutions group objects lacking aesthetic interest with objects having it; sunsets, faces, proofs also have aesthetic properties. [source: SEP-Def §1]
- SEP: enumerative (list-like) definitions are to be avoided if possible since they explain nothing and do not extend to new cases; non-disjunctive definitions are preferable, other things equal; definitions allowing borderline cases are preferable. [source: SEP-Def §1]
- SEP's overall map: conventionalist definitions (institutional, historical) handle modern art well but struggle with art's universality (art outside "our" Western institutions, or our species); aesthetic definitions handle universality but struggle with revolutionary modern art. [source: SEP-Def §§4, 5]
- Closing quotation in SEP from Kendall Walton (2007): "It is not at all clear that these words – 'What is art?' – express anything like a single question, to which competing answers are given, or whether philosophers proposing answers are even engaged in the same debate…." [source: SEP-Def §5]

## 2. Imitation (mimesis): Plato
- *Mimêsis* is most often translated "imitation"; also "representation" and "emulation". Pappas: "imitation" is serviceable if one remembers it does not mean everything *mimêsis* does. *Mimêma* = the product only (a copy). [source: SEP-PlAes §2]
- Before Plato, Aristophanes used *mimeisthai/mimêsis* pejoratively of tragic stagecraft (e.g. *Women at the Thesmophoria*); Pappas sees Aristophanes as a precursor of Plato's critique. [source: SEP-PlAes §2.1]
- **Republic III (impersonation).** Homer alternates narration in his own voice with speeches in which he "makes himself like" the character, speaking "as if he were someone else" (393b); the poet "hides himself" (393d); example: Agamemnon's rebuke to Chryses (393a–c). Main argument: performing base types fosters the traits imitated (395c–397e); the list of things unworthy of imitation includes women, slaves, animals, instruments, pulleys and sounds of water. [source: SEP-PlAes §2.2]
- Republic 398a (Pappas's translation): a man whose wisdom lets him "become everything and to mimic all things" would be worshipped "as someone holy and wonderful and pleasant", told there is no such man in the city, and sent "off to another city after pouring myrrh on his head and crowning him with wool." [source: SEP-PlAes §2.2]
- INTERPRETIVE DISAGREEMENT on how much Book III bans: Pappas reads 398a as banning "all mimetic works"; Griswold says only part of imitative poetry was banned in Book III (398a1–b4) and that at 595a5 Socrates "mischaracterizes the results of the earlier discussion" by claiming all imitative poetry was banished. [source: SEP-PlAes §2.2; SEP-PlRhet §3.3]
- **Republic X (copy-making), 595a–608b.** Three theses (Pappas): (1) poetic mimêsis, like painting's, imitates appearance only and ranks far below truth (596e–602c); (2) so it corrupts the soul, weakening reason's control (602c–608b); (3) so it should be banned from the good city. [source: SEP-PlAes §2.3]
- The bed/couch hierarchy: Form (made by a god) → particular couches (made by a carpenter looking "toward" the Form, 596b) → paintings (made by imitators). The painter paints not the couch but its appearance from one viewpoint (598a); a painted shoemaker is drawn by someone ignorant of shoemaking (598b–c). Second triad (601c–602a): user who knows → maker with correct belief → ignorant imitator (flute, bridle). [source: SEP-PlAes §2.3; SEP-PlRhet §3.3]
- "Third remove": the imitator is "third from the king and the truth" (597e; SEP-PlRhet gives 597e3–4, 6–7, also "at the third generation from nature"); the language echoes Book IX, where the tyrant is "at the third remove" and his pleasure "a third-place idol" (587c). [source: SEP-PlAes §2.3; SEP-PlRhet §3.3]
- IMPORTANT NUANCE (Pappas): Book X never calls the carpenter's couch an imitation, so the painting is not, in Plato's own terms, "the popular phrase" an "imitation of an imitation"; it is an "imitation of appearance". The Greek counting is inclusive ("third"), whereas popular English summaries say "twice removed" (WP-Mimesis does). Both describe the same position. [source: SEP-PlAes §2.3; WP-Mimesis]
- Homer as "the first teacher and leader of all these fine tragic things" (595b10–c2); "it is necessary that the good poet, if he is going to make fair poems about the things his poetry concerns, be in possession of knowledge when he makes his poems" (598e3–5); Homer fails examination on governance and education (599c–600e). [source: SEP-PlRhet §3.3 (translation apparently Bloom's; Griswold cites Bloom 1968)]
- Psychological argument: a painting's illusion fools one part of us while reason knows better; by Book IV's principle of opposites (436b, recalled 602e) the deceived part is not reason. Tragedy shows a hero weeping publicly for a dead son (603e–604a); spectators' reason is appalled but other impulses rejoice (605c–e); "the pitying part [of the soul], fed strong on these examples, is not easily held down in one's own sufferings" (606b); comedy likewise (606c). [source: SEP-PlAes §2.3; SEP-PlRhet §3.3]
- Exemption: hymns to the gods and encomia of good men are allowed (607a). [source: SEP-PlAes §§3, 4]
- The quarrel: "there is an old quarrel between philosophy and poetry" (*Republic* 607b5–6), supported by quoted poetic insults against philosophers ("yelping bitch shrieking at her master", "great in the empty eloquence of fools"). Translator not named in SEP-PlRhet; Griswold's citations elsewhere follow Allan Bloom (1968). Glenn Most (2011) asked "What Ancient Quarrel between Philosophy and Poetry?" (some identify comedy as the antagonist). [source: SEP-PlRhet §1; SEP-PlAes §2.1; search]
- Conclusion of the Book X critique: "We are, at all events, aware that such poetry mustn't be taken seriously as a serious thing laying hold of truth, but that the man who hears it must be careful, fearing for the regime in himself" (608a6–b2). [source: SEP-PlRhet §3.4]
- SEP-Def summary: for Plato artworks present "only an appearance of an appearance of the Forms"; artistic experience cannot yield knowledge; makers do not work from knowledge; the arts are not for Plato the primary sphere of beauty (the Form of Beauty is closer to the erotic). [source: SEP-Def §2.1]

## 3. Imitation: Aristotle's *Poetics* (keep brief; cross-reference *Aristotle*)
- *Poetics* 4 opens: poetry has two natural causes — the instinct all humans have for mimesis and the pleasure they take in objects of mimesis (1448b4–5); plus an instinct for melody and rhythm (1448b20–21). What the two causes are exactly is disputed. [source: SEP-ArAes §3.1]
- Butcher's wording (confirmed only via search snippets of the Gutenberg Butcher text): "the instinct of imitation is implanted in man from childhood", man being "the most imitative of living creatures" who learns his earliest lessons through imitation. [source: search; cf. ancient--aristotle.md]
- The abject-animal paradox: seeing such an animal is painful, seeing a picture of it pleasant. Destrée's translation: "Seeing a likeness is pleasurable, because in contemplating it, people come to understand (*manthanein*) through inference, what each of its details are: for example, that the man there is so-and-so" (1448b15–17); "In case you never saw the man before, you will not derive any pleasure from his likeness qua representation. But you will get pleasure from the brilliant execution or the colours, or for some other such reason" (1448b17–19). [source: SEP-ArAes §3.1]
- DEBATE: "cognitivist" reading (pleasure of art = pleasure of learning; Gallop 1990 even reads "so-and-so" as an essence) vs. deflationary reading (Lear 1988; Ferrari 1999): the "understanding" is mere recognition of someone you already know. [source: SEP-ArAes §3.1]
- Aristotle also counts music as mimesis: music can resemble movements of the soul and imitate emotions and character (Politics 1340a); so for the ancients "there is no such thing as non-representational art" (IEP's gloss). [source: IEP-Def §2a.i]
- Poetry "is more philosophical and more worthy than writing history. For poetry tends towards a general picture (*katholou*), whereas history tells us of particular case studies" (9, 1451b5–7, Destrée's translation); whether *katholou* means "universals" in the technical sense is contested. [source: SEP-ArAes §3]
- Aristotle and Plato: no explicit rebuttal of Plato survives, but "it is difficult not to take Aristotle as responding to Plato's critique"; a fragment of the lost *On Poets* notes Socratic dialogues are themselves mimetic (Athenaeus took this as a jab at Plato). [source: SEP-ArAes §§1, 2]
- Catharsis (one paragraph only): appears in the definition of tragedy (6, 1449b25–28); Aristotle never explains it. Politics VIII 7, 1342a11–15: those prone to pity and fear "undergo a certain katharsis and get a pleasant feeling of relief". Readings: medical purgation (Jakob Bernays; his nephew by marriage Freud adapted it); purgation to a virtuous mean (Janko 1992; but Politics VIII separates cathartic music from educational music); "clarification" (Martha Nussbaum; no textual proof); "consolation" (Jonathan Lear 1988); minimalist: release of tension (Ferrari 1999, 2019) or physical outlet of emotion such as audiences' weeping, by analogy with Aristotle's biological use of *katharsis* for discharges. [source: SEP-ArAes §3.3]
- Date discrepancy: SEP-ArAes dates Bernays' *Grundzüge der verlorenen Abhandlung des Aristoteles über die Wirkung der Tragödie* to 1858 (Breslau); ancient--aristotle.md (from WP-Catharsis) gives 1857. Say "in the late 1850s" or omit the year. [source: SEP-ArAes bibliography; ancient--aristotle.md]

## 4. Imitation theory as a definition, and its problems
- Mimesis dominated for "around two millennia" (IEP's phrase). Charles Batteux, *Les Beaux-arts réduits à un même principe* (1746), popularised "fine arts" (*beaux-arts*) and held their essence to be imitation of beautiful nature; he said non-representational music or dance "goes astray" (*s'égarer*), just as concerto and sonata were emerging. [source: IEP-Def §2a.ii]
- Objection 1 (not necessary): abstract, non-representational art exists (IEP example: Yayoi Kusama's infinity nets); instrumental music. Batteux's verdict on music conflates descriptive and evaluative senses. [source: IEP-Def §2a.iii]
- Reply: broad mimesis — Roger Scruton (1999) and Philippe Schlenker (2017) argue all music, however abstract, represents or is a metaphor for real-world events (movement, emotional change); criticised by Zangwill (2010). [source: IEP-Def §2a.iii]
- Objection 2 (not sufficient): passports, souvenir key rings, any meaningful sentence, instruction manuals are representations but not art. [source: IEP-Def §2a.iii; SEP-Def §2]
- SEP caution: classical definitions are embedded in whole philosophical systems, so "brief summaries are invariably somewhat misleading". [source: SEP-Def §2]

## 5. Expression: Tolstoy
- *What Is Art?* (Russian *Chto takoye iskusstvo?*) was completed in Russian in 1897 but first published in English in 1898 because of the Russian censors. (IEP cites it as 1898.) [source: WP-WIA; IEP-Def]
- Translations: Aylmer Maude (with Tolstoy's supervision; search sources say first published 1898/1899 — exact date not settled here) and Richard Pevear & Larissa Volokhonsky (Penguin 1995), which WP-WIA quotes. [source: WP-WIA; search]
- Definition (Maude's wording, as quoted in IEP from a 2023 reprint, p. 52, and matched by search snippets of the Standard Ebooks Maude text, ch. V): "To evoke in oneself a feeling one has once experienced, and having evoked it in oneself, then, by means of movements, lines, colors, sounds, or forms expressed in words, so to transmit that feeling that others may experience the same feeling—this is the activity of art." [source: IEP-Def §2b.i; search]
- Pevear–Volokhonsky wording (WP-WIA): "Art begins when a man, with the purpose of communicating to other people a feeling he once experienced, calls it up again within himself and expresses it by certain external signs". [source: WP-WIA]
- Tolstoy's view is inclusive ("jokes", "home decoration", "church services" can be art if they convey feeling) and amoral at the level of definition: "[f]eelings … very bad and very good, if only they infect the reader … constitute the subject of art". The artist's "sincerity" (the extent to which he "experiences the feeling he conveys") affects the infection. [source: WP-WIA (P–V translation)]
- IEP: Tolstoy's version is "particularly strong" because it requires BOTH that the artist feels and that the audience comes to feel; "until the audience feels what the artist feels, there is no art". [source: IEP-Def §2b.i]
- Good vs bad art: definition broad, but evaluation strictly moral, based on art's role in the "evolution of feelings", replacing lower feelings with kinder ones. He prized Christian art (from "the consciousness of sonship to God and the brotherhood of men") and "universal" art accessible to all; examples he praised include *Uncle Tom's Cabin*, *A Tale of Two Cities*, *Adam Bede*, Dostoevsky's *House of the Dead*, Millet's paintings. [source: WP-WIA]
- He dismissed Wagner, Liszt, Richard Strauss, Nietzsche, Oscar Wilde (and elsewhere Beethoven, Shakespeare, Dante), and called his own works "bad art" except the stories "God Sees the Truth" and "Prisoner of the Caucasus". He was excommunicated from the Russian Orthodox Church in 1901. [source: WP-WIA]
- Four marks of counterfeit art: borrowing, imitation (excessive realistic detail), effectfulness ("strikingness"), diversion (intellectual interest). [source: WP-WIA]
- Objections: "cold" art that communicates no affect (Malevich's *White on White*; Warhol's 8-hour film *Empire*; Dürer's *Hare*); Poe's account (in "The Philosophy of Composition", 1846) of writing "The Raven" "with the precision and rigorous logic of a mathematical problem"; not sufficient — love letters, insults, emoticons express feeling but are not art. [source: IEP-Def §2b.i, iii]
- Critical reception: Jahn (1975) notes "often confusing use of categorisation" and no definition of emotion. [source: WP-WIA]

## 6. Expression: Collingwood
- R. G. Collingwood (22 Feb 1889 – 9 Jan 1943), Waynflete Professor of Metaphysical Philosophy at Oxford; *The Principles of Art* (1938); influenced by Croce, Gentile, de Ruggiero. [source: WP-Coll]
- "Art proper" is distinguished from things "falsely" called art: craft, representation, magic and amusement. The master argument: these are all crafts, and art is not craft. [source: SEP-Coll §1]
- Craft (the "technical theory", which Collingwood attributes to Plato and Aristotle) has six marks, none individually necessary: means/ends distinction; planning vs execution; ends precede means in planning, means precede ends in execution; raw material vs finished product; form vs matter; hierarchies of crafts. Some works of art involve none of these "without its detracting from their status as art". [source: SEP-Coll §1.1]
- Magic = arousing emotion to be discharged in practical life (e.g. leaving the theatre indignant about some cause); amusement = arousing emotion by make-believe to be "earthed" within the experience. Most of Shakespeare and most literature are largely amusement, though art proper can co-exist in the same work. "Pleasure has a name; the 'artist' who busies himself with arousing that particular emotion is merely a craftsman" (Kemp's paraphrase, against Hume). [source: SEP-Coll §§1.3–1.4]
- EXPRESSION vs AROUSAL: arousal is a craft (preconceived effect, chosen means); expression is not. Collingwood (Principles pp. 109–10): a person "is conscious of … a perturbation or excitement which he feels going on within him, but of whose nature he is ignorant… 'I feel … I don't know what I feel.' From this helpless and oppressed condition he extricates himself by doing something which we call expressing himself… His mind is somehow lightened and eased." So: to express is to become conscious of the emotion (no prior plan possible); expression individualises; the "lightening" is clarity, not catharsis. Expression differs from "betraying" emotion (tears, blushing). [source: SEP-Coll §2]
- Art as imaginative creation: the work of art proper is an "imaginary" object; "the making a tune is an instance of imaginative creation" (134); so artworks, strictly, are not artefacts. [source: SEP-Coll §3]
- Objection (Kemp; the standard "Croce-Collingwood" objection): identifying the artwork with the imaginative experience "makes nonsense of the idea that the spectator interprets the artwork" correctly or incorrectly, or that two people can argue about the same work. [source: SEP-Coll §§3, 8]
- IEP: Collingwood (unlike Tolstoy) does not require the audience to feel anything; Susanne Langer later required only that the artist can imagine (know) the feeling, expressed in "symbolic form". [source: IEP-Def §2b.ii]

## 7. Form: Bell, Fry, Hanslick (Kant as background only)
- Clive Bell (16 Sep 1881 – 17 Sep 1964), Bloomsbury Group; theory of "significant form" first published in *Art* (1914). (IEP-Form cites "1913"; WP and IEP-Def give 1914 — use 1914.) [source: WP-Bell; WP-SigForm; IEP-Def; IEP-Form]
- Quotation (Bell, *Art*, p. 22 in IEP-Def's edition; IEP-Form gives p. 5 of its edition): "There must be some one quality without which a work of art cannot exist; possessing which, in the least degree, no work is altogether worthless. What is this quality? What quality is shared by all objects that provoke our aesthetic emotions? What quality is common to Sta. Sophia and the windows at Chartres, Mexican sculpture, a Persian bowl, Chinese carpets, Giotto's frescoes at Padua, and the masterpieces of Poussin, Piero della Francesca, and Cézanne? Only one answer seems possible – significant form." Continuation (IEP-Form): "In each, lines and colours combined in a particular way, certain forms and relations of forms, stir our aesthetic emotions. These relations and combinations of lines and colours, these aesthetically moving forms, I call 'Significant Form'; and 'Significant Form' is the one quality common to all works of visual art." [source: IEP-Def §2c.i; IEP-Form §2a]
- Bell: "To appreciate a work of art we need bring with us nothing from life, no knowledge of its ideas and affairs, no familiarity with its emotions… we need bring with us nothing but a sense of form and colour and a knowledge of three-dimensional space." [source: IEP-Form §1]
- For Bell we recognise art only by our feeling for it; representation is irrelevant to value; Bell's agenda included defending abstract and Post-Impressionist art. Note the list's global sweep (Mexican sculpture, Persian bowl, Chinese carpets). [source: IEP-Form §2a]
- Roger Fry (1866–1934): Bloomsbury; coined "Post-Impressionism"; organised "Manet and the Post-Impressionists" at the Grafton Galleries, London, November 1910; his key theoretical statement is "An Essay in Aesthetics", collected in *Vision and Design* (1920), arguing the response to art comes from form (line, mass, colour, design); founded the Omega Workshops (1913). Bell developed significant form after meeting Fry. [source: WP-Fry; WP-Bell; IEP-Form bibliography]
- Eduard Hanslick (11 Sep 1825 – 6 Aug 1904), Prague-born Viennese critic; *Vom Musikalisch-Schönen* (On the Musically Beautiful), 1854; champion of Brahms and absolute music against Wagner and Liszt. [source: WP-Hanslick]
- Hanslick's thesis: musical beauty "consists simply and solely of tones and their artistic combination"; the "representation of a specific feeling or emotional state is not at all among the characteristic powers of music"; emotions are defined by concepts, which music cannot convey: "Music can, in fact, whisper, rage, and rustle. But love and anger occur only within our hearts." (Translation as quoted in IEP-Mus; Payzant 1986 is the standard English translation in IEP-Mus and SEP-Mus bibliographies.) [source: IEP-Mus §3]
- Hanslick's formula *tönend bewegte Formen*: Payzant renders "tonally moving forms"; Rothfarb and Landerer's newer translation "sonically moved forms" (search; reviews of the new translation). [source: search]
- Kant background (one line only): Kant distinguished free beauty (*pulchritudo vaga*, no concept of what the object should be: flowers, some birds, crustacea, wallpaper patterns, musical fantasias) from dependent beauty (*pulchritudo adhaerens*: a church, palace); formalists read free beauty as pure formal beauty. Critique of the Power of Judgment, 1790, §16. Leave the rest to *Beauty and Taste*. [source: IEP-Form §4]
- Objections to formalism: (a) not necessary — conceptual works lack relevant formal properties (Duchamp's *Fountain*, Binkley 1977; Beuys's *I Like America and America Likes Me*, three days caged with a coyote); (b) not sufficient — elegant non-art artefacts; (c) Bell's "aesthetic emotion" seems "otherworldly" and unargued (McLaughlin 1977). [source: IEP-Def §2c.ii; IEP-Form §2]
- Circularity objection: commonly stated as — significant form is defined as the form that arouses aesthetic emotion, and aesthetic emotion as the emotion aroused by significant form. NOT found in the sources read in these words; see NOT VERIFIED. IEP-Form does note "for Bell we have no other means of recognising a work of art than our feeling for it", which is the basis of the charge.
- Danto against formalism (argument + objection + reply in SEP-AesC): for most any artwork one can imagine (a) a perceptually indiscernible non-artwork and (b) a perceptually indiscernible artwork of different value; so "form alone neither makes an artwork what it is nor gives it whatever value it has" (Danto 1981, 94–95). Shelley's reply: this shows the limits of form, not of the aesthetic, unless one assumes formal = aesthetic; Kant ranked poetry highest for its "aesthetic ideas"; Hume required critics to place themselves in the original audience's situation; Hutcheson and Gerard counted theorems and theories as objects of taste. [source: SEP-AesC §2.3]

## 8. Scepticism: Weitz and family resemblance (ARGUMENT 1)
- Morris Weitz (24 July 1916 – 1 Feb 1981), Brandeis; "The Role of Theory in Aesthetics", *Journal of Aesthetics and Art Criticism* 15 (1956): 27–35; it reversed the view of his 1950 *Philosophy of the Arts*, which Margaret MacDonald among others had criticised. [source: WP-Weitz; SEP-Def bibliography]
- Margaret MacDonald, a student of Wittgenstein, was "historically the first" to apply family resemblance to art (1952): artworks are like "a family having different branches rather than … a class united by common properties". [source: IEP-Def §3a]
- Wittgenstein, *Philosophical Investigations* (1953) §§66–67: games share no common feature, only "family resemblances". [source: IEP-Def §3a]
- Weitz's famous sentence (wording confirmed via search of scanned copies, p. 28): art, "as the logic of the concept shows, has no set of necessary and sufficient properties, hence a theory of it is logically impossible and not merely factually difficult." Weitz surveyed Formalism, Voluntarism, Emotionalism, Intellectualism, Intuitionism, Organicism. [source: search]
- STEP BY STEP (SEP-Def's reconstruction of the Open Concept Argument):
  - P1. A concept is open if a case can be imagined that would call for a decision to extend the concept to cover it or to close the concept and invent a new one.
  - P2. All open concepts are indefinable (have no necessary and sufficient conditions).
  - P3. There are cases calling for such a decision about "art" (IEP: sub-concepts like "novel" must be revised for *Finnegans Wake* or Annie Ernaux's *Les Années*; so "art" is open too).
  - C. Art is indefinable. Weitz added that defining art would hinder creativity. [source: SEP-Def §3.1; IEP-Def §3a]
- Objections: (a) change does not rule out identity over time; decisions to extend a concept may be principled; a definition can build in a novelty requirement (SEP). (b) Dickie (1969): from the openness of sub-concepts it does not follow that the super-concept is open ("insect" is closed though new dragonfly species keep appearing; "game" may be open while "chess" is closed). (c) Adajian (2003): scepticism has few resources to show art has no essence. [source: SEP-Def §3.1; IEP-Def §3c]
- Mandelbaum's reply: Maurice Mandelbaum (9 Dec 1908 – 1 Jan 1987, Johns Hopkins), "Family Resemblances and Generalization Concerning the Arts", *American Philosophical Quarterly* 2, no. 3 (July 1965): 219–228 (title sometimes "Generalizations"). He complains that Weitz made no attempt to analyse, clarify or defend the family-resemblance notion; family resemblance must be grounded in properties not directly exhibited (relational, non-exhibited properties), so the failure to find common *exhibited* properties does not show there are no common properties. His idea was used by Dickie to justify a relational definition. [source: WP-Weitz; WP-Mandel; search]
- Further objection to family resemblance as a positive theory: everything resembles everything in some respect; if the respect is specified it becomes a definition (SEP). Carroll (1999): Duchamp's *In Advance of the Broken Arm* resembles a shovel more than any artwork, so either it is not art or all shovels are. [source: SEP-Def §3.2; IEP-Def §3a]
- Other sceptical arguments (SEP): cognitive-science prototype theory (Dean 2003); purpose-relativity of concepts (Mag Uidhir and Magnus 2011); Marxian (Eagleton 1990: the aesthetic is ideological); feminist (canon dominated by men; the concept of genius excluded women — Battersby 1989, Korsmeyer 2004; domestic "crafts" marginalised; Brand 2000); Lopes 2014 (stalemate over hard cases like Duchamp's *Bottlerack* and Cage's *4′33″*). [source: SEP-Def §3.1]

## 9. Duchamp's *Fountain* (1917)
- A porcelain urinal signed "R. Mutt 1917", submitted in April 1917 to the inaugural exhibition of the Society of Independent Artists at the Grand Central Palace, New York; Duchamp reoriented it 90 degrees from its position of use. [source: WP-Fountain]
- Society: founded 1916 on the model of the French Société des Artistes Indépendants; open to anyone paying a six-dollar membership-and-entry fee, no juries or prizes; founders included Walter Arensberg, Duchamp, Katherine Dreier, William Glackens, Albert Gleizes, Man Ray and others; First Annual Exhibition, Grand Central Palace, 10 April – 6 May 1917, more than 2,000 works hung alphabetically. [source: WP-SIA]
- Not formally "rejected": under Society rules it could not be; it was kept out of the show area / "placed behind a partition" — Duchamp's word was "suppressed" (Cabanne interviews, 1971 English ed.). Duchamp, a director, had submitted it under a pseudonym; he resigned from the board. A Boston Evening Transcript report (25 April 1917) said directors cited the "no jury" rule on one side and indecency on the other, "and the majority voted it down". [source: WP-Fountain; WP-SIA]
- Duchamp's later explanation of the name: "Mutt" from the Mott Works (sanitary equipment manufacturer) altered after the cartoon *Mutt and Jeff*; "Richard" as French slang for money-bags. Alternative readings: German *Armut* (poverty), *Urmutter*/*Mutter*. [source: WP-Fountain]
- Stieglitz photographed it at his 291 gallery; that photograph is the only image of the original. *The Blind Man* no. 2 (1917) published it with the editorial "The Richard Mutt Case" (possibly by Beatrice Wood): "Whether Mr Mutt with his own hands made the fountain or not has no importance. He CHOSE it. He took an ordinary article of life, placed it so that its useful significance disappeared under the new title and point of view—created a new thought for that object." [source: WP-Fountain]
- The original was lost soon after; Calvin Tomkins's best guess is that Stieglitz threw it out. [source: WP-Fountain]
- Replicas: miniatures from 1935 (for the *Boîte-en-valise*); first full-size replica authorised 1950 (for Sidney Janis's gallery; now Philadelphia Museum of Art, acquired 1998); 1953 (Paris, location unknown); 1963 (made by Ulf Linde for Stockholm, signed 1964, Moderna Museet); 1964 Galleria Schwarz (Milan) edition of eight plus proofs, glazed earthenware painted to resemble porcelain — examples at SFMOMA, Tate Modern (bought 1999), National Gallery of Canada, Centre Pompidou, Indiana University, Kyoto. *Cabinet* magazine counts 17 authorised versions. So every *Fountain* in a museum is a replica. [source: WP-Fountain]
- Duchamp (1964 interview with Otto Hahn): the choice "sprang from the idea of making an experiment concerned with taste: choose the object which has the least chance of being liked." SEP-Def: "Duchamp famously asserted that his urinal, *Fountain*, was selected for its lack of aesthetic features." [source: WP-Fountain; SEP-Def §4.4]
- December 2004: voted the most influential artwork of the 20th century by 500 selected British art-world professionals (ahead of *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* and Warhol's *Marilyn Diptych*) (BBC, 1 Dec 2004). [source: WP-Fountain]
- CONTESTED ATTRIBUTION (present as a claim, not a fact): Duchamp to his sister Suzanne, 11 April 1917: "Une de mes amies sous un pseudonyme masculin, Richard Mutt, avait envoyé une pissotière en porcelaine comme sculpture" ("One of my female friends under a masculine pseudonym, Richard Mutt, sent in a porcelain urinal as a sculpture"). Candidates proposed for the friend: Rrose Sélavy (discredited: that alter ego dates from the 1920s), Elsa von Freytag-Loringhoven (proposed by Irene Gammel, *Baroness Elsa*, 2002; promoted by Siri Hustvedt, Guardian 2019; Glyn Thompson and Julian Spalding, *Art Exposed*, 2023, argue the urinal model was sold only in Philadelphia, where Elsa then lived, and that "R. Mutt" is a German pun), or Louise Norton (whose address is partly visible on the entry tag). Counter-points WP records: Duchamp wrote "sent" not "made"; "there is no documentary or testimonial evidence that suggests von Freytag created *Fountain*". WP-Elsa calls the attribution "speculative", resting on "circumstantial evidence". Note: in Nov 2026 the WP-Fountain short description credits Elsa while the lead says "attributed to Marcel Duchamp" — a sign the article is being edited back and forth; do not cite WP's short description as settling anything. [source: WP-Fountain; WP-Elsa]
- Elsa von Freytag-Loringhoven (née Else Plötz; 12 July 1874 – 14 Dec 1927), German Dada artist and poet active in Greenwich Village 1913–1923; co-made *God* (1917) with Morton Schamberg (Philadelphia Museum now credits her). [source: WP-Elsa; WP-Fountain]

## 10. Warhol's *Brillo Box* and Danto (ARGUMENT 2: indiscernibles)
- Warhol's Brillo boxes: silkscreen (and house paint) on plywood, replicating the supermarket cartons; first shown at the Stable Gallery, New York, in 1964 (Phillips gives 26 April 1964); gallery filled like a stockroom; about 100 made (estimates vary). The commercial carton's designer is often named as James Harvey, an Abstract Expressionist painter — single-sourced in this research. [source: search (Phillips; Guy Hepner; Artchive)]
- Arthur C. Danto (1 Jan 1924 – 25 Oct 2013), Columbia professor; art critic for *The Nation* 1984–2009. [source: WP-Danto]
- "The Artworld", *Journal of Philosophy* 61, no. 19 (15 Oct 1964): 571–584 (symposium paper); coined "artworld" = "an atmosphere of art theory" (SEP's gloss). Key sentence (search-verified wording): "To see something as art requires something the eye cannot descry—an atmosphere of artistic theory, a knowledge of the history of art: an artworld." [source: SEP-Def §4.2; WP-Danto; search]
- Danto's definition as glossed by SEP (Danto, Carroll): something is a work of art iff (i) it has a subject (ii) about which it projects an attitude or point of view (a style) (iii) by rhetorical ellipsis (usually metaphorical) engaging the audience in filling in what is missing, and (iv) the work and its interpretations require an art-historical context. Criticisms: makes highly rhetorical criticism art; needs an account of "art-historical context"; does not fit music. [source: SEP-Def §4.2]
- *The Transfiguration of the Commonplace* (Harvard UP, 1981): the "indiscernibles" method (e.g. a set of identical red squares, some artworks with different titles and meanings, some mere objects); artworks are "about" something and embody their meaning; later *What Art Is* (Yale UP, 2013). [source: SEP-Def bibliography; search]
- Danto (2003, *The Abuse of Beauty*, p. 7) on Duchamp's urinal and an ordinary one: aesthetics "could not explain why one was a work of fine art and the other not, since for all practical purposes they were aesthetically indiscernible: if one was beautiful, the other one had to be beautiful, since they looked just alike." [source: SEP-AesC §2.3]
- STEP BY STEP (built from SEP-AesC and IEP-Def):
  - P1. Warhol's *Brillo Box* is an artwork; the Brillo cartons in the stockroom are not.
  - P2. They are (more or less) perceptually indiscernible.
  - P3. If two things are perceptually indiscernible, no perceptual (formal, "exhibited") property can explain why one is art and the other not.
  - C1. What makes something art is not a perceptual property.
  - C2. It must be a relational, non-exhibited property: an interpretation made possible by an artworld/theory and art history.
- Objection 1 (Shelley, SEP-AesC): the inference only defeats aesthetic theories if "aesthetic" means "formal/perceptual"; on Hume's and Kant's own broader notions, grasping a work's art-historical context can be a precondition of aesthetic judgment, so the argument refutes formalism, not aesthetics. Reply open to Danto: then "aesthetic" is no longer doing the defining work. [source: SEP-AesC §2.3]
- Objection 2 (IEP): Danto's view implies the maker needs knowledge of the artworld; a child dipping a tie in blue paint cannot make art but Picasso could; Danto accepted that Lascaux painters were not making art (Danto 1964, 195) — counterintuitive, and it excludes most non-Western art and *art brut*. [source: IEP-Def §4a]
- Objection 3: Wollheim and Tilghman rejected the indiscernibles method itself (search, secondary). Also note SEP-AesC's "(more or less)": Warhol's boxes are not literally indistinguishable (plywood vs cardboard), but Danto's argument needs only conceivable indiscernibles. [source: search; SEP-AesC §2.3]
- Danto also argued a Hegelian "end of art": not that art stops being made, but that a certain history of Western art ended (1984 address to the World Congress of Aesthetics, Montreal). [source: WP-Danto]

## 11. Dickie's institutional theory
- George Dickie (12 Aug 1926 – 24 Mar 2020), University of Illinois at Chicago. Works: "Defining Art", *American Philosophical Quarterly* 6, no. 3 (1969): 253–256; *Art and the Aesthetic: An Institutional Analysis* (Cornell UP, 1974); *The Art Circle* (Haven, 1984). [source: WP-Dickie; IEP-Def references]
- 1969 version (IEP quotes): "A work of art in the descriptive sense is (1) an artifact (2) upon which some society or some sub-group of a society has conferred the status of candidate for appreciation." Conferral compared to declaring someone a candidate for alderman. [source: IEP-Def §4a]
- 1974 version (search-verified wording, *Art and the Aesthetic* p. 34): "A work of art in the classificatory sense is (1) an artifact (2) a set of the aspects of which has had conferred upon it the status of candidate for appreciation by some person or persons acting on behalf of a certain social institution (the artworld)." [source: SEP-Def §4.2 (paraphrase); search]
- 1984 version (*The Art Circle*), five interlocking definitions: (1) an artist is a person who participates with understanding in the making of a work of art; (2) a work of art is an artifact of a kind created to be presented to an artworld public; (3) a public is a set of persons prepared in some degree to understand an object presented to them; (4) the artworld is the totality of all artworld systems; (5) an artworld system is a framework for the presentation of a work of art by an artist to an artworld public. Conferral is dropped in favour of presentation. [source: SEP-Def §4.2; search]
- Dickie admits the circularity but argues it is not vicious because the artworld can be described historically, organisationally and sociologically. [source: IEP-Def §4a]
- Advantages: covers bad art, readymades, art brut, and answers "my 5-year-old could do that". [source: IEP-Def §4a.i]
- Objections: (a) vicious circularity; cannot distinguish the artworld from structurally similar institutions (D. Davies 2004: the "commerceworld" fits too); (b) art outside any institution seems possible (Levinson 1979; Monseré 2012 — Aloïse Corbaz and Judith Scott made art before Dubuffet/Breton or art-brut curators recognised them; the Pirahã of Amazonia have no artworld but sophisticated songs; Chauvet cave); (c) the artworld seems capable of error; (d) if anyone who sees herself as a member is a member, almost anything can be art; (e) feminist (Peg Brand Weiser 2000): authorities historically white men, so quilts would not be art until the 1970s. [source: SEP-Def §4.2; IEP-Def §§4a.ii, 5b]
- WOLLHEIM'S DILEMMA (Richard Wollheim, *Art and Its Objects*, 2nd ed. 1980, pp. 157–166): either artworld members have reasons for conferring art status or they do not. If they do, those reasons (not the conferral) make something art, so the institution is redundant; if they do not, conferral is arbitrary and we have no reason to take it seriously. So institutionalism is either redundant or arbitrary. Partial replies: Abell (2011/2012), Fokt (2017); Matravers's strong vs weak institutionalism (2000). [source: IEP-Def §4a.ii; SEP-Def §4.2]

## 12. Historical, aesthetic, cluster and disjunctive definitions
- Levinson (intentional-historical): "Defining Art Historically", *British Journal of Aesthetics* 19 (1979) (page range given as 232–250 by Oppy, as 21–33 by PhilPapers/IEP — unresolved; omit pages). Formulation (IEP quoting Levinson 1989, "Refining Art Historically", p. 21): "[A]n artwork is a thing (item, object, entity) that has been seriously intended for regard-as-a-work-of-art, i.e., regard in any way preexisting artworks are or were correctly regarded." Levinson's isolated maker (IEP's "Mowgli" illustration) can make art without the concept of art. [source: IEP-Def §4b; SEP-Def §4.3; search]
- Objections to historicism: the first artworks (stipulated as art, Levinson 1979, 249) raise a Euthyphro problem (Davies 1997); Currie's Martian art (art older than ours with no historical tie) (IEP cites Currie 1993 and 1989); a child's fridge drawing has similar intentions; autonomous art traditions are either excluded (reductio) or concede a trans-historical concept. Levinson (2002) replies it is a historical, not conceptual, truth that art traditions involve the aesthetic. Carroll (1993) offers a narrative version without intention. [source: IEP-Def §4b.ii; SEP-Def §4.3]
- Beardsley (aesthetic definition): an artwork is "either an arrangement of conditions intended to be capable of affording an experience with marked aesthetic character or (incidentally) an arrangement belonging to a class or type of arrangements that is typically intended to have this capacity" (*The Aesthetic Point of View*, 1982, p. 299). Beardsley flatly denied the readymades were art. [source: SEP-Def §4.4]
- Other aesthetic/functional definitions: Zangwill (1995; "moderate formalism"; readymades as "second-order" or "parasitic" art); Eldridge (1985). Objections: too narrow (readymades, Robert Barry's *All the things I know but of which I am not at the moment thinking – 1:36 PM; June 15, 1969*), too broad (cars, lawns, cake decoration), trouble with bad art; Dickie's attack on the "aesthetic attitude" (Dickie 1965 "Beardsley's Phantom Aesthetic Experience"). [source: SEP-Def §4.4; IEP-Def §6]
- Gaut's cluster account ("'Art' as a Cluster Concept", in Carroll ed., *Theories of Art Today*, 2000): ten properties, none necessary, some subsets sufficient: (1) positive aesthetic properties; (2) expressive of emotion; (3) intellectually challenging; (4) formally complex and coherent; (5) capacity to convey complex meanings; (6) individual point of view; (7) original / exercise of creative imagination; (8) product of high degree of skill; (9) belonging to an established artistic form; (10) product of an intention to make an artwork. [source: SEP-Def §3.2; IEP-Def §3b]
- Objections to the cluster view: logically equivalent to a long finite disjunction, so really a definition (Davies 2006); no principle for what goes on the list ("costs a lot"? "made on a Tuesday"? Beardsley's "flamp" = flower or lamp); criterion 9 regenerates the question; how many criteria are enough (philosophy papers have 3–6). [source: SEP-Def §3.2; IEP-Def §3c]
- Disjunctive: symptomatic (Bond 1975; Dutton 2006) vs synthetic (Stecker 1997/2005; Stephen Davies 2015); Longworth and Scarantino (2010) formalise disjunctive definitions. [source: IEP-Def §8; SEP-Def §4.5]
- Stephen Davies's definition (2015, "Defining Art and Artworlds", JAAC 73: 375–84, pp. 377–8): something is art "(a) if it shows excellence of skill and achievement in realizing significant aesthetic goals, and either doing so is its primary […] identifying function, or (b) if it falls under an art genre or art form established and publicly recognized within an art tradition, or (c) if it is intended by its maker/presenter to be art and its maker/presenter does what is necessary and appropriate to realizing that intention." Clause (a) "does the important work of getting the art-ball rolling" for first art. SEP calls it "cladistic" (lineage from prehistoric art ancestors); worries: art traditions could never die; nonhumans could never make art. [source: IEP-Def §8b; SEP-Def §4.5]
- Lopes's "buck-passing" (*Beyond Art*, 2014): x is a work of art iff x is a work of K, where K is an art; theory of the individual arts does the work; "coffee mug objection". [source: SEP-Def §4.5; IEP-Def §7]
- Evolutionary: Ellen Dissanayake's "making special" / "artifying", rooted in mother–infant interaction (formalisation, repetition, exaggeration, elaboration, manipulation of expectation); worry that it covers everything humans find interesting (Davies 2012). [source: SEP-Def §4.6]

## 13. Is "art" a modern European category? Non-Western and prehistoric art
- Paul Oskar Kristeller (22 May 1905, Berlin – 7 June 1999), Renaissance scholar, Columbia. "The Modern System of the Arts: A Study in the History of Aesthetics", *Journal of the History of Ideas* 12 (Oct 1951): 496–527 (Part I) and 13 (1952): 17–46 (Part II). Thesis (as quoted in SEP): the modern system of the five major arts [painting, sculpture, architecture, poetry, music] "which underlies all modern aesthetics … is of comparatively recent origin and did not assume definite shape before the eighteenth century, although it had many ingredients which go back to classical, mediaeval, and Renaissance thought." [source: WP-Krist; SEP-Def §3.1; search]
- Greek *tekhnê* and Latin *ars* also covered trades like carpentry; medieval "liberal arts" included mathematics and astronomy; the Hindu notion of *kalā* covers sixty-four practices, not all of which we would call artistic (Ganguly 1962). [source: IEP-Def §1]
- Larry Shiner, *The Invention of Art: A Cultural History* (University of Chicago Press; SEP dates it 2001, WP-FineArt 2003): before the 18th century "an artist or artisan was a skilled maker", art meant roughly *techne*; theorists who think art an 18th-century European invention regard non-European objects as outside the extension of "art". Bourdieu and Eagleton made similar claims. [source: SEP-Def §4; WP-FineArt]
- James I. Porter, "Is Art Modern? Kristeller's 'Modern System of the Arts' Reconsidered", *British Journal of Aesthetics* 49, no. 1 (2009): 1–24: Kristeller's history can be questioned; the "modern system" was neither a system nor an agreed entity but a construct of Kristeller's; its impact on aesthetics is unproven; a better account of ancient aesthetic thought is possible. IEP cites Porter "against the idea that the concept of art has emerged only with the notion of 'fine arts.'" Replies: Shiner (2009, BJA) says Porter misrepresents Kristeller; Kivy also replied; James O. Young (2015, BJA, "The Ancient and Modern System of the Arts") argues the ancient imitative arts overlap Batteux's fine arts. [source: IEP-Def §1; search]
- IEP's position: lacking a word co-referent with "art" does not mean lacking the concept, and making art is "arguably a typical human activity" (Dutton 2006). [source: IEP-Def §1]
- Japan: the word *bijutsu* (美術) was coined in the early Meiji period as a translation of the world's-fair classification terms (German *Kunstgewerbe*, *Kunst*, *bildende Kunst*; fine art) for Japan's participation in the 1873 Vienna World Exposition; sources differ on whether it first appeared in 1872 (in the Japanese programme published in 1872) or 1873. Kitazawa Noriaki and Satō Dōshin are the scholars associated with showing this. [source: search; WP-JapArt (Japan's world's-fair participation began with Vienna 1873)]
- Denis Dutton (9 Feb 1944 – 28 Dec 2010), University of Canterbury, NZ, founder of *Arts & Letters Daily*. "A Naturalist Definition of Art" (JAAC 64, 2006: 367–377); "But They Don't Have Our Concept of Art" (in Carroll ed. 2000); *The Art Instinct: Beauty, Pleasure, and Human Evolution* (Bloomsbury Press, New York, 2009; paperback 2010; WP-Dutton says 2010 — use 2009) argues art appreciation stems from Pleistocene adaptations. [source: WP-Dutton; IEP-Def; SEP-Def; search]
- Dutton's twelve "criteria for recognition" (2006, pp. 369–372; reused in *The Art Instinct*): (i) direct pleasure; (ii) skill or virtuosity; (iii) style; (iv) novelty and creativity; (v) criticism; (vi) representation; (vii) "special" focus; (viii) expressive individuality; (ix) emotional saturation; (x) intellectual challenge; (xi) art traditions and institutions; (xii) imaginative experience. Modelled on symptoms in a diagnostic manual: none necessary. Objection: what unifies the list, and why weight imagination (xii) over emotional saturation (ix)? [source: IEP-Def §8a]
- Stephen Davies (University of Auckland; ASA president 2007–08): *Definitions of Art* (1991), *The Artful Species: Aesthetics, Art, and Evolution* (OUP 2012); "Non-Western Art and Art's Definition", in Carroll ed., *Theories of Art Today* (2000), pp. 199–216. Davies 1997 "First Art and Art's Definition"; his 2015 definition gives prehistoric "first art" status through clause (a). [source: WP-SDavies; SEP-Def; IEP-Def; search]
- Prehistoric cases used in the literature: Lascaux (Danto denies art status), Chauvet cave and the Venus of Willendorf (Levinson-style "first arts"). [source: IEP-Def §§4a, 4b]
- Feminist and Black aesthetics: the fine-art categories marginalised quilts and Otomi embroidery; many African cultures do not sharply separate making, interpreting and contemplating (Paul C. Taylor 2016); Radka Donnell and quilts as art. [source: IEP-Def §5]

## Debates
- How much poetry does the *Republic* ban (all mimetic poetry vs part of it in Book III)? Pappas vs Griswold. Whether Book X's psychology (illusion → irrational part) works is "a complex and fertile debate" (Nehamas 1982, Moss 2007). Whether Plato's attack targets only *skiagraphia*. [source: SEP-PlAes §2.3; SEP-PlRhet §3.3]
- Whether Aristotle's pleasure in mimesis is cognitive learning (Gallop) or mere recognition (Lear, Ferrari); catharsis as purgation, moral purification, clarification, consolation, or physical release. [source: SEP-ArAes]
- Whether art can be defined at all: Weitz/MacDonald vs Mandelbaum, Dickie, Adajian. [source: SEP-Def; IEP-Def]
- Conventionalism (Danto, Dickie, Levinson) vs aesthetic functionalism (Beardsley, Zangwill) vs hybrids (Davies, Stecker). [source: SEP-Def §§4–5]
- Whether Danto's indiscernibles refute aesthetic theories or only formalism (Danto vs Shelley). [source: SEP-AesC §2.3]
- Whether "art"/"fine art" is a modern European invention (Kristeller, Shiner) or a concept with ancient and cross-cultural equivalents (Porter, Young, Dutton, Davies). [source: SEP-Def; IEP-Def; search]
- Who made *Fountain* (Duchamp vs Freytag-Loringhoven): an open, contested claim; mainstream museums still credit Duchamp. [source: WP-Fountain; WP-Elsa]
