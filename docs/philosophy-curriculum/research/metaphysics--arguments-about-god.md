## 1. Framing: what would count as success?
- Ontological arguments argue for God "from premises which are supposed to derive from some source other than observation of the world—e.g., from reason alone" (a priori); cosmological and design arguments start from facts about the world. [source: SEP-OA intro; SEP-Cos §2]
- Russell (1946: 586): "it is much easier to be persuaded that ontological arguments are no good than it is to say exactly what is wrong with them" (SEP's paraphrase/quotation). [source: SEP-OA intro]
- Plantinga's own modest verdict on his modal ontological argument: "They cannot, perhaps, be said to prove or establish their conclusion. But since it is rational to accept their central premise, they do show that it is rational to accept that conclusion." (*The Nature of Necessity*, 1974: 221). Oppy et al. object that atheists reject at least one premise, so the argument cannot move them. → Good framing: many modern defenders aim at *rational permissibility*, not proof. [source: SEP-OA §4]
- Swinburne distinguishes a "correct C-inductive argument" (premises raise the probability of the conclusion) from a "correct P-inductive argument" (premises make it more probable than not, > 1/2). [source: SEP-Cos §9; WP-The_Existence_of_God_(book) citing Swinburne 2004: 6, 13]
- Plantinga (1967) concluded "that this piece of natural theology is ineffective", and later, from reformed epistemology, unnecessary since belief in God can be properly basic. [source: SEP-Cos §1]
- Hacking (1975) called Pascal's Wager "the first well-understood contribution to decision theory". [source: SEP-PW §1]

## 2. Ontological arguments
### 2a. Anselm (recap only; see *Early Scholasticism*)
- *Proslogion*, c. 1077–78 (SEP timeline: 1078), "followed soon after by Gaunilo's critique *On Behalf of the Fool*". [source: SEP-OA §1]
- Mann's translation of *Proslogion* 2 (Mann 1972: 260–1), full crucial passage: "Thus even the fool is convinced that something than which nothing greater can be conceived is in the understanding, since when he hears this, he understands it; and whatever is understood is in the understanding. And certainly that than which a greater cannot be conceived cannot be in the understanding alone. For if it is even in the understanding alone, it can be conceived to exist in reality also, which is greater. Thus if that than which a greater cannot be conceived is in the understanding alone, then that than which a greater cannot be conceived is itself that than which a greater can be conceived. But surely this cannot be. Thus without doubt something than which a greater cannot be conceived exists, both in the understanding and in reality." Alternative translations: Barnes 1972, Campbell 1976, Charlesworth 1965. [source: SEP-OA §5 area] (Published chapter already quotes the first two sentences in Mann's translation — consistent.)
- SEP's island parody can be run on any maximum ("that score on this test than which no greater score ... can be conceived"), answering Himma-style "no intrinsic maximum" replies. [source: SEP-OA §11]
- Aquinas criticised "an argument which somehow descends from Anselm" (SEP timeline 1264). [source: SEP-OA §1]

### 2b. Descartes and Leibniz (recap; see *Descartes*, *Leibniz*)
- Fifth Meditation: "no less contradiction in conceiving a supremely perfect being who lacks existence than ... conceiving a triangle whose interior angles do not sum to 180 degrees". [source: SEP-OA intro]
- Leibniz: Descartes's argument fails "unless one first shows that the idea of a supremely perfect being is coherent"; since perfections are unanalyzable, they cannot be shown incompatible, so all can coexist in one being (SEP timeline cites *New Essays*, 1709 [sic; written c. 1704, publ. 1765 — SEP's own bibliography gives "first published, 1765"]). [source: SEP-OA intro, §1, bibliography]
- Hume, *Dialogues* Part IX: "a general attack on a priori arguments", including "a purported demonstration that no such arguments can be any good" (SEP timeline lists it under 1776, the year Hume finished revising; published 1779). [source: SEP-OA §1]

### 2c. Kant: "existence is not a real predicate"
- *Critique of Pure Reason* A598/B626 (Meiklejohn translation, as quoted by IEP): "Being is evidently not a real predicate, that is, a conception of something which is added to the conception of some other thing. It is merely the positing of a thing, or of certain determinations in it. Logically, it is merely the copula of a judgement. The proposition, God is omnipotent, contains two conceptions ... the word is, is no additional predicate ... if I take the subject (God) with all its predicates (omnipotence being one), and say, God is, or There is a God, I add no new predicate to the conception of God, I merely posit or affirm the existence of the subject with all its predicates". [source: IEP-OA §2d] Page reference: a real predicate "goes beyond the concept of the subject and enlarges it" (A598/B626); existence is "positing" (A598/B626). [source: SEP-KR §3.1.2.1]
- Kemp Smith's wording (from earlier verified notes, WS): "'Being' is obviously not a real predicate; that is, it is not a concept of something which could be added to the concept of a thing." [source: docs/philosophy-curriculum/research/early-modern--kant.md] Guyer–Wood wording NOT seen.
- Hundred dollars/thalers: existence adds nothing to the concept of a hundred dollars; otherwise "my concept would not express the entire object" (A599/B627); "what would exist would not be the same as what I had thought in my concept" (A600/B628). [source: SEP-KR §3.1.2.1]
- Count of objections: SEP-KR says the CPR contains **four** distinct objections (first two treat "God exists" as analytic, A594/B622–A597/B625; last two as synthetic, A597/B625–A602/B630); SEP-OA's timeline says **three**. Published *Kant* chapter: "sources count them differently" — consistent. [sources: SEP-KR; SEP-OA §1]
- Kant's objections "rarely mentioned" claim: the analytic-rendering objections matter because a common complaint is that Kant's critique does not touch the Anselmian version (Plantinga 1966, Forgie 1975). [source: SEP-KR §3.1.2.1]
- Malcolm (1960) on existence as perfection: "The doctrine that existence is a perfection is remarkably queer ... what could it mean to say that it will be a better house if it exists than if it does not?" [source: IEP-OA §2d]
- Frege (*Foundations of Arithmetic*, 1884): existence is a second-order predicate (a property of concepts), so ontological arguments with first-order existence conclusions are "doomed" (SEP's gloss). Consistent with published *Birth of Analytic Philosophy*. [source: SEP-OA §1]

### 2d. ARGUMENT: Plantinga's modal ontological argument (*The Nature of Necessity*, Clarendon, 1974)
- Background: modal versions by Hartshorne (*Man's Vision of God*, 1941; 1965), Malcolm ("Anselm's Ontological Arguments", 1960), Plantinga (1974); Hartshorne's "allegedly derived from *Proslogion* 3". SEP timeline calls Plantinga's "'victorious' modal Ontological argument". [source: SEP-OA §1, §8]
- Definitions (IEP): a being is **maximally excellent** in a world W iff omnipotent, omniscient and morally perfect in W; **maximally great** in W iff maximally excellent in every possible world. So maximal greatness entails existence in every possible world. [source: IEP-OA §3] SEP: "a being is maximally great if and only if it exists necessarily and is essentially omnipotent, omniscient, and morally perfect (Plantinga 1974)". [source: SEP-OA §8]
- Step by step (IEP schematic):
  1. The concept of a maximally great being is self-consistent.
  2. If 1, then there is at least one possible world in which a maximally great being exists.
  3. So there is at least one possible world in which a maximally great being exists.
  4. If a maximally great being exists in one possible world, it exists in every possible world.
  5. So a maximally great being (God) exists in every possible world — including the actual one. [source: IEP-OA §3]
- SEP's compact form: (i) Possibly, God exists (possibility premise); (ii) Necessarily, if God exists, it is necessary that God exists (necessity premise); (iii) so necessarily God exists — via S5's ◇□p → □p (S5's characteristic axiom ◇p → □◇p entails it); then by axiom M (□p → p) God exists. A weaker system B (◇□p → p) would also do (Leftow 2005). [source: SEP-OA §8] **Plain-words step for the writer**: in S5, what is possible does not vary from world to world; so if necessary existence is possible from here, it is possible from every world, and a being that exists necessarily in one world exists in all.
- Three lines of objection: (1) S5 may not capture metaphysical modality (Chandler 1976; Salmon 1989; defenders: Pruss & Rasmussen 2018, Williamson 2016, Hale 2013, Leftow 1991); (2) necessity premise (trivial for "maximally great", substantive for "God"; Swinburne 2012 denies God is necessary; Rasmussen 2016 replies); (3) **possibility premise** — "perhaps the most important objection": the **symmetry problem**. [source: SEP-OA §8]
- **Objection (reverse MOA)**: (i*) Possibly, God doesn't exist; (ii) as before; (iii*) so necessarily God doesn't exist — equally valid in S5. Given (ii) and S5, (i) and (i*) are incompatible, yet "seem epistemically on par"; "Absent such a symmetry breaker ... the MOA is dialectically toothless". [source: SEP-OA §8]
- **Reply**: offer a symmetry breaker favouring (i) (e.g. that maximal greatness is coherent, Leibniz-style "perfections are compatible"); but many symmetry breakers are not a priori, which threatens the argument's ontological status, or become distinct arguments in their own right. The debate "has centred around symmetry breaking". [source: SEP-OA §8] Plantinga's own reply (above): rational to accept the central premise, so rational to accept the conclusion. [source: SEP-OA §4]
- IEP adds: objection that premise 4 is an instance of controversial S5 (Sennett: "all propositions bear their modal status necessarily"); IEP replies that Plantinga builds necessary existence into maximal greatness, so premise 4 is stronger than and not merely an instance of S5's axiom. Also the coherence worry: some argue omniscience + omnipotence are incompatible (power to create free beings vs knowing their choices). [source: IEP-OA §§3–4]
- Further reading on Plantinga's MOA: Adams 1988, Chandler 1993, Oppy 1995, Tooley 1981, van Inwagen 1977, Rasmussen 2018. [source: SEP-OA §8]
- Plantinga: "At first sight, Anselm's argument is remarkably unconvincing if not downright irritating; it looks too much like a parlor puzzle or word magic." (as quoted in IEP; verified in earlier notes) [source: IEP-OA via docs/.../medieval--early-scholasticism.md]

### 2e. Gödel (briefly)
- Gödel's "Ontologischer Beweis", which "he entrusted to Dana Scott in 1970 (though it appears to have been written earlier)"; printed in Gödel, *Collected Works* (1995), pp. 403–404. [source: SEP-Göd; SEP-OA §1 timeline: "inspired by the argument that Leibniz devised"]
- Representative Gödelian argument (SEP): positive properties; (1) A positive only if ¬A not positive; (2) positive properties closed under entailment; (3) having as essential properties all and only the positive properties is positive; (4) necessary existence is positive; hence, in S5, a necessarily existent being with all and only the positive properties exists. [source: SEP-OA §9]
- Key objection: no necessarily uninstantiated property can be positive, so whether omnipotence etc. are positive is exactly what theist and atheist dispute; "Unless we have an independent, non-question-begging way to discern which properties are positive, this looks like game over for Gödelian ontological arguments." [source: SEP-OA §9]
- Lewis, "Anselm and Actuality" (1970): all ontological arguments are either invalid or question-begging. [source: SEP-OA §1]

## 3. Cosmological arguments
### 3a. Map and history
- Earliest Western formulation in Plato's *Laws* 893–96; classical roots in Aristotle *Physics* VIII.4–6 and *Metaphysics* XII.1–6. Two Islamic types: the *falāsifa*'s argument from contingency (Ibn Sīnā), taken up by Aquinas (ST I q.2 a.3; SCG I.13); and the *mutakallimūn*'s temporal argument from the impossibility of an infinite temporal regress — "now referred to as the kalām cosmological argument" — influenced by John Philoponus (Davidson 1969). It entered Christian tradition through Bonaventure. [source: SEP-Cos §1]
- Descartes's cosmological argument (Meditation 3) also guarantees God is no deceiver; Leibniz and Samuel Clarke reaffirmed it with the PSR. [source: SEP-Cos §1]
- 20th c.: Smart (1955) called it "an absurd request for the nonsensical postulation of a logically necessary being"; critics include Mackie (1982), Martin, Rundle (2004), Morriston, Oppy (2006); defenders Koons (1997), Craig, Loke (2017, 2022), Gale & Pruss (weak PSR), Swinburne (inductive). [source: SEP-Cos §1]

### 3b. Avicenna's Proof of the Truthful (recap; full in *Falsafa*)
- *Burhān al-Ṣiddīqīn*, "the demonstration of the truthful"; unique in that it "does not appeal to the impossibility of infinite causal series" and "even entertains the possibility of an actual infinity of causes existing simultaneously"; shows the world depends on God "at every moment that it exists, even infinitely into the past". Key terms: *wājib al-wujūd bi-dhātihi* (necessary existent through itself) vs *mumkin al-wujūd bi-dhātihi* (possible through itself). Sources: *al-Najāt* 261–5, 271–3. [source: SEP-AIR §2.2]
- *Ishārāt* version "has been defined as a priori or ontological (Mayer 2001; cf. Marmura 1980)"; every chain needs "an extreme—the Necessary Existent Principle—which is not caused". [source: SEP-ISM §5.2]

### 3c. Leibniz's PSR and the contingency argument; Hume's and Russell's objections
- PSR: "no fact can be real or existing and no statement true without a sufficient reason for its being so and not otherwise" (*Monadology* §32); the sufficient reason for the "series of things comprehended in the universe of creatures" (§36) must lie outside the series, in a necessary being, God (§38). (Published *Leibniz* uses IEP's rendering of §32 — different translation, same content; name the translation used.) [source: SEP-Cos §1]
- **Hume's objection (twenty particles)**: "But the whole, you say, wants a cause. I answer that the uniting of these parts into a whole … is performed merely by an arbitrary act of the mind, and has no influence on the nature of things. Did I show you the particular causes of each individual in a collection of twenty particles of matter, I should think it very unreasonable should you afterwards ask me what was the cause of the whole twenty. This is sufficiently explained in explaining the parts." (*Dialogues* Part IX, 1779). Rowe calls the principle the "Hume-Edwards Principle" and objects. [source: SEP-Cos §5.3]
- Hume's Part IX targets Clarke; Demea's version: "Whatever exists must have a cause or reason of its existence ... we must either go on in tracing an infinite succession, without any ultimate cause at all, or must at last have recourse to some ultimate cause, that is necessarily existent" (D 9.3/188); God as "necessarily existent Being, who carries the Reason of his existence in himself; and who cannot be supposed not to exist without an express contradiction" (D 9.3/189). Cleanthes replies that matter could be the necessary being (any particle can be conceived annihilated — D 9.7/190 is Clarke's premise). [source: SEP-HR §4/Part IX discussion]
- **Russell's objection (the universe "just is")**: the universe needs no explanation; it is "just there, and that's all" (Russell 1948 [1964]: 175 — the BBC debate with Copleston); reiterated by Hawking (1987). Russell also charges a fallacy of composition. **Reply**: Koons (1997): we need not experience every member of a class to generalise (the tuba-player example); Morriston replies the universe is unlike its contents; defenders reply both are contingent. [source: SEP-Cos §5.2]
- **Kant's objection**: the cosmological argument (taking Leibniz's "If something exists, an absolutely necessary being also has to exist. Now I myself, at least exist; therefore, an absolutely necessary being exists", A604/B633) hides "a nest of dialectical presumptions" (A609/B637) and, to identify the necessary being with the most real being, relies on the ontological argument. [source: SEP-KR §3.1.2.2] Reply (Smart 1955 noted first point): the first part already establishes existence; the argument needs only factual, not logical, necessity. [source: docs/.../medieval--aquinas.md citing SEP-Cos §5.5]
- "Who created God?" (Hawking 1988: 174): theists who hold God factually (not logically) necessary say the PSR addresses factual contingency, so the demand to explain God's existence "is out of place" (Davis 1997); Hick: if "logically necessary existence" is meaningless, so is "just happens to exist". Mackie (1982: 84) and O'Connor (2008) press that a merely factually necessary God still invites "Why does God exist?". [source: SEP-Cos §5.5]

### 3d. ARGUMENT: the kalām cosmological argument (al-Ghazālī; Craig 1979)
- Islamic form (SEP-AIR): 1. Everything that begins to exist after not having existed has a cause for its existence. 2. The world began to exist after not having existed. 3. So the world has a cause, which is God. Premise 1 ~ "nothing comes from nothing", linked to *tarjīḥ* (preponderation). Proponents: al-Ashʿarī, al-Juwaynī, al-Ghazālī (*Moderation in Belief* / *al-Iqtiṣād*, 27–41), al-Shahrastānī, "and even certain philosophers like ... al-Kindī". Al-Fārābī, Ibn Sīnā, Ibn Rushd held the world eternal and rejected premise 2. [source: SEP-AIR §2.1]
- Medieval anti-infinity arguments (from Philoponus): (a) if the world were eternal the Moon would have made twelve times as many revolutions as the Sun, i.e. "twelve times infinity" — reason "recoils"; philosophers replied these are potential, not actual, infinities; (b) theologians answered: on the philosophers' own views (eternal species + immortal souls) there would now be an actual infinity of human souls. [source: SEP-AIR §2.1]
- Al-Ghazālī "draws on arguments ... developed by ... John Philoponus (c. 490–c. 570)" in *Incoherence* Discussion 1; Philoponus's arguments had entered Arabic in the 9th century (Davidson 1987). [source: SEP-Gh]
- Prototype quoted by Craig (1994: 80) from al-Ghazālī: "Every being which begins has a cause for its beginning; now the world is a being which begins; therefore, it possesses a cause for its beginning." [source: WP-Kalam_cosmological_argument, citing Craig] (Second-hand: Craig's rendering, al-Ghazālī work not named in WP — SEP-Cos attributes the argument to al-Ghazālī citing Craig 1979 part 1.)
- William Lane Craig (born 23 Aug 1949), *The Kalām Cosmological Argument* (London: Macmillan, 1979). [sources: SEP-Cos bibliography; WP-William_Lane_Craig]
- Craig's formulation (Craig & Sinclair 2009; Craig & Smith 1993): 1. Everything that begins to exist has a cause of its existence. 2. The universe began to exist. 3. So the universe has a cause of its existence. 4. No scientific explanation (physical laws + initial conditions) can causally account for the very beginning, since they are part of the universe. 5. So the cause must be personal. [source: SEP-Cos §8]
- Premise 1 support: "the metaphysical intuition that something cannot come out of nothing"; "No one sincerely believes that things, say, a horse or an Eskimo village, can just pop into being without a cause" (Craig & Sinclair 2009: 182). [source: SEP-Cos §8.1]
- Premise 2, a priori support (i): an actual infinite cannot exist; a beginningless series of events is an actual infinite; so there is none. Craig's infinite library: as many red books as red-and-black books; removing books does not reduce the collection — absurd. [source: SEP-Cos §8.2] Craig also uses **Hilbert's Hotel** (Craig 1994: 118–120). [source: WP-Kalam] Hilbert's Hotel: introduced by David Hilbert in a 1924–25 lecture; a full hotel with infinitely many rooms can take a new guest by moving each guest from room n to n+1, and infinitely many by moving n to 2n. [source: WP-Hilbert's_paradox_of_the_Grand_Hotel]
- **Objection** (Rundle 2004; Smith in Craig & Smith 1993: 85; Cantorian mathematicians): these are paradoxes, not contradictions; one-to-one correspondence with a proper subset is a *defining characteristic* of infinite sets (noted as early as Galileo); Craig begs the question by assuming finite-set intuitions hold for infinite sets. [source: SEP-Cos §8.2] Feser: on presentism past events no longer exist, unlike hotel rooms existing all at once; Morriston and Malpass: the past/future asymmetry is arbitrary. Craig replies they beg the question. [source: WP-Kalam]
- Premise 2 (ii): successive addition cannot form an actual infinite (Craig 1979: 103); the Tristram Shandy debate (Russell 1937: 358) with Oderberg's quantifier-shift diagnosis. [source: SEP-Cos §8.3]
- Premise 2 a posteriori: Big Bang as beginning of matter, energy, space and time. **Objection**: the Big Bang is not an event (no prior time; Hawking 1988; Grünbaum 1994; Rundle 2004). [source: SEP-Cos §§8.4–8.5]
- **Quantum objection** to premise 1: electrons appear to come into existence without determinable cause (Davies 1984; Smith in Craig & Smith 1993: 121–23); Davies: the universe arose from "an excited vacuum state", "a ferment of quantum activity". **Craig's replies**: quantum events still have necessary causal conditions (a vacuum is not nothing); "most of the available interpretations of ... [Quantum Mechanics] are fully deterministic" (Craig & Sinclair 2009: 183); unpredictability ≠ causelessness. Morriston: what makes a cause out of merely necessary conditions? SEP verdict: depends on whether indeterminacy is ontological or epistemic. [source: SEP-Cos §§8.1, 8.6]
- **"Who caused God?" confusion**: premise 1 concerns only what *begins* to exist; God is held to be beginningless, so the argument does not imply God needs a cause; the question is legitimate only against a principle "everything has a cause", which the kalām does not use. (This is the standard defenders' point; SEP-Cos §8.1 notes the advantage of basing the argument on the narrower causal principle rather than the PSR (Morriston 2000: 149).) [source: SEP-Cos §8.1; §5.5 on "Who created God?"]
- **Objection to step 5 (personal cause)**: Morriston (2000: 163–68): a timeless intention sufficient to create would yield an eternal universe just as a non-personal cause would; Craig (2002) replies the agent must also exercise causal power. Michael Martin (1990: 103): at most "some personal agent or agents created the universe" — "for all he shows, there may have been trillions of personal agents". [sources: SEP-Cos §8.6; WP-Kalam quoting Martin 1990]
- Note: SEP-Cos §1 attributes the whole temporal argument to al-Ghazālī "(Craig 1979: part 1)". Jewish-philosophy notes: do NOT trace the modern kalām to Saadia specifically (not verified), though Saadia's time argument ("If time was endless, the present would not be able to exist since the actual moment would not have been able to traverse infinity") is the same family, from Philoponus. [sources: SEP-Saa §2.2; docs/.../medieval--jewish-philosophy.md]

### 3e. Maimonides and Saadia (recap; see *Jewish Philosophy*)
- Saadia, *Book of Beliefs and Opinions* (933): four proofs of creation *ex nihilo* "borrowed from John Philoponus"; "there is no means of proving the existence of a Creator other than that of creation". [source: SEP-Saa §2.2]
- Maimonides' proofs "are versions of the cosmological argument (GP 2.1)"; one: eternal heavenly motion needs an infinite power, which cannot be in a finite body, so a being "neither a body nor a force in a body". It shows *that* God is, not *what* God is. [source: SEP-Mai]
- Negative theology: "God is powerful" = "God does not lack power or possess it in a way that makes it comparable to other things" (GP 1.58); GP 1.59: "the more you can prove inapplicable to God, the better: and the more you affirm of Him, the more you anthropomorphize"; highest praise is silence (Psalm 65). [source: SEP-Mai] (Translator of GP 1.59 not named in SEP — standard is Pines 1963; not verified.)

## 4. Design arguments
### 4a. Analogical schema and Hume (recap *Berkeley and Hume*)
- SEP Schema 1: (1) natural entity e is like human artifact a in relevant respects R; (2) a has R because designed; (3) like effects, like causes; (4) so probably e has R because designed by relevantly human-like agency. [source: SEP-Tel §2.1]
- Hume's objections (SEP summary): weak analogy (cosmos more like an organism); chance and other alternatives; at most finite, perhaps imperfect designer or "a committee"; regress (the designer needs explaining); designer ≠ creator of matter; our only universe, seen in a tiny fraction. Hume's residual concession: "the cause or causes of order in the universe probably bear some remote analogy to human intelligence" (1779 [1998], 88). [source: SEP-Tel §2.1.1]
- Cleanthes's version is "a fuller and quite nice early statement" (1779 [1998], 15); the design argument "in extremely clipped form comprises one of Aquinas's Five Ways". [source: SEP-Tel §2]
- Kant: the design argument "always deserves to be mentioned with respect" and is "the oldest, the clearest, and the most accordant with the common reason of mankind" (A623/B651); establishes at most "an architect of the world", not a creator. [source: SEP-KR §3.1.2.3]

### 4b. Paley's watch
- William Paley (July 1743 – 25 May 1805); *Natural Theology: or, Evidences of the Existence and Attributes of the Deity*, London: J. Faulder, 1802. [sources: WP-William_Paley; WP-Natural_Theology]
- Opening (as quoted in WP): "In crossing a heath, suppose I pitched my foot against a stone, and were asked how the stone came to be there; I might possibly answer, that, for anything I knew to the contrary, it had lain there forever ... But suppose I had found a watch upon the ground, and it should be inquired how the watch happened to be in that place; I should hardly think of the answer I had before given ... There must have existed, at some time, and at some place or other, an artificer or artificers, who formed [the watch] for the purpose which we find it actually to answer; who comprehended its construction, and designed its use. ... Every indication of contrivance, every manifestation of design, which existed in the watch, exists in the works of nature; with the difference, on the side of nature, of being greater or more, and that in a degree which exceeds all computation." [source: WP-Watchmaker_analogy]
- SEP quotes (Paley 1802 [1963]): the watch's "several parts are framed and put together for a purpose ... the inference we think is inevitable, that the watch must have had a maker"; "there is precisely the same proof that the eye was made for vision as there is that the telescope was made for assisting it" (p. 13); "up to the limit, the reasoning is as clear and certain in the one case as in the other" (p. 14). [source: SEP-Tel §2.2]
- Interpretation debate: usually read as analogical, but SEP argues Paley gives two chapters to the watch alone, then in ch. 3 ("Application of the Argument") applies the *same inference* to nature — a non-analogical "Schema 2"; it has been argued Paley deliberately structured it to avoid Hume's attacks on analogy (Gillispie 1990). [source: SEP-Tel §2.2]
- Fifth Way ≠ Paley: it starts from end-directed regularity in non-intelligent things, not complexity. [source: docs/.../medieval--aquinas.md, WP-Five_Ways]
- Plagiarism charge (*The Athenaeum*, 1848): the watch image was used by many before Paley, including Nieuwentyt ("in the middle of a Sandy down ... any one should find a Watch"); Jantzen (2014) concludes Paley "reworked" Nieuwentyt's argument. [source: WP-Watchmaker_analogy] (Use cautiously; one scholar's verdict.)
- **Darwin**: studied Paley at Cambridge; in 1859 wrote to John Lubbock, "I do not think I hardly ever admired a book more than Paley's *Natural Theology*: I could almost formerly have said it by heart" (letter 22 Nov 1859, Darwin Correspondence Project). Autobiography: "The old argument of design in nature, as given by Paley, which formerly seemed to me so conclusive, fails, now that the law of natural selection has been discovered. We can no longer argue that, for instance, the beautiful hinge of a bivalve shell must have been made by an intelligent being, like the hinge of a door by man." [source: WP-Natural_Theology (autobiography quoted via Hitchens 2007 anthology — second-hand)]
- SEP: natural selection could account for many or all of the Rs in biology; but "undercutting and explaining away are not necessarily the same thing"; Dawkins (1987 subtitle): "Why the evidence of evolution reveals a universe without design". [source: SEP-Tel §3]

### 4c. ARGUMENT: fine-tuning, and the multiverse/anthropic replies
- Examples: gravity vs electromagnetism (slightly weaker → no supernovae; stronger → small short-lived stars); strong force (≈50% stronger → hydrogen burned early; carbon and oxygen production sensitive: Hoyle et al. 1953); up/down quark mass difference; weak force (weaker by ~10 → no long-lived hydrogen-burning stars). [source: SEP-FT §1.1.1]
- **Cosmological constant**: theory expects a vacuum energy density larger than actual "by an immense number of magnitudes" — between 10^50 and 10^123 depending on assumptions — yet only values a few orders of magnitude larger than actual permit galaxies (Weinberg 1987). [source: SEP-FT §1.1.1]
- Collins's version (IEP): "Prime Principle of Confirmation": if observation O is more probable under H1 than H2, O gives reason to prefer H1; fine-tuning is near-certain on design, very improbable on the "Atheistic Single-Universe Hypothesis". [source: IEP-Des §c] Robin Collins, "The teleological argument: an exploration of the fine-tuning of the cosmos", in Craig & Moreland (eds.), *The Blackwell Companion to Natural Theology* (2009), 202–281. [source: SEP-FT bibliography]
- Step by step (Bayesian form, SEP-FT §3.1; proponents Holder 2002, Craig 2003, Swinburne 2004, Collins 2009):
  1. R: the constants, laws and initial conditions are right for life.
  2. P(R | no designer) is very low, given the narrow life-permitting ranges.
  3. P(R | designer) is not far from 1: a powerful good designer would be expected to enable intelligent life.
  4. So R confirms design: P(R|D) > P(R|¬D) (the likelihood inequality; Sober 2003 calls this the core).
  5. Unless the prior strongly favours no designer, the posterior favours design.
- **Objection 1 (anthropic/observation selection; Sober 2003, 2009)**: we could not observe a life-hostile universe (weak anthropic principle, Carter 1974: "our location in the universe is necessarily privileged to the extent of being compatible with our existence as observers"); so the relevant comparison conditional on our existence gives probability 1 on both hypotheses. **Reply: Leslie's firing squad** (Leslie, *Universes*, Routledge 1989: 13f.): a prisoner expects execution by a firing squad, finds himself alive after all have fired, and wonders whether they meant to miss; he could only observe this if he survived, yet suspecting a plan seems rational. Leslie, Weisberg (2005), Kotzen (2012) argue Sober's analysis gives the wrong verdict here. [source: SEP-FT §3.2]
- **Objection 2 (normalisation)**: if any real number is a logically possible value, a uniform measure gives every finite range probability zero, so "improbable" is ill-defined (McGrew et al. 2001; Colyvan et al. 2005). [source: SEP-FT §2.1] Also: improbable outcomes don't always call for explanation (coin tosses; Gould 1983); Leslie concedes this but says available candidate explanations mean we should not "dismiss it as how things just happen to be" (Leslie 1989: 10). Old-evidence problem (Glymour 1980). [source: SEP-FT §§2.2, 3.3]
- **Objection 3 (multiverse)**: if there are many universes with varying constants, a life-friendly one is expected somewhere, and we must find ourselves in one: P(R|M) ≈ 1. Supporters of inferring a multiverse: Susskind, Greene, Tegmark; Leslie, Smart, Parfit, Bradley. [source: SEP-FT §4, §4.2]
- **Reply to the multiverse (inverse gambler's fallacy)**: Hacking (1987): inferring many past trials from one remarkable outcome is a fallacy; White (2000: 263): the argument wrongly supposes "that the existence of many other universes makes it more likely that this one—the only one that we have observed—will be life-permitting". Counter-reply (McGrath 1988): the right analogy is someone admitted to the casino *only when* the remarkable outcome occurs; defenders also Leslie 1988, Bostrom 2002, Bradley 2009 and others. [source: SEP-FT §4.3]
- Theists also reply that a multiverse-generating mechanism may itself need fine-tuning; NOT verified in SEP-FT text read — do not use without source.
- SEP-FT: "many regard the argument from fine-tuning for a designer as the strongest version of the teleological argument that contemporary science affords". [source: SEP-FT §3]

### 4d. Swinburne's cumulative Bayesian case (briefly; cross-ref *Induction*)
- Richard Swinburne (born 26 Dec 1934); *The Existence of God* (Oxford, 1979; reissued 1991; 2nd ed. Clarendon 2004). [sources: WP-Richard_Swinburne; WP-The_Existence_of_God_(book)]
- He rejects deductive arguments and the strong PSR; uses Bayes's theorem; the cosmological argument is C-inductive; evidence in the cumulative case: the universe's existence and orderliness, consciousness, miracle reports, religious experience. Simplicity: God is "the simplest kind of person there can be" — infinite power, knowledge, freedom; "It is always simpler to postulate infinite or zero degrees of some property than a certain precise finite value of it" (Swinburne 1983: 385). [source: SEP-Cos §9]
- "Theism does not make [certain phenomena] very probable; but nothing else makes their occurrence in the least probable, and they cry out for an explanation. A priori, theism is perhaps very unlikely, but it is far more likely than any rival supposition." (Swinburne 1979: 290). [source: SEP-Cos §9]
- Swinburne (2004: 342): with religious experience added, theism comes out more probable than not. [source: WP-The_Existence_of_God_(book)] (WP wording is garbled; state as: Swinburne concludes the total evidence makes theism more probable than not.)
- Objections: Michael Martin (1986): the prior probability of a complex universe is undefined/ambiguous; God is compatible with any universe or none, so God does not raise its probability. Mackie questions personal explanation. [source: SEP-Cos §9]

## 5. Religious experience
- William James, *The Varieties of Religious Experience: A Study in Human Nature* (1902), Gifford Lectures, Edinburgh, 1901–02. [source: WP-Varieties] Four marks of mysticism: ineffability ("it defies expression…its quality must be directly experienced; it cannot be imparted or transferred to others"), "noetic quality", transiency, passivity (V 380); asks whether these states are "windows through which the mind looks out upon a more extensive and inclusive world" (V 428). [source: SEP-Jam]
- James: mystical states are authoritative for those who have them but give no grounds to those who only hear the report ("my experience is evidence for me, but not for you"; Bovens 2012 develops this). [source: SEP-RE §on testimony]
- Argument from Religious Experience (SEP): religious experiences are in relevant respects like sensory experiences; sensory experiences ground beliefs about the world; so religious experiences ground religious beliefs (Swinburne 1979; Alston 1991; Plantinga 1981, 2000; Netland 2022). [source: SEP-RE §3]
- **Swinburne's Principle of Credulity**: "one is justified in believing that what seems to one to be present actually is present, unless some appropriate defeater is operative" (SEP's paraphrase); to reject it one must show defeaters always obtain in the religious case. [source: SEP-RE]
- William P. Alston (29 Nov 1921 – 13 Sep 2009), *Perceiving God: The Epistemology of Religious Experience* (Cornell UP, 1991). [sources: WP-Perceiving_God; WP-William_Alston] Doxastic practices (perceptual practice cannot be justified non-circularly; we trust it because it is socially established and yields consistent outputs); Christian mystical practice meets the same standard; he admits other religions' practices might too. [source: SEP-RE; IEP-RefE §3a]
- **Objection (conflicting experiences)**: "Religious diversity is a prima facie defeater ... in the same way that wildly conflicting eyewitness reports undermine each other"; reports range from "nothingness as the ultimate reality" to "a vast impersonal consciousness" to "an infinitely perfect, personal creator". Also naturalistic explanations. **Replies**: (a) common core — Hick (1989), Gellman (2001); (b) Alston (1991) and Plantinga (2000): from within a tradition one gains epistemic resources others lack ("travelling to the heart of a jungle"). Axtell: holding one's own view is "counter-inductive". [source: SEP-RE]
- Yandell (1993) classifies experiences as monotheistic, nirvanic (Buddhist), kevalic (Jain), moksha (Hindu), and nature experiences — useful for the global angle. [source: SEP-RE §2]

## 6. The Indian debate
- Nyāya "specifically takes it upon itself to defend the existence of God (Īśvara)", mainly by design inferences; Udayana, *Nyāyakusumāñjali* ch. 5: "Things like the earth have a maker as their cause, because they are products (kāryatvāt)" (Dasti's rendering). Buddhists, Mīmāṃsakas and later Jains charge violation of inferential bounds; Vācaspati (NVT 4.1.21) replies even ordinary inference reaches new kinds of thing. [source: IEP-Nya §3] (Already in published *Classical Indian Schools* — recap.)
- Udayana's dates: "c. 975" (IEP-Nya); "eleventh century" (SEP-EpI). → late 10th–11th century. [sources: IEP-Nya §1; SEP-EpI]
- Gaṅgeśa's (14th c.) form, after Vācaspati (c. 950): "Earth and the like have a conscious agent as an instrumental cause, since they are effects, like a pot and unlike an atom". Clever three-way division (Vācaspati): uncreated things (atoms), clearly made things (pots), disputed things (earth, growing grass) — counterexamples cannot be drawn from the disputed class. Buddhist *upādhi* (having a perceptible body) — Mokṣākaragupta; Patil 2009; Gaṅgeśa's regress reply favouring a bodiless creator with ungenerated knowledge. [source: SEP-Gan §5.4] (Already in published chapter — recap.)
- **New for this chapter — Udayana's dependence argument (NK 1.4)**: "a very abbreviated version" of the cosmological argument; since the universe "has parts that come into existence at one occasion and not another, it must have a cause"; an infinite regress could be admitted only with evidence for it; lacking that, God is the non-dependent cause. Objection: God, being bodiless and immobile, cannot properly cause; reply: God could assume a body, and need not create as humans do (Potter 1977: 100–07). [source: SEP-Cos §1] Udayana argues effects that occur only sometimes (*kādācitka*) must be caused. [source: docs/.../ancient--indian-dissenters.md citing SEP naturalism-india]
- Nyāya's further argument: the Veda, like all sentences, has a speaker; Īśvara is "the best or only candidate, according to Udayana"; Mīmāṃsā "is atheistic, viewing the Veda as primordial" (authorless). [source: SEP-EpI §6 area]
- NK has 5 chapters; ch. 1–4 refute anti-theists (some map them to Cārvāka, Mīmāṃsā, Buddhist, Jain, Sāṃkhya); ch. 5 positive proofs (eight headings in NK 5.1: from effects, combining of atoms, support, etc.). Chemparathy (1972): most elaborate Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika work on Īśvara. [source: docs/.../medieval--classical-indian-schools.md (WP; WS)] (Second-hand; use only "several proofs".)
- **Mīmāṃsā (Kumārila, 7th c.)**: his defence of the eternity of language "involves critiques of theism that are, indeed, very much like some characteristically Buddhist critiques of theism" (Arnold, citing Krasser 1999, "Dharmakīrti's and Kumārila's Refutations of the Existence of God: A Consideration of their Chronological Order"); Jayanta Bhaṭṭa worried Kumārila made "an essentially atheistic case for the eternality of the Vedas"; Arnold notes nothing intrinsically anti-theistic in his epistemology (Alston's is similar). [source: SEP-Kum §3.1]
- Kumārila's specific arguments (Ślokavārttika, Sambandhākṣepaparihāra vv. ~74–82): restates an opponent's design inference (body like a house); objects that God's own body would be a counterexample (made, configured, yet not made by a further agent — else infinite regress); ridicules a creator Prajāpati before matter: without a body how could he desire? [source: WS-5 (Krasser; jainqq summaries) — SECOND-HAND; also Bilimoria's reading in published chapter]
- **Buddhist (Dharmakīrti, c. 600–660 / 6th–7th c.; dates disputed)**: argument from existence (*sattvānumāna*): a permanent thing would be causally inert, producing effects neither all at once nor serially; if a permanent cause plus changing circumstances produced new effects, the circumstances would be the real cause (co-presence/co-absence test). "The logic in these arguments against permanence can be generalized to ... the non-Buddhist idea of the permanent creator God (īśvara) who remains the same throughout time while supposedly producing a series of effects." [source: SEP-Dhk §1.3] (SEP's own analogy: an epidemiologist discounts factors constant before and during an outbreak.)
- Dharmakīrti's refutation of a creator is in the *Pramāṇasiddhi* chapter (PV II) of the *Pramāṇavārttika*, aimed at an "omniscient, permanent, independent" Īśvara; Roger Jackson, "Dharmakīrti's Refutation of Theism"; Krasser compares it with Kumārila. [source: WS-4 — second-hand; verse numbers not verified]
- **Ratnakīrti** (11th c., Vikramaśīla; disciple of Jñānaśrīmitra), one of the last great Buddhist philosophers in India; Parimal G. Patil, *Against a Hindu God: Buddhist Philosophy of Religion in India* (Columbia UP, 2009) studies his critique of the Nyāya inference. [sources: SEP-Yog (dates, teacher); WS-6 (publisher description); SEP-Gan bibliography]
- **Sāṃkhya**: "a strong Indian example of metaphysical dualism, but unlike many Western counterparts it is atheistic"; "Sāṅkhya analyzes the cosmos into a dualistic, and atheistic scheme" (prakṛti + many puruṣas, both eternal); its gods are "extra-lo[cal]" beings within the world, not a creator. [source: IEP-Sank §§1, 3] (Yoga, by contrast, has Īśvara — published chapter.)
- **Jains**: "frequently said to be atheistic. What is denied is a creator god above all. The universe is eternal, matter and souls being equally uncreated"; gods exist but are subject to karma. [source: IEP-Jain]
- **Jinasena quotation**: attributed to Jinasena's *Mahāpurāṇa* (*Ādipurāṇa*), ch. 4, vv. 16–31, 38–40; English in *Sources of Indian Tradition*, vol. 1 (Columbia UP, 1958; ed. Wm. Theodore de Bary; Jain section often credited to A. T. Embree in WP refs), pp. 80–82; made famous as an epigraph in Carl Sagan's *Cosmos* (1980, ch. 10). Wording as printed: "Some foolish men declare that a creator made the world. The doctrine that the world was created is ill-advised, and should be rejected. If God created the world, where was he before the creation? If you say he was transcendent then and needed no support, where is he now? ... How could God have made this world without any raw material? If you say that he made this first, and then the world, you are faced with an endless regression." Further verses: if perfect, how could the will to create arise?; if out of love, "why did he not make creation wholly blissful"? Jinasena c. 770–850 CE, Digambara; Mahāpurāṇa completed by his pupil Guṇabhadra, 9th century. [sources: WP-Mahapurana_(Jainism); WP-Jinasena; WP-Jainism_and_non-creationism; WS-1] **Status**: attribution and chapter/verse are consistent across WP pages but rest on WP; the *translator* is NOT verified (WP cites "A.T. Embree" as editor/compiler; Sproul's *Primal Myths* 1979 reprints it). Writer: attribute as "a passage attributed to Jinasena's *Mahāpurāṇa*, in the translation printed in *Sources of Indian Tradition* (1958)".

## 7. Pascal's Wager
- Blaise Pascal (1623–1662). *Pensées* left unfinished as notes "pinned together"; the "Infinite—nothing" (*Infini rien*) fragment is "two pieces of paper covered on both sides by handwriting going in all directions" (Hacking 1972: 24). Published posthumously (Port-Royal edition 1670 — SEP-Fid cites "1670"). [sources: WP-Pascal's_wager; SEP-PW §1; SEP-Fid §2.2.1]
- Numbering: Brunschvicg **233** = Lafuma **418** = Sellier 680; Trotter's translation (1910) uses 233; SEP quotes Trotter §233. Thirouin calls it "fragment 418". [sources: SEP-PW intro; WS-3; WP-Pascal's_wager]
- SEP: Pascal "apparently presents **four** such arguments, each of which might be called a 'wager'—it is only the **third** of these that is traditionally referred to as 'Pascal's Wager'"; uses "for almost the first time in history" probability and decision theory. [source: SEP-PW intro]
- Key wording (Trotter): "'God is, or He is not.' But to which side shall we incline? Reason can decide nothing here. There is an infinite chaos which separated us. A game is being played at the extremity of this infinite distance where heads or tails will turn up… Let us weigh the gain and the loss in wagering that God is… If you gain, you gain all; if you lose, you lose nothing. Wager, then, without hesitation that He is." Then: "That is very fine. Yes, I must wager; but I may perhaps wager too much." And: "But there is here an infinity of an infinitely happy life to gain, a chance of gain against a finite number of chances of loss, and what you stake is finite." [source: SEP-PW §§2–4]
- ARGUMENT (SEP's standard reconstruction, the third argument): Premise 1 (decision table): wager for God → ∞ if God exists, f1 if not; wager against → f2 if God exists, f3 if not (f's finite). Premise 2: rationality requires a positive, finite probability that God exists. Premise 3: rationality requires maximising expected utility. Conclusion: E(wager for) = ∞·p + f1(1−p) = ∞; E(wager against) finite; so rationality requires wagering for God. [source: SEP-PW §4]
- Pascal's guiding insight: works "whatever your probability for God's existence is, provided that it is non-zero and finite". [source: SEP-PW §4]
- **Many-gods objection** (the most important): the partition God/no God is too coarse; Diderot (1746): "An Imam could reason just as well this way"; Mackie (1982: 203): the church "is not necessarily the Church of Rome, but perhaps that of the Anabaptists or the Mormons or the Muslim Sunnis or the worshippers of Kali or of Odin"; Cargile (1966): a god for each real number. **Replies**: probability as tie-breaker (Schlesinger 1994: "In cases where the mathematical expectations are infinite, the criterion for choosing the outcome to bet on is its probability"); Jordan: outlandish gods lack "backing of tradition"; Lycan & Schlesinger 1989: simplicity favours an "absolutely perfect" God; Bartha 2012 deliberational dynamics. Saka (2018) replies that Pascal's peers knew many religions. [source: SEP-PW §5.1]
- **Infinite-utility objections**: salvation's utility could not be infinite or could only be finitely appreciated (Jeffrey 1983; McClennen 1994); more infinities in the table (a forgiving God rewards everyone — Rescher 1985; −∞ for wagering against); **mixed strategies** (Duff 1986; Hájek 2003): "Toss a fair coin: heads, you wager for God" also has infinite expectation, as does any strategy with positive probability of wagering for God — so the argument is invalid as stated; St Petersburg paradox; refinements by expected differences (Hájek, Colyvan) or utility ratios (Bartha 2007). [source: SEP-PW §§5.1–5.3]
- Other objections: probability may be undefined ("epistemic nullity", Morris) or zero (strict atheists, Oppy 1990); belief is not voluntary — Pascal's answer: act "as if they believed, taking the holy water, having masses said, etc."; "vous abêtira" (Trotter: "will deaden your acuteness"; literally "will make you a beast"); moral objections (Clifford; Penelhum 1971); God might not reward mercenary belief (James). [source: SEP-PW §§5.2, 5.5, 7]
- Precursors: Abelard's **Slave's Wager** in the *Collationes* ("apparently the earliest-known version of Pascal's Wager"); Sirmond (1637) and Chillingworth (1638) (Franklin 2018); al-Juwaynī's *Kitāb al-irshād* discussed a version (WP). [sources: SEP-Ab; SEP-PW §1; WP-Pascal's_wager (Juwaynī second-hand)]
- Voltaire called the wager as a proof "indecent and childish": "the interest I have to believe a thing is no proof that such a thing exists". [source: WP-Pascal's_wager] (Translator not given.)
- "God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob", not "of the philosophers" — from the Memorial, the scrap of paper sewn in Pascal's coat (1654 experience). [source: SEP-Fid §2.2.1]

## 8. Faith and reason: evidentialism, James, reformed epistemology, fideism
- Clifford (1845–1879), "The Ethics of Belief", *Contemporary Review* 1877. Wording varies across reliable sources: SEP-EpR (citing Clifford 1879, *Lectures and Essays*: 186): "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence"; SEP-EB: "...for anyone to believe anything on insufficient evidence"; IEP-Evid: "...for anyone to believe anything upon insufficient evidence"; SEP-Fid (via James): "for every one". **Use the SEP-EpR form** (matches published *American Pragmatism*). [sources: SEP-EpR §2; SEP-EB §2; IEP-Evid; SEP-Fid §3; WS-2]
- Hume: "A wise man ... proportions his belief to the evidence." [source: IEP-Evid; published *Berkeley and Hume*]
- James's "The Will to Believe" (1896): recap only (fully set out in *American Pragmatism*). Peirce later deplored "willing not to exert the will (willing to believe)". [source: docs/.../nineteenth--pragmatism.md]
- **Evidentialist objection** (IEP-RefE): (1) belief in God requires the right kind of evidence to be rational; (2) no such evidence exists; (3) so belief in God is not rational. Traditional theists reject (2); reformed epistemologists reject (1). [source: IEP-RefE §1]
- Reformed epistemology "first clearly articulated" in *Faith and Rationality* (1983), ed. Alvin Plantinga and Nicholas Wolterstorff; Plantinga, "Reason and Belief in God" (in that volume); Calvin's *sensus divinitatis*. [source: IEP-RefE §§1–2]
- Plantinga's self-referential argument: classical foundationalism is "self-referentially inconsistent" (Plantinga 1983: 60): it is not justified to hold a belief according to which that very belief is unjustified. [source: SEP-EpR §2]
- Properly basic: sensory beliefs are treated as foundational without infallibility; beliefs formed by the *sensus divinitatis* ("M-beliefs") can be properly basic by parity. [source: SEP-RE §3]
- Wolterstorff: "Our beliefs are rational unless we have reason for refraining; ... They are innocent until proved guilty". [source: IEP-RefE §3c]
- Alvin Plantinga (born 15 Nov 1932; Calvin College, Notre Dame); *Warranted Christian Belief* (Oxford UP, 2000), third of the warrant trilogy after *Warrant: The Current Debate* and *Warrant and Proper Function* (both 1993); "Aquinas/Calvin model": the Holy Spirit heals a cognitive disorder so Christian belief is formed immediately, non-inferentially; if true, it is warranted — "One cannot attack claims of religious experience without first addressing the question as to whether the religious claims are true." [sources: WP-Alvin_Plantinga; WP-Warranted_Christian_Belief; SEP-EpR §7; SEP-RE §3]
- **Great Pumpkin objection**: if belief in God can be properly basic, why not Linus's belief in the Great Pumpkin? First raised by Plantinga himself ("Reason and Belief in God", 74–78); reply: we can tell meaningless sentences ("'Twas brillig...") without general criteria; Michael Martin's stronger "Son of the Great Pumpkin" version: other communities could make their beliefs properly basic by parallel reasoning. [source: IEP-RefE §5a]
- Criticism (Forrest): Plantinga's later position "largely ignores the question of justification, or reasonableness". [source: SEP-EpR §7]
- **Fideism** — Plantinga's definition: "exclusive or basic reliance upon faith alone, accompanied by a consequent disparagement of reason" (1983: 87). Usually linked with Pascal, Kierkegaard, James and Wittgenstein (SEP: "how well the label fits" is assessed). [source: SEP-Fid §§1, 2.2]
- Tertullian: "What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem?"; **misattribution**: he never said "credo quia absurdum"; his words (*De carne Christi* 5): "credibile est quia ineptum est" — "by all means to be believed, because it is absurd… the fact is certain, because it is impossible"; Osborn: "Not only did he never say 'credo quia absurdum', but he never meant anything like it"; "a most improbable fideist". [source: SEP-Fid §2] (SEP prints "credible est" — a typo for *credibile*.)
- Kierkegaard: pseudonymous; *The Point of View* (1848): the whole work concerns "the problem 'of becoming a Christian'" against the illusion of Christendom; speculative philosophy (Hegel) distorts faith. Cross-ref *Schopenhauer and Kierkegaard* (do not repeat Abraham; do not say "leap of faith"). [source: SEP-Fid §2.2.2]
- Halevi as Jewish fideist; al-Ghazālī's *Deliverance* "light which God Most High cast into my breast" — published chapters; cross-ref.

## 9. ARGUMENT against: divine hiddenness (Schellenberg 1993)
- J. L. Schellenberg (born 1959), *Divine Hiddenness and Human Reason* (Cornell UP, 1993); later the terms "reasonable/inculpable nonbelief" were refined into "nonresistant nonbelief" (2007). [sources: WP-J._L._Schellenberg; SEP-DH §1; IEP-DH]
- Two senses: the existential experience of God's absence (Psalms; Jesus's cry of dereliction; "dark night of the soul") vs the philosophical argument from nonbelief. [source: SEP-DH §1]
- Step by step (SEP): (1) There are people capable of relating personally to God who, through no fault of their own, fail to believe. (2) If there is a personal God who is unsurpassably great, there are no such people. (3) So there is no such God. Support for (2): (2a) unsurpassably great → unsurpassably loving; (2b) a perfectly loving God would make a relationship available to anyone capable who is not culpably resisting; (2c) relating personally to God requires believing God exists; (2d) so no inculpable nonbelief. [source: SEP-DH §2]
- **Replies** (theodicy-like): her moral freedom or development would be thwarted if God gave belief now (Hick 1966; Murray 2002; Swinburne 1979); belief now would lessen desire for God or breed complacency (Kierkegaard 1844); presumptuous relation without contrition (Pascal, Fragments 234, 378, 427 ...); God's love need not be like human love / divine transcendence (Rea 2016, 2018). Rowe-style "no reason we know of" inference also contested (skeptical theism). [source: SEP-DH §§1, 3–4]
- Related: Maitzen (demographics of nonbelief), Marsh ("natural nonbelief") — naturalism explains the distribution better. [source: SEP-DH §1]

## 10. Religious diversity
- Basinger's three categories: **exclusivism** (one's view on a disputed religious claim is true or closer to truth than others); **inclusivism** (one's religion is fullest, others partially true; some — Pinnock 1992 — hold only the one religion saves, others — Rahner 1966 — do not); **pluralism** (no religion has primacy; Hick 2004); "radical" pluralists (Heim 1995) reject a single ultimate reality. [source: SEP-RP §2]
- Plantinga's defence of exclusivism: exclusivists need not concede epistemic parity; if their beliefs are true they are probably better placed (Plantinga 1997 "Ad Hick"; 2000: 189). [source: SEP-RP §3]
- John Hick (1922–2012); *An Interpretation of Religion: Human Responses to the Transcendent* (Macmillan/Yale, 1989; 2nd ed. 2004), based on his 1986–87 Gifford Lectures. [source: IEP-Hick; SEP-RP bibliography]
- Hick's hypothesis: the world is "religiously ambiguous"; Kantian distinction between the Real as humanly experienced (personae: Yahweh, Trinity, Allah, Shiva, Vishnu; impersonae: Brahman, Dharmakaya) and the Real *an sich*, which "cannot be said to be one or many, person or thing, substance or process, good or evil, purposive or non-purposive" (IR 246); salvation = "the transformation of human existence from self-centredness to Reality-centeredness" (IR 300). [source: IEP-Hick §4]
- Main argument: "transformation parity"; each tradition "constitutes a valid context of salvation/liberation; but none constitutes the one and only such context" (Hick 1984). [source: SEP-RP §6]
- **Objections**: secular commitments transform lives too (Clark 1997); Hick's ethical criterion is arbitrary and makes him an "inclusivist in disguise" (Heim; Meeker 2003); Mavrodes: why not polytheism, "all the gods ... are real in the same sense that cantaloupes are real on the Kantian view"; Rowe and Plantinga: for every pair of contradictory properties one must literally apply to the Real (Plantinga: the Real is literally not a tricycle). [sources: SEP-RP §6; IEP-Hick §5]
- **Blind men and elephant**: often used to illustrate pluralism (a commentator, Keith E. Johnson, applied it to Hick — WP). Oldest datable version is **Buddhist** (Tittha Sutta, Udāna 6.4), where the Buddha uses it *against* quarrelling sectarians who come to blows; Rumi's "Elephant in the Dark"; Saxe's 19th-c. poem. [sources: WP-Blind_men_and_an_elephant; WP-John_Hick; published *Jains...*] WP's claim that it is "found in the most ancient Jain agams before 5th century BCE" is unsourced and contradicts the published chapter — do not use.
