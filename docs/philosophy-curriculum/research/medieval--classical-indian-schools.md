## 1. "Six darśanas": a later doxographic scheme (FLAG)
- *Darśana* = "vision"; conventionally used for systematic philosophical views. "A convention has developed over the centuries to count systematic Hindu philosophy as being comprised of six (āstika, or Veda recognizing) darśanas": Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṅkhya, Yoga, Pūrvamīmāṃsā and Vedānta. How many darśanas there are "depends largely on the organizational question of how one is to enumerate" them. [source: IEP-Hindu §3]
- IEP-Hindu: "most of the schools of Hindu philosophy have existed side by side", so the conventional order of exposition is **not a historical order**. [source: IEP-Hindu §3]
- The āstika/nāstika distinction "was not clearly defined until the late medieval period"; the *ṣaḍdarśana* ("six systems") schema "was created between the 12th and 16th centuries by Vedantins", then "adopted by the early Western Indologists, and pervades modern understandings of Indian philosophy" (citing Andrew Nicholson, *Unifying Hinduism*, 2010, pp. 2–5). Satoshi Ogura (2024) also points to Persianate classifications (the *Ā'īn-i Akbarī*) behind the "six systems" category in Western Indology. [source: WP-Hindu_philosophy]
- Earlier doxographies counted differently. The Jain Haribhadra's *Ṣaḍdarśanasamuccaya* ("Compendium of Six Views") lists Bauddha, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya, Jaiminīya (Mīmāṃsā) and Jaina, with Lokāyata treated separately or as an appendix (sources differ). Haribhadra's date: 5th–6th c. (Nicholson, via WP) or 8th c. (Encyclopedia of Buddhism). [source: WS-4 (Bodleian catalogue entry; encyclopediaofbuddhism.org); WP-Haribhadra; WP-Āstika_and_nāstika] — **flag as second-hand.**
- Haribhadra (per Nicholson) did **not** use acceptance of the Vedas as the criterion: a *nāstika* is one "who says there is no other worlds, there is no purpose in charity, there is no purpose in offerings". [source: WP-Āstika_and_nāstika, citing Nicholson 2013, pp. 172–175]
- The fourteenth-century *Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha* (ascribed to Mādhava) presents sixteen systems (already established in the published "Jains, Ājīvikas and Materialists" chapter). [source: published Periergia text, see §12]
- WP-Āstika_and_nāstika: the orthodox/heterodox terminology "is a construct of Western languages, and lacks scholarly roots in Sanskrit". Halbfass (1988) and Richard King (1999) criticise the frame as Brahmanical and polemical. [source: WP-Āstika_and_nāstika]
- Yoga as a distinct school: "the first reference to Yoga itself as a distinct school seems to be in the writings of Śaṅkara in the 9th century C.E."; in the Upaniṣads and Epic, Sāṃkhya and Yoga "simply refer to the two distinct paths of salvation by meditation and salvation by knowledge". [source: IEP-Yoga §1]
- Pairing: Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika became syncretic in later centuries (e.g. Śaṅkara Miśra, 15th c.; Annaṃbhaṭṭa's *Tarkasaṃgraha*, 17th c.); Nyāya adopted Vaiśeṣika's categories "with some modifications". [source: IEP-Hindu §3a; IEP-Nyāya §2; WP-Nyaya]
- Safe chapter wording: "The neat list of 'six orthodox schools' is a later doxographic grouping, which hardened in the late medieval period and was taken up by European Indologists; the schools themselves grew up side by side, in constant argument with Buddhists, Jains and each other."

## 2. Root texts and dates (give as ranges)
**General caution:** "scholars are confident about the approximate dates that the texts ... were written ... [but] not in many cases as confident about the age of the schools themselves." [source: IEP-Hindu §3]

- **Nyāya-sūtra**, attributed to Gautama, also called Akṣapāda.
  - IEP-Nyāya: "c. 200 C.E."; SEP-Log: "c. 2nd ce, the actual redaction of which is thought by some to date to the third century ce"; IEP-Hindu: Gautama "2nd cent. C.E."; SEP-Chron: 2nd century CE. [sources as named]
  - WP: estimates range from the 6th c. BCE to the 2nd c. CE; some scholars see layers (Books 1 and 5 earliest). Terminus ante quem c. 2nd c. CE, because Nāgārjuna engages it. [source: WP-Nyāya_Sūtras citing Potter 2004, Fowler 2002]
  - Structure: five books of two chapters each, about 528 sūtras (Chowkhamba edition; manuscripts differ slightly). [source: WP-Nyāya_Sūtras]
  - Opens with a list of sixteen topics (means of knowledge, objects of knowledge, doubt, purpose, example, tenet, members of an argument, tarka, ascertainment, vāda, jalpa, vitaṇḍā, pseudo-reasons, quibble, futile rejoinders, points of defeat), grouped by Dasti as epistemology, metaphysics, procedures of inquiry, and debate theory. [source: IEP-Nyāya §1; IEP-Hindu §3a]
  - NS 1.1.2: the goal is liberation, through removing wrong knowledge. [source: WP-Nyāya_Sūtras] Book 5 (24 "futile rejoinders", *jāti*, NS 5.1) and much of Book 1 concern debate. [source: SEP-Log §3.1.3, §3.3]
  - Nāgārjuna's *Vaidalya-prakaraṇa* "is organized around the key terms listed in the first aphorism of the Nyāya-sūtra" and argues each is incoherent. [source: SEP-Log §3.1.3]
- **Vaiśeṣika-sūtra**, attributed to Kaṇāda ("atom-eater", also Kaṇabhakṣa; Ulūka in Chinese sources).
  - SEP-Log: c. 1st century CE; IEP-Hindu: Kaṇāda 1st c. CE; SEP-Chron: Kaṇāda 2nd c. BCE; WP: 6th–2nd c. BCE (Fowler), Riepe "before 300 CE". **Wide disagreement; say "the last centuries BCE or the first century CE".** [sources as named; WP-Kaṇāda, WP-Vaisheshika]
  - Ten books. [source: WP-Vaisheshika] Opens by announcing an exposition of *dharma* (VS 1.1.1–2), which results in prosperity and the supreme good; VS 1.1.4 names six categories. [source: IEP-Hindu §3b]
- **Sāṃkhya-kārikā** of Īśvarakṛṣṇa.
  - IEP-Sāṅkhya: "ca. 350 C.E.", after the Nyāya-sūtra and before Vasubandhu; "Of Īśvarakṛṣṇa we know nothing". IEP-Yoga: 4th–5th c. CE. IEP-Hindu: 4th c. CE. Larson: Gupta period, 320–540 CE. [sources as named; WP-Samkhyakarika citing Larson]
  - 72 verses in the āryā metre; the last verse says the original had 70 (the "Golden Seventy"); Paramārtha's Chinese translation with commentary (557–569 CE) is the oldest surviving version. [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §1; WP-Samkhyakarika]
  - Sāṃkhya is "essentially a one-book school": the Kārikā "ousted all previous Sāṅkhya writings, of which only stray quotations remain". Its last verse calls it a condensation of the lost *Ṣaṣṭi-tantra*. [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §1]
  - Commentaries: Gauḍapāda's *Bhāṣya*, Māṭhara's *Vṛtti*, the *Jayamaṅgalā*; the *Yukti-dīpikā* (c. 700, the most important, little known outside Kashmir); Vācaspati Miśra's *Sāṅkhya-tattva-kaumudī* (c. 980 per Ruzsa). The *Sāṅkhya-sūtra* is late (15th c.), with a commentary by Vijñānabhikṣu (16th c.). [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §1]
  - Legendary founders: Kapila and his disciple Āsuri, "known only through ancient legends"; Pañcaśikha perhaps historical. [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §1]
- **Yoga-sūtra** of Patañjali.
  - IEP-Yoga (Bryant): "Most scholars seem to date the text shortly after the turn of the common era, (c. 1st – c. 2nd century C.E.)", though placed as early as several centuries BCE; it "does not postdate the 5th century C.E." IEP-Sāṅkhya: "ca. 300 C.E."; IEP-Hindu: "roughly 3rd century C.E." [sources as named]
  - Philipp Maas dates the *Pātañjalayogaśāstra* (sūtras plus *bhāṣya*, which he argues are one work by one author) to about 400 CE, partly from synchronisms with Vasubandhu; Woods proposed this in 1914; WP calls this dating "accepted widely". Bryant (2009) contests late dates as "problematic". **Name this as a live debate.** [source: WP-Yoga_Sutras_of_Patanjali]
  - About 1,200 words in 195 sūtras (Vyāsa's count; others count 196), in four chapters (*pādas*): samādhi, sādhana, vibhūti, kaivalya. [source: IEP-Yoga §1; WP-Yoga_Sutras]
  - Tradition (first in Bhoja, 11th c.) identifies him with the grammarian Patañjali of the *Mahābhāṣya* (2nd c. BCE); the identification is disputed (Louis Renou argued against it). [source: IEP-Yoga §1; WP-Yoga_Sutras]
- **Mīmāṃsā-sūtra** of Jaimini.
  - SEP-Kum: Jaimini "thought to have flourished near the beginning of the first millennium ce"; IEP-Hindu: 1st c. CE; SEP-Chron and Hiriyanna (via WP): c. 3rd–2nd c. BCE. **Range: 3rd c. BCE – 1st c. CE.** [sources as named; WP-Purva_Mimamsa_Sutras]
  - Twelve chapters (*adhyāyas*), sixty sections. [source: WP-Purva_Mimamsa_Sutras citing Hiriyanna]
  - "Pūrva" ("prior") Mīmāṃsā = inquiry into the earlier, ritual portion of the Veda (culminating in the Brāhmaṇas); also called Karma Mīmāṃsā. "Uttara" Mīmāṃsā = Vedānta. [source: SEP-Kum §1.2]

## 4. Nyāya: the pramāṇas and the debates over them
- *Nyāya* = "rule or method of reasoning". Nyāya's epistemology is "a sophisticated precursor to contemporary reliabilism" centred on knowledge-sources (*pramāṇa*); metaphysically it is robustly realist (universals, selves, substances), "largely in debate with Buddhist anti-realists"; its thinkers were "India's most sophisticated natural theologians". [source: IEP-Nyāya intro]
- *Pramāṇa* = means/process generating veridical cognition (*pramā*); pramāṇa-terms are factive: "no knowledge source ever generates a false belief. Only pseudo-sources do." [source: SEP-Epi §1.1; IEP-Nyāya §1]
- **Nyāya's four pramāṇas**: perception (*pratyakṣa*), inference (*anumāna*), analogy/comparison (*upamāna*) and testimony (*śabda*). Nyāya holds these four "are the only irreducible sources of knowledge". [source: IEP-Nyāya §1]
- How many pramāṇas each school accepts:
  - Cārvāka: perception only. Buddhists (Dignāga, Dharmakīrti): perception and inference. Vaiśeṣika: perception and inference (testimony reduced to inference). [source: SEP-Kum §2.1; SEP-Epi §6; WP-Hindu_philosophy]
  - Sāṃkhya: three — perception, inference, reliable testimony (*āpta-vacana*), the last understood as the Vedas only and "never used to derive or confirm philosophical theses". [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §3]
  - Yoga: the same three (YS 1.7). [source: IEP-Yoga §5]
  - Nyāya: four.
  - Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā: five (adds *arthāpatti*, presumption); Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā: six (adds *anupalabdhi/abhāva*, non-cognition). [source: WP-Mīmāṃsā; WP-Hindu_philosophy; SEP-Epi §7.3; SEP-Kum §4]
- **Perception**, NS 1.1.4 (Dasti's translation): "A perceptual cognition arises by means of the connection between sense faculty and object, is not dependent on words, is non-deviating, and is determinate." Uddyotakara lists six kinds of sense–object connection. Perception is the "eldest" pramāṇa (*jyeṣṭha-pramāṇa*) because the others depend on it. Unlike Dignāga, Nyāya holds ordinary perception is conceptual. Vācaspati reads the definition as covering both non-propositional and propositional perception. [source: IEP-Nyāya §1a]
- Extraordinary perception (from Jayanta on): yogic perception; perception of a universal through an individual (Nyāya's answer to the problem of induction: seeing all smoke *qua* smoke as tied to fire); memory-mediated perception ("seeing" sandalwood as fragrant). [source: IEP-Nyāya §1a.ii]
- **Inference**, NS 1.1.5: "[An inferential cognition] is preceded by that [perception], and is threefold: from cause to effect, from effect to cause or from that which is commonly seen." Anumāna = "after-cognizing". It needs *pakṣadharmatā* (the mark is in the subject) and *vyāpti* (invariable concomitance). [source: IEP-Nyāya §1b]
- **Five-membered argument** (cross-refer to "What Is Philosophy?", which gives the Caraka example; do not repeat at length). Nyāya stock example: thesis (*pratijñā*) "There is fire on the hill"; reason (*hetu*) "because there is smoke"; illustration (*udāharaṇa*) "Wherever there is smoke, there is fire, like a kitchen hearth and unlike a lake"; application (*upanaya*) "This hill is likewise smoky"; conclusion (*nigamana*). Often truncated to three steps. [source: IEP-Nyāya §1b.ii]
  - Gillon: the Caraka-saṃhitā (CS 3.8.31), like NS 1.1.32, has a five-part syllogism with four of the five names identical; the early forms are analogical; deductively valid forms appear in Buddhist debate manuals; Vasubandhu's *Vāda-vidhi* makes the example state an "indispensability relation". [source: SEP-Log §3.2]
  - Nyāya does not stress validity vs soundness: both formal flaws and false premises are *hetvābhāsa* ("pseudo-provers"). Five common kinds: deviating, contradictory, unestablished, rebutted, counterbalanced. [source: IEP-Nyāya §1b.iii]
  - Inference for oneself vs for others: terms coined by Dignāga, distinction "implicit" in the NS. [source: IEP-Nyāya §1b.i]
- *Tarka* (suppositional/reductio reasoning) is not a pramāṇa but an "assistant to the pramāṇas" (Vātsyāyana). [source: IEP-Nyāya §1b.iv]
- **Analogy**, NS 1.1.6: "Analogy makes an object known by similarity with something already known." Standard example: told that a *gavaya* (wild ox/buffalo) is like a cow, one later recognises the animal and learns the word's reference. For Nyāya it is mainly vocabulary acquisition; most other schools reduce it or (Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta) treat it as the means of knowing similarity itself. [source: IEP-Nyāya §1c; SEP-Epi §7.1]
- **Testimony**, NS 1.1.7: "Testimony is the assertion of a qualified speaker" (Dasti) / "the (true) statement of an expert (āpta)" (Phillips). Vātsyāyana: an āpta knows the truth and wants to convey it without deception; "even foreigners—'barbarians,' mleccha—can be the experts". Nyāya (with Mīmāṃsā) holds testimony gives knowledge directly, not by inference, against Vaiśeṣika and Buddhist inferentialists. [source: IEP-Nyāya §1d; SEP-Epi §6]
  - NOTE: IEP-Nyāya prints "NS 1.1.5" for the testimony sūtra; SEP-Epi gives 1.1.7. 1.1.7 is correct by the sūtra order (1.1.4 perception, 1.1.5 inference, 1.1.6 analogy). Use 1.1.7.
- **Nyāya vs Mīmāṃsā on validity**: Nyāya holds *parataḥ prāmāṇya* (validity is certified extrinsically: knowing does not entail knowing that you know), but treats veridicality as the default ("innocent until reasonably challenged"); doubt triggers checking ("Where there is doubt, there must be ongoing examination", NS 2.1.7). Vātsyāyana rebuts Nāgārjuna's regress charge under NS 2.1.20: if certifying every pramāṇa led to infinite regress, "everyday action and discourse would not go on". [source: SEP-Epi §3; IEP-Nyāya §1f]
- Nyāya on scepticism: error and doubt are "conceptually parasitical on true cognition"; the sceptic relies on pramāṇas in voicing his argument. [source: IEP-Nyāya §1f.iv]
- Gaṅgeśa's definition of veridical cognition: "a cognitive state with predication content x about something in fact qualified by x". [source: IEP-Nyāya §1f.iii]
- Absences: Gautama and Vātsyāyana say absences are inferred; Uddyotakara and later Nyāya say they can be perceived (seeing the absence of a mug on the desk); Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā posits a separate pramāṇa, non-cognition. [source: SEP-Epi §7.3; IEP-Nyāya §2g]

## 5. Vaiśeṣika: categories, atoms and inherence
- *Padārtha* = "thing denoted by a word": all that exists, is knowable and nameable. [source: WP-Vaisheshika; IEP-Nyāya §2]
- **Six categories** of VS 1.1.4: substance (*dravya*), quality (*guṇa*), action/motion (*karma*), universal (*sāmānya*), particularity/individuator (*viśeṣa*), inherence (*samavāya*). [source: IEP-Hindu §3b] A **seventh**, absence (*abhāva*), was added later (WP names Śrīdhara, Udayana, Śivāditya). Mature Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika lists seven. [source: WP-Vaisheshika; IEP-Nyāya §2; SEP-Epi §7.1]
- **Nine substances**: earth, water, fire, air, ether (*ākāśa*), time, space/direction (*dik*), self (*ātman*), mind (*manas*). [source: WP-Vaisheshika] Dasti's list of paradigm substances includes God "a unique ātman". [source: IEP-Nyāya §2a]
- Qualities: 17 in the sūtra; Praśastapāda added 7, giving the standard 24 (adding heaviness, fluidity, viscosity, merit, demerit, sound, disposition). Qualities are non-repeatable tropes; universals are repeatable. [source: WP-Vaisheshika; WP-Prashastapada; IEP-Nyāya §2b]
- Udayana's six "universal-blockers" (*jātibādhaka*): a real universal must have more than one instance; two with the same instances are one; cross-cutting is excluded; no universal that would cause regress (universal-hood); none for individuators; none for inherence. [source: IEP-Nyāya §2d]
- **Inherence** relates qualities, actions, universals and individuators to substances, universals to qualities and actions, and wholes to their parts (a whole inheres in its parts). Nyāya holds it is "self-linking", to block Bradley-style regress objections, pressed in India by Śaṅkara. [source: IEP-Nyāya §2e] Kumārila too criticised inherence as opening an infinite regress, and held instead that universal and particular are aspects of the same thing (ŚV ākṛtivāda 8 ff.). [source: SEP-Kum §3.2] Praśastapāda defines it as the relation between inseparable things related as container and contained; it is inferred, not perceived (Vaiśeṣika view; later Nyāya holds it perceptible via the qualifier–qualified relation). [source: WP-Vaisheshika; IEP-Nyāya §1a]
- **Viśeṣa** ("individuator"): what ultimately distinguishes one atom (or self) from another of the same kind; Dasti compares it to haecceity. The school is named from it. [source: IEP-Nyāya §2f; SEP-Nat §2.1]
- **Atoms** (*paramāṇu*): of earth, water, fire, air; eternal, partless, spherical, of minimal magnitude; each kind has a specific quality (earth odour, water taste, fire colour, air touch). Wholes are non-eternal and are "new entities inhering in their own parts". [source: SEP-Nat §2.1]
- **Argument for atoms (step by step)**, SEP-Nat §2.1:
  1. Every visible substance has parts; so does the smallest visible thing (the mote in a sunbeam).
  2. A part is smaller than its whole; the parts of the smallest visible thing are invisible.
  3. Division must stop, or there is a vicious regress: if infinitely divisible, "a mountain and a mustard seed will then be equal in size", both having countless parts.
  4. It cannot stop at nothing, since a whole cannot be built from nothing ("as absurd a notion as digging a hole in empty space").
  5. So there are partless, indivisible, imperceptible atoms.
- Composition: two atoms make a dyad (*dvyaṇuka*); three dyads a triad (*tryaṇuka*), the smallest perceptible object. Perceptible size arises from the **number** of components, not their size. [source: SEP-Nat §2.1]
- Causation: Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika *ārambhavāda* / *asatkāryavāda*: an effect is the counter-correlative of its prior absence, a genuinely new product (opposed to Sāṃkhya satkāryavāda). Three kinds of cause: inherent (atoms/threads), non-inherent (conjunction of threads), efficient/instrumental (God and *adṛṣṭa* for the world; the weaver for cloth). [source: SEP-Nat §2.1; IEP-Nyāya §2h]
- God in Vaiśeṣika: Kaṇāda does not directly mention Īśvara; Praśastapāda treats Īśvara as cause of the universe. [source: WP-Prashastapada] (Second-hand; use cautiously.)

## 6. Nyāya arguments for God (Udayana) and the objections
- Nyāya "specifically takes it upon itself to defend the existence of God (Īśvara)", mainly by versions of the design inference. [source: IEP-Nyāya §3]
- Uddyotakara (NV 4.1.21): "Primordial matter, atoms and karma function when guided by a conscious agent because they are insentient ... like an axe." [source: IEP-Nyāya §3]
- Udayana, *Nyāyakusumāñjali* ("A Handful of Flowers of Logic", 5th chapter): "Things like the earth have a maker as their cause, because they are products (kāryatvāt)." [source: IEP-Nyāya §3]
- Structure of the NK: 73 verses in five chapters (*stabakas*) with prose; chapters 1–4 refute five anti-theist positions (some scholars map them to Cārvāka, Mīmāṃsā, Buddhist, Jain, Sāṃkhya); chapter 5 gives the positive proofs. Chemparathy (1972) calls it the most elaborate Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika work on Īśvara. [source: WP-Nyayakusumanjali citing Chemparathy]
- NK 5.1 lists the proofs as *kāryāyojanadhṛtyādeḥ padāt pratyayataḥ śruteḥ / vākyāt saṃkhyāviśeṣāc ca* ... — i.e. from effects (*kārya*), from the combining of atoms (*āyojana*), from support/sustaining (*dhṛti*) etc., from traditional usages (*pada*), from authoritativeness (*pratyaya*), from scripture (*śruti*), from Vedic sentences (*vākya*), from the special number (*saṃkhyāviśeṣa*). [source: WS-1/2 (citing a 2020 *Journal of Indian Philosophy* article and jainqq.org)] **Eight headings in the verse**; WP-Udayana lists nine (adding *adṛṣṭāt*) and gives garbled glosses of *padāt* and *saṃkhyāviśeṣāt* — see NOT VERIFIED. Udayana says the list "can be doubled" by reading the verse two ways (Ruzsa 2022, per WS-1).
- SEP-Cosm: an "abbreviated" cosmological argument from dependence is in NK 1.4: since the universe "has parts that come into existence at one occasion and not another, it must have a cause"; lacking evidence for an infinite regress, God is the non-dependent cause (citing Potter 1977). Udayana argues every event has a cause because it is "occasional" (*kādācitka*). [source: SEP-Cosm; SEP-Nat §1]
- **The kāryatva argument, step by step** (Gaṅgeśa's version, following Vācaspati; SEP-Gaṅg §5.4):
  1. Earth and the like are effects (products).
  2. Whatever is an effect has a conscious agent as an instrumental cause — like a pot, and unlike an atom (an uncaused thing with no agent).
  3. So earth and the like have a conscious agent as instrumental cause: Īśvara.
  - Division of things: uncreated (atoms), created with an evident maker (pots), and things whose maker is not evident (earth, growing grass). The third set is the subject (*pakṣa*). [source: SEP-Gaṅg §5.4]
- **Objection 1 (Buddhist counterexample):** growing grass is an effect with no agent. **Reply:** grass falls within the disputed subject class, so citing it begs the question. [source: SEP-Gaṅg §5.4]
- **Objection 2 (Buddhist *upādhi*, refined over centuries; Mokṣākaragupta; Patil 2009):** every agent we know has a perceptible body. "Having a body" pervades the probandum but not the reason (grass is an effect with no embodied maker), so the concomitance fails. **Reply (Gaṅgeśa, after Vācaspati):** deny that agency requires a body; if the creator needed a body, and bodies are made of atoms, an earlier creator would be needed *ad infinitum*; a bodiless creator with non-generated knowledge is simpler. [source: SEP-Gaṅg §5.4]
- **Objection 3 (Buddhist, Mīmāṃsā, later Jain):** the argument overreaches "inferential boundaries": ordinary products and makers cannot support a unique world-maker. **Reply (Vācaspati, NVT 4.1.21):** even ordinary induction involves inference to the best explanation, which allows new kinds of entities. [source: IEP-Nyāya §3]
- SEP-Cosm summarises objections "because of God's nature" (immobile and bodiless, so not a cause), with the Nyāya reply that God could assume a body or need not create as humans do. [source: SEP-Cosm]
- **Mīmāṃsā objections (Kumārila):** Kumārila's defence of the eternity of language "involves critiques of theism that are, indeed, very much like some characteristically Buddhist critiques" (Krasser 1999). [source: SEP-Kum §3.1] In the Ślokavārttika he argues it is implausible to hold both a divine creator and a beginning of the world; if God's body was created, who created it (regress)? If uncreated, why not say the same of ours? [source: WP-Kumārila_Bhaṭṭa citing Bilimoria 2001; WS-3] (Second-hand: no primary text read.)
- Nyāya vs Mīmāṃsā on the Veda's author: almost all Nyāya philosophers and Vedāntic theists hold the Veda, like all sentences, was composed by a speaker: the Lord (for Udayana, "the best or only candidate"). Mīmāṃsā holds it authorless. [source: SEP-Epi §6] Jayanta attacked the idea of authorless texts (e.g. in his play *Āgamaḍambara*); Arnold says this "misses the mark" against Kumārila's epistemology. [source: SEP-Kum §3.1]
- NK chapter 2 argues against intrinsic validity: "Validity of cognition is not intrinsic, but extrinsic." [source: WP-Nyayakusumanjali]
- Jain philosophers too refuted the Nyāya arguments (consistent with the published "Jains, Ājīvikas and Materialists" chapter). [source: IEP-Nyāya §3]

## 7. The debate with the Buddhists over the self
- Buddhist position (as IEP-Nyāya states it): no enduring self; diachronically, things last a moment; synchronically, wholes are heaps (chariot example in the *Milindapañha*); moral continuity is secured by causal connections within one stream. [source: IEP-Nyāya §2a]
- **NS 1.1.10**: "Desire, aversion, volition (prayatna), pleasure, pain, and cognition are characteristics of the self." (translation as given by Chadha) [source: SEP-Pers §1.3]
- **Argument from desire/qualities (step by step)**, IEP-Nyāya §2a and SEP-Gaṅg §5.4:
  1. Desire, cognition, pleasure, pain and volition are qualities.
  2. Every quality inheres in a substance.
  3. So there is a substance in which they inhere.
  4. It is none of the material substances (earth, water etc.): if desire were a property of earth, it would be perceived like colour in earthen things; nothing material has desire outside living bodies.
  5. So it is a distinct, non-material substance: the self.
- **Argument from memory/recognition (diachronic)**: recognition ("this is that Devadatta I saw yesterday") needs one subject spanning both times; "one person does not have another's memories". Uddyotakara, following Vātsyāyana: if I were a different self from yesterday's "me", I could not remember what that "me" experienced. NS 1.1.10 and commentaries. [source: IEP-Nyāya §2a; SEP-Gaṅg §5.4]
- Desire argument with memory: one who has experienced pleasure desires it again; desire presupposes remembered past experience in the same subject (Yoga's analysis of *rāga* is parallel: desire arises in one "who remembers past experiences of pleasure"). [source: IEP-Yoga §3] (Nyāya's precise NS 3.1 wording not read; keep general.)
- **Synchronic argument**: cross-modal recognition ("I am touching what I see") needs one experiencer who unifies the senses (NS 3.1.1–3). [source: IEP-Nyāya §2a; SEP-Gaṅg §5.4]
- Vātsyāyana's negative-only inference: "Every living body has a self, since every living body has breath, unlike a pot." [source: SEP-Gaṅg §5.4; IEP-Nyāya §1b.ii]
- Udayana's *Ātmatattvaviveka* ("Determining the Truth of the Self") is the "apex" of early Nyāya arguments for the self; its four chapters refute theories denying a permanent soul. [source: IEP-Nyāya §2a; WP-Udayana]
- Kumārila's version (following Śabara): the notion "I" (*ahaṃ-pratyaya*) has a real referent, directly encountered in the first-person content of memory, which is genuine recognition (*pratyabhijñā*) (ŚV ātmavāda 107–110). Unlike Nyāya, he claims the self is *encountered* in memory, not merely inferred. [source: SEP-Kum §3.3]
- **Buddhist reply**: memory is explained by causal continuity in one mental stream; Vasubandhu uses "seeds" metaphorically for "the capacity for recollection to arise originating from experiential knowledge", like a sprout from a seed (AKBh ad V.1d–2a). Dignāga and Dharmakīrti take memory's reflexive character as evidence only that moments of awareness are self-intimating (*svasaṃvitti*), not of a self. [source: SEP-MindB; SEP-Kum §3.3]
- Further objection: the Nyāya self, totally distinct from its changing qualities and dependent on mind–body contact for all activity, is "by itself causally impotent (this is, indeed, exactly the Buddhist argument against the existence of the Nyāya self)"; in liberation it lacks consciousness, "an insentient stone-like condition" (Dravid 1995). [source: SEP-Pers §1.3] Chadha's own criticism of Dasti; present as her interpretation.
- Nyāya reply on wholes: composites have capacities beyond their parts (NS 2.1.35); if wholes were mere heaps of imperceptible atoms, we could not perceive them at all (NS 2.1.36). [source: IEP-Nyāya §2a]

## 8. Sāṃkhya: puruṣa, prakṛti, the guṇas
- "Sāṅkhya" from *saṅkhyā* "number" / "taking account"; "very fond of sets". "Two millennia ago it was the representative Hindu philosophy." It is "a strong Indian example of metaphysical dualism, but unlike many Western counterparts it is atheistic." [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya intro, §1]
- Two kinds of entity: Prakṛti (Nature), singular; puruṣas (persons/selves), many. Both eternal and independent. Puruṣas are "unchangeable, inactive, conscious"; Prakṛti is unconscious but purposeful, acting "for the purpose of" the puruṣas. [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya intro]
- Twenty-five *tattvas*: puruṣa; unmanifest prakṛti; and 23 manifest evolutes: intellect (*buddhi*/*mahat*), ego (*ahaṃkāra*), mind (*manas*), five sense capacities, five action capacities, five subtle elements (*tanmātras*), five gross elements. [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §§4, 4d; SEP-Nat §2.4]
- **Three guṇas**: *sattva* (light, goodness, intelligence; pleasure), *rajas* (change, energy, passion; pain), *tamas* (inertia, heaviness, darkness; dullness/despair). They cooperate "like the parts of a lamp – the wick, the oil and the flame" (SK 13). Not ordinary qualities and not substances. [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §4b; IEP-Hindu §3c; WP-Samkhyakarika]
- Interpretations of the guṇas: B. N. Seal (form, energy, mass) vs K. C. Bhattacharya and J. N. Mohanty (affective/psychological reading); SEP-Nat thinks the psychological reading "has a closer fit with the text". [source: SEP-Nat §2.4]
- Starting point: universal suffering (three kinds); Vedic ritual is no lasting remedy. [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §2; WP-Samkhyakarika SK 1–2]
- **Proofs of puruṣa (SK 17)**, Ruzsa's paraphrase: complex things serve another (a bed is for someone to lie on); something opposite to the three guṇas must exist; coordination needs a supervisor; there must be an enjoyer; liberation needs something to be liberated. Colebrooke's translation (via WP): "Because the assemblage of empirically observed objects is for another's use ...; because there must be one to enjoy; because there is a tendency to abstraction; therefore soul exists." [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §4c; WP-Samkhyakarika]
- **Plurality of puruṣas (SK 18)**: different births, deaths and organs; not all act at once; different guṇa-mixtures. [source: SEP-Pers §1.2 (trans. adapted from Phukan 1960); IEP-Sāṅkhya §4c]
- **Lame man and blind man (SK 21)**: "For the sake of puruṣa's perception of prakṛti and for the sake of his release, a union of the two takes place, which resembles the union of the lame and the blind" (translation adapted from Majumdar 1930, as given by Chadha). [source: SEP-Pers §1.2]
- Other images: milk flows unconsciously to nourish the calf (SK 57); a dancer stops dancing once seen (SK 59); the potter's wheel keeps turning after liberation until death. [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §§4d, 5; SEP-Nat §2.4]
- SK 62 (Colebrooke, via WP): "No soul (Purusha) therefore is bound, no one released, likewise no one transmigrates; Only nature (Prakriti) in its various forms transmigrates, is bound and is released." [source: WP-Samkhyakarika]
- Liberation is *kaivalya* ("isolation"): discriminating knowledge of the difference between puruṣa and prakṛti, practised as "this constituent is not me; it is not mine; I am not this". [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §5]
- The "unbalanced" dualism: drop puruṣa and you still have "a fairly complete picture of the world", since prakṛti produces mind and intellect: "a full materialist account ... with the passive, unchanging principle of consciousness added almost as an afterthought" (Ruzsa). [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §4]
- Interaction problem: how can an inactive soul influence matter? Later answer (Vācaspati, Vijñānabhikṣu): reflection, like a mirror image. SEP-Nat: if puruṣa and prakṛti are always in contact, "the start of the creation process remains inexplicable". [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §4c–d; SEP-Nat §2.4]
- **Debate**: is puruṣa strictly unchanging? Many modern scholars say yes (A. B. Keith thought this made Sāṃkhya "a hopeless bundle of contradictions"; Larson: "contentless consciousness", outside time and causality); Ruzsa argues SK's *akriya* ("without activity") means only no spatial or mechanical action, and the strict reading reflects later Advaita influence. [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §4c]
- Theism: classical Sāṃkhya is atheist (Ruzsa); Bryant notes Sāṃkhya "was, in point of fact, widely theistic in its early expressions" and in the Purāṇas and Gītā; the late *Sāṅkhya-sūtra* argues against God. [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §1; IEP-Yoga §7]
- Relation to the Vedas: Sāṃkhya "is silent about the Vedas, about their guardians (the Brahmins) and ... the whole caste system", and "slightly inimical" to animal sacrifice (Ruzsa). [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §1]
- Antecedents: Aśvaghoṣa's *Buddhacarita* makes Arāḍa Kālāma, the Buddha's teacher, follow archaic Sāṃkhya; Sāṃkhya terms in the Kaṭha and Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣads; guṇa-like ideas in Chāndogya. [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §1]

## 9. Satkāryavāda: SK 9, argued step by step
- Sanskrit of SK 9: *asadakaraṇād upādānagrahaṇāt sarvasambhavābhāvāt / śaktasya śakyakaraṇāt kāraṇabhāvāc ca satkāryam* (from sanskritdocuments.org, via WP). [source: WP-Samkhyakarika; WP-Satkaryavada]
- Satkāryavāda = "existent effect theory": the effect pre-exists in its material cause; the stock example is clay and pot. Ruzsa suggests "the effect of existent [causes]", a moderate determinism: "there is nothing absolutely new in the product". [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §4a]
- **The five reasons** (gloss following Halbfass 1992, as given in WP; cf. Ruzsa):
  1. *asadakaraṇāt*: what does not exist cannot be produced (a non-existent "sky-flower" can never be made).
  2. *upādānagrahaṇāt*: an effect requires a (specific) material cause — clay for pots, milk for curds.
  3. *sarvasambhavābhāvāt*: not everything arises from everything (otherwise pots from threads).
  4. *śaktasya śakyakaraṇāt*: a potent cause produces only what it is capable of (oil from sesame, not sand; a potter cannot make jewellery).
  5. *kāraṇabhāvāt*: the effect has the nature of the cause (is essentially identical with it).
  - Conclusion: *satkāryam*, the effect exists (in the cause before production).
  [sources: WP-Satkaryavada citing Halbfass 1992 pp. 28, 56; IEP-Sāṅkhya §4a; SEP-Nat §2.4]
- SEP-Nat's reconstruction adds the relation argument: a cause produces an effect only by being related to it; a relation needs two relata; so the effect must already exist. The opponent's "potency" reply fails: the potency must be related to the effect, and "causal efficiency is nothing other than the existence of the effect in the material cause in a latent form". [source: SEP-Nat §2.4]
- Purpose: reason 5 lets Sāṃkhya infer the attributes of the imperceptible ultimate cause (prakṛti) from the manifest world, and (against Vedānta) that evolution is real, not illusory. [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §4a; SEP-Nat §2.4]
- **Objection (Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika, asatkāryavāda/ārambhavāda):** every effect is preceded by its prior absence and is a new product; taken literally, satkāryavāda "is not tenable—if the cause existed, why was it not perceived prior to the point called its production?" (Ruzsa's statement of the difficulty); and causal activity would seem pointless if the effect already exists. **Sāṃkhya reply:** causal operation only *manifests* what is latent (*āvirbhāva* from *tirobhāva*). Śaṅkara presses: if the effect pre-exists, how is there genuine change? [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §4a; SEP-Nat §2.1, 2.4; WP-Satkaryavada]
- Bronkhorst: the fifth reason (*kāraṇabhāvāt*) "is ambiguous, and allows of at least three different interpretations". [source: WS-6 (jainqq.org copy of Bronkhorst's article "The Last Reason for Satkāryavāda")] — second-hand.

## 10. Patañjali's Yoga: citta, the eight limbs, kaivalya
- Yoga shares Sāṃkhya's metaphysics but differs on method: Sāṃkhya the path of reasoning, Yoga the path of meditation; Yoga does not think metaphysical knowledge alone suffices. Larson calls classical Yoga a "neo-Sāṃkhya". [source: IEP-Yoga §§1–2]
- Patañjali "is not the founder or inventor of Yoga"; he systematised existing practice (YS 1.1 *atha yogānuśāsanam*, "now the teaching of yoga" continued). Shared vocabulary with Buddhist texts. [source: IEP-Yoga §1]
- **YS 1.2**: *yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ*. Woods (1914): "Yoga is the restriction of the fluctuations of mind-stuff"; Taimni (1961): "Yoga is the inhibition of the modifications of the mind." YS 1.3 (Taimni): "Then the Seer is established in his own essential and fundamental nature." [source: WP-Yoga_Sutras citing Woods and Taimni; WS-5] (Woods's wording not checked against the scan: archive.org unreachable.)
- *Citta*: Yoga treats buddhi, ahaṃkāra and manas as functions of one citta, not three layers. Five *vṛttis*: right knowledge, error, verbal imagination, deep sleep, memory (YS 1.5–11). Five *kleśas*: ignorance, ego(-sense), attachment, aversion, clinging to life (YS 2.3–9); ignorance is the root. [source: IEP-Yoga §§2–3]
- **Eight limbs** (YS 2.29): *yama* (restraints), *niyama* (observances), *āsana* (posture), *prāṇāyāma* (breath control), *pratyāhāra* (withdrawal of the senses), *dhāraṇā* (concentration), *dhyāna* (meditation), *samādhi* (absorption). [source: IEP-Yoga §4; IEP-Hindu §3d]
- Yamas: non-violence, truthfulness, non-stealing, celibacy, non-possessiveness; *ahiṃsā* is the root (Vyāsa: like the elephant's footprint covering all others); a "great vow" not limited by class, place, time or circumstance (YS 2.31). Niyamas: cleanliness, contentment, austerity, study, devotion to Īśvara. [source: IEP-Yoga §§4, 6; IEP-Hindu §3d]
- Āsana: only three sūtras, nine words, "less than 1% of the text"; it means "seat"; posture-centred yoga "is essentially a modern Western phenomenon" (Bryant; cf. Singleton, *Yoga Body*, 2010). [source: IEP-Yoga §§1, 4]
- Samādhi: stages of *saṃprajñāta* (with support) and finally *asaṃprajñāta* ("without support", "seedless", YS 1.51). [source: IEP-Yoga §4]
- **Īśvara**: "a special self (puruṣa-viśeṣa) untouched by" kleśas, karma and its fruits (YS 1.24); omniscient, teacher of the ancients; expressed by *om*. Devotion to Īśvara is optional in YS 1.23 but required as a niyama and in kriyā-yoga, so Bryant argues Patañjali requires theistic practice at the preparatory stage. [source: IEP-Yoga §7]
- **Kaivalya** ("aloneness/isolation", YS 4.34; title of book 4): the seer isolated from the contents of the mind. Preceded by *dharma-megha-samādhi*. Critics read it as social isolation; Ranganathan replies it is the culmination of a moral path. [source: IEP-Hindu §3d; WP-Yoga_Sutras]
- Debate: Yoga vs Sāṃkhya on puruṣa: for Sāṃkhya puruṣas are "mere spectators" (SK 62); for Yoga puruṣa is "lord of the mind" (YS 4.18) whose effort brings liberation (Ranganathan's reading). [source: IEP-Hindu §3d]
- Al-Bīrūnī (973–1050) translated the Yoga-sūtra into Arabic; manuscript found in Istanbul in the 1920s. [source: IEP-Yoga §1]

## 11. Mīmāṃsā: language, ritual and the authority of the Veda
- MS 1.1.1 names "the desire to know dharma" (*dharmajijñāsā*) as the topic; early commentary defines dharma as what "connects a person with the highest good". Dharma is always "yet to be" (*bhaviṣyat*). [source: SEP-Kum §1.2]
- **MS 1.1.2**: "dharma is a goal defined by [Vedic] injunction (codanā)" (Arnold's translation). Injunction is the only valid way to know dharma. [source: SEP-Kum §2.1, §1.3] Ranganathan: dharma is "an injunction (codana) that has the distinction (lakṣaṇa) of bringing about welfare (artha)". [source: IEP-Hindu §3e]
- Paradigm injunction: "one desirous of heaven should perform the agnihotra sacrifice". Perception bears only on what is present (MS 1.1.4, *satsaṃprayoge*); only language can make known what is "to be realized". [source: SEP-Kum §2.1]
- Śabara: a determinate cognition produced by a sentence may be acted on unless explicitly falsified. [source: SEP-Kum §2.1]
- **Svataḥ prāmāṇya (intrinsic validity)** — Kumārila's "signal epistemological contribution". ŚV codanā 47 (Arnold's translation): "It should be understood that all pramāṇas' have the property of being pramāṇas intrinsically; for a capacity not already existing by itself (svataḥ) cannot be produced by anything else." [source: SEP-Kum intro, §2.2]
  - **Argument, step by step** (Arnold's reconstruction):
    1. Suppose a cognition counts as knowledge only once some further cognition certifies it.
    2. That certifying cognition would itself need certifying, and so on: infinite regress.
    3. And if the first cognition lacked the capacity to justify, no later cognition could give it, unless the later one has it intrinsically, in which case why not grant it to the first?
    4. So cognitions justify intrinsically; they are overturned only by a defeating cognition (*bādhaka-jñāna*) or a recognised fault in their cause.
    - Commentators: otherwise "the whole world would be blind". Upshot: "it's not demonstration but falsification that's ultimately decisive". [source: SEP-Kum §2.2–2.3]
  - Objection (Buddhists, Dharmakīrti): a cognition is confirmed only by "pragmatic efficacy" (carrying water in the jar). **Reply:** that confirming awareness is just another cognition; why credit it intrinsically and not the first? [source: SEP-Kum §2.2]
  - Objection: endless possible revision means we never know. **Reply:** common sense; doubt needs a specific reason, or "there would be the unwanted consequence of annihilating all worldly discourse" (Sucaritamiśra). [source: SEP-Kum §2.3]
  - Interpretive debate: Uṃveka (validity arises from the cognition's causes) vs Pārthasārathimiśra (11th c.; *Svataḥprāmāṇyanirṇaya*: validity is prima facie justification, which "appears" rather than "exists"); Arnold prefers Pārthasārathi; McCrea 2018 differs. [source: SEP-Kum §2.4]
  - Bhāṭṭa vs Prābhākara: Prabhākara holds no cognition is wholly wrong, only a confusion, and the same causes produce a cognition and knowledge of its validity; Bhāṭṭas say validity is known via inference from the "cognizedness" (*jñātatā*) of the object. Both hold falsity is known extrinsically. [source: SEP-Epi §3; SEP-Kum §3.3]
  - Comparisons drawn by Arnold: Thomas Reid, William Alston and "reformed epistemology"; Popper on falsification. [source: SEP-Kum intro, §2.3]
- **Eternity of word–meaning relation**: MS 1.1.5: "the relations between words and their referents are primordial (autpattika)" — meant to underwrite the Vedas' authorlessness (*apauruṣeya*). [source: SEP-Kum §3.1] Ranganathan's MS 1.1.5 rendering: instruction in the Veda is "infallible regarding all that is imperceptible; it is a valid means of knowledge, as it is independent". [source: IEP-Hindu §3e]
- Śabara on the eternity of words (as quoted by Ranganathan from Śabara on MS 1.1.12–19): "If the word ceased to exist as soon as uttered then no one could speak of any thing to others ... Whenever the word 'go' (cow) is uttered, there is a notion of all cows simultaneously. From this it follows that the word denotes the Class." The relation cannot have been created, since the creator would need to use the word "go" to fix its meaning. [source: IEP-Hindu §3e] (Translator of the passage not named on the IEP page; Ganganatha Jha's translation is the standard one — not verified.)
- Kumārila's argument for eternal language: a convention-founding first speech act is incoherent, since proposing "let this be called 'cow'" already uses language; Arnold compares Wittgenstein, *Philosophical Investigations* §32 on Augustine. Against: Buddhists' stock inference "śabda is impermanent, because it is produced". Kumārila held even phonemes eternal and rejected the grammarians' *sphoṭa* theory. [source: SEP-Kum §3.1–3.2]
- Why the Veda alone cannot be falsified: human statements can be overridden by the speaker's limits or lies; the Veda has no author, so no such defeater. Hence Mīmāṃsakas argued against Buddhist and Jain claims to omniscience. Arnold stresses this is "logically independent" of the epistemology. [source: SEP-Kum §3.1]
- Sentence meaning: Kumārila's *abhihitānvaya-vāda* ("a series of expressed word-meanings") vs Prabhākara's *anvitābhidhāna-vāda* (sentences the basic unit). [source: SEP-Kum §3.2]
- Arthāpatti (presumption): fat Devadatta who does not eat by day must eat at night; something like inference to the best explanation; used to posit *apūrva*, the unseen potency linking a ritual act to its distant result (Arnold: "hard not to consider a basically ad hoc category"). [source: SEP-Kum §3.3; SEP-Epi §7.2]
- **Atheism of (early) Mīmāṃsā**: "Mīmāṃsā, however, is atheistic, viewing the Veda as primordial" (Phillips). Kumārila's critique of theism resembles Buddhist ones. [source: SEP-Epi §6; SEP-Kum §3.1] Early Mīmāṃsā did not make liberation the goal: "the very topic of liberation is nowhere discussed in the foundational text", first recognised by Kumārila (ŚV). [source: IEP-Hindu §3e; SEP-Kum §1.2] Later "theistic Mīmāṃsā" (Vedānta Deśika's *Seśvara Mīmāṃsā*) existed. [source: WP-Mīmāṃsā]
- Ideological critique: Sheldon Pollock calls Mīmāṃsā "the dominant orthodox discourse of traditional India" (1989); Arnold says its appeal to an authorless Veda "can be understood as rather insidious"; Cārvākas claimed the Vedic worldview was a Brahmin livelihood. [source: SEP-Kum §4]
- Influence: on Śaṅkara (fl. c. 710 per SEP-Kum; "eighth century"), on law, hermeneutics and poetics; Dharmakīrti elaborated Dignāga partly in reply to Kumārila. [source: SEP-Kum intro]

## Debates
- **The six-darśana scheme**: a convention (IEP-Hindu) vs a late-medieval Vedāntin doxography adopted by Indologists (Nicholson 2010); earlier lists (Haribhadra, Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha) differ; Yoga as a separate school first in Śaṅkara (Bryant). [sources: §1]
- **Dating the root texts**: Nyāya-sūtra (6th c. BCE – 2nd/3rd c. CE, layered); Vaiśeṣika-sūtra (6th c. BCE – 1st c. CE); Yoga-sūtra (Bryant: 1st–2nd c. CE; Maas: c. 400 CE, sūtras and bhāṣya one work); Mīmāṃsā-sūtra (3rd c. BCE – 1st c. CE). [sources: §2]
- **Is puruṣa strictly changeless?** Larson/Keith vs Ruzsa. [source: IEP-Sāṅkhya §4c]
- **Interpreting svataḥ prāmāṇya**: Uṃveka vs Pārthasārathimiśra (Arnold prefers the latter; McCrea 2018 differs); and whether Kumārila's epistemology needs the eternity-of-Veda thesis (Arnold: logically independent; Jayanta "misses the mark"). [source: SEP-Kum §§2.4, 3.1]
- **The God argument**: Nyāya design/causal inference vs the Buddhist *upādhi* ("having a body") and Mīmāṃsā regress objections; whether the "extrapolation" is legitimate inference to the best explanation (Vācaspati). [sources: IEP-Nyāya §3; SEP-Gaṅg §5.4]
- **The Nyāya self**: inferred (Nyāya) vs encountered in memory (Kumārila) vs a causal stream (Buddhists); Chadha vs Dasti on whether the Nyāya self is a real agent. [sources: SEP-Kum §3.3; SEP-Pers §1.3]
- **Theism in Sāṃkhya**: atheist classical school (Ruzsa) vs theistic early and popular Sāṃkhya (Bryant); Chadha even speaks of an "absolute puruṣa (īśvara)" in Sāṃkhya (unusual; do not use without qualification). [sources: IEP-Sāṅkhya; IEP-Yoga §7; SEP-Pers §1.2]
- **The guṇas**: physical (Seal) vs affective (K. C. Bhattacharya, Mohanty). [source: SEP-Nat §2.4]
- **Ideology**: Pollock and Arnold on Mīmāṃsā's authorless Veda as a tool of Brahmin dominance. [source: SEP-Kum §4]
- **Kaivalya as isolation**: moral criticism (Bharadwaja) vs Ranganathan's reply. [source: IEP-Hindu §3d]
- **Patañjali the grammarian = Patañjali of the Yoga-sūtra?** Tradition (Bhoja) vs Renou and others. [source: IEP-Yoga §1; WP-Yoga_Sutras]
