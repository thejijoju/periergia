## Francis Bacon and the "new organon"

- Francis Bacon lived 1561–1626; born 22 January 1561 at York House in the Strand, London; died at Highgate on 9 April 1626. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- He was a lawyer, MP from age twenty to sixty, attorney-general (1613), lord keeper (1617), lord chancellor (January 1618), Baron Verulam (1618) and Viscount St Alban (January 1621); in spring 1621 Parliament impeached him for taking bribes and he confessed (the SEP calls him "a victim of a political campaign against the king's chief favorite", Buckingham). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- His programme was the *Instauratio magna* ("Great Instauration"), a plan in six parts for renewing all knowledge; the term first appears c. 1602–3. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- The *Instauratio magna* volume appeared in October 1620 and contained the preface, the "Plan", the two books of the *Novum organum*, "A Preparative for a Natural and Experimental History" and a "Catalogue of Particular History". [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- Full Latin title: *Novum Organum, sive Indicia Vera de Interpretatione Naturae* ("The new Organon, or true directions concerning the interpretation of nature"), Part II of the Great Instauration; written in Latin as two books of aphorisms; the title answers Aristotle's logical works, the *Organon*; parts V and VI of the Instauration were never written and the *Novum Organum* itself is unfinished. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Novum_Organum&action=raw ; https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- The title page shows a ship passing between the Pillars of Hercules. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Novum_Organum&action=raw]
- Bacon named four faults his new logic would fix: the deceitfulness of the senses, the unsoundness of concepts, the poverty of induction, and "the means of discovering and testing" as "the very mother of error" (NO I.69). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- Experiments remedy the senses: "the sense judges only the experiment whereas the experiment judges the thing". [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- The four idols (SEP summary): Idols of the Tribe, founded in human nature (the senses are "like an enchanted glass" that distorts, NO I.41, 45–52); Idols of the Cave, illusions of the individual shaped by education and circumstance (NO I.42, 53–58); Idols of the Market, illusions imposed by words, which Bacon thought "the greatest nuisance" (NO I.43, 59–60); Idols of the Theatre, "the dogmas of philosophers and misguided laws of demonstrations", targeting Aristotelianism, empiricism and superstition (NO I.44, 61–67). The first two are inherent in human nature, the last two social. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- Bacon first sketched the doctrine of idols in unpublished writings before 1609. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- Ants, spiders and bee: the empirics (alchemists) are like "the ant, only to store up and use things"; the dogmatists (Aristotelians) like "spiders, spin webs from their own entrails"; the true philosopher like "the bee", gathering material from flowers and "to convert and digest it" (NO I.95; Oxford Francis Bacon vol. XI p. 153). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- Bacon dismissed induction by simple enumeration as "a childish thing"; science needs "a form of induction which will unbind experience and separate it out, and reach necessary conclusions by proper exclusions and rejections"; hence his method is usually called eliminative induction. Axioms are to be educed "successively and step by step", the most general last. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- The three tables (NO II.11–13): a table of presence (all known instances of the nature studied), a table of absence (cases where the nature would be expected but is absent), and a table of degrees or comparative table (where it varies). Example: heat. The sun's rays are in the first table, the moon's rays (light without heat) in the second, so light can be excluded as the form of heat. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- Bacon's summary (NO II.16, OFB XI 255, SEP's translation): "the first job of true Induction is to Reject or Exclude individual natures which we cannot find in any instance where the nature under investigation is present … Then, once we have performed Rejection and Exclusion in the right ways, the residue left … will be a form affirmative, solid, and both true and well defined." [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- Book II also lists 27 "prerogative instances", experimental guides some see as "forerunners of later standard scientific procedures" (Jardine). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- In NO II.20 Bacon allows a provisional hypothesis, the "first vintage" (*vindemiatio prima*), and concludes that the form of heat is a kind of motion (expansive, restrained, acting on small particles); the aphorism opens with a line usually translated "Truth emerges more readily from error than from confusion" (translations vary). [source: WebSearch results incl. https://www.logicmuseum.com/authors/baconfrancis/novumorganum-II-1-37.htm and https://users.manchester.edu/Facstaff/SSNaragon/Online/texts/318/Bacon, Aphorisms.pdf — snippets only; check wording against a modern translation (e.g. Jardine & Silverthorne 2000) before quoting]
- Knowledge and power: "human knowledge and human power come to the same thing" (NO I.3, SEP's translation); the end of knowledge is "the benefit and use of life", "the reliefe of Mans estate" (Advancement of Learning, 1605). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- "Knowledge is power": the Latin *ipsa scientia potestas est* ("knowledge itself is power") is in Bacon's *Meditationes Sacrae* (1597); the exact phrase *scientia potentia est* first appears in the Latin *Leviathan* (1668) of Thomas Hobbes, who had been Bacon's secretary. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Knowledge_is_power&action=raw]
- Bacon's knowledge was to be collaborative and institutional. The founding of the Royal Society in 1660 "marked the peak of Bacon's reputation" (Pérez-Ramos: the "victory of the Baconian project of collaboration, utility, and progress"). Hartlib, Comenius and Dury promoted Baconian ideas in the 1640s–50s. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- Later reputation: praised by Voltaire, Rousseau, Condorcet and d'Alembert; criticised in the 19th–20th centuries as "a champion of what science has never been and will never be" (Rossi); the Frankfurt School blamed him for the domination of nature; recent scholarship restores his centrality. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/]
- Mill (*System of Logic*) criticised Baconians for neglecting what scientists actually do; Whewell sought to "renovate" Bacon (*Novum Organon Renovatum*). Bacon's method "has been criticized as impractical and too inflexible for the practicing scientist". [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-method/]
- Popper rejected "the Baconian/Newtonian insistence on the primacy of 'pure' observation": all observation is selective and theory-laden; science starts from problems, not observations. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/]

## Before Bacon: method in other traditions (handle evenhandedly)

- Medieval Latin writers who worked on observation and induction include Albertus Magnus (1206–1280), Robert Grosseteste (1175–1253), Roger Bacon (1214/1220–1292) and William of Ockham (1287–1347) (dates as given by the SEP). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-method/]
- Note: the SEP "Scientific Method" entry's historical survey runs "Aristotle to Mill" and does not mention Ibn al-Haytham or any non-European figure — itself evidence of the Eurocentrism the chapter may name. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-method/]
- Ibn al-Haytham (Latin Alhazen), c. 965–c. 1040, born in Basra; worked in Cairo; legend says he feigned madness under the caliph al-Ḥākim (d. 1021) and wrote the *Book of Optics* (*Kitāb al-Manāẓir*, seven books, written 1011–1021) during that time; Latin printed edition by Friedrich Risner, 1572 (*Opticae thesaurus*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ibn_al-Haytham&action=raw] (Perception chapter already covers the optics.)
- He relied systematically on testing (*iʿtibār*; Latin translators rendered *iʿtibār / muʿtabir* as *experimentatio / experimentator*), combining physics with geometry. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ibn_al-Haytham&action=raw ; WebSearch snippet on Latin renderings]
- Example: he showed light travels in straight lines by observing beams entering dark rooms through holes, visible in the dust, and by setting a straight stick or taut thread beside the beam. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_scientific_method&action=raw]
- *Doubts Concerning Ptolemy* (*al-Shukūk ʿalā Baṭlamyūs*, also "Aporias against Ptolemy"), written between 1025 and 1028, criticised contradictions in the *Almagest*, *Planetary Hypotheses* and *Optics*; he aimed to repair, not replace, Ptolemy's system. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ibn_al-Haytham&action=raw]
- Matthias Schramm (*Ibn al-Haythams Weg zur Physik*, 1963) held he "was the first to make a systematic use of the method of varying the experimental conditions in a constant and uniform manner" (the moonlight-through-two-apertures experiment); G. J. Toomer's 1964 review accepted he was "a major figure … particularly in the creation of experimental techniques" but said the claim that he founded modern physics could not be assessed until his influence on later medieval writers was studied, warning against reading passages anachronistically. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ibn_al-Haytham&action=raw]
- The "first scientist" debate: Jim Al-Khalili called him "the first true scientist" (BBC News, January 2009) and wrote in *Nature* (2015) that many historians consider him the first true proponent of the modern scientific method; Bradley Steffens's popular book is titled *Ibn al-Haytham: First Scientist*. Against this: A. I. Sabra regarded his experiments as "confirmatory" rather than discovery experiments; Elaheh Kheirandish (*Early Science and Medicine*, 2009, "Footprints of 'Experiment' in Early Arabic Optics") finds traces of "experiment" in Arabic optics before him (e.g. Aḥmad ibn ʿĪsā), and the idea and term may derive from Arabic translations of Ptolemy. Roger Bacon is a rival "first scientist" candidate in popular writing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ibn_al-Haytham&action=raw ; https://www.nature.com/articles/518164a (via WebSearch); https://ekheirandish.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/footprints-%E2%80%98experiment%E2%80%99-early-arabic-optics (title via WebSearch; page not readable here)] — present as a debate; the phrase "first scientist" should be attributed, not asserted.
- UNESCO's 2015 International Year of Light marked 1,000 years of his optics; Director-General Irina Bokova called him "the father of optics". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ibn_al-Haytham&action=raw]
- Al-Bīrūnī (Persian, 1020s–30s) worried about systematic errors from small instruments and human observers and recommended repeated observations. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_scientific_method&action=raw] (Wikipedia section is uneven; use cautiously.)
- Shen Kuo (1031–1095), Northern Song polymath and official; in the *Dream Pool Essays* (*Mengxi bitan*, 1088) he gave the first description of the magnetic-needle compass and of magnetic declination (true north vs magnetic north), and inferred long-term land formation from inland marine fossils and climate change from petrified bamboo found where bamboo no longer grew. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Shen_Kuo&action=raw]
- Joseph Needham (1900–1995) launched *Science and Civilisation in China* (vol. 1, 1954; 27 volumes so far) and posed the "Needham Question": why China, after long leadership, did not produce the Scientific Revolution. His collaborator Nathan Sivin (1995) answered that the counterfactual question has no useful answer: it is "analogous to the question of why your name did not appear on page 3 of today's newspaper." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Joseph_Needham&action=raw]
- India: Nyāya inference, *vyāpti*, Cārvāka scepticism and the *anvaya/vyatireka* method are already covered in the Induction chapter (cross-refer). The *Carakasaṃhitā* (classical Indian medicine) lists four means of examination, perception, trustworthy testimony, inference and *yukti* (reasoning about a plurality of causes), Sūtrasthāna 11; the commentator Cakrapāṇi held *yukti* is not a separate means of knowledge but serves inference. [source: WebSearch results incl. https://carakasamhitaonline.com/index.php?title=Pramana and https://cahc.jainuniversity.ac.in/assets/ijhs/Vol51_2016_1_Art06.pdf — secondary sources of mixed quality; use only as a brief, hedged mention]
- Hansson (SEP) stresses that fact-finding practices exist outside science: journalism, criminal investigation, mechanics' fault-finding, and in indigenous societies traditional agricultural experimentation and animal tracking (Liebenberg 2013). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]

## Confirmation: hypothetico-deductivism and Bayes (brief; ravens/grue are in the Induction chapter)

- The hypothetico-deductive (HD) idea is "deduction-in-reverse": evidence confirms a hypothesis when the hypothesis, with auxiliary assumptions, entails the evidence (and the auxiliaries alone do not); it disconfirms it when hypothesis plus auxiliaries entail the evidence is false. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confirmation/]
- HD was advanced by Whewell in the 19th century and Nicod (1924); Hempel's 1966 account (*Philosophy of Natural Science*), illustrated by Semmelweis's work on childbed fever, is the standard presentation: deduce "test implications", reject the hypothesis if they fail; their success does not prove it. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-method/]
- Whewell (1794–1866) vs Mill (1806–1873) is "the canonical methodological debate" of the 19th century: Whewell's "colligation of facts" and "consilience of inductions" against Mill's inductivism and his methods of agreement, difference etc. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-method/]
- Irrelevant conjunction (tacking) paradox: if e HD-confirms h, it also HD-confirms h-and-c for any irrelevant c, because adding a conjunct preserves entailment. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confirmation/]
- Problem of old evidence: raised by Zahar (1973), made famous by Glymour (1980) against Bayesianism; textbook case: the long-known anomaly of Mercury's perihelion supported general relativity although it was not new. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confirmation/]
- Bayesian (relevance) confirmation: e confirms h relative to k iff P(h | e∧k) > P(h | k). The "surprising prediction theorem": if h with k entails e and e was not certain, e confirms h, and the more surprising e was, the stronger the confirmation; if e was already certain it confirms nothing. The SEP: under total evidence, Bayesian relevance confirmation "turns out to embed a squarely predictivist version of hypothetico-deductivism". This gives a Bayesian rationale for Popper's preference for risky predictions. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confirmation/]
- Einstein stressed that the Mercury result was derived "without having to posit any special [auxiliary] hypotheses at all" (Norton 2011), a point the Bayesian can use to answer the old-evidence problem. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confirmation/]
- Bayesians have treated Duhem's problem directly (Jon Dorling 1979, "Bayesian Personalism, the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes, and Duhem's Problem"). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemology-bayesian/ (bibliography)]

## Popper: falsifiability (new emphases only; base story is in the Positivism chapter)

- Karl Raimund Popper, born 28 July 1902 in Vienna; in 1919 heavily involved in left-wing politics and for a time a Marxist; served briefly as a voluntary social worker in one of Adler's clinics in the 1920s; heard Einstein lecture on relativity; PhD 1928 under Karl Bühler; New Zealand (Canterbury) from 1937; *The Open Society and Its Enemies* 1945; LSE 1946; knighted 1965; died 1994. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/]
- Title: *Logik der Forschung. Zur Erkenntnistheorie der modernen Naturwissenschaft* ("Logic of Research: On the Epistemology of Modern Natural Science"), Julius Springer, 1934 (imprint 1935); Popper's own English rewriting *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, Hutchinson, 1959. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=The_Logic_of_Scientific_Discovery&action=raw]
- In 1933 Popper introduced the German term *Abgrenzungsproblem*, "the request for a criterion to differentiate between 'empirical-scientific' and 'metaphysical' assertions" (*Erkenntnis* 3: 426–428); in *Logik der Forschung* it was to "distinguish between the empirical sciences on the one hand, and mathematics and logic as well as 'metaphysical' systems on the other". Pseudoscience was not mentioned in these texts. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- In a 1957 lecture Popper said he had worked on the problem since 1919 to "distinguish between science and pseudo-science". [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- The lecture "Science: Conjectures and Refutations" was given at Peterhouse, Cambridge, in summer 1953 for a British Council course; first published as "Philosophy of Science: a Personal Report" in *British Philosophy in Mid-Century* (1957); reprinted as ch. 1 of *Conjectures and Refutations* (1962/1963). In it Popper says his student circle was "thrilled with the result of Eddington's eclipse observations which in 1919 brought the first important confirmation of Einstein's theory of gravitation". Versions differ on whether his reflections began in autumn 1919 or winter 1919–20. [source: WebSearch results incl. https://abel.math.harvard.edu/archive/NatureEvidence_2012/r05.pdf and https://www.stephenhicks.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/PopperK-Science-as-Falsification-text-full.pdf (snippets)]
- The Adler anecdote (C&R ch. 1): in 1919 Popper reported a case to Adler, who analysed it in terms of inferiority feelings "although he had not even seen the child"; asked how he could be so sure, Adler said "Because of my thousandfold experience", and Popper replied that with this new case it was presumably "thousand-and-one-fold". [source: WebSearch snippets, same PDFs as above]
- The "risky prediction" point (SEP paraphrase): Einstein's theory implied light is deflected towards massive bodies, improbable on Newtonian physics, so it could have failed; psychoanalytic theories could accommodate any behaviour, an apparent strength that is "in fact a critical weakness". Popper also: "real support can be obtained only from observations undertaken as tests (by 'attempted refutations'); and for this purpose criteria of refutation have to be laid down beforehand" (1963: 38 n.3). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/]
- Marxism, by contrast, was "initially scientific" (genuinely predictive) but, when its predictions failed, was rescued by ad hoc hypotheses and became "reinforced dogmatism" (1963: 334). Popper (1974, p. 985): Marxism "broke the methodological rule that we must accept falsification, and it immunized itself against the most blatant refutations of its predictions. Ever since then, it can be described only as non-science—as a metaphysical dream, if you like, married to a cruel reality." [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/ ; https://iep.utm.edu/pop-sci/]
- Popper's classification (SEP): physics, chemistry and non-introspective psychology are sciences; psychoanalysis is a "pre-science"; astrology and phrenology are pseudo-sciences. Popper is "not a positivist": unscientific theories may be enlightening, myths have had a valuable function. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/]
- Logical form: scientific laws are prohibitions: "All As are X" is equivalent to "No A is not-X"; conservation of energy can be put as "There is no perpetual motion machine". Falsifiability means being "logically incompatible with some basic statements", singular existential statements of the form "There is an X at Y" that are intersubjectively testable. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/ ; https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Popper's own statement of the asymmetry (LSD, as quoted on Wikipedia): an asymmetry "between verifiability and falsifiability; an asymmetry which results from the logical form of universal statements. For these are never derivable from singular statements, but can be contradicted by singular statements." Also: "non-reproducible single occurrences are of no significance to science … We shall take it as falsified only if we discover a reproducible effect which refutes the theory." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=The_Logic_of_Scientific_Discovery&action=raw]
- SEP: "an exception, far from 'proving' a rule, conclusively refutes it." [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/]
- Logic vs methodology: Popper distinguished the logic of falsification (one counter-instance suffices) from its methodology, where a single conflicting instance "is never sufficient methodologically", and theories are often retained despite anomalies; a scientist "does not … abandon the present theory until such time as he has a better one". [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/]
- Basic statements are accepted by decision; the swamp image: "The piles are driven down from above into the swamp, but not down to any natural or 'given' base; and if we stop driving the piles deeper, it is not because we have reached firm ground. We simply stop when we are satisfied that the piles are firm enough to carry the structure, at least for the time being." (LSD 2002: 94); the jury analogy (2002: 92). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/]
- Darwinism: in 1976 Popper called it "not a testable scientific theory, but a metaphysical research program" (*Unended Quest*); in a 1977 Darwin College lecture (published 1978) he retracted the tautology charge and called natural selection testable though "difficult to test". Creationists exploited the earlier remark. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/ ; https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creationism/]
- Popper's late statement: falsifiability "only has to do with the logical structure of sentences and classes of sentences" (1989/1994); earlier he said "what was a metaphysical idea yesterday can become a testable scientific theory tomorrow" (1974). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]

## Duhem–Quine underdetermination

- Pierre Duhem (1861–1916), French physicist and historian of science; *La théorie physique, son objet et sa structure* (Paris 1906; 2nd ed. 1914; English *The Aim and Structure of Physical Theory*, trans. Philip P. Wiener, Princeton 1954; German translation 1908 by Friedrich Adler). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/duhem/]
- Duhem's key passage ([1914] 1954, 187): "the physicist can never subject an isolated hypothesis to experimental test, but only a whole group of hypotheses; when the experiment is in disagreement with his predictions, what he learns is that at least one of the hypotheses constituting this group is unacceptable and ought to be modified; but the experiment does not designate which one should be changed." [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-underdetermination/]
- Consequence: no "crucial experiment" (*experimentum crucis*). Foucault's experiment (light slower in water than in air) was taken to refute the particle (emission) theory of light, but Duhem says it condemns only the whole group of Newton's, Laplace's and Biot's assumptions; also, even if one hypothesis were isolated, light might be neither projectiles nor waves: "is it forbidden to be anything else at all?" ([1914] 1954, 189). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-underdetermination/]
- Duhem thought the predicament applied to theoretical physics; later philosophers extended it to all fields. Duhem's remedy was scientists' "good sense" (*bon sens*), a faculty perfected by studying the history of science. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-underdetermination/ ; https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/duhem/]
- Quine (1951) generalised it: total science "is like a field of force whose boundary conditions are experience"; "The unit of empirical significance is the whole of science" (1951, 42). (Already quoted in Positivism chapter; cross-refer.) [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-underdetermination/]
- The Uranus/Neptune vs Mercury/Vulcan contrast: when Newton's theory failed to predict Uranus's orbit, scientists blamed the auxiliary assumption of seven planets; Adams and Le Verrier calculated a new planet and Neptune was found in 1846. The same strategy failed for the advance of Mercury's perihelion (the hypothetical planet "Vulcan"), explained only by general relativity. Hence which hypothesis to give up is not a foregone conclusion. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-underdetermination/]
- Holist vs contrastive underdetermination: holist starts from a theory and says its revision in response to evidence is underdetermined; contrastive starts from evidence and says more than one theory may be well supported by it. Mill had stated the contrastive worry. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-underdetermination/]
- Laudan (1990) argued underdetermination's significance is "greatly exaggerated": the claim that any hypothesis can be kept "come what may" is true only as a psychological possibility; the strong claim that it is always *rationally defensible* to keep any hypothesis has never been argued for; deductive logic alone may not single out one response, but logic plus ampliative standards of good reasoning may. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-underdetermination/]

## After Popper: Lakatos, Kuhn, Feyerabend (demarcation aspects only)

- Imre Lakatos (1922–1974), Hungarian-born, born 9 November 1922 to Jewish parents. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/lakatos/]
- Research programmes have a "hard core" protected by a "protective belt" of auxiliary hypotheses; the negative heuristic forbids directing modus tollens at the core. Lakatos: Newton, Einstein, quantum mechanics, Marxism, Freudianism "are all research programmes … All theories, in this sense, are born refuted and die refuted. But are they [all] equally good?" ("Science and Pseudoscience", 1973 talk, publ. 1974/1978). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/lakatos/]
- A programme "is progressive if it is both theoretically and empirically progressive, and degenerating if it is not": each new theory must predict novel facts and some must be corroborated. Example: Halley's comet's predicted return 72 years later. Degenerating programmes count as pseudoscientific. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/lakatos/ ; https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Lakatos called Popper's criterion "a rather stunning one. A theory may be scientific even if there is not a shred of evidence in its favour, and it may be pseudoscientific even if all the available evidence is in its favour" (1981, 117). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Lakatos's challenge to crucial tests: had Galle not found Neptune, Newtonian physics would not have been abandoned (the failure could be blamed on the atmosphere, a hiding asteroid belt, etc.). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/]
- Lakatos on Lysenko: "No rationality theory will ever solve problems like why Mendelian genetics disappeared in Soviet Russia in the 1950s" — an "external" (socio-political) episode. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/lakatos/]
- Kuhn's criterion (1974): puzzle-solving in normal science. Astrology "has never been a science" because "particular failures did not give rise to research puzzles, for no man, however skilled, could make use of them in a constructive attempt to revise the astrological tradition" (Kuhn 1974, 804). Popper: astrologers solve puzzles too; Kuhn's criterion is "the major disaster" of replacing "a rational criterion of science by a sociological one" (Popper 1974, 1146–7). Kuhn observed that his and Popper's criteria nevertheless classify nearly the same cases (1974, 803). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Feyerabend: any methodological prescription would stifle progress; the only rule that leaves enough freedom is "the vacuous 'anything goes'"; science might threaten a free society (*Science in a Free Society*, 1978). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-method/]

## The demarcation problem: Laudan's "demise" and the revival

- Larry Laudan (16 October 1941, Austin, Texas – 23 August 2022, Lexington, Kentucky), American philosopher of science (Pittsburgh, Virginia Tech, Hawaiʻi, UNAM). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Larry_Laudan&action=raw]
- Laudan, "The Demise of the Demarcation Problem", in R. S. Cohen and L. Laudan (eds.), *Physics, Philosophy and Psychoanalysis* (Dordrecht: Reidel, 1983), pp. 111–127 (a Festschrift for Adolf Grünbaum). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/ (bibliography, which misspells Cohen as "Cohan")]
- Laudan's argument: historically demarcation criteria have failed ("philosophy has failed to deliver the goods"); many well-founded beliefs are not scientific and many scientific conjectures are not well-founded; the real question is reliable vs unreliable knowledge; demarcation criteria served as *machines de guerre* in polemics; the problem is a pseudo-problem. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Demarcation_problem&action=raw]
- Against Popper, Laudan (1983, 121): falsifiability "has the untoward consequence of countenancing as 'scientific' every crank claim which makes ascertainably false assertions". [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2017/entries/pseudo-science/ ; https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/ (paraphrase)]
- Laudan's conclusion (1983, p. 125): we ought to drop terms like "pseudo-science" and "unscientific" from our vocabulary; they are "hollow phrases which do only emotive work for us", more suited to the rhetoric of politicians and "Scottish sociologists of knowledge" than of empirical researchers. [source: WebSearch result summarising https://sites.pitt.edu/~jdnorton/teaching/Phil_Sci_Core/HPS_2501_2020/handouts/Laudan-Demise.pdf — the key phrase "hollow phrases which do only emotive work" confirmed; check the full sentence in the original before quoting at length]
- The 2017 SEP version: work on demarcation "seems to have waned after Laudan's (1983) much noted death certificate according to which there is no hope of finding a necessary and sufficient criterion of something as heterogeneous as scientific methodology. In more recent years, the problem has been revitalized." [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2017/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Massimo Pigliucci and Maarten Boudry (eds.), *Philosophy of Pseudoscience: Reconsidering the Demarcation Problem* (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2013); it includes Pigliucci, "The Demarcation Problem: A (Belated) Response to Laudan", pp. 9–28. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/ (bibliography)]
- Family-resemblance approach: Dupré (1993) proposed science is a Wittgensteinian family-resemblance concept; Hacking and Pigliucci have made similar points; Schindler (2018) objects that similarities cannot justify kind membership "without some prior relevance determination". [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Demarcation_problem&action=raw]
- Boudry (2022) proposes "Diagnosing Pseudoscience—by Getting Rid of the Demarcation Problem": "there are comparatively fewer ways to create a false impression of epistemic warrant, and these ways are largely similar across different fields of inquiry" (*Journal for General Philosophy of Science* 53: 83–101). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Thagard (1978, "Why Astrology Is a Pseudoscience"): pseudoscientific if it fails to progress (vs alternatives, over a long time) and its community "makes little attempt to develop the theory towards solutions of the problems, shows no concern for attempts to evaluate the theory in relation to others, and is selective in considering confirmations and disconfirmations". [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Multicriterial lists: many lists of 5–10 criteria exist (Langmuir 1953, Bunge 1982, Kitcher 1982, Thagard 1988, Ruse 1996, Mahner 2007/2013, etc.); Mahner listed twenty and suggested up to thirty or fifty; Fasce (2017) compiled 70 criteria from 21 lists. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Hansson's 1983 seven-item list (via SEP): belief in authority; unrepeatable experiments; handpicked examples; unwillingness to test; disregard of refuting information; built-in subterfuge; explanations abandoned without replacement. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Merton's ethos of science ([1942] 1973): universalism, "communism" (better "communality"), disinterestedness, organized scepticism; Merton meant these as sociological descriptions; Bright and Heesen (2023) propose communality as a demarcation criterion and explicitly call non-open commercial research pseudo-scientific. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Reisch (1998): a field is pseudoscientific if it cannot be integrated into the network of established sciences. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- The verificationists' criterion was aimed at science vs metaphysics, not science vs pseudoscience; including it in accounts of the latter "is not historically quite accurate". [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]

## What pseudoscience is (Hansson)

- Oldest known English use of "pseudoscience": 1796, historian James Pettit Andrew calling alchemy "a fantastical pseudo-science" (OED); Latin *pseudoscientia* in the first half of the 17th century; frequent use since the 1880s; the word has always been defamatory. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Cicero's *De divinatione* already argued against forms of divination in ways resembling modern criteria (Fernandez-Beanato 2020). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Hansson's definition: something is pseudoscientific if (1) it is not scientific and (2′) "it is part of a non-scientific doctrine whose major proponents try to create the impression that it is scientific". Refined (1′): "it is at variance with the most reliable knowledge about its subject matter that is currently available." [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Non-, un- and pseudo-scientific: "unscientific" implies conflict with science; "pseudoscientific" is narrower still; mistakes by scientists trying and failing are bad science, not pseudoscience. Mahner's term "parascience" covers non-scientific practices that are not pseudoscientific. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- The three biochemists (Hansson 1996): one-off error (bad science), sustained sloppy promotion of a rejected claim (pseudoscience), general sloppiness without a doctrine (bad science): the difference is a deviant doctrine. Career fraud is seldom called pseudoscience because fraudsters want to conform. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Time-bound: yesterday's science can become today's pseudoscience: Newton's gravitation was excellent 19th-century science; claiming its validity today (except as an approximation) would be pseudoscientific. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Why the concept matters: pseudoscience "comes with immunizing strategies and defence mechanisms" (Boudry and Braeckman 2011); contrast the creationist who believes humans lived with *T. rex* with a paleontologist who misidentifies a femur and will be persuaded by colleagues. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Two forms (Hansson 2017): pseudo-theory promotion (homeopathy, astrology, ancient astronauts) and science denial(ism); creationism combines both but "as practiced today" is predominantly denialism. Denialism characteristically manufactures false controversies (relativity deniers in the 1930s, tobacco-industry-sponsored disease denial, climate science denial); pseudo-theory promoters instead claim to conform to mainstream science. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- "Holocaust denial" was in use by the early 1980s; "climate change denial" became common around 2005. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Three senses of "scepticism": philosophical scepticism, scientific scepticism (defence of science), and so-called "climate sceptics" (science denial); Torcello (2016) proposed "pseudoscepticism". [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Diethelm and McKee (2009) list five tactics of denialism: conspiracy theories, cherry-picking, fake experts, moving the goalposts, other logical fallacies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Denialism&action=raw]
- Hansson notes "virtual unanimity" that creationism, astrology, homeopathy, Kirlian photography, dowsing, ufology, ancient astronaut theory, Holocaust denialism, Velikovskian catastrophism and climate change denialism are pseudosciences, with some controversy over psychodynamic theories; the paradox is agreement on cases despite "almost complete disagreement on the general criteria". [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Critics of Popper note most pseudosciences have been tested and *falsified* rather than being unfalsifiable: astrology has been tested and refuted (Carlson 1985, *Nature* 318: 419–425; Culver and Ianna 1988). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Carlson's 1985 double-blind test involved a committee of scientists and one of astrologers and found natal astrology performed no better than chance. In 1975 "Objections to Astrology" (Bok, Jerome, Kurtz) in *The Humanist* was signed by 186 scientists. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Astrology_and_science&action=raw]

## Case: Lysenkoism

- Trofim Lysenko (29 September 1898, Karlivka, Poltava Governorate – 20 November 1976, Moscow). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Trofim_Lysenko&action=raw]
- From 1928 Lysenko, rejecting Mendelian genetics and natural selection, promoted vernalization (treating wheat seeds with humidity and cold), species transformation, inheritance of acquired characteristics and "vegetative hybridisation"; Soviet propaganda trumpeted faked results against the background of the 1932–33 famine. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lysenkoism&action=raw]
- Nikolai Vavilov, geneticist and president of the Agriculture Academy, was arrested in 1940 and died in prison in 1943. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lysenkoism&action=raw]
- 31 July – 7 August 1948: VASKhNIL session, organised by Lysenko and approved by Stalin, declared Lysenkoism "the only correct theory"; Lysenko announced "the Central Committee of the Communist Party has examined my report and approved it"; genetics was labelled "bourgeois"/"fascist". Stalin personally edited Lysenko's speech. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lysenkoism&action=raw]
- More than 3,000 biologists were dismissed or imprisoned and numerous scientists executed (Wikipedia figure; treat as approximate). Lysenkoism was adopted to varying degrees in Poland, Czechoslovakia, East Germany, and in China 1949–1956 (criticism allowed at a 1956 genetics symposium). The Soviet ban on criticism was lifted in the mid-1960s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Lysenkoism&action=raw]
- Philosophical point to draw (hedged): Lysenkoism is a case where state power, not open criticism (Merton's organized scepticism), decided scientific questions; Lakatos treated it as "external" history. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/lakatos/ ; https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]

## Case: creationism in court

- *McLean v. Arkansas Board of Education*, 529 F. Supp. 1255 (E.D. Ark.), decided 5 January 1982 by Judge William Overton, struck down Act 590 (the 1981 Balanced Treatment for Creation-Science and Evolution-Science Act) under the Establishment Clause. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=McLean_v._Arkansas&action=raw]
- Expert witnesses for the plaintiffs included theologian Langdon Gilkey, geneticist Francisco Ayala, paleontologist Stephen Jay Gould and philosopher Michael Ruse; the ACLU decided only at the last minute to call a philosopher. State witnesses included Chandra Wickramasinghe and theologian Norman Geisler. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creationism/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=McLean_v._Arkansas&action=raw]
- Overton's "essential characteristics" of science (from Ruse's testimony): (1) it is guided by natural law; (2) it has to be explanatory by reference to natural law; (3) it is testable against the empirical world; (4) its conclusions are tentative; (5) it is falsifiable. He found creation science failed all of them. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creationism/]
- Laudan, "Science at the Bar—Causes for Concern", *Science, Technology, & Human Values* 7(41): 16–19 (1982), criticised the ruling: rather than call creation science non-science it would have been more cogent to show it is bad (false) science; creation science makes testable claims that have been tested and failed; criteria lists also give creationists a target to reshape their claims to. Ruse replied in the same issue, "Response to the Commentary: Pro Judice", pp. 19–23. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=McLean_v._Arkansas&action=raw ; WebSearch results citing the journal]
- Ruse's own account (SEP): critics like Laudan "thought Creationism false"; defenders reply that the US Constitution "does not bar the teaching of false science. It bars the teaching of non-science, especially non-science which is religion by another name"; Popperian falsifiability "may be a somewhat rough and ready way of separating science and religion, but it is good enough for the job at hand, and in law that is what counts." [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creationism/]
- In 1987 the US Supreme Court decided a similar Louisiana case the same way (Edwards v. Aguillard). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creationism/ (names "a similar case involving Louisiana")]
- *Kitzmiller v. Dover Area School District*, 400 F. Supp. 2d 707 (M.D. Pa.), decided 20 December 2005 by Judge John E. Jones III: intelligent design "is not science" and fails "on three different levels": it invokes and permits supernatural causation; irreducible complexity uses "the same flawed and illogical contrived dualism that doomed creation science in the 1980s"; its negative attacks on evolution have been refuted. Philosopher Robert Pennock testified for the plaintiffs; sociologist Steve Fuller gave a qualified defence of ID's scientific status. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kitzmiller_v._Dover_Area_School_District&action=raw]

## Case: the 1919 eclipse

- Total solar eclipse of 29 May 1919; expeditions organised by Astronomer Royal Frank Dyson and Arthur Eddington: Eddington and E. T. Cottingham on Príncipe (Gulf of Guinea), Andrew Crommelin and C. R. Davidson at Sobral, Brazil. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Eddington_experiment&action=raw]
- General relativity (1915) predicts about 1.75 arcseconds of deflection for starlight grazing the Sun, twice the Newtonian-based value; Einstein's 1911 prediction had been the lower value. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Eddington_experiment&action=raw]
- Results announced at a joint Royal Society–Royal Astronomical Society meeting on 6 November 1919, published in *Phil. Trans.* 1920; Einstein became internationally famous. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Eddington_experiment&action=raw]
- At Sobral the main (astrographic) telescope's images were blurred and were discarded though they gave values closer to the Newtonian prediction; the small 4-inch backup gave a value slightly above Einstein's. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Eddington_experiment&action=raw]
- Debate: Earman and Glymour, "Relativity and Eclipses" (*Historical Studies in the Physical Sciences* 11, 1980, 49–85) argued the data selection suggested bias; the charge was popularised by Collins and Pinch, *The Golem* (1993). Kennefick ("Not Only Because of Theory", 2007 preprint/2009; *No Shadow of a Doubt*, Princeton 2019) argues the selection was defensible and was not Eddington's decision (Dyson's team analysed Sobral); a 1979 re-measurement of the Sobral plates supported the original result. Schindler (2013) disputes Kennefick's defence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Eddington_experiment&action=raw ; WebSearch results incl. https://arxiv.org/pdf/0709.0685 and https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.13744 (abstracts/snippets)]
- Philosophical point: the "crucial test" Popper admired itself depended on auxiliary judgements about instruments, a Duhemian lesson. (Interpretive framing for the writer, consistent with the sources above.)

## Current contested topics (dated; present evenhandedly)

- Climate: IPCC AR6 Working Group I Summary for Policymakers (August 2021), statement A.1: "It is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land." [source: https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_SPM.pdf (via WebSearch)]
- Literature surveys: Cook et al. 2013 found 97% of abstracts taking a position endorsed human-caused warming (66.4% took no position); a 2016 reanalysis called the 97% figure robust; Lynas et al. 2021 estimated over 99% of 2012–2020 papers agree. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scientific_consensus_on_climate_change&action=raw]
- Hansson (SEP, 2025 revision) states that the consensus "leaves no room for reasonable doubt" and that denial has delayed climate action; he also notes that the relation between academic epistemic relativism and denial is "controversial" (some relativists deny it encourages denial). Evenhandedness note: distinguish genuine scientific disagreement (e.g. on magnitudes, impacts, policy) from denial of the basic finding; policy choices are value questions science alone does not settle. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Alternative medicine: homeopathy was conceived in 1796 by Samuel Hahnemann; Australia's NHMRC (2015) found "no health conditions for which there is reliable evidence that homeopathy is effective"; NHS England funding ceased in 2017; a 2005 *Lancet* meta-analysis found no effect beyond placebo. Proponents argue for an as-yet-undefined biophysical mechanism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Homeopathy&action=raw] Evenhandedness note: "alternative medicine" is heterogeneous; some traditional remedies have been tested and adopted, so judgements should be claim by claim (Hansson: doctrines or specific claims, e.g. "the claim that MMR vaccine gives rise to autism"). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Haack's position (reported): rather than labelling work "pseudo-scientific", it is better to say exactly what is wrong with it. [source: WebSearch result on Laudan/Pigliucci debate; secondary]

## Step-by-step argument (for the writer)

Popper's argument from the asymmetry, framed for demarcation (sources: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=The_Logic_of_Scientific_Discovery&action=raw ; https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/):
1. Scientific laws are universal statements, equivalent to prohibitions ("All As are X" = "No A is not-X").
2. No finite set of singular observation statements entails a universal statement, so laws can never be verified (Hume).
3. But a universal statement is contradicted by a single true basic statement ("There is an A that is not X here"), so by modus tollens it can be refuted.
4. Therefore the logical mark that lets theories "clash with experience" is falsifiability, not verifiability.
5. A theory compatible with every possible observation (Popper's view of Freud and Adler), or kept compatible by ad hoc rescue (his view of later Marxism), forbids nothing and so is not empirical science; a theory that sticks its neck out (Einstein's light-bending) is.

Objection (Duhem, Quine; https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-underdetermination/): step 3 fails in practice. No hypothesis is tested alone; a failed prediction refutes only the conjunction of theory + auxiliaries + instrument assumptions, and logic does not say which to drop. So any theory can be saved, and falsifiability does not divide theories cleanly (Uranus/Neptune saved Newton; Mercury/Vulcan did not; Lakatos: had Galle not found Neptune, nobody would have abandoned Newton).

Popper's reply (https://iep.utm.edu/pop-sci/ ; https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/): falsifiability is a property of logical form plus a methodological rule. Saving a theory with an auxiliary hypothesis is legitimate if the auxiliary is independently testable (Neptune's predicted position); it is illegitimate if it is ad hoc, yielding no new testable predictions (his Marxism example). Basic statements are accepted by intersubjective decision, revisable like a jury verdict. Falsificationism is normative, not a description of all practice.

Counter-reply (Lakatos; Laudan): this turns the criterion into a judgement about whole research programmes over time (progressive vs degenerating), which is Lakatos's position, not a one-shot logical test; and (Laudan 1983) many pseudosciences are falsifiable and false, so falsifiability is neither necessary nor sufficient. Revival (Pigliucci & Boudry 2013; Hansson): no single criterion, but clusters of features and relation to the best available knowledge still sort cases.

## Debates

- Was Popper's criterion about meaning, science vs metaphysics, or science vs pseudoscience? In 1933–35 it was science vs metaphysics; the pseudoscience framing dates from 1957; Bartley (1968) stressed these are different problems, Popper did not. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Is falsifiability too strict and too lax? Hansson (2006) says it excludes legitimate science; Agassi (1991), Mahner (2007) and Laudan say it admits falsifiable pseudosciences; Popper's reply: a doctrine loses scientific status when its promoters break "the methodological rule that we must accept falsification" (1974, 985). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- Was Popper fair to Freud? Adolf Grünbaum (1984) argued Freud showed "a keen appreciation of methodological pitfalls" and that Popper's critique reflects "obliviousness to Freud's actual writings"; Grünbaum held psychoanalysis is testable and has fared badly, a different objection. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/]
- Was Popper fair to Marx? Cornforth (1968) and Hudelson (1980) dispute his reading. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/]
- Is Popper's account of basic statements a form of conventionalism, in tension with his realism? Lakatos: a "falsified" theory in Popper's conventional sense "may still be true". [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/]
- Demarcation dead or alive? Laudan (1983) vs Pigliucci, Mahner, Hansson, Boudry (2013 onward); Boudry (2022) suggests dropping "demarcation" while keeping diagnosis of pseudoscience; Hansson argues the "demarcation" metaphor misleads by suggesting no expertise is needed. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
- McLean: Ruse vs Laudan on whether "not science" or "false science" was the right verdict. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creationism/]
- 1919 eclipse: Earman & Glymour / Collins & Pinch (bias) vs Kennefick (defensible; not Eddington's call) vs Schindler (Kennefick's defence fails). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Eddington_experiment&action=raw ; WebSearch]
- Ibn al-Haytham as "first scientist": Al-Khalili, Steffens, Schramm vs Toomer's caution, Sabra's "confirmatory" reading, Kheirandish's earlier precedents. [sources above]
- Underdetermination: how serious? Quine/Duhem vs Laudan (1990). [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-underdetermination/]
- Epistemic relativism and pseudoscience: Hansson and Pennock link some relativists to denialist causes; others (Burningham and Cooper 1999) deny that relativism encourages denial. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/]
