## 1. Names: "Confucius", "Confucianism", ru
- "Confucius" is a Latinized combination of the surname Kong 孔 with the honorific *fuzi* 夫子 ("Master"). [source: SEP-Conf intro]
- The Latinization was coined by Jesuit missionaries to China in the late 16th century; the common modern Chinese name is Kongzi 孔子, "Master Kong". His given name was Qiu 丘 and his courtesy name Zhongni 仲尼. [source: WP-Confucius]
- IEP: "The Latinization of Kong(fu)zi to 'Confucius' originates with the interpretation of Chinese culture and thought by Jesuit missionaries". [source: IEP-Conf §7]
- "Mencius" is likewise a Latinization coined by 17th-century Jesuits, of Mengzi, "Master Meng"; his full name was Meng Ke. [source: SEP-Men §1]
- The Chinese term *Ru* 儒 predates Confucius; it connoted specialists in ritual and music, and later experts in Classical Studies. It is routinely translated "Confucian", but Csikszentmihalyi avoids the word "Confucian" because English uses it for many things (sage kings, temple cults, "bureaucracy", "meritocracy") and the term "is not well-defined". [source: SEP-Conf intro]
- Chinese names for the tradition (Rujia "the Ru school", Rujiao, Ruxue) do not use the name of Confucius at all. Some modern scholars prefer "Ruism"/"Ruists"; Robert Eno argues "Confucianism" has been "burdened ... with the ambiguities and irrelevant traditional associations". David Schaberg has suggested "Traditionalist" for *ru*. [source: WP-Confucianism]
- "Confucianism" is described by Rosenlee as "the inexact translation of its Chinese counterpart, Ru", a "much debated inexact translation" (citing Jensen 1997; Standaert 1999; Csikszentmihalyi 2020). [source: SEP-Gen intro and §1]
- Lionel Jensen, *Manufacturing Confucianism* (Duke, 1997) argues that the Jesuits (above all Matteo Ricci) created both the name "Confucius" and the idea of "Confucianism"; Nicolas Standaert replied with "The Jesuits Did NOT Manufacture 'Confucianism'" (1999). [source: WS-8 summary of reviews; Standaert 1999 and Jensen 1997 also cited in SEP-Gen]
- Historian Yan Buke argues that the Ru tradition began from the office of "Music master" (*yueshi*) described in the *Rituals of Zhou*. [source: SEP-Conf §3]

## 2. Confucius: dates, life and legend
- Dates: SEP gives "trad. 551–479 BCE"; IEP: "his traditional lifetime (551-479 BCE)". **Flag as traditional.** The 551 BCE birth date comes from the *Gongyang Commentary* to the *Spring and Autumn Annals*. [source: SEP-Conf intro and §2; IEP-Conf §1]
- Wikipedia gives c. 551 – c. 479 BCE, and "It is thought" he was born on 28 September 551 BCE (citing Huang 2013, Creel 1949); died aged 71 or 72. Treat the exact day as traditional. [source: WP-Confucius]
- Birthplace: SEP says born in the domain of Zou, in modern Shandong, south of Lu; his father came from Lu. Wikipedia: Zou 陬, in modern Qufu, Shandong, effectively under the lords of Lu. IEP: born in "the small state of Lu". (Minor disagreement about whether Zou counts as part of Lu.) [source: SEP-Conf §2; WP-Confucius; IEP-Conf §1]
- Period: Spring and Autumn period (IEP: 770–481 BCE); known mostly from Warring States texts (IEP: 403–221 BCE). [source: IEP-Conf §1]
- Father died when he was a small child, leaving the family poor but of some status; he became known young for expertise in Zhou ritual. Wikipedia: father Kong He (Shuliang He) died when Confucius was three; raised by his mother Yan Zhengzai. [source: SEP-Conf §2; WP-Confucius]
- Class: Confucius arose from the *shi* ("retainer" or "knight") class, many of whom became itinerant antiquarians in and out of office. [source: IEP-Conf §1]
- Offices (the sources disagree, which is itself the point): the *Zuozhuan* for 509 and 500 BCE names him Director of Corrections (*Sikou*) and assistant at rituals for a visiting Qi dignitary; the *Mencius* says he was Foodstuffs Scribe and Scribe in the Field (granary and herd accounts); Sima Qian adds Steward of Zhongdu, Minister of Works and even acting Chancellor. Csikszentmihalyi: the "inconsistent accounts of the offices in which he served" may reflect a proliferation of texts linking him to different traditions. [source: SEP-Conf §2]
- Wikipedia's narrative (following Creel, Dubs, the *Zuozhuan* and *Shiji*): appointed governor of a town in 501 BCE, rose to Minister of Crime; tried to have the three noble families' city walls demolished (498 BCE); left Lu in 497 BCE. Treat details as traditional/legendary. [source: WP-Confucius]
- The *Shiji* story: Qi sent 100 horses and 80 dancing girls to the Lu ruler, who neglected duties for three days; when the sacrificial meat was not sent to Confucius, he left. SEP gives the same story (a high Lu official stayed away from court for three days after Qi's gift of female entertainers; cf. Analects 18.4). [source: WP-Confucius; SEP-Conf §3]
- Wandering: stories place him in Wei, Song, Chen, Cai and Chu; Sima Qian turned them into a serial narrative of rulers failing to appreciate him. Wikipedia: travels c. 497–484 BCE, returning to Lu at 68 at the invitation of Ji Kangzi. [source: SEP-Conf §2; WP-Confucius]
- Disciples: Sima Qian lists 77 direct disciples (tradition also says 72) and some 3,000 students. [source: SEP-Conf §2; WP-Confucius]
- Sima Qian (c. 145–c. 86 BCE), *Records of the Grand Historian* (*Shiji*), chapter "Hereditary House of Confucius", is the first biography, written about three centuries after Confucius's death from anecdotes with didactic purposes; tying his philosophy to his biography is "risky and potentially circular". [source: SEP-Conf §2]
- IEP: "it is ironic that so little can be known about Confucius"; all sources postdate him "by a few decades at least and several centuries at most". [source: IEP-Conf intro and §1]
- Legend: Han hagiographies made him a superhuman "uncrowned king" (*suwang*), born with special body markings, later deified; Feng Youlan suggested that had these images prevailed he might have become a figure like Jesus. Wang Chong (27–c. 97 CE) used "uncrowned king" of him. [source: IEP-Conf §7; SEP-Conf §5]
- Analects 11.9 on the death of his favourite disciple (Yan Hui): "Heaven has forsaken me!" (SEP translation) / "Heaven has abandoned me! Heaven has abandoned me!" (IEP). [source: SEP-Conf §5; IEP-Conf §3]
- His birthday is celebrated as Teachers' Day in some parts of Asia. [source: SEP-Conf §4]

## 3. The Analects: what it is and how it was composed
- Chinese title *Lunyu* 論語; *lun* here means "to edit/select", *yu* "sayings": "edited conversations" or "selected speeches". [source: WP-Analects]
- The transmitted text has 20 chapters (books). In the Han there were a Lu version (20 chapters), a Qi version (22 chapters, two extra) and an "Old Text" version found in a wall of Confucius's house; Zhang Yu (d. 5 BCE), tutor to the crown prince, made a composite based on the Lu text. [source: WP-Analects; SEP-Conf §1 for Zhang Yu]
- Traditional account: Ban Gu (39–92 CE per SEP; 32–92 CE per SEP-Xun) said it was compiled by first- and second-generation disciples and then transmitted privately; Zhu Xi held the same. [source: SEP-Conf §2; WP-Analects]
- **Debate.** Challenges are old: Cui Shu (Qing) dated the last five books later; Itō Jinsai split "upper" (1–10) from "lower" (11–20) Analects; Arthur Waley thought books 3–9 earliest. [source: WP-Analects]
- **Brooks and Brooks.** E. Bruce Brooks and A. Taeko Brooks (*The Original Analects*, Columbia UP, 1998) treat the text as accretive: only Book 4 (and only part of it) reflects the historical Confucius; other books come from students and family members over time, with different, sometimes incompatible agendas. They hold that the oldest parts make *ren* pre-eminent and de-emphasize ritual, while a later trend headed by Confucius's descendants elevated ritual as the key to ren. The theory "has drawn appreciation and disagreement" (e.g. Slingerland 2000). [source: SEP-Eth §2.2; publication data SEP-Conf bibliography]
- WS-6 (second-hand): the Brooks date the text's composition over 230 years, 479–249 BCE, book by book. [source: WS-6, wsproject.org / reviews]
- **Zhu Weizheng and Hunter.** Zhu Weizheng's influential 1986 article argued that the lack of attributed quotations from and references to the Analects before the 2nd century BCE meant its status as the oldest stratum was undeserved. Michael J. Hunter (*Confucius beyond the Analects*, Brill, 2017) and others showed writers took "an acute interest in the Analects only in the late second and first centuries BCE". Csikszentmihalyi: this is "an attack on the authoritativeness of the Analects", not on the historicity of Confucius. [source: SEP-Conf §2]
- Wikipedia: because the Analects is not named in any source before the early Han, some scholars propose a date as late as 140 BCE for final compilation; most think it was compiled over about 200 years in the Warring States period and achieved final form in the Han. [source: WP-Analects]
- 75% of the Confucius sayings quoted in the *Mencius* are not in the received Analects. [source: WP-Analects]
- Manuscripts: Dingzhou (excavated 1973, tomb of 55 BCE); Pyongyang (Jongbaekdong, c. 62–45 BCE); Marquis of Haihun tomb (excavated 2011–15, 59 BCE), thought to contain the two lost Qi chapters ("Understanding the Way", "Questions about Jade"). These "confirm the sudden wide circulation of the Analects in the middle of the first century BCE". [source: SEP-Conf §2]
- Newer finds: the Anhui University "Zhong Ni said" manuscript (about two dozen sayings, seven overlapping the Analects; WP says 25 sayings, partly published 2022) and the Wangjiazui (Hubei, excavated 2019–21) "Kongzi said" manuscript; both c. 300 BCE. Wikipedia: they confirm that by the mid-Warring States period the tradition of preserving and organizing Confucius's sayings existed. [source: SEP-Conf §2; WP-Analects]
- Csikszentmihalyi's conclusion: the Analects "may well have been in part a topical selection from a larger and more diverse set of available Confucius-related materials"; the "essential core of the teachings of Confucius is historically underdetermined" and "still avidly debated". [source: SEP-Conf §2]
- Three ways of reading (Wong): the sinological approach of the Brooks; the philosopher's reading of the "influential received text" with a "textual author" (Tao Jiang 2022); and a third approach treating different strata as different thinkers on common problems. [source: SEP-Eth §2.2]
- IEP: "Most scholars remain confident that it is possible to extract from the Analects several philosophical themes ... that may be safely attributed" to Confucius. [source: IEP-Conf intro]
- Van Norden uses "Confucius" as shorthand for "Confucius as represented in the received text of the Analects" (citing Kim and Csikszentmihalyi 2014). Useful convention for the chapter. [source: SEP-Men note 2]

## 4. Confucius: key concepts
### Transmitter, not innovator
- Analects 7.1: "I transmit rather than innovate. I trust in and love the ancient ways" (Slingerland 2003, 64). IEP: Confucius "transmits, but does not innovate". Van Norden: he nonetheless called for "sometimes radical" changes, like modern revivalists. [source: SEP-Men §1; IEP-Conf §2]
- 3.14: Confucius "followed Zhou". [source: SEP-Conf §3]
### Ren
- Translations: "benevolence" (D. C. Lau 1970), "Good"/"Goodness" (Waley; Slingerland 2003), "humaneness", "co-humanity" (Peter Boodberg), "authoritative conduct"-type renderings are not covered here. [source: SEP-Eth §2.1; IEP-Conf §6]
- Before Confucius, *ren* referred to the aristocracy of bloodlines (the strong and handsome appearance of an aristocrat); in the Analects it is a moral excellence anyone can potentially achieve. [source: SEP-Eth §2.1]
- It is used both for comprehensive virtue and as one virtue among others; 12.22 defines it as "caring for others" (SEP-Conf) / "to care for people" (SEP-Eth). Confucius never claimed ren for himself. [source: SEP-Conf §4; SEP-Eth §2.1]
- WP: of about 60 discussions of ren in the Analects, very few have the later meaning "kind"; Confucius used it for an all-encompassing virtue no living person fully attained. [source: WP-Analects]
- The graph combines "person" and "two"; IEP warns folk etymologies mislead but says here they are instructive. [source: IEP-Conf §6]
- 12.1 (Slingerland 2003): "Restraining yourself and returning to the rites constitutes ren. If for one day you managed to restrain yourself and return to the rites, in this way you could lead the entire world back to ren. The key to achieving ren lies within yourself—how could it come from others?" Chenyang Li reads *wei* ("constitutes") as "makes/results in". [source: SEP-Eth §2.2]
- 12.1 also: "Do not look or listen, speak or move, unless it is in accordance with the rites" (SEP-Conf translation) / "Do not look at, do not listen to, do not speak of, do not do whatever is contrary to ritual propriety" (IEP). [source: SEP-Conf §4; IEP-Conf §4]
- 15.9: a gentleman would rather die than compromise ren. 7.30: "If I simply desire ren, I find that it is already there". [source: SEP-Conf §4; SEP-Eth §2.2]
### Li (ritual propriety)
- *Li* originally meant formal ceremonies (e.g. offerings to ancestors), expanding to etiquette and ethics generally. [source: SEP-Men §1]
- Ritual requires the right emotion. 3.26 (Slingerland 2003): "Someone who lacks magnanimity when occupying high office, who is not respectful when performing ritual, and who remains unmoved by sorrow when overseeing mourning rites—how could I bear to look upon such a person?" [source: SEP-Men §1]
- 3.3: "A man who is not ren—what has he to do with ritual?" (translator not named). 9.3: he accepts a cheaper silk cap but rejects bowing after ascending the stairs. 3.17: "though you care about the sheep, I care about the ritual" (SEP-Conf translation). [source: SEP-Eth §2.2; SEP-Conf §3]
- **Debate on ren and li (Kwong-loi Shun 1993):** the "instrumental" reading (ritual cultivates/expresses a ren already there) versus the "definitionalist" reading (li defines ren); Herbert Fingarette (1972) reads ritual as an end in itself, a shared, beautiful ceremony of human community. [source: SEP-Eth §2.2]
- Ritual psychology: Confucius innovated in his *rationale* for ritual, stressing the inner states of the performer (reverence, *jing*) rather than external benefits; Philip J. Ivanhoe: ritual is not just an indicator of values but an "inculcator" of them. [source: SEP-Conf §3]
- 8.8: "Raise yourself up with the Classic of Odes. Establish yourself with ritual. Complete yourself with music." (SEP-Conf translation). [source: SEP-Conf §4]
- Comparison: Aristotelian habituation broadly corresponds to Confucian cultivation, but Aristotle has no comparable emphasis on ritual. [source: SEP-Eth §2.2]
### Junzi
- *Junzi* originally "lord's son"/"prince's son"; in the Analects, ethical nobility. Translations: "gentleman" (early), "exemplary person" (Ames and Rosemont 1998), "profound person" (Tu Wei-ming). Opposed to the *xiaoren*, "small person". [source: SEP-Eth §2.1; IEP-Conf §6]
- 4.16 (IEP translation): "The profound person understands what is moral. The small person understands what is profitable." [source: IEP-Conf §6]
- 1.2: "The gentleman works at the roots. Once the roots are established, the way comes to life" (SEP-Conf); Slingerland 2003: "The gentleman applies himself to the roots. 'Once the roots are firmly established, the Way will grow.' Might we not say that filial piety and respect for elders constitute the root of Goodness?" (said by the disciple Youzi/You Ruo). [source: SEP-Conf §4; SEP-Men §1]
- 2.4 (IEP translation), the stages of life: "From the age of fifteen on, I have been intent upon learning; from thirty on, I have established myself; from forty on, I have not been confused; from fifty on, I have known the mandate of Heaven; from sixty on, my ear has been attuned; from seventy on, I have followed my heart's desire without transgressing what is right." [source: IEP-Conf §6]
- Five central virtues of the gentleman: ren, yi, li, zhi (wisdom), xin (trustworthiness); by the Han they were treated as a complete set. [source: SEP-Conf §4]
### Yi (rightness)
- *Yi* is "righteousness", "what is fitting"; D. C. Lau: ren is an attribute of agents, yi of actions. A gentleman "thinks of righteousness when faced with gain" (16.10, 19.10). [source: IEP-Conf §6; SEP-Conf §4]
### Xiao (filial piety)
- 2.7 (Slingerland 2003): "Nowadays 'filial' means simply being able to provide one's parents with nourishment. But even dogs and horses are provided with nourishment. If you are not respectful, wherein lies the difference?" [source: SEP-Men §1]
- 2.5: filial piety means "not contesting": serving parents in a ritually proper way while alive, and burying and sacrificing to them properly after death. [source: SEP-Conf §5]
- 4.18: remonstrate gently; most translations (Lau 1979, Slingerland 2003, Waley 1938) then say one should not oppose them if unpersuaded, but Legge (and Huang 2013) read it as not abandoning the effort to persuade. [source: SEP-Eth §2.4]
- 17.21: the three-year mourning matches the three years parents carry an infant. [source: SEP-Conf §5]
- 13.18, the sheep-stealing father (Slingerland 2003): "Among my people, those who we consider 'upright' are different from this: fathers cover up for their sons, and sons cover up for their fathers. 'Uprightness' is to be found in this." Doctrine of "care with distinctions" (often "love with distinctions"). [source: SEP-Men §1; SEP-Eth §2.4]
- Xunzi 29.1–2 allows following morality over a father's wishes. [source: SEP-Eth §2.4]
### Shu and zhong; the "silver rule" (15.24)
- 4.15: Zengzi summarizes the Master's Way as *zhong* and *shu*. IEP translation: "The Master's Way is nothing but other-regard and self-reflection." *Zhong* conventionally means "loyalty" (to a ruler); *shu* is glossed in 15.24. [source: IEP-Conf §6]
- **15.24, translations verified in a source read this session:**
  - David Hinton (as quoted in WP): Zi Gong asked: "Is there any one word that could guide a person throughout life?" The Master replied: "How about 'shu' [reciprocity]: never impose on others what you would not choose for yourself?" [source: WP-Golden_Rule]
  - IEP (translator not named): "What you do not desire for yourself, do not do to others." (15.24) [source: IEP-Conf §6]
  - SEP-Conf (Csikszentmihalyi's own, quoting 12.2 and citing 5.12, 15.24): "Do not impose upon others those things that you yourself do not desire". [source: SEP-Conf §4]
  - James Legge (second-hand, via WebSearch summary of a Stanford-hosted text): "Is not RECIPROCITY such a word? What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others." Use only with this caveat. [source: WS-10, chinesetexts.stanford.edu]
- Same principle appears in 12.2 and (WP) 5.12 and 6.30 (a positive form: "a man with ren, desiring to establish himself, helps others establish themselves"; WP numbers this 6.28). [source: SEP-Eth §2.2; WP-Analects; WP-Golden_Rule]
- **Numbering caution:** Wikipedia's Analects article cites the passage as "§15.23", its Golden Rule article as XV.24. Numbering differs by edition (Legge's numbering runs one lower in Book 15). Cite as 15.24 (as SEP, IEP, Slingerland) and note the variation.
- The negative form is "sometimes called the Silver Rule"; it asks one only to refrain from harming others. [source: WP-Golden_Rule]
- **Debate:** E. Bruce Brooks and others think these "Golden Rule" passages may be interpolations, since axiomatic rules are unlike the Analects' situational teaching; their reading as abstract principles has been shaped by perceived similarity to Matthew 7:12 and Luke 6:31; the *Records of Ritual* version concerns how a ruler's example works. [source: SEP-Conf §4]
- WS-10 (second-hand): Slingerland renders *shu* as "understanding". [source: WS-10, taylorfrancis chapter abstract]
### Zhengming (rectification of names)
- Analects 13.3 stresses the need to rectify the names (*ming*) of social roles, because rituals had come to express self-aggrandizement by the powerful. IEP: Confucius considered it "prior to all other philosophical activity (Analects 13.3)". [source: SEP-TI; IEP-Men §4]
- 12.11 (IEP translation): "Let the ruler be a ruler, the subject a subject, a father a father, and a son a son." [source: IEP-Conf §4]
- Mencius 1B8 is IEP's example of rectifying names (see §6). Xunzi wrote a chapter "Rectifying Names" (ch. 22): "Names have no inherent appropriateness" (22.2g, Goldin translation), but its concern is moral, not linguistic. [source: IEP-Men §4; SEP-Xun §7]
### De and government by virtue
- *De* originally meant a kind of political charisma a king accumulated through good character and generosity; conventionally translated "Virtue" (capital V). [source: SEP-Men §1]
- **2.3 (Slingerland 2003):** "If you try to guide the common people with coercive regulations and keep them in line with punishments, the common people will become evasive and will have no sense of shame. If, however, you guide them with Virtue, and keep them in line by means of ritual, the people will have a sense of shame and will rectify themselves." [source: SEP-Men §1]
- 2.3 (IEP translation, second half only): "Direct the people with moral force and regulate them with ritual, and they will possess shame, and moreover, they will be righteous." [source: IEP-Conf §4]
- Confucius did not completely dismiss force but treated it as a last resort. [source: SEP-Men §1]
- 2.1 (IEP translation): "One who rules by moral force may be compared to the North Star – it occupies its place and all the stars pay homage to it." [source: IEP-Conf §5]
- 12.19 (IEP translation): "The moral force of the profound person is like the wind; the moral force of the small person is like the grass. Let the wind blow over the grass and it is sure to bend." [source: IEP-Conf §6]
- 12.7: trust is more important than food or weapons: "If the people do not find the ruler trustworthy, the state will not stand" (SEP-Conf translation). [source: SEP-Conf §4]
### Heaven, human nature, learning
- Confucius inherited the Zhou doctrine of the Mandate of Heaven (*tianming*); his own failure arguably served as a counterexample; Robert Eno notes Heaven becomes "increasingly naturalized" (17.19, "what need does Heaven have to speak?"). [source: SEP-Conf §5]
- 6.22: show reverence for ghosts and spirits "while maintaining one's distance" (SEP-Conf translation); Confucius's silence on the divine is attested at 5.13, 7.21, 11.12; A. C. Graham: Confucius seems "of two minds" about Tian. [source: SEP-Conf §4; IEP-Conf §3]
- 17.2: "By nature people are close, by habituation they are miles apart" (SEP-Conf translation); Confucius does not use the term *renxing* and was "notably silent" on human nature (5.13). [source: SEP-Conf §4; IEP-Conf §9]
- 2.15: "If you learn without reflecting, you will be lost. If you reflect without learning, you will be in danger" (cf. Slingerland 2003, 13; Van Norden's modified version). [source: SEP-Men §1]
- 9.18: "I have yet to meet a man who loves Virtue as much as he loves sex" (cf. Slingerland 2003, 92). [source: SEP-Men §1]
- 7.7: he would teach anyone who brought "a bundle of dried meat". [source: SEP-Conf §3]

## 5. Mencius: life and text
- Dates: traditionally 372–289 BCE (Legge); "many recent scholars put his lifetime about twenty years earlier"; D. C. Lau finds no convincing evidence of events in the *Mencius* before 319 BCE; Van Norden thinks he was probably born around 390 BCE. Wikipedia gives c. 371 – c. 289 BCE. **Flag as traditional; give the debate.** [source: SEP-Men note 6; IEP-Men §1; WP-Mencius]
- Born about a century after Confucius died, in the state of Zou (modern Shandong); said to have studied under Confucius's grandson Zisi (IEP: "most modern scholars doubt this"); famous stories of his mother's devotion to his education. [source: SEP-Men §1; IEP-Men §1]
- Held office in Qi (2B6–7; IEP thinks probably an honorary title); involved in Qi's invasion of Yan; resigned when the ruler would not reform. Dialogues with King Hui of Liang (r. 370–319) and King Xuan of Qi (r. 319–301); encounters datable 323–314 BCE. [source: SEP-Men §1; IEP-Men §1, §4, §7]
- Known as the "Second Sage"; elevated by Neo-Confucians; Han Yu called him the "most pure" follower of Confucius. [source: SEP-Men intro and §5]
- Text: the *Mengzi*, probably compiled by his disciples or their disciples, edited and shortened by Zhao Qi in the 2nd century CE; seven books, each in two parts (A/B), so 1A1 is the first passage and 7B38 the last. IEP: most scholars agree it was assembled by Mencius and immediate disciples, perhaps shortly after his death; the philological controversy is "far less intense" than for the Analects. [source: SEP-Men §1; IEP-Men §1]
- Opponents: Mozi ("impartial caring", *jian ai*) and Yang Zhu ("for oneself"). 3B9 (Van Norden 2008): "Yang Zhu is 'for oneself.' This is to not have a ruler. Mozi is 'impartial caring.' This is to not have a father. To not have a father and to not have a ruler is to be an animal." [source: SEP-Men §4]
- 1A1 (Van Norden 2008): "Why must Your Majesty speak of 'profit'? Let there simply be benevolence and righteousness." [source: SEP-Men §4]
- "Climbing a tree in search of a fish" (1A7) for conquest by force. [source: SEP-Men §1]
- 7B3 (Van Norden 2008): "It would be better not to have the Documents than to believe everything in it." [source: SEP-Men §1]

## 6. Mencius: the argument for the goodness of human nature (reconstruction)
- Slogan: *xing shan*, "[human] nature is good". It is often misquoted as "fundamentally good" (*xing ben shan*), a gloss from Zhu Xi that entered the *Three Character Classic*. "Nature" occurs in only 17 passages; the phrase "nature is good" in only two (3A1, 6A6). [source: SEP-Men §3]
- What "nature" means: following A. C. Graham (1967), the *xing* of X is what X will develop if given a healthy environment for its kind (like an orange seed's nature to bear fruit, though most seeds never germinate). So "human nature is good" means humans tend to become good in a healthy environment. [source: SEP-Men §3]
- **Child at the well, 2A6 (Van Norden 2008):** "The reason why I say that all humans have hearts that are not unfeeling toward others is this. Suppose someone suddenly saw a child about to fall into a well: anyone in such a situation would have a feeling of alarm and compassion—not because one sought to get in good with the child's parents, not because one wanted fame among one's neighbors and friends, and not because one would dislike the sound of the child's cries. From this we can see that if one is without the feeling of compassion, one is not human." [source: SEP-Men §4]
- IEP's translation (translator not named) continues the passage: "...without a heart-mind that sympathizes one is not human; without a heart-mind aware of shame, one is not human; without a heart-mind that defers to others, one is not human; and without a heart-mind that approves and condemns, one is not human." And: "A heart-mind that sympathizes is the sprout of co-humanity [ren]; a heart-mind that is aware of shame is the sprout of rightness [yi]; a heart-mind that defers to others is the sprout of ritual propriety [li]; a heart-mind that approves and condemns is the sprout of wisdom [zhi].... If anyone having the four sprouts within himself knows how to develop them to the full, it is like fire catching alight, or a spring as it first bursts through." [source: IEP-Men §6–7]
- Step-by-step reconstruction (built from SEP-Men §§2–4 and IEP-Men §§6–7):
  1. Anyone who suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well feels alarm and compassion (appeal to experience).
  2. The feeling is not explained by self-interest: not to win the parents' favour, not for reputation, not from dislike of the noise (this rules out the egoist account, i.e. Yang Zhu's).
  3. So all humans have a heart that "is not unfeeling toward others"; one without it "is not human".
  4. Likewise all have feelings of shame/disdain, of deference/respect, and of approval/disapproval.
  5. These four feelings are the "sprouts" (*duan*) of ren, yi, li and zhi.
  6. Human nature is what a thing develops given healthy conditions; these sprouts grow into virtues if cultivated ("extended" or "filled out", by reflection, *si*).
  7. Therefore human nature is good: it has a built-in tendency toward virtue. Badness comes from injuring or neglecting the sprouts, "not the fault of their potential" (6A6).
  Note IEP's point: Mencius claims only the *feeling*, not that the bystander acts. [source: IEP-Men §6]
- 6A6 (Van Norden 2008): "As for what they are inherently [qíng], they can become good. This is what I mean by calling their natures [xìng] good. As for their becoming not good, this is not the fault of their potential.... Benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and wisdom are not welded to us externally. We inherently have them. It is simply that we do not reflect upon [sī] them." [source: SEP-Men §3]
- Kwong-loi Shun's summary: humans "have a constitution comprising certain emotional predispositions that already point in the direction of goodness... One's becoming bad is not the fault of one's constitution" (Shun 1997, 219–20). [source: SEP-Men §3]
- Extension: King Xuan spared an ox led to slaughter (1A7); Mencius urges him to extend that compassion to his people. Saying he can care for an ox but not for his people is like saying "my strength is sufficient to lift heavy weight, but not enough to lift a feather" (adapted from Lau 1970). David Nivison revived the debate about how recognizing similarity can motivate (Nivison 1980). [source: SEP-Men §2; SEP-Eth §2.5]
- Explaining wrongdoing (6A15, Van Norden 2008): "It is not the function of the ears and eyes to reflect, and they are misled by things.... But the function of the heart is to reflect. If it reflects, then it will get it. If it does not reflect, then it will not get it." [source: SEP-Men §3]
- Prerequisites for extension: material needs met (1A7: punishing the starving who turn to crime "is to trap them"), ethical education (3A4), individual effort (6A9, the go-player analogy). [source: SEP-Men §2]
- Ox Mountain (6A8) is named in SEP only as "the xing of a mountain ecosystem (6A8)"; the parable itself was not read in a source this session. [source: SEP-Meta]
- **Objection: Gaozi (6A1–6A4).** Gaozi is known only from the *Mencius*; ca. 420–350 BCE (WP; dates uncertain); he held human nature is neither good nor bad. [source: IEP-Men §9; WP-Gaozi]
  - Willow analogy, 6A1 (Legge 1895, as quoted in WP): Gaozi: "Man's nature is like the [qi]-willow, and righteousness is like a cup or a bowl. The fashioning benevolence and righteousness out of man's nature is like the making cups and bowls from the [qi]-willow." Mencius: "Can you, leaving untouched the nature of the willow, make with it cups and bowls? You must do violence and injury to the willow, before you can make cups and bowls with it. If you must do violence and injury to the willow in order to make cups and bowls with it, on your principles you must in the same way do violence and injury to humanity in order to fashion from it benevolence and righteousness! Your words, alas! would certainly lead all men on to reckon benevolence and righteousness to be calamities." [source: WP-Gaozi]
  - Water analogy, 6A2 (Legge, via WP): Gaozi: "Man's nature is like water whirling round in a corner. Open a passage for it to the east, and it will flow to the east... Man's nature is indifferent to good and evil, just as the water is indifferent to the east and west." Mencius: "Water indeed will flow indifferently to the east or west, but will it flow indifferently up or down? The tendency of man's nature to good is like the tendency of water to flow downwards.... by striking water and causing it to leap up, you may make it go over your forehead... but are such movements according to the nature of water? It is the force applied which causes them. When men are made to do what is not good, their nature is dealt with in this way." [source: WP-Gaozi]
  - 6A3: Gaozi says nature is what is inborn/alive; Mencius's reductio: then dogs, oxen and humans would have the same nature. [source: SEP-Men §4]
  - 6A4–5: whether rightness (yi) is internal or external; the uncle and younger-brother-impersonating-an-ancestor example (6A5) shows respect follows the agent's inner orientation. [source: IEP-Men §7]
  - Kwong-loi Shun (via Chong 2007, second-hand): Gaozi compares nature to the willow's pliability; Mencius to the willow's own nature. [source: WS-7]
- Wong's point: the water metaphor (develops unless interfered with) and the sprout metaphor (needs nurture) are "significantly different"; Xunzi's criticism bites against the water version more than the sprout version. [source: SEP-Eth §2.5–2.6]

## 7. Mencius on rulers and the right to depose tyrants
- 1B8 (IEP translation, translator not named): King Xuan asks whether Tang and Wu rebelled against and killed Jie and Zhou; Mencius says yes. "Is it permissible for a vassal to murder his lord?" Mencius: "One who robs co-humanity [ren] you call a 'robber'; one who robs the right [yi] you call a 'wrecker'; and one who robs and wrecks you call an 'outlaw.' I have heard that [Wu] punished the outlaw Zhou – I have not heard that he murdered his lord." IEP reads this as rectification of names. [source: IEP-Men §4]
- WP's version (translator not named): "I have merely heard of killing a villain Zhou, but I have not heard of murdering [him as] the ruler." [source: WP-Mencius]
- 7B14 (IEP translation): "The people are to be valued most, the altars of the grain and the land ... next, the ruler least." 5B9: ministers of royal kin may depose a ruler who will not listen to repeated admonition. [source: IEP-Men §4]
- IEP: Mencius endorses a "right of revolution" but "is no democrat"; his ideal is a sage-king, accepted by Heaven and people (5A5). [source: IEP-Men §4]
- **Debate:** Bai Tongdong (2008) invokes Mencius as a basis for (limited) democracy; Justin Tiwald (2008, "A Right of Rebellion in the Mengzi?") cautions against overestimating his championing of popular will. [source: SEP-Men §5]
- Well-field system (3A3): nine squares, eight families, central field farmed for the government's tax. [source: SEP-Men §1]

## 8. Xunzi: life and text
- Name Xun Kuang; called "the most revered of teachers" (*zui wei laoshi*). Born around 310 BCE (if he went to Qi at 15 *sui*, the more plausible reading, rather than 50); died after 238 BCE, when his patron Lord Chunshen was assassinated. Wikipedia: c. 310 – after 238 BCE, born in Zhao, studied at the Jixia Academy in Qi, later magistrate of Lanling (Chu). IEP gives c. 310 – c. 220 BCE. **Flag dates as uncertain.** [source: SEP-Xun §1; WP-Xunzi_philosopher; IEP-Xun title; IEP-Conf §9]
- Sima Qian's biography "is known to contain serious distortions" (Kern 2015). [source: SEP-Xun §1]
- Text: all editions derive from Liu Xiang (79–8 BCE), who found 322 bundles and eliminated 290 as duplicates; consensus: "predominantly authentic essays" not organized by Xunzi. IEP: 32 books, about 25 considered mostly or wholly authentic; mostly sustained essays, unlike the dialogues of the *Mencius*. [source: SEP-Xun §1; IEP-Xun §1]
- Students: Han Fei (d. 233 BCE) and Li Si (d. 208 BCE), plus transmitters of the classics; this association with Legalism later damaged his reputation; Zhu Xi linked him to Shen Buhai and Shang Yang. Today "the tide has reversed almost completely". [source: SEP-Xun §9]
- He wrote against Mencius, who was "probably long dead"; Mencius never met him. [source: SEP-Xun §2; IEP-Men §9]

## 9. Xunzi: "human nature is bad" (reconstruction)
- Chapter 23 title *Xing'e* 性惡: "Human Nature is Evil" (Goldin) / "Human Nature Is Bad" (Hutton's 2014 title, per SEP-Men and SEP-Eth). [source: SEP-Xun §2; SEP-Eth §2.6]
- Xunzi defines *xing* differently from Mencius: "What is so by birth is called xing" (22.1b, Goldin). [source: SEP-Xun §2]
- Core claim (Goldin's translation, 23.1a): "Human xing is evil; what is good is artifice. Now human xing is as follows. At birth there is fondness for profit in it. If one follows this, contention and robbery arise, and deference and courtesy are destroyed. ... There must be the transformation [brought about by] the methods of a teacher and the Way of ritual and morality; this will result in deference and courtesy, converge on refinement and principles, and come to rest in order." [source: SEP-Xun §2]
- Against Mencius (as quoted in SEP-Men from Ivanhoe and Van Norden 2005, 299): "Mengzi says: When people engage in learning, this manifests the goodness of their nature. I say: This is not so.... Those things in people that cannot be learned and cannot be worked at are called their 'nature.' Those things in people that they become capable of through learning and that they achieve through working at them are called their 'deliberate efforts.'" [source: SEP-Men §5]
- **Warped wood** (Ivanhoe and Van Norden 2005, 298, via SEP-Men): "crooked wood must await steaming and straightening on the shaping frame, and only then does it become straight. Blunt metal must await honing and grinding, and only then does it become sharp." [source: SEP-Men §5]
- **Potter**: just as a potter's vessel comes from his conscious activity, not his original nature, the sages generated rituals and standards of righteousness from their conscious activity. [source: SEP-Eth §2.6; IEP-Xun §3b, §3d]
- Step-by-step reconstruction (from SEP-Xun §2, SEP-Eth §2.6, IEP-Xun §3a):
  1. Nature (*xing*) is what we have at birth, unlearned and unworked-at (the eye's seeing, the ear's hearing; desires for profit, sights, sounds, flavours).
  2. If these inborn desires are simply followed, they lead to contention, robbery and disorder, and courtesy is destroyed.
  3. What leads to disorder when followed cannot be called good; so nature is *e* (bad/detestable).
  4. Goodness (deference, ritual order) exists, but it requires teachers, ritual and righteousness, i.e. "artifice" (*wei*, conscious activity, deliberate effort).
  5. Whatever requires effort and learning is by definition not part of nature.
  6. So "human nature is bad; what is good is artifice". Further arguments (IEP): if people were good by nature, the sages would not have needed to create ritual; and people desire the good because they lack it (one desires what one does not have).
  7. Cultivation is therefore *transforming* nature (*huaxing*), like steaming crooked wood or firing clay, not growing a sprout.
- Main objection and reply: Mencius and Xunzi may be talking at cross-purposes because they define "nature" differently (Graham 1967); both agree all humans can become good (Graham 1989; Shun 1997). Reply: there may still be a substantive disagreement, since Xunzi explicitly denies innate moral sprouts and locates moral models outside the self. [source: SEP-Men §5; SEP-Xun §2–3]
- A further objection (Wong): if nature is only self-interested, how can people come to love ritual for its own sake? Xunzi's ritual chapter allows natural feelings (love for parents, grief) that ritual expresses, giving morality a foothold. [source: SEP-Eth §2.6]
- IEP: Xunzi "does not believe that people are evil" in the sense of deliberately and perversely violating morality; they are "morally blind by nature". Yao and Jie had the same original nature. [source: IEP-Xun §3a]
- **Translating *e*:** its basic meaning is close to "detestable" (as a verb, *wu* 惡 "to hate"); "evil" is acceptable only if an Augustinian conception is not intended; some scholars prefer "bad". WP adds Michael Nylan's "odious" and Kurtis Hagen's "problematic", "uncontrolled", "crude". [source: SEP-Xun §2; WP-Xunzi_philosopher]
- Authenticity debate: the chapter is "occasionally impugned as corrupt or inauthentic"; Zhou Chicheng 2015 argues Xunzi did not write it. [source: SEP-Xun §2]
- The Guodian manuscript *The Xing Emerges from the Endowment* (c. 300 BCE) defines *xing* much as Xunzi does, suggesting Mencius's usage may have been the eccentric one. [source: SEP-Xun §2]

## 10. Xunzi: ritual's origin and Heaven as natural
- Origin of ritual (Goldin translation, 4.12): "If people follow their desires, then boundaries cannot contain them and objects cannot satisfy them. Thus the Former Kings restrained them and established for them ritual and morality in order to divide them [into classes]." Superficially "reminiscent of Hobbes or Rousseau"; IEP compares his state of nature to Hobbes. [source: SEP-Xun §3; IEP-Xun §3a]
- But rituals are not arbitrary conventions: they accord with "that which makes humans human" (the distinctions, *bian*, humans naturally draw) and they foster moral and psychological development. Three-year mourning (19.9a): "When a wound is colossal, its duration is long; when pain is profound, the recovery is slow." [source: SEP-Xun §3]
- Rituals are like the markers fording parties leave in rivers: the sages "marked the Way" (17.11). [source: SEP-Xun §4; IEP-Xun §3d]
- Heaven: never intercedes in human affairs; "The revolutions of the sun, moon, and stars, and the cyclical calendar—these were the same under Yu and Jie" (17.4). Knowing Heaven is impossible; knowing the Way is open to all. [source: SEP-Xun §4]
- Rain sacrifice (17.8, Goldin): "If the sacrifice for rain [is performed], and it rains, what of it? I say: It is nothing. Even if there had been no sacrifice, it would have rained.... Thus the noble man takes [these ceremonies] to be embellishment, but the populace takes them to be spiritual." IEP version (translator not named): "You pray for rain and it rains. Why? For no particular reason, I say. It is just as though you had not prayed for rain and it rained anyway." [source: SEP-Xun §6; IEP-Xun §2]
- Portents: shooting stars and groaning trees are merely "shifts in Heaven and Earth"; the real dangers are "human portents" (bad plowing, malicious government) (17.7). [source: SEP-Xun §6]
- Chapter 17 is "Discourse on Heaven" (also translated "Discourse on Nature"); Knoblock translates *tian* as "nature". [source: IEP-Xun §2; SEP-Eth §2.6]
- **Debate:** is Xunzi's Way discovered or constructed? "Probably the greatest controversy in Xunzi studies today" (Goldin), raised by A. C. Graham; key text 8.3. Wong: Xunzi could be a constructivist and an objectivist (Nivison 1991). [source: SEP-Xun §5; SEP-Eth §2.6]
- The heart-mind (*xin*) has emptiness, unity and tranquility; it "issues commands but does not receive commands" (21.6a). [source: SEP-Xun §8]
- Xunzi is "the first to offer an organized Confucian curriculum" (IEP). He accepted that some punishments are needed. [source: IEP-Xun §3b, §5]

## 11. Women and the Confucian canon
- There is no indigenous conceptual equivalent of "gender"; man and woman are kinship- and ritual-dependent roles marked by *nei* (inside, domestic) and *wai* (outside, public). [source: SEP-Gen intro, §1]
- Analects passage grouping women with *xiaoren* (Chinese 唯女子與小人為難養也) as hard to deal with/nurture; "much-discussed", with commentators debating whether it means all women. (Passage number 17.25 not confirmed in a source read; English wording not verified.) [source: WP-Confucianism]
- Sin Yee Chan (2000): Analects and Mencius assume women's roles are inner, but give no argument that women have lesser capacities; this suggests a Confucianism in which women can become junzi. Raphals (1998) and Nylan (2000) find early texts relatively positive about women compared with later Neo-Confucian sources; the *Lienü zhuan* portrays women with intellectual virtues. [source: SEP-Eth §2.10]
- Chenyang Li (1994) compares ren with the feminist ethics of care (Gilligan, Noddings). Rosenlee defends *xiao* as the root of a care ethic and asks men to share caring. [source: SEP-Eth §2.10]
- Li-Hsiang Lisa Rosenlee, *Confucianism and Women: A Philosophical Interpretation* (SUNY Press): dated 2006 in SEP-Gen, 2007 in SEP-Eth. Rosenlee herself (SEP-Gen) argues imposing Western paradigms misconstrues gender oppression in Confucianism. [source: SEP-Gen; SEP-Eth]
- Ban Zhao (SEP: c. 45–117 CE; WP: 45 or 49 – c. 117/120 CE) completed the *History of Han* (*Book of Han*) after her brother Ban Gu's death; instructed palace women and male scholars; advised Empress Dowager Deng. Her *Nüjie* (*Admonitions/Lessons for Women*, c. 106 CE) emphasizes modesty and compliance but argues that girls should receive classical education, "probably the earliest extant text in human history advocating for equal education for women" (Swann 1932, cited by Rosenlee); she quietly misused the *Liji* to support this. [source: SEP-Gen §3.3; WP-Ban_Zhao]
- Later developments: from the Han, the "three obediences"; Song-era emphasis on chastity, Cheng Yi's "To starve to death is a small matter, but to lose one's chastity is a great matter". Joseph Adler: Neo-Confucian writings do not necessarily reflect actual practice. [source: WP-Confucianism]

## 12. Han sponsorship, the Five Classics and later reception
- Five Classics (*Wujing*): *Odes* (305 poems), *Documents*, *Rites*, *Changes* (*Yijing*), *Spring and Autumn Annals* (chronicle of Lu, 722–481 BCE). Traditionally Confucius edited them; this is doubted. [source: WP-Four_Books_and_Five_Classics; SEP-Conf §5]
- IEP: the Five Classics "were established as the basis for the imperial civil service examinations in 136 BCE". WP: in 136 BCE Emperor Wu "founded what became the Imperial University". (The published History chapter gives 124 BCE for the Imperial Academy; see contradictions.) [source: IEP-Conf §8; WP-Emperor_Wu_of_Han]
- SEP-Men: Han Confucianism was a "syncretic form" including Daoism, Legalism, yin-yang and Five Phases; Dong Zhongshu (ca. 198–ca. 107 BCE) was deeply influential. [source: SEP-Men §5; SEP-Xun §9]
- First commentaries to the Analects were by tutors to the crown prince; experts in the Five Classics received government positions. [source: SEP-Conf §1]
- Neo-Confucianism (Learning of the Way, *Daoxue*): a revival provoked by Buddhism; Han Yu (768–824) the flashpoint; Zhu Xi (1130–1200) grouped the *Great Learning*, *Analects*, *Mencius* and *Mean* as the Four Books; his interpretation became the basis of the civil service examination in 1313; exams abolished in China in 1905. Neo-Confucians reinterpreted Mencius with Buddhist-influenced concepts (Pattern, *li* 理), criticized by Dai Zhen (1724–1777). [source: SEP-Men §5; SEP-SM §2.2; WP-Confucianism]
- May Fourth Movement: student protests in Beijing, 4 May 1919; New Culture leaders blamed Confucian values for national weakness, calling for "Mr. Science" and "Mr. Democracy" in place of "Mr. Confucius"; pejorative "Confucius's family shop" (*Kongjiadian*). Mencius's reputation fell with Confucianism; under Mao it was "feudal". [source: WP-May_Fourth_Movement; WP-Confucianism; SEP-Men §5]
- New Confucianism: Xiong Shili (1885–1968) and followers Mou Zongsan (1909–1995), Tang Junyi (1909–1978), Xu Fuguan (1903–1982); the New Confucian Manifesto (drafted by Tang, with Zhang Junmai; WP: published 1958). Mou saw Kant as illuminating Mencius but thought Confucian ethics transcends Kant's limits. [source: SEP-Mod intro; SEP-Men §5; WP-New_Confucianism]
- Europe: Jesuits portrayed Confucius as inspired by natural theology; Leibniz (1646–1716) praised him; Jesuits Intorcetta, Couplet and others published translations of several Confucian works in Paris in 1687 (*Confucius Sinarum Philosophus*); Ruggieri had begun translating after returning to Italy in 1588. Hegel condemned Confucius; Mao castigated him. Hu Shi (1891–1962) wrote an early history of Chinese philosophy on the Western model. [source: SEP-Conf §1; WP-Confucius; IEP-Conf §7]

## 13. Comparison with Aristotle's virtue ethics
- It is "relatively uncontroversial" that virtues are a focus in Confucian texts in a broad sense, but contested whether Confucianism is a "virtue ethic" in the narrow Western theoretical sense; Roger Ames (2011, 2021) and Robert Neville (2016) are critics, Ames proposing "role ethics". [source: SEP-Eth §2.1]
- Henry Rosemont and Roger Ames contrast prescribed roles with "virtue ethics". [source: SEP-Conf §5]
- Jiyuan Yu (2007): *dao* plays a role analogous to *eudaimonia*. [source: SEP-Eth §2.1]
- Bryan Van Norden, *Virtue Ethics and Consequentialism in Early Chinese Philosophy* (2007), attends to analogies between Confucius's way and Aristotle's "good life". [source: SEP-Conf §4]
- Virtue-ethics readings of Mencius began with Lee Yearley (1990, *Mencius and Aquinas*). Similarities with Aristotle: wisdom as flexible responsiveness (Mencian wisdom "somewhat like Aristotelian phronēsis"), cultivation over rules, distinguishing real virtues from counterfeits. Differences: Aristotle values theoretical contemplation, which Confucians do not; Confucians treat family life as intrinsic to living well. [source: SEP-Men §2, §5]
- Comparative works: May Sim, *Remastering Morals with Aristotle and Confucius* (2007); Jiyuan Yu, *The Ethics of Confucius and Aristotle: Mirrors of Virtue* (2007). [source: SEP-TI; SEP-Men bibliography]

## Debates
- How much of the Analects is Confucius's own: traditional view (disciples compiled it soon after his death; Ban Gu, Zhu Xi) vs. accretion (Brooks and Brooks: only part of Book 4 authentic, composition 479–249 BCE) vs. late fixing (Zhu Weizheng 1986; Hunter 2017: interest only from late 2nd/1st c. BCE; possibly as late as c. 140 BCE). Manuscripts of c. 300 BCE (Anhui "Zhong Ni said", Wangjiazui "Kongzi said") show collections of sayings existed by the mid-Warring States, but only partly overlapping the received text. [source: SEP-Conf §2; SEP-Eth §2.2; WP-Analects]
- Whether the Golden/Silver Rule passages (12.2, 15.24) are interpolations (E. Bruce Brooks) and whether they state a general principle at all. [source: SEP-Conf §4]
- Ren and li: instrumental vs. definitionalist (Shun 1993); Fingarette (1972) on ritual as end in itself; Chenyang Li's "cultural grammar" reading. [source: SEP-Eth §2.2]
- Whether Confucian ethics is a virtue ethics (Van Norden, Yu, Sim, Yearley) or a role ethics (Ames, Rosemont; Neville). [source: SEP-Eth §2.1; SEP-Conf §5; SEP-Men §5]
- Whether Mencius and Xunzi really disagree or define *xing* differently (Graham 1967; Shun 1997; Goldin). [source: SEP-Men §5; SEP-Xun §2]
- Translating *e* as "evil" vs. "bad" vs. "detestable"/"odious". [source: SEP-Xun §2; WP-Xunzi_philosopher]
- Whether Xunzi's Way is discovered or constructed (Graham; Hagen; Goldin). [source: SEP-Xun §5]
- Mencius and democracy / right of rebellion (Bai 2008 vs. Tiwald 2008); nepotism in Shun stories 5A3, 7A35 (Liu 2007 vs. Guo 2007). [source: SEP-Men §5]
- Confucianism and women: patriarchal ideology vs. early texts relatively open, later Neo-Confucian hardening (Raphals, Nylan, Sin Yee Chan); care-ethics affinities (Chenyang Li, Rosenlee). [source: SEP-Eth §2.10; SEP-Gen]
- Whether the Jesuits "manufactured" Confucianism (Jensen 1997 vs. Standaert 1999). [source: WS-8; SEP-Gen]

## Consistency with published Periergia texts
Checked: `philosophy--history-of-philosophy--overview.sql`, `philosophy--what-is-philosophy.sql`, `history--ancient-civilizations--ancient-china.sql` ("Ancient China"; this is the History chapter covering Shang, Zhou and the Hundred Schools), `history--classical-antiquity--han-china.sql`.
- **Overview** gives "Confucius (551–479 BCE)" (text and image caption) without "traditional". Not wrong, but this chapter should mark the dates as traditional (SEP "trad."; IEP "traditional lifetime").
- **Overview**: Confucianism "later synthesized with Buddhist metaphysics as Neo-Confucianism". Sources present Neo-Confucianism as an anti-Buddhist revival that *reinterpreted* classics using Buddhist-influenced concepts (SEP-Men §5), whose cosmology "marked the tradition off from those of Buddhism and Daoism" (SEP-Conf §1). "Synthesized with" is a simplification; this chapter should say "revival influenced by Buddhism".
- **Ancient China (History)**: Mencius "about 371–289 BCE". Consistent with WP; SEP/IEP say traditional 372–289, and SEP notes recent scholars move his life about 20 years earlier (born c. 390). Suggest this chapter give "traditionally 372–289 BCE" and mention the earlier dating.
- **Ancient China**: Xunzi "about 310–after 238 BCE" — consistent with SEP and WP (IEP's c. 310–c. 220 differs).
- **Ancient China** body: Analects "traditionally said to have been compiled by his disciples, though most scholars think it was put together by later followers over two centuries or more" — consistent with WP; SEP adds the Hunter/Zhu Weizheng view of a 2nd-century-BCE fixing. **But the image caption in the same chapter says "His teachings, collected by his disciples in the Analects"**, stating the traditional view as fact and contradicting its own body text and the scholarship. Record as a contradiction (not to be repeated here).
- **Ancient China**: "I transmit but do not create" (7.1), "in one common rendering" — fine; Slingerland: "I transmit rather than innovate".
- **Ancient China**: Mencius said an oppressive ruler "loses the Mandate of Heaven and may rightly be overthrown" — broadly consistent with 1B8 and 5B9, though IEP says he is "no democrat" and Tiwald questions a "right of rebellion".
