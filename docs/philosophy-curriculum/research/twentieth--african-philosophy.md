## 1. Framing: the "great debate"
- The question that started the debates: whether there was philosophy proper in traditional (pre-modern) sub-Saharan Africa. "The publication in 1945 of Placide Tempels' *La Philosophie Bantoue* (English translation *Bantu Philosophy*) triggered much of the debate." [source: SEP-AF]
- Context: colonial domination justified by racial ranking, aided by canonical philosophers: Hume's footnote in "Of National Characters" (1748) on the "natural inferiority" of Black people, and Kant (1764; anthropology 1798). For most Europeans the idea of African philosophy was "utterly preposterous". [source: SEP-AF]
- The debates (mid-1940s through the 1980s) over whether Africans have or can do philosophy "have been resolved—or are no longer taken seriously—and given way to explorations of other concerns". [source: SEP-AF]
- Karp and Masolo's three stages of post-independence African philosophy: the 1970s were about the ethnophilosophy debates, the 1980s about criticising African cultural production, and the 1990s about the crisis of the postcolonial state (Karp & Masolo 2000). [source: SEP-AC §7.3]
- Wiredu: African philosophy got traction in African universities only in the mid-to-late 1960s, with "significant numbers of post-independence African academics" (Wiredu 2004, 2). [source: SEP-AC]
- IEP-HAP periodisation (one author's scheme): early period 1920s–1960s; **middle period 1960s–1980s "characterized by the Great Debate"** between those defending the early (traditionalist) positions and those criticising them; later 1980s–1990s; new era since the 1990s. Names in the Great Debate include C. S. Momoh, Robin Horton, Peter Bodunrin, Kwasi Wiredu, Kwame Gyekye, Richard Wright, Barry Hallen, Paulin Hountondji, Odera Oruka and Sophie Oluwole. [source: IEP-HAP §1]
- The two camps are usually called "Traditionalists" and "Universalists" (Bodunrin 1985: Traditionalist vs Modernist; Wiredu used a similar division). [source: IEP-HAP §2, §4]
- Institutions: degree programmes with an African-philosophy emphasis in Kenya, Nigeria, Zaire/DRC, Ivory Coast, Benin, Senegal and South Africa; national associations (Ghana, Kenya, Nigeria, Egypt); international bodies including the Inter-African Council of Philosophy and the Afro-Asian Philosophy Association (headquarters Cairo). [source: SEP-AF] Oruka founded the Philosophical Association of Kenya and was vice-president of the Inter-African Council of Philosophy; Wiredu was its vice-president too. [source: WP-Henry_Odera_Oruka; WP-Kwasi_Wiredu]
- Oleko and Smet (1978) proposed four trends: ideological (African personality, Pan-Africanism, Négritude, African socialism, Consciencism, "authenticity"), traditionalist, critical, and synthetic (hermeneutic). [source: SEP-AF]

## 2. Precursors (cross-reference; keep brief)
- **Anton Wilhelm Amo**: SEP-AF gives "(c. 1703–1758)", brought from present-day Ghana to the Netherlands "at age three". WP and Periergia's published chapters give c. 1703 – c. 1759. **Use "c. 1703 – after 1753" or "c. 1703 – c. 1759", noting that sources differ.** The published *Enlightenment and Rousseau* and History *Enlightenment* chapters already treat him (1729 disputation "On the Rights of Moors in Europe"; 1734 Wittenberg dissertation placing sensation in the body; return to the Gold Coast in 1747). [source: SEP-AF; WP-Anton_Wilhelm_Amo; Periergia SQL]
- IEP-HAP treats Amo, Zera Yacob and Augustine as part of a "pre-systematic epoch" and asks whether being African by birth makes one an African philosopher. [source: IEP-HAP §1]
- **Zera Yacob / the *Hatata***: SEP-AF names "Zera Yacob and Walda Heyat, two seventeenth century philosophers (Sumner 1976–78)" without discussing authorship. The authenticity debate (Conti Rossini 1920; Mittwoch 1934; Sumner 1976; Wion 2021; Getatchew Haile 2017; Lee, Worku and Belcher 2023) is laid out in `early-modern--early-modern-beyond-europe.md`. **Cross-refer and say the authorship is disputed.** [source: SEP-AF; earlier notes]
- **Edward Wilmot Blyden** (3 Aug 1832 – 7 Feb 1912): already in *Philosophy in an Age of Empire* ("African Personality"; *Christianity, Islam and the Negro Race*, 1887). [source: WP-Edward_Wilmot_Blyden; Periergia SQL]
- **John S. Mbiti** (30 Nov 1931 – 5 Oct 2019), Kenyan theologian; *African Religions and Philosophy* (1969). [source: WP-John_Mbiti] The saying "I am because we are, and since we are, therefore I am" is credited to Mbiti (IEP-UB cites Mbiti 1969: 215; page numbers differ between editions, so do not give one). [source: IEP-HAP §3a; IEP-UB]

## 3. Négritude
- Origin: the meeting in Paris in the late 1920s and early 1930s of three Black students: **Aimé Césaire** (1913–2008, Martinique), **Léon-Gontran Damas** (1912–1978, French Guiana) and **Léopold Sédar Senghor** (1906–2001, Senegal). Césaire met Senghor at the Lycée Louis-le-Grand. [source: SEP-N §1]
- Women: at the salon of the Martinican sisters **Jane, Paulette and Andrée Nardal** they met Harlem Renaissance writers (Langston Hughes, Claude McKay). Jane Nardal's article "Internationalisme noir" (1928) came more than ten years before Senghor's first theoretical article (1939). T. Denean Sharpley-Whiting (2000) criticises the "masculinist genealogy" that erased the Nardals; Paulette Nardal complained "bitterly" in 1960. [source: SEP-N §1]
- Conventional founding moment: the journal *L'Étudiant noir* (1934–35), where Césaire coined "négritude", reclaiming the slur *nègre*. [source: SEP-N §2] WP: first used in its present sense in the third issue (May–June 1935). [source: WP-Négritude] (Consistent with Periergia *The Interwar Period*: "Césaire first used the word in its present sense in 1935".)
- Césaire and Damas stressed poetic revolt; Senghor defined Négritude as "the sum total of the values of civilization of the Black World", i.e. as an ontology, aesthetics, epistemology and politics. [source: SEP-N §2]
- Césaire: "Négritude, in my eyes, is not a philosophy. Négritude is not a metaphysics … It is a way of living history within history" (Césaire 2004, 82, Diagne's translation). [source: SEP-N §5]
- Anthologies: Damas, *Poètes d'expression française* (1947); Senghor, *Anthologie de la nouvelle poésie nègre et malgache de langue française* (1948), with Sartre's preface "Black Orpheus" (*Orphée noir*), which called Négritude an "anti-racist racism" and a stage to be "shed … to the profit of the Revolution"; Diagne calls it "the kiss of death". Senghor and Césaire "forcefully dismissed" the "anti-racist racism" label. [source: SEP-N §3–4, §9] (Consistent with Periergia *Existentialism*.)
- Senghor's ontology: "The whole system … is founded on the notion of vital force" (lecture "On Négritude", Lovanium, Kinshasa; Senghor 1993, 19). Senghor embraced Tempels' book, which matched his 1939 essay. [source: SEP-N §5]
- Senghor was president of Senegal from 6 Sep 1960 to 31 Dec 1980, and the first African member of the Académie française (installed 29 March 1984). [source: WP-Léopold_Sédar_Senghor]
- Senghor's "African socialism" came from a "Negro African re-reading of Marx" that favoured the early (1844) Marx. [source: SEP-N §8]
- Césaire's *Letter to Maurice Thorez* (1956), resigning from the French Communist Party: "There are two ways to lose oneself: walled segregation in the particular or dilution in the 'universal'" (Césaire 2010, 152). [source: SEP-N §8]
- *Discourse on Colonialism*: first published in Paris in 1950; edited and republished in *Présence africaine* in 1955; English translation by Joan Pinkham (Monthly Review, 1972; 2000 edition introduced by Robin D. G. Kelley). Its fourth section cites the missionary Placide Tempels among colonialism's "watchdogs". [source: WP-Discourse_on_Colonialism] SEP-N cites the Tempels passage as "his 1955 *Discours*". [source: SEP-N §5]
- **Critiques of Négritude:** essentialism (a shared, enduring African-ness); Marxists said it distracted from class and was "an ideology for neocolonialism" (aimed especially at Senghor); the Créolité writers (Bernabé, Chamoiseau, Confiant 1990: "Neither Europeans, nor Africans, nor Asians, we proclaim ourselves Creoles") and Glissant's creolisation; and postcolonial anti-essentialism. Diagne warns against dismissing it too quickly, citing Senghor's *métissage* ("everyone must be mixed in their own way"). [source: SEP-N §4, §9]
- Wole Soyinka's jibe at the African Writers Conference at Makerere, Kampala, June 1962: "A tiger does not proclaim his tigritude; he pounces" (wording varies; a 1960 essay has "the duiker will not paint 'duiker' on his beautiful back"). Soyinka later said the distinction was "purely a literary one". [source: WP-Négritude; WS] See also Quotations / NOT VERIFIED.
- Wiredu on Senghor: "Senghor has celebrated the fact our (traditional) mind is of a non-analytical bent; which is very unfortunate, seeing that this mental attribute is more of a limitation than anything else" (Wiredu 1980: 12). [source: IEP-W §4]

## 4. Placide Tempels and *Bantu Philosophy*
- Placide (born Frans) Tempels, OFM (18 Feb 1906 Berlaar, Belgium – 9 Oct 1977 Hasselt). He was a Franciscan from 1924, ordained in 1930, posted to the Belgian Congo in 1933, and stayed 29 years, returning in April 1962. [source: WP-Placide_Tempels] SEP-N: "a Belgian Franciscan priest who went to the Congo as a missionary". [source: SEP-N §5]
- **Publication history:** written in (Flemish) Dutch, with chapters first published in the journals *Aequatoria* and *Band*. The French translation by **Antoine Rubbens** was published by **Lovania (Elisabethville) in 1945**. A Dutch book, *Bantoe-filosofie*, appeared in 1946 (De Sikkel, Antwerp). Tempels revised the French for the **Présence Africaine edition of 1949**. There was a German translation in 1956 and the **English translation by Colin King (Présence Africaine, 1959)**. [source: WP-Bantu_Philosophy; WP-Marcien_Towa note] SEP-AF bibliography: Tempels 1945, A. Rubbens (trans.), Éditions africaines, "Originally in Dutch", English tr. Colin King, Présence Africaine 1959. [source: SEP-AF] **Contradiction:** SEP-N says it was "published in French in 1945 by Présence africaine"; WP says the 1945 edition was Lovania's and the Présence Africaine edition was 1949. Say "published in French in 1945".
- Aim: as a missionary, Tempels thought he must understand the principles underlying Bantu beliefs, law and customs in order to preach more effectively. He claimed one could go beyond ethnographic description to "a Bantu philosophy of being". [source: SEP-N §5]
- Thesis: for the Bantu, force does not belong to being; force *is* being. "The notion 'force' takes for them the place of the notion 'being' in our philosophy" (Tempels, *Bantu Philosophy*, p. 52, Colin King translation, 1959; quoted by WP). There is a hierarchy of vital forces that can be strengthened or weakened. [source: WP-Bantu_Philosophy; SEP-N §5 (Apostel's propositions: "To be is to be a force"; forces can be "rein-forced or de-forced")]
- Tempels claimed the Bantu "did not have conscious knowledge of their philosophy"; he extracted it using his philosophical training. [source: SEP-AF]
- Effect: "one of the very first times that an African people was associated with philosophy". [source: SEP-N §5] It "was a serious challenge to the racialized philosophical ontology-cum-anthropology that undergirded colonial domination", but Tempels "tempered" that challenge with his claim that the Bantu lacked conscious knowledge of their philosophy. [source: SEP-AF]
- Colonial aims: the book's last (seventh) chapter is titled "Bantu philosophy and our mission to civilize" (Hountondji's rendering). The book was addressed to European "missionaries, magistrates, administrators", not to Africans. [WS — via Hountondji's essay and a Leiden study] In it Tempels concedes that Europeans had seen themselves "like adults before the newly born" who started "with a 'tabula rasa'". [WS — wording unverified]
- **Césaire's critique** (*Discourse on Colonialism*): his summary of Tempels is accurate ("Bantu ontology is based on the truly fundamental notions of a life force and a hierarchy of life forces"), but he mocks the political use: "Since Bantu thought is ontological, the Bantu only ask for satisfaction of an ontological nature. Decent wages! Comfortable housing! Food! These Bantu are pure spirits, I tell you …". The result: "the Bantu god will take responsibility for the Belgian colonialist order, and any Bantu who dare to raise his hand against it will be guilty of sacrilege" (Césaire 2000b, 58, Pinkham translation, as quoted in SEP-N). [source: SEP-N §5]
- Other critics: Franz Crahay's "Le décollage conceptuel: conditions d'une philosophie bantoue" (1965) said Bantu thought had only an "impetus for" philosophy and had not yet "taken off". Hountondji was "the first philosopher to distance himself from Crahay's position". [source: SEP-AF; SEP-SAGE §4] Innocent Asouzu speaks of the "Tempelsian Damage". [source: IEP-HAP]
- **Alexis Kagame** (15 May 1912 – 2 Dec 1981), Rwandan Catholic priest, philosopher and historian; doctorate at the Gregorian University in Rome. *La Philosophie bantu-rwandaise de l'être* (1956), an ontology read off the categories of Kinyarwanda grammar, and *La Philosophie bantu comparée* (1976). Critics charge that he imposed Aristotelian categories (Masolo 1994). [source: WP-Alexis_Kagame; WP-Marcien_Towa note: Brussels 1956] IEP-HAP renders the 1956 title in English as "The Bantu-Rwandan Philosophy (1956)". No published English translation was found, so give the French title.
- Hountondji's targets as ethnophilosophers: Tempels and his "disciples", among them Marcel Griaule, Alexis Kagame and John Mbiti. By the end of the 1960s Tempels, Mbiti (1969) and Griaule's *Conversations with Ogotemmêli* (English 1965) were well received in Anglophone Africa. [source: SEP-SAGE §4]

## 5. The word "ethnophilosophy"
- SEP-SAGE: "Although it had been used previously by other writers, the term 'ethnophilosophy' acquired its notoriety in the work of the Beninois philosopher Paulin Hountondji (1970, 1983). Perhaps unaware that the term had been used earlier …" [source: SEP-SAGE §4]
- Wiredu: the term was "most commonly deployed, most prominently by Paulin Hountondji, 'as a kind of negative characterization of … the traditionalist approach'" (Wiredu 2004, 3). [source: SEP-AC §7.2]
- Hountondji's first use: "Remarques sur la philosophie africaine contemporaine", *Diogène* 71 (1970) (English "Comments on contemporary African philosophy", *Diogenes*). [source: SEP-SAGE bibliography; WS]
- **Who coined it is unsettled.** Several scholars say **Kwame Nkrumah** used "ethno-philosophy" earlier, as a student in the US, in a thesis or doctoral project. One French source calls him "probably the real inventor of the term". The often-repeated date "1943" and the exact thesis title were **not** confirmed. [WS] **Marcien Towa** (1931–2014, Cameroon) criticised ethnophilosophical reconstructions in *Essai sur la problématique philosophique dans l'Afrique actuelle* (1971), after Hountondji's 1970 article. No source found says Towa coined the term. [source: WP-Marcien_Towa]
- Writer's formula: "Hountondji made the word famous in 1970; others, possibly including the young Nkrumah, had used it before."

## 6. Paulin Hountondji
- Paulin Jidénu Hountondji (11 Apr 1942 Abidjan – 2 Feb 2024 Cotonou), Beninese. Studied at the École Normale Supérieure, Paris (graduated 1966; doctorate 1970 on **Husserl**), where his teachers included Louis Althusser and Jacques Derrida. He taught at the Université Nationale du Bénin, was briefly Minister of Education and then of Culture and Communications in the early 1990s (resigned 1994), and won the Prince Claus Award in 1999. [source: WP-Paulin_J._Hountondji]
- *Sur la « philosophie africaine » : critique de l'ethnophilosophie* (Paris: Maspéro, 1976). English: *African Philosophy: Myth and Reality*, tr. **Henri Evans** (SEP-SAGE, SEP-AF; WP adds Jonathan Rée as co-translator), Indiana University Press, **1983**; second edition 1996, with a new preface (and an introduction by Abiola Irele, per WP). [source: SEP-AF; SEP-SAGE; WP-Paulin_J._Hountondji]
- The book's chapter "Philosophy and its revolutions" began as talks at the National University of Zaire (June 1973) and at the University of Nairobi (November 1973), on Oruka's invitation. It praises Paul Radin's verbatim recording of individual Winnebago informants and Griaule's single-sage method over Tempels, whose study was "behind the anthropology of the time"; Radin's work was "the most lucid ethnological critique of the theoretical assumptions of ethnophilosophy" (*African* 76, 79). [source: IEP-SAGE §2]
- Ethnophilosophy (Hallen's summary of the target): "presents itself as a philosophy of peoples rather than of individuals; in African societies one is therefore given the impression that there can be no equivalent to a Socrates or to a Zeno … Ethnophilosophy's sources are in the past … parables, proverbs, poetry, songs, myths … placing minimal emphasis upon the rigorous argumentation and criticism" (Hallen 2004, 122). [source: SEP-AC §7.2]
- Hountondji called ethnophilosophy "a hybrid discipline without a recognizable status in the world of theory" (WP cites "[1997], p. 52"; edition unclear). [source: WP-Paulin_J._Hountondji] (WP-only.)
- **Unanimism**: ethnophilosophy portrays traditional Africa as a place of philosophical unanimity, and that picture was used to legitimise one-party states. [source: SEP-SAGE §4] "Unanimism" and "extraversion" are among Hountondji's key critical concepts. [WS] "Extraversion": the ethnophilosophers were foreigners, or wrote for a foreign audience and answered debates set abroad. [source: IEP-SAGE §7]
- Hountondji's definition (1976; 1996: 33): African philosophy is a set of texts written by Africans themselves and described as philosophical by their authors themselves. [WS — the gist is consistent across several results, but the exact Evans wording was not seen] Later he said he would put "produced, either in writing or orally" instead of "written". [WS]
- IEP-HAP classes this as a "racial criterion" ("philosophy done by Africans"), held by Hountondji, Oruka (in part) and the early Bodunrin, and "criticized as inadequate, incorrect and exclusivist". [source: IEP-HAP §2]
- **Revision**: in *The Struggle for Meaning* (2002) Hountondji said his "earlier rejection of collective thought was excessive": collective culture must be taken seriously, but roots should not become a "prison house" (*The Struggle* 128, 151–52, 204–05). [source: IEP-SAGE §7] He later worked on "endogenous knowledge" (*Les savoirs endogènes*, 1994). [source: WP-Paulin_J._Hountondji; IEP-W]
- Critics on the Left: Olabiyi Yai (1977) charged that his work lacked ideological content. [source: IEP-W §2] Hountondji in turn (1981) reduced Towa's philosophy to ideology and said Nkrumah "professes an instrumental conception of philosophy". [source: WP-Marcien_Towa]

### ARGUMENT 1 (step by step): Hountondji against ethnophilosophy
Assembled from SEP-AF, SEP-AC (Hallen), SEP-SAGE §4, IEP-SAGE §7 and WP. The chapter should say this is a reconstruction.
1. Philosophy is a body of explicit discourse (texts) produced by individual thinkers who argue, criticise and can be criticised; a "Socrates or Zeno" is its model. [SEP-AC/Hallen; WS on "texts"]
2. Ethnophilosophy (Tempels, Kagame, Griaule, Mbiti) attributes to a whole people an unwritten, unconscious, shared world-view, which the outside scholar reconstructs. Tempels admits the Bantu do not know their own philosophy. [SEP-AF; SEP-SAGE]
3. A collective, implicit and supposedly unanimous world-view is not open to argument and criticism; describing it is ethnology, not philosophy (a "hybrid discipline"). [SEP-AF: "really more ethnological studies … than philosophical articulations"; WP]
4. The picture of a unanimous Africa is false (Africans disagree like anyone else) and politically dangerous, because it lends support to one-party rule and to treating dissent as un-African. [SEP-SAGE §4]
5. Ethnophilosophy is "extraverted": written by or for Europeans and answering European questions. Tempels' final chapter ties it to "our mission to civilize". [IEP-SAGE §7; WS]
6. So "African philosophy" should mean the philosophical texts that Africans actually produce: a literature to be built in the future, not a hidden system to be dug up.
- **Objection A (elitism / Eurocentrism):** this definition makes philosophy a written, academic, Western-style activity and so repeats the colonial denial of African thought. Oruka "was especially wary" of professional philosophers whose view of traditional thought was "analogous to European bias in denying reason to Africans" (Bodunrin was his main example). His sage interviews aimed to show individual critical thinkers in oral cultures. [source: SEP-SAGE §3–4] IEP-HAP calls the "racial criterion" "inadequate … exclusivist". [IEP-HAP §2]
- **Objection B (proverbs and folk thought are philosophical sources):** Gyekye (*An Essay*), Claude Sumner and Workineh Kelbessa argued that proverbs carry argument and reflection. [source: IEP-SAGE §2] Paget Henry and Appiah call for a "critical ethnophilosophy" that takes cultural concepts seriously without freezing them "in the precolonial amber". [source: SEP-AC §7.2]
- **Objection C (unanimity is not passivity):** Wiredu shows that Akan consensus came from "frequently protracted disputations among elders"; "consensus was not imposed, but relentlessly pursued". [source: SEP-SAGE §4, citing Wiredu 1996: 182ff] (This supports Hountondji's anti-unanimism, but also shows that communal thought need not be uncritical.)
- **Replies:** Hountondji did not deny that Africans reason. He was the first to reject Crahay's "conceptual take-off" thesis, arguing that local knowledge was already separate from myth. [SEP-SAGE §4] He later conceded that his rejection of collective thought had been "excessive" and widened "texts" to oral production (2002; later remarks). [IEP-SAGE §7; WS] Even critics such as IEP-HAP's author grant that what is called ethnophilosophy "can comply with the analytic and argumentative standards that people like Bodunrin, Hountondji, and Wiredu insist upon". [source: IEP-HAP §4]

## 7. Kwasi Wiredu
- Kwasi Wiredu (3 Oct 1931, Kumasi – 6 Jan 2022, United States), Ghanaian. University of Ghana (graduated 1958), then University College, Oxford (B.Phil.; taught by Gilbert Ryle, his supervisor, Peter Strawson and Stuart Hampshire). Taught at the University of Ghana, then the University of South Florida (Distinguished University Professor). [source: WP-Kwasi_Wiredu; IEP-W gives 1931–2022] Often called the greatest African philosopher of his generation. [source: WP]
- Books: *Philosophy and an African Culture* (Cambridge UP, 1980; Ghana National Book Award 1982); *Cultural Universals and Particulars: An African Perspective* (Indiana UP, 1996); ed. with Gyekye, *Person and Community: Ghanaian Philosophical Studies* (1992); ed., *A Companion to African Philosophy* (Blackwell, 2004; WP says 2003). [source: WP-Kwasi_Wiredu; SEP-AC; SEP-SAGE] Also *Conceptual Decolonisation in African Philosophy* (1995). [source: IEP-W]
- The term "conceptual decolonisation" was first used in a 1980 lecture, "Teaching and Research in African Philosophy: Some Suggestions" (per Futter 2023). [source: WP-Kwasi_Wiredu]
- Definition (Wiredu, quoted in Oladipo 2002: 328): "the purging of African philosophical thinking of all uncritical assimilation of Western ways of thinking. That, of course, would be only part of the battle won. The other desiderata are the careful study of our own traditional philosophies and the synthesising of any insights obtained from that source with any other insights that might be gained from the intellectual resources of the modern world." [source: IEP-W §2]
- *Cultural Universals and Particulars*, p. 136: conceptual decolonisation means "avoiding or reversing through a critical conceptual self-awareness the unexamined assimilation in our thought … of the conceptual frameworks embedded in the foreign philosophical traditions that have had impact on African life and thought…[and] exploiting as much as is judicious the resources of our own indigenous conceptual schemes in our philosophical meditations on even the most technical problems of contemporary philosophy." And p. 138: "There is no pretense…that recourse to the African vernacular must result in instantaneous philosophic revelation." [source: SEP-SAGE, Conclusion]
- IEP-W gives two aims: (1) to undermine unhelpful aspects of traditional ("tribal") culture embedded in modern African thought; (2) to remove unnecessary Western epistemologies from African philosophical practice. [source: IEP-W intro, §2]
- Examples: "there is no one word in Akan for truth"; no Akan word for "fact"; "a theory of truth is not of any real universal significance unless it offers some account of the notion of being so" (Wiredu 1985: 46, "The Concept of Truth in the Akan Language"). [source: IEP-W §3] "The Akan Concept of Mind" (1983): "the Akans most certainly do not regard mind as one of the entities that go to constitute a person"; the Akan separation of *okra* from *adwene* (mind/thought). [source: IEP-W §3]
- Wiredu (1987) argued that the Akan conceptual scheme would not, in the normal course of things, generate the Cartesian mind–body problem. [source: SEP-AF] Wiredu used the Cartesian cogito as an example of a claim that seems self-evident only in European languages. [WS — the locative *wɔ hɔ* ("to be there") version of this argument was NOT verified]
- Wiredu opposed both ethnophilosophy and philosophic sagacity as methods: all cultures have folk-beliefs, which must be distinguished from philosophising, though folk thought can feed genuine philosophy. [source: WP-Kwasi_Wiredu] He was critical of tradition: "traditional medicine is terribly weak in diagnosis and weaker still in pharmacology" (Wiredu 1980: 12). [source: IEP-W §4]
- He called Nkrumah, Senghor, Nyerere and Sékou Touré "the philosopher-kings of early post-independence Africa" (Wiredu 1995: 14). [source: IEP-W §3] He "denounce[d]" socialism as an unfit ideology. [source: IEP-W §2]
- **Consensual democracy** ("Democracy and Consensus in African Traditional Politics: A Plea for a Non-Party Polity"): the Ashanti system "was a consensual democracy. It was a democracy because government was by the consent, and subject to the control, of the people as expressed through the representatives. It was consensual because, at least, as a rule, that consent was negotiated on the principle of consensus. (By contrast, the majoritarian system might be said to be, in principle, based on consent without consensus.)" [source: IEP-W §4]
- Language: "Until Africa can have a lingua franca, we will have to communicate suitable parts of our work in our multifarious vernaculars … while using the metropolitan languages for international communication" (Wiredu 1995: 20). [source: IEP-W §3]

### ARGUMENT 2 (step by step): Wiredu's conceptual decolonisation
1. African philosophers were trained in colonial languages and Western traditions, so they absorbed Western concepts (mind, truth, fact, being) without examining them. [SEP-SAGE; IEP-W]
2. Some philosophical problems arise from features of particular languages, not from the nature of things. A test: try to state the problem in an African language such as Akan. [IEP-W §3; SEP-AF on the mind–body problem]
3. In Akan there is no single word for "truth" and none for "fact"; truth is expressed as "being so". Mind is not an entity that makes up a person. So debates that assume these concepts are not obviously universal. [IEP-W §3]
4. Conclusion (negative task): African philosophers should reverse the "unexamined assimilation" of foreign frameworks. (Positive task): they should use "as much as is judicious" the resources of indigenous schemes, even on technical problems, while subjecting those schemes to criticism too ("no pretense … of instantaneous philosophic revelation"). [SEP-SAGE quoting CUP 136, 138]
- **Objection (Osha, IEP-W):** comparing component parts of English and Akan is "irrevocably restrictive" and does not show "real universal significance". The project of recovering a precolonial heritage struggles with "the hybridity of the postcolonial condition". Wiredu's attack on tradition sits uneasily with a project that "requires the recuperation of vital elements in traditional culture". A further charge is that his analytic training made him neglect postcolonial theory and African feminisms. [source: IEP-W §2–4] One critic (WS) argues that his claim of the superiority of the Akan conception of mind fails.
- **Reply:** the method is critical, not nostalgic. Indigenous concepts are tested like any others, and the aim is a "reflective integration of the traditional and the modern". The point is not that Akan is right and English wrong, but that a concept which feels inevitable in one language may be optional. [source: IEP-W §2; SEP-SAGE]

## 8. H. Odera Oruka and sage philosophy
- Henry Odera Oruka (1 June 1944, Siaya County – 9 Dec 1995, Nairobi), Kenyan. Studied at Uppsala (with Ingemar Hedenius, per IEP-SAGE) and Wayne State University (PhD). Returned to Kenya in 1970 as one of the first two African philosophy faculty at the University of Nairobi. He fought to separate Philosophy from Religious Studies there (achieved July 1980; he became founding chair). He was killed by a motorist as a pedestrian in Nairobi in December 1995. [source: WP-Henry_Odera_Oruka; IEP-SAGE §1, §8] SEP-SAGE: "(1944–1995)".
- His early essays (1972, 1975) denounced much "African philosophy" as dressed-up myth; he later called them "simplistic and unnecessarily offensive". [source: IEP-SAGE §1]
- The Sage Philosophy Project began in the mid-1970s at the University of Nairobi. Researchers took tape recorders to villages and interviewed recognised wise persons in their own languages, challenging each "wise statement". [source: IEP-SAGE intro; WP-Henry_Odera_Oruka]
- *Sage Philosophy: Indigenous Thinkers and Modern Debate on African Philosophy* (Leiden: E. J. Brill, 1990). [source: SEP-AF; SEP-SAGE]
- **Four trends**: the paper "Four Trends in Current African Philosophy", given at a symposium on 30 August 1978 and published in A. Diemer (ed.), *Philosophy in the Present Situation of Africa* (Wiesbaden: Steiner, 1981). [source: SEP-AF bibliography; SEP-AC] One WS source dates a presentation to the William Amo Symposium, Accra, 24–29 July 1978 [WS]. The trends: (i) ethnophilosophy, (ii) philosophic sagacity, (iii) nationalist-ideological philosophy, (iv) professional philosophy. [source: SEP-AF; SEP-SAGE §3] Later lists add hermeneutic and literary/artistic philosophy as a fifth and sixth; the date of the expansion was **not** verified (one WS source says 1998). [WS]
- Examples by trend (WP): ethnophilosophy: Tempels, Senghor, Mbiti, Kagame; nationalist-ideological: Nkrumah, Sékou Touré, Nyerere, Kaunda; professional: Wiredu, Hountondji, Bodunrin, and Oruka himself. [source: WP-Henry_Odera_Oruka]
- The project aimed to refute three claims: that unlike Greek sages, African sages do not think philosophically; that philosophy requires literacy; and that African traditions enforce unanimity and discourage individual critical thought. [source: SEP-SAGE §1]
- **Folk sage vs philosophic sage:** a philosophic sage is a person "of traditional African culture, capable of the critical, second-order type of thinking about the various problems of human life and nature; persons, that is, who subject beliefs that are traditionally taken for granted to independent rational reexamination and who are inclined to accept or reject such beliefs on the authority of reason rather than on the basis of a communal or religious consensus" (*Sage Philosophy*, pp. 5–6). Griaule's Ogotemmêli and Hallen and Sodipo's Yoruba *onisegun* count as folk sages. [source: SEP-SAGE §5]
- Sages: Paul Mbuya Akoko (Luo) on communalism as "of a co-operative nature" (*Sage Philosophy*, p. 141); Okemba Simiyu Chaungo (Bukusu) on truth (p. 111), read by Masolo as a correspondence theory; Oruka Rang'inya (Oruka's own father); Chaungo Barasa, a young water engineer. Oruka also wrote on Oginga Odinga (1992) as a sage. [source: SEP-SAGE §1, §5; IEP-SAGE §8–9]
- The trained philosopher "plays the role of philosophical provocation" (*Sage Philosophy*, p. 47), on the model of Socrates as midwife. [source: SEP-SAGE §2]
- Oruka on unanimity: "while rulers everywhere will always crave unanimity, thinkers thrive in dialogue and diversity"; one-party regimes outlawed opposition as un-African by appeal to unanimity. [source: SEP-SAGE §4]
- **Critiques:** Bodunrin said sages are unlike literate Greek philosophers, and that the result is a joint product of interviewer and sage ("The Question of African Philosophy", *Philosophy* 56, 1981). Lansana Keita said folk philosophy may itself result from earlier individual creativity. Kaphagawani (1987) called sagacity parasitic on ethnophilosophy. Masolo (*African Philosophy in Search of Identity*, 1994) said many sayings are common sense, not sustained argument. Bruce Janz said the questions were leading and imported Western paradigms. W. J. Ndaba said the stress on individual critique is itself a Western notion. [source: IEP-SAGE §6; WP-Henry_Odera_Oruka]
- Self-contradiction: as an expert witness in the 1986 S. M. Otieno burial case, Oruka said "in a traditional communal society there were very few rebels" (*Sage Philosophy* 1990, 70), despite championing dissent in his academic work. [source: IEP-SAGE §7]

## 9. Nationalist-ideological philosophy: Nkrumah, Nyerere, Cabral
- **Kwame Nkrumah** (21 Sep 1909 – 27 Apr 1972). Lincoln University (BA 1939, then assistant lecturer in philosophy); University of Pennsylvania (MA, MS; doctoral thesis uncompleted); briefly enrolled at UCL to write on "Knowledge and Logical Positivism". He co-organised the 1945 Manchester Pan-African Congress. In 1964 a constitutional amendment made the CPP the only legal party, with Nkrumah president for life (99.91% vote). [source: WP-Kwame_Nkrumah] Overthrown 24 Feb 1966 (consistent with Periergia *Modern African History since 1860*).
- ***Consciencism***: first published London: Heinemann, **1964**, as *Consciencism: Philosophy and Ideology for Decolonization and Development with Particular Reference to the African Revolution*; revised edition 1970 (Monthly Review Press; Panaf in London), titled *… for De-colonization*. [WS; WP-Kwame_Nkrumah lists 1970] IEP-HAP wrongly gives 1965. **Use 1964.**
- Nkrumah on communalism and socialism: "From the ancestral lines of communalism, the passage to socialism lies in reform, because the underlying principles are the same … in communalistic society, socialism is not a revolutionary creed, but a restatement in contemporary idiom of the principles underlying communalism" (from *Consciencism*, quoted via Biney 2011). He moved towards revolution after the 1966 coup. Nkrumah "rejected the idealized view of pre-colonial African societies that were classless" but accepted a communal and humanist spirit. [source: WP-Nkrumaism]
- Nkrumaism's critics: Henry Bretton (1964) called it "a verbalization of the requirements of the personal political machine". [source: WP-Nkrumaism] *Neo-Colonialism, the Last Stage of Imperialism* (1965) is covered in History *Decolonization*.
- **Julius Nyerere** (13 Apr 1922 Butiama – 14 Oct 1999 London). Prime minister of Tanganyika 1961–62, president 1962–64, president of Tanzania 1964–1985. Known as "Mwalimu" (teacher; not checked). [source: WP-Julius_Nyerere]
- "Ujamaa – The Basis of African Socialism", a TANU pamphlet of **April 1962**, reprinted in *Ujamaa: Essays on Socialism* (OUP, Dar es Salaam, 1968). Opening: "Socialism – like democracy – is an attitude of mind." [WS; SEP-AE cites Nyerere 1968] Nyerere (1962): "We, in Africa, have no more need of being 'converted' to socialism than we have of being 'taught' democracy. Both are rooted in our past – in the traditional society which produced us." [source: WP-Julius_Nyerere, citing Molony] *Ujamaa* is Swahili for "familyhood" (IEP-HAP: "meaning familyhood"); WP glosses it as "fraternity". [source: IEP-HAP; WP-Ujamaa]
- **Arusha Declaration**, 5 Feb 1967: TANU's "Policy on Socialism and Self-Reliance". Its parts are the TANU Creed, the Policy of Socialism, the Policy of Self-Reliance, TANU Membership and the Arusha Resolution. Its creed begins "That all human beings are equal". Nationalisation of banks, natural resources and major industries followed. [source: WP-Arusha_Declaration; WP-Ujamaa]
- Outcomes: villagisation was at first voluntary; forced villagisation (Operation Vijiji) ran from 1973 to 1975/76. Per Nyerere as quoted by John Shao, over 7 million people were moved between Sept 1973 and June 1975, and 4 million more by the end of 1976. Adult literacy rose from 17% (1960) to 63% (1975); life expectancy from 37 (1960) to 52 (1984); infant mortality fell from 138 per 1,000 (1965) to 110 (1985). There was one-party rule and detention without trial; his successor Ali Hassan Mwinyi reversed many policies after 1985. [source: WP-Ujamaa; WP-Operation_Vijiji; WP-Julius_Nyerere] (Consistent with Periergia *Modern African History since 1860*.)
- **Amílcar Cabral** (12 Sep 1924 – 20 Jan 1973), Guinea-Bissau/Cape Verde; "The Weapon of Theory" speech at the 1966 Tricontinental Conference, Havana. [source: WP-Amílcar_Cabral] IEP-HAP lists him with Nyerere and Nkrumah in the nationalist-ideological school. Keep to one sentence.
- **Fanon**: covered in *Existentialism* (and History *Decolonization*). Wiredu contrasts his conceptual decolonisation with Fanon's "programme of complete disorder" (Fanon 1963: 20). Fanon was "the most astute theoretician of decolonization" among the philosopher-king generation (Osha). [source: IEP-W §3] Cross-refer; do not repeat.

## 10. Ubuntu
- The maxim: Nguni (Zulu/Xhosa/Ndebele) **"umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu"**, "a person is a person through other persons"; Shona *munhu munhu muvanhu*; Sotho/Tswana *motho ke motho ka batho (babang)*. [source: IEP-UB; WP-Ubuntu_philosophy] Desmond Tutu uses a Xhosa form, *ungamntu ngabanye abantu*. [source: IEP-UB]
- Word: root *-ntu* "person" with the class-14 abstract prefix *ubu-*, exactly parallel to "humanity"; *botho* (Sotho), *hunhu* (Shona). It appears in South African written sources from the mid-19th century meaning "human nature, humanness … kindness". [source: WP-Ubuntu_philosophy; IEP-UB]
- It was popularised as a "philosophy" from the 1950s (Jordan Kush Ngubane in *African Drum*) and as "African humanism" from the 1970s. The first book on it as a philosophy was Stanlake and Tommie Marie Samkange, *Hunhuism or Ubuntuism: A Zimbabwe Indigenous Political Philosophy* (1980). [source: WP-Ubuntu_philosophy; IEP-UB]
- The epilogue (postamble) of South Africa's **Interim Constitution of 1993**: "there is a need for understanding but not for vengeance, a need for reparation but not for retaliation, a need for *ubuntu* but not for victimisation". [source: WP-Ubuntu_philosophy]
- **TRC**: Tutu, *No Future without Forgiveness* (1999): "the single main ingredient that made the achievements of the TRC possible was a uniquely African ingredient – Ubuntu" (quoted via Richardson 2008: 67, so secondhand). Tutu (1999): "A person with Ubuntu is open and available to others … diminished when others are humiliated or diminished". Michael Onyebuchi Eze: "the TRC used Ubuntu as an ideology to achieve political ends" (2011: 12). [source: IEP-UB; WP-Ubuntu_philosophy] (Consistent with Periergia *History of Peace and Peacemaking*: Tutu "framed its work in terms of *ubuntu*"; *Apartheid and Its End*: 7,111 amnesty applications, 849 granted.)
- **Mogobe B. Ramose** (South African; PhD KU Leuven 1983, where he lived in exile; University of South Africa): *African Philosophy through Ubuntu* (1999). He defines ubuntu as "humaneness" and analyses *ubu-* (being) and *-ntu* (Ramose 1999: 50). [source: WP-Mogobe_Ramose; IEP-UB]
- **Thaddeus Metz** (b. 1968, American; Johannesburg 2015–20, then Pretoria): "Toward an African Moral Theory", *Journal of Political Philosophy* 15(3) (2007): 321–341. Its principle: an action is right insofar as it respects or promotes harmonious relationships in which people **identify** with one another (shared identity) and show **solidarity**/good-will; wrong insofar as it fails to. [WS; SEP-AC; IEP-UB (Metz 2007: 323)] The exact formula "promotes shared identity among people grounded on good-will" was NOT seen in the original, so paraphrase it.
- **The Metz–Ramose debate**: one side treats ubuntu as a resource for ethics and political philosophy, out of respect for African ideas; the other is suspicious of its sudden "mainstreaming" by a largely white professoriate in a majority-Black country, often by scholars who have not learned the languages (Ramose 2015). [source: SEP-AC §7.2]
- **Critiques**: Matolino and Kwindingwi, "The end of ubuntu", *South African Journal of Philosophy* 32(2) (2013), argued that ubuntu belongs to a bygone pre-modern way of life and that elites invoke it for self-serving ends. Metz replied with "Just the beginning for ubuntu" (SAJP 33(1), 2014), and Matolino answered again (SAJP 34(2), 2015). [WS] Onyebuchi Eze criticises the "romanticisation" of a "unanimous past" (2010: 93). Mboti (2015) calls the definition "consistently and purposely fuzzy". Some African scholars note elements that "marginalize and disempower" women. [source: IEP-UB; WP-Ubuntu_philosophy] Christian Gade (2011–12) traced the history of the term and found 1970s texts calling it "African humanism" without explaining it. [source: IEP-UB]

## 11. Personhood and community: Menkiti, Gyekye, Wiredu
- **Ifeanyi Menkiti** (24 Aug 1940 Onitsha – 17 June 2019). PhD Harvard 1974 (dissertation on collective responsibility); taught at Wellesley; also a poet who owned the Grolier Poetry Book Shop. [source: WP-Ifeanyi_Menkiti]
- "Person and Community in African Traditional Thought", in Richard A. Wright (ed.), *African Philosophy: An Introduction*, 3rd edn (Lanham, MD: University Press of America, 1984), pp. 171–182. [source: SEP-AE bibliography; WP-Ifeanyi_Menkiti] Later: "On the Normative Conception of a Person" in Wiredu (ed.), *Companion* (2004/5). [source: WP]
- Key quotation (Menkiti 1984: 176, as quoted by Gyekye in SEP-AE): "The various societies found in traditional Africa routinely accept this fact that personhood is the sort of thing which has to be attained, and is attained in direct proportion as one participates in communal life through the discharge of the various obligations defined by one's stations. It is the carrying out of these obligations that transforms one from the it-status of early child-hood, marked by an absence of moral function, into the person-status of later years, marked by a widened maturity of ethical sense—an ethical maturity without which personhood is conceived as eluding one." [source: SEP-AE §4]
- Menkiti also held that the community, not "some isolated static quality of rationality, will, or memory", defines the person; called the African conception "maximal" against the Western "minimal" one; and described a "processual" becoming through social and ritual incorporation (p. 172). [WS — secondary summaries; exact wording unverified] He framed it with Mbiti's "I am, because we are". [source: WP-Ifeanyi_Menkiti]
- Linguistic evidence: Akan *onipa* means both "human being" and "person"; of a cruel or selfish person one says *onnye onipa* ("he is not a person"); of a good one *oye onipa paa* ("he is truly a person"). Yoruba: *eniyan*, *Ki i se eniyan* (Gbadegesin 1991: 27). [source: SEP-AE §4; SEP-AP §1]
- Wiredu's version: Kaunda praised Margaret Thatcher as "truly a person", explaining that "personhood is not an automatic quality of the human individual; it is something to be achieved" (Wiredu 1992a, 104). There are two tiers: being human (possessing the *okra*) is necessary but not sufficient for personhood, and gives a baseline of respect; personhood comes in degrees through achievement. Evidence: the Akan hold no funeral rites for infants, who are "limbo people" eligible for selective reincarnation; ancestors are the culmination. [source: SEP-AP §1]
- **Kwame Gyekye** (1939 – April 2019), Ghanaian. Educated at Mfantsipim and the University of Ghana; PhD Harvard (on Greco-Arabic philosophy); emeritus at the University of Ghana; visiting at Temple. [source: WP-Kwame_Gyekye] Works: *An Essay on African Philosophical Thought: The Akan Conceptual Scheme* (1987) [source: SEP-AF bibliography]; "Person and Community in African Thought" in Wiredu & Gyekye (eds), *Person and Community* (1992); *Tradition and Modernity: Philosophical Reflections on the African Experience* (1997) [source: SEP-AC]; author of SEP "African Ethics" (2010).
- Gyekye's objection to personhood-as-achievement: "[W]hat a person acquires are status, habits, and personality or character traits: he, qua person acquires and thus becomes the subject of acquisition, and being thus prior to acquisition process, he cannot be defined by what he acquires. One is a person because of what he is, not because of what he acquires" (Wiredu & Gyekye 1992, 108). He appeals to Kant's kingdom of ends and the capacity for reason as the basis of equal moral worth, and reads *onnye onipa* as a judgment of character, not of personhood. [source: SEP-AP §1] Yet in SEP-AE he himself describes "moral personhood" as "earned in the ethical arena", while children are "persons only potentially". **The writer should note this tension, or present SEP-AE's "moral personhood" as a normative sense alongside his metaphysical one.** [source: SEP-AE §4]
- Gyekye on individual and community: "[I]ndividual persons … may find that aspects of those cultural givens are inelegant, undignifying or unenlightening and can thoughtfully be questioned and evaluated. The evaluation may result in individual's affirming or amending or refining existing communal goals, values and practices; but it may or could also result in the individual's total rejection of them" (Wiredu & Gyekye 1992, 112 n. 20); the self can "participate in the determination or definition of its own identity". [source: SEP-AP §3]
- **Moderate communitarianism**: Gyekye labels Menkiti's view (and that of Nkrumah, Senghor and Nyerere) "radical" communitarianism, which makes the community ontologically constitutive of the person and overrides rights and autonomy. His moderate view keeps the social nature of persons but grants them rights and the capacity for self-assertion and evaluation. He developed this mainly in *Tradition and Modernity* (1997). [source: WP-Kwame_Gyekye; WS] Akan proverb he cites: "All persons are children of God; no one is a child of the earth." [source: WP-Kwame_Gyekye] (WP section unreferenced; use with care.)

### ARGUMENT 3 (step by step): Menkiti's normative (processual) personhood, Gyekye's objection, and the replies
1. In African languages "person" has a normative use: one can be human yet "not a person" (Akan *onnye onipa*, Yoruba *Ki i se eniyan*). [SEP-AE §4]
2. Young children are not yet treated as persons ("it-status"; Akan funerals are not held for infants). [SEP-AE §4 Menkiti quote; SEP-AP §1]
3. What turns a human into a person is moral maturity, gained "in direct proportion as one participates in communal life through the discharge of the various obligations defined by one's stations" (Menkiti 1984: 176). [SEP-AE]
4. So personhood is attained over time, comes in degrees and can be lost or failed; it is defined by the community, not by an inner metaphysical property such as rationality or memory. The community is prior. [SEP-AE; WS]
- **Objection (Gyekye 1992/1997):** (a) acquisition presupposes an acquirer: there must already be a person who acquires the status, so personhood cannot be defined by what is acquired (Wiredu & Gyekye 1992, 108). (b) Moral equality and rights rest on a capacity for reason that all humans share (Kantian kingdom of ends). (c) Individuals can evaluate and even reject their community's values, so they are not wholly constituted by it. Radical communitarianism therefore threatens rights and autonomy, and a "moderate" version should replace it. [SEP-AP §1, §3; WS]
- **Reply 1 (Wiredu's two tiers):** the human/person distinction accommodates both sides. Possession of the *okra* gives every human, infants included, irreducible respect and rights, while personhood in the full normative sense is achieved. "All persons are human beings but not all human beings are persons." Moral reformers who reject communal values with reasons are themselves full persons. [SEP-AP §1, §3]
- **Reply 2 (Matolino 2009, "Radicals versus Moderates: A Critique of Gyekye's Moderate Communitarianism", *South African Journal of Philosophy* 28(2): 160–170):** Gyekye's moderate view does not differ in philosophical substance from the radical one, because he still grants the community priority over the individual. So objections to the radicals apply to him too. Matolino later proposed a "limited communitarianism" (2014). [WS]
- **Reply 3 (Ikuenobe 2018: "An Examination of Menkiti's Conception of Personhood and Gyekye's Critique", and "Radical versus moderate communitarianism: Gyekye's and Matolino's misinterpretations of Menkiti", *Filosofia Theoretica* 7(2): 79–100):** Gyekye misreads Menkiti. Menkiti's claim is normative, about moral personhood, not that the community metaphysically creates persons or overrides rights. [WS]
- **Later work:** Motsamai Molefe (South Africa; *An African Philosophy of Personhood, Morality, and Politics*; *African Personhood and Applied Ethics*, 2020) treats personhood as a moral quality earned through conduct. [source: WP-Motsamai_Molefe] Edwin Etieyibo and Polycarp Ikuenobe (eds), *Menkiti on Community and Becoming a Person* (2020). [WS]

## 12. Women philosophers, and other figures
- **Sophie Bosede Oluwole** (12 May 1935 Igbara-Oke – 23 Dec 2018). The first person to earn a doctorate in philosophy at a Nigerian university (University of Ibadan; year not verified). She taught at the University of Lagos. She called herself a "critical traditionalist", compared Socrates with Ọ̀rúnmìlà (the Ifá sage, treated as a historical thinker rather than a god), argued that literacy is not required for philosophy, and argued for Ifá as a rational corpus with a "binary complementary logic". [source: WP-Sophie_Oluwole] Book: *Socrates and Ọ̀rúnmìlà: Two Patron Saints of Classical Philosophy* (Lagos: Ark Publishers) — **first edition 2014 per most sources** (Guardian Nigeria gives 2015; a third edition 2017). [WS] IEP-HAP lists her among the participants in the Great Debate; IEP-SAGE lists her among those who treated Oruka's project substantively. [source: IEP-HAP; IEP-SAGE §6]
- **Oyèrónkẹ́ Oyěwùmí** (b. 10 Nov 1957, Ogbomoso), Nigerian sociologist at Stony Brook. *The Invention of Women: Making an African Sense of Western Gender Discourses* (University of Minnesota Press, 1997; ASA Distinguished Book Award in Sex and Gender, 1998). [source: WP-Oyèrónkẹ́_Oyěwùmí; SEP-AC] Its "central claim is that gender is not an indigenous organizing principle in Yorùbáland, that the 'body-reasoning' and 'bio-logic' of the West are present neither in the language nor in the epistemology of the Yorùbá" (Ficek 2006, 543). Yoruba social organisation is based on seniority. "One of the most famous and at the same time contested scholars in African feminist thought" (Coetzee and Halsema 2018). It met pushback in the special African feminisms issue of *Quest* (2006). [source: SEP-AC §7.1; WP]
- **Nkiru Nzegwu** (b. 22 Mar 1954, Onitsha), Nigerian (Igbo) philosopher and art historian, SUNY Binghamton; PhD Ottawa 1989 (WP: "citation needed"). *Family Matters: Feminist Concepts in African Philosophy of Culture* (2006): the "dual-sex system" of the Igbo, and colonial rule dismantling women's autonomy. [source: WP-Nkiru_Nzegwu] SEP-AC notes her work on art, community and beauty (Nzegwu 2004, in Wiredu's *Companion*). [source: SEP-AC]
- **Paulette and Jane Nardal**: see §3.
- **V. Y. Mudimbe** (8 Dec 1941 – 22 Apr 2025), Congolese; PhD Louvain 1970; Duke. *The Invention of Africa: Gnosis, Philosophy, and the Order of Knowledge* (Indiana UP, 1988). Its influence on African studies is compared to that of Said's *Orientalism*. It examines the Western discourses (anthropology, missionary writing) that "invented" Africa. [source: WP-V._Y._Mudimbe; SEP-AC] SEP-AC lists it among the germinal 1980s works.
- **Kwame Anthony Appiah** (b. 8 May 1954): *In My Father's House: Africa in the Philosophy of Culture* (Methuen/OUP, 1992; Herskovits Award 1993). [source: WP-Kwame_Anthony_Appiah] SEP-AC cites it as "1993" (likely a different printing). **Use 1992**, consistent with Periergia *Philosophy in an Age of Empire* (on Du Bois). Appiah joins Paget Henry in calling for a "critical ethnophilosophy". [source: SEP-AC §7.2]
- **Achille Mbembe** (b. 1957, Cameroon; Wits Institute for Social and Economic Research): *On the Postcolony* (French 2000; English 2001); *Necropolitics* (2019). He theorised "Afropolitanism" in the early 2000s: "the meaning of 'being African' had to be dislodged from race … and be opened to the flows of global networks and worldly hybridities" (Balakrishnan 2017, paraphrasing). [source: WP-Achille_Mbembe; SEP-AC §7.3]
- **Barry Hallen and J. Olubi Sodipo**, *Knowledge, Belief and Witchcraft* (1986): conversations with Yoruba *onisegun* (herbalists), using Quine's indeterminacy of translation against anthropologists' charges of contradiction in African thought. [source: SEP-SAGE §5; SEP-AF bibliography]
- **Shaaban bin Robert** (Swahili poet-philosopher, Tanganyika): an independent indigenous thinker on ideal personhood (*Utubora Mkulima*) and an equal society (*Kusadikika*). [source: SEP-SAGE §4] (SEP has a separate entry, `shaaban-robert-ethics`.)

## Debates
- **Is ethnophilosophy philosophy?** Tempels, Kagame, Mbiti and Senghor against Hountondji, Towa, Wiredu, Bodunrin and the early Oruka. Middle positions: Oruka's sage philosophy; the "critical ethnophilosophy" of Appiah and Henry; Hountondji's own 2002 softening. [SEP-AF; SEP-AC; SEP-SAGE; IEP-SAGE]
- **Who coined "ethnophilosophy"?** The earlier user may have been Nkrumah; Hountondji (1970) made it famous. [SEP-SAGE; WS]
- **Is Tempels' book a defence of Africans or a colonial instrument?** Some anti-colonial intellectuals took it as a "vindication"; Césaire saw it as reforming colonialism to perpetuate it. [SEP-AF; SEP-N]
- **Négritude: essentialism or a strategic, Bergsonian humanism?** Sartre, Marxists, Soyinka and Créolité against Diagne's rereading. [SEP-N]
- **Sage philosophy**: is it philosophy (Oruka) or common sense and joint product (Bodunrin, Masolo, Kaphagawani, Keita, Janz)? [IEP-SAGE]
- **What makes philosophy "African"?** A "racial" criterion (Hountondji; the early Bodunrin) against a "tradition" criterion; and the language question (Ngũgĩ, Tangwa). [IEP-HAP §2]
- **Personhood**: Menkiti's normative/processual view against Gyekye's moderate communitarianism; Wiredu's two-tier view; Matolino's collapse argument; Ikuenobe's charge of misreading. [SEP-AP; SEP-AE; WS]
- **Ubuntu**: a moral theory (Metz) or an appropriated concept (Ramose); "the end of ubuntu" (Matolino & Kwindingwi) against "just the beginning" (Metz); romanticisation (Eze); ideological use in the TRC (Eze). [SEP-AC; IEP-UB; WS]
- **African socialism**: Nkrumah and Nyerere claimed socialism continued traditional communalism. Critics: Bretton on Nkrumaism as a personal machine; the record of Tanzania's one-party state and forced villagisation; Wiredu rejected socialism; Hountondji and Oruka saw the unanimity myth as serving one-party rule. [WP-Nkrumaism; WP-Ujamaa; IEP-W; SEP-SAGE]
- **Gender**: Oyěwùmí's claim that gender was not a Yoruba organising principle, and the pushback (*Quest* 2006). [SEP-AC]
- **Periodisation and schools** are contested (Oruka's 4 or 6 trends; Oleko & Smet's 4; Momoh's 5; Bodunrin's 2; IEP-HAP's movements). [SEP-AF; IEP-HAP]
