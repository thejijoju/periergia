## 1. Falsafa: the word, and the debate about its origins
- *Falsafa* is the Arabic loan word for Greek *philosophia*. [source: SEP-Greek intro]
- Scholars disagree about the role of Greek sources: some regard the Qur'an and Islamic tradition as the main source of *falsafa*, which they derive from Qur'anic *ḥikma* ("wisdom"), with the Greek texts as instruments; "most scholars side with the opinion that what gave rise to the intellectual tradition of falsafa was the so-called movement of translation from Greek." [source: SEP-Greek intro] (The Periergia "What Is Philosophy?" chapter already names this debate: consistent.)
- By the time of Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī (12th c.) *falsafa* had a narrower meaning tied to Avicenna's distinctive teachings (e.g. necessary, eternal emanation of the universe from God); the more generic term was *ḥikma*, "wisdom" (Griffel 2021). [source: SEP-FDR §1]

## 2. The Graeco-Arabic translation movement
- **Syriac background.** Before Islam, Christian elites of Syria had already moved "from antagonism to assimilation" (Brock) of Greek philosophy, especially Aristotle. In the schools of Edessa and Nisibis (4th–5th c.) exegetical works, then Aristotle's logic and Porphyry's *Isagoge*, were translated into Syriac. Sergius of Reshʿaynā (d. 536), a former student of Ammonius in Alexandria, translated some thirty Galen treatises and other works into Syriac. [source: SEP-Greek §1]
- After the Arab conquest of Damascus (635) and Syria (636), Christian communities under the Umayyads (661–750) kept studying Aristotle's logic with the *Isagoge*; this tradition lay behind the Syriac Christians who took part in the early ʿAbbasid translations. Translations into Syriac continued alongside those into Arabic, mostly in Baghdad. [source: SEP-Greek §1]
- **Umayyad precursors:** Sālim Abū l-ʿAlāʾ, secretary to the caliph Hishām (r. 724–743), began translating the pseudo-Aristotelian letters to Alexander, the nucleus of the *Sirr al-asrār* (Latin *Secretum secretorum*). [source: SEP-Greek §2]
- **Early ʿAbbasid translations:** Under al-Manṣūr (r. 754–775), who founded Baghdad (762), his secretary Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ (d. 756) is credited with a compendium of logic (Porphyry's *Isagoge*, *Categories*, *De interpretatione*, *Prior Analytics*); some scholars (Gabrieli 1932, Kraus 1934) attribute it instead to his son. If the father made it, it was from Middle Persian, not Greek. Al-Mahdī (r. 775–785) had the *Topics* translated by the Nestorian Patriarch Timothy I (d. 823). Under Hārūn al-Rashīd (r. 786–809) a certain Sallām al-Abrash translated the *Physics* (lost). [source: SEP-Greek §2]
- **Context under al-Maʾmūn (r. 813–833):** his "new interpretation of Islam and of the role of the caliph" and the elites' interest in the secular sciences created the setting for the translations. [source: SEP-Greek §3]
- **Two main groups of translators in the 9th century:** the "Kindī circle" (al-Kindī as mediator between patrons and translators, many of whom were Syrian Christians or of Syrian extraction) and the group of Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq. [sources: SEP-Kindi §1.1; SEP-Greek §§3–4]
- **Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq**: Nestorian Christian physician and scientist, d. 873; his son Isḥāq ibn Ḥunayn d. 911. Besides Galen's medical corpus, the group made available in Syriac and/or Arabic works of Plato, Aristotle, Theophrastus, Galen, Alexander of Aphrodisias, Porphyry and perhaps Iamblichus, Themistius, Proclus and Philoponus. [source: SEP-Greek §4] Birth: WP-Hunayn gives both 808 (lead) and 809 (short description), born at al-Ḥīra, Iraq; known in Latin as Johannitius; called "Sheikh of the Translators". WRITER: "born c. 808/9 at al-Ḥīra". Many translations went Greek → Syriac → Arabic (e.g. SEP-Greek lists Ḥunayn's Syriac versions later put into Arabic by pupils).
- **10th century:** the Nestorian Christian Abū Bishr Mattā ibn Yūnus (d. 940; WS-7 gives d. 328 AH/939–40) and pupils produced another wave; he did not know Greek and worked from Syriac; in Baghdad his pupils included al-Fārābī (a Muslim) and Yaḥyā ibn ʿAdī (a Jacobite Christian). [source: SEP-Greek §5]
- **Duration:** mid-8th to late 10th century, in Baghdad. [source: WP-Graeco-Arabic_translation_movement] The movement involved Muslim, Christian, Sabian and Jewish scholars. [same]
- Gutas (1998) is the standard account; his title: *Greek Thought, Arabic Culture: The Graeco-Arabic Translation Movement in Baghdad and Early ʿAbbāsid Society (2nd–4th/8th–10th centuries)*, London: Routledge. [source: SEP-Sina and SEP-Greek bibliographies]

### The "House of Wisdom" (Bayt al-Ḥikma): the debate
- SEP-Greek: "Even though the court library (Bayt al-hikma, 'House of Wisdom') cannot be viewed as an institutional center for the translations of Greek works into Arabic, a close relationship existed between the activities of the first group of translators and the court." Note 23 lists scholars "with different emphasis on the scope and nature of the Bayt al-hikma": Balty-Guesdon (1992), Micheau (1997), Gutas (1998) 58–60, Di Branco (2012). [source: SEP-Greek §3 and notes]
- Gutas's reconstruction (WS-1, from summaries quoting the book): it was a library, most likely set up as a "bureau" under al-Manṣūr on a Sasanian administrative model; under al-Maʾmūn it may have gained a function connected with astronomy and mathematics; it was not an "academy" for teaching the translated sciences, and the Graeco-Arabic translation movement was unrelated to its activities; Ḥunayn's own first-hand report on the translations does not mention it. [WS-1] WRITER: attribute to Gutas, say "Gutas argues"; the SEP-backed line is "a court library, not the institutional centre of the Greek translations".
- WP-House_of_Wisdom itself notes that the image of a university-like academy "has been challenged by some historians, who argue this image was constructed over the course of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries by Orientalist scholars", and that the House of Wisdom was unlikely to be the sole centre of translation. The rest of that WP article repeats the older "academy" picture uncritically: do not use its details (e.g. Ḥunayn placed in charge of it; WP-Hunayn itself notes one scholar finds no evidence for this).
- Gutas's explanation of the movement's causes: the ʿAbbasid elite's adoption of Sasanian imperial ideology, itself influenced by Greek thought [source: WP-Graeco-Arabic, citing Gutas]; SEP-Sina speaks of "the rationalistic outlook in political and social affairs espoused by the ʿAbbāsid dynasty upon its accession to power in 750".
- The Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258 is the conventional end of the institution [WP-House_of_Wisdom]; the "river ran black with ink" and "bridge of books" stories are literary tropes according to historian Michal Biran [same]. Not needed in this chapter.

## 3. Falsafa and kalām (brief)
- *Kalām* = rational/speculative Islamic theology; its practitioners are *mutakallimūn*. Arguments for God from temporal coming-to-be (*ḥudūth*) are typical of kalām (al-Ashʿarī, al-Juwaynī, al-Ghazālī, al-Shahrastānī) "and even certain philosophers like … al-Kindī"; the "kalām cosmological argument" was revived in modern philosophy of religion by William Lane Craig. [source: SEP-Rel §2.1]
- **Muʿtazilites**: the main theologians of the 9th century; they held a strict view of divine simplicity (no attributes distinct from God's essence) and that the Qur'an is created. Al-Kindī's austere view of divine attributes has been compared (Ivry 1974, Adamson 2003) to theirs, and Muʿtazilite influence may lie behind his view that creation is "bringing to be of being from non-being" and cannot be eternal. [source: SEP-Kindi §§2.2, 3.1] On evil, Muʿtazilites held that God, being just, must have justifying reasons for suffering (compensation etc.), and most came to say God does what is best. [source: SEP-Rel §7 area]
- **The Miḥna**: inquisition begun by al-Maʾmūn in 833 to enforce the doctrine of the created Qur'an on judges and scholars; continued under al-Muʿtaṣim and al-Wāthiq; reversed by al-Mutawakkil from 847 (fully c. 851). [source: WP-Mihna] (WP-Mu'tazilism is partisan in tone; use only these dates.)
- **Ashʿarites**: school founded by Abū l-Ḥasan al-Ashʿarī (d. 935/6) [source: SEP-Rel]; occasionalist: virtually all causal efficacy belongs to God alone (Muʿtazilites reserved some for rational agents; philosophers held essential/natural causation). [source: SEP-Rel §3.2] Al-Ghazālī and Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī were Ashʿarites. [sources: SEP-FDR §1; WP-Ash'arism]
- Avicenna's essence/existence distinction drew on kalām debates: Muʿtazilites (Basran) treated the "thing" (*shayʾ*) as distinct from existence (even non-existent things are "things"); Ashʿarites held that to be is to be something. Avicenna sided with the Ashʿarites that nothing is real without existing, while rejecting their "equivocity of existence". [sources: SEP-SinaMet §3.2; SEP-Ess §4]

## 4. Al-Kindī (c. 800 – c. 870)
- Full name Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb ibn Isḥāq al-Kindī; SEP: "ca. 800–870 CE", "the first self-identified philosopher in the Arabic tradition". Died after 866, usually put in the early 870s; birth "around 800". [source: SEP-Kindi intro, §1.1] WP: c. 801–873, death in Baghdad 873 (Corbin: died "a lonely man"). McGinnis gives "d. ca. 866". [sources: WP-Al-Kindi; SEP-Rel §2.1] WRITER: "c. 800 – c. 870".
- **Birthplace disagreement:** SEP: "born in Basra and educated in Baghdad". WP: born in Kufa, father governor of Basra, first educated in Basra. WRITER: avoid naming the birthplace, or say "from Basra or Kufa, educated in Baghdad". [sources: SEP-Kindi §1.1; WP-Al-Kindi]
- Member of the Arab tribe of Kinda; "His lineage earned him the title 'philosopher of the Arabs' among later writers." [source: SEP-Kindi §1.1] WP quotes Ibn al-Nadim (or later bio-bibliography) calling him "the Philosopher of the Arabs". Latin name Alkindus. [WP-Al-Kindi]
- Served under al-Maʾmūn (r. to 833); associated with the court of al-Muʿtaṣim (r. 833–842), to whom he dedicated *On First Philosophy*, and whose son Aḥmad he tutored. [source: SEP-Kindi §1.1]
- Later fortunes: his star waned under al-Wāthiq and especially al-Mutawakkil (r. 847–861); "at one point al-Kindi was beaten and his library temporarily confiscated." [source: WP-Al-Kindi; a WP-only detail, say "according to later reports"]
- Works: Ibn al-Nadīm's *Fihrist* (10th c.) lists hundreds of treatises; scientific titles outnumber philosophical ones; most extant philosophical works survive in a single Istanbul manuscript (translated in Adamson and Pormann 2012). WP: at least 260 books (Ibn al-Nadīm). [sources: SEP-Kindi §1.2; WP-Al-Kindi]
- *On First Philosophy* survives incomplete (first part, four sections): (1) exhortation to honour Greek wisdom; (2) the eternity of the world; (3)–(4) existence of a "true One" (God) and the inapplicability of language to it. [source: SEP-Kindi §1.2]
- Also: *On the Intellect* (first Arabic taxonomy of types of intellect), *On Sleep and Dream*, *On Dispelling Sorrows*, *On Definitions* (building an Arabic philosophical vocabulary), optics (*On Perspectives*, Latin only), and much on astrology, music, medicine, mathematics. [source: SEP-Kindi §1.2]
- His summary of Aristotle's *Metaphysics* in *On the Quantity of Aristotle's Books* makes it a work of theology (affirming God's oneness, His names, His being the agent cause of the universe): "a wholly accurate description of al-Kindi's own conception" of metaphysics, not of Aristotle's. [source: SEP-Kindi §2.1]
- First philosophy = "the knowledge of the first truth who is the cause of all truth." [source: SEP-Kindi §2.1]
- Sources: Aristotle (main authority), Neoplatonic translations (the *Theology of Aristotle* = Arabic Plotinus, produced in his circle; the Arabic Proclus behind the *Book of Causes*), and above all John Philoponus on the world's beginning. He played down any tension among Greek authorities, giving "no sign that his position on the eternity of the world departs from that of Aristotle". [source: SEP-Kindi intro, §2.1]
- His writings "might be thought of as a sustained public relations campaign" for Greek thought before a 9th-century Muslim audience. [source: SEP-Kindi §1.1, Adamson]
- **True One argument (Section 3):** every kind of predicate (genus, species, difference, individual, proper accident, common accident — after Porphyry) involves both unity and multiplicity; their association cannot be chance nor caused by any member of the set of things that are both one and many; so there must be an external cause that is "essentially" one, "one in truth", the "true One", God. Section 4: no predicate applies to it — "only pure unity". [source: SEP-Kindi §3.1]
- Creation: God's proper act is "the bringing-to-be of beings from non-being" ("origination"); God is the only "true" agent, others "metaphorical" agents. Creation *ex nihilo* needs no substrate and no time. [source: SEP-Kindi §3.2]
- He wrote against the Christian Trinity using the *Isagoge*; Yaḥyā ibn ʿAdī replied. He commented on Qur'an 36:79–82 and on 55:6 ("the stars and the trees prostrate themselves"). [source: SEP-Kindi §2.2]
- Psychology: the soul is an immaterial substance; *Discourse on the Soul* is a hortatory doxography urging the soul to cleanse itself and ascend to the "world of the intellect". *On Recollection* argues we "remember" intelligible forms (Platonic). [source: SEP-Kindi §4]
- **Prophecy and philosophy:** WP (citing Adamson 2005, pp. 46–47, and Corbin) reports four contrasts: the philosopher needs long study, prophecy is God-given; the philosopher reaches truth by his own effort, the prophet by revelation; the prophet's understanding is clearer and more comprehensive; and his way of expressing it to ordinary people is superior. "The crucial implication is that the *content* … is the *same*." [source: WP-Al-Kindi] SEP confirms only that he "discusses the special nature of prophetic knowledge" in *On Why the Higher Atmosphere is Cold* and comments on Qur'an 36:79–82. [source: SEP-Kindi §2.2] WRITER: present the four contrasts as Adamson's summary.
- *On Sleep and Dream* gives a naturalistic account of prophetic dreams via the imagination; he embraces prophetic dreams "but does not claim that they come to us from God". [source: SEP-Kindi §4.2]

### ARGUMENT: al-Kindī against the eternity of the world (from SEP-Kindi §3.3)
- Background: Aristotle, the Stoics and orthodox Neoplatonists held the world had always existed; Philoponus (6th c.) was the exception; al-Kindī follows Philoponus' strategy of "using Aristotle against himself". Davidson 1969/1987 and Staley 1987 discuss.
- Premise 1: There can be no actual infinite (Aristotle).
- Premise 2: The body of the cosmos is finite in magnitude.
- Premise 3: Nothing predicated of a finite body can be infinite.
- Premise 4: Time is predicated of the body of the cosmos.
- Conclusion: time is finite, so the world is not eternal (it had a beginning).
- Objection (an Aristotelian one, set out by Adamson): this ignores the actual/potential infinity distinction. An eternal past implies only a *potential* infinite: "the world has already existed for N years" is true for any N, as a body can be divided as finely as one wishes; no infinity is ever actually present.
- Reply (al-Kindī, also in Philoponus): to reach the present moment an actually infinite number of moments must already have elapsed, and "the infinite cannot be traversed" (Aristotle himself).
- Counter: Adamson finds it "unclear" whether this works; it seems to presuppose picking an infinitely distant past point, which Aristotle would refuse: any past point is only finitely far away.
- Al-Kindī ignored Philoponus' attack on the fifth element: he accepted that the heavens are made of an incorruptible fifth element yet said God brought them into being with a beginning in time.
- Related: SEP-Rel §2.1 gives the later kalām version, including a "second kind of argument" from an actually infinite number of immortal souls if humans had existed for ever.

## 5. Abū Bakr al-Rāzī (865–925): a dissenting voice
- Dates 865–925 CE (251–313 AH); from Rayy, near modern Tehran; one of the greatest physicians of the Islamic tradition. [source: SEP-Razi intro] (SEP-FDR gives "d. 920": record as minor inconsistency; use 925.) Latin name Rhazes (WP).
- Cosmos produced through five "eternal principles": God, Soul, matter, time and place; opponents saw this as positing four eternal principles alongside God. [source: SEP-Razi intro, §1]
- Extant philosophy: ethics, in *The Spiritual Medicine* and *The Philosophical Way of Life*. [source: SEP-Razi]
- Religion: the main (hostile) source is Abū Ḥātim al-Rāzī (d. 934), *Proofs of Prophecy*, recounting face-to-face debates. Al-Rāzī reportedly argued that singling out some people for prophetic knowledge would be unjust, that appointing *imāms* causes dissension, that following religious law is *taqlīd* (uncritical acceptance on authority), and that God gives reason (*ʿaql*) to everyone equally; he reportedly rejected miracles, including the inimitability of the Qur'an. [source: SEP-Razi §5]
- **Debate:** some scholars celebrate him as a "freethinker" (Urvoy 1996, Stroumsa 1999) or as having pagan sympathies (Vallat); against this, Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī quotes him interpreting the Qur'an in line with his philosophy (Rashed 2000, 2008); Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa suggested a supposed anti-religious work may have been forged by enemies. [source: SEP-Razi §5]

## 6. Al-Fārābī (c. 870 – 950/1)
- "We know little that is really reliable about al-Fārābī's life." Probably born 870 CE (AH 257) at a place called Farab or Farayb; moved in youth to Iraq and Baghdad; went to Syria and Damascus in 943 (AH 331); may have visited Egypt; died in Damascus in December 950 or January 951 (AH 339). [source: SEP-Farabi intro]
- **Ethnicity debated:** "Some claimed he was Turkish but more recent research points to him being a Persian (Rudolph 2017: 536–45)." [source: SEP-Farabi] WP adds: several places were called Farab (Otrar on the Syr Darya in Kazakhstan; a Farab near Türkmenabat; Faryab in Khurasan); Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa (13th c.) calls his father a Persian army commander; Ibn Khallikān (*Wafayāt*, completed 1271) calls him Turkic; these accounts date ~300 years after his death; Gutas thinks the evidence insufficient to decide. [source: WP-Al-Farabi] WRITER: "of Central Asian origin; whether Persian or Turkic is disputed (recent research leans Persian)". Kazakhstan and Turkey today claim him as a national figure — not verified, do not state.
- Teachers: studied logic with the Nestorian cleric Yuḥannā ibn Ḥaylān (autobiographical passage preserved by Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa); associated in Baghdad with Abū Bishr Mattā and Yaḥyā ibn ʿAdī. [sources: WP-Al-Farabi; SEP-Greek §5]
- Known as "the second master" (Aristotle being the first) — Arabic *al-muʿallim al-thānī*, also rendered "Second Teacher". [sources: SEP-Farabi intro; WP-Al-Farabi note citing Netton 1994]
- Two main interests: philosophy (logic especially) and music; his *Great Book of Music* (*Kitāb al-mūsīqā al-kabīr*) is "the most important medieval musical treatise in Islamic lands". [source: SEP-Farabi]
- Key works: *Enumeration of the Sciences* (*Iḥṣāʾ al-ʿulūm*; two Latin versions, by Gerard of Cremona and Dominicus Gundissalinus); *Book of Letters* (*Kitāb al-Ḥurūf*); *The Opinions of the People of the Virtuous City* (*Mabādiʾ ārāʾ ahl al-madīna al-fāḍila*, Walzer's "Perfect State") and *The Political Regime* — possibly among his latest works; *Attainment of Happiness*, *Philosophy of Plato*, *Philosophy of Aristotle* (a trilogy, transl. Mahdi 1969); *Book of Religion*; *Summary of Plato's Laws*; *On the Aims of the Metaphysics*; *On the Intellect*. [sources: SEP-Farabi; SEP-FarMet; SEP-FarSoc]
- Chronology of his works is largely unknown, so tensions between works may reflect development (Janos 2019), an exoteric/esoteric distinction, or human limitation. [source: SEP-Farabi §1]

### Political philosophy
- He did not draw on Aristotle's *Politics* (apparently never translated into Arabic) but on Plato's *Republic* and *Laws*, though it is doubtful that full Arabic translations of them existed; his *Summary of Plato's Laws* covers only the first eight books. [source: SEP-Farabi §7] (Consistent with Periergia *Plato* chapter, which says "the dialogue's opening books".)
- He transforms Plato: instead of a monolingual, monoethnic city-state, "a vast multicultural, multilingual, and multireligious empire" (Orwin 2017); he "sees the necessity to make of the philosopher king a philosopher prophet ruler." [source: SEP-Farabi §7]
- *Falsafa madaniyya* ("philosophy of society/political philosophy") covers human perfection and the city (*madīna*), judged by how well it guides citizens to happiness and imitates the cosmic order. [source: SEP-FarSoc intro]
- The city is ordered like a body and like the cosmos; the ruler is "the most perfect part of the city" (*Perfect State* V.15.5, transl. Walzer). [source: SEP-FarSoc §3]
- Religion (*milla*) is "opinions and actions, determined and restricted with stipulations and prescribed for a community by their first ruler" for the sake of ultimate happiness (*Book of Religion* 1, Butterworth transl., slightly modified); religion is "an instrument of rulership"; it teaches through "affinity and symbolic representation" what philosophy teaches demonstratively; philosophy is epistemically superior, religion practically indispensable because most people need symbols. [source: SEP-FarSoc §4.1]
- The ideal ruler is "at once an accomplished philosopher, prophet, supreme ruler, lawgiver, and imam" who can translate demonstrative knowledge into persuasive symbols. [source: SEP-FarSoc §4.2]
- Non-virtuous cities: ignorant, wicked (immoral) and errant [source: WP-Al-Farabi]; SEP-FarSoc discusses the "immoral cities" whose people know better but pursue status, honour, domination or pleasure (*Political Regime*).
- **Prophecy via imagination:** normally the active intellect actualises intelligibles in the material intellect; in rare cases it gives intelligibles to the imaginative faculty in the form of particular sensibles; the highest perfection of imagination is prophecy (awareness of present and future events and visions of divine beings); lower grades include dreams. [source: SEP-FarPsy, citing *Virtuous City* pp. 220–223]
- **Interpretive debate (Straussian reading):** Leo Strauss's followers (Mahdi, Butterworth, Parens) read al-Fārābī esoterically, e.g. Parens (1995) argued he treats metaphysics as rhetoric; Butterworth: "Alfarabi's Goal: Political Philosophy, Not Political Theology" (2011). Whether to read him this way "is still a debated question". [source: SEP-Farabi §7]

### Harmonisation of Plato and Aristotle; the authorship question
- *The Harmonization of the Two Opinions of the Two Sages: Plato the Divine and Aristotle* (*Kitāb al-jamʿ bayna raʾyay al-ḥakīmayn*) argues, following an Alexandrian tradition, that apparent contradictions between Plato and Aristotle are easily resolved; it relies on the *Theology of Aristotle* (really Plotinus) and on Proclus Arabus. [source: SEP-Farabi §7]
- **Marwan Rashed (2009)** put "into serious doubt the authenticity" of the treatise; Cecilia Martini Bonadeo (2008 critical edition) defended al-Fārābī's authorship; Genequand (2012) objected to Rashed; Cherni (2015) rejected authenticity. The question affects how one reads al-Fārābī's Aristotelianism vs Neoplatonism. Butterworth's English translation: *The Political Writings*, vol. I (2001). [source: SEP-Farabi §7]
- The Periergia Overview says "Al-Fārābī (d. 950) harmonized Plato's political philosophy with Islam" — loose but not wrong; the writer should not cite the *Harmonization* as uncontested al-Fārābī.

### Logic and grammar: the Baghdad debate of 932
- A disputation "on the relative merits of grammar and logic convened for the amusement of the Vizier": the grammarian Abū Saʿīd al-Sīrāfī confounded Abū Bishr Mattā with grammatical subtleties; Mattā's view "weathered Sīrāfī's storm of criticism badly". [source: SEP-Lang §1.2]
- Date: 932 (320 AH), in Baghdad, before the vizier Ibn al-Furāt [sources: WP-Abu_Bishr_Matta; WS-7, Margoliouth 1905 accepting AH 320 because Abū l-Fatḥ Ibn al-Furāt was vizier that year]. The account comes from Abū Ḥayyān al-Tawḥīdī's *Kitāb al-Imtāʿ wa-l-muʾānasa*, transmitted by Yāqūt; accounts are biased toward al-Sīrāfī (WP; WS-7). Al-Sīrāfī d. 368 AH (978/9) [WS-7]. Al-Sīrāfī's point: logic is Greek, tied to the Greek language, of no use to Arabic speakers. [WP-Abu_Bishr_Matta; WS-7]
- Translation of the full debate: Margoliouth 1905 (JRAS). Scholars disagree about its importance: Elamrani-Jamal, Endress, Mahdi think it important; Frank (and Street) relatively insignificant. [source: SEP-Lang notes 5–6]
- **Al-Fārābī's answer** (*Iḥṣāʾ al-ʿulūm*, transl. Black 1998): logic relates to intellect and intelligibles as grammar relates to language and expressions; logic's subject matter is "intelligibles in so far as they are signified by expressions, and expressions in so far as they signify intelligibles"; grammar gives rules "specific to the expressions of a given community", logic "common rules that are general for the expressions of every community" — logic as a kind of universal grammar (Black). [source: SEP-Lang §2.1.1] He also claimed logic indispensable for jurisprudence and theology, a claim later taken up by al-Ghazālī. [source: SEP-Lang §1.2]
- Al-Fārābī sees logic as the path to happiness (Germann 2015). [source: SEP-Farabi §3]

## 7. Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā): life and works
- Name: Abū ʿAlī al-Ḥusayn ibn ʿAbdallāh Ibn Sīnā; Latin Avicenna. [source: SEP-Sina]
- **Birth date debated.** SEP (Gutas): born in Afshana, a village outside Bukhara, "some time in the 70s of the tenth century, perhaps as early as 964"; heading "ca. 970–1037"; note 3: "The year 980 (370 of the Hijra) that is traditionally given in some sources is based on a miscalculation (Gutas 1987–1988/2014b-II)." IEP and WP: c. 980. [sources: SEP-Sina §1.1 and note 3; IEP-Avic; WP-Avicenna] WRITER: "born c. 970 (traditionally 980)". Died 1037 in Hamadhān (WP: 22 June 1037; IEP: of colic, on campaign to Hamadan); buried there. [SEP-Sina; IEP-Avic; WP]
- Father from Balkh, a Samanid official (governor of the district of Kharmaythan); IEP: father "may have been Ismaili". Family moved to Bukhara, the capital of the Persian Samanid dynasty (SEP: 819–1005), which pursued "a deliberate agenda of Persian linguistic revival". [sources: SEP-Sina §1.1; IEP-Avic §1]
- **Persian identity:** he "spoke Persian natively and did use it to write philosophy" (Adamson 2016, quoted in WP-Avicenna); wrote mostly in Arabic but a Persian summa, the *Dāneshnāme-ye ʿAlāʾī* (*Philosophy for ʿAlāʾ al-Dawla*). [sources: WP-Avicenna; SEP-Sina §1.2; IEP-Avic §2] Modern Uzbekistan, Tajikistan and Iran all honour him (e.g. a 1999 Tajik banknote shows him [WP-Proof_of_the_Truthful image caption]); keep this light.
- **Autobiography:** dictated to his student al-Jūzjānī, who continued it as a biography after Avicenna's death (Gohlman 1974 edition/translation). [source: SEP-Sina §1.1] Gutas argues it is partly a literary device showing that knowledge can be reached by study and intuition (*ḥads*). [source: IEP-Avic §1]
- Claims in it: studied the philosophical sciences by himself and mastered them by about 18; treated the Samanid ruler (Nūḥ ibn Manṣūr, r. 976–997 per IEP) and was allowed into the palace library, where he "read those books, mastered their teachings, and realized how far each man had advanced in his science" (Gohlman 1974, 36; transl. Gutas 2014a, 18). [source: SEP-Sina §1.1; IEP-Avic §1]
- He understood the purpose of Aristotle's *Metaphysics* only after reading al-Fārābī's short treatise on it (*On the Aims of the Metaphysics*). [source: IEP-Avic §1] The detail that he had read the *Metaphysics* forty times and had it by heart, and bought al-Fārābī's booklet for three dirhams, is in WS-2 summaries only (not read in a source text): use with "he says" and without the numbers, or leave out.
- IEP: when stuck on a problem he went to the mosque to pray, "and drinking wine at times", for inspiration. [IEP-Avic §1]
- Career: 999 the Turkic Qarakhanids ended Samanid rule; "necessity led me to forsake Bukhara" (Gohlman 1974, 40–41). Then Gurganj in Khwārazm (999?–1012), Jurjān (1012–1014?), Rayy (1014?–1015), Hamadhān (1015–1024?; vizier to the Buyid Shams al-Dawla per IEP), Isfahan (1024?–1037) at the court of the Kakuyid ʿAlāʾ al-Dawla. Physician, political counsellor, "scientist-in-residence". [sources: SEP-Sina §1.1; IEP-Avic §1]
- In Rayy he served the Buyid amir Majd al-Dawla and his mother **Sayyida Shīrīn**, "the de facto ruler of the realm", treating Majd al-Dawla's melancholia. [source: WP-Avicenna citing Gutas 1987 and Adamson 2013] (A woman in the story, as patron and ruler; WP-only.)
- He was imprisoned for four months in the fortress of Fardajān near Hamadhān (c. 1023) on suspicion of corresponding with ʿAlāʾ al-Dawla. [source: WP-Avicenna citing Gutas 1987, Adamson 2013]
- Honorific *al-shaykh al-raʾīs*, "the Preeminent Master"; Gutas: "In his influence on the intellectual history of the world in the West (of India), he is second only to Aristotle". [source: SEP-Sina intro]
- **Works:** fewer than 100 authentic works by Gutas's tally (medieval lists give nearly 300). Major innovation: the philosophical *summa*. *The Cure* (*al-Shifāʾ*): logic, physics, mathematics, metaphysics, 22 volumes in the Cairo edition (1952–83); IEP: solicited by students in Hamadhān in 1016, completed in Isfahan by 1027. *The Salvation* (*al-Najāt*), an abridgement; *Philosophy for ʿAlāʾ al-Dawla* in Persian; *Eastern Philosophy* (*al-Ḥikma al-mashriqiyya*, mostly lost); *Pointers and Reminders* (*al-Ishārāt wa-l-tanbīhāt*), his last summa, deliberately allusive, read with commentaries (Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī); dated by Gutas to Isfahan early 1030s, by Michot earlier. *The Canon of Medicine* (*al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb*) was the basis of medical teaching in European universities until the 17th century. [sources: SEP-Sina intro, §1.2; IEP-Avic §2]
- Translated titles: *Shifāʾ* = *The Cure* (Gutas, IEP) or *The Healing* (WP, Marmura's translation *The Metaphysics of The Healing*, 2005). *Ishārāt* = *Pointers and Reminders* (SEP, IEP) or *Remarks and Admonitions* (Inati).

## 8. Essence and existence
- Two senses of "existence": the existence that something is established (*al-wujūd al-ithbātī*) and its "proper existence" (*al-wujūd al-khāṣṣ*) = its reality, nature, essence (*dhāt*), quiddity (*māhiyya*) or "thingness" (*shayʾiyya*) (*Ilāhiyyāt* I.5). [source: SEP-SinaMet §3.2]
- Main argument (the "conceptual test"): we can know what humans, horses or triangles are without knowing whether they exist; if x can be conceived without y, y is not essential to x; so existence is distinct from essence (*Remarks and Admonitions*, 121). [source: SEP-Ess §4]
- Second argument: if "to exist" meant "to be something", "horses exist" would mean "horses are horses". [source: SEP-Ess §4]
- Background in Aristotle (*Posterior Analytics* II 92b10: whether a thing is vs what it is), al-Fārābī's *Book of Letters*, Yaḥyā ibn ʿAdī and Muʿtazilite kalām. [source: SEP-SinaMet §3.2]
- Everything other than God is "possible in itself, necessary through another" (*mumkin al-wujūd bi-dhātihi*, *wājib bi-ghayrihi*); only God's essence is identical with existence — God has no quiddity beyond existence. [sources: SEP-SinaMet §§3.3, 4.1; SEP-Ess §4]
- "Indifference of essence": horseness in itself is "only horseness" (*Ilāhiyyāt* V.1; Latin *equinitas ergo in se est equinitas tantum*); in itself neither universal nor particular; universal in the mind, particular in things. [sources: SEP-SinaMet §3.4; SEP-Infl]
- Mental existence (*wujūd dhihnī*): essences can exist in the mind as well as outside it. [source: SEP-Ess §4]
- Existence is a "necessary concomitant" (*lāzim*) of essence; Avicenna is a necessitarian: everything that exists does so necessarily, through its causes. [source: SEP-Ess §4]
- **Debate about its meaning:** (a) real vs only conceptual distinction: Avicenna moves from a conceptual difference to a real one but "never establishes an extensional distinction"; "does not present a clear-cut argument in favor of a real distinction"; most post-Avicennan thinkers accepted only a conceptual distinction ("conceptualists"), with regress arguments from ʿUmar al-Khayyām (d. 1123–24), al-Abharī (d. 1264) and Suhrawardī. [source: SEP-Ess §§4–5] (b) Status of pure quiddity: some read it as a third realm (comparable to Latin *esse essentiae*, Muʿtazilite "Meinongianism"); others (Druart, McGinnis, Bertolacci, Lizzini, Benevich) say it has only definitional, not ontological, priority. [source: SEP-Ess §4] (c) Is existence an "accident" of essence? Lizzini notes composition "is not the result of an addition" — an essence is not a thing to which existence is added. [source: SEP-SinaMet §3.4] IEP: "Later arguments raged concerning whether the distinction was mental or real". [IEP-Avic §5]
- Influence: Aquinas adopts it in *De ente et essentia* IV. [source: SEP-Infl; IEP-Avic] (Periergia Overview says Aquinas took "essence/existence from Avicenna": consistent.)

## 9. ARGUMENT: the proof of the Necessary Existent (*burhān al-ṣiddīqīn*)
- Name: *burhān al-ṣiddīqīn*, "the demonstration of the truthful" (McGinnis) / "Proof of the Truthful". [sources: SEP-Rel §2.2; WP-Proof_of_the_Truthful] Found in *al-Najāt* (261–5, 271–3), *Ishārāt* (fourth "class", the most concise and influential form), and *Ilāhiyyāt* I.6–7. In the *Ishārāt* Avicenna contrasts a proof drawn from reflecting on existence itself (that of "the truthful", more solid and noble) with proofs from God's creations or acts (Inati 2014). [sources: SEP-Rel §2.2; WP-Proof_of_the_Truthful]
- Distinctiveness: unlike most cosmological arguments it "does not appeal to the impossibility of infinite causal series" and even entertains an actual infinity of simultaneous causes; it shows the world depends on God at every moment, even if the world is past-eternal. [source: SEP-Rel §2.2] WP (Adamson 2016): "Avicenna's argument does not preclude the possibility of an infinite regress". (Note IEP-Avic's summary instead uses the no-infinite-regress step; SEP-SinaMet on *Ilāhiyyāt* I.6–7 also mentions that one cannot proceed *ad infinitum*. WRITER: present the *Ishārāt/Najāt* "whole collection" version from McGinnis, and note that other versions use a no-regress step.)
- Step by step (McGinnis' reconstruction, SEP-Rel §2.2):
  1. Every existent is, considered in itself, either necessary through itself (cannot not exist) or possible through itself (exists through another). (Exhaustive division.)
  2. Preliminary: a necessary existent through itself must be simple: a whole exists through its parts, and parts are other than the whole, so a composite would exist "through another" — contradiction.
  3. Something exists, x. If x is necessary through itself, done.
  4. If x is possible, take W = the whole of all and only the possible existents that currently exist.
  5. W exists; W is not necessary through itself, because it has parts (possible existents) and a necessary existent is simple. So W is possible and exists through some other, o.
  6. o is either inside W or outside it. If inside, o would be a possible existent (W contains only possibles) yet the cause of all of W, including itself — so it would exist through itself, i.e. be necessary: contradiction.
  7. So o is outside W; the only thing outside the totality of possibles is a necessary existent through itself.
  8. Further arguments derive the attributes: uniqueness (two necessary existents could not be distinguished either by an essential or by an accidental property without one being caused), simplicity, immateriality, intellect, goodness etc., identified with God. [sources: SEP-Rel; SEP-SinaMet §4.2; WP-Proof_of_the_Truthful]
- Objection Avicenna himself anticipates: the whole need not share its parts' contingency (a set of numbers is not a number). Reply: then the whole is necessary — "in a certain way, this is the very thing that is sought" (quoted via WP from Adamson 2016, 128; translation not verified). [source: WP-Proof_of_the_Truthful]
- Objection: it proves at most a necessary existent, which could be the universe itself or many beings; Avicenna adds separate arguments for God's attributes, and a critic may accept the necessary existent but reject the identification with God (Adamson's "piecemeal" point). [source: WP-Proof_of_the_Truthful, citing Adamson 2013, 2016]
- Objection (al-Ghazālī): the necessary existent's causation must itself be necessary, incompatible with God's free will (Ashʿarite); he argued God's free choice is shown by the arbitrary size of the universe and moment of its creation. [source: WP-Proof_of_the_Truthful, citing Adamson 2016] Al-Ghazālī's general strategy in the *Incoherence*: the philosophers' arguments are not demonstrative but rest on unproven premises (see §12). [SEP-Ghaz]
- Objection (Averroes): God's existence should be shown from physics (motion), as Aristotle did, not from metaphysics. [source: WP-Proof_of_the_Truthful, citing Adamson 2016]
- Objection (Mullā Ṣadrā, d. 1636): earlier proofs begin with concepts or essences (necessity, possibility), which are mental, and "illicitly infer from concepts of things to their existence"; one must begin with existence itself. [source: SEP-Rel §2.3]
- Modern objections: IEP lists the Kantian objection (is existence meaningful in itself / a predicate?) and that Cantor's treatment of infinity undermines arguments from the impossibility of actual infinites. [IEP-Avic §5] Also: one may deny the starting modal framework — the universe could just exist (Adamson). [WP-Proof]
- Classification debated: ontological (Morewedge, Legenhausen, Badawi, Sharif) vs cosmological (Davidson, Goodman, Marmura, Sheikh, Afnan) — Mayer 2001. [source: WP-Proof_of_the_Truthful; SEP-SinaMet §4.2 notes Hourani, Morewedge, Marmura, Davidson read *Ilāhiyyāt* I.6–7 as possibly an ontological proof, since metaphysics can establish God's existence without sense data, *Ilāhiyyāt* I.3]
- Reception: Adamson calls it one of the most influential medieval arguments for God and Avicenna's biggest contribution; taken up by Aquinas, Scotus and Maimonides; *wājib al-wujūd* became a standard name for God even among critics. [source: WP-Proof_of_the_Truthful]

## 10. God, emanation and God's knowledge of particulars
- The world is eternal yet created: "continuously" brought into existence without temporal precedence; *ibdāʿ* ("instauration"): being "after" non-being by posteriority of essence, not of time. [source: SEP-SinaMet §5.3] IEP: he "rejected creation ex nihilo" in time; the cosmos follows God "merely in logical order". [IEP-Avic §5]
- Emanation (*fayḍ*): from the One only one proceeds (*ex uno non fit nisi unum*); the first intellect's twofold nature begins multiplicity; chain of celestial intelligences, souls and bodies. [source: SEP-SinaMet §5.4]
- **God knows particulars "in a universal way"** (*Ilāhiyyāt* VIII.6; Marmura 1962 is the classic study). [WS-6; IEP-Avic §6: "in a 'universal manner'"] God, as pure immaterial intellect, cannot know particulars through sensation or as changing in time; IEP (Belo 2006) gives three senses: intellectual not sensory; prior to and causative of its objects; extends to their general qualities. [source: IEP-Ghaz §b] SEP-Rel: for Avicenna (and al-Fārābī and Ibn Rushd) "God does not know particulars in their particularity, e.g., that you are reading this sentence at this very moment"; yet Avicenna quotes Qur'an 10:61 ("not even the weight of a dust speck … escapes your Lord's notice") as satisfied by God's universal knowledge (*al-Najāt*, 247). [source: SEP-Rel §3.2.1]
- Eclipse example: an astronomer knows "an eclipse will occur", "is occurring", "has occurred" — three changing states; knowing them temporally would change the knower; God knows the eclipse through its causes. [source: IEP-Ghaz §b; WS-6 says the example is in the *Taʿlīqāt*]
- Al-Ghazālī's formulation of the view: "If the object known changes, knowledge changes; and if knowledge changes, the knower inescapably changes. But change in God is impossible." (*Incoherence*, Thirteenth Discussion, p. 135, transl. Marmura 2000) [source: IEP-Ghaz]
- Why al-Ghazālī condemned it: "it entails that if Zayd, for example, obeys or disobeys God, God would not know what of his states has newly come about, because He does not know Zayd in his particularity." (*Incoherence*, 136, Marmura) — it "uprooted religious laws in their entirety"; contradicts Qur'an 34:3 ("Not an atom's weight is hidden from Him"). His reply: the changes are relational, not in God's knowledge itself. [source: IEP-Ghaz §b]
- Debate: Marmura (1962) concluded God does not know all particulars individually; others (e.g. Acar 2004, Adamson 2005, Zadeh) argue Avicenna says God knows all things. [WS-6; SEP-FDR bibliography lists Acar 2004 and Adamson 2005 "On Knowledge of Particulars"]

## 11. The soul, the flying man, the internal senses and the active intellect
- Avicenna "unequivocally posited an independent, self-conscious, substantial and immortal soul", following Plato rather than Aristotle on the separation of soul and body; the soul is not the essential form of the body but accidentally related to it; it is emanated by the separate intelligences when a suitable body comes to be; individuated by its body; survives death with its individuality. [source: SEP-Mind §3] IEP: he rejected the soul's pre-existence. [IEP-Avic §2]
- He attributes self-consciousness to the soul, "an ego that has self-awareness and is not to be identified unilaterally with the rational faculty" (Rahman 1952). [source: SEP-Mind §3]

### ARGUMENT: the flying (floating, suspended) man
- Locations: opening chapter of the psychology of the *Shifāʾ* (*De anima* I.1, Rahman ed. p. 16) and again at V.7 (p. 255); also the *Ishārāt*, the *Discussions* (*Mubāḥathāt*) and notes on Aristotle's *De anima*. [sources: SEP-Mind note 27; SEP-Suhr §3.2; IEP-Avic §7]
- Set-up (IEP; SEP-Mind; WP-Floating_man): imagine a human created all at once, fully formed, with sight veiled, floating in air or a void so that no air currents are felt, limbs separated so they do not touch one another.
- Step by step:
  1. Such a person has no sensory awareness of any body, his own included.
  2. Yet he would not fail to affirm his own existence (his self).
  3. He would not affirm the existence of any limb, organ, heart or brain, nor that his self has length, breadth or depth; if he imagined a hand, he would not take it as part of himself.
  4. What is affirmed is distinct from what is not affirmed.
  5. So the self (soul) is something other than the body; Avicenna takes it to show the soul is an independent intelligent substance. [sources: IEP-Avic §7; SEP-Mind §3; WP-Floating_man]
- Purpose: to "show the ability of a person to deduce the soul's existence intellectually, without assistance from the sensory or imaginative, material faculties"; a departure from Aristotle's hylomorphism. [source: SEP-Mind §3]
- **Objection: begs the question / invalid inference.** Marmura ("Avicenna's 'Flying Man' in Context", *The Monist* 69, 1986) held that the argument presupposes that the flying man would be self-aware, and called the move "an unwarranted swerve from the hypothetical to the categorical". [WS-3, WS-4] Second objection (Adamson 2015, via WP): even granting self-awareness, the self could be the brain; he might be aware through his nervous system without being aware of it. [WP-Floating_man] IEP: "how do we know that the knowing subject is the self?", an objection "which may also be levelled at Descartes". [IEP-Avic §7] (A modern label for the inference worry is the "masked man fallacy": not knowing that A is B does not show A ≠ B; Kaukua 2020 raises a masked-man concern [WS-3].)
- **Replies:** self-awareness is a premise the reader is meant to grant from their own experience, not a conclusion (Kaukua; *Self-Awareness in Islamic Philosophy*, 2015); Adamson and Benevich ("The Thought Experimental Method: Avicenna's Flying Man Argument", *Journal of the American Philosophical Association*, 2018) argue it is not circular and works given two "admittedly controversial assumptions"; Kaukua replied (JAPA 2020, "The Flying and the Masked Man, One More Time"). [WS-3] Also, Avicenna himself says the experiment serves as an alert or reminder (*tanbīh*) for someone able to notice the soul, not a strict demonstration — NOT VERIFIED in a source read; omit unless checked.
- **Descartes comparison:** IEP: "This proof for the self in many ways prefigures by 600 years the Cartesian cogito"; Druart, "The Soul and Body Problem: Avicenna and Descartes" (1988). WP: "Whether these similarities are deep or trivial is a matter of scholarly disagreement." [sources: IEP-Avic §7; SEP-Mind note 27; WP-Floating_man] Difference to note: Descartes doubts the body methodically; Avicenna imagines a person who simply lacks bodily awareness. (This contrast is the writer's to draw carefully; no source read states it in these words.)
- Latin reception: the "Flying Man" was influential in Latin (Hasse 2000, 80–92), through the Latin translation of the *Shifāʾ*'s *De anima* by Dominicus Gundissalinus with the Jewish scholar Avendauth in Toledo. [source: SEP-Infl] No source read shows Descartes knew it: do not claim influence.
- The thought experiment's subject is Arabic *insān*, a generic human; McGinnis's translation renders an example as "Adam or Eve" (McGinnis 2010, 145–147). [WP-Floating_man] Optional.
- Suhrawardī reworked it into his doctrine of self-awareness and "knowledge by presence" (*ʿilm ḥuḍūrī*), rejecting it as a proof of the immaterial soul in Avicenna's sense. [source: SEP-Suhr §3.2]

### Internal senses
- Five external senses (after Aristotle) and five internal senses located in the brain's ventricles: common sense; retentive/formative imagination (*khayāl*, *muṣawwira*); compositive imagination (*mutakhayyila*; called cogitative, *mufakkira*, in humans when it works rationally); estimation (*wahm*, Latin *aestimatio*) — grasping non-sensible "intentions" (*maʿānī*), e.g. a sheep perceiving the wolf's hostility or the feelings toward a friend or child; and memory (*ḥāfiẓa*) with recollection. [sources: SEP-Mind §3; SEP-Sina §4; IEP-Avic §7] Estimation was Avicenna's innovation; Averroes later denied humans have it. [source: SEP-Infl]
- Wolfson coined the terms "retentive" and "compositive" imagination. [SEP-Mind note 30]

### The active intellect and intuition
- The human intellect starts as pure potentiality ("material intellect"); intelligibles are acquired from the active intellect ("acquired intellect", *al-ʿaql al-mustafād*), which thinks them eternally; the human intellect cannot "store" intelligibles; acquisition is "contact" (*ittiṣāl*) with the active intellect, the "divine effluence" (*al-fayḍ al-ilāhī*). [source: SEP-Sina §4]
- Gutas: "the 'flow' has nothing mystical about it"; Avicenna: "The active principle [i.e. the active intellect] lets flow upon the [human rational] soul form after form in accordance with the demand by the soul; and when the soul turns away from it … then the effluence is broken off" (*Shifāʾ*, *De anima* 245–246; transl. Gutas 2014a, 377). [source: SEP-Sina §4]
- Intuition (*ḥads*): the ability to hit suddenly upon the middle term of a syllogism (Gutas locates its source in Aristotle, *Posterior Analytics* 89b10–11); prophets have it to the highest degree and acquire knowledge "either at once or nearly so", "not an uncritical reception … merely on authority, but rather … in an order which includes the middle terms" (*De anima* 249–250, transl. Gutas 2014a, 183–184); the prophet's strong imagination puts this knowledge into images and language for the many. [sources: SEP-Sina §4; SEP-Mind §3; IEP-Avic §8]
- Mysticism debate: some Iranian interpreters read Avicenna as a mystic (lost *Eastern Philosophy*, last sections of the *Ishārāt*); Gutas "most vehement in his denial"; Rizvi (IEP) thinks he "flirts with the possibilities of mystical knowledge". [IEP-Avic §8]

## 12. Al-Ghazālī's verdict (brief, forward to next chapter)
- *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* discusses twenty teachings (16 metaphysical, 4 natural-scientific), aiming to show none is demonstrated. Three teachings of Avicenna are condemned as unbelief: (1) the world has no beginning; (2) God's knowledge includes only universals, not particulars; (3) souls never return to bodies (no bodily resurrection). A *fatwā* at the end declares anyone teaching these publicly an unbeliever (*kāfir*) and apostate "who can be killed". [source: SEP-Ghaz] Al-Ghazālī d. 1111 (IEP: c. 1056–1111). The full story belongs to "Al-Ghazālī, Averroes and the Quarrel over Reason".
- Al-Ghazālī's *Maqāṣid al-falāsifa* (*Doctrines/Goals of the Philosophers*) was based on Avicenna's Persian *Dāneshnāme*. [IEP-Avic §2]

## 13. Reception: Latin West and the later Avicennan tradition
- Latin translations of al-Kindī, the *Liber de causis*, al-Fārābī, Isaac Israeli, al-Ghazālī and Avicenna "assumed full pace in Toledo in the second half of the twelfth century" (Gerard of Cremona; Dominicus Gundisalvi); al-Fārābī's *Enumeration of the Sciences*, translated twice, may have served as a model for a translation programme; Gundisalvi worked with the Jewish scholar Avendauth on Avicenna's *De anima*. [source: SEP-Infl]
- *Avicenna Latinus*: the *Shifāʾ* sections were translated in 12th–13th-century Spain (Toledo and Burgos); some Latin manuscripts predate extant Arabic ones. Influence on William of Auvergne, Albert the Great, Aquinas. [IEP-Avic §3] Modern edition: Simone van Riet (e.g. *Liber de anima* 1972; *Liber de philosophia prima* 1977–1980). [IEP-Avic references]
- From about 1220 the whole Avicennian system of faculties (external and internal senses etc.) appears in Latin treatises and remains standard into the 1490s (*Margarita philosophica*). [SEP-Infl]
- "Augustinisme avicennisant" (Gilson 1926/27): scholastics who identified the active intellect with God on the authority of Avicenna and Augustine (Jean de la Rochelle, 1230s; Roger Bacon, Pecham…); criticised by Bonaventure and Aquinas. [source: SEP-Infl]
- Theses inspired by Avicenna were among those condemned at Paris in 1277. [SEP-Infl] (IEP says "Avicennism was later proscribed in 1210": see NOT VERIFIED.)
- The *Canon* remained a medical textbook in Europe into the early modern period (Padua). [IEP-Avic §3; SEP-Sina intro]
- **Islamic East:** "la pandémie avicennienne" (Michot); al-Shahrastānī (d. 1153) wrote *Struggling against the Philosophers*; the *Cure* and the *Pointers* became the basis of the madrasa philosophical curriculum, with commentaries "into the 20th century". [IEP-Avic intro, §9]
- **Suhrawardī** (c. 1154–1191): born in Suhraward, NW Iran; trained in Avicennan philosophy; founder of the "Illuminationist" (*ishrāqī*) tradition; *Philosophy of Illumination* completed 1186; executed at Aleppo at the end of 1191 (or early 1192) on charges of heresy; rejected the real distinction between essence and existence (existence a "merely mental consideration", *iʿtibār ʿaqlī*); ontology of lights; his works never reached Latin. [source: SEP-Suhr §1.1, §4.1, intro]
- **Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī** (1149–1210): born in Rayy; Ashʿarite theologian; first lemmatised commentary on an Arabic philosophical work (*Sharḥ al-Ishārāt*), plus a critical abridgement (*Lubāb al-Ishārāt*, 1201); unlike al-Ghazālī he did not treat the philosophers as apostates; dialectical method of arguing every side — Rūmī compared it to "tearing science apart like bread and feeding it to the birds"; huge Qur'an commentary *Mafātīḥ al-ghayb* (Ibn Taymiyya: it "contains everything apart from exegesis of the Quran"). [source: SEP-FDR §1]
- Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī's commentary on the *Ishārāt* defended Avicenna. [SEP-Sina §1.2 names Ṭūsī's commentary as standard]
- Mullā Ṣadrā (d. 1636/1641 — SEP-Rel gives 1636, IEP-Avic 1641) later modified Avicennan metaphysics (primacy and gradation of existence). [sources: SEP-Rel; IEP-Avic] (Periergia "What Is Philosophy?" has c. 1571–1636.)

## 15. Consistency with published Periergia chapters
- **Avicenna's birth date — contradiction between published chapters.** *Aristotle* gives "Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā, c. 970–1037)" (follows SEP-Gutas); History *The Rise of Islam and the Caliphates* gives "Ibn Sina (Avicenna, 980–1037)". Both have support (SEP: c. 970, 980 a miscalculation; IEP/WP: c. 980). WRITER: "c. 970 (traditionally 980)–1037" and name the disagreement.
- *Rise of Islam*: "al-Ma'mun (813–833) is credited with supporting a 'House of Wisdom' … Dimitri Gutas [has] argued that this was mainly a palace library, and that the movement was driven by a wide range of patrons and practical needs, not by one institution." Consistent with SEP-Greek and Gutas. Reading list there includes Gutas 1998: consistent.
- *Aristotle*: translation "much via Syriac"; "Hunayn ibn Ishaq (d. 873), his son and their associates made the whole corpus available" (SEP-Greek §4 title says "the complete Aristotelian corpus": consistent); "Arabic philosophers called Aristotle 'the First Teacher'" (SEP-Sina: *al-muʿallim al-awwal*: consistent); the *Theology of Aristotle* = adapted Plotinus: consistent.
- *Plotinus and Neoplatonism*: *Theology of Aristotle* reworked in al-Kindī's circle "probably in the 830s or 840s"; quotes al-Kindī "We must not be ashamed to admire the truth or to acquire it, from wherever it comes." attributed "(trans. Peter Adamson)". SEP-Kindi quotes this same wording (Adamson's entry; Adamson & Pormann 2012 is the published translation). Consistent. It also says Avicenna "doubted the *Theology*'s authenticity yet annotated it" (not re-checked here).
- *Plato*: al-Fārābī drew on *Republic* and *Laws*, unclear whether full translations existed, *Summary of Plato's Laws* "covering only the dialogue's opening books", "philosopher-prophet-ruler suited to a multi-ethnic, multi-religious empire": consistent with SEP-Farabi §7 (first eight books; Orwin 2017).
- *Augustine*: forward reference "Avicenna's 'flying man' … is another forerunner" of the cogito. Acceptable if this chapter names the debate over how deep the resemblance is (IEP vs WP "deep or trivial"); do not claim Descartes knew Avicenna.
- *History of Philosophy* overview: al-Fārābī (d. 950); Avicenna (d. 1037); essence/existence "what a thing is never explains *that* it is, except in one case"; Necessary Existent; flying man "a man created floating, senseless, would still affirm his own existence", "unmistakable family resemblance to Descartes's cogito six centuries later". All consistent with SEP/IEP; "unmistakable" is stronger than WP's "deep or trivial is disputed" — the chapter should present the resemblance and the dispute. The overview's "translation movement … (8th–10th c.)" is consistent.
- *What Is Philosophy?*: "Islamic philosophy is usually said to begin with al-Kindī in the ninth century … scholars debate how far it grew from the Greek inheritance and how far from Qur'anic wisdom": consistent with SEP-Greek; Mullā Ṣadrā "c. 1571–1636" (SEP-Rel d. 1636; IEP-Avic d. 1641: a source disagreement, not this chapter's problem).
- History *Central Asia*: Samanids "819–999", ruling from Samarkand then Bukhara (after 892), patrons of "the young Ibn Sina (Avicenna), born near Bukhara". SEP-Sina gives the Samanids "819–1005" (the dynasty's last remnants) and notes the Qarakhanid takeover of Bukhara in 999. Not a real contradiction: say "the Samanids, who lost Bukhara in 999".
- History *Islamic Commercial Revolution*: paper mills in Baghdad by the 790s (relevant to book culture); no conflicting claims about philosophers.

## Debates
- **Origins of falsafa**: Greek translation movement (most scholars) vs Qur'anic *ḥikma*. [SEP-Greek]
- **House of Wisdom**: university-like academy and translation centre (older view; popular accounts) vs a court library, not the institutional centre of Greek translation (SEP-Greek; Gutas 1998, 58–60; Balty-Guesdon, Micheau). [SEP-Greek; WP-House_of_Wisdom; WS-1]
- **Al-Kindī's eternity argument**: does the actual/potential infinite distinction defeat it? (Adamson: unclear whether al-Kindī's reply succeeds.) [SEP-Kindi]
- **Abū Bakr al-Rāzī**: freethinker who rejected prophecy (Urvoy, Stroumsa; pagan sympathies: Vallat) vs a philosopher who read the Qur'an as agreeing with him (Rashed). [SEP-Razi]
- **Al-Fārābī's ethnicity**: Persian (Rudolph 2017; SEP Druart) vs Turkic (Ibn Khallikān's late report) vs unknowable (Gutas). [SEP-Farabi; WP-Al-Farabi]
- **How to read al-Fārābī**: Straussian esoteric reading (Mahdi, Butterworth, Parens) vs critics; development (Janos) vs exoteric/esoteric. [SEP-Farabi]
- **Authorship of the *Harmonization of the Two Sages***: M. Rashed 2009 and Cherni 2015 against; Martini Bonadeo 2008 and Genequand 2012 for. [SEP-Farabi]
- **Significance of the Mattā–Sīrāfī debate**: important (Elamrani-Jamal, Endress, Mahdi) vs relatively insignificant (Frank, Street). [SEP-Lang note 6]
- **Avicenna's birth date**: c. 970 (Gutas: 980 a miscalculation) vs c. 980 (traditional; IEP, WP). [SEP-Sina; IEP-Avic]
- **Autobiography as literary device** (Gutas) vs straightforward record. [IEP-Avic]
- **Essence/existence**: real or conceptual distinction; status of pure quiddity (third realm vs definitional priority only). [SEP-Ess; SEP-SinaMet; IEP-Avic]
- **Proof of the Truthful**: ontological or cosmological (Mayer 2001's lists). [WP-Proof]
- **God's knowledge of particulars**: Marmura (God does not know all particulars individually) vs readings that he knows all things in a universal mode (Acar, Adamson, others). [WS-6; IEP-Ghaz]
- **Flying man**: question-begging/invalid (Marmura 1986; masked-man worry) vs premise-not-conclusion and a working thought experiment (Kaukua; Adamson & Benevich 2018). Resemblance to Descartes deep or superficial. [WS-3; IEP-Avic; WP-Floating_man]
- **Was Avicenna a mystic?** Gutas (no) vs Iranian interpreters/Corbin tradition; Rizvi in between. [IEP-Avic §8]

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use
- That Avicenna wrote the flying-man argument while imprisoned at Fardajān (WP-Floating_man states it with "Citation needed"; the argument opens the *Shifāʾ* psychology, a work whose writing spanned 1016–1027).
- "Read the *Metaphysics* forty times" and "bought al-Fārābī's booklet for three dirhams": only from WS-2 search summaries (an old, unnamed translation of the Autobiography); the IEP confirms only that al-Fārābī's treatise unlocked the *Metaphysics* for him.
- IEP-Avic's statement that "Avicennism was later proscribed in 1210" at Paris: the 1210 Sens/Paris ban targeted Aristotle's natural philosophy (and commentaries) per the Periergia *Aristotle* chapter; SEP-Infl mentions Avicenna-inspired theses condemned only in 1277. Do not say Avicenna was banned in 1210.
- That al-Kindī was born in Kufa (WP) or Basra (SEP): sources disagree; do not state either flatly.
- WP-House_of_Wisdom details (Ḥunayn "placed in charge" of the House of Wisdom; 116 works; departments; chief librarian Sahl ibn Hārūn; al-Ṭūsī rescuing 400,000 manuscripts): unreliable or contested.
- Al-Maʾmūn's dream of Aristotle (reported in WP-House_of_Wisdom from Ibn al-Nadīm): not checked against SEP/Gutas; if used, say "a story told by Ibn al-Nadīm", ideally with Gutas's reading of it as propaganda (not verified here).
- "Avicenna wrote 450 works" (WP): Gutas counts fewer than 100 authentic works; medieval lists nearly 300.
- That al-Fārābī was certainly Turkish (older reference works cited in WP, e.g. Braudel) or certainly Persian: debated.
- Exact wording "in a certain way, this is the very thing that is sought" (Avicenna, via WP from Adamson 2016): translation not seen.
- That Descartes read or was influenced by Avicenna's flying man: no evidence in sources read.
- That the Avicennan flying man is described as a "tanbīh" (reminder) rather than a proof: plausible but not read in a source.
- Any named woman philosopher of classical falsafa: none found.
- Abū Bakr al-Rāzī's death date 920 (SEP-FDR in passing) vs 925 (SEP-Razi, dedicated entry): use 925.
- Gutas's first edition of *Avicenna and the Aristotelian Tradition* = 1988: likely but not directly confirmed in a source read; cite the 2014 2nd edition.
