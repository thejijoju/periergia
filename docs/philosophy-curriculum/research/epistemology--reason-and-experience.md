## 1. The three distinctions and how they pair (the chapter's toolkit)

- **A priori / a posteriori** is *epistemological*: it concerns how, or on what basis, a proposition can be known or justifiably believed. A priori = justification "independent of experience" / from "pure thought or reason"; a posteriori = needs sense observation (or introspection). [source: IEP-AP §§1–2; SEP-AP §1]
- Standard qualification: a priori knowledge need not be independent of *all* experience; experience may be needed to grasp the concepts ("enabling" experience). Kant's own formula was "knowledge that is absolutely independent of all experience" (B3); SEP-AP calls that "too stringent" and prefers "independent of all experience beyond what is needed to grasp the relevant concepts". [source: SEP-AP §3]
- **Analytic / synthetic** is *logical or semantic*: what makes a proposition true. Kant's containment test: analytic if the predicate is "(covertly) contained" in the subject concept ("All bodies are extended"); synthetic if it "lies entirely outside" it ("All bodies are heavy") (*Critique of Pure Reason* B10–11). Kant's second test: an analytic judgment can be drawn out "in accordance with the principle of contradiction" (B11). Katz noted the two tests differ (the contradiction test includes all deductive consequences, e.g. "Bachelors are unmarried or the moon is blue"). [source: SEP-AS §1.1; IEP-AP §2]
- Kant did not invent the idea from nothing: there are precursors "in Leibniz, and in Locke and Hume in their talk of 'relations of ideas'". [source: SEP-AS §1.1]
- **Necessary / contingent** is *metaphysical*: a necessary truth is true in every possible world; a contingent one is true in some and false in others. [source: IEP-AP §3]
- **The traditional pairing** (a priori = analytic = necessary; a posteriori = synthetic = contingent): in some contexts "analytic truth", "necessary truth" and "a priori truth" were used interchangeably, especially by empiricists, who used analyticity to explain both necessity and a priori knowability. [source: IEP-Q intro]
- **Ways they come apart (worked examples for the chapter):**
  - Synthetic a priori (candidate): "if something is red all over then it is not green all over" — knowable by reflection, but "being green all over is not part of the definition of being red all over". [source: IEP-AP §2] Kant's own candidate: 7 + 5 = 12 (see §6). [source: SEP-AS §1.1]
  - Analytic but known a posteriori: believing "a cube has six sides" on a reliable person's testimony. [source: IEP-AP §2]
  - Contingent a priori: Kripke's standard metre (see §2). Necessary a posteriori: Hesperus = Phosphorus; water = H2O (see §2). [source: IEP-AP §3; SEP-RD §§1.1, 3.2]
  - Possibly unknowable truths (e.g. Goldbach's conjecture, cited by Kripke) show the distinctions are not even co-extensive in principle. [source: IEP-AP §2]
- A paired-examples table usable in the chapter (SEP-AP §1): "All bachelors are unmarried males" vs "All bachelors in the U.S. are taxed at a different rate"; "Green is a color" vs "Grass is green"; "2 + 2 = 4" vs "2 quarts of any liquid added to 2 more quarts of any liquid = 4 quarts" (false: water + carbon tetrachloride do not make four quarts, SEP-AP §3); "No object can be red and green all over at the same time" vs ripe tomatoes; "If A is taller than B and B than C, A is taller than C" vs a fact about basketball players. [source: SEP-AP §§1, 3]
- **A priori justification is fallible and defeasible (now the majority view in SEP-AP's survey):** Kant may have been a priori justified in "every event has a cause", which many physicists now reject; sorites paradoxes show we can be a priori justified in each of an inconsistent set of premises (Sosa; Bealer); a careful proof can be undercut by evidence that one is mad (Hawthorne). Kitcher (1983) and Putnam (1983) held stronger, indefeasible conceptions; SEP-AP: "It's hard to see how either view is defensible". [source: SEP-AP §3]

## 2. Kripke's worked examples (cross-ref *Analytic Philosophy since 1950*, which already states them)

- *Naming and Necessity*: three lectures at Princeton, 20, 22 and 29 January 1970; transcript first published 1972 in *Semantics of Natural Language* (ed. Donald Davidson and Gilbert Harman, Reidel); book 1980 (Harvard UP; Blackwell in UK). Kripke 1940–2022. [source: WP-Naming_and_Necessity; WP-Saul_Kripke; SEP-RD bibliography]
- Kripke coined "rigid designator": a term that designates the same object in every possible world in which it exists. [source: SEP-RD §1.1]
- **Necessary a posteriori, step by step (Hesperus/Phosphorus):** (1) "Hesperus" was the name for a heavenly body seen in the evening; "Phosphorus", unknown to its first users, named the same body seen in the morning: Venus. (2) Names are rigid, so each names Venus in every possible world. (3) Venus = Venus in every world. (4) So "Hesperus = Phosphorus" is true in every world: necessary. (5) Yet it was discovered empirically: a posteriori. The only sense in which it "might have turned out false" is epistemic, like an unproved theorem that "might or might not" be provable. Contrast the non-rigid "the brightest non-lunar object in the evening sky": "Hesperus = the brightest …" is true but contingent (Venus might have been obscured by cosmic dust). [source: SEP-RD §1.1]
- **Water = H2O** (and gold, rubies = Al2O3) as theoretical identities: widely held necessary but knowable only empirically; dissenters say the necessary truth is the general "the essence of a kind of matter is given by its basic elements", while the particular discovery is contingent. On the standard view the meaning of "water" is fixed by a reference-fixing description ("the stuff, whatever it is, that, in the actual world, quenches thirst, fills lakes …"), knowable a priori, while water's essence is discovered empirically. [source: SEP-AP §2; WP-Naming_and_Necessity; IEP-AP §3]
- **Contingent a priori, step by step (the standard metre):** (1) Someone fixes the reference of "one metre" as the length of stick S (in Paris) at time t0. (2) "One metre" is rigid: it names that length in every world. (3) "The length of S at t0" is not rigid: had S been heated or cooled, it would have had another length. (4) So "S is one metre long at t0" is contingent. (5) But the person who fixed the reference knows it without investigation: a priori. Kripke 1972: 274–275 (SEP-AP's citation). [source: SEP-RD §3.2; SEP-AP §2]
- **Objection:** "Many dispute that the examples really are examples of the contingent a priori" (Donnellan 1977; Plantinga 1974; Salmon 1987–88; Hughes 2004); Donnellan: even if genuine they are not "scary". Kripke defended them in a 2019 London address. Other candidates: John Turri's "The most unlikely possible event is not presently occurring" (2011); Gareth Evans's "If actually p, then p". [source: SEP-RD §3.2; SEP-AP §2]
- The contingent a priori "has not been as widely discussed as the necessary a posteriori". [source: SEP-RD §3.2]

## 3. Rationalism vs empiricism: what the dispute is, and the label

- The dispute "primarily concerns" the *sources* of concepts and knowledge, i.e. how far we depend on experience. [source: SEP-RE §1]
- **Three theses** (SEP-RE): **Intuition/Deduction** (some propositions in subject S are knowable by intuition alone, others by deduction from intuited ones); **Innate Knowledge** (we know some truths in S "as part of our nature"); **Innate Concept** (some concepts used in S are part of our rational nature). To be a rationalist is to adopt at least one of the innate theses (since most empiricists also accept Intuition/Deduction, restricted to relations among our own ideas). Two further rationalist theses: **Indispensability of Reason** and **Superiority of Reason**. The **Empiricism Thesis**: "We have no source of knowledge in S or for the concepts we use in S other than experience." [source: SEP-RE §§1, 1.1, 1.2]
- Rationalism and empiricism are relative to a subject area: one can be a rationalist about mathematics and an empiricist about physics; they conflict only about the same subject. [source: SEP-RE §1.2]
- Empiricism does not entail we *have* empirical knowledge: Hume accepted that experience cannot give knowledge of causation and concluded that it is not known but presupposed by habit. [source: SEP-RE §1.2]
- Two notions of experience: sense experience vs reflective experience (awareness of our own mental operations); rationalist critiques often ignore the second. Even the Cogito depends on "reflective, intuitive awareness" of occurrent thought. [source: SEP-RE intro]
- A third source, neither reason nor sense experience (e.g. divine revelation), shows the dichotomy is not exhaustive. [source: SEP-RE §1.2]
- **The label is a later construction (a debate; give the range):**
  - SEP-RE: "Descartes, Spinoza and Leibniz are the Continental Rationalists in opposition to Locke, Hume, and Reid, the British Empiricists. Such general classification schemes should only be adopted with great caution" (citing Loeb 1981 and Kenny 1986). Descartes had "empiricist leanings" in natural philosophy (Clarke 1982); Locke put reason on equal footing for moral truths (*Essay* 4.3.18) and accepted intuition/deduction for God's existence, mathematics and morality. "This distinction, initially applied by Kant, is responsible for giving us a very restrictive philosophical canon"; there is "no clear way of grouping Hobbes with either camp, let alone Elizabeth of Bohemia, Anne Conway, George Berkeley, Émilie du Châtelet, or Mary Shepherd". "Unless restricted to debates regarding the possibility of innate knowledge, this distinction is best left unused." [source: SEP-RE §1.2]
  - IEP-BE: "British Empiricism" and "Continental Rationalism" are "analysts', and not actors', categories": none of these thinkers used the terms of themselves; Berkeley was more critical of Locke than of Descartes; Leibniz said of Berkeley's *Principles* "[t]here is much here that is correct and close to my own view" (AG 307); "there is evidence that the contrast between these traditions, as we know it today, was invented in the 1850's by the German historian Kuno Fischer (1824–1907) (see Mercer 2020 … Loeb 2010, Norton 1981, Vanzo 2016)". [source: IEP-BE §1a]
  - Vanzo (*Journal of the History of Ideas* 77:2, 2016, 253–282), as summarised: Kant helped introduce the distinction; Reinhold had a historiographical framework built on it by the early 1790s; Tennemann fleshed it into a full history early in the nineteenth century; the narrative became standard in English-speaking philosophy only c. 1895–1915, not through any single author. [source: WS-2 (philarchive VANEAR-2 abstract; Vanzo's Otago blog)]
  - Critiques: Louis E. Loeb, *From Descartes to Hume: Continental Metaphysics and the Development of Modern Philosophy* (Cornell UP, 1981), attacking "the Standard Theory"; David Fate Norton, "The Myth of British Empiricism", *History of European Ideas* 1 (1981): 331–44. [source: SEP-RE bibliography (Loeb); WS-3 (Norton details)]
  - Writer note: say the labels are a nineteenth-century historians' grouping whose roots go back to Kant and his followers; sources disagree on who fixed it (Kant/Reinhold/Tennemann per Vanzo; Kuno Fischer in the 1850s per IEP-BE/Mercer). Do NOT credit Reid with the dichotomy (no source read supports it). Note SEP-RE's odd placement of Reid among the "British Empiricists" (Reid was a critic of the "way of ideas"; not checked here) — avoid listing Reid as an empiricist.
- Aristotle's place: Aristotelianism rejects Platonic nativism because it rejects the poverty-of-the-stimulus premise (forms are conveyed to the mind in perception); "it would obscure too many differences … to put Aristotle at the head of the Empiricist line". The "Peripatetic axiom" (nothing in the intellect not first in the senses) was shared by Aquinas, Hobbes (*Leviathan* 1.1), Locke and Amo; Arnauld rejected it. [source: SEP-IH §2; IEP-BE §§2.1, Amo section]

## 4. Plato's *Meno*: the first argument for innate knowledge (cross-ref *Plato*; it gives the bare episode)

- *Meno* written "probably about 385 B.C.E.", set in 402. [source: IEP-Meno §1]
- **Meno's paradox** (80d–e): we cannot learn either what we know (no need) or what we don't know (we couldn't recognise it). Plato calls it a "trick argument" (80e). [source: IEP-Meno; SEP-RE §3]
- **Recollection, as first stated (81a–e):** the soul is immortal, has learned everything in former lives; learning is remembering — introduced citing unnamed "priests and poets". [source: IEP-Meno]
- **The geometry lesson (82a/82b–85b), step by step:** (1) Socrates draws a square with 2-foot sides (area 4) and asks for the side of a square double its area (8). (2) The boy guesses the side should be doubled to 4 feet; shown that gives 16, too much. (3) He guesses 3 feet (area 9), still too much, and is at a loss ("numbed", which Socrates says has done him good, 84b–c). (4) Socrates adds three more squares to make a 4×4 square and draws the diagonals of the four small squares; the boy sees the inner square on the diagonals has area 8. (5) Socrates claims the boy has not been taught but has "recollected". [source: WP-Meno (citing 84b, 84c, 86a); IEP-Meno] Socrates' slogan "there is no teaching but recollection" (82a). [source: IEP-Meno]
- **Argument as reconstructed for the chapter:** P1 The boy was never taught geometry. P2 Asked only questions, he produced a true, necessary geometrical answer. P3 What is produced without being taught must already be in the learner. C1 The knowledge (or true belief) was already in him. P4 He did not acquire it in this life. C2 He acquired it before this life: learning is recollection. [built from SEP-RE §3; SEP-IH §2; IEP-Meno]
- **Objections:** (a) Socrates "clearly asks 'leading questions,' and eventually even shows the slave the answer in the form of a question (84e)" (IEP-Meno); sceptics see the questioning as "implicitly feeding the slave the right answers" (SEP-IH). (b) The lesson refutes the paradox's false dichotomy (there are states between full knowledge and pure ignorance) but "clearly does not demonstrate the reminding of something that was learned in a previous life" (IEP-Meno). (c) It does not say how the soul learned the theorem in the first place (SEP-RE §3). SEP-IH: the *Meno* "has remained famous as a pedagogical tour de force, but not as a compelling defense for the doctrine of recollection".
- **Reply / reinterpretation:** after the lesson Socrates restates the doctrine: the boy's mind is "always in a state of having learned" (86a), which turns recollection into "the beginnings of a theory of innate ideas"; the truth about reality is "always in our minds" (86b), which IEP-Meno calls "even roughly compatible with modern science". Socrates disclaims confidence in the details (86b–c), and the dialogue ends agreeing that men have "neither knowledge nor true belief" by nature (98c–d). [source: IEP-Meno]
- Plato is the "ur-Nativist"; the *Meno* introduces (i) the form of the **poverty of the stimulus argument** and (ii) the focus on mathematics; the *Phaedo* (equal sticks) argues the concept of equality cannot come from experience. Whitehead's remark that everything is anticipated in Plato is cited. [source: SEP-IH §2]
- Chomsky named the language-learning puzzle "Plato's problem" after the *Meno* (IEP-Ch says in *Aspects*, 1965; WP says the approach is outlined in ch. 1 of *Knowledge of Language*, 1986 — see Discrepancies). [source: IEP-Ch §UG; WP-Poverty_of_the_stimulus]

## 5. Early modern: Descartes, Leibniz, Locke, Hume (cross-ref each; give only the problem)

- **Descartes' three kinds of idea** (Third Meditation): "Among my ideas, some appear to be innate, some to be adventitious, and others to have been invented by me. My understanding of what a thing is, what truth is, and what thought is, seems to derive simply from my own nature. But my hearing a noise … or seeing the sun, or feeling the fire, comes from things which are located outside me … Lastly, sirens, hippogriffs and the like are my own invention" (AT VII 37–8; CSM II 26). [source: SEP-DI §3]
- Innate ideas recognised by scholars: God, (finite) mind, (indefinite) body; to Elisabeth (21 May 1643) he adds the union of mind and body, calling such "primitive notions" "the patterns on the basis of which we form all our other conceptions" (AT III 665; CSMK III 218). [source: SEP-DI §3]
- **Argument for the innate idea of God:** not adventitious (no sense experience of God); not invented (it represents an actual infinity, while the mind can only fabricate a potential infinity: AT VII 46–7); and the idea of the infinite is prior to the idea of the finite: "how could I understand that I doubted or desired … unless there were in me some idea of a more perfect being which enabled me to recognize my own defects by comparison" (Third Meditation). [source: SEP-DI §3; SEP-RE §4]
- **Innateness as a faculty:** to Hobbes (Third Replies): "when we say that an idea is innate in us, we do not mean that it is always there before us … We simply mean that we have within ourselves the faculty of summoning up the idea" (AT VII 189; CSM II 132); *Comments on a Certain Broadsheet* (1648) treats innateness as a faculty and argues even sensory ideas of colours and pains "must be all the more innate" (AT VIIIB 359; CSM I 304). He compares innate ideas to gout running in a family. [source: SEP-DI §3; SEP-IH §3]
- **The wax** (Second Meditation; cross-ref *Descartes*): the wax's sensible qualities all change by the fire, yet it is the same wax, so it is grasped by the mind, not the senses; "perception involves judgment"; mind better known than body. [source: PN-Desc §4 (IEP-D §4b; SEP-Ep §9.1)] Use only as a pointer.
- **Leibniz** (*New Essays on Human Understanding*, finished 1704, published 1765 [PN-Locke]): (a) argument from necessity — "the senses never give anything but instances, that is to say particular or individual truths … all the instances which confirm a general truth … are not sufficient to establish the universal necessity of this same truth … necessary truths, such as we find in pure mathematics … must have principles whose proof does not depend on instances … although without the senses it would never have occurred to us to think of them" (Preface; Morris & Parkinson trans. pp. 150–151). (b) The **veined marble**: against "the mind as a blank slate", "a block of marble whose veins already mark out the shape of Hercules" (Preface, RB 52 per SEP-IH); innate truths are a "disposition, an aptitude, a preformation, which determines our soul and brings it about that [necessary truths] are derivable from it" (A VI vi 80/RB 80). [source: SEP-RE §§2, 4; SEP-IH §3; SEP-Leib §6.3]
- Leibniz's argument as a premise list (SEP-RE §2): P1 We know some mathematical truths. P2 What we know there, we know to be necessarily true. P3 Experience cannot warrant beliefs about what is necessarily the case. C Experience cannot be the source; best explanation is intuition/deduction (or innateness). Leibniz extends it to logic, metaphysics and morals (experience gives "is", not "ought"). Strength varies: weak for controversial metaphysics, "clearly has legs" for mathematics and some moral truths. [source: SEP-RE §2]
- **Locke (Essay Book I; cross-ref *Locke*, which sets out the argument fully):** Book I argues there is no innate knowledge; "No proposition can be said to be in the mind, which it never yet knew, which it never yet was conscious of" (*Essay* 1.2.5); children and "idiots" "have not the least apprehension or thought of" "What is, is" or "It is impossible for the same thing to be and not to be" (I.2.5); "It seems to me a near Contradiction to say that there are truths imprinted on the Soul, which it perceives or understands not". **Locke's dilemma** (SEP-RE §3): a narrow reading of "innate" (consciously known) faces counterexamples; a generous reading (capacity to know) makes all knowledge innate: "this great point will amount to no more, but only an improper way of speaking" (1.2.5). Locke treated innateness as an empirical hypothesis lacking evidence. [source: SEP-RE §3; SEP-Locke §2.1]
- **"White paper", not "tabula rasa":** *Essay* II.1.2 likens the mind to "white paper"; the *Essay* does not use "tabula rasa" (a later label; SEP-Locke itself glosses "tabula rasa or blank sheet"). Full sentence ("Let us then suppose the Mind to be, as we say, white Paper, void of all Characters, without any Ideas; How comes it to be furnished?") is supported only by WS-5 and PN-Locke; quote only "white paper". [source: WP-Tabula_rasa; SEP-Locke §2; PN-Locke §4; WS-5]
- Locke still held we are born with "a variety of faculties to receive and abilities to manipulate or process the content". [source: SEP-Locke §2]
- **Modern reply to Locke (Carruthers 1992):** innate knowledge = knowledge "innately determined to make its appearance at some stage in childhood" (p. 51), fixed by evolution; children haven't reached the stage; in cognitively impaired people development has broken down. Warrant via reliabilism: "Innate beliefs will count as known provided that the process through which they come to be innate is a reliable one" (p. 77). **Residual objection (SEP-RE's verdict):** the rationalist still owes a principled difference between innate and non-innate knowledge (both are triggered by experience; both contingently reliable), so "Locke's criticism … still stands, in the face of the best rationalist defense". [source: SEP-RE §3]
- **Empiricist account of concepts and its troubles:** Locke: simple ideas passively received in sensation/reflection, complex ones built from them; Hume's own counterexample, the **missing shade of blue** (*Enquiry* 2): a person who has seen every shade but one can imagine the missing one. Carruthers: shades of red share no common feature, so "red" can't be abstracted; abstraction presupposes the concept; Locke's account of the idea of cause (*Essay* 2.26.1) seems circular. [source: SEP-RE §4]
- **Hume's fork** (cross-ref *Berkeley and Hume*): "All the objects of human reason or inquiry may naturally be divided into two kinds, to wit, 'Relations of Ideas,' and 'Matters of Fact.' … Propositions of this kind are discoverable by the mere operation of thought, without dependence on what is anywhere existent in the universe. Though there never were a circle or triangle in nature, the truths demonstrated by Euclid would forever retain their certainty and evidence. … The contrary of every matter of fact is still possible" (*Enquiry* 4.1). Morals: "Beauty, whether moral or natural, is felt more properly than perceived" (12.3). [source: SEP-RE §2]
- The fork as the "classic empiricist response": mathematics and logic are known by intuition and deduction but are "not substantive knowledge of the external world", only of relations of our ideas. [source: SEP-RE §2]

## 6. Kant's synthesis (cross-ref *Kant*; the chapter already has the argument)

- Kant takes synthetic a priori knowledge as given and asks how it is possible; answer: the structure of experience is our contribution (forms of intuition, space and time; categories), so such knowledge holds of appearances, not things in themselves. SEP-IH's analogy: the pixel grid a digital camera imposes on every photograph. Samet: "In my view, Kant is a Nativist", though "critical of Rationalist versions of the Innateness doctrine at every turn". [source: SEP-IH §5]
- **7 + 5 = 12 (B15–16):** "We might, indeed, at first suppose that the proposition 7 + 5 = 12 is a merely analytical proposition … The concept of 12 is by no means already thought in merely thinking this union of 7 and 5; and I may analyze my concept of such a possible sum as long as I please, still I shall never find the 12 in it" (Kemp Smith trans., quoted in SEP-Log at B16). (The *Kant* chapter quotes the Guyer–Wood version.) For Kant arithmetic's apriority comes from the pure form of time; Euclidean geometry's from the pure form of space. [source: SEP-Log §1; SEP-AS §1.1]
- Copernican passage (B xvi) and the fact that Kant never used the phrase "Copernican revolution": already in *Kant*. [source: PN-Kant §2]
- B1 ("all our cognition begins with experience" but not all "arises from" it): verified in PN-Kant §3 only (not re-read here).

## 7. Mathematics and logic: where does that knowledge come from? (the chapter's second test case)

- **Mill's empiricism** (*A System of Logic*, 1843): mathematical propositions assert genuine facts; "there is in every step of arithmetical or algebraical calculation a real induction, a real inference of facts from facts" (*System*, CW VII: 254); 2 + 1 = 3 is established by generalisation from instances (two pebbles and one pebble make three, two horses and one horse …); "All numbers must be numbers of something: there are no such things as numbers in the abstract" (VII: 254); geometry's points and lines are idealisations of experienced space. Mill aimed to drive the a priori school "from its stronghold" (*Autobiography*). He accepted the consequence that there may be no necessary truths. [source: SEP-Mill §3.4]
- Frege mocked such accounts as "gingerbread and pebble" arithmetic (*Grundlagen* 1884, vii). [source: SEP-Q §3.2]
- **Frege's logicism**: arithmetic is analytic, not synthetic; Frege's analyticity concerns justification — trace a proof back to primitive truths; if only "general logical laws and … definitions" are reached, the truth is analytic (*Grundlagen* §3). So 7 + 5 = 12 comes out analytic for a Fregean but not for Kant. Frege held geometry synthetic a priori and outside logicism. Russell's letter of 16 June 1902 revealed the contradiction in Basic Law V. [source: SEP-Log §1; SEP-Frege]
- **Logical positivists (cross-ref *Logical Positivism and Its Critics*):** "There can be no a priori knowledge of reality. For … the truths of pure reason, the propositions which we know to be valid independently of all experience, are so only in virtue of their lack of factual content" (Ayer, *Language, Truth and Logic*, 1952 Dover edn, pp. 86, 93–94). Every meaningful sentence is either a tautology or empirically verifiable. [source: SEP-RE §2] The program (Schlick, Ayer, Carnap) used Frege's logic to derive mathematics from logic plus synonymy and to analyse empirical claims into confirming experiences. [source: SEP-AS §2.2]
- **Objection to the positivist view (BonJour; IEP-AP §4):** analytic-only accounts cannot cover all a priori claims (transitivity, red/green); and if "analytic" is stretched to cover them, grasping them still seems to need something like rational insight: seeing 7 + 5 = 12 "does not amount to grasping the definitions of the relevant terms". [source: IEP-AP §4]
- **Philosophy of mathematics in brief:** the three early-twentieth-century schools were logicism, formalism and intuitionism. **Intuitionism** (L. E. J. Brouwer; Kant-inspired): mathematics is mental construction; rejects the completed infinite and non-constructive existence proofs, hence the unrestricted law of excluded middle. **Formalism** (David Hilbert): numbers can be taken to be symbols (strokes |, ||); Hilbert's program to prove consistency by finitary means was undercut by Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931). **Platonism** (Gödel): mathematical objects and concepts exist objectively, outside space and time; we have a quasi-perceptual "mathematical intuition", fallible and trainable (Frege's Basic Law V shows it can mislead). [source: SEP-PM §§2.2–2.3, 3.1]
- **Benacerraf's dilemma ("Mathematical Truth", *Journal of Philosophy* 70 (1973): 661–679) — worked example:** two constraints: **Semantic** — the account of mathematical truth must fit "a homogeneous semantical theory in which semantics for the propositions of mathematics parallel the semantics for the rest of the language" (p. 661); **Epistemological** — it must "mesh with a reasonable epistemology" (p. 661). Horn 1: accounts that treat mathematical sentences like others (platonism: "2 + 3 = 5" is about objects called numbers) satisfy the first "at the cost of leaving it unintelligible how we can have any mathematical knowledge whatsoever" (p. 662). Horn 2: "combinatorial" accounts (truth = provability from axioms) make knowledge easy but fail "to connect these conditions with any analysis of the sentences which shows how the assigned conditions are conditions of their truth" (p. 662). Benacerraf: "almost all accounts … can be identified with serving one or another of these masters at the expense of the other" (pp. 661–2). [source: IEP-Ben §1; WP-Paul_Benacerraf]
- Benacerraf's epistemological premise originally assumed the causal theory of knowledge (abstract objects cannot cause our beliefs). **Objection:** few now accept the causal theory. **Reply:** the problem is "remarkably robust": under reliabilism it becomes explaining how our beliefs about abstracta are reliable; Hartry Field (1989) restated it without any theory of knowledge, as a demand to explain the reliability of mathematicians' beliefs; Field's version is widely seen as the improvement (Burgess and Rosen 1997; Liggins 2010), though not universally (Kasa 2010; Nutting 2020). Field himself is a fictionalist. "Plenitudinous platonism" (Balaguer 1998; Linsky and Zalta 1995) answers that every consistent theory truly describes some mathematical objects. [source: SEP-PM §§3.4–3.5; IEP-Ben §3]
- Benacerraf: Paris-born 26 March 1930, died 13 January 2025; taught at Princeton from 1960. Not to be confused with his 1965 "What Numbers Could Not Be" problem. [source: WP-Paul_Benacerraf; IEP-Ben intro]
- The same worry applies to rational intuition generally (SEP-RE §2): "That the number three is prime does not appear to cause anything, let alone our intuition that it is prime"; Huemer (2005: 123) on moral intuitionism: the challenge is "to explain how it would be anything more than chance if my moral beliefs were true, given that I do not interact with moral properties". [source: SEP-RE §2]

## 8. Quine's "Two Dogmas" and the reply (ARGUMENT; cross-ref *Logical Positivism and Its Critics*, which already sets it out — keep shorter here and use it to ask whether there is any a priori)

- Read at the APA's annual meeting in December 1950; published *Philosophical Review* 60 (1951): 20–43; reprinted in *From a Logical Point of View* (1953), pp. 20–46. Quine 1908–2000. Quine had doubted the distinction privately since at least 1933 (Carnap's diary, 31 March 1933). Morton White's "The Analytic and the Synthetic: An Untenable Dualism" appeared first and is footnoted by Quine. [source: IEP-Q §§1–2; SEP-Q bibliography]
- The two dogmas: (i) a fundamental cleavage between analytic and synthetic truths; (ii) **reductionism**, "the belief that each meaningful statement is equivalent to some logical construct upon terms which refer to immediate experience" / that each statement "taken in isolation from its fellows, can admit of confirmation or infirmation at all". [source: WP-Two_Dogmas; SEP-AS §3.4]
- **The circularity argument, step by step:** (1) Analytic = "true by virtue of meanings"; meanings are obscure ("meaning is what essence becomes when it is divorced from the object of reference and wedded to the word"). (2) Two classes: logical truths ("No unmarried man is married") and those turned into logical truths by substituting synonyms ("No bachelor is married"). The second class needs **synonymy**. (3) Definition? Except for pure stipulative abbreviation, "definition rests on synonymy rather than explaining it". (4) Interchangeability *salva veritate*? In an extensional language it yields only co-extension ("creature with a heart"/"creature with a kidney"); it works only in a language with "necessarily", but that adverb makes sense only if "we have already made satisfactory sense of 'analytic'". (5) Carnap's semantical rules for an artificial language L0? They either use the word "analytic" unexplained or pick out an arbitrary class; "we might just stop tugging at our bootstraps altogether". (C) Every explanation presupposes analyticity or a notion in its circle: analyticity, synonymy, definition, necessity, semantic rule form a "closed curve in space" (p. 30). So: "a boundary between analytic and synthetic statements simply has not been drawn. That there is such a distinction to be drawn at all is an unempirical dogma of empiricists, a metaphysical article of faith." [source: IEP-Q §2; WP-Two_Dogmas; SEP-AS §3.5]
- **Holism**: "Our statements about the external world face the tribunal of sense experience not individually, but only as a corporate body" (1953, p. 41; credited to Duhem); "any statement can be held true come what may, if we make drastic enough adjustments elsewhere in the system" (1953, 43); "no statement is immune to revision" (1951, 43), including logic (Quine mentions revising excluded middle to simplify quantum mechanics: WP; and the shifts by which "Kepler superseded Ptolemy, or Einstein Newton, or Darwin Aristotle": WS-1 only). [source: SEP-AS §3.4; SEP-Duhem; SEP-Q §3.3; WP-Two_Dogmas]
- **Quine's positive account of the apparent a priori:** logic and arithmetic sit near the centre of our web of belief; abandoning arithmetic would mean abandoning our whole system, which is "unimaginable", so it *appears* necessary; no particular experience confirms 2 + 2 = 4, so it *appears* a priori; but it rests on "our experience as a whole". [source: SEP-Q §3.2] ("Web of belief" is Quine and Ullian's later book title, not a phrase of "Two Dogmas": PN-LP.)
- **Objection — Grice and Strawson, "In Defense of a Dogma", *Philosophical Review* 65(2) (April 1956): 141–58:** (a) people agree in classifying indefinitely many new cases as analytic ("Lawyers are attorneys", "A fortnight is a period of fourteen days"), so it is "unlikely that so intuitively plausible a distinction should turn out to have no basis at all in fact"; (b) Quine's scepticism about synonymy implies scepticism about meaning: if it makes sense to ask "What does it mean?", two sentences are synonymous iff the true answer for one is the true answer for the other; and talk of correct translation would become impossible. [source: SEP-AS §3.5; SEP-Q §3.3; WP-Two_Dogmas]
- **Further objections:** Rey (SEP-AS): Quine "offers no argument" for extending holism to mathematics and logic; there have been "virtually no revisions of mathematics on empirical grounds"; Einstein's adoption of Riemannian geometry is misread (non-Euclidean geometries were conceptual developments; only their application to physical space was empirical); Bayesian confirmation theory itself presupposes logic and probability with probability 1. Quine himself noted unrevisable beliefs that are not analytic ("There have been black dogs") and trivial analytic ones that are not central. Putnam (1965) also held the distinction must have some basis. [source: SEP-AS §§3.4–3.6]
- **Replies on Quine's behalf:** scepticism about *meanings* is not scepticism about *meaningfulness*; Quine's standard of clarity is naturalistic explanation (Lugg 2012). Later Quine accepted a modest analyticity: a sentence is analytic for a speaker "if he learned the truth of the sentence by learning to use one or more of its words" (1991, 270), so "Bachelors are unmarried" and perhaps first-order logic qualify, but not mathematics; "I recognize the notion of analyticity in its obvious and useful but epistemologically insignificant applications" (1991, 271). [source: SEP-Q §3.3]
- Influence: Putnam (1968) argued for revising elementary logic in light of quantum mechanics; Putnam (1962) imagined discovering that cats are robots controlled from Mars. Peter Godfrey-Smith: "sometimes regarded as the most important in all of twentieth-century philosophy". Carnap's reply "Quine on Analyticity" was not published until 1990. [source: SEP-AS §3.4; WP-Two_Dogmas]
- Kripke's separation of a priori, analytic and necessary (§2) is one reason the debate moved on; Soames argues Quine's circularity argument needs two positivist theses (all necessary and a priori truths are analytic; analyticity is needed to explain necessity) which are now "antiquated". [source: WP-Two_Dogmas]

## 9. BonJour and moderate rationalism (contemporary defence of the a priori)

- Laurence BonJour (b. 31 Aug 1943; emeritus, University of Washington); *The Structure of Empirical Knowledge* (Harvard, 1985) defended coherentism; *In Defense of Pure Reason: A Rationalist Account of A Priori Justification* (Cambridge UP, 1998); earlier "Toward a Moderate Rationalism", *Philosophical Topics* 23 (1995). [source: WP-Laurence_BonJour; SEP-AP bibliography]
- A **rational insight** is "an immediate, non-inferential grasp, apprehension, or 'seeing' that some proposition is necessarily true" (1998: 106); what justifies is the **apparent** rational insight, so a priori justification is fallible and defeasible (1998: 112–113) — hence "moderate" rationalism. After Boghossian's comments (2001) BonJour said these appearances are non-propositional, more like perceptual sensations. Errors are corrected internally by further reflection and coherence (1998: 116–119). [source: SEP-AP §4.4, §6.3]
- BonJour sorts views into Quine's radical empiricism, moderate empiricism (a priori = analytic) and rationalism, and argues that only moderate rationalism avoids scepticism: radical empiricism leaves no way to justify going beyond direct observation (including induction), and moderate empiricism tacitly presupposes rational insight. [source: WS-4 (Cambridge chapter list; reviews) — paraphrase only; IEP-AP §4 for the objections to moderate empiricism]
- **Argument for the chapter (BonJour-style, built from IEP-AP §4 and SEP-RE §2):** P1 We are justified in some beliefs that go beyond what we have observed (predictions, generalisations, mathematics). P2 Such justification requires an inference whose acceptability is not itself observed. P3 If no inference can be justified a priori, none of these beliefs is justified (scepticism). C Unless we accept scepticism, some justification is a priori. **Objection:** the "crystal ball" worry — what can intuitions be checked against, except other intuitions? (Cummins 1998); naturalists (Devitt 2005–6) deny any a priori. **Reply:** perception and memory too can only be checked against themselves (Goldman 2007), yet justify; critics answer that different senses check each other (Weatherson 2003). [source: SEP-AP §6.3; IEP-AP §4]
- Other recent rationalists/positions: Bealer (rational intuitions as intellectual seemings; 1998), Huemer's phenomenal conservatism (2001, 2005: "an intuition that p is a state of its seeming to one that p that is not dependent on inference … and that results from thinking about p"), Casullo (*A Priori Justification*, OUP 2003), Kitcher (*The Nature of Mathematical Knowledge*, OUP 1983) as an empiricist about mathematics. [source: SEP-RE §2; SEP-AP bibliography]

## 10. Chomsky and the modern nativism debate (ARGUMENT)

- Noam Chomsky (b. 1928, Philadelphia). Review of B. F. Skinner's *Verbal Behavior* (1957) published in *Language*, 1959 — "has actually become far better known than the original book"; regarded as a founding document of cognitive psychology. *Syntactic Structures* 1957; *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (MIT Press, May 1965) introduced deep/surface structure and competence/performance. [source: IEP-Ch; WP-Verbal_Behavior; WP-Aspects_of_the_Theory_of_Syntax]
- Chomsky called his view a "rationalist conception of the nature of language" (1975, p. 129) and cites Plato, Descartes and Leibniz. [source: SEP-RE §3; IEP-Ch]
- The phrase "poverty of the stimulus" was coined by Chomsky in 1980 (WP); the SEP-IL treats the fullest discussions as Chomsky 1968, 1980, 1986. [source: WP-Poverty_of_the_stimulus; SEP-IL §2]
- **The POS argument schema (SEP-IL §2):** 1. Children acquire linguistic target T. 2. Children are exposed to primary linguistic data (PLD). 3. The child has domain-general learning mechanisms LM. 4. T is not acquirable from PLD and LM. IC. So some factors relevant to acquiring T are innate. (A further step is needed to show the innate factor is *specific to language*.) [source: SEP-IL §2]
- **Worked example: question formation and structure dependence** (Chomsky 1975: 30–33): "The man is tall" → "Is the man tall?". Candidate rules: (i) front any auxiliary; (ii) front the first auxiliary; (iii) front the main auxiliary. "The man who is tall is in the room" → "Is the man who is tall in the room?", not "*Is the man who tall is in the room?". Rule (ii) predicts the ungrammatical one; (i) allows both; only (iii), stated over structure not word order, is right. Children never seem to try (ii); POS: they rarely if ever hear the decisive sentences, so the bias toward structure-dependent rules is innate. [source: SEP-IL §2]
- **Objection — Pullum and Scholz, "Empirical assessment of stimulus poverty arguments", *The Linguistic Review* 19 (2002): 9–50:** nativists must establish empirically how poor the stimulus is; in a child-directed corpus they found sentences like "Where's the other dolly that was in here?" (main auxiliary fronted past an embedded one). [source: SEP-IL §6.1; WP-Poverty_of_the_stimulus (volume given as 19 in one citation, 18 in another; DOI is tlir.19.1-2.9, so 19)]
- **Replies:** Legate and Yang (2002): such sentences are about 0.05% of the corpus, so a child might never meet them, and a learner sensitive enough to learn from so few would also learn from noise. Lasnik and Lidz (2016): positive evidence that the main auxiliary *can* move doesn't show it *must*; the impossibility can't be learned from positive evidence alone. Counter: that is a different POS argument, and general learning processes such as pre-emption might do the work. [source: SEP-IL §6.1]
- **Empiricist alternatives:** statistical learning (Saffran, Aslin and Newport 1996: infants segment words from transitional probabilities). **Connectionism**: the Rumelhart and McClelland (1986) past-tense network vs Pinker and Prince's (1988) critique; SEP-IL: that debate "was not strictly about innateness" but about the format of rules; even if early networks failed, "it doesn't follow that such rules must be innate". Elman et al. (1996) and Griffiths doubt the innate/acquired distinction itself. [source: SEP-IL §§4.5, 6.2; SEP-Con §3.3]
- **Large language models (hedged):** LLMs have no language-specific innate biases yet behave like competent speakers, so some read them as a "possibility proof" against strong nativism (e.g. Piantadosi 2024, "Modern Language Models Refute Chomsky's Approach to Language"); but they show only learnability in principle, and they train on "tens of trillions of tokens" while children hear roughly 2–7 million words a year (Gilkerson et al. 2017) — "poor examples" against the human POS argument. Ablation experiments (Warstadt and Bowman 2022; Misra and Mahowald 2024) and small, child-scale models are the more relevant tests. [source: SEP-IL supplement A.1]
- **Is Chomsky a rationalist in the traditional sense? (debate):** SEP-RE: Chomsky's learners "do not know particular propositions describing a universal grammar"; he offers "a theory of innate learning capacities or structures rather than a theory of innate knowledge"; Cottingham (1984: 124): it is "by no means clear that Chomsky is correct in seeing his theory as following the traditional rationalist account"; capacity-nativism is "akin to an empiricist take" (Locke and Reid also posited inborn faculties). IEP-Ch: even the rationalists Chomsky cites did not treat innate and learned as exclusive (Leibniz: arithmetic is "in us" yet learned by demonstration). [source: SEP-RE §3; IEP-Ch]
- **Fodor's concept nativism:** Jerry Fodor (1935–2017), *The Language of Thought* (1975) and a 1981 paper (in *RePresentations*): all concept learning is hypothesis testing; the hypothesis must already use the concept to be learned; so the concept must be available beforehand. 1981: only complex concepts can be assembled from constituents; lexical concepts lack structure; so "virtually all lexical concepts must be innate" — **radical concept nativism** (e.g. even CARBURETOR, per the standard illustration: not verified here). "Few if any have endorsed Fodor's radical conclusions"; replies construct accounts of learning primitive concepts (Margolis 1998; Laurence and Margolis 2002; Carey 2009). Fodor 2008 changed his view: no concepts are *learned*, but they need not be innate; they are acquired by "merely biological" processes. Objection: culturally specific concepts like PURGATORY are acquired via psychological processes (Margolis and Laurence 2011). [source: SEP-Con §3.3; WP-Jerry_Fodor]

## 11. Developmental psychology: core knowledge (brief and hedged)

- "Core knowledge" / "core cognition": a post-Piagetian approach (Spelke 1994, 2003, 2022; Baillargeon; Wynn 1998; Carey 2009). Carey: three evolved systems (objects, agents, number), domain-specific and encapsulated like Fodor's modules, using innate concepts but supporting learning. Spelke, *What Babies Know, Volume 1: Core Knowledge and Composition* (OUP, 2022): six systems — object, number, place, form, agent, social being; language lets us combine their outputs. [source: SEP-IC §core cognition]
- Classic experiment (Baillargeon, Spelke and Wasserman 1985): 4-month-olds habituated to a screen rotating 180°; then a block is placed behind it; infants look longer when the screen rotates through the space the block occupies (block secretly removed) — read as an expectation that objects persist, i.e. "a poverty of the stimulus argument for an innate concept of object". [source: SEP-IC]
- Hedge: these are interpretations of looking-time data; the "mixed economy" view (innate core plus learning) faces Prinz's (2012) simplicity objection, revived from Locke: a theory with one acquisition mechanism is simpler than one with two. [source: SEP-IC]
- Cain: core cognition's benefits are that it handles abstract concepts, fits evolution, and allows flexibility. Do not present it as settled. [source: SEP-IC]

## 12. India (cross-ref *The Classical Indian Schools*, *Mahāyāna Philosophy*, *Jains, Ājīvikas and Materialists*)

- The organising question differs: classical Indian epistemology (*pramāṇa-śāstra*) asks which **pramāṇas** (knowledge sources) there are, rather than "reason vs experience" as such. [source: SEP-EI intro]
- **Cārvāka** accepts perception only: inference depends on generalisations "which outstrip perceptual evidence"; testimony presupposes a truthful speaker and fails the same way (as reported in Mādhava's *Sarva-darśana-saṃgraha*). Standard reply is pragmatic: the sceptic relies on generalisation every time he uses words with repeatable meanings (Gaṅgeśa). The Cārvāka problem of induction was turned by Buddhists and Naiyāyikas into fallibilism about inference. [source: SEP-EI §2]
- **Nyāya: four pramāṇas** — perception, inference, analogy/comparison (*upamāna*), testimony. Nyāya restricts *upamāna* to learning what a word refers to: told by a forester that a *gavaya* (a wild ox/buffalo) is "like a cow", a person later meeting one judges "This, which is similar to a cow, is the meaning of the word 'gavaya'" (Nyāya-sūtra 1.1.8, Vātsyāyana; Gaṅgeśa). Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta use analogy as the pramāṇa for knowledge of similarity; Buddhists (Yogācāra) don't count it as separate. [source: SEP-EI §§4, 7.1]
- **Nyāya on innate ideas:** "the mind or self does not, according to the realists, have any innate ideas (unlike then Yogācāra, which postulates a collective 'storehouse consciousness', ālaya-vijñāna). Concepts are the records of previous experiences"; "the classificational power of the mind … is not innate … but is rather the product of presentational experience (anubhava)". A striking empiricist moment: worth a sentence. [source: SEP-EI §4]
- **Buddhist epistemologists Dignāga (c. 480–c. 540 CE) and Dharmakīrti (early 7th c.)**: "just as there are only two kinds of objects of knowledge, real particulars and fictional universals, there are only two … sources of knowledge (pramāṇa), viz., 'perception' (pratyakṣa) and 'inference' (anumāna)"; perception is non-conceptual, directly caused by particulars; conceptual thought "superimposes" universals and is in an important way "distorted" (*bhrānta*). Testimony is treated as a kind of inference (published *Classical Indian Schools*). [source: SEP-Dh §2; WP-Dignāga]
- Dharmakīrti's "internal pervasion" (*antar-vyāpti*) in inferences from essential nature can be thought of as "similar to the a priori of Western philosophy", but SEP-EI warns it is "actually a technical point" about the inductive base. Use with that caveat. [source: SEP-EI §5]
- **Mīmāṃsā:** the Veda is authorless (*apauruṣeya*), "primordial", heard and memorised by rishis; so it lacks the faults of human speakers; Nyāya theists instead make God its author (Udayana, 11th c.). **Intrinsic validity** (*svataḥ prāmāṇya*): cognitions are self-certifying, defeated only by contrary cognition or a fault in their causes; Prābhākara (from late 7th c.) and Bhāṭṭa (Kumārila) versions differ. Already set out in *Classical Indian Schools* (with Arnold's translation of Kumārila). Use here to show a third answer to "where does knowledge come from": a source neither reason nor ordinary experience. [source: SEP-EI §§2, 6]

## 13. The Islamic world (cross-ref *Falsafa* and *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes and the Quarrel over Reason*)

- **Avicenna (Ibn Sīnā)**: the human intellect at birth is "rather like a tabula rasa, a pure potentiality that is actualized through education"; knowledge comes through empirical familiarity with objects from which universals are abstracted (IEP-Av). But intelligibles are *acquired from* the active intellect, "the locus of all intelligibles", in a "contact" (*ittiṣāl*) when the prepared intellect hits upon the middle term of a syllogism (his theory of *ḥads*, "hitting upon" the middle term, from Aristotle's *Posterior Analytics* I.34 *eustochia*); Gutas: this "flow" (*fayḍ*) "has nothing mystical about it"; "The active principle lets flow upon the [human rational] soul form after form in accordance with the demand by the soul" (*Shifāʾ*, De anima 245–246, trans. Gutas). [source: SEP-IS; IEP-Av]
- **The flying (floating) man** (cross-ref *Falsafa*, which sets it out with objections): a person created all at once, sight veiled, floating so that nothing is felt, would still affirm his own existence; Avicenna takes this to show the soul is an independent intellectual substance knowable without sense experience. IEP-Av: it "in many ways prefigures by 600 years the Cartesian cogito"; no source shows Descartes knew it. [source: SEP-AIM; IEP-Av; PN-Falsafa §11]
- Teaching point: Avicenna is empiricist about the *content* of concepts (abstraction from sense) but holds that one item of self-knowledge is independent of sense — another thinker who fits neither label.
- **Al-Ghazālī**: his *Deliverance from Error* doubts sense, then the "primary truths" of reason (via the dream argument), and is cured by "a light which God Most High cast into my breast" (McCarthy trans.). Already in *Scepticism and the Answers to It*; mention only as a third route (illumination). [source: scepticism notes §6 (IEP-Gh), not re-read]

## 14. China (cross-ref *Confucius, Mencius and Xunzi*; *Beyond Europe*)

- Mencius (c. 371–c. 289 BCE): all humans have "innate but incipient tendencies toward benevolence, righteousness, wisdom, and propriety", called "sprouts" (2A6); the child-at-the-well thought experiment (2A6, Van Norden 2008: 46): "anyone in such a situation would have a feeling of alarm and compassion—not because one sought to get in good with the child's parents …". Sprouts must be "extended" (*tui*) or "filled out" (7B31). [source: SEP-Men; WP-Mencius]
- **Liangzhi** (Mencius 7A15): "Among babes in arms there are none that do not know to love their parents"; this is "pure knowing" which humans "know without pondering" (Tiwald and Van Norden 2014: 218); paired with "capable without learning". Lu Xiangshan adopted the phrase; **Wang Yangming** (1472–1529) made it central: "The mind is Pattern. Is there any affair outside the mind?"; "extending knowledge" is "simply to extend fully the 'pure knowing' of my own mind" (Tiwald and Van Norden 2014: 248). Nivison: Wang's "extending" is "more like extending one's arm than … extending one's vocabulary". Translations: "pure knowing", "innate knowledge", "conscience". [source: SEP-WY §§2–4; WP-Wang_Yangming]
- Xunzi (c. 310–after 238 BCE): "Human *xing* is evil; what is good is artifice" (23.1a, Goldin's trans. in SEP); *xing* = "What is so by birth" (22.1b); "evil" is close to "detestable", some prefer "bad". Both thinkers held all humans *can* become good (Graham; Shun); they define *xing* differently; Guodian manuscripts (c. 300 BCE) define *xing* much as Xunzi does. Authenticity of the "Human Nature Is Evil" chapter questioned by some (Zhou Chicheng 2015). [source: SEP-Xz §2; WP-Xunzi]
- **Teaching point / caution:** this is a debate about *moral* knowledge and motivation, not about mathematics or concepts; the innate/acquired question is the same shape (Mencius ≈ nativist about moral responses; Xunzi ≈ goodness acquired through ritual and teachers), but avoid equating sprouts with Cartesian innate ideas. Mou Zongsan later read *liangzhi* as a moral "intellectual intuition" that Kant denied humans (published *Modern Chinese and Indian Philosophy*). Do not repeat the full child-at-the-well reconstruction (already in *Confucianism*).

## Debates
- **Are "rationalist" and "empiricist" useful labels?** SEP-RE (Markie and Folescu): best left unused except for the innate-knowledge debate; Loeb 1981, Norton 1981, Kenny 1986 critical; IEP-BE keeps "British Empiricism" as an analyst's category with explicit criteria. Origin: Kant (SEP-RE), Kant/Reinhold/Tennemann with standardisation c. 1895–1915 (Vanzo 2016, WS), Kuno Fischer in the 1850s (IEP-BE citing Mercer 2020). [sources above]
- **Is there any a priori knowledge?** Quine and naturalists (Devitt) no; moderate rationalists (BonJour, Bealer, Huemer) yes, fallible; moderate empiricists (Ayer) yes but only analytic. [SEP-AP; IEP-AP]
- **Does Quine refute the analytic/synthetic distinction?** Quine 1951 vs Grice and Strawson 1956, Putnam 1965, Carnap (unpublished till 1990), Katz; later Quine's partial concession; Soames: the argument relied on positivist theses now abandoned. [SEP-AS; SEP-Q; WP]
- **Are Kripke's contingent a priori cases genuine?** Kripke vs Donnellan, Plantinga, Salmon, Hughes. [SEP-RD §3.2]
- **Is Chomsky a rationalist about knowledge, or only a nativist about capacities?** Cottingham, Markie/Folescu vs Chomsky's self-description. [SEP-RE §3]
- **How poor is the stimulus?** Pullum and Scholz 2002 vs Legate and Yang 2002, Lasnik and Lidz 2016; LLMs as possibility proofs vs data-quantity objection. [SEP-IL]
- **Radical concept nativism**: Fodor 1975/1981 vs Carey, Margolis and Laurence; Fodor's own 2008 retreat. [SEP-Con]
- **Is the Meno a defence of nativism?** Pedagogical tour de force (SEP-IH) vs a sketch of innate belief compatible with modern science (IEP-Meno). [SEP-IH; IEP-Meno]
- **Is Kant a nativist?** Samet says yes, with a different role for the innate. [SEP-IH §5]
- **Benacerraf's challenge:** causal version (dated) vs Field's reliability version; plenitudinous platonism as a reply. [SEP-PM; IEP-Ben]
- **Mencius vs Xunzi:** real disagreement vs different definitions of *xing* (Graham; Shun); authenticity of Xunzi 23. [SEP-Xz]

## Surprises (commonly believed, contradicted by sources)
- Locke never wrote "tabula rasa" in the *Essay*; his image is "white paper". [WP; PN-Locke]
- "Continental Rationalists vs British Empiricists" is a later historians' scheme (Kant and his followers; standard only c. 1900 per Vanzo, or Kuno Fischer per IEP); none of the "members" saw themselves that way, and Descartes and Locke had "remarkably similar views on the nature of our ideas". [SEP-RE; IEP-BE]
- Most empiricists accepted intuition and deduction too; the real dividing line is innate knowledge/concepts. [SEP-RE]
- Chomsky's nativism is arguably closer to Locke's inborn faculties than to Descartes' innate ideas. [SEP-RE §3]
- Kripke's contingent a priori is much more disputed than his necessary a posteriori. [SEP-RD]
- Quine later accepted a limited analyticity. [SEP-Q]
- Mill thought arithmetic is inductive generalisation from pebbles and horses; Frege's jibe "gingerbread and pebble arithmetic". [SEP-Mill; SEP-Q]
- Fodor ended up denying that concepts are either learned or innate. [SEP-Con]
- Classical Nyāya explicitly denies innate ideas: concepts are traces of past experience. [SEP-EI]
- Avicenna, often cited for the flying man, also described the intellect at birth as "like a tabula rasa". [IEP-Av]
