## 1. Life: dates, family, name
- DATES ARE DISPUTED; give a range. SEP-Plato (2026): "Plato (429?–347 B.C.E.)". [source: SEP-Plato]
- SEP-Soc (Debra Nails) timeline: "Plato (424/3–347)" and "424/3 birth of Plato"; it adds that Plato was "about twenty-five" at Socrates' death in 399. [source: SEP-Soc]
- IEP-Plato title: "Plato (427–347 B.C.E.)"; text: "widely accepted" that he was born 428–7 and died at 80 or 81 in 348–7, dates "based upon Eratosthenes' calculations" and "traditionally ... accepted". IEP lists the conflicting ancient data: Diogenes Laertius (following Apollodorus) says Plato was born the year Pericles died (= 429) and died at 84 (= birth 430/431); DL 3.6 says he was 28 when Socrates died (= 427). [source: IEP-Plato §1a]
- Wikipedia: born "between 428 and 423 BC" (citing Nails 2002 p. 246; Waterfield 2023); died 348/347 BC. [source: WP-Plato]
- Safe formulation: born between c. 429 and 423 BCE (traditionally 428/427), died 348/347 BCE in Athens. Death year 347 (or 348/7) is not in serious dispute across sources. [sources above]
- Aristocratic, wealthy, politically active Athenian family. Parents Ariston and Perictione; brothers Glaucon and Adeimantus (both speakers in the Republic, probably older than Plato), sister Potone; after Ariston's death Perictione married her uncle Pyrilampes (an associate of Pericles), and had Antiphon, Plato's half-brother. [source: IEP-Plato §1b; WP-Plato]
- Through his mother Plato descended from Solon. [source: WP-Plato]
- Relatives in the oligarchy of the Thirty (404 BCE): his mother's brother Charmides was a member, and Critias, whom IEP calls Charmides' uncle, was its leader (IEP: "Charmides' own uncle, Critias, was the leader of the Thirty"; WP: "two of Plato's relatives, Critias and Charmides"). [source: IEP-Plato §1b; WP-Plato]
- Name: Diogenes Laertius says his given name was Aristocles (after his grandfather) and "Plato" (from *platos*, "broad") was a nickname, for his build, his style or his forehead. IEP repeats this as "apparently"; Wikipedia says the Aristocles story is "widely regarded as false by modern scholarship" (Notopoulos 1939, Nails 2002, Guthrie, Waterfield 2023). Present as a disputed ancient anecdote. [source: IEP-Plato §1b; WP-Plato]
- Ancient tradition (DL 3.5) that he first wrote tragedies/poetry and burned them on meeting Socrates: IEP says "Whether or not any of these stories is true"; treat as anecdote. [source: IEP-Plato §3a; WP-Plato]
- According to the Seventh Letter (324b–326b, authenticity disputed), he had intended a political career; he was invited to join the Thirty but was disillusioned by their crimes, including their attempt to involve Socrates in the arrest of Leon of Salamis; the prosecution of Socrates (399) ended his political plans. [source: WP-Plato; WP-Seventh]
- After Socrates' death he went to Megara (to Euclides of Megara, c. 396 per Waterfield via WP); later stories add Cyrene, Italy, Sicily and even Egypt (Strabo 17.29), the Egypt story being weakly supported. [source: IEP-Plato §1c; WP-Plato]
- Seventh Letter 324e calls Socrates "the justest man alive" (IEP's rendering). [source: IEP-Plato §2d]
- Died 348/347 and, per Diogenes Laertius (3.41), was buried at the Academy; IEP notes the grave has not been found. He was succeeded as head of the Academy by his nephew Speusippus (son of his sister Potone). [source: IEP-Plato §1d; WP-Plato]
- A 2024 press report (The Guardian, 29 Apr 2024, on a newly read Herculaneum papyrus of Philodemus' *History of the Academy*) says Plato was sold into slavery on Aegina, possibly in 404 or 399 BCE, and describes his last evening. This is reported only via Wikipedia citing a newspaper; IEP calls the older story of Dionysius I having Plato sold into slavery "doubtful stories from later antiquity" (Diodorus 15.7; Plutarch, Dion 5; DL 3.19–21). Use only with that caveat, if at all. [source: WP-Plato; IEP-Plato §1c]

## 2. Sicily
- First visit to Italy/Sicily "about forty" (Seventh Letter 324a), i.e. c. 388–385 (IEP's dialogue table puts the first trip in 387; WP says Syracuse "in roughly 385 BC" citing Riginos). There he met and taught Dion, brother-in-law of the tyrant Dionysius I. He also knew the Pythagorean statesman Archytas of Tarentum. [source: IEP-Plato §§1c, 2c, 3b; WP-Plato]
- Second visit: after Dionysius I died (367), Dion persuaded Dionysius II to invite Plato (c. 366) to help make him the sort of philosopher-ruler described in the Republic; within months Dion was exiled and Plato was effectively under house arrest before being allowed home (Seventh Letter 328b–330b, 338a). [source: IEP-Plato §1d; WP-Plato]
- Third visit: 361–360; Dionysius again effectively detained him; he escaped with help from Archytas and his Tarentine friends (Seventh Letter 350a–b). [source: IEP-Plato §1d; WP-Plato]
- Dion later overthrew Dionysius (ruled c. 357–354) and was assassinated (354/353) by Callippus, an Athenian whom the Seventh Letter insists had no connection with the Academy. [source: WP-Plato; WP-Seventh]
- Nearly all the Sicilian narrative depends on the Letters, above all the Seventh, whose authenticity is disputed (see Debates). [source: SEP-Plato §§3, 10; IEP-Plato]

## 3. The Academy
- Named after its site, a grove sacred to the hero Academus/Hecademus, about a mile outside the Athenian walls (c. 1.5 km north of the Dipylon gate, near Colonus); source of our word "academic". [source: IEP-Plato §1c; WP-Academy]
- FOUNDING DATE: no record of an exact date. WP-Academy: "founded by Plato in ca. 387 BC"; "modern scholars generally agree that the time was the mid-380s, probably sometime after 387" (Guthrie; Dancy); Mueller allows "some time between the early 380s and the middle 360s". WP-Plato (following Nails 2002): "in roughly 383 BC". Nails: informal gatherings earlier; a formal Academy recognised only once Eudoxus arrived in the mid-380s. Write "founded in the 380s BCE (traditionally c. 387)". [source: WP-Academy; WP-Plato]
- Aristotle arrived in 367 and stayed twenty years, until Plato's death (367–347). [source: WP-Academy; WP-Plato]
- In Plato's time it charged no fees, had probably no fixed doctrine or formal curriculum; Plato and others posed problems for members to solve; dialectic was the usual method; there is evidence of lectures, notably Plato's public lecture "On the Good". Mathematics was studied (e.g. Eudoxus; Simplicius reports Plato set the problem of "saving the appearances" of planetary motion). [source: WP-Academy]
- Two women are recorded as studying with Plato at the Academy: Axiothea of Phlius and Lasthenia of Mantinea (WP cites Reeve 2001). [source: WP-Academy]
- After Plato the Academy became, in the Hellenistic period, a sceptical school, ending as an institution after the death of Philo of Larissa (83 BCE); later Platonists taught in Athens until Justinian's measures of 529 CE. [source: WP-Academy]
- Middle Comedy mocked Academy members, e.g. a fragment of Epicrates shows students debating the genus of a pumpkin, parodying Platonic division. [source: WP-Plato]

## 4. The writings and the dialogue form
- Plato's complete works are believed to have survived (unlike those of nearly all his contemporaries). [source: WP-Plato]
- Nearly everything is a dialogue; the Apology (Socrates' defence speech, 399) is the striking exception. A collection of 13 letters is transmitted with the works; their authenticity is "not universally accepted" and "many or most of them are almost certainly not his" (Burnyeat and Frede 2015). [source: SEP-Plato §3]
- Plato never wrote treatises and never speaks in his own voice in the dialogues: "Strictly speaking, he does not himself affirm anything in his dialogues"; "Whatever he wishes to communicate to us is conveyed indirectly." [source: SEP-Plato §5]
- Many interlocutors are real historical figures (Nails 2002). Socrates speaks in nearly all dialogues, absent only from the Laws; in Sophist, Statesman and Timaeus his role is small (a visitor from Elea leads Sophist and Statesman; an unnamed Athenian leads Laws). [source: SEP-Plato §§3–5]
- Plato was one of several authors of "Socratic discourses" (others: Xenophon, Aeschines, Antisthenes, Eucleides, Phaedo); Aristotle's term. [source: SEP-Plato §4; IEP-Plato §3a]
- Phaedrus 274e–276d: Socrates warns against relying on books; written texts are best used as reminders of discussions one has had. SEP reads Plato as wanting his writings to be "supplementary aids to philosophical conversation". [source: SEP-Plato §6]
- Cross-references show Plato expects readers to know other dialogues (e.g. Phaedo 73a–b alludes to the Meno; Theaetetus–Sophist–Statesman are linked; Timaeus opens by recalling the Republic). [source: SEP-Plato §8]
- SEP-Plato: Plato is "far more exploratory, incompletely systematic, elusive, and playful" than Aristotle, Aquinas or Kant; readers receive "a few key ideas together with a series of suggestions and problems". Dialogues that most conspicuously leave work for the reader: Euthyphro, Laches, Charmides, Euthydemus, Theaetetus, Parmenides. [source: SEP-Plato §2]
- Yet SEP-Plato argues we can and must attribute views to Plato: dialogues where one character persuades the rest (Phaedo, Symposium, Republic, Phaedrus, Theaetetus, Sophist, Statesman, Timaeus, Philebus, Laws) are best explained as vehicles for views Plato recommends. [source: SEP-Plato §6]
- Citation system: Henri Estienne (Stephanus) published the dialogues in 1578; each page is divided into sections a–e, giving references like "Republic 511d". [source: IEP-Plato §3c]

## 5. Chronology of the dialogues
- Hard external evidence is slight: Aristotle (Politics 2.6, 1264b24–27) says the Laws was written after the Republic; DL 3.37 says "some say" the Laws was unfinished at his death and left on wax tablets; internal links order Sophist–Statesman and Timaeus–Critias. [source: IEP-Plato §3b; SEP-Laws §1]
- Stylometry (the quantitative study of features of Plato's prose) most clearly separates a late group: Sophist, Statesman, Timaeus, Critias, Philebus, Laws. This six-dialogue group was recognised in the nineteenth century; computer counts later supported it (Brandwood 1990; Young 1994; Ledger 1989). [source: SEP-Plato §11; SEP-Laws §1; WP-Seventh for Ledger]
- SEP-Laws reports the stylometric three groups (alphabetical within groups; no internal order): Group 3 (latest): Critias, Laws, Philebus, Sophist, Statesman, Timaeus. Group 2: Parmenides, Phaedrus, Republic, Theaetetus. Group 1: Apology, Charmides, Cratylus, Crito, Euthydemus, Euthyphro, Gorgias, Hippias Major, Hippias Minor, Ion, Laches, Lysis, Menexenus, Meno, Phaedo, Protagoras, Symposium. NB stylometry puts Phaedo and Symposium with the "early" group, while content analysis usually makes them "middle". [source: SEP-Laws §1; IEP-Plato §6c on Phaedo]
- The conventional "early / middle / late" scheme (associated especially with Gregory Vlastos, Socrates: Ironist and Moral Philosopher, 1991): early "Socratic" dialogues (Charmides, Crito, Euthydemus, Euthyphro, Gorgias, Hippias Major and Minor, Ion, Laches, Lysis, Protagoras) end without positive doctrine; middle dialogues (Phaedo, Cratylus, Symposium, Republic, Phaedrus) present Forms, immortality, the tripartite soul; late as above. [source: SEP-Plato §11]
- IEP's own grouping (combining stylometry and content): Early (before the first Sicily trip, 387): Apology, Charmides, Crito, Euthydemus, Euthyphro, Gorgias, Hippias Major, Hippias Minor, Ion, Laches, Lysis, Protagoras, Republic I. Early-transitional (c. 387–380): Cratylus, Menexenus, Meno. Middle (c. 380–360): Phaedo, Republic II–X, Symposium. Late-transitional (c. 360–355): Parmenides, Theaetetus, Phaedrus. Late (c. 355–347): Sophist, Statesman, Philebus, Timaeus, Critias, Laws. These dates are IEP's estimates. [source: IEP-Plato §3b]
- SEP-Plato: "it remains a matter of dispute whether the division ... into three periods ... does correctly indicate the order of composition, and whether it is a useful tool" (citing Cooper 1997); Plato may have written "negative", puzzle-raising dialogues throughout his life alongside doctrinal ones. [source: SEP-Plato §11]
- Republic Book I may have been written as a separate, earlier work (Brandwood; Nails notes anachronisms suggesting long composition/revision). [source: WP-Republic; IEP-Plato §6a]
- Republic usually dated "around 375 BC" (WP, citing IEP's middle period c. 380–360). Symposium written no earlier than 385 (Aristophanes' speech at 193a alludes to the dissolution of Mantinea, 385/4; Dover 1965); WP dates it c. 385–370. Meno written "probably about 385" (IEP-Meno), set in 402. [source: WP-Republic; WP-Symp; IEP-Meno]
- Laws: longest dialogue, roughly 20% longer than the Republic, about 345 Stephanus pages, 12 books; edited after Plato's death by Philip of Opus (also generally thought author of the Epinomis). [source: SEP-Laws §1–2; IEP-Plato §7f; WP-Plato]

## 6. The theory of Forms
- Core distinction: "between the many observable objects that appear beautiful (good, just, unified, equal, big) and the one object that is what beauty (goodness, justice, unity) really is"; "Nearly every major work of Plato is, in some way, devoted to or dependent on this distinction." Forms are eternal, changeless, not in space or time, and in some sense paradigms for the sensible world. [source: SEP-Plato §1]
- Plato never gives a detailed account of Forms; "the theory of Forms" is a scholarly "rational reconstruction". [source: SEP-Meta §2]
- Phaedo 65d–66a is the first passage widely agreed to introduce Forms (justice itself, the beautiful, the good, never seen with the eyes, grasped by intellect "itself by itself"). [source: SEP-Meta §2]
- Phaedo 100c (Forms as causes/explanations): "if anything else is beautiful besides Beauty Itself, it is beautiful on account of nothing else than because it partakes of Beauty Itself." (SEP author's translation) [source: SEP-Meta §2]
- Principles used in reconstruction (SEP-Parm): One-over-Many ("For any plurality of F things, there is a form of F-ness by virtue of partaking of which each member of the plurality is F", cf. Republic 596a6–7); Uniqueness (one Form per property, from the "Third Bed" argument, Republic 597c–d); Self-Predication ("the F is F": the beautiful is beautiful, e.g. Hippias Major 292e, Phaedo 100c); Separation/Non-Identity ("No form is identical to anything that partakes of it"); Purity (Forms cannot have contrary properties). [source: SEP-Parm §§3–4]
- Range of Forms disputed: Plato may have begun with Forms only for a small set (equal, large, beautiful, just) and widened them; Republic X (596b) has a Form of Bed. [source: IEP-Plato §6b; SEP-Meta §6 heading]
- Parmenides and Heraclitus as background: IEP: Forms "plainly intended to satisfy the Parmenidean requirement of metaphysical unity and stability"; Aristotle (Metaphysics 987a32) and DL say Plato had early contact with Heracliteanism (Cratylus), influencing his view of the sensible world as changing. [source: IEP-Plato §§2a–b; SEP-Meta §1]

### ARGUMENT 1 (step by step): the argument from equality / compresence of opposites, Phaedo 74a–c
- Setting: part of the recollection argument for the soul's pre-existence (Phaedo 72e–78b). [source: SEP-Meta §§2, 11]
- P1. There is something, the Equal itself, that we know (Simmias agrees, 74a–b). [source: SEP-Meta §11]
- P2. Sensible equals (sticks, stones) "can sometimes appear equal and sometimes unequal", whereas the Equal itself "never appears unequal". [source: SEP-Meta §11]
- P3. Things with different properties are not identical (implicit). SEP-Parm: "whereas sensible things that are equal are also unequal, the equal is not unequal, and hence the equal is not identical to any equal sensible thing" (Phaedo 74b–c). [source: SEP-Parm §3]
- C1. So the Equal itself is something other than any sensible equal thing. [source: SEP-Parm §3]
- C2. Since we judge sensible equals to fall short of the Equal, and could not have derived knowledge of it from them, we must have known it before perception, i.e. before birth (the recollection conclusion). [source: SEP-Meta §11]
- Objection (SEP-Meta): to compare sticks with the Form we must already have the Form in mind, so the argument seems to presuppose rather than explain our grasp of Equality; and Plato never explains why the Form "cannot appear unequal". Interpretive split: the "broad" (innatist) reading says all concept-use involves recollection; the "narrow" reading (Scott 1995; Bobonich 2002) says only philosophers' grasp of Forms does. [source: SEP-Meta §11]
- Alternative (veridical/"incomplete properties") reading (Fine; Irwin; Gosling): the problem is not that each stick is both equal and unequal, but that sensible property types explain F-ness in some cases and its opposite in others (bright colour makes some things beautiful, others ugly), so knowledge needs non-sensible explanations. [source: SEP-Meta §14]

### ARGUMENT 2 (step by step): the Third Man, Parmenides 132a–b
- Plato never calls it the "Third Man"; the name comes from Aristotle (Metaphysics 990b17 = 1079a13; 1039a2; Sophistical Refutations 178b36 ff.). The dialogue's example is largeness, not "man". [source: SEP-Parm §4.3]
- Setting: an aged Parmenides cross-examines a young Socrates, "a promising, young philosopher in need of further training". [source: SEP-Plato §9]
- Reconstruction (SEP-Parm): Take large things A, B, C. By One-over-Many there is a Form of largeness L1 by partaking of which they are large. By Self-Predication, L1 is large. So A, B, C and L1 form a new plurality of large things; by One-over-Many there is a Form L2 over them. By Non-Identity, L2 ≠ L1. Repeat: L3, L4 ... an infinite hierarchy of Forms of largeness. Parmenides concludes each Form "is no longer one, but infinitely many". [source: SEP-Parm §4.3]
- "Most commentators agree that the reasoning relies on at least three principles: One-over-Many, Self-Predication, and Non-Identity"; the literature was "initiated by the groundbreaking analysis" of Gregory Vlastos (1954, "The Third Man Argument in the Parmenides", Philosophical Review). [source: SEP-Parm §4.3]
- Why vicious? (a) epistemic: an infinite regress cannot explain F-ness (Vlastos; Fine 1993); (b) it contradicts Uniqueness; (c) Rickless (2007): it makes each Form many, contradicting Oneness and Purity. [source: SEP-Parm §4.3]
- Replies/readings of the whole dialogue: (1) "record of honest perplexity" (Vlastos 1954, 343; Gill; Allen): Plato saw no answer when he wrote; (2) Meinwald (1991; 1992; 2014) and Peterson: "X is F" is ambiguous between F "in relation to itself" (*pros heauto*, F is definitionally true of X) and "in relation to the others" (*pros ta alla*, X displays F), so self-predication is harmless and the regress equivocates (criticised by Frances 1996, Durrant 1997); (3) Rickless (2007): the criticisms are valid; Plato saves the Forms by abandoning Purity, Uniqueness and No-Causation-by-Contraries while keeping One-over-Many and Separation. "Scholars are deeply divided." [source: SEP-Parm §7]
- Aristotle believed the Third Man refuted the theory; whether Plato did is a long controversy. The Timaeus' use of Forms matters: if it is later than the Parmenides, Plato did not regard the objection as decisive. [source: IEP-Plato §7b]
- Parmenides then raises "the greatest difficulty" (133a–134e): if Forms are as described, humans cannot know them and gods cannot know human affairs. After the criticisms Parmenides says a "very gifted man" could still defend the Forms; part two runs eight "Deductions" on the One. [source: SEP-Parm §§1, 4.6, 7]
- Socrates at 132b suggests Forms might be thoughts in minds (the "noematic" thesis); Parmenides rejects it; SEP notes it lies at the root of the later tradition of Forms as ideas in God's mind. [source: SEP-Parm §4.4]
- IEP: in the late dialogues Forms seem modified; they are "wholly unmentioned" in the Theaetetus and seem to drop out of the Laws; Sophist and Statesman explore logical relations among abstract kinds, with the method of "collection and division" (first at Phaedrus 265e). SEP-Plato: the Eleatic visitor still takes Forms for granted. Whether Plato changed his mind about Forms is open. [source: IEP-Plato §7a–b; SEP-Plato §§8–9]

## 7. Knowledge and opinion; sun, line, cave
- Meno's paradox (80d–e), as set out by SEP: either one knows F or not; if one knows, one cannot inquire; if not, one cannot inquire; so no inquiry. Socrates' answer: recollection. [source: SEP-Meta §10]
- Meno slave-boy (82a–85b): an uneducated slave is led by questions to find the side of a square double the area of a 2-foot square; he first guesses 4 feet, then 3, then (via the diagonal) finds the 8-square-foot square. Socrates claims he has recollected. IEP-Meno: Socrates "clearly asks 'leading questions'" and even shows the answer at 84e; the lesson refutes the paradox's false dichotomy but "clearly does not demonstrate" memory of a previous life; after it Socrates says the soul is "always in a state of having learned" (86a), the beginnings of a theory of innate ideas. [source: WP-Meno; IEP-Meno]
- Meno 97–98: true belief vs knowledge (SEP illustrates with a modern road-directions example); knowledge is true belief "tied down" by "an account of the reason why" (*aitias logismos*, 98a), "perhaps the first attempt to offer a justified true belief account of knowledge" (SEP's claim). [source: SEP-Meta §10]
- Republic V (474b–480a): philosophers vs "sight-lovers". Knowledge is set over "what completely is", ignorance over "what completely is not", opinion/belief (*doxa*) over what "is and is not"; capacities differ by objects and by what they do (knowledge infallible; belief true or false). [source: SEP-Meta §12]
- Debate on the "is": existential reading (little support); predicative "Two Worlds" reading (knowledge only of Forms, belief only of particulars, so no knowledge of the sensible and no beliefs about Forms); veridical reading (Gail Fine 1978, 1990): knowledge = set of true propositions, belief = all propositions, allowing knowledge and belief about the same things. [source: SEP-Meta §12]
- Sun (508b–509c): the Good is to the intelligible realm as the sun to the visible. 508e (SEP translation): "What gives truth to the things known and the power to know to the knower is the Form of the Good. And though it is the cause of knowledge and truth, it is also an object of knowledge." 509b: the Good is "not being, but beyond it (*epekeina*) in rank and power" (SEP-Eth's rendering). [source: SEP-Meta §13; SEP-Eth; WP-Cave for line numbers]
- Divided line (509d–511e): visible section divided into images (*eikasia*, imagination) and physical objects (*pistis*, belief/conviction); intelligible section into *dianoia* (reasoning from hypotheses with images, as in mathematics) and *noēsis*/understanding of Forms, rising to an unhypothetical first principle (510b). [source: SEP-Meta §13; WP-Cave]
- Cave (514a–520a, Book VII), "arguably the most famous analogy in the history of philosophy" (SEP): prisoners chained from childhood see shadows cast by carved objects carried before a fire; one is freed, sees the objects and fire, is dragged out, first sees shadows and reflections, then things, finally the sun. Plato says it compares "the effect of education (*paideia*) and the lack of it on our nature". [source: SEP-Meta §13; WP-Cave]
- The freed prisoner must go back down; philosophers must be compelled to rule (see §10). [source: SEP-Rep §2.3]
- Philosophers' education: largely mathematical studies, dialectic late in life (521c ff.); in the ideal city, education to 35, then 15 years in lesser offices, rule from 50 (Republic 520, 540; Jowett via WP). [source: SEP-Meta §14; WP-PK]
- The Form of the Good is "a shadowy presence"; SEP-Rep argues Socrates takes goodness to be unity (Hitchcock 1985), which fits reports of Plato's lecture "On the Good" (Aristoxenus, Elementa Harmonica II). [source: SEP-Rep §2.3]

## 8. The soul: immortal and divided
- The soul is distinct from the body, can function without it, and grasps Forms best when unencumbered (Phaedo); recollection and reincarnation appear in Meno, Phaedo, Phaedrus, Republic X (myth of Er), Timaeus, Laws; IEP: "No traces" of recollection or transmigration in the early dialogues. In the Apology Socrates says no one knows what becomes of us after death. [source: SEP-Plato §§1, 11; IEP-Plato §6c]
- Phaedo's arguments for immortality: the cyclical argument (70c–72d), recollection (72e–78b), affinity (78b–84b: the soul resembles the invisible, unchanging and divine), and the final argument (102a–107a: soul brings life, so cannot admit death, hence "deathless"). Cebes (88b) objects that greater durability is not indestructibility. [source: SEP-Meta §2; SEP-Soul §3]
- Tripartite soul (Republic IV, 435b–445b): reason (loves truth, should rule), spirit (*thumos*; loves honour and victory), appetite (food, drink, sex; loves money as means). [source: IEP-Plato §6d; SEP-Rep §2.1]

### ARGUMENT 3 (step by step): the argument from conflict, Republic IV 436b–441c
- P1 (principle of non-opposition, 436b8–9): "the same thing will not be willing to do or undergo opposites in the same respect, in relation to the same thing, at the same time" (SEP's translation; SEP compares Aristotle's principle of non-contradiction). [source: SEP-Rep §2.1]
- P2. A thirsty person can at once desire to drink and, from calculation that it is bad, refuse to drink (439a–d). [source: SEP-Rep §2.1]
- C1. So the attitudes belong to different parts: appetite and reason. [source: SEP-Rep §2.1]
- P3. Leontius, desiring to look at corpses, was angry with himself for the desire (439e–440b). C2. So spirit is distinct from appetite (and, by further argument, from reason). [source: SEP-Rep §2.1]
- Significance: it opens the door to *akrasia* (weakness of will), which Socrates denied in the Protagoras; contrast with the "intellectualism" of the early dialogues. [source: SEP-Rep §2.1; IEP-Plato §6d]
- Objections/debates: whether the "parts" are merely conceptual (Shields 2001; Price 2009) or agent-like (Kamtekar 2006); whether Leontius really warrants a third part; whether appetite must be further partitioned (Whiting 2012; Kamtekar 2008). Disembodied soul may be simple (611a–612a). [source: SEP-Rep §2.1]

## 9. The Republic: justice
- Book I: Cephalus (justice = honesty and returning what is owed; refuted by returning weapons to a man gone mad, 331c), Polemarchus, Thrasymachus (343c–344c: justice is conventionally established by the strong; the strong do better ignoring it). [source: SEP-Rep §§1.1–1.2; WP-Republic]
- Book II challenge: Glaucon and Adeimantus want justice shown worth choosing for its own sake, regardless of reputation and rewards, so that the perfectly just man thought unjust is better off than the perfectly unjust man thought just (360d–361d). Socrates places justice in the best class of goods, loved "both for its own sake and for the sake of its consequences" (358a1–3, SEP's translation). [source: SEP-Rep §1.1]
- Ring of Gyges (359a–360d): Glaucon's story of an ancestor of Gyges of Lydia, a shepherd who finds a ring of invisibility in a tomb and uses it to seduce the queen, kill the king and seize power; claim: anyone with such a ring would act unjustly. (Herodotus 1.7–13 tells a different Gyges story.) Socrates later calls it "the ring of Gyges" (612b). [source: WP-Gyges]
- Strategy: look for justice in the city first, as "bigger", then in the soul (368c–369b), via a city–soul analogy whose exact grounds are disputed (Williams 1973; Lear 1992; Ferrari 2003). [source: SEP-Rep §1.3]
- Definitions (Book IV): wisdom = reason's knowledge of what benefits each part and the whole (442c); courage = spirit holding to reason's judgment about what is fearsome (442b–c); moderation = agreement among the parts that reason should rule; justice = each part doing its own work (441d–e; 443c–e). [source: SEP-Rep §2.2]
- Main objection (David Sachs, "A Fallacy in Plato's Republic", 1963): Socrates was asked about doing just acts but answers about having a harmonious soul; he must show that the psychologically just do not act unjustly and do what justice requires. Replies look to Books V–VII (knowledge of the Form of the Good motivates just action, e.g. Irwin; Kraut) or to early education. [source: SEP-Rep §2.2–2.3]
- Socrates' three "proofs" that the just life is happier (Books VIII–IX): psychological health/the tyrant's misery, and two arguments from pleasure (580c–588a); plus the image of the soul as little human, lion and many-headed beast (588b ff.). [source: SEP-Rep §3.1]

## 10. The ideal city, guardian women, philosopher-rulers, democracy
- First "healthy city" (369b–372e), which Glaucon calls a "city of pigs"; then the purified city, later called "Kallipolis" ("beautiful city", 527c2), with three classes: rulers, auxiliaries (together guardians) and producers. Principle of specialisation: each does the task he is best suited to. [source: SEP-Rep §4.1]
- Noble lie / myth of the metals (414b–415d): citizens told they contain gold, silver or bronze. SEP calls the rulers' control of culture and the noble lie a "totalitarian concern". [source: SEP-Rep §4.4; WP-Republic]
- Guardians live without private property and private families ("friends possess everything in common", 423e–424a); criticised from Aristotle (Politics II 1–5) on. [source: SEP-Rep §4.2]
- Book V: women may be guardians and rulers: sex is as irrelevant to the job as long or short hair (454b–456b); same education. Aristophanes' Ecclesiazusae (c. 391) had already mocked "sharing women and children". [source: SEP-Rep §4.3; WP-Plato]
- Philosopher-rulers: a three-class city without philosopher-rulers "cannot be an ideal city" (473b–e). Socrates insists eight times that philosophers must be compelled to rule (473d4, 500d4, 519e4, 520a8, 520e2, 521b7, 539e3, 540b5); they are the best rulers because they prefer not to rule (520e–521b). [source: SEP-Rep §§2.3, 4.1]
- SEP: Socrates repeatedly says the ideal city could exist (450c–d, 456b–c, 473c, 499b–d, 502a–c, 540d–e), though realising it is very unlikely; at 592a–b it is a model (*paradeigma*) whether or not it exists. [source: SEP-Rep §4.1]
- Book VIII–IX decline: aristocracy → timocracy (honour, Sparta-like) → oligarchy (wealth) → democracy → tyranny; matching souls ruled by reason, spirit, necessary appetites, unnecessary appetites, lawless appetites. Not a historical account and not deterministic. [source: SEP-Rep §§2.1, 5; WP-Republic]
- Critique of democracy: "democracy honors all pursuits equally, which opens the city to conflict and disorder"; without publicly entrenched standards of value "chaos and strife are unavoidable"; the democrat "treats all desires and pleasures as equally valuable"; a demagogue exploits this and tyranny follows. The Republic also distances Plato from oligarchy (criticising Spartan and Athenian oligarchs). Republic VI's "ship of state" (488a–489d) likens democracy to sailors without navigational skill fighting for the helm. [source: SEP-Rep §§2.1, 5; WP-PK; WP-Republic]
- Contrast: the Statesman ranks democracy above oligarchy; the Laws gives unwise citizens more political role. [source: SEP-Rep §5]
- Karl Popper charged the Republic with totalitarianism in *The Open Society and Its Enemies*, vol. I, *The Spell of Plato* (1945). Vlastos (1977) replied that the city's good is the citizens' aggregate good; SEP favours a mixed view (Morrison 2001), concluding Kallipolis has "clearly totalitarian features" (concentrated power, control of culture, noble lie) but its aims are not obviously totalitarian. [source: SEP-Rep §4.4]

## 11. Love and beauty: the Symposium
- Dramatic date 416 BCE, at the poet Agathon's party celebrating his first dramatic victory at the Dionysia; a series of speeches on Eros (Phaedrus, Pausanias, Eryximachus, Aristophanes, Agathon, Socrates), then the drunken Alcibiades. [source: WP-Symp]
- Aristophanes' speech gives the image of lovers as each other's "other half". [source: IEP-Plato §6f; WP-Symp]
- Socrates attributes his account to Diotima of Mantinea, "the one who taught me the art of love" (201d5). Love is not a god but a *daimōn* between mortal and divine; we love the good, wanting it to be ours forever; mortals achieve this by "giving birth in beauty" (*tokos en kalō*, 206b). [source: SEP-Friend §4; WP-Symp]
- Ladder of love (*scala amoris*, 210a–211d): love of one beautiful body → all beautiful bodies → beautiful souls → laws and practices → kinds of knowledge → the beautiful itself, unchanging and unmixed. Diotima's story is explicitly about "loving boys correctly" (211b). [source: SEP-Aes; SEP-Friend §4]
- Debate: the ladder can seem to treat beloved individuals as merely instrumental ("When one has climbed the ladder ... one should kick it—and them—away"); SEP-Friend replies that the lover comes to value the beloved appropriately rather than discard him. Martha Nussbaum (The Fragility of Goodness) considers that Plato may be criticising Socratic abstraction through Alcibiades' speech. IEP: Plato regards physical sexual expression as degraded. [source: SEP-Friend §4; WP-Symp; IEP-Plato §6f]
- Diotima: most scholars think Plato invented her; Mary Ellen Waithe argued she may have been real (already stated in the published Socrates chapter; keep consistent). [source: published Periergia chapter, based on its own research notes]
- Phaedrus: love as "divine madness" by which the soul's wings regrow. [source: IEP-Plato §6f]

## 12. Timaeus and the Laws
- Timaeus: the cosmos is the work of a divine Craftsman (*dēmiourgos*, 28a6) who, imitating an unchanging eternal model, imposes mathematical order on a pre-existent chaos; teleological explanation; the Craftsman is "supremely good" (29a). The Demiurge does not create from nothing. Four elements built from two kinds of triangles into regular solids; a "receptacle" (49a, 52a) as a third kind. Timaeus calls his account only a "likely account/story" (*eikōs logos/muthos*). [source: SEP-Tim; IEP-Plato §7e]
- Literal vs metaphorical creation: Aristotle (Physics 251b14–26) read it as a beginning of time and rejected it; most ancient Platonists from the Old Academy to Plotinus read it metaphorically; still debated. [source: SEP-Tim §2]
- Date debate: G. E. L. Owen (1953) argued the Timaeus is a middle dialogue, earlier than Theaetetus and Parmenides; Harold Cherniss defended the traditional late date; "Over time the orthodox view appears to have held its own", reinforced by computer stylometry. [source: SEP-Tim §3]
- Calcidius' fourth-century Latin translation made the Timaeus "the only text of Plato available in the Middle Ages in the Latin West until the twelfth century". [source: SEP-Tim]
- Atlantis myth: first told in the Timaeus, continued in the unfinished Critias. [source: IEP-Plato §7d]
- Laws: 12 books; conversation on Crete among an unnamed Athenian visitor, Megillus of Sparta and Kleinias of Crete, walking to the temple of Zeus. Kleinias is to help found a new colony, Magnesia; laws are preceded by persuasive "preludes" (*prooimia*). Called the "second-best" city (relative to the Republic's). SEP-Laws calls the council traditionally known as the "Nocturnal Council" the "Dawn Council" (12.951d). [source: SEP-Laws §§2–3, 6]
- In the Laws private families and marriage return (regulated); Susan Okin (1979) argued this reduces women's roles relative to Kallipolis; women lack independent property rights but are citizens, share the same education and military training, have their own common meals, and may hold office. [source: SEP-Laws §6.6]
- 19th-century scholars (e.g. Georg Ast) doubted the Laws' authenticity; Aristotle recognises it as Plato's and "almost no serious scholar" doubts it now. [source: SEP-Laws §1]

## 13. Reception
- Aristotle, Plato's student for 20 years, criticised the Forms (including the Third Man) and the Republic's communism (Politics II). [source: WP-Academy; SEP-Parm; SEP-Rep §4.2]
- Neoplatonism: modern label for late-antique Platonists (mid-3rd to mid-7th century CE); they called themselves simply "Platonists". Plotinus (204/5–270), Egyptian-born, is commonly regarded as founder; taught in Rome from c. 245. [source: SEP-Neo]
- Christian Platonism: Origen may have studied with Plotinus' teacher Ammonius Saccas; the Cappadocian Fathers studied in Athens; Augustine of Hippo (354–430) "was intimately familiar with the writings of Plotinus and Porphyry". [source: SEP-Neo §1]
- Islamic falsafa: al-Fārābī (probably born 870, died Damascus Dec 950/Jan 951) took "some inspiration from Plato's Republic and Laws" (not from Aristotle's Politics, apparently not available in Arabic), though "there is some doubt that a full Arabic translation of them ever existed"; he wrote a *Summary of Plato's Laws* (first eight books only) and *The Philosophy of Plato*; he transformed the philosopher-king into a "philosopher prophet ruler" for a multi-ethnic, multi-religious empire. Averroes wrote a commentary of sorts on the Republic. Whether the *Harmonization of the Opinions of the Two Sages* (Plato and Aristotle) is by al-Fārābī was put in doubt by Marwan Rashed (2009). [source: SEP-Farabi]
- Islamic and Jewish authors from the Middle Ages adapted the philosopher-king idea. [source: WP-PK]
- Renaissance: about 250 Byzantine manuscripts of Plato survive; Gemistos Plethon brought Plato's texts to Florence; Marsilio Ficino's Latin translation was printed in 1484 (1,025 copies). [source: WP-Plato]
- Whitehead's "footnotes to Plato" (1929): see Quotations.

## Debates
- Dates of Plato's birth: 429? (SEP-Plato, Kraut 2026) vs 428/427 (traditional, Eratosthenes; IEP) vs 424/423 (Nails; SEP-Soc). Wikipedia gives 428–423. [sources: SEP-Plato; IEP-Plato; SEP-Soc; WP-Plato]
- Seventh Letter: Wikipedia: "widely considered the only one that might be authentic"; IEP: "the Seventh seems the best candidate for authenticity". For authenticity: R. Ledger (computer stylometry, 1989), Anthony Kenny (inclined to accept). Against: Burnyeat and Frede (2015), Malcolm Schofield, Terence Irwin, George Boas, Julia Annas (who calls it "such an unconvincing production that its acceptance by many scholars is best seen as indicating the strength of their desire to find, behind the detachment of the dialogues, something ... to which Plato is straightforwardly committed"). SEP-Plato (2026): "many or most of them are almost certainly not his". [source: WP-Seventh; IEP-Plato §4c; SEP-Plato §3]
- How to read the dialogues (IEP's four views): Unitarian (one philosophy throughout; more popular early 20th century; Kahn 1996 a recent version); literary atomist (each dialogue read alone); developmentalist (the most widely held; Plato's views change); historicist (early dialogues represent the historical Socrates; Aristotle seems to read them so). SEP-Laws: unitarians may accept stylometric groups but deny significant change of view; developmentalists affirm it. SEP-Plato (Kraut) argues against a purely "minimalist" reading and for treating the dialogues as products of "a single mind ... though perhaps one who changes his mind". [source: IEP-Plato §5a; SEP-Laws §1; SEP-Plato §§6, 10]
- Did Plato change his mind about Forms (Parmenides) and about politics (Republic vs Laws; Bobonich 2002)? Open. [source: SEP-Plato §§9–10]
- Unwritten doctrines: Aristotle (Physics 209b13–15) mentions Plato's "so-called unwritten doctrines" (*agrapha dogmata*); Aristoxenus reports the lecture "On the Good", whose theme was the One. The Tübingen School (Hans Joachim Krämer, Konrad Gaiser, Thomas Szlezák; Giovanni Reale in Milan) reconstructs an oral "theory of principles": the One and the Indefinite Dyad ("the Great and the Small", Aristotle Metaphysics 987b). Critics: Harold Cherniss (*The Riddle of the Early Academy*, 1945), Gregory Vlastos (1963 review of Krämer), and others; moderate sceptics accept some oral teaching but call the reconstruction speculative; the radical sceptical view is that Plato taught nothing orally beyond the dialogues. Evidence used: Phaedrus' critique of writing and Seventh Letter 341b–342a (if authentic). [source: WP-Unwritten]
- Was the Republic proto-feminist? For: Wender 1973 ("Plato: Misogynist, Paedophile, and Feminist"). Against: Julia Annas 1976 ("Plato's Republic and Feminism"), Buchan 1999 — indifference to actual women, disparaging remarks (e.g. 455c, men better at almost everything), reasons concern the city, not women. Okin 1977 ("Philosopher Queens and Private Wives") links the proposal to abolishing the family. Vlastos 1989 distinguishes Plato's view of women around him from women in better conditions. SEP-Rep verdict: "The broad claim that Plato or the Republic is feminist cannot be sustained", yet two features "can be reasonably called feminist" (sex irrelevant to qualification, 454b–456b; abolition of the private family freeing women for education and work), so he may be "deeply prejudiced against women and yet committed to some plausibly feminist principles". [source: SEP-Rep §4.3]
- Totalitarianism (Popper 1945 vs Vlastos 1977, Morrison 2001): see §10. [source: SEP-Rep §4.4]
- Is the ideal city meant as realisable or as a mere model? SEP-Rep: Socrates' "considered view" is that it could exist. [source: SEP-Rep §4.1]
- Timaeus literal or metaphorical; Timaeus' date (Owen vs Cherniss). [source: SEP-Tim §§2–3]
- Diotima real or invented (see §11). [source: published Socrates chapter]

## Consistency check against published Periergia texts
- Overview (`philosophy--history-of-philosophy--overview.sql`) says Plato "built the first complete philosophical system". SEP-Plato (2026) stresses he is "far more exploratory, incompletely systematic, elusive, and playful" than Aristotle, Aquinas or Kant and that his works are rarely "mere advocacy of a cut-and-dried group of propositions". The seed summary repeats "first complete philosophical system". Recommend: keep the claim but qualify it (systematic in scope and interconnection, as IEP says the middle dialogues form "an interconnected and systematic philosophy", but written as open, exploratory dialogues). [source: SEP-Plato §2; IEP-Plato intro]
- Overview says the Parmenides "contains the most devastating criticisms of the Forms ever produced". - Overview: Whitehead's line "a series of footnotes to Plato" — wording matches. Overview: "Aristotle, Plato's student for twenty years" — consistent (367–347).
