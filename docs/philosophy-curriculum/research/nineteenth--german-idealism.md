## 1. The immediate reception of Kant (brief)

- **Reinhold** (Karl Leonhard, b. Vienna 26 Oct 1757 — "many older sources erroneously give 1758" — d. Kiel 1823): ex-Barnabite priest, converted to Protestantism in Leipzig 1783, Freemason and Illuminatus; his *Letters on the Kantian Philosophy* began appearing in 1786 and made his name; first holder of the chair of Critical Philosophy at Jena, 1787–94; then Kiel. [source: SEP-R §1]
- Reinhold's **Elementarphilosophie** ("Elementary Philosophy", expounded 1789–91) tried to give Kant's philosophy a single self-evident first principle, the **Principle of Consciousness**: "in consciousness, the subject distinguishes the representation from the subject and the object and relates it to both". He claimed it uncovered the "common root" of sensibility and understanding Kant had called unknowable. Much criticised, but his demand for one first principle spurred Fichte to seek a more "fundamental" one; Reinhold later conceded Fichte's "fact-act" was superior. [source: SEP-R intro, §§1–3]
- **Jacobi** (Friedrich Heinrich, b. Düsseldorf 25 Jan 1743 – d. Munich 10 Mar 1819): *David Hume on Faith, or Idealism and Realism* (1787) contains the famous verdict on Kant's thing in itself: "Without that presupposition [of the thing in itself], I could not enter into [Kant's] system, but with it I could not stay within it." He later turned the same diagnosis on Fichte. Jacobi "forged the philosophical meaning of the concept of 'nihilism'"; his 1799 open letter to Fichte equated Fichte's philosophy (and philosophy generally) with "nihilism". He urged a *salto mortale* (leap) into faith. [sources: SEP-J §1, §3; SEP-F §1] (Consistent with the published *Kant* chapter: "critics, from Jacobi in 1787, ask how we could know that unknowable things exist and affect us".)
- **Schulze** (Gottlob Ernst, 23 Aug 1761 – 14 Jan 1833): published anonymously *Aenesidemus* (1792), a sceptical polemic against Kant's first Critique and Reinhold's Elementary Philosophy (full title: *Aenesidemus, or on the Foundations of the Elementary Philosophy Delivered by Professor Reinhold in Jena, together with a Defence of Scepticism against the Pretensions of the Critique of Reason*). Key charge: Kant uses causality (the thing in itself as cause of our representations) beyond the limits he himself set. Later Schopenhauer's teacher at Göttingen (advised him to study Plato and Kant; winter 1810–11). Fichte's review of *Aenesidemus* (1794) first hinted at his new position; IEP: Fichte "to his consternation, found himself in agreement with much of Schulze's critique". [sources: WP-Gottlob_Ernst_Schulze; SEP-F §1; IEP-F] (Cross-ref sibling *Schopenhauer and Kierkegaard*.)
- **Maimon** (Salomon, 1753 – 22 Nov 1800): born near Mir in Lithuania into a poor Jewish family; Talmudic training; reached Berlin's Haskalah circle around Moses Mendelssohn (who asked him to leave Berlin in 1783 for his open Spinozism and bohemian life); sent comments on the Critique to Kant via Markus Herz; *Essay on Transcendental Philosophy* (1790); *Autobiography* (1792/3). Kant replied that "none of my critics understood me and the main questions as well as Herr Maimon does". Maimon doubted both the *quid facti* and the *quid juris* of Kant's Deduction, giving "Hume's skepticism ... its complete power". Fichte: his admiration for Maimon's talent "knows no limit"; "Maimon has completely overturned the entire Kantian philosophy as it has been understood by everyone until now". [source: SEP-M §§1–2, §7] (A Jewish philosopher at the centre of this story; cross-ref *Jewish Philosophy* chapter for the Maimonides from whom he took his name — SEP-M on the name not checked; do not assert.)
- SEP-F: it was "the need to respond to the sharp criticisms" of Maimon and Schulze, plus Reinhold's call for a single first principle, that led Fichte to construct the Wissenschaftslehre (name coined spring 1794). [source: SEP-F §1, §2]

## 2. Fichte: life

- Johann Gottlieb Fichte, b. 19 May 1762, Rammenau (Upper Lusatia, Saxony), eldest son of poor, pious ribbon weavers; a local baron (von Miltitz) sponsored his schooling at Pforta, then Jena and Leipzig; poverty forced him into tutoring. [sources: SEP-F §1; WP-Johann_Gottlieb_Fichte]
- Began giving lessons on Kant in summer 1790; the encounter produced what he called a "revolution" in his thinking, reconciling "head" and "heart" (freedom vs "intelligible fatalism"). [source: SEP-F §1]
- After a "disappointing interview with Kant" in Königsberg he wrote, in a few weeks, *Attempt at a Critique of All Revelation*; Kant arranged its publication by his own publisher in 1792. The first edition appeared "without the author's name and preface" — "for reasons that have never been satisfactorily explained" — and was "quickly and widely hailed as a work by Kant himself"; when the author was revealed, Fichte was "catapulted from total obscurity to philosophical celebrity". Its thesis: the only revelation consistent with the critical philosophy is the moral law itself. [source: SEP-F §1]
- 1793–94: anonymous political tracts, *Reclamation of the Freedom of Thought from the Princes of Europe* (1793) and *Contribution to the Rectification of the Public's Judgment of the French Revolution* (1793, 1794), defending the Revolution's principles and a right of revolution; earned a reputation, "not wholly deserved, as a radical 'Jacobin'". [source: SEP-F §1]
- Called to the Jena chair of Critical Philosophy (vacated by Reinhold); arrived May 1794; Jena years 1794–99. Works: *Concerning the Concept of the Wissenschaftslehre* (1794); *Foundation of the Entire Wissenschaftslehre* (1794/95, his best-known treatise, written as lecture handouts); *Wissenschaftslehre nova methodo* (lectured 1796–99, unpublished in his life); *Foundations of Natural Right* (1796–97); *System of Ethics* (1798). [source: SEP-F §1]
- **Many versions**: "more than a dozen different full-scale presentations" of the Wissenschaftslehre — new versions in 1801/2, three in 1804, then 1805, 1807, 1810, 1811, 1812, 1813, 1814; only the 1810 outline was published in his lifetime. "'The Wissenschaftslehre' is not the name of a book; it is the name of a system." [source: SEP-F §1]
- **Atheism dispute (Atheismusstreit), 1798–99** (WP: lasting 1798–1800): his essay "On the Basis of Our Belief in a Divine Governance of the World" (1798, *Philosophical Journal*), occasioned by F. K. Forberg's essay in the same issue, identified God with the moral world order; an anonymous pamphlet accused both of atheism and demanded Fichte's dismissal; the issue was suppressed; princes threatened to keep students from Jena (WP: Saxony and Prussia threatened, Russia and Austria already boycotting). Fichte "badly miscalculated" — he had threatened to resign if reprimanded — and was forced out, reaching Berlin in summer 1799. Fichte later: "It is not my atheism which they are persecuting, it is my democratism." Weimar minister Voigt wrote to Goethe that the resignation threat gave only the "pretense" to "get rid of" him. [sources: SEP-F §1; IEP-F; WP-Atheism_dispute]
- 1799: Kant publicly disavowed the Wissenschaftslehre in an open letter (logic alone cannot yield substantive knowledge); Jacobi's "nihilism" letter the same year. [source: IEP-F] (Consistent with the *Kant* chapter: "Kant publicly repudiated Fichte in 1799".)
- Berlin: *The Vocation of Man* (1800), *The Closed Commercial State* (1800, autarkic, quasi-socialist), *Sun-Clear Report* (1801); 1806 popular lectures. Fled the French to Königsberg 1806–07 (*Machiavelli*, 1807). [source: SEP-F §1]
- **Addresses to the German Nation**: delivered in occupied Berlin, winter 1807–08 (WP: between December 1807 and March 1808, in the circular hall of the Prussian Academy of Sciences); published 1808. NB SEP-F gives "delivered in Berlin in 1806" in one sentence but "winter of 1807/8 ... (published in 1808)" in another; IEP says "given in 1808". Use 1807–08. [sources: SEP-F §1; IEP-F; WP-Addresses_to_the_German_Nation; WP-Johann_Gottlieb_Fichte] (Consistent with the History chapter on nationalism.)
- 1810: first head of the philosophical faculty and first elected rector of the new University of Berlin (his own plans for it lost to Humboldt's). In 1813 he cancelled lectures so students could join the war against Napoleon. Died 29 January 1814 of an infection caught from his wife, a volunteer nurse in a military hospital (WP classes it as typhus). [sources: SEP-F §1; WP-Johann_Gottlieb_Fichte]

## 3. Fichte: the I and the not-I (ARGUMENT, step by step)

- **Task:** reconcile freedom with necessity — explain how free, responsible agents are also part of a causally ordered world; to "explain the basis of the system of representations accompanied by a feeling of necessity" (First Introduction). He called the Wissenschaftslehre the first "system of human freedom". [source: SEP-F §2]
- **Against the thing in itself:** Fichte held that a mind-independent "thing in itself" as external cause of sensations "is indefensible on Critical grounds" (taking Jacobi, Maimon and Schulze to heart). [source: SEP-F §2]
- **Two possible starting points (1797 Introductions):** "idealism" (begin with the pure I = freedom) vs "dogmatism" (begin with the thing in itself = necessity, determinism). Neither can refute the other theoretically; but dogmatism cannot get from things to representations, while idealism can in principle explain experience from the necessary acts of the intellect. Hence: "the kind of philosophy one chooses depends upon the kind of person one is." [source: SEP-F §3]
- **Steps (Foundation 1794/95, as reconstructed by SEP/IEP):**
  1. "The I posits itself" — more precisely "posits itself as self-positing"; "to posit" means to be aware of / reflect on, not to create. Consciousness presupposes self-consciousness (Kant's "I think"). This original act is a *Tathandlung* ("fact/act"): act and product at once; the I is not a thing or substance but "what it posits itself to be". [source: SEP-F §4.1]
  2. The I finds within its representations a difference between those "accompanied by a feeling of necessity" and those "accompanied by a feeling of freedom"; to recognise this is "to posit a distinction between the I and the not-I", i.e. the self and whatever exists independently of it. [source: IEP-F]
  3. The I cannot be the ground of its own limitation; it simply meets a "check" (*Anstoß*) on its activity, which it then posits as feeling, sensation, intuition of a thing and concept. The not-I is posited "precisely in order to 'explain'" the check — the check is "not something foreign to the I" but its encounter with its own finitude. [source: SEP-F §4.1]
  4. Conclusion: the "absolute I" is an abstraction; the only actual I is "a finite, empirical, embodied, individual self", free yet limited, whose freedom becomes "endless striving"; "the practical power is the innermost root of the I". [sources: SEP-F §4.1; IEP-F]
  5. **Intersubjectivity:** to posit itself as an individual the I must find itself "summoned" by another free being to limit its freedom out of respect for the other's; "mutual recognition of rational individuals" is a condition of I-hood (*Foundations of Natural Right*). This is the root of Hegel's theory of recognition. [sources: SEP-F §4.4; SEP-Rec §1.1]
- **Objection:** that Fichte is a solipsist creating the world from the ego. **Reply (Breazeale):** "Despite widespread misunderstanding ... the Wissenschaftslehre is not a theory of the absolute I"; transcendental philosophy cannot deduce the *Anstoß* or why objects have the particular properties they do; Fichte insisted "there is no conflict between transcendental idealism and the commonsense realism of everyday life". [source: SEP-F §2, §4.1] A further objection (Schelling, Hegel): a one-sided "subjective" idealism; Breazeale calls Hegel's history "self-serving" and notes Fichte was long treated as "a superseded rung on the ladder 'from Kant to Hegel'". [source: SEP-F §5]
- Fichte's quarrel with Schelling (c. 1800–02) was over whether transcendental philosophy permits a speculative a priori philosophy of nature. [source: SEP-F §4.2]

## 4. Fichte and nationalism (debate)

- The *Addresses* "later obtained a place of dubious honor as founding documents in the history of German nationalism", but (Breazeale) are mainly about national identity, language and national education, "understood by Fichte as means toward a larger, cosmopolitan end". [source: SEP-F §1]
- Russell (*History of Western Philosophy*, 1945, p. 718): Fichte laid the theoretical foundations of German nationalism. [source: WP-Addresses_to_the_German_Nation]
- WP (citing Allen Wood 2016): Fichte's nationalism was cultural, built on Herder; the Nazis later claimed him as a forerunner; his 1793 French Revolution book argued against civil rights for Jews ("a state within a state"), but a footnote there pleads for their free exercise of religion, and he resigned as Berlin rector when colleagues refused to punish harassment of Jewish students. The *Addresses*' aim: to "found an empire of spirit and reason, and to annihilate completely the crude physical force that rules of the world" (Cambridge 2008 trans., p. 496 per WP). [source: WP-Johann_Gottlieb_Fichte §Nationalism] CAVEAT: WP calls the 1793 text an "unpublished letter", which contradicts SEP-F and WP-Atheism_dispute (published anonymously, 1793/94). Say "his anonymously published 1793 defence of the French Revolution".
- Late Fichte: SEP: later practical writings "betray a darker view of human nature and defend a more authoritarian view of the state". [source: SEP-F §1]
- Published History chapter (Nationalism) says "one influential reading holds that Fichte ... pushed German nationalism in an ethnic rather than a civic direction" — compatible; present Breazeale's cosmopolitan reading as the counter-view.

## 5. Schelling

- Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph (von) Schelling, b. Leonberg near Stuttgart 27 Jan 1775; d. Bad Ragaz, Switzerland, 20 Aug 1854. Tübingen seminary 1790–95, close friend of Hegel and Hölderlin; Leipzig 1797; professor at Jena 1798–1803 (via Goethe's influence; WP: called Oct 1798 aged 23); Würzburg 1803–06; mainly Munich from 1806 (Erlangen 1820–27); Berlin 1841. [sources: SEP-S §1; WP-Friedrich_Wilhelm_Joseph_Schelling]
- Early Fichtean works: *On the Possibility of a Form of Philosophy in General* (1794), *Of the I as Principle of Philosophy* (1795), *Philosophical Letters on Dogmatism and Criticism* (1795). [sources: SEP-S §2; WP]
- **Naturphilosophie** (from 1797): *Ideas for a Philosophy of Nature* (1797), *On the World-Soul* (1798), *First Outline of a System of the Philosophy of Nature* (1799). Nature in itself is "productivity", appearing only in "products" that are the productivity inhibiting itself, "like the eddies in a stream"; nature is an ascending series of "potencies" each with an internal polarity — the model is a magnet; "the 'principle of all explanation of nature'" is "universal duality". Nature "could be construed as a kind of 'super-subject'" that produces the subjectivity by which it understands itself; "sometime the point must come where mind and matter are One". [sources: SEP-S §2; WP-Naturphilosophie] SEP: "many of its empirical claims are now indefensible". [SEP-S intro]
- **System of Transcendental Idealism (1800)**: a "history of self-consciousness" in which an "absolute I" comes retrospectively to know itself, its founding stages "unconscious"; "a version of the model ... will be adopted by Hegel in the Phenomenology"; art is the culmination of the system, the route to what cannot be an object of knowledge (WP quotes Schelling calling art "the sole document and the eternal organ of philosophy", German given as *das einzige wahre und ewige Organon zugleich und Dokument der Philosophie*). [sources: SEP-S §2; WP-Schelling §Reception]
- **Identity philosophy**: usually dated from the 1801 *Presentation of My System of Philosophy* to before the 1809 *Freedom* essay; the I is a *result*, not (as in Fichte) the originating act — a move towards Spinoza; subject and object must "in some way already be the same" for judgement to be possible; the Absolute as an "absolute indifference" prior to the subject–object distinction (IEP). [sources: SEP-S §3; IEP-S]
- *Philosophical Investigations into the Essence of Human Freedom* (1809) — "the last book published during his life". [source: WP-Schelling]
- **Hegel's jab**: Phenomenology Preface (§16 in Miller's numbering, per WS-2): the Absolute as "the night in which ... all cows are black" (see Quotations). SEP-S: this "has often been understood" as Hegel's verdict on Schelling, but "This is not a valid interpretation of Schelling's argument"; Schelling had himself written that "most people see in the essence of the Absolute nothing but pure night" and promised "to show here ... how that night of the Absolute can be turned into day for knowledge" (SW I/4, p. 403). Hegel did not name Schelling; Schelling wrote asking whether the target was his followers or himself, and Hegel never replied; the friendship ended. [sources: SEP-S §3; SEP-H §1; WP-Schelling §Munich; WP-The_Phenomenology_of_Spirit]
- **Late philosophy and Berlin**: after Hegel's death Schelling publicly attacked Hegelianism (1834 preface to a Cousin translation); "negative" (logical, rational) vs "positive" philosophy (of existence, mythology and revelation): the identity of thought and being "cannot be articulated within thought"; existence is not a consequence of the concept. Invited to Berlin in 1841 to Hegel's old chair, "reputedly because the government ... had wanted to counter the influence" of Hegelianism. His lectures "were initially attended by such luminaries as Kierkegaard, Engels, Bakunin, Ranke, Burkhardt, and Alexander von Humboldt" but he was soon "largely ignored". Kierkegaard complained he talked "quite insufferable nonsense" (WP); Bakunin found them "interesting but rather insignificant" (WP). His critique of Hegel influenced Kierkegaard, Marx, Nietzsche and Heidegger. [sources: SEP-S intro, §1, §5; SEP-H §1; WP-Schelling] (Cross-ref sibling *Schopenhauer and Kierkegaard*; *Marx*.)
- Schelling's "philosophical Proteus" reputation vs. Bowie: "in fact often an impressively rigorous logical thinker" — a debate over continuity. [sources: SEP-S intro; WP-Schelling]

## 6. Women in the Jena circle

- **Caroline Schelling** (née Michaelis; widowed Böhmer; divorced Schlegel), b. Göttingen 2 Sep 1763 – d. Maulbronn 7 Sep 1809 (dysentery). Daughter of the orientalist J. D. Michaelis; in Mainz joined Georg Forster's circle; imprisoned by the Prussians after they retook the Republic of Mainz (July 1793); married August Wilhelm Schlegel 1796; their Jena house was a centre of early Romanticism; credited with contributing to many of A. W. Schlegel's ~300 reviews (1796–99); divorced and married Schelling 2 June 1803; published reviews in her own name 1805–07; extensive Romantic correspondence. Her daughter Auguste Böhmer died in 1800; some blamed Schelling (Richards argues his role was likely irrelevant). [sources: WP-Caroline_Schelling; WP-Schelling]
- SEP-FS: Caroline Böhmer was "an important influence on the young [Friedrich] Schlegel". [source: SEP-FS §1]
- **Dorothea Schlegel** (born Brendel Mendelssohn, Berlin 24 Oct 1764 – Frankfurt 3 Aug 1839), daughter of Moses Mendelssohn; left Simon Veit (divorced 1799); lived with Friedrich Schlegel in Jena in the Schlegel household; novel *Florentin* (1801); married Schlegel 1804; both converted to Catholicism in April 1808. Friedrich's *Lucinde* (1799), widely thought autobiographical, caused a scandal; he wrote "On Philosophy, to Dorothea". [sources: WP-Dorothea_von_Schlegel; WP-Caroline_Schelling; SEP-FS §1 and bibliography]
- WRITER: present them as writers, critics and hosts of the Romantic circle; I found no source treating either as a systematic philosopher of German Idealism.

## 7. Hegel: life

- Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel, b. Stuttgart 27 Aug 1770, capital of the Duchy of Württemberg; d. Berlin 14 Nov 1831. [sources: SEP-H §1; WP-Hegel]
- Tübingen Stift (Protestant seminary) 1788–93 (SEP); roommates (WP: "had as roommates") Hölderlin (1770–1843) and Schelling (1775–1854), five years his junior. Probably attended because it was state-funded; had "a profound distaste for the study of orthodox theology" (Beiser). Enthusiastic about the French Revolution; reportedly toasted the storming of the Bastille every 14 July (Pinkard; SEP-SP repeats it). [sources: SEP-H §1; WP-Hegel; SEP-SP]
- House tutor in Bern 1793–96 and Frankfurt 1797–1800 (reunited with Hölderlin). Jena 1801: *Privatdozent* with the dissertation *De Orbitis Planetarum*; first book *The Difference between Fichte's and Schelling's System of Philosophy* (1801), siding with Schelling; co-edited the *Critical Journal of Philosophy* with Schelling 1802–03, which gave him a reputation as a "mere" follower of Schelling. Unsalaried extraordinary professor 1805. [sources: SEP-H §1; WP-Hegel]
- Illegitimate son Ludwig Fischer born Feb 1807 to Christiana Burkhardt; joined Hegel's household 1817; died in Dutch service in Batavia in 1831 before Hegel. [source: WP-Hegel (Pinkard 2000)]
- **Jena 1806 and Napoleon**: letter to Niethammer, Jena, 13 October 1806 (the day the French occupied Jena; the battle of Jena–Auerstedt was 14 October): "I saw the Emperor – this world-soul [*Weltseele*] – riding out of the city on reconnaissance. It is indeed a wonderful sensation to see such an individual, who, concentrated here at a single point, astride a horse, reaches out over the world and masters it." — trans. Clark Butler & Christiane Seiler, *Hegel: The Letters* (Indiana UP, 1984), p. 114. The proverbial "world-soul on horseback" (*Weltseele zu Pferde*) is a later paraphrase, often further changed to "world spirit on horseback". [sources: WP-Hegel (citing Hegel 1984c p. 114); WP-World_spirit; date: WS-1 (marxists.org letter header, Lukács, EB1911)] Pinkard: the remark is striking because Hegel had already written the *Phenomenology* passage saying the Revolution had passed to another land to be completed "in thought". [WP-Hegel citing Pinkard 2000 pp. 228–29]
- **Phenomenology of Spirit** (1807; publisher Joseph Anton Goebhardt, Bamberg and Würzburg): manuscript finished in haste for an October 1806 deadline; main body finished the night before the battle and sent through French lines to the printer in Bamberg. [sources: WP-The_Phenomenology_of_Spirit; SEP-H §1]
- Bamberg newspaper editor (*Bamberger Zeitung*, pro-French) 1807–08; headmaster of a Nuremberg gymnasium Nov 1808–1816 (SEP: "1808 to 1815"); married Marie von Tucher (1791–1855) in 1811. [sources: WP-Hegel; SEP-H §1; IEP-HS]
- **Science of Logic**: 1812, 1813, 1816 (Book I revised 1831, published 1832). **Encyclopaedia of the Philosophical Sciences**: 1817 (Heidelberg), revised 1827 and 1830; three parts: Logic, Philosophy of Nature, Philosophy of Spirit. [sources: WP-Hegel; SEP-H §1, §3.2]
- Heidelberg chair 1816; Berlin chair 1818 (vacant since Fichte's death in 1814), "the most prestigious position in the German philosophical world"; rector Oct 1829–Sept 1830; Order of the Red Eagle 1831. [sources: SEP-H §1; WP-Hegel]
- **Philosophy of Right**: appeared 1820; title page dated 1821 (SEP-H and SEP-SP say "published in 1821"; SEP-FH: "1820 ... titlepage has 1821"). WRITER: "published in 1820 (dated 1821)". [sources: SEP-FH bibliography; WP-Elements_of_the_Philosophy_of_Right; SEP-H §3.2.2]
- Lectures on aesthetics, religion, history of philosophy and philosophy of world history were published posthumously from his and students' notes. The world-history lectures were given five times (1822–23, 1824–25, 1826–27, 1828–29, 1830–31); first edited by Eduard Gans in 1837, second edition by his son Karl Hegel 1840 (SEP-H mentions only Karl's text). [sources: WP-Hegel lecture list; WP-Lectures_on_the_Philosophy_of_World_History; SEP-H §3.2.2] (Consistent with the History chapter: "lectures of the 1820s, published in 1837".)
- **Death**: cholera reached Berlin in Aug 1831; Hegel left for Kreuzberg, returned for the October semester believing it had subsided; died 14 Nov. "The physicians pronounced the cause of death as cholera, but it is likely he died from another gastrointestinal disease" (Pinkard 2000). Buried 16 Nov in the Dorotheenstadt cemetery next to Fichte and Solger. Heine's reported last words ("only one man who ever understood me...") are described as apocryphal. [source: WP-Hegel] WRITER: "officially of cholera, though his biographer Pinkard thinks another stomach illness more likely".

## 8. The Phenomenology: what kind of book

- A propaedeutic leading the reader to "the standpoint of purely conceptual thought"; compared to a *Bildungsroman*; "shapes of consciousness" are tested and collapse through internal contradiction into new ones; later chapters become "configurations of human social life", mapping "the history of western European civilization from the Greeks to his own time", ending in Absolute Knowing. Chapters: Consciousness (Sense-certainty, Perception, Understanding), Self-consciousness, Reason, Spirit, Religion, Absolute Knowing. [sources: SEP-H §3.1.1; WP-The_Phenomenology_of_Spirit]
- Sense-certainty: even the most immediate contents are grasped conceptually ("this", "here", "now" are universals); compared to Sellars' critique of the "myth of the given". [source: SEP-H §3.1.1]
- Hegel's method: immanent critique — consciousness supplies its own criterion and the investigation is "a comparison of consciousness with itself"; the path is a "pathway of despair" for natural consciousness. [source: WP-The_Phenomenology_of_Spirit (Stern 2013)]
- English translations: Baillie (1910), Miller (1977), Inwood (2018) use "Lordship and Bondage"; Pinkard (2018) and Fuss & Dobbins (2019) use "Mastery and Servitude". [source: WP-Lord–bondsman_dialectic note]

## 9. Dialectic: what Hegel actually describes

- **"Thesis–antithesis–synthesis" is not Hegel's formula.** Kaufmann (1959, p. 154): "Fichte introduced into German philosophy the three-step of thesis, antithesis, and synthesis, using these three terms. Schelling took up this terminology. *Hegel did not.* He never once used these three terms together to designate three stages in an argument or account in any of his books." The terminology was spread by Heinrich Moritz Chalybäus (1796–1862), whose *Historische Entwicklung der spekulativen Philosophie von Kant bis Hegel* (1837; lectures given in Dresden 1835–36) characterised Hegel's dialectic as thesis–antithesis–synthesis. Classic critique: G. E. Mueller, "The Hegel Legend of 'Thesis-Antithesis-Synthesis'", *Journal of the History of Ideas* 19:3 (1958), 411–14. Hegel warned against reducing dialectic to a "lifeless schema" (WP-Dialectic, via Dafermos). Popper attributed the formula to Hegel. [sources: WP-Hegel §Thesis–antithesis–synthesis; WP-Heinrich_Moritz_Chalybäus; WP-Dialectic; SEP-D §3] Fichte's own triadic use: WP-Fichte cites his 1794 *Aenesidemus* review (Breazeale trans., *Early Philosophical Writings*, p. 63).
- SEP-D: the "textbook" Being–Nothing–Becoming triad fits the thesis–antithesis–synthesis reading, but "we must be careful ... not to apply this textbook example too dogmatically"; other sections (Measure → Essence; Purpose; the two-part section on Cognition) do not fit. [source: SEP-D §3]
- **Three moments** (Encyclopaedia Logic §§79–82): (1) the moment of the **understanding** — fixed, seemingly stable determinations; (2) the **dialectical** moment — a one-sidedness comes to the fore and the determination "passes into its opposite" ("self-sublation", EL §81); (3) the **speculative** or positively rational moment — grasps the unity of the opposed determinations. [source: SEP-D §2]
- **Aufheben / sublation**: has "a doubled meaning: it means both to cancel (or negate) and to preserve at the same time" (PhG §113; SL). Becoming sublates Being and Nothing: replaces them yet depends on them for its definition. [source: SEP-D §2–3]
- **Determinate negation**: unlike scepticism, which "only ever sees pure nothingness in its result", the result is "the nothingness of that from which it results"; "the result is conceived as it is in truth, namely, as a determinate negation [bestimmte Negation]; a new form has thereby immediately arisen" (PhG-M §79, Miller). Hence "nothing extraneous is introduced" and the method claims necessity, which is why Hegel calls it logic. [source: SEP-D §2]
- **Being–Nothing–Becoming (worked example)**: pure Being, wholly indeterminate, "is really no different from" pure Nothing (EL §87); each passes into the other; their unity is Becoming, "the immediate vanishing of the one in the other" (SL-M 83). Not a reductio leading to nothing, but a positive new concept. [source: SEP-D §3]
- Why dialectic: against Kant's confinement of reason to "our heads" — Hegel holds reason is "the soul of the world, inhabits it, and is immanent in it" (lecture quotation in SEP-D); he took Kant's antinomies (reason producing contradictions) as a positive engine rather than a warning. [source: SEP-D §1]
- Logic debates: Priest reads Hegel's logic as dialetheic/paraconsistent; Redding finds parallels with intuitionistic logic. [source: SEP-H §2.4]

## 10. Lordship and bondage (MAIN ARGUMENT, step by step)

Location: Phenomenology ch. IV, "Independent and Dependent Self-Consciousness: Lordship and Bondage" (Miller §§178–196 per WP's Miller citations at §§179–192), first subsection of "Self-Consciousness", after "Life" and "Desire", followed by Stoicism, Scepticism and the Unhappy Consciousness. [source: WP-Lord–bondsman_dialectic]

- **P1 (self-consciousness needs recognition).** Following an idea in Fichte, Hegel makes consciousness of objects depend on self-consciousness, and self-consciousness depend on "recognition (or acknowledgment—Anerkennung) of other self-conscious subjects", who in turn recognise oneself. [source: SEP-H §3.1.1] Desire alone (consuming objects) cannot give self-certainty. [source: WP-Lord–bondsman_dialectic §Recognition]
- **P2 (the encounter).** Two self-consciousnesses first meet "like ordinary objects ... submerged in the being of Life"; each wants its being-for-self acknowledged by the other while regarding the other as an "unessential, negatively characterized object" (Miller §186). [source: WP-Lord–bondsman_dialectic, Miller ¶¶179–189]
- **P3 (life-and-death struggle).** Each proves its freedom by showing that its normative status matters more to it than "any of her (animal) desires, including ... her desire to live". [source: SEP-Rec §1.1] If one dies, recognition fails ("abstract negation"). [source: WP-Lord–bondsman_dialectic]
- **P4 (lordship).** One, fearing death, submits: the one who risked life becomes lord, the other bondsman. [sources: IEP-HS; SEP-Rec]
- **P5 (the lord's failure).** The lord gets only "deficient recognition", since he cannot see himself reflected in one he does not recognise as free; "the recognizer has proven to be a 'mere' slave who does not count as an autonomous and competent judge"; the lord is dependent on the bondsman and on enjoying the bondsman's products. [sources: IEP-HS; SEP-Rec §1.1]
- **P6 (the bondsman's labour and formation).** Through fear of death, discipline and work, the bondsman "transforms his subservience into a mastery over his environment"; "in objectifying himself in his environment through his labor the bondsman in effect realizes himself". [source: IEP-HS]
- **C (conclusion).** Asymmetric recognition cannot satisfy; "adequate recognition can only be achieved within an institutionalized order of rights that secures genuinely mutual recognition" (Williams); the bondsman's self-determination is "limited and incomplete" until "a realm of ethical life" in which universal freedom is reflected in each member. [sources: SEP-Rec §1.1; IEP-HS] Brandom's gloss: asymmetric recognition is "authority without responsibility, on the side of the Master, and responsibility without authority, on the side of the Slave". [source: WP-Lord–bondsman_dialectic (Brandom interview 2008)]
- Lectures on world history: "Humankind has not liberated itself *from* servitude but *by means of* servitude" (German, Lasson ed.: "Es ist die Menschheit nicht sowohl aus der Knechtschaft befreit worden, als vielmehr durch die Knechtschaft"; English per WP, translator unnamed). [source: WP-Lord–bondsman_dialectic]
- Earlier version: Jena *Realphilosophie* (1805/06) recast Hobbes' state-of-nature struggle as a struggle "not of self-interest but of recognition". [source: SEP-Rec §1.1]
- **Interpretive debate:**
  - Kojève (lectures in Paris 1933–39; *Introduction à la lecture de Hegel*, ed. Queneau, 1947; abridged English trans. J. H. Nichols, ed. Allan Bloom, 1969) made the passage famous; SEP-H: the topic "was first made prominent by Alexandre Kojève". His anthropological reading: history begins with the first struggle and ends when master and slave are overcome in the "universal and homogeneous state" (Napoleon). Audience included Queneau, Bataille, Merleau-Ponty, Breton, Lacan, Aron; influenced Sartre and Lacan. Fukuyama's "end of history" (1989/1992) drew on Hegel, Marx and Kojève. [sources: WP-Alexandre_Kojève; WP-Lord–bondsman_dialectic; SEP-H §1, §3.1.1; SEP-Rec; published History chapter *Post-Cold War*]
  - Non-Kojèvean recognition readings: Honneth (1995), Williams (1992, 1997), Pippin (2008), Ikäheimo & Laitinen (2011). [source: SEP-H §3.1.1]
  - Text-internal objection (Renault, *Radical Philosophy* 210): Hegel's "Mastery and Servitude" "does not actually concern a struggle to the death of slave" — undercutting readings of it as a slave-revolt narrative. [WS-11, weaker]
  - **Buck-Morss's Haiti thesis**: "Hegel and Haiti", *Critical Inquiry* 26:4 (Summer 2000), 821–865; expanded as *Hegel, Haiti, and Universal History* (Pittsburgh, 2009): Hegel must have known of the Saint-Domingue revolt (1791–1804) through contemporary reports, notably in the German journal *Minerva*. Priority dispute: Pierre-Franklin Tavarès, "Hegel et Haïti" (*Chemins critiques*, 1992), which Buck-Morss acknowledged. Anders Stephanson (*New Left Review* II/61) calls *Minerva* her "Exhibit A" and says the claim needs more argument. [sources: WP-Susan_Buck-Morss; WP-Lord–bondsman_dialectic; WS-11 (weaker)] (Consistent with *The Enlightenment and Rousseau*, which calls the book "contested" and points to this chapter.)
  - **Feminist readings**: Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* drew on the lord–bondsman relation (via Kojève); Patricia Jagentowicz Mills (ed.), *Feminist Interpretations of G.W.F. Hegel* (Penn State UP, 1996); Hutchings & Pulkkinen (eds.), *Hegel's Philosophy and Feminist Thought: Beyond Antigone?* (Palgrave, 2010). [sources: WP-Lord–bondsman_dialectic; WP-Hegel; SEP-SP bibliography]
  - **Fanon** (*Black Skin, White Masks*): in a footnote, the colonial master differs from Hegel's: he wants from the slave "not recognition but work" (translations vary; WS-14 locates it c. pp. 220–21 of Markmann's 1967 translation). Scholars dispute whether Fanon rejects Hegelian recognition or (Bird-Pollan) accepts it and shows how racism distorts it. [sources: WP-Hegel §Influence; WS-14 (weaker)]

## 11. Objective spirit, Philosophy of Right, history

- Encyclopaedia's Philosophy of Spirit = subjective spirit (psychology), objective spirit (social institutions; expanded as the *Philosophy of Right*), absolute spirit. [source: SEP-H §3.2.2]
- PR structure: Abstract Right (property as reciprocal recognition, §71; wrong; punishment as "negation of the negation" §§97–104), Morality, Ethical Life (*Sittlichkeit*, §142): family (love, §§158–60), civil society, state. Hegel follows Fichte in a recognitive analysis of property. Civil society's unfettered market "produces a class caught in a spiral of poverty" (Hegel took this from Smith but rejected "trickle down"; Marx later drew a different conclusion). Constitutional monarch's "I will" closes the "recognitive circle". [source: SEP-H §3.2.2]
- **Doppelsatz** (Preface): IEP-HS gives "What is rational is actual and what is actual is rational" (translator not named); Nisbet (1991, p. 20) per WS-12: "What is rational is actual; and what is actual is rational". "Actual" (*wirklich*) means not the merely existent but what corresponds to its rational concept. Read by Haym (1857) as justifying absolutist Prussia; Popper called Hegel "the father of modern historicism and totalitarianism", relying on the "march of God in the world", which is from a student Addition (§258 Add.), not Hegel's text; Avineri (1972) and Knox (1970) gave the "liberal" reading; SEP-SP: "Today, most commentators agree that Popper and others were wrong". [sources: IEP-HS §7; SEP-SP; SEP-H §2.2; WS-12]
- "Philosophy ... is its own time comprehended in thoughts" (PR Preface, Nisbet p. 21). [source: SEP-H §2]
- **Women (PR §§165–166)**: Hegel saw men and women as embodying different features (§165); "only men have the opportunity to engage outside the home in civil society and fully participate in the state"; "rightly ... subjected to widespread criticism by feminists" (Ravven 1988, Halper 2001, Knowles 2002, Stone 2002, Hutchings & Pulkkinen 2010). [source: SEP-SP §Family] The famous sentences are in the **Addition** to §166 (students' lecture notes): Knox trans. (1973 printing, p. 263) as quoted by SEP-FH — see Quotations. (Consistent with the published *Women Philosophers of the Early Modern Era*, which quotes Knox and says "§166 with its lecture addition".) Hegel forbids arranged marriages (§162), permits divorce (§163 Add.). [SEP-SP]
- **Philosophy of history**: world history is the development of spirit's consciousness of freedom; history is presented as a theodicy; "cunning of reason"; history as "slaughter-bench"; "the periods of happiness in it are the blank pages of history". The one/some/all scheme: "the Orientals ... only know that One is free"; Greeks and Romans "only knew that Some ... are free"; "The Germanic nations, with the rise of Christianity, were the first to realize that All men are by nature free" (translation per WP, apparently the Nisbet 1975 *Introduction*, translator not stated by WP). "World history travels from east to west; for Europe is the absolute end of history, just as Asia is the beginning." [source: WP-Lectures_on_the_Philosophy_of_World_History] IEP-HS: four realms — Oriental, Greek, Roman, Germanic (Germany, Nordic peoples, France, Italy, Spain, England); milestones Reformation, Enlightenment, French Revolution. [IEP-HS]
- SEP-H: "Hegel's is a clearly Eurocentric account of history" (Pinkard 2017 tries to divest the basic account of it); philosophy of history takes empirical history as material, not deducing it. [source: SEP-H §3.2.2]
- **Africa**: WP-Hegel: the Anthropology (Encyclopaedia) contains comments "now ... recognized as openly racist", e.g. claims about the "naturally" lower development of Black people, which Hegel tied to climate rather than fixed race (Fritzman). The world-history lectures exclude Africa from history: "no historical part of the World" in the old Sibree translation (WS-9; same wording already used in the published History *Thematic history overview*). Scholarship: Robert Bernasconi, "Hegel at the Court of the Ashanti" (in Barnett ed., *Hegel After Derrida*, 1998) argues Hegel pushed already-biased travel sources (e.g. Bowdich 1819) further in a negative direction; exchange with Joseph McCarney, *Radical Philosophy* 119 (2003) ("Hegel's Racism? A Response to Bernasconi" / Bernasconi, "Hegel's racism: A reply to McCarney"); Teshale Tibebu, *Hegel and the Third World: The Making of Eurocentrism in World History* (Syracuse UP, 2011): Hegel ranks Africans at the stage of sense, Asians of understanding, Europeans of reason. Defence (Fritzman): the east-to-west prejudice is "quite incidental to the substance" of Hegel's position. Bernasconi notes the old composite (Gans/Karl Hegel) edition complicates attribution; the "At this point we leave Africa" line is contested. [sources: WP-Hegel; WS-9, WS-10 (weaker)]
- Hegel on Kojève/Fukuyama "end of history": objection that freedom's scope and content have since expanded (women, colonised peoples). [source: WP-Hegel (Fritzman)]

## 12. Absolute Spirit: art, religion, philosophy

- The last 25 paragraphs of the Encyclopaedia's Philosophy of Spirit; expanded into three Berlin lecture series. [source: SEP-H §3.2.3]
- **Art**: "consciousness of the Absolute first takes shape" in art (PM §556), in sensuous form; symbolic (Persia, India, Egypt), classical (Greek), romantic (Christian/modern). "Art, considered in its highest vocation, is and remains for us a thing of the past" (Aesthetics 1:11, Knox) — not that art ends, but that it no longer has ultimate authority; "death of art" is a misreading (SEP-Aes; WP-Hegel: Hegel "never said any such thing"). Unknown Heidelberg transcripts (by F. W. Carové, 1816–18) found in 2022 by Klaus Vieweg in the Freising library. [sources: SEP-H §3.2.3; SEP-Aes; WP-Hegel]
- **Religion**: same content as philosophy (God) in the form of images (*Vorstellungen*); Christianity "the consummate religion"; suspected of pantheism/atheism. [source: SEP-H §3.2.3]
- **Philosophy**: "unity of Art and Religion" (PM §572). [source: SEP-H §3.2.3]
- Traditional reading: God as Absolute Spirit becoming self-conscious through finite minds (Taylor 1975, Rosen, Beiser 2005); Hegel: philosophy "has no other object but God and so is essentially rational theology" (Aes I:101). [source: SEP-H §2.2]

## 13. Metaphysical vs non-metaphysical (post-Kantian) readings (DEBATE)

- **Traditional metaphysical**: dominant to mid-20th c.; British Hegelians (Green, Bradley); Hegel as pre-critical metaphysician of Absolute Spirit; critics Russell, Moore, Popper. Recent: Taylor (1975), Rosen (1984), Beiser (2005), Horstmann (2006). [source: SEP-H §1, §2.2, §2.4]
- **Post-Kantian ("non-metaphysical")**: Klaus Hartmann (1972), Henrich, Horstmann (Germany); Pippin (*Hegel's Idealism*, 1989) and Pinkard (*Hegel's Phenomenology: The Sociality of Reason*, 1994): Hegel completes Kant's critique, "deindividualizing and historicizing Kant's idea of freedom"; Brandom and McDowell (via Sellars). The label "non-metaphysical" "came to be dropped for the unwanted suggestion that Hegel espoused no metaphysics"; Pippin (2019): a metaphysics "in a new sense". [source: SEP-H §2.3–2.4]
- **Revised metaphysical**: Houlgate (2005, 2022), Stern, Westphal, Kreines, Karen Ng (2020), McNulty: Hegel accepts Kant's critique of rationalism without abandoning metaphysics; Houlgate: the Logic starts with "thought itself as pure being" — an ontology. Critics of the post-Kantians say they project contemporary views onto Hegel. [source: SEP-H §2.4]
- Redding (SEP author) sees the debate shifting to metaphilosophy. [source: SEP-H §2.4]

## 14. After Hegel: Right, Centre and Left Hegelians

- After 1831 followers split into right, centre and left, initially over religion (Toews 1985). **Strauss**, *Das Leben Jesu* (*The Life of Jesus Critically Examined*), 1835–36: Gospel narratives as "myths" of the early Christian community; Strauss himself coined the labels, borrowing the French parliament's seating: Right (Old Hegelians) = Gospels fully compatible with Hegel; Centre; Left (Young Hegelians) = incompatible. "No Hegelians of the period ever referred to themselves as 'Right Hegelians'." [sources: SEP-H §3.2.3; WP-Young_Hegelians; WP-Right_Hegelians]
- Right: Göschel, Gabler, Hinrichs, Daub, Henning, Hotho (Rosenkranz, Gans, Michelet with reservations); associated with Berlin University, Protestant orthodoxy and Restoration politics. Recent studies question the left/right paradigm (Löwith). [source: WP-Right_Hegelians]
- Left/Young (late 1830s–mid-1840s, Berlin): **Feuerbach** (1804–1872), *The Essence of Christianity* (1841): God is a projection of the human "species-essence" ("the richer is God, the poorer is man"); **Bruno Bauer** (6 Sep 1809 – 13 Apr 1882), philosophy of "infinite self-consciousness", religion as alienation; dismissed from Bonn in spring 1842 for his New Testament writings; opposed Prussian Jewish emancipation 1842–43; **Arnold Ruge** edited the *Hallische Jahrbücher*; Cieszkowski (*Prolegomena zur Historiosophie*, 1838) coined *praxis* and turned the owl of Minerva into an "Eagle of Apollo"; Moses Hess; Max Stirner (*The Ego and Its Own*, 1844); **Marx** broke with Bauer in "On the Jewish Question" (1843). Movement dissolved by end of 1844. [sources: WP-Young_Hegelians; SEP-Feu intro; SEP-B intro, §1; SEP-Marx §4] (Cross-ref *Marx*.)
- SEP-H: Marx "inverted" Hegel's teleological history into a materialist theory culminating in communism; Western Marxists (Lukács 1923) later re-imported Hegelian elements. [source: SEP-H intro, §1]

- *Spinoza*: Hegel's "to be a follower of Spinoza is the essential commencement of all philosophy" and "either a Spinozist or not a philosopher at all"; Pantheism Controversy (Jacobi 1785) — consistent; SEP-S also dates Jacobi's book 1785 (2nd ed. 1789).
- *The Enlightenment and Rousseau*: Buck-Morss "contested", forward reference to this chapter — consistent. Also Hegel blamed the general will for the Terror.
- *Women Philosophers of the Early Modern Era*: PR (1820), §166 with lecture addition, Knox — consistent; use the same translation.
- *History of Philosophy* overview: "Hegel (d. 1831)", master–slave as "most famous set piece", owl of Minerva at dusk — consistent. Its phrase "stages of contradiction and resolution" is fine; avoid adding "thesis–antithesis–synthesis".
- History *Napoleon*: Hegel saw Napoleon "the day before the battle" and called him "this world-soul" — consistent (letter 13 Oct, battle 14 Oct 1806).
- History *Nationalism*: Addresses in occupied Berlin 1807–08 — consistent (SEP-F's stray "1806" is an internal slip).
- History *Modern Africa* / *What Is History* / *Thematic overview*: Hegel's lectures "of the 1820s, published in 1837"; Africa "no historical part of the World" (old translation) — consistent with WP (Gans 1837) and WS.
- History *Post-Cold War*: Fukuyama drew on Hegel, Marx and Kojève — consistent.
- *Neoplatonism*: Hegel called Proclus "the culminating point" — not checked here (not needed).
- No contradictions found.

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use
- Exact Miller wording of Phenomenology §178 ("Self-consciousness exists in and for itself when, and by the fact that, it so exists for another; that is, it exists only in being acknowledged"): WS-13 found variant renderings ("as something acknowledged" in Beiser's quotation); could not open Miller. Paraphrase instead.
- Exact Sibree wording "The History of the world is none other than the progress of the consciousness of Freedom": only WS-8 summaries (Brians/WSU "Sibree, adapted by Michael Neville"; Philosophy Now); marxists.org has "World history is the progress of the consciousness of freedom". If used, say "in Sibree's translation, roughly:" or paraphrase; the WP translation "represents the development of the spirit's consciousness of its own freedom and of the consequent realization of this freedom" (translator unnamed) is the safer verified line.
- Fuller Africa passage ("it has no movement or development to exhibit", "At this point we leave Africa"): not seen in any source text; the latter's attribution is itself contested (Bernasconi, WS-10).
- Nisbet's wording of the owl of Minerva sentence and of §166 Addition (WS-12, WS-12b inconclusive). Use Dyde (owl) and Knox (§166).
- "World-soul on horseback" as Hegel's words: a later paraphrase (WP-World_spirit); Hegel wrote "this world-soul ... riding out of the city". The original German "zu Pferde sitzend" not seen.
- Heine's report of Hegel's last words ("only one man understood me ...") — apocryphal per WP.
- That Hegel died "of cholera" stated flatly — official cause only; Pinkard doubts it.
- Neighbourly claims that Hegel, Schelling and Hölderlin shared a single room above the Stift entrance: WP caption says only "believed to have shared".
- That Caroline Schelling co-translated Shakespeare with A. W. Schlegel — not in sources read.
- Fanon's exact English wording and page — WS only, translations vary.
- Fichte's death "of typhus": only a WP category; SEP says "fatal infection" caught from his wife.
- That Kierkegaard attended Schelling's lectures in "winter 1841–42" specifically — SEP says only that Schelling moved to Berlin in 1841 and Kierkegaard attended.
- Kaufmann book title/year (WP cites "Kaufmann 1959 p. 154" — likely a different work); cite as Kaufmann without a title.
