## 1. The question: is beauty objective?
- SEP-Beauty frames "the most familiar basic issue in the theory of beauty" as whether beauty is subjective ("in the eye of the beholder") or an objective feature of things; "A pure version of either of these positions seems implausible". [source: SEP-Beauty §1]
- Case against pure subjectivism: if anything anyone finds beautiful is beautiful (as James Kirwan, *Beauty*, 1999, asserts), the word seems to communicate nothing beyond approval; yet judgements coincide remarkably ("it would be odd or perverse ... to deny that a perfect rose or a dramatic sunset was beautiful") and we argue and give reasons about beauty. [source: SEP-Beauty §1]
- Case against pure objectivism: it would imply that a world with no perceivers could be beautiful, or that beauty could be detected by instruments; "All plausible accounts of beauty connect it to a pleasurable or profound or loving response". [source: SEP-Beauty §1]
- Until the 18th century most accounts treated beauty as objective. Augustine, *De vera religione*, asks whether things are beautiful because they give delight or give delight because they are beautiful, and "emphatically opts for the second". [source: SEP-Beauty §1]
- 18th-century British shift: pleasure becomes "not the effect but the origin of beauty", influenced by Locke's primary/secondary quality distinction (colour as mind-dependent; variation among perceivers as an argument). [source: SEP-Beauty §1]
- The "antinomy of taste" in SEP-Beauty's general sense: taste is proverbially subjective (*de gustibus non est disputandum*), yet we dispute about taste and treat some people as exemplars of good or bad taste. Hume and Kant both treat judgements of beauty as neither purely subjective nor purely objective but "inter-subjective". [source: SEP-Beauty §1]

## 2. Classical and medieval views
- The "classical conception": beauty "consists of an arrangement of integral parts into a coherent whole, according to proportion, harmony, symmetry, and similar notions" — "a primordial Western conception of beauty". [source: SEP-Beauty §2.1]
- Aristotle: *Poetics* 1450b34 — "to be beautiful, a living creature, and every whole made up of parts, must ... present a certain order in its arrangement of parts"; *Metaphysics* 1078a36 — "The chief forms of beauty are order and symmetry and definiteness, which the mathematical sciences demonstrate in a special degree" (Barnes ed. translation). [source: SEP-Beauty §2.1]
- Polykleitos' *Canon* (late 5th/early 4th c. BCE) was both a statue and a now-lost treatise on proportion; Galen describes it as specifying the proportions "of everything to everything" (quoted in Pollitt 1974). Greek *symmetria* meant measurable harmonious proportion, richer than modern bilateral symmetry; Plato's *Sophist* 228c–e calls virtuous souls symmetrical. Vitruvius gives the canonical Roman formulation (order, arrangement, proportion, symmetry, decor, distribution). [source: SEP-Beauty §2.1]
- Tatarkiewicz's "Great Theory": Władysław Tatarkiewicz (1886–1980) named the proportion theory "the Great Theory" of beauty — beauty as proportion and arrangement of parts — in "The Great Theory of Beauty and Its Decline" (*Journal of Aesthetics and Art Criticism*, 1972) and in *A History of Six Ideas* (English 1980; Polish *Dzieje sześciu pojęć*, 2nd ed. 1976), whose chapter on beauty has sections "The Great Theory" and "Crisis of the Great Theory"; on his account it held from classical Greece until its decline in the late 18th century. [sources: WP-Władysław_Tatarkiewicz (book dates); WS-6 (article title/year and content, secondary — hedge as "Tatarkiewicz argued")]
- Hippias Major: Socrates and the sophist Hippias try to define *to kalon*; Hippias offers three answers (e.g. "a beautiful young woman is beautiful", 287e; gold; being rich, healthy, honoured and well buried), then Socrates tries his own: the appropriate (*to prepon*, 290d–294e), the useful, the beneficial, and "pleasure through hearing and sight"; all fail and the dialogue ends with the proverb "beautiful things are difficult". [sources: SEP-PlatoAes §1.1; WP-Hippias_Major]
- *Kalon* is broader than "beautiful" (also fine, noble); Paul Woodruff translates it "the fine". [source: SEP-PlatoAes §1; WP-Hippias_Major]
- Authenticity: considered Platonic in antiquity, doubted in the 19th–20th centuries (Dorothy Tarrant's 1928 edition argued spuriousness; G.M.A. Grube defended it); "Most scholars agree that Plato wrote the Hippias Major" today. [source: SEP-PlatoAes §1.1; WP-Hippias_Major]
- SEP-PlatoAes notes that Socrates' "appropriate" definition anticipates the 18th-century question of whether beauty depends on satisfying the concept of the object — Kant's "dependent beauty" (§16). [source: SEP-PlatoAes §1.1]
- Plato's Form of beauty: beauty is Plato's most frequent example of a Form; beautiful particulars are beautiful only equivocally (they fade, depend on context, provoke disagreement), and "Beauty alone is both a Form and a sensory experience" (Phaedrus 250d). [source: SEP-PlatoAes §1.4]
- Symposium ladder (210a–211d; the ascent culminates in the vision of "the beautiful itself" and the claim that life is worth living for one who sees it): one body → all bodies → souls → laws and institutions → learning → beauty itself. SEP-Beauty contrasts this "idealist conception" (beauty as perfect unity) with the classical proportion view. [source: SEP-Beauty §2.2, quoting Symposium 210a–211d in Hamilton & Cairns]
- Plotinus, Ennead I.6 ("On Beauty"), against the symmetry theory: "Almost everyone declares that the symmetry of parts towards each other and towards a whole, with, besides, a certain charm of colour, constitutes the beauty recognized by the eye... But think what this means. Only a compound can be beautiful, never anything devoid of parts... All the loveliness of colour and even the light of the sun, being devoid of parts and so not beautiful by symmetry, must be ruled out of the realm of beauty. And how comes gold to be a beautiful thing? And lightning by night, and the stars, why are these so fair?" (MacKenna & Page trans.). The argument has two prongs: (i) simple things (colours, sunlight, gold, single tones) are beautiful but have no parts; (ii) a beautiful whole cannot be "constructed out of ugliness", so the parts must be beautiful too, which proportion cannot explain. [source: SEP-Beauty §2.2]
- Plotinus' positive view: beauty is "communion in Ideal-Form"; ugliness is matter "not yielding at all points" to form; fire is the most beautiful body; the response is "wonderment and a delicious trouble, longing and love and a trembling that is all delight". [source: SEP-Beauty §1, §2.2]
- Medieval continuations: Pseudo-Dionysius (6th c.) presents creation as yearning toward God as beauty; Umberto Eco (1959, 2004) argued a mystical delight in beauty coexisted with ascetic anti-aestheticism through the Middle Ages; Abbot Suger of St-Denis (12th c.) describes being "transported" anagogically by the gems of his church. [source: SEP-Beauty §2.2]
- Augustine on beauty: God is the highest beauty; in physical things, equality (proportion) and unity make things beautiful (two windows on a wall should match). [source: IEP-Taste §1c]
- Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I, q. 39, a. 8: "There are three requirements for beauty. Firstly, integrity or perfection—for if something is impaired it is ugly. Then there is due proportion or consonance. And also clarity: whence things that are brightly coloured are called beautiful" (English Dominican Fathers' translation, as quoted by SEP-Beauty, which cites it as "Summa Theologica I, 39, 8"). Latin terms: *integritas sive perfectio*, *debita proportio sive consonantia*, *claritas*. The article's context is the attribution of beauty to the Son within the Trinity (writer may say so only generally; context not read in primary). [sources: SEP-Beauty §2.1; WS-7 for the Latin, secondary]
- Aquinas's other famous dictum, "beautiful things are those which please when seen" (*pulchra enim dicuntur quae visa placent*), is at ST I, q. 5, a. 4, ad 1; IEP stresses that "seen" implies contemplation, an act of the intellect, not mere subjective liking. [sources: IEP-Taste §1c (dictum and interpretation); WS-7 (reference I.5.4 ad 1, secondary)]
- IEP renders Aquinas's three conditions as "proportion, wholeness, and radiance" and glosses radiance as how an object "shines" before the beholder. [source: IEP-Taste §1c]

## 3. Eighteenth-century taste theories
- Shaftesbury (Anthony Ashley Cooper, 3rd Earl, 1671–1713): *Characteristicks of Men, Manners, Opinions, Times* (first ed. 1711, anonymous, 3 vols.); his theory of taste is chiefly in the dialogue *The Moralists, a Philosophical Rhapsody*. [sources: IEP-Taste §3b; WP-Shaftesbury; SEP-18C §1.1]
- Shaftesbury's proto-disinterestedness: enjoyment of beauty must be "of the rational kind", unlike "interested" enjoyments that look to use or possession (sexual desire at a human form; a coin valued for what it buys rather than its design). SEP-Beauty also gives the landscape example: seeing a valley as real estate is not seeing its beauty. SEP-Beauty calls *The Moralists* "One important source of the concept of aesthetic disinterestedness". [sources: SEP-18C §1.1; SEP-Beauty §1]
- Shaftesbury's "Three Degrees or Orders of Beauty": "dead Forms" (made by man or nature), "the Forms which form" (minds), and the third order which forms even the forms which form (God); all beauty "resolves it-self" into the divine mind — hence his realism. [source: SEP-18C §1.1]
- Shaftesbury's "inward EYE": "No sooner is an object of the right kind placed before the mind than straight an inward EYE distinguishes, and sees the Fair and Shapely..." (*Moralists*). The idea of an inner sense of beauty goes back through Augustine and Plotinus (I.6.7–9) to Plato. [source: SEP-18C §1.1]
- Francis Hutcheson (1694–1746), *An Inquiry into the Original of Our Ideas of Beauty and Virtue* (1725), whose title page announces the principles of "the late Earl of Shaftsbury". [sources: SEP-18C §1.2; IEP-Taste §3c]
- Hutcheson's formula, verbatim (1725, p. 29 in Liberty Fund ed.): "What we call Beautiful in Objects, to speak in the Mathematical Style, seems to be in a compound Ratio of Uniformity and Variety; so that where the Uniformity of Bodys is equal, the Beauty is as the Variety; and where the Variety is equal, the Beauty is as the Uniformity." His usual short phrase is "uniformity amidst variety". [sources: SEP-Beauty §2.1; SEP-18C §1.2; IEP-Taste §3c]
- Hutcheson's examples: a square is more beautiful than an equilateral triangle, a hexagon more than both (more uniformity); a square more beautiful than a rhombus. Euclid's theorems and Newton's gravitation are prime beauties ("Such is Gravitation, in Sir Isaac Newton's Scheme"). [sources: IEP-Taste §3c; SEP-Beauty §2.1]
- Hutcheson's arguments for an internal "sense" of beauty: the pleasure is immediate and necessary — it "does not arise from any Knowledge of Principles, Proportions, Causes, or the Usefulness of the Object" and no "Prospect of Advantage" can vary it; people with perfect external senses may be insensible to beauty; and theorems (not objects of the five senses) are beautiful. He clarified (1727 *Essay on the Passions*) that a sense is "internal" because its ideas depend on prior ideas, and later replaced "internal" with "reflex" or "subsequent". [source: SEP-18C §1.2; IEP-Taste §3c]
- Hutcheson treated beauty like a Lockean secondary quality: "were there no Mind with a Sense of Beauty to contemplate Objects, I see not how they could be call'd beautiful"; God might have given us a sense responsive to irregularity, but gave us one that rewards the pursuit of knowledge by theorems. [source: SEP-18C §1.2]
- Hume in the *Treatise* (1739–40): "Beauty is such an order and construction of parts as, either by the primary constitution of our nature, by custom, or by caprice, is fitted to give a pleasure and satisfaction to the soul... Pleasure and pain, therefore, are not only necessary attendants of beauty and deformity, but constitute their very essence" (T 299). Against Hutcheson, Hume held usefulness contributes to beauty; judgements of taste need only be non-self-interested. [sources: SEP-Beauty §2.4; SEP-18C §1.3]
- Edmund Burke (1729–1797), *A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful*, 1757; 2nd edition 1759 added the "Introduction on Taste". [sources: SEP-Burke §2; SEP-18C §2.2; WP-A_Philosophical_Enquiry] (WP-Sublime_(philosophy) gives 1756 — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Burke's definition: "by beauty I mean, that quality or those qualities in bodies, by which they cause love, or some passion similar to it" (Burke 1757, 83 in OUP 1990 ed.). [source: SEP-Beauty §2.3]
- Burke's refutation of proportion theory: flowers come in every shape; the rose is large on a small shrub, the apple blossom small on a large tree, yet both are beautiful; the swan has a long neck and short tail, the peacock the reverse; "You may assign any proportions you please to every part of the ... human body; and I undertake, that a painter shall observe them all, and notwithstanding produce, if he pleases, a very ugly figure." Also: "I have great reason to doubt, whether beauty be at all an idea belonging to proportion." [sources: SEP-Beauty §2.1; WP-A_Philosophical_Enquiry]
- Burke's psychology: passions of society turn on pleasure, passions of self-preservation on pain. The beautiful excites love (a social passion); the sublime excites "astonishment" — the passion that arises when pain or danger threatens from a relatively safe distance, combining awe and "tranquility shadowed with horror". The beautiful tends to the small, smooth, various, delicate, clear and bright; the sublime to the opposite (vast, rugged, obscure, etc.). Burke explained these physiologically (e.g. large objects strike many retinal rays). [sources: SEP-18C §2.2; WP-A_Philosophical_Enquiry]
- Burke calls the pleasure of the sublime "delight", a "negative" pleasure arising from the removal or distance of pain, distinct from positive pleasure; he was the first to argue that sublimity and beauty are mutually exclusive. [source: WP-Sublime_(philosophy)]
- Burke contradicted Shaftesbury and Hutcheson's sixth-sense theory: the mind forms ideas of beauty from ideas of pleasure. [source: SEP-Burke §2]
- Other 18th-century names: Joseph Addison's "Pleasures of the Imagination" (*Spectator*, 1712) — greatness, novelty, beauty; Alexander Gerard (1728–1795) and Archibald Alison (1757–1839), associationists; Thomas Reid. [sources: SEP-18C contents and §2.1; IEP-Taste §3f]
- Taste theories "rose up in the 18th century and diminished almost as quickly"; the 19th century moved to "aesthetic attitude" (Schopenhauer's will-less contemplation; Edward Bullough's "psychical distance", 1912). [source: IEP-Taste §4; SEP-Beauty §2.5]

## 4. Hume, "Of the Standard of Taste" (1757)
- Publication: in *Four Dissertations* (London: A. Millar, 1757), with "The Natural History of Religion", "Of the Passions" and "Of Tragedy". [sources: WP-Four_Dissertations; early-modern--berkeley-and-hume.md citing SEP-Hume §1]
- Origin: Hume wrote it "in some haste" to fill out a volume after removing "Of the Immortality of the Soul" and "Of Suicide" on the advice of Philip Stanhope; the publisher found the volume too slim. Hume made nearly 200 corrections over later editions, mostly punctuation, but "never altered his argument". [source: SEP-HumeAes §4]
- Kant "read the essay in translation"; Gracyk: it stands to Kant's aesthetic theory "in much the way that Hume's account of cause and effect stands to the Critique of Pure Reason". [source: SEP-HumeAes §4]
- The subjectivist "species of philosophy" Hume reports: "Beauty is no quality in things themselves: It exists merely in the mind which contemplates them; and each mind perceives a different beauty. One person may even perceive deformity, where another is sensible of beauty; and every individual ought to acquiesce in his own sentiment, without pretending to regulate those of others." [source: SEP-Beauty §1 (Hume 1757, 136)]
- Hume's distinction: sentiments refer to nothing beyond themselves and are "always right"; determinations of the understanding refer beyond themselves and can be true or false. Judgements of beauty are sentiments. [source: IEP-Taste §3g; SEP-HumeAes §4 ("Verdicts of sentiment lack a truth-value")]
- The paradox: yet Hume calls many judgements of taste "absurd and ridiculous" (SOT 269). His example: "Whoever would assert an equality of genius and elegance between Ogilby and Milton, or Bunyan and Addison, would be thought to defend no less an extravagance, than if he had maintained a mole-hill to be as high as Teneriffe, or a pond as extensive as the ocean." [sources: WP-John_Ogilby (quoting the 1757 first edition); SEP-HumeAes §4 (SOT 269); IEP-Taste §3g]
  - Glosses for readers: John Ogilby (1600–1676), Scottish translator of Virgil and Homer whose reputation was demolished by Dryden's *MacFlecknoe* and Pope's *Dunciad*; John Bunyan (author of *The Pilgrim's Progress*) vs Joseph Addison (polished essayist); Teneriffe = Tenerife (Mount Teide). [source: WP-John_Ogilby; dates of Ogilby from same]
- Hume's aim: a "standard of taste" giving rules for "confirming one sentiment, and condemning another" (SOT 268). [source: SEP-HumeAes §4]
- The colour analogy: just as we count an object's true colour as its "appearance in day-light to the eye of a man in health", there are conditions under which an object's beauty appears as it is. [source: SEP-18C §1.3 (Hume 1757 [1987, 233–234])]
- Sancho's kinsmen (from *Don Quixote*): two kinsmen of Sancho Panza are asked to judge a hogshead of wine supposed to be excellent; one tastes a slight taste of leather, the other of iron; both are ridiculed, but when the hogshead is emptied "an old key with a leathern thong tied to it" is found at the bottom. Hume's moral: delicacy of taste is real; general rules of beauty play the role of the key — "To produce these general rules or avowed patterns of composition is like finding the key with the leathern thong", which vindicates the critic and confounds the pretender; and the kinsmen's taste was equally delicate before the key was found, only harder to prove. [sources: SEP-HumeAes §4 (SOT 272–73, "Hume's 'evidence' that there are general rules of taste"); WS-1 and WS-10 (secondary summaries of the passage). Exact full wording NOT verified — paraphrase except the two short phrases.]
- Gracyk's reading of the story: rules of taste might be causal generalisations ("An aftertaste of iron or leather reduces the pleasure taken in an otherwise good wine"); they help separate true from pretend critics; and practice heightens awareness of both disruptive impressions (iron) and missing ones. "Surprisingly, Hume never offers a clear case of one" (a rule). [source: SEP-HumeAes §4]
- The five qualities (exact wording, verified identically in three sources): "Strong sense, united to delicate sentiment, improved by practice, perfected by comparison, and cleared of all prejudice, can alone entitle critics to this valuable character; and the joint verdict of such, wherever they are to be found, is the true standard of taste and beauty." [sources: SEP-Beauty §1 (Hume 1757, 144); SEP-HumeAes §4 (SOT 278); IEP-Taste §3g]
- In order of the essay (SEP-18C's list): (1) delicacy of taste, (2) practice, (3) comparison, (4) freedom from prejudice, (5) good sense (sound understanding). Shelley's analysis: (1), (2) and (5) ensure the object is grasped with all and only its aesthetically relevant properties; (3) and (4) ensure a properly calibrated sentiment. "We take an object's beauty to appear as it is ... when and only when the right sentiment arises from the object rightly grasped." [source: SEP-18C §1.3]
- True judges are "rare" (SOT 280); "few are qualified to give judgment on any work of art" (SOT 278); so the standard is the consensus of qualified judges over time and across cultures (SOT 271, 280) — the "test of time", emphasised by Savile (1982) and Levinson (2002). SEP-Beauty: this accounts for "the enduring veneration of the works of Homer or Milton". [sources: SEP-HumeAes §4; SEP-Beauty §1]
- "Blameless" variation: even ideal critics differ through "innocent and unavoidable" preferences (SOT 281). Hume names two sources: "basic dispositions of character" (e.g. age, temperament) and "moral differences arising from cultural differences" (SOT 280). Where differences arise from such sources, there is no prejudice and no fault. Gracyk adds a third Humean source: different objects reflect different species of beauty (comparing Milton with Addison across genres makes no sense). [source: SEP-HumeAes §4]
- Cultural prejudice: "You will never convince a man, who is not accustomed to Italian music, and has not an ear to follow its intricacies, that a Scotch tune is not preferable" (from Hume's "Of Simplicity and Refinement", S 217); the critic of another age "must place himself in the same situation as the audience" (SOT 276). [source: SEP-HumeAes §4]
- Moral judgement enters aesthetic evaluation for Hume (SOT 283–84; the moral failings of plays and of the Koran "considered as a literary 'performance'", SOT 267). [source: SEP-HumeAes §4]
- Vulgar vs refined taste: both rule-governed, but refined taste is better informed, more stable, takes "a certain point of view" (SOT 276); a prejudiced taste "loses all credit and authority" (SOT 277). [source: SEP-HumeAes §4]

### ARGUMENT 1 (reconstruction for the chapter): Hume's standard of taste
Reconstruction assembled from SEP-HumeAes §4, SEP-18C §1.3, SEP-Beauty §1 and IEP-Taste §3g; the numbering is ours.
1. Beauty is not a quality in objects; a judgement of beauty expresses a sentiment of pleasure that certain qualities of objects are fitted to cause in us.
2. Yet some judgements of taste are plainly wrong ("absurd and ridiculous"): Ogilby is not equal to Milton.
3. There are general principles (rules) of taste: certain qualities naturally please the human mind when perceived properly, as experience across ages shows (the test of time).
4. These principles operate reliably only in good conditions, as colours appear truly only in daylight to a healthy eye; small defects of the organ or the circumstances disturb them (most people notice only "the grosser and more palpable qualities", SOT 278).
5. The conditions are met by a judge with strong sense, delicacy, practice, comparison and freedom from prejudice.
6. Therefore the joint verdict of such judges, wherever found, is the standard of taste; departures from it (beyond blameless variation) are defects of the judge.
- Objection A (circularity): true critics are identified by their endorsement of the best art, while the best art is defined as what true critics endorse (Brown 1938, Noxon 1961). [source: SEP-HumeAes §4]
- Reply: the five qualities can be identified independently of any particular verdict (e.g. delicacy is shown by detecting the iron and leather, confirmed by the key; prejudice and lack of comparison are detectable). Later commentators following Peter Kivy (1967) think the real difficulty is a regress rather than a circle: deciding whether a judge's responses are "sufficiently delicate, grounded in the right comparisons", etc. raises new evaluative questions. [source: SEP-HumeAes §4]
- Objection B (Levinson's "real problem", 2002): even granting the standard, why should an ordinary appreciator, who is not an ideal critic, have reason to prefer or value what ideal critics prefer? Secondary summaries add Levinson's worry that the more "ideal" we become, the more our preferences converge, so that valuing a distinctive aesthetic personality gives a reason not to pursue the ideal; Levinson answered that ideal critics can still differ (e.g. in their aesthetic histories). Debate continued with J. Wieand, "Hume's Real Problem" (2003) and Levinson, "The Real Problem Sustained: Reply to Wieand", JAAC 61 (2003). [sources: SEP-HumeAes bibliography (Levinson 2002, JAAC 60: 227–238; 2003 reply, JAAC 61: 398–99); WS-4 (content summary, secondary — hedge)]
- Objection C (blameless variation): if true judges blamelessly disagree, there may be no single "joint verdict" for many cases; interpreters dispute whether Hume's true critics are idealised judges who never disagree (Shelley 1994, 2003; Costelloe 2007) or real people with intransigent biases (disputed by Guyer 2005, Ross 2008, Galgut 2012, Durà-Vilà 2015). Costelloe (2007) argues Hume's real standard is the "general rules of art" rather than the joint verdict. Note "ideal critic" is a commentators' phrase; it "does not appear in Hume". [source: SEP-HumeAes §4]
- Interpretive debate: is Hume still a subjectivist? Gracyk: "nothing is said to deny his earlier support for subjectivism and there are no direct endorsements of realism"; others read a dispositional analysis (beauty = a disposition of objects to produce the sentiment) — Savile (1982), Mothersill (1984), Levinson (2002); disputed by Baxter (1990). [source: SEP-HumeAes §3.2, §4]

## 5. Kant, Critique of the Power of Judgment (1790)
- Title and translation: *Kritik der Urteilskraft* (1790), also translated *Critique of Judgment*. Recommended translations: Werner Pluhar (Hackett, 1987) and *Critique of the Power of Judgment*, ed. Paul Guyer, trans. Paul Guyer and Eric Matthews (Cambridge Edition of the Works of Immanuel Kant, Cambridge University Press, 2000). Older: J.H. Bernard (1892, rev. 1914), J.C. Meredith (1952). Standard citation: § number plus Academy edition vol. 5 page (Ak. 5:xxx), printed in the margins of both modern translations. [sources: SEP-KantAes Bibliography; WP-Critique_of_Judgment]
- Kinds of aesthetic judgement: of the agreeable, of beauty (= judgements of taste), of the sublime. [source: SEP-KantAes §2]
- First moment (§§1–5): disinterested pleasure — "does not depend on the subject's having a desire for the object, nor does it generate such a desire"; distinguishes the beautiful from the agreeable and the good. [source: SEP-KantAes §2.1.1] Citations: Kant 1790, Ak. 5:204–210 [Guyer & Matthews 2000: 90–96]. [source: SEP-AesJudg §3.1]
- Kant's examples in §2 (Bernard trans., quoted SEP-Beauty): "we do not want to know whether anything depends or can depend on the existence of the thing... Everyone must admit that a judgement about beauty, in which the least interest mingles, is very partial and is not a pure judgement of taste." [source: SEP-Beauty §1]
- Kant's summary of the first moment (Bernard trans.): "Taste is the faculty of judging an object or mode of representing it by an entirely disinterested satisfaction or dissatisfaction. The object of such satisfaction is called beautiful." [source: SEP-Beauty §2.5]
- "Interest" in Kant's sense is pleasure bound up with desire; Zangwill distinguishes Kant's less ambitious claim (pleasure in beauty is disinterested) from the more ambitious one (only it is). [source: SEP-AesJudg §3.1]
- Second moment (§§6–9): universal validity "not based on concepts"; judgements of beauty cannot be proved (§§32–33); "beauty is not a concept of the object" (§38, Ak. 5:290). [source: SEP-KantAes §2.1.2] The moment ends with the claim that the beautiful is what pleases (is liked) universally without a concept. [source: WS-8, secondary; exact Guyer wording NOT verified]
- Kant on the person who calls something beautiful: "he judges not merely for himself, but for everyone, and speaks of beauty as if it were a property of things... he does not count on the agreement of others... but rather demands it from them" (Ak. 5:212–213; Guyer & Matthews 2000: 98). Contrast with the agreeable: Canary wine is pleasant only "to me"; "Each to their own taste" applies to the agreeable. [source: SEP-AesJudg §1.2]
- Third moment (§§10–17): "purposiveness" (*Zweckmäßigkeit*) without the representation of a purpose (*Zweck*); "purposiveness without a purpose" is a phrase "widely used by commentators"; Kant's own terms "merely formal purposiveness" / "the form of purposiveness". §14 is the main evidence for Kant's formalism. [source: SEP-KantAes §2.1.3]
- Fourth moment (§§18–22): necessity, "exemplary" (§18, Ak. 5:237), based on a "common sense" (*sensus communis*), "a subjective principle which allows us to judge by feeling rather than concepts" (§20). [source: SEP-KantAes §2.1.4] IEP stresses that Kant's "common sense" is not everyday good sense but the a priori principle of taste. [source: IEP-KantAes]
- Free play: pleasure in the beautiful depends on the "free play" or "free harmony" of imagination and understanding (introduced §9): imagination harmonises with understanding without being constrained by any particular concept. [source: SEP-KantAes §2.2]
- Kant's question (§36, Ak. 5:288): "how is a judgment possible which, merely from one's own feeling of pleasure in an object, independent of its concept, judges this pleasure as attached to the representation of the same object in every other subject, and does so a priori"? The "Deduction of Taste" is §§31–39, core at §38; prefigured at §9 and §22. [source: SEP-KantAes §2.2]
- Kant's position between traditions: against the "empiricist" (Hume, Hutcheson, Burke: taste as feeling without cognitive content) and the "rationalist" (Baumgarten, Meier: taste as cognition of an objective perfection). [source: SEP-KantAes §2.2]
- Kant explicitly cites Hume (§34, Bernard trans.): "Although, as Hume says, all critics can reason more plausibly than cooks, yet the same fate awaits them"; no syllogism can prove beauty — "I must immediately feel the pleasure in the representation of the object". [source: SEP-Beauty §1]
- Free vs adherent (dependent) beauty (§16): free beauty presupposes no concept of what the object should be; adherent (*anhängend*) beauty presupposes such a concept and is judged as beautiful "qua belonging to this or that kind". Free beauties include "designs a la grecque, foliage for borders or on wallpaper... fantasias in music" and all music without a text (§16, Ak. 5:229); Kant seems to make all representational art adherent, hence impure. Objection: Kant would rank wallpaper above the Sistine Chapel; replies by Schaper (1979), Guyer (2005), Allison (judgements of adherent beauty contain a pure core), Tuna (2018: works of genius can be free beauties). [source: SEP-KantAes §2.5] SEP-Beauty also lists flowers among free beauties and illustrates dependent beauty with "A beautiful ox would be an ugly horse" (Sartwell's illustration; do not present as Kant's own words). [source: SEP-Beauty §1]
- Impure judgements can also involve "charm" (*Reiz*) or emotion (*Rührung*) (§13). [source: SEP-KantAes §2.5]
- Beauty as "symbol of morality" (§59, Ak. 5:353–354). [source: SEP-KantAes §2.8]

### ARGUMENT 2: Kant's deduction and the antinomy of taste
- The deduction, step by step (SEP-KantAes §2.2): (1) Pleasure in beauty is the felt free harmony of imagination and understanding. (2) This harmony is the subjective condition of cognition in general (§9, Ak. 5:218; §21, 238; §38, 290). (3) We are entitled to demand that everyone share our cognitions (that the object is green, square). (4) So we must be entitled to demand agreement in the subjective condition of such cognition. (5) Hence we may demand that everyone share our disinterested pleasure: the judgement of taste rightly claims universal validity.
- Main objection, the dilemma (Guyer 1979; Meerbote 1982; Allison 2001; Rind 2002): either free play is involved in all perception, in which case everything should be beautiful (the "everything is beautiful" problem), or it is not, in which case step (4) does not license step (5). [source: SEP-KantAes §2.2]
- Replies: Ameriks (1982/2003) treats judgements of taste as objective; Allison (2001) says the deduction shows only that such claims can in general be legitimate, not that any particular one is; Savile (1987) and Chignell (2007) require entitlement case by case and draw on aesthetic ideas; Elliott, Crawford, Kemal and Savile ground it in morality; Rind (2002) and Kalar (2006) prefer the §21 "common sense" version; Gracyk (1986), Ginsborg (2017) and Breitenbach (2021) accept that anything can legitimately be judged beautiful. Meerbote and Guyer (1979) judged the dilemma fatal. [source: SEP-KantAes §2.2]
- The antinomy of taste (§§55–57, "Dialectic of the Aesthetic Power of Judgment"). Kant starts from two commonplaces: "everyone has his own taste" and "there is no disputing about taste" (*de gustibus non est disputandum*), and identifies a missing middle one: one can quarrel (contend) about taste though not dispute (decide by proofs). [source: WS-2, WS-9 (secondary summaries)]
  - Thesis: the judgement of taste is not based on concepts, for otherwise one could dispute about it (decide by proofs).
  - Antithesis: the judgement of taste is based on concepts, for otherwise one could not even quarrel about it (lay claim to others' necessary agreement).
  - Resolution (§57): "concept" is used in two senses. The thesis is true of determinate concepts (concepts of the understanding, usable in proofs); the antithesis is true of an indeterminate concept — Kant says the judgement rests on an "indeterminate concept" (§57, Ak. 5:341), the concept of the supersensible substratum (of nature and of humanity). Both are true, so the antinomy is only apparent. [sources: SEP-KantAes §2.1.2 (§57, 341, "indeterminate concept"; Ginsborg notes Kant here diverges from his standard use of "concept"); IEP-KantAes (role of the supersensible as "determinable" but not "determined"); WS-2/WS-9 for the two-senses structure]
  - Objection: the "indeterminate concept of the supersensible" is obscure and adds nothing a reader can use; it also seems to retract §38's "beauty is not a concept of the object" (SEP-KantAes notes the tension). Reply (sympathetic): Kant's point is only that the claim to agreement rests on something shared by all judges (the same cognitive faculties), which cannot be specified as a rule; Zangwill suggests separating "Kant's problem from his solution": the problem of how a feeling-based judgement can claim correctness ("the Big Question in aesthetics") remains even if the solution fails. [sources: SEP-KantAes §2.1.2; SEP-AesJudg §1.3, §1.5]
- Kant's broader criticisms list (to be used briefly; partly in Kant chapter): disinterestedness (we care about preserving beautiful things — Crawford 1974); formalism (influential via Hanslick); Dickie's "myth of the aesthetic attitude" (1964), which Zangwill (1992) thinks misplaced against Kant; Wollheim's "empty cognitive stock" (1980). [source: SEP-KantAes §2.4]

## 6. The sublime
- Longinus, *On the Sublime* (*Peri hypsous*): Roman-era Greek treatise of literary criticism, dated to the 1st century CE; author unknown ("Longinus" or "Pseudo-Longinus"); the only manuscript (Paris. gr. 2036, 10th c.) heads it "by Dionysius Longinus"; the old attribution to the 3rd-century Cassius Longinus was dropped in the 19th century. Dedicated to Postumius Terentianus. [source: WP-On_the_Sublime]
- Longinus's five sources of sublimity: "great thoughts, strong emotions, certain figures of thought and speech, noble diction, and dignified word arrangement" (first two inborn, others acquired); the sublime leads hearers "not to persuasion, but to ecstasy" (1.4); sublimity is "the echo of a great soul" (*megalophrosunēs apēchēma*); he quotes Genesis ("Let there be light") at 9.9 and Sappho's "Ode to Jealousy". [source: WP-On_the_Sublime]
- Reception: first printed by Francis Robortello, Basel, 1554; Nicolas Boileau's French translation (1674) launched its modern career; William Smith's English translation 1739. [source: WP-On_the_Sublime]
- British 18th-century sublime of nature: John Dennis crossing the Alps (published 1693): delight "mingled with Horrours, and sometimes almost with despair"; Addison: "The Alps fill the mind with an agreeable kind of horror". [source: WP-Sublime_(philosophy)]
- Burke: see §3 (astonishment, delight, terror; sublime and beautiful opposed).
- Kant's sublime: mathematical (magnitude overwhelms imagination; reason's demand for totality; "indicates a faculty of the mind which surpasses every standard of sense", §26, Ak. 5:254) and dynamical (nature as "a power that has no dominion over us", §28, Ak. 5:260; examples: overhanging cliffs, thunder clouds, volcanoes, hurricanes, §28, 261). Pleasure mixed with displeasure — a "rapidly alternating repulsion from and attraction to one and the same object" (§27, 258); "a pleasure which is possible only by means of a displeasure" (§27, 260); "negative liking". Sublimity "is not contained in anything in nature, but only in our mind" (§28, 264); so no deduction is needed. Kant calls the theory of the sublime "a mere appendix" (§23, 246). [source: SEP-KantAes §2.7] (Basic Kant sublime material overlaps the Kant chapter; keep short.)
- Debates: whether art can be sublime for Kant (Abaci 2008 no; Clewis 2010 yes); Lyotard (1988) applied the Kantian sublime to Barnett Newman's paintings. Rudolf Otto compared the sublime to the "numinous". [sources: SEP-KantAes §2.7; WP-Sublime_(philosophy)]
- Hume recognised the sublime as an artistic category (SOT 281) but offered no theory of it. [source: SEP-HumeAes §1]

## 7. Objections to disinterestedness: Nietzsche and Bourdieu
- Nietzsche, *On the Genealogy of Morality* (1887), Third Treatise ("What do ascetic ideals mean?"), §6: criticises aestheticians — Kant and Schopenhauer especially — who approach beauty "purely from the standpoint of the 'spectator'" (KSA 5:346) rather than from the experience of the artist (creator); he rejects disinterested aesthetic appreciation (by 1886 at the latest, cf. *Beyond Good and Evil* 33). [source: SEP-NietzscheAes §4; WP-On_the_Genealogy_of_Morality (Third Treatise title)]
- In the same section Nietzsche quotes approvingly Stendhal's definition "the beautiful is a promise of happiness" and calls Stendhal a "genuine spectator", in contrast to Kant and Schopenhauer. [source: SEP-NietzscheAes §4] (On Stendhal's source see Debates/NOT VERIFIED.)
- Interpretations: Zangwill (2013) reads the target as the universality of taste — "some beauty, higher beauty, can be grasped only by a select few"; Reginster (2014), Soll (1998) and Ridley (2011) read Nietzsche as rejecting the passive "spectator" model, with beauty as what impels new creation; Reginster draws on Nehamas (2007). Nietzsche also approved Plato's definition of love as "reproduction and birth in beauty" (Symposium 206e; *Twilight* "Skirmishes" 22–23). [source: SEP-NietzscheAes §4]
- Zangwill's framing of the dispute (SEP-AesJudg §3.2): Nietzsche "protests that the idea of pleasure in beauty [being] cut-off from the particularities and idiosyncrasies of our passionate lives is unrealistic and undesirable"; Kant might reply that pleasures springing from what varies between people would make the claim to correctness "fraudulent" ("ought" implies "can"); the issue becomes "Do we want aristocratic or democratic aesthetics?" A distinct objection: Kant admits empirical and intellectual interests in beauty (§§41–42) which do not refute disinterestedness, a point "Critics of Kant sometimes miss". [source: SEP-AesJudg §3.2]
- Pierre Bourdieu (1930–2002), *La Distinction: Critique sociale du jugement* (1979; English *Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste*, 1984) — a pointed reply to Kant's Critique of Judgment in its subtitle. Based on surveys from 1963–1968 (the "Kodak survey" 1963 and "Taste survey" 1967), analysed by correspondence analysis. In 1998 the International Sociological Association voted it the sixth most important sociology book of the 20th century. [source: WP-Distinction_(book); WP-Pierre_Bourdieu]
- Bourdieu's thesis: judgements of taste are acts of social positioning; tastes are formed by *habitus* within class; "working-class people expect objects to fulfil a function", while dominant classes value the "pure gaze" that appreciates objects for their own sake; dominant taste is imposed as legitimate ("symbolic violence"); cultural capital is acquired by "total, early, imperceptible learning ... within the family". IEP: for Bourdieu "there is no value that guides one's aesthetic taste; it is developed within a person's class". [sources: WP-Distinction_(book); WP-Pierre_Bourdieu; IEP-Taste §5a]
- Wider political critique (SEP-Beauty §3): French revolutionaries associated beauty with aristocratic Rococo; Adorno: "in the ugly, art must denounce the world that creates and reproduces the ugly in its own image" (*Aesthetic Theory*, 1970); Max Ernst on Dada as "total subversion". [source: SEP-Beauty §3.1]

## 8. Is beauty objective? Contemporary debates
- Decline: after Santayana's *The Sense of Beauty* (1896) — beauty is "pleasure regarded as the quality of a thing", "objectified pleasure" — beauty dropped out of English-language philosophy for most of the 20th century; A.J. Ayer treated "beautiful" as expressing feeling with no "objective validity". Arthur Danto, *The Abuse of Beauty* (2003) explores the decline; revival in the 1990s (critic Dave Hickey: "the issue of the 90s will be beauty", 1993). [source: SEP-Beauty §1, §2.4]
- Frank Sibley, "Aesthetic Concepts", *Philosophical Review* 68(4) (1959): 421–450 (reprinted in Sibley, *Approach to Aesthetics*, 2001). Thesis: aesthetic concepts (balanced, tragic, comic, joyous, delicate, graceful...) are not "condition-governed" — no set of non-aesthetic features suffices to apply them; "one has to look and see"; taste is needed, comparable to seeing that a book is red; yet we give reasons by pointing out non-aesthetic features to get others to see. His 1965 "Aesthetic/Nonaesthetic" pioneered the dependence (supervenience) of aesthetic on non-aesthetic properties. [sources: SEP-AesJudg bibliography and §2.2, §2.4; SEP-AesConcept §2.2]
- Arnold Isenberg, "Critical Communication" (1949): critics' descriptions are "directions for perceiving", not premises in a deduction: "There is not in all the world's criticism a single purely descriptive statement concerning which one is prepared to say beforehand, 'If it is true, I shall like that work so much the better'". Generalists: Beardsley (unity, intensity, complexity), Dickie, Stephen Davies, John Bender. [source: SEP-AesConcept §2.2; SEP-AesJudg §2.4]
- Zangwill's account of what any theory must explain: judgements of taste are (a) subjective (based on pleasure/displeasure) and (b) normative (claim correctness: "some are correct, others incorrect"), plus mind-independence ("Thinking it so does not make it so") and dependence/supervenience on non-aesthetic properties; there are no laws of taste (Mothersill's "First Thesis", *Beauty Restored*, 1984). [source: SEP-AesJudg §§1–2]
- Positions on the normativity: "The realist about beauty will say that the feeling has normativity built into it in virtue of its representational content; the feelings themselves can be more or less veridical"; "A Humean sentimentalist will probably say that normativity is something we somehow construct or foist upon our pleasures"; Kant appeals to cognitive states that are not beliefs. Realism makes beauty a genuine property of the world that need not be universally available; Zangwill concedes realism "might seem metaphysically extravagant" but the alternative is "Not easy!". [source: SEP-AesJudg §1.4, §3.2]
- Nick Zangwill, *The Metaphysics of Beauty* (Cornell UP, 2001); defends "moderate formalism" and, in music, an explicitly realist position (*Music and Aesthetic Reality*, 2015). Eddy Zemach, *Real Beauty* (Penn State), "an extreme realist view". [sources: WP-Nick_Zangwill; SEP-AesJudg bibliography; SEP-Beauty bibliography]
- Zangwill against relativism: professed sceptics about taste "do not practice what they preach"; "Only those who think that there is a right and wrong in judgment can modestly admit that they might be wrong." Experimental philosophers Cova & Pain (2012) found ordinary people do not treat aesthetic judgements as correct/incorrect; Zangwill disputes the inference. [source: SEP-AesJudg §1.2]
- Response-dependence/dispositional middle ground: Hume read as giving a dispositional analysis (beauty = power to please qualified observers) by Savile, Mothersill, Levinson (2002). [source: SEP-HumeAes §3.2] (Use "response-dependence" as the modern label with care; no SEP entry read defines it for beauty.)
- Relational views: Crispin Sartwell, *Six Names of Beauty* (2004) and *Beauty: A Quick Immersion* (2022), locates beauty in the relation between subject and object and their situation; he defines beauty as "the object of longing". [source: SEP-Beauty §1, §2.3]
- Alexander Nehamas (b. 1946), *Only a Promise of Happiness: The Place of Beauty in a World of Art* (Princeton UP, 2007); title taken from Stendhal's definition. Beauty is an invitation to further exploration and interpretation, not a verdict about a surface; irreducibly social: "Aesthetic judgment, I believe, never commands universal agreement... Beauty creates smaller societies, no less important or serious because they are partial... orthodox, however, without thinking of all others as heresies" (2007, 80–81); "I think of beauty as the emblem of what we lack" (77). He also says "Works of art can be beautiful because everything can be beautiful, but that doesn't mean that anything can be a work of art." [sources: SEP-Beauty §1, §2.3; WP-Alexander_Nehamas]
- Roger Scruton (1944–2020), *Beauty* (Oxford UP, 2009): a modified Kantianism: "We call something beautiful when we gain pleasure from contemplating it as an individual object, for its own sake, and in its presented form" (2009, 26); compares experiencing beauty to a kiss. Scruton (1979) on validity: "Inwardly, we affirm our preference as valid". [sources: SEP-Beauty §2.3; SEP-AesJudg §1.4; WP-Roger_Scruton]
- Elaine Scarry, *On Beauty and Being Just* (Princeton UP, 1999), from her 1998 Tanner Lectures on Human Values; argues the relation of justice to aesthetic notions such as balance, symmetry and "fairness" is "more than analogies" — our political and moral ideas are "irreducibly aesthetic"; she defends beauty against its 20th-century disparagement. [sources: SEP-Beauty §2.2, §3.4; WP-Elaine_Scarry]
- Feminist critiques: beauty norms as control of women's bodies (Wollstonecraft 1792, Grimké 1837); Laura Mulvey, "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema" (1975): "It is said that analyzing pleasure, or beauty, destroys it. That is the intention of this article"; Naomi Wolf, *The Beauty Myth* (1991); Irigaray; the 1914 slashing of Velázquez's *Rokeby Venus* by a suffragette. Peg Zeglin Brand (ed.), *Beauty Matters* (2000): beauty as "a double-edged sword". [source: SEP-Beauty §3.2, §3.4]
- Since around 1990 an "explosion of interest in beauty" among feminists; work focuses on norms of bodily appearance (race, disability, age, sexual morphology); Janet Wolff's "aesthetics of 'uncertainty'" (2006); Sherri Irvin urges retraining aesthetic preferences away from standard conventions. [source: SEP-FemAes]
- Race: Marcus Garvey (1925) on white beauty standards and colourism; Malcolm X on "conking" his hair as "my first really big step toward self-degradation" (*Autobiography*, 1964); "Black is beautiful" slogan around 1970; Edward Said, *Orientalism* (1978) on appropriation of "exotic" beauty; Tobin Siebers on disability and "counter-beauties" (2005). [source: SEP-Beauty §3.3–3.4]
- Paul C. Taylor (b. 1967), *Black Is Beautiful: A Philosophy of Black Aesthetics* (Wiley-Blackwell, 2016), winner of the American Society for Aesthetics Outstanding Monograph Prize 2017; SEP-FemAes cites it as a study of "the complexities of the phrase 'Black is beautiful'" and of "the black gaze". Taylor taught at Penn State and Vanderbilt and moved to UCLA in 2023. [sources: WP-Paul_C._Taylor; SEP-FemAes]
- Evolutionary aesthetics (hedge heavily): theories in evolutionary psychology holding that basic human aesthetic preferences evolved to enhance survival and reproduction — e.g. a reported preference for savanna-like landscapes with trees and water (the ancestral environment), and sexual-selection explanations of art (the "peacock's tail"). Denis Dutton, *The Art Instinct: Beauty, Pleasure, and Human Evolution* (2009) is the best-known philosophical defence. Wikipedia itself flags parts of the topic as synthesis; findings such as cross-cultural "preference" for realistic landscape painting were attributed by the study's own authors to the influence of Western calendar art. Treat as contested hypotheses. [sources: WP-Evolutionary_aesthetics; WP-Denis_Dutton]

## 9. Everyday and environmental beauty (brief)
- Yuriko Saito (b. 1953, Sapporo; Rhode Island School of Design 1981–2018): *Everyday Aesthetics* (Oxford UP, 2007); *Aesthetics of the Familiar: Everyday Life and World-Making* (OUP, 2017; ASA Outstanding Monograph Prize 2018); *Aesthetics of Care* (2022). [source: WP-Yuriko_Saito]
- Saito (SEP): from the 19th century Western aesthetics narrowed to the fine arts; everyday aesthetics is better seen as "restoring the scope of aesthetics rather than opening a new arena"; many traditions (Inuit, Navajo; Japanese and Chinese) integrate aesthetic considerations into daily life. [source: SEP-Everyday intro]
- Environmental aesthetics: Ronald Hepburn, "Contemporary Aesthetics and the Neglect of Natural Beauty" (1966) set the agenda. Allen Carlson criticised the "landscape model" (appreciating nature like a landscape painting) and proposed the "natural environmental model" (1979): appreciate an environment as the kind of environment natural science says it is. "Positive aesthetics" (every part of nature, properly seen, is beautiful) — Carlson (1984) argues scientific understanding reveals order, so nature so viewed appears aesthetically good; Saito (1998) argues anything becomes interesting through an explanatory story; critics say it blocks comparative assessments. [source: SEP-EnvAes §1, §3.2]

## Debates
- Is Hume a subjectivist, a dispositionalist or a quasi-realist about beauty? Gracyk: no endorsement of realism; Savile, Mothersill, Levinson: dispositional analysis; Baxter against. [source: SEP-HumeAes §3.2, §4]
- Hume's true judges: ideal critics who never disagree (Shelley, Costelloe) vs real, partly biased people (Guyer, Ross, Galgut, Durà-Vilà); joint verdict vs "general rules of art" as the real standard (Costelloe). Circularity (Brown, Noxon) vs regress (Kivy). [source: SEP-HumeAes §4]
- Levinson's "real problem" (2002) vs Wieand (2003). [source: SEP-HumeAes bibliography; WS-4]
- Kant's deduction: fatal dilemma (Guyer 1979, Meerbote) vs various rescues (Ameriks, Allison, Savile, Chignell, Rind, Kalar; "everything is beautiful" accepted by Gracyk, Ginsborg, Breitenbach). [source: SEP-KantAes §2.2]
- Free/adherent beauty and whether Kant's theory applies to art (Allison, Schaper, Guyer, Tuna). [source: SEP-KantAes §2.5]
- Status of the sublime in Kant (an "appendix" vs central for moral feeling — Clewis, Matherne); sublime art (Abaci vs Clewis). [source: SEP-KantAes §2.7]
- Nietzsche's target in GM III.6: universality (Zangwill 2013) vs the passive spectator model (Reginster 2014, Ridley 2011, Soll 1998). [source: SEP-NietzscheAes §4]
- Realism vs sentimentalism vs Kantian accounts of aesthetic normativity (Zangwill); experimental philosophy (Cova & Pain 2012) vs Zangwill. [source: SEP-AesJudg]
- Particularism (Isenberg, Sibley's "Aesthetic Concepts", Mothersill) vs generalism (Beardsley, Sibley's "General Reasons", Dickie, Davies, Bender). [source: SEP-AesConcept §2.2]
- Positive aesthetics of nature (Carlson, Saito, Parsons, Rolston) vs critics (Thompson, Godlovitch, Budd). [source: SEP-EnvAes §3.2]
- Source of Stendhal's phrase: SEP-NietzscheAes says Nietzsche quotes it from *Rome, Naples et Florence* (1817); quotation sites and an article by Weintraub (per WS-12) place "La beauté n'est que la promesse du bonheur" in a footnote to ch. 17 of *De l'amour* (1822), and note Nietzsche's "a promise" vs Stendhal's "the promise". Safe wording: "Stendhal's phrase 'beauty is the promise of happiness' (*promesse de bonheur*), which Nietzsche quotes in the Genealogy". [sources: SEP-NietzscheAes §4; WS-12]
