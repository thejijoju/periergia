## Gottlob Frege: life
- Friedrich Ludwig Gottlob Frege, born 8 November 1848 in Wismar (then Mecklenburg-Schwerin); died 26 July 1925 in Bad Kleinen (Mecklenburg). German mathematician, logician and philosopher who worked at the University of Jena [source: SEP-F §1; IEP-F §1]
- Father (Karl) Alexander Frege headed a girls' school; mother Auguste (née Bialloblotzky) later ran it; brought up Lutheran [source: SEP-F §1; IEP-F §1]
- Entered Jena 1869; mentor Ernst Abbe; moved to Göttingen 1871 (IEP: attended Hermann Lotze's lectures on philosophy of religion); PhD in mathematics 1873 (dissertation on a geometrical representation of imaginary forms in the plane, under Ernst Schering); Habilitation 1874 and Privatdozent (unsalaried lecturer) at Jena 1874 [source: SEP-F §1; IEP-F §1]
- Promoted to außerordentlicher Professor after the *Begriffsschrift* (1879). Honorary ordinary professor: SEP-F says 1896, IEP-F says 1894 (sources differ). Supported by a stipend from the Carl Zeiss Stiftung (IEP) [source: SEP-F §1; IEP-F §1]
- Married Margaret Lieseburg (1856–1905); children died young; adopted a son, Alfred [source: IEP-F §1]
- His books were poorly received: the *Begriffsschrift*'s two-dimensional notation was hard to read; the *Grundlagen* (1884) was "virtually ignored"; vol. II of the *Grundgesetze* was published at his own expense [source: IEP-F §1; SEP-F §1]
- Retired from Jena: SEP-F says 1917, IEP-F says 1918 (sources differ; say "around 1917–18"). Moved to Bad Kleinen near Wismar [source: SEP-F §1; IEP-F §1]
- Rudolf Carnap was his student 1910–1913 and took notes of two of his courses [source: IEP-F §1; SEP-F §1]
- Late papers: "The Thought" (Der Gedanke, 1918), "Negation" (1918), "Compound Thoughts" (1923) [source: SEP-F §1; IEP-F §1]
- In 1924 he gave up logicism, concluding that the set-theoretic paradoxes made it impossible, and tried to base arithmetic on Kantian (geometrical) intuitions of space [source: IEP-F §1]
- Bequeathing his papers to Alfred he wrote: "I believe there are things here which will one day be prized much more highly than they are now. Take care that nothing gets lost." Alfred gave them to Heinrich Scholz at Münster; they were destroyed in an Allied bombing raid on 25 March 1945, though Scholz had copied some [source: IEP-F §1]
- He did not live to see his influence; his logic superseded older forms "due to the championship of Russell" [source: IEP-F §1]

## Frege's 1924 diary and antisemitism (handle per sources)
- SEP-F: "his last years saw him become more than just politically conservative – his diary for a brief period in 1924 show sympathies for fascism and anti-Semitism." Published as "Diary: Written by Professor Gottlob Frege in the Time from 10 March to 9 April 1924", ed. G. Gabriel and W. Kienzler, trans. R. Mendelsohn, *Inquiry* 39 (1996): 303–342 [source: SEP-F §1 and bibliography]
- IEP-F: right-wing, "distrustful of foreigners and rather anti-semitic"; "seems to have wanted to see all Jews expelled from Germany, or at least deprived of certain political rights"; this "has gravely disappointed some of Frege's intellectual progeny" [source: IEP-F §1]
- German edition 1994 (*Deutsche Zeitschrift für Philosophie* 42); content: opposition to parliamentary democracy, universal suffrage, socialists and liberals; hostility to Catholics, the French and Jews; Frege wrote that Jews should "get lost, or better would like to disappear from Germany" (WP citing Sherratt, *Hitler's Philosophers*, 2013, p. 60); sympathy for General Ludendorff [source: WP Gottlob_Frege]
- Michael Dummett read the surviving transcriptions in 1954 and was deeply shocked; in the Preface to *Frege: Philosophy of Language* (1973, p. xii) he called Frege at the end of his life a "virulent anti-Semite" of "extreme right-wing opinions" [source: WP Gottlob_Frege, citing Dummett 1973]
- Gershom Scholem was among Frege's students [source: WP Gottlob_Frege] — single source.
- CAUTION: WP mentions an entry "dated 5 May 1924" praising an article that praised Hitler, which conflicts with the published diary's span (10 March–9 April 1924). Do not use the 5 May detail or the Hitler connection.

## Frege's logic: the *Begriffsschrift* (1879)
- Full title *Begriffsschrift, eine der arithmetischen nachgebildete Formelsprache des reinen Denkens* (Concept-Script: a formula language of pure thought, modelled on that of arithmetic), Halle, 1879 [source: SEP-F §1, bibliography]. English translation by S. Bauer-Mengelberg in van Heijenoort (ed.), *From Frege to Gödel* (1967) [source: SEP-F bibliography]
- Frege's system "in effect, constituted the first 'predicate calculus'"; he "developed an analysis of quantified statements and formalized the notion of a 'proof' in terms that are still accepted today" [source: SEP-F intro]. IEP-F: "He invented modern quantificational logic, and created the first fully axiomatic system for logic" [source: IEP-F intro]
- He replaced the subject–predicate analysis of Aristotelian logic with function and argument: predicates are "incomplete" or "unsaturated" expressions signifying functions; sentences denote truth-values (the True, the False) [source: SEP-F §2.1; IEP-F §4]
- Worked example: in Aristotelian logic the step from "John loves Mary" to "Something loves Mary" and that to "John loves something" need different rules; Frege's quantifier treats both alike [source: SEP-F §2.2.3]. (Consistent with the Periergia *Aristotle* chapter.)
- He was trying to realise Leibniz's idea of a universal formal language [source: SEP-F §2.1]. (Consistent with Periergia *Leibniz*.)
- The *Begriffsschrift* already contained a logical analysis of mathematical induction (the "ancestral" of a relation) [source: SEP-AN §6; IEP-F §3]
- Peirce, with his student O. H. Mitchell, independently developed quantification (1883 "Note B"; full form in "On the Algebra of Logic", 1885); "Peirce and Frege, independently of each other, took us from the traditional Aristotelian logic to modern logic" [source: SEP-PL §1]
- SEP-F reports Susanne Bobzien's (2021) cumulative but "no smoking gun" argument that Frege borrowed heavily, without attribution, from Stoic logic and semantics as reported in Prantl's history and Diogenes Laertius [source: SEP-F §3.3]. (Consistent with Periergia *Hellenistic Philosophy*, which says the likeness is "especially striking".)

## Frege's *Grundlagen* (1884): numbers, the context principle, Hume's Principle
- *Die Grundlagen der Arithmetik* (Breslau, 1884); English *The Foundations of Arithmetic* by J. L. Austin (Blackwell; 2nd rev. edn 1953) [source: SEP-F bibliography]. Written informally, on Carl Stumpf's advice [source: IEP-F §1]
- A statement of number ("there are eight planets") is a statement about a concept; "How many?" is answerable only once a concept is supplied (1 army, 5 divisions, 20 regiments..., §46) [source: SEP-F §2.5]
- Context Principle: "never ask for the meaning of a word in isolation, but only in the context of a proposition" (*Grundlagen*, Introduction p. x/xxii and §62) [source: SEP-F §1]
- Hume's Principle: the number of Fs equals the number of Gs if and only if there is a one-to-one correspondence between the Fs and the Gs (the name is from the secondary literature) [source: SEP-F §1, §2.6; SEP-FT intro]
- Numbers are objects for Frege; 0 and 1 "can't be given to us in sensation" (1884, 101), against Kant's dictum that without sensibility no object would be given to us [source: SEP-F §2.7]
- Existence statements: "Unicorns do not exist" says the concept *unicorn* has no instances; existence is a second-level concept; Frege notes this diagnoses the ontological argument (*Grundlagen* §53) [source: SEP-AN §6]
- Frege's definition: a truth is analytic if its proof depends only on general logical laws and definitions; arithmetic is analytic, against Kant's view that it is synthetic [source: SEP-AN §6; SEP-F §2.7]. (Consistent with Periergia *Kant* and *Leibniz*.)
- Dummett's "linguistic turn" thesis: Michael Dummett (1991) dated "the precise moment" of the linguistic turn to *Grundlagen* §62, where, asking how numbers are given to us, Frege defines the sense of a proposition containing a number term [source: SEP-AN6 §8]

## Frege's *Grundgesetze* (1893/1903) and Basic Law V
- *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik*, vol. I 1893, vol. II 1903 (Jena: Pohle); complete English translation by P. Ebert and M. Rossberg (with C. Wright), OUP 2013 [source: SEP-F bibliography]
- Logicism: mathematical concepts definable in purely logical terms and mathematical principles derivable from logical laws alone [source: SEP-F §2.4.2]
- Basic Law V: the course-of-values (for concepts, the extension) of f is identical to that of g if and only if f and g have the same value for every argument [source: SEP-F §2.4.1]
- Basic Law V implies a contradiction (Russell's paradox) [source: SEP-F §2.4.1]
- Frege's Theorem: Frege used Law V only to get Hume's Principle; from Hume's Principle he validly derived the Dedekind/Peano axioms (shown by Richard Heck, 1993; Crispin Wright 1983 showed how; name due to George Boolos) [source: SEP-F §2.6; SEP-FT intro]
- "Few philosophers today believe that mathematics can be reduced to logic in the way Frege had in mind"; whether Hume's Principle is analytic is debated (Boolos 1997, Wright 1999) [source: SEP-F §2.4.2, §2.7]
- Frege's proposed repair of Law V in the appendix was later shown to imply that there is only one object, so it fails [source: WP Gottlob_Frege]

## Frege, "On Sense and Reference" (1892) — the argument step by step
- "Über Sinn und Bedeutung", *Zeitschrift für Philosophie und philosophische Kritik* 100 (1892): 25–50; English "On Sense and Reference" translated by Max Black, in P. Geach and M. Black (eds), *Translations from the Philosophical Writings of Gottlob Frege* (Blackwell, 1952; 3rd edn 1980, pp. 56–78) [source: SEP-F bibliography; SEP-NM bibliography]. The distinction first appears briefly in "Function and Concept" (1891) [source: IEP-F §4]
- Translation of *Bedeutung* is disputed: "meaning" (chosen in 1970 as the standard in Blackwell editions), "reference" (Dummett), "significance" (Tugendhat); SEP-F uses "denotation" [source: IEP-FL; SEP-F §3]
- Step 1 (data): "a = a" (e.g. "Mark Twain = Mark Twain", "117+136 = 117+136") can be known by inspection; "a = b" ("Mark Twain = Samuel Clemens", "the morning star is the evening star", "117+136 = 253") can be informative, requiring empirical or arithmetical work [source: SEP-F §3.1.1; IEP-F §4]
- Step 2: if a true identity statement just said that the object is identical with itself, "a = a" and a true "a = b" would say the same thing [source: SEP-F §3.1.1]
- Step 3: they do not (one is trivial and a priori, the other a discovery) [source: IEP-F §4]
- Conclusion: names have, besides a reference (*Bedeutung*), a sense (*Sinn*), "the way by which one conceives of the denotation" / "mode of presentation"; "the morning star" and "the evening star" have one reference (Venus) and two senses [source: SEP-F §3.2; IEP-F §4]
- Second puzzle (attitude reports): from "John believes that Mark Twain wrote Huckleberry Finn" and "Mark Twain = Samuel Clemens" it does not follow that John believes Samuel Clemens did; Frege: after "believes that" words refer to their ordinary sense (indirect reference) [source: SEP-F §3.1.2, §3.2]
- A sentence's reference is its truth-value; its sense is a thought (*Gedanke*); "Pegasus" has a sense but no reference [source: SEP-F §3.2; IEP-F §4]
- "Hesperus"/"Phosphorus" = Evening Star/Morning Star (Venus) [source: SEP-NM §2.2]
- Objection (Kripke, *Naming and Necessity*, 1972/1980): against "classical descriptivism" (associated with Frege 1892 and Russell 1911) — (a) modal: "Socrates might not have taught Plato" is true, but "the teacher of Plato might not have taught Plato" is not, so names are rigid and descriptions are not; (b) epistemic: if "Socrates" meant "the teacher of Plato" it would be a priori that Socrates taught Plato, which it is not; (c) semantic: speakers refer with "Feynman" knowing only "a physicist", and reference passes along a chain of use [source: SEP-REF §2.1; SEP-NM §§2.3–2.4, 2.6]
- Replies: rigidified ("actually") descriptions; descriptions that only fix reference; Strawson's community/expert clusters (Kripke replies to this); Chalmers & Jackson's "apriorist" account [source: SEP-REF §2.1; SEP-NM §§2.3, 2.6]
- Debate: whether Frege himself was a "descriptivist" is not settled in the sources read; SEP-F notes the question whether Frege believed a name's sense varies from person to person (May 2006). Present Kripke's target as "descriptivist theories associated with Frege and Russell".

## Frege, "The Thought" (1918)
- "Der Gedanke" (1918–19), translated as "Thoughts" (in McGuinness ed., *Collected Papers*, 1984) and "Thought" (Beaney ed., *The Frege Reader*, 1997, pp. 325–45) [source: IEP-FL bibliography]
- Argues that thoughts belong neither to the inner world of ideas nor to the outer world of perceivable things but to "a third realm" of objective, non-physical entities [source: IEP-FL]
- These late papers were later drafts of work begun in the 1890s [source: IEP-F §1]

## Russell's paradox and the letter of 1902
- Russell discovered the paradox in (late spring) 1901 while working on *The Principles of Mathematics* and studying Cantor's proof that there is no greatest cardinal; his own accounts give "June 1901" (1944), "spring of 1901" (1959) and May 1901 (1969 Autobiography) [source: SEP-RP §2.1; WP Russell's_paradox]
- The paradox: the set of all sets that are not members of themselves is a member of itself if and only if it is not [source: SEP-R §2; SEP-RP §1]. Zermelo found a similar contradiction perhaps between 1897 and 1902 (some say as early as 1900; Kanamori: perhaps as late as 1902) [source: SEP-RP §2.1]. SEP-R calls it "also known as the Russell-Zermelo paradox (Linsky 2013)".
- The "village barber who shaves all and only villagers who don't shave themselves" is a popular illustration "later associated" with the paradox [source: SEP-RP §1]
- Russell wrote to Frege on 16 June 1902, when vol. II of the *Grundgesetze* was in press: "Let w be the predicate of being a predicate that cannot be predicated of itself. Can w be predicated of itself? From either answer follows its contradictory. ... under certain circumstances a definable set does not form a whole" (van Heijenoort 1967, 125) [source: SEP-RP §2.4; SEP-F §1]
- Frege replied on 22 June 1902 (van Heijenoort 1967, 126–128) [source: WP Russell's_paradox]
- Frege added a hastily composed appendix to vol. II (1903) [source: SEP-RP §2.4]
- Frege, after Russell's news, "never fully recovered"; his attempts to restrict Law V failed; he produced little after about 1906 [source: SEP-F §1; IEP-F §1]
- Russell later (letter quoted in van Heijenoort 1967, 127): "As I think about acts of integrity and grace, I realise that there is nothing in my knowledge to compare with Frege's dedication to truth" [source: SEP-RP §2.4]
- Russell then wrote "Appendix B: The Doctrine of Types" for *Principles* (1903) [source: SEP-RP §2.4]

## Russell: life (brief, as sources support)
- Bertrand Arthur William Russell, born 18 May 1872 at Ravenscroft, Trelleck, Monmouthshire; died 2 February 1970 at Penrhyndeudraeth, Wales, aged 97 [source: SEP-R §1]
- Grandson of Lord John Russell, the former Prime Minister; orphaned young and raised by his grandmother at Pembroke Lodge [source: SEP-R §1]. J. S. Mill was asked to be his secular godfather (died the following year) [source: WP Bertrand_Russell]
- Trinity College, Cambridge from 1890 (met Whitehead); first-class BA in Mathematics 1893; Moral Sciences Tripos Part II 1894 [source: SEP-R §1]
- Break with idealism: "It was towards the end of 1898 that Moore and I rebelled against both Kant and Hegel. Moore led the way, but I followed closely in his footsteps" (*My Philosophical Development*, 1959, 54); Moore cared most about rejecting idealism, Russell about rejecting monism, via a doctrine of external relations needed for asymmetrical relations in mathematics [source: SEP-R §3]
- 1900: met Peano at the First International Congress of Philosophy, Paris [source: SEP-R §1]
- *The Principles of Mathematics* (1903): "The fact that all Mathematics is Symbolic Logic is one of the greatest discoveries of our age" (1903, 5); it included an appendix on Frege, which made Frege better known [source: SEP-R §2; IEP-F §1]
- 1916: fined £100 for an anti-war pamphlet and dismissed from Trinity [source: SEP-R §1]
- 1918: second conviction for suggesting American troops might be used to intimidate strikers in Britain; "five months in Brixton Prison as prisoner No. 2917" [source: SEP-R §1]. WP says a sentence of six months and gives a different account of the offence; use SEP. Wrote *Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy* (1919) in prison [source: SEP-R §2]
- 1940: City College New York appointment revoked after a court found him "morally unfit" [source: SEP-R §1]
- Order of Merit 1949; Nobel Prize for Literature 1950; Russell–Einstein Manifesto 1955; first president of CND 1958; jailed for a week in 1961 for anti-nuclear protest [source: SEP-R §1]. Nobel citation: "in recognition of his varied and significant writings in which he champions humanitarian ideals and freedom of thought" [source: WP Bertrand_Russell]
- Russell and Moore are "generally recognized as one of the founders of modern analytic philosophy" [source: SEP-R intro]

## Type theory and Principia Mathematica
- Type theory developed 1903–1908: sentences arranged in a hierarchy (individuals, sets of individuals, sets of sets...), with a vicious circle principle; "simple" theory 1903, "ramified" theory in "Mathematical Logic as Based on the Theory of Types" (1908) [source: SEP-R §2]
- Axiom of reducibility introduced to recover mathematics; criticised as ad hoc [source: SEP-R §2]. Wittgenstein's *Tractatus* (6.1233) said it is not a tautology; Ramsey (1925) showed how to do without the ramified theory (preceded by Chwistek 1921) [source: SEP-RA §2]
- *Principia Mathematica*, A. N. Whitehead and B. Russell, 3 vols, Cambridge University Press, 1910, 1912, 1913; 2nd edn 1925–27 [source: SEP-PM §2.1; SEP-R §2]
- Division of labour: Russell did the philosophical parts (Introduction, theory of descriptions, no-class theory); "There is hardly a line in all the three volumes which is not a joint product" (Russell 1959, 74) [source: SEP-PM §2.1]
- Cambridge University Press estimated a £600 loss; the Press took half, the Royal Society gave £200; the authors paid £50 each [source: SEP-PM §2.1]
- Initial print run 750 copies of vol. I and 500 of each later volume [source: SEP-PM §2.1]
- A fourth volume, on geometry, was planned but never completed [source: SEP-R §2]
- 1+1=2: ✱54.43 in vol. I (1st edn p. 379) states "From this proposition it will follow, when arithmetical addition has been defined, that 1 + 1 = 2"; the proof is completed in vol. II (1st edn p. 86) at ✱110.643, with the comment "The above proposition is occasionally useful" [source: WP Principia_Mathematica; SEP-PM §5 gives ✱110·643]. NB SEP-PM elsewhere says "∗110·04" — an internal inconsistency; use ✱54.43 (vol. I, p. 379) and ✱110.643 (vol. II, p. 86). The popular claim that it takes "362/379 pages to prove 1+1=2" is misleading: the proof is not finished until vol. II.
- PM's influence "was enormous from 1910 to 1950"; by the 1950s it was no longer used as a textbook [source: SEP-PM §2.1]
- Frege (letter to Jourdain, 1912) complained of PM's decline in formal rigour [source: SEP-PM §2.2]

## Gödel (1931) and the fate of logicism
- Kurt Gödel (28 April 1906 – 14 January 1978); "Über formal unentscheidbare Sätze der Principia Mathematica und verwandter Systeme, I", *Monatshefte für Mathematik und Physik* 38 (1931): 173–198 [source: SEP-G]
- First theorem: an arithmetic statement neither provable nor refutable in Peano arithmetic though true; second: the consistency of arithmetic cannot be proved in arithmetic itself [source: SEP-G §2.2]
- Logicism, in Fregean and Russellian forms, "went into decline" around 1930, "largely because of the discovery of Gödelian incompleteness, and the ascendancy of Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory"; neo-logicism (Wright, Hale) revived core ideas from the 1980s [source: SEP-LG §1]
- IEP-F calls Gödel's result "a serious blow to more ambitious forms of logicism, such as Frege's" [source: IEP-F §3]

## Russell, "On Denoting" (1905) — the theory of descriptions step by step
- Published in *Mind* 14 (October 1905): 479–493 [source: SEP-R bibliography; WP On_Denoting]
- Before 1905 Russell, like Meinong, held that every denoting phrase ("the golden mountain") stands for some entity [source: SEP-R §4]
- Problem sentence: "The present King of France is bald" (France had no king) [source: SEP-R §4; WP On_Denoting]
- Analysis: "The F is G" = (a) there is an F; (b) at most one thing is F; (c) whatever is F is G; formally ∃x(Kx & ∀y(Ky → y = x) & Bx) [source: SEP-D §2; SEP-R §4]
- Definite descriptions are "incomplete symbols" that have no meaning in isolation but contribute to the meaning of sentences containing them [source: SEP-R §4]
- Puzzle 1 (excluded middle): either "The present King of France is bald" or "...is not bald" seems true. Russell: the first is false (no king); "is not bald" is ambiguous by scope — the narrow-scope reading "the King is not-bald" is false, the wide-scope "it is not the case that there is a unique present King of France who is bald" is true. Russell's terms: "primary" and "secondary occurrence" [source: SEP-R §4; SEP-D §3.1; WP On_Denoting]. Quip: "Hegelians, who love a synthesis, will probably conclude that he wears a wig" [source: WP Analytic_philosophy, citing Russell 1905]
- Puzzle 2 (identity/substitution): George IV wanted to know whether Scott was the author of *Waverley*, not whether Scott was Scott [source: SEP-R §4]
- Puzzle 3 (negative existentials): "The golden mountain does not exist" is true without any golden mountain [source: SEP-R §4]
- Against Meinong: Russell objected that Meinong's objects violate non-contradiction (the round square is round and not round) and that "the existent present King of France" both exists and does not; Meinong replied that beingless objects need not obey the law of non-contradiction (in its ontological form) [source: SEP-ME §5.4]. Meinong (1853–1920), Graz; *Gegenstandstheorie* 1904 [source: SEP-ME]
- Russell rejected Frege's sense/reference solution; scope does the work [source: WP On_Denoting]
- Ramsey called it "that paradigm of philosophy" [source: WP On_Denoting, citing Ramsey, *Foundations of Mathematics*, p. 263]. Stebbing's Cambridge School treated the theory of descriptions as "the paradigm of analysis" [source: SEP-AN6 §6]
- Objection (P. F. Strawson, "On Referring", *Mind* 59, 1950): the sentence is neither true nor false when uttered now; it presupposes, rather than asserts, that there is a king; meaning belongs to sentences, truth to uses; also incomplete descriptions ("the table is covered with books") [source: SEP-D §5.1; WP On_Denoting]
- Replies: Russell (1957): an atheist who used Strawson's doctrine to avoid calling "the Ruler of the Universe is wise" false "would be regarded as a somewhat shifty character"; Neale (1990): "My mother is dating the present king of France" seems plainly false; Strawson himself (1964) doubted "brisk little formal argument[s]" could settle the matter [source: SEP-D §5.1]
- Donnellan (1966): referential vs attributive uses ("Smith's murderer is insane"); Kripke (1977) replied that the difference is pragmatic (speaker's meaning, after Grice) [source: SEP-D §5.2]

## Knowledge by acquaintance and logical atomism
- "Knowledge by Acquaintance and Knowledge by Description", *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 11 (1910–11): 108–128 [source: SEP-KAD bibliography]; given partly in response to a talk by Emily Elizabeth Constance Jones to the Cambridge Moral Sciences Club a few months earlier [source: SEP-R §3]
- "I say that I am acquainted with an object when I have a direct cognitive relation to that object, i.e. when I am directly aware of the object itself" (1911, 209) [source: SEP-R §3]
- "Every proposition which we can understand must be composed wholly of constituents with which we are acquainted" (*The Problems of Philosophy*, 1912, ch. 5) [source: SEP-KAD]
- Supreme maxim: "Whenever possible, logical constructions are to be substituted for inferred entities" (1914) [source: SEP-R §3]
- "The Philosophy of Logical Atomism": lectures given in 1918 (published 1918–19); opening caveat: "The things I am going to say in these lectures are mainly my own personal opinions" (PLA 178); world of atomic facts; Russell admitted negative and general facts [source: SEP-LA; SEP-R §3; WP Bertrand_Russell]. He credited many ideas to Wittgenstein (SEP-R: logical atomism "crucial for influencing Wittgenstein's theory of the same name")

## G. E. Moore: life
- George Edward Moore, born 4 November 1873, raised in Upper Norwood, South London; Dulwich College; died in Cambridge 24 October 1958 [source: IEP-M §1]. Hated his first names; his wife called him "Bill" [source: SEP-M intro]
- Trinity College, Cambridge 1892 to read Classics; under Russell and McTaggart added philosophy; first class 1896; Prize Fellowship 1898–1904 [source: SEP-M §1; IEP-M §1]
- Lecturer at Cambridge from 1911; professor 1925 (succeeding James Ward); retired 1939, succeeded by Wittgenstein; in the USA 1940–44 [source: SEP-M §1; IEP-M §1]
- Editor of *Mind* from 1921 (retired 1944 per SEP-M; 1947 per IEP-M — sources differ) [source: SEP-M §1; IEP-M §1]
- Friend of the Bloomsbury Group (Lytton Strachey, Leonard Woolf, Keynes) [source: SEP-M §1]
- Married Dorothy Ely, his former student, in 1916; Order of Merit 1951 [source: IEP-M §1]
- Baldwin: Moore, Russell and Wittgenstein were the "trinity of philosophers at Trinity College Cambridge" [source: SEP-M intro]

## Moore: "The Refutation of Idealism" (1903) and Principia Ethica (1903)
- *Mind* 1903: idealism rests on *esse est percipi*; Moore distinguishes the act of consciousness from its object (blue vs the sensation of blue); "when we try to introspect the sensation of blue, all we can see is the blue"; "Merely to have a sensation is already to be outside that circle" [source: SEP-M §2; IEP-M §2]
- *Principia Ethica* (1903): goodness is indefinable and non-natural; identifying it with a natural property is "the naturalistic fallacy" [source: SEP-M §3; SEP-MM §1]
- Open-question argument: if "good" meant "pleasant", "pleasure is good" would be as empty as "pleasure is pleasure" and "x is pleasant but not good" would be contradictory; but "it is pleasant, but is it good?" remains open [source: SEP-MM §1]. Main objection: concepts vs properties (water/H2O) [source: SEP-MM §1]. Cross-ref *Metaethics* chapter; Periergia *Utilitarianism* already uses Moore on Mill.

## Moore: "A Defence of Common Sense" (1925) and "Proof of an External World" (1939) — argument step by step
- "Defence" (1925): lists truisms (e.g. "the earth had existed also for many years before my body was born"), says he knows them for certain, and separates their truth from their analysis, which may be as radical as one likes [source: SEP-M §6]
- "Proof of an External World": British Academy lecture, 1939 (*Proceedings of the British Academy* 25: 273–300) [source: SEP-M §6; IEP-M bibliography]
- External objects = things whose existence does not depend on our experience [source: SEP-M §6; IEP-M §3]
- The proof: "By holding up my two hands, and saying, as I make a certain gesture with the right hand, 'Here is one hand', and adding, as I make a certain gesture with the left, 'and here is another'" (p. 166 in the reprint) [source: SEP-M §6; IEP-M §3]
  - P1 Here is one hand. P2 Here is another. (So there are at least two hands.) P3 Hands are material, external objects. C So at least two external objects exist; there is an external world [source: IEP-M §3; WP Here_is_one_hand]
- Moore's conditions for a rigorous proof: premises different from the conclusion, premises known, conclusion follows [source: WP Here_is_one_hand]. "How absurd it would be to suggest that I did not know it, but only believed it" [source: SEP-M §6]
- Moore later said he meant to refute "there are no material things", not "Nobody knows for certain that there are any material things" ("A Reply to my Critics", 1942, 668) [source: SEP-M §6; IEP-M §3]
- Objection 1 (begging the question): the premises are known only if scepticism is already false [source: IEP-M §3]. Crispin Wright: justification for "This is a hand" fails to "transmit" to "there is an external world"; James Pryor defends Moore [source: WP Here_is_one_hand]
- Objection 2 (Wittgenstein, *On Certainty*, written in the last months before his death in 1951, published 1969): "Moore's mistake lies in this—countering the assertion that one cannot know that, by saying 'I do know it'" (§521) [source: IEP-M §3; WP Here_is_one_hand]
- Replies: the "G. E. Moore shift" (term coined by William Rowe): turn the sceptic's modus tollens into a modus ponens; it is more reasonable to believe one has hands than the sceptic's premise [source: IEP-M §3; WP Here_is_one_hand]. Baldwin: the proof shows the empirical truth of a truism but leaves its analysis open [source: SEP-M §6]
- In "Certainty" Moore conceded (with reservations) he could not know he was not dreaming [source: SEP-M §6]
- Moore's paradox ("It's raining but I don't believe it") [source: SEP-M §7]

## The term "analytic philosophy" and its contested origins
- Beaney: what characterises analytic philosophy "as it was founded by Frege and Russell" is logical analysis, depending on modern logic; it is "'analytic' much more in the sense that analytic geometry is 'analytic'" than in a decompositional sense [source: SEP-AN §6]
- Dummett's "fundamental axiom of analytical philosophy": "the only route to the analysis of thought goes through the analysis of language" (1993, 128). Beaney: Frege and Russell were "hostile to ordinary language"; the linguistic turn "was only properly taken" in the *Tractatus* and consolidated by Carnap in the early 1930s [source: SEP-AN6 §8]
- Dummett: better called "Anglo-Austrian" than "Anglo-American" (*Origins of Analytical Philosophy*, 1993, p. 2); Austrian precursors Bolzano, Brentano, Meinong and the early Husserl [source: WP Analytic_philosophy]
- Frege as "grandfather" (not father) of analytical philosophy: attributed to Dummett's *Origins* by secondary commentators (search snippets only, see NOT VERIFIED); Beaney (2013) calls Frege the father [source: WP Analytic_philosophy]
- Usual starting point: rejection of British idealism (Bradley, *Appearance and Reality* 1893; T. H. Green) [source: WP Analytic_philosophy; SEP-M §2]
- Beaney also notes analytic-style concerns in medieval scholasticism and early modern Indian (Navya-Nyāya) philosophy, and in Bentham's "paraphrasis" [source: SEP-AN §6]

## Wittgenstein's arrival (brief; cross-ref *Wittgenstein*)
- Born Vienna 26 April 1889; aeronautical engineering at Manchester from 1908; interest in the foundations of mathematics led him to Frege; on Frege's advice went to Cambridge in 1911 to study with Russell; there 1911–1913 [source: SEP-W §1]
- IEP: wrote to Frege and Russell in 1911 with a solution to the paradox; visited Frege in Jena "in late 1911" ("wiped the floor" with him) [source: IEP-F §1]. Order of events differs between SEP-W and IEP-F; avoid exact sequence.
- Russell on meeting him: "obstinate and perverse, but I think not stupid"; later: "I love him and feel he will solve the problems I am too old to solve" (Monk 1990) [source: SEP-W §1]

## Debates
- What is analytic philosophy and when did it begin? Logical analysis via the new logic (Beaney); the analysis of language as the route to thought, a "linguistic turn" at *Grundlagen* §62 (Dummett) versus a turn properly taken only with the *Tractatus* and Carnap (Beaney); "Anglo-Austrian" roots in Bolzano, Brentano and Meinong (Dummett); usual story of a revolt against British idealism [source: SEP-AN §6; SEP-AN6 §8; WP Analytic_philosophy]
- Frege's Stoic sources (Bobzien 2021) — "cumulative" evidence, "no smoking gun" [source: SEP-F §3.3]
- Is Hume's Principle analytic? (Boolos vs Wright; neo-logicism) [source: SEP-F §2.7; SEP-LG]
- Russell vs Strawson on "the present King of France": falsehood vs presupposition failure; Donnellan vs Kripke on referential uses [source: SEP-D §§5.1–5.2]
- Russell vs Meinong on non-existent objects [source: SEP-ME §5.4]
- Kripke vs descriptivism (Frege/Russell); whether Frege was a descriptivist is itself contested [source: SEP-REF §2.1; SEP-NM]
- Moore's proof: anti-sceptical failure, or a proof only of external things, not of knowledge (Baldwin)? Begging the question (Wright's transmission failure) vs Pryor; Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* [source: SEP-M §6; IEP-M §3; WP Here_is_one_hand]
- The open-question argument: refuted by the concept/property distinction or defensible [source: SEP-MM §1]
- Effect of Gödel: decline of logicism vs neo-logicist revival [source: SEP-LG §1]
