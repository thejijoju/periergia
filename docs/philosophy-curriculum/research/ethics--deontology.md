## 1. What "deontology" means; deontology vs consequentialism
- Word from Greek *deon* (duty) + *logos* (study). Deontology is a family of theories about which choices are required, forbidden or permitted, opposed to consequentialism; deontic (assessing choices) vs aretaic (assessing character) theories. [source: SEP-D intro]
- Term history: Bentham coined "deontology" before 1816 (*Chrestomathia*, 1816) in a different sense ("that which is fit, proper"); the current specialised sense is credited to C. D. Broad, *Five Types of Ethical Theory* (1930). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deontology]
- Consequentialists rank states of affairs ("the Good") and make the right whatever produces most good (the Good is prior to the Right); deontologists say some choices cannot be justified by their effects — "the Right is said to have priority over the Good." [source: SEP-D §§1–2]
- Two criticisms of consequentialism that motivate deontology: (1) too demanding — no room for permissions, supererogation, partiality (Williams 1973 on alienation); (2) too permissive — would require killing, lying to or robbing innocents for greater benefit. [source: SEP-D §1]
- Stock cases (SEP-D's labels): **Transplant** (surgeon kills one healthy patient to save five with his organs), **Fat Man** (pushing a large man in front of a runaway trolley to save five), **Trolley** (diverting a runaway trolley onto one to save five). Most regard switching in Trolley as permissible, Transplant as condemned, and Fat Man largely likewise. [source: SEP-D §§1, 2.2]
- Origin of trolley cases: Philippa Foot, "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect", *Oxford Review* 5 (1967; reprinted in *Virtues and Vices*, 1978); "the trolley problem" named by Judith Jarvis Thomson, "Killing, Letting Die, and the Trolley Problem", *The Monist* 59 (1976); Thomson, "The Trolley Problem", *Yale Law Journal* 94 (1985). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trolley_problem] (SEP-DA cites Foot as 1978 = the reprint.)
- Foot's original pair: a judge who could frame and execute an innocent man to stop a riot that would kill five, vs a trolley *driver* choosing between killing one and killing five. Foot explained it by doing vs allowing; Thomson (1985/86) switched to a *bystander*, who chooses between killing one and letting five die, yet switching still seems permissible — undermining Foot's explanation. Thomson (2008) later changed her mind and argued the bystander may *not* turn the trolley. [source: SEP-DA §3]
- Anscombe coined "consequentialism" in "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958), to mean denial of any significant moral difference between intended and merely foreseen results. [source: IEP-Ans]

## 2. Kant's categorical imperative — the formulas (Gregor translation, Cambridge Edition)
- *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (*Grundlegung zur Metaphysik der Sitten*), 1785; cited by Akademie (Ak) volume 4 pagination. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwork_of_the_Metaphysics_of_Morals; SEP-KM]
- Only thing good without limitation: a good will (G 4:393). Good will "like a jewel, it would still shine by itself, as something that has its full worth in itself" (4:393). [source: IEP-K §5a; SEP-KM §§2,3]
- **Formula of Universal Law (FUL):** "act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law" (G 4:421). [source: SEP-KM §5; IEP-K §5a — both Gregor wording]
  - Hackett/Ellington wording of the same: "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorical_imperative]
- **Formula of the Universal Law of Nature (FLN):** "Act as if the maxims of your action were to become through your will a universal law of nature" (4:421; Ellington wording as quoted by WP). Gregor's exact wording NOT verified (see NOT VERIFIED). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorical_imperative]
- **Formula of Humanity (FH):** "So act that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means" (4:429). [source: IEP-K §5a (Gregor); same wording in https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwork_of_the_Metaphysics_of_Morals]
  - Ellington wording: "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means to an end, but always at the same time as an end." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorical_imperative]
- **Formula of Autonomy (FA):** "the Idea of the will of every rational being as a will that legislates universal law" (G 4:432); Kant does not state it as an imperative; SEP's imperative paraphrase: "Act so that through your maxims you could be a legislator of universal laws." [source: SEP-KM §7]
- **Formula of the Kingdom of Ends (FKE):** "act in accordance with the maxims of a member giving universal laws for a merely possible kingdom of ends" (G 4:439); kingdom of ends = "systematic union of different rational beings under common laws" (G 4:433). Favoured recently by Rawls (1971) and Hill (1972). [source: SEP-KM §8]
- Unity: Kant says these "are basically only so many formulations of precisely the same law, each one of them by itself uniting the other two within it", differences "more subjectively than objectively practical", meant "to bring an Idea of reason closer to intuition ... and thus nearer to feeling" (G 4:435). Kant says there are three principles (4:431–2; 4:435–6); WP counts at least eight further variant formulations in the text. [sources: SEP-KM §9; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorical_imperative]
- Interpretations of "equivalence": equivalent in meaning (O'Neill 1975/1990; Engstrom 2009; Sensen 2011) vs doubts (Wood 1999, 2007; Cureton 2013); most straightforward = they generate all and only the same duties (Allison 2011); Kant used the same examples under FUL and FH. [source: SEP-KM §9]
- Kant on the Golden Rule (4:430n, Gregor): "Let it not be thought that the trite *quod tibi non vis fieri etc.* can serve as norm or principle here ... for a criminal would argue on this ground against the judge punishing him". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kantian_ethics (cites Cambridge Edition, trans. Gregor, AA 4:430)]

## 3. The universalisability tests (ARGUMENT)
- Four-step CI procedure (SEP follows O'Neill 1975, 1989 and Rawls 1980, 1989): (1) formulate your maxim; (2) recast it as a universal law of nature binding all rational agents; (3) ask whether the maxim is even *conceivable* in that world; (4) if so, ask whether you could *rationally will* to act on it in that world. [source: SEP-KM §5]
- Fail step 3 (**contradiction in conception**) → **perfect duty**, admitting "no exception in favor of inclination" (G 4:421). Fail step 4 (**contradiction in the will**) → **imperfect duty**. Hill (1971): perfect = "One must never (or always) φ … in C"; imperfect = "One must sometimes and to some extent φ in C". [source: SEP-KM §5]
- Four *Groundwork* examples (4:421–23), one per category: suicide from self-love (perfect, to self); lying promise to get money (perfect, to others); neglecting one's talents (imperfect, to self); refusing to help others (imperfect, to others). [source: SEP-KM §5; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorical_imperative]
- Suicide example (Ellington trans., Hackett 1993 pp. 30–31): maxim "from self-love I make as my principle to shorten my life when its continued duration threatens more evil than it promises satisfaction"; Kant: "a contradiction in a system of nature whose law would destroy life by means of the very same feeling that acts so as to stimulate the furtherance of life". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorical_imperative]
- Beneficence example (Ellington, p. 32): such a law of nature "could subsist", but "it is impossible to will that such a principle should hold everywhere as a law of nature. For a will that resolved in this way would contradict itself, inasmuch as cases might often arise in which one would have need of the love and sympathy of others". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorical_imperative]
- **Argument, step by step: FUL applied to the lying promise** (SEP-KM's reconstruction):
  1. My maxim: to make a deceptive promise (of repayment) in order to get money I need.
  2. Universalise it as a law of nature: everyone, by natural necessity, makes deceptive promises whenever it gets them what they want — and, being a law of nature, this is common knowledge.
  3. In such a world no practice of giving one's word could arise; everyone knows no one's word can be taken.
  4. But my maxim requires, as a necessary means, an existing practice of taking others' word, so that someone takes mine.
  5. So I am trying to conceive of a world with a promising practice and without one — a contradiction. The maxim cannot even be conceived as a universal law (contradiction in conception).
  6. Therefore I have a perfect duty never to act on it.
  Note: there is no self-contradiction in the maxim itself ("I will make lying promises when it achieves something I want"); the contradiction appears only when it is combined with its universalised version. [source: SEP-KM §5] IEP-K gives the same structure for "I shall lie when it will get me out of trouble": the lie requires the presupposition that people tell the truth, which universal lying destroys; "the mark of immorality ... is that one makes an exception for oneself". [source: IEP-K §5a]
- *Objection (Mill)* — see §6: universalising shows only bad consequences, not contradiction.
- *Objection (false positives/negatives)*: MacIntyre (*After Virtue*) — trivial or immoral maxims pass, e.g. "Keep all your promises throughout your entire life except one"; "Let everyone except me be treated as a means" can be universalised. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kantian_ethics]
- *Reply (Kantian)*: the test is applied to genuine maxims (the agent's real principle, described at the right level of generality), not gerrymandered descriptions; and the Humanity formula supplies content the formal test lacks. (See the Kant notes, §8, for Allison and Kleingeld on this; not re-verified here.) [source: research/early-modern--kant.md §8]
- Talents example: SEP-KM calls Kant's argument (rational beings necessarily will all their talents developed) "hardly convincing"; reconstructs it via (a) we necessarily will our own happiness (G 4:415), (b) developed talents (ours and others') are necessary means to it, (c) rational agents will necessary means; so one cannot will a world where no one develops talents. [source: SEP-KM §5]

## 4. The Humanity formula: "merely as a means"
- FH does **not** forbid using people as means — we do that constantly; it forbids treating humanity *merely* as a means. SEP's example: horse vs taxi driver — we may use both for transport, but the driver's humanity must "at the same time be treated as an end in itself", e.g. by paying an agreed price he could consent to. [source: SEP-KM §6]
- It is "humanity" in persons — capacities for self-directed rational action and setting ends — that must be treated as an end. [source: SEP-KM §6]
- Humanity is an end primarily in a *negative* sense — something not to be acted against, a limit (source of perfect duties) — following Korsgaard (1996)'s self-preservation analogy; and in a *positive* sense something to realise or further (source of imperfect duties: develop one's talents, further others' ends). [source: SEP-KM §6]
- Respect for persons = Darwall's (1977) "recognition respect", not "appraisal respect"; "persons cannot lose their humanity by their misdeeds". [source: SEP-KM §6]
- Lying promise under FH: making a false promise to get money treats the other's rational nature as a mere means — a perfect duty violated; failing to give to charity fails to advance the end of humanity — an imperfect duty. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwork_of_the_Metaphysics_of_Morals]
- FH and consent: "any action taken against another person to which he or she *could not possibly* consent" violates perfect duty (WP's gloss on Kant; e.g. theft). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorical_imperative]
- Patient-centred deontologists use FH as "the Means Principle": a right against being used without consent as a means for producing good consequences (Nozick 1974; Quinn 1989; Kamm 1996; Alexander; Steiner; Vallentyne). [source: SEP-D §2.2]
- SEP-D: each branch of deontology can claim Kant — agent-centred (moral quality in maxims, good will), patient-centred (not using others merely as means), contractualist (maxims willable as universal law by all rational agents). [source: SEP-D §2.4]

## 5. The murderer at the door; "On a Supposed Right to Lie" (1797)
- Essay: "Über ein vermeintes Recht aus Menschenliebe zu lügen", *Berlinische Blätter* 1 (1797), 301–314; Ak 8:425–430; Gregor translation "On a supposed right to lie from philanthropy" in *Practical Philosophy* (1996), pp. 611–15. Older Abbott translation (1898) titled "On a Supposed Right to Tell Lies from Benevolent Motives". [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_a_Supposed_Right_to_Tell_Lies_from_Benevolent_Motives ; WS-4 (Ak pages and Gregor pages)]
- Occasion: reply to Benjamin Constant, *Des réactions politiques* (1797), who had cited "a German philosopher" as holding it a crime to lie even to a murderer asking whether one's friend is hiding in one's house. [source: WP essay article; WS-4 (cites AA 8:425)]
- Kant (Abbott translation): "a lie always harms another; if not some human being, then it nevertheless does harm to humanity in general, inasmuch as it vitiates the very source of right … All practical principles of right must contain rigorous truth … such exceptions would destroy the universality on account of which alone they bear the name of principles." Lie defined as "an intentionally untruthful declaration to another man" without the jurists' extra condition of harm (*mendacium est falsiloquium in praeiudicium alterius*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_a_Supposed_Right_to_Tell_Lies_from_Benevolent_Motives] Kant defines the lie at 8:426. [WS-4]
- Kant's consequence argument (as reported by Rachels via WP): because we cannot know the consequences, the liar becomes responsible for whatever harm follows, while one who tells the truth is blameless for harmful results. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kantian_ethics] (Kant's own text of the "friend slipped out of the house" scenario not read — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Reception: Allen Wood: "famous (or infamous)" (Wood, *Kant*, 2008, 187n2). Helga Varden (2010): the example is "a cherished source of scorn ... and a source of deep puzzlement", sharpened after WWII by substituting a Nazi officer; Varden argues Kant has been misrepresented — the essay concerns right (legal philosophy), not the wrong done to the person lied to ("Kant and Lying to the Murderer at the Door . . . One More Time", *Journal of Social Philosophy* 41:4, 403–421). Jens Timmermann wrote the first monograph, *Kant and the Supposed Right to Lie* (Cambridge UP; WP gives 2025). [source: WP essay article]
- Absolutists on lying to the murderer include Augustine, Aquinas, Kant, Newman, Geach; Constant, Mill, Sidgwick, Bok hold it permissible. [source: SEP-Lie §on moral definitions]
- **Korsgaard, "The Right to Lie: Kant on Dealing with Evil", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 15:4 (1986), 325–349** (reprinted in *Creating the Kingdom of Ends*, 1996). Summary from secondary sources (article itself not readable here — WS-1):
  - Under FUL the lie to the murderer *passes*: the murderer himself relies on deception (he hides his intent); a universal practice of lying to deceivers to thwart their deception remains effective so long as the murderer does not know you know what he is — so no contradiction in conception.
  - Under FH the lie seems forbidden, since deception (with coercion) is the paradigm of treating someone merely as a means; so the formulas diverge here.
  - Korsgaard's "double-level" theory: FH/Kingdom of Ends is the ideal for a world of full compliance; FUL sets a floor that may govern when dealing with evil (non-ideal conditions); in this case one may lie.
  - Known limit: if the murderer already knows you know his purpose, her FUL argument gives no permission. [WS-1: philosophyetc.net; Dialogue/Cambridge article; Northwestern PDF]
- Julia Driver: reformulate the maxim ("I will lie to save an innocent life") and it can be universalised, but it may still treat the murderer merely as a means. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kantian_ethics]

## 6. Objections to Kant: empty formalism, Mill, rigorism
- **Hegel (empty formalism):** the moral law is solely a principle of non-contradiction, lacking content (*Philosophy of Right* §135, per the Kant notes). Examples used by Hegel and followers: the deposit — FUL cannot settle whether a property system is good, since either answer can be made consistent; and helping the poor — if everyone helped the poor, there would be no poor, so beneficence would be self-defeating if universalised. Second Hegelian criticism: Kant sets reason against desire and gives no reason to be moral. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kantian_ethics (citing Singer 1983; Brooks 2012); research/early-modern--kant.md §8 (Freyenhagen 2011 calls the debate a "stand-off")]
- **Mill**, *Utilitarianism* ch. 1 (articles in *Fraser's Magazine* 1861; book 1863): when Kant deduces actual duties "he fails, almost grotesquely, to show that there would be any contradiction, any logical (not to say physical) impossibility, in the adoption by all rational beings of the most outrageously immoral rules of conduct. All he shows is that the consequences of their universal adoption would be such as no one would choose to incur." [sources: WS-3 (several copies of the text agree on wording; one edition has "derive" for "deduce"); dates https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarianism_(book)]
  - Kantian reply: the contradiction-in-conception test is not about bad consequences but about the maxim's own purpose being defeated / the practice it relies on being impossible (SEP-KM's lying-promise reconstruction; "practical" vs "logical" readings of the contradiction, Korsgaard). [sources: SEP-KM §5; research/early-modern--kant.md §8]
- **Rigorism / absolutism:** Kant endorses "fiat iustitia, pereat mundus" ("do justice though the world perish"), *Toward Perpetual Peace* (1795), Ak 8:378 — SEP-D calls absolutism "the Kantian response" to moral catastrophes. Kant: "a conflict of duties … is inconceivable" (*Metaphysics of Morals*, 1797, Ak 6:224). [source: SEP-D §3]
- Bernard Williams: Kant abstracts persons from character; Foot counted Kant among those responsible for neglect of virtue. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kantian_ethics]
- Anscombe ("Modern Moral Philosophy"): talk of moral obligation without a divine lawgiver is incoherent. [source: IEP-Ans]

## 7. Agent-centred constraints and options; the paradox of deontology (ARGUMENT)
- Deontologists typically combine non-consequentialist **obligations** (constraints, "restrictions") with non-consequentialist **permissions** (options, "prerogatives") (Scheffler 1982); strong permissions: one may do some acts even though they produce less good than alternatives (Moore 2008). [source: SEP-D §2]
- Agent-relative reason: a reason for a particular agent that need not be a reason for anyone else (Nagel 1986); e.g. a parent's special obligations and permission to save his own child rather than two strangers' children. "We are each enjoined to keep our own moral house in order." [source: SEP-D §2.1]
- Taxonomy: agent-centred vs patient-centred (rights-based) deontology (Scheffler 1988; Kamm 2007), plus contractualist deontology. Agent-centred versions focus on intention (→ double effect), on action/causation (→ doing/allowing), or both ("not to murder, that is, to kill in execution of an intention to kill", Moore 2008). [source: SEP-D §§2.1–2.3]
- Common objections to agent-centred views: a "narcissistic flavor" (keeping one's own hands clean while the world gets worse); the distinctions (intending/foreseeing, doing/allowing, etc.) seem morally insignificant or incoherent (Rachels 1975; Kagan 1989; Bennett 1981); "avoision" — Leo Katz's (1996) term for legalistic manipulation of the categories. [source: SEP-D §2.1]
- **Samuel Scheffler**, *The Rejection of Consequentialism* (Oxford UP, 1982; rev. 1994): distinguishes the **agent-centred prerogative** (permission not to maximise) from **agent-centred restrictions** (forbidding some acts even when they would have the best outcome). He finds a rationale for the prerogative but argues restrictions have "an air of paradox" against "maximizing rationality"; he does not find a satisfactory rationale and rejects them — his "hybrid" view. Scheffler (b. 1951), student of Nagel, Berkeley then NYU. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Scheffler ; WS-2 (OUP book page, commentaries); SEP-Cons §6 cites Scheffler 1982 on options]
- **Nozick**, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974; National Book Award 1975): rights as **side constraints** on others' pursuit of goals, grounded in each person's inviolability and the Kantian ban on treating humanity merely as a means: "Individuals have rights, and there are things no person or group may do to them (without violating their rights)" (ix). He contrasts this with a "utilitarianism of rights", which would permit violating a right to minimise total rights violations. Nozick also coined the problem of "moral catastrophes" for absolutist constraints. [sources: SEP-Rights §6.1; SEP-D §§1, 3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarchy,_State,_and_Utopia ; WS-6]
- **The paradox, step by step** (SEP-D's "moral form", present for all deontologies):
  1. Violating a deontological constraint (e.g. killing an innocent) is wrong/bad.
  2. If violations are bad, more violations (by others, or by one's own future self) are worse than fewer.
  3. Sometimes one violation by me would prevent several comparable violations by others.
  4. Deontology forbids my one violation even then.
  5. So deontology requires me to act so that the world contains more of the very wrongs it condemns — "extremely paradoxical."
  "Virulent" (conceptual) form: for agent-neutral rights theories, if everyone's rights matter equally, why not violate John's to protect Mary's and Susan's? (Scheffler 1988; Heuer 2011.) [source: SEP-D §§2.2, 3]
- Replies:
  - (a) Make constraints **agent-relative**: each of us may not use John, even to minimise usings of John by others; your using now cannot be traded off against others' usings — this answers the virulent form. [source: SEP-D §2.2]
  - (b) Deny that wrongness translates into badness of states of affairs that can be summed (Brook 2007), appealing to the **separateness of persons** (cf. Taurek 1977). *Cost*: if wrongs cannot be summed, neither can harms, so five deaths would not be worse than one and there would be no reason to switch in Trolley (Tenenbaum 2023); "If the numbers don't count, they seemingly don't count either way." [source: SEP-D §3]
  - (c) Allow wrongness to count as badness but treat norms as Raz-style "protected/exclusionary reasons" that exclude the competing maximising reasons; needs much defence (Moore 2025). [source: SEP-D §3]
  - SEP-D verdict: how to account for numbers without collapsing into consequentialism or reviving the paradox "will remain a huge thorn in the deontologist's side". [source: SEP-D §3]
  - Status/inviolability reply: Nagel (2002) defends a status view of rights but admits "it has proven extremely difficult to account for such a basic, individualized value such that it becomes morally intelligible", and that "there are evils great enough so that one would be justified in murdering or torturing an innocent person to prevent them". [source: SEP-Rights §6.1]
- Catastrophes: six possible deontological responses (two plausible): **absolutism** ("bite the bullet"; the Kantian response) or **threshold (moderate) deontology** — constraints hold until consequences cross a threshold, then consequentialism takes over (Moore 1997); e.g. A may not torture B to save two lives but may to save a thousand if the threshold lies between. [source: SEP-D §3]
- Conflicts of duties: answered by **specificationism** (build exceptions into norms) or by Ross's **prima facie** duties. [source: SEP-D §3]

## 8. Doing vs allowing
- Question: is doing harm morally worse than merely allowing it? If not, no objection to active euthanasia where passive is allowed, and an outcry is due at failing to prevent millions of child deaths from malnutrition, dehydration and measles. Consequentialists deny a difference; anti-consequentialists "almost universally" affirm one. [source: SEP-DA intro]
- **Rachels (1975) bathtub cases** (contrast strategy): Smith drowns his young cousin in the bath for an inheritance; Jones plans to, finds the boy already slipped under and lets him drown. Rachels: Smith is no worse than Jones, so killing is not per se worse than letting die. [source: SEP-DA §1]
- Replies: Kagan (1988) — the inference assumes the "Additive Assumption" (factors contribute the same everywhere); Kamm (2007) — it would be permissible to kill Smith to bring the child back but not Jones, so the cases differ morally; Quinn (1989) also replies. [source: SEP-DA §1]
- SEP-D's five agency distinctions (each part of the "doctrine of doing and allowing"): causing vs omitting; causing vs allowing (removing a defence one oneself provided — e.g. a doctor disconnecting equipment); causing vs enabling another's harm; causing vs redirecting an existing threat (Trolley); causing vs accelerating a death about to happen anyway. [source: SEP-D §2.1]
- Woollard, *Doing and Allowing Harm* (OUP 2015) is the standard recent monograph (author of SEP-DA). [source: SEP-DA bibliography]

## 9. The doctrine of double effect (DDE)
- **Origin:** Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 64, a. 7, on killing in self-defence: "Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention … the act of self-defense may have two effects: one, the saving of one's life; the other, the slaying of the aggressor." The act is lawful "since one's intention is to save one's own life"; but "if a man in self-defense uses more than necessary violence, it will be unlawful". Augustine had earlier held private self-defence impermissible ("can only proceed from some degree of inordinate self-love"). [source: SEP-DDE §1 — translator not named in SEP; the wording is that of the Fathers of the English Dominican Province translation — translator attribution NOT verified]
- Aquinas is "credited with introducing" the principle; it later became a "long-established doctrine of Catholic theology". [sources: SEP-DDE §1; SEP-D §2.1]
- **Four conditions** (*New Catholic Encyclopedia*, per Connell): (1) the act itself must be morally good or at least indifferent; (2) the agent may not positively will the bad effect but may permit it (and should get the good without the bad if possible); (3) the good effect must flow from the action at least as immediately as the bad — i.e. not be produced *by* the bad effect (no bad means to a good end); (4) the good must be sufficiently desirable to compensate for allowing the bad (proportionality). Joseph Mangan (1949, p. 43) gives a parallel four-condition version with explicit "the good effect and not the evil effect be intended". [source: SEP-DDE §1]
- Walzer (*Just and Unjust Wars*, 1977): those who cause foreseen harm must also accept risk/costs to minimise it. [source: SEP-DDE §1]
- **Tactical (strategic) vs terror bomber:** the terror bomber aims at civilian deaths to weaken enemy resolve (deaths intended as means); the tactical bomber aims at military targets foreseeing civilian deaths. Even with equal certainty and equal deaths, DDE judges terror bombing impermissible and tactical bombing permissible. [source: SEP-DDE §2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_double_effect]
- Other applications: euthanasia vs pain relief that hastens death (SEP notes the assumption that opioids for pain tend to hasten death is mistaken, §5.1); abortion vs hysterectomy of a pregnant woman with cancer; pre-emptive killing vs self-defence; suicide vs heroic shielding (throwing oneself on a grenade); Fat Man vs Switch. [source: SEP-DDE §2]
- Anscombe defended DDE in "War and Murder" and opposed Oxford's honorary degree for Truman (1956) as one who had deliberately authorised bombing non-combatants. [source: IEP-Ans]
- **Misreadings:** DDE does not say foresight-without-intention suffices — proportionality is always required; it is distinct from doing/allowing; it does not claim intending harm is always wrong in itself. [source: SEP-DDE §3]
- **Criticisms:**
  - Consequentialist: only consequences matter (Bennett 1981; Davis 1984 also argue foreseeing with certainty ≈ intending). [sources: SEP-DDE §4.1; SEP-D §2.1]
  - **Problem of closeness:** hard to tell harms foreseen as side effects from harms so close to the means that they must be intended (e.g. craniotomy/abortion vs hysterectomy — Boyle 1991; Davis 1984); "no clear resolution". [source: SEP-DDE §4.2]
  - **Side-effect (Knobe) effect:** people call a harmful side effect "intentional" more readily when they disapprove of it (Harman 1976; Knobe 2003, 2006), so the intended/foreseen classification may follow moral judgment rather than ground it. [source: SEP-DDE §4.3]
  - Quinn (1989) recasts DDE as direct vs indirect *agency* (harm to victims involved "as intentional objects"), non-absolutist. [source: SEP-DDE §4.4]
  - Scanlon (2008, *Moral Dimensions*) and McCarthy (2002): intentions bear on how well an agent deliberated, not on whether the act is permissible. [source: SEP-DDE end §6]

## 10. W. D. Ross and prima facie duties
- Sir William David Ross (b. 15 Apr 1877 Thurso, Scotland – d. 5 May 1971 Oxford). *The Right and the Good* (1930); *Foundations of Ethics* (1939); *Kant's Ethical Theory* (1954), a commentary on the *Grundlegung*. General editor of the Oxford translation of Aristotle (11 vols, 1908–1931); translated the *Metaphysics* and *Nicomachean Ethics*. [sources: SEP-R §1; IEP-R]
- **The list (RG 21, 1930)** — Ross initially lists seven: (1) fidelity (keep promises; IEP adds truthfulness), (2) reparation (correct a previous wrong), (3) gratitude (return services), (4) justice (distribute happiness in proportion to merit, RG 21, 26), (5) beneficence (promote the general good), (6) self-improvement (in virtue and knowledge), (7) non-maleficence / non-injury. [sources: IEP-R §4c; SEP-R §4.1, §4.3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deontology]
- **Seven or five? (debate)** Ross argues justice and self-improvement can be subsumed under beneficence (RG 27; FE 288–89), so SEP (following Hurka, McNaughton, Phillips, Stratton-Lake) counts **five** foundational duties: fidelity, reparation, gratitude, beneficence, non-maleficence. IEP: in *Foundations of Ethics* beneficence, self-improvement and justice are subsumed under a single duty to promote intrinsic value, reducing seven to five; Audi lists ten. [sources: SEP-R §§4.1, 4.3; IEP-R §4c]
- Prima facie duty: not an actual duty but a consideration with "a certain degree of obligatoriness or weight" (Phillips 2019); Ross admits "prima facie" is an unfortunate phrase (RG 20; FE 84–85) — it is not a merely apparent duty. One's actual duty ("duty proper") is found by weighing; "the decision rests with perception" (RG 42, borrowing Aristotle, NE 1109b23). [sources: SEP-R §4.1; IEP-R §4c, §2]
- Weights: non-maleficence weightier than beneficence: "We think the principle 'do evil to no one' more pressing than the principle 'do good to every one', except when the evil is very substantially outweighed by the good" (FE 75); fidelity, reparation, gratitude generally weightier than promoting general good. [source: SEP-R §4.1]
- Against ideal utilitarianism (Moore): neighbours stand to me not only as possible beneficiaries but "in the relation of promisee to promiser, of creditor to debtor, of wife to husband, of child to parent, of friend to friend, of fellow countryman to fellow countryman, and the like; and each of these relationships is the foundation of a prima facie duty" (RG 19); utilitarianism "ignores … the highly personal character of duty" (RG 22). [sources: IEP-R §6; SEP-R §4.1]
- Epistemology: basic duties are self-evident "in the sense that when we have reached sufficient mental maturity and have given sufficient attention to the proposition it is evident without any need of proof"; "both in mathematics and in ethics we have certain crystal-clear intuitions" (FE 144). [source: SEP-R §5]
- Objection (Rawls 1971, 41): without a weighting rule, "an intuitionist conception of justice is … but half a conception". [source: SEP-R §4.3]
- SEP-D: Ross's prima facie duties are one of two standard ways of avoiding conflicts of duty; danger of collapse into consequentialism or specificationism; epistemic vs pro tanto readings. [source: SEP-D §3]

## 11. Rights theories (brief)
- Hohfeld (1879–1918): four "incidents" — privilege, claim, power, immunity. [source: SEP-Rights §2.1]
- Function: **will theory** (right-holder as "a small scale sovereign", Hart 1982: 183 — control over another's duty) vs **interest theory** (rights protect interests; Raz). [source: SEP-Rights §2.2]
- Dworkin's metaphor: rights as "trumps" over social aims; Raz: claim-rights give "exclusionary reasons". [source: SEP-Rights §5.1]
- Status theories (Nozick 1974; Kamm; Nagel) ground rights in inviolability/dignity; instrumental theories (e.g. rule utilitarianism) treat rights as useful rules. Bentham: natural-rights doctrine "is from beginning to end so much flat assertion". [source: SEP-Rights §6]
- Patient-centred deontology = rights-based; core "Means Principle" right against being used without consent. Loop Trolley (Thomson 1985): on the using-based view one may not switch (the one's body is needed to stop the trolley); agent-relative views may still allow it. [source: SEP-D §2.2]

## 12. Contractualism (Scanlon) and the numbers problem
- T. M. Scanlon (b. 28 June 1940), Harvard (Alford Professor until retirement 2016); *What We Owe to Each Other*, Harvard University Press, **1998**; earlier "Contractualism and Utilitarianism" in Sen & Williams (eds), *Utilitarianism and Beyond* (CUP 1982), 103–28. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._M._Scanlon ; SEP-C bibliography]
- Core formula: "An act is wrong if its performance under the circumstances would be disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced, general agreement." (Scanlon 1998, p. 153). [source: SEP-C §1]
- Wrongness = unjustifiability to others; the value realised is "mutual recognition"; "wrong" is the primary predicate. Contractualism interprets the Kantian idea of treating persons as ends as treating them by principles they could not reasonably reject. [source: SEP-C §1]
- **Contractarianism vs contractualism:** contractarianism (Hobbes; David Gauthier 1986) — mutual advantage, each maximising self-interest; contractualism — principles no one could reasonably reject, motivated by the desire to justify oneself to others. [source: SEP-C §2]
- **Individualist restriction:** only "various individuals' reasons for objecting to that principle and alternatives to it" count (1998, p. 229); no interpersonal aggregation of complaints — honours Rawls's "separateness of persons". Reasonableness: if a principle imposes burden b1 on me but every alternative imposes a greater b2 on someone else, I cannot reasonably reject it. [source: SEP-C §3, §3.1]
- Deontological constraints: which prohibitions hold "is best answered by considering what principles licensing others to take our lives could be reasonably rejected" (1998, p. 85); one may accept "that the positive value of saving others does not justify killing someone" (p. 84). Scanlon's own case: an innocent technician receiving electric shocks vs interrupting millions' enjoyment of a televised World-Cup-style sporting event (1998, p. 235). [source: SEP-C §§3.1, 5]
- **The numbers problem / Taurek:** John M. Taurek, "Should the Numbers Count?", *Philosophy and Public Affairs* 6(4) (1977): 293–316. Taurek's case: one person (David) needs all of a drug to survive, five others need one-fifth each; Taurek argues one need not save the five and that tossing a coin gives each an equal chance — five people's losses do not add up to a loss suffered by anyone. [sources: SEP-C & SEP-D bibliographies; WS-5 (Taurek's text not read)] Interpretations differ on how numbers-sceptical Taurek is (Sung 2022). [WS-5]
- SEP-C's version ("The Rocks", "drawn from" Taurek): five swimmers on one rock, one on another; time to reach only one. Contractualism seems to deadlock (each person's complaint is the same, "leaves me to die"), suggesting a coin toss. Scanlon-style **tie-breaker argument**: if one-vs-one is a coin toss, applying it to five-vs-one treats it as if the extra people were not there, failing to respect each of them; their claims are "un-trumped", so save the five (critique: Otsuka 2001). [source: SEP-C §8]
- Parfit (2003) aggregation case: one person suffering agony 100 years vs a million suffering 100 years minus a day; the individualist restriction seems to favour the million suffering. [source: SEP-C §8]
- Redundancy objection: if one may reject principles because they permit wrong acts, the contractualist test presupposes the wrongness it is meant to explain. [source: SEP-C §6]
- SEP-D: contractualism may be better read as metaethical than first-order; Harsanyi (1973) argued contractors would choose utilitarianism. [source: SEP-D §2.3]
- Few deontologists accept Taurek's conclusion; others propose saving the many, weighted lotteries, or coin flips (Kamm 1993; Scanlon 2003; Otsuka 2006; etc.). [source: SEP-D §3]

## 13. Non-Western duty ethics (with debates on the label "deontological")
### Bhagavad Gītā: svadharma and niṣkāma karma
- The Gītā opens the sixth book of the *Mahābhārata* (formulated around the start of the Common Era); dialogue between the warrior Arjuna and Kṛṣṇa, his charioteer and an avatar of Viṣṇu; 18 chapters. [source: IEP-BG intro, §1]
- Arjuna's three arguments against fighting (ch. 1), read by Ranganathan as virtue-ethical, consequentialist (death of kin, no joy in victory, 1.34–36) and deontological/social-order (war produces lawlessness endangering women and children, 1.41). [source: IEP-BG §§1, 7]
- **Karma yoga** = in Ranganathan's reading "Deontology formulated as doing duty without the motive of consequence"; "better one's own duty poorly performed than someone else's well performed" (Gītā 2.38, 47, 18.47); duty "suited to one's nature" — caste duty (18.41). Kṛṣṇa also recommends **bhakti yoga** (devotion), which Ranganathan treats as a distinct, fourth kind of theory, and at the end Kṛṣṇa recommends abandoning all dharmas to seek him (Rāmānuja found this troubling); Ranganathan cites 18.57 for giving up moral rules as a sacrifice to the ideal. (Verse number 18.66 for "abandon all dharmas" NOT verified.) [source: IEP-BG §§7–8]
- **Svadharma** ("one's own dharma/law/duty"): Gītā 3.35 (Aurobindo trans.): "Better is one's own law of works, swadharma, though in itself faulty than an alien law well-wrought out; death in one's own law of being is better, perilous is it to follow an alien law." 18.47 repeats the first clause. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svadharma]
- **Niṣkāma karma** ("desireless action", *nih* + *kāma* + *karma*): Gītā 2.47 (Radhakrishnan trans.): "To action alone hast thou a right and never at all to its fruits; let not the fruits of action be thy motive; neither let there be in thee any attachment to inaction." Glossed as "duty for duty's sake". Gandhi treated *niṣkāmakarma* as central. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nishkama_Karma ; IEP-BG §7]
- **Debate:** Amartya Sen (*The Idea of Justice*, 2009; "Consequential Evaluation and Practical Reason", *J. Phil.* 2000) reads Arjuna as attending to consequences and Kṛṣṇa as an archetypal deontologist; Joshua Anderson ("Sen and the Bhagavad Gita", *Asian Philosophy* 22:1, 2012) criticises Sen's reading [WS-7; IEP-BG §9 notes Sen's "deontological interpretation" was "floated and criticized (Anderson 2012)"]. Others read Kṛṣṇa's promise that duty brings good outcomes as rule-consequentialist (Sreekumar, *J. Indian Phil.* 40:3, 2012; Theodor 2010). Ranganathan argues that reading the Gītā through the Western trio distorts it: it critiques "conventional morality" (virtue, consequences and conventional deontology) and defends two *procedural* theories, karma yoga and bhakti. Also, in South Asian thought Pūrva Mīmāṃsā is a deontological tradition (Clooney 2017), and Yāmunācārya (916–1041) read the first hexad of the Gītā as karma yoga, "a deontological perfection of duty". [source: IEP-BG §§2, 3, 9]
- Note for the writer: svadharma in the Gītā is tied to caste (varṇa) duty (18.41–47) — a point critics and defenders both stress; the chapter should say so plainly. [source: IEP-BG §8]

### Confucian li as role-duties
- *Li* (禮): originally religious sacrifice, then "rite/ritual, ceremony, decorum, propriety" (Wing-tsit Chan); ranges in the Analects from ancestor rites and burial of parents to everyday courtesy and court protocol. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_(Confucianism) ; SEP-CE §2.2]
- Practising li lets people fulfil their roles (parent, child, ruler, subject …) sustaining the five relationships (Gardner 2014, via WP). Analects 12.11: "Let the ruler be a ruler, the subject a subject, a father a father, and a son a son" (IEP rendering, translator not named). Analects 12.1 (Slingerland 2003): "Restraining yourself and returning to the rites constitutes *ren*." [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_(Confucianism) ; IEP-Conf §4; SEP-CE §2.2]
- Analects 13.18: the "Straight Body" who reported his father for stealing a sheep; Confucius: in his village uprightness lies in fathers and sons covering for each other (contrast Euthyphro). [source: SEP-CE §1]
- **Debate:** is Confucian ethics a virtue ethics, a **role ethics** (Roger Ames, *Confucian Role Ethics: A Vocabulary*, U. of Hawai'i Press 2011; Henry Rosemont; Robert Neville 2016), or something else? Wong: there is "not enough discussion in the Confucian texts … addressed to the kind of questions" Western virtue, deontological and consequentialist theories answer; li is relational rather than autonomy-based. Calling li "deontological" is therefore at best an analogy (role-specific duties, not universal rules derived from reason). [source: SEP-CE §§2.1, 2.3; research/ancient--confucianism.md]

## Surprises
- **Ross's list is not settled at seven.** Textbooks list seven prima facie duties (RG 21), but Ross himself subsumes justice and self-improvement under beneficence, and SEP's entry counts five foundational duties; IEP says *Foundations of Ethics* (1939) reduces the seven to five. The chapter should say "seven, which Ross later reduced to five".
- **Foot's trolley was not the bystander case.** Foot (1967) had a trolley *driver* (choosing between killing one and killing five); the bystander-at-the-switch version is Thomson's, and Thomson herself later (2008) argued the bystander may *not* switch.
- **The famous "Rocks" lifeboat-style case in textbooks is not Taurek's own example**: Taurek's case is a drug that one person needs entirely or five need a fifth each; SEP's "Rocks" is "drawn from" it.
- **"Deontology" was coined by Bentham** (the great utilitarian), in a different sense; the modern sense comes from C. D. Broad (1930).
- **Korsgaard does not straightforwardly defend Kant's verdict**: on her reading the Universal Law formula *permits* lying to the murderer, and it is the Humanity formula that seems to forbid it — the reverse of what many expect.
- **The opioid example for double effect rests on a factual mistake**: SEP notes that properly titrated opioids for pain relief do not typically hasten death.
- **Consequentialism the word** was coined by Anscombe (1958), a deontological critic.
