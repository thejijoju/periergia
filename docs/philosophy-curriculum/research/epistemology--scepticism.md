## 1. What scepticism is, and its kinds

- Philosophical scepticism, as opposed to ordinary scepticism, says we do not know things we ordinarily think we know. Ordinary: we don't know whether the number of stars in the Milky Way is even. Philosophical: we don't know that the sun will come out tomorrow. [source: SEP-Sk §1]
- Comesaña and Klein define scepticism about a field F as the claim that "the only justified attitude with respect to propositions in F is suspension of judgment". Suspension of judgement is a genuine attitude alongside belief and disbelief, not just having no opinion. [source: SEP-Sk §1]
- **Three kinds by scope** (the labels are used "without any particular claim to historical accuracy"): **Humean** scepticism grants knowledge from experience and deduction but denies knowledge by non-deductive (e.g. inductive) inference; **Cartesian** scepticism denies that we are justified in believing any proposition about the external world, allowing knowledge only of our own mental states ("it seems to us that we have hands"); **Pyrrhonian** scepticism is universal: we know no proposition, not even that it seems to us we have hands. [source: SEP-Sk §2]
- **Local vs radical (global):** local scepticism targets one subject matter (e.g. abstract objects, the conclusions of inductive arguments); radical scepticism afflicts most of our beliefs. Pritchard also distinguishes epistemological scepticism (about whether beliefs are known/justified) from ontological scepticism (about whether some entity, e.g. the self or God, exists). [source: IEP-Skep intro]
- Pritchard: the two historically most influential forms are the Pyrrhonian (counter every ground for belief with equally persuasive grounds against) and the Cartesian (sceptical hypotheses show we cannot be certain); modern discussion treats scepticism as a **paradox**: individually plausible claims that jointly lead to a devastating conclusion. [source: IEP-Skep intro]
- Is global (Pyrrhonian) scepticism self-refuting? It is formally consistent; it is self-refuting only given a "Commitment Iteration Principle" (being committed to p commits you to belief being justified about p), which the Pyrrhonian also suspends judgement on. Comesaña and Klein: contemporary philosophers find Pyrrhonism interesting "not because they take seriously the possibility of its truth" but because the replies to its arguments shape modern epistemology. [source: SEP-Sk §2]
- **Induction (Humean scepticism)** in brief: inductive knowledge depends on a Principle of the Uniformity of Nature; it cannot be known demonstratively (it is not necessary) nor inductively (circular); so no inductive knowledge. Replies: inductive justification of induction (van Cleve 1984), rationalist (Kant), concessive (Popper 1935). **Cross-ref *Induction, Probability and Inference to the Best Explanation*; do not repeat.** [source: SEP-Sk §3]
- **Other minds:** the subjective/objective distinction behind modern other-minds scepticism "is not envisaged in ancient thought", though Pyrrhonist talk of "affections" anticipates it (Fine). Mention briefly as a further local scepticism; no argument needed. [source: SEP-Anc §5.3]
- Etymology: Greek *skepsis* means investigation; a "skeptic" is literally an inquirer. Ancient sceptics aimed at "a life without belief". [source: SEP-Anc intro]
- Ancient vs modern: early modern external-world scepticism involves a creator who made the world and our faculties; "if this assumption is crucial to external world skepticism, then the ancients do not conceive of this skeptical problem" (Burnyeat 1980, 1982; Williams 1988; Fine 2003). Framing scepticism as a paradox one thinker feels torn by is "foreign to antiquity", where sceptics and their opponents were different people arguing. Fine argues Sextus' "externally underlying things" (*ta ektos hupokeimena*, PH 1.61 etc.) may give the ancients their own version. [source: SEP-Anc §5.4] (A debate: name it.)

## 2. Pyrrhonism (cross-ref *Hellenistic Philosophy*; keep the history short)

- Pyrrho of Elis: c. 365/360 – c. 275/270 BCE; wrote nothing; went with Alexander's expedition to India. [source: SEP-Pyr; SEP-Anc §4.1]
- Timon of Phlius, his follower: 325/20 – 235/30 BCE. [source: SEP-Anc §4.1]
- The Aristocles passage (Eusebius, *PE* 14.18.1–5; trans. Bett 2000 with changes, as given in SEP-Anc): the happy person must ask three questions, what things are like by nature, how we should be disposed to them, what results; Pyrrho shows things are "equally indifferent and unstable and indeterminate (*adiaphora kai astathmêta kai anepikrita*)", so perceptions and beliefs neither tell the truth nor lie; we should be "without opinions and without inclinations and without wavering", saying of each thing that it "no more is than is not, or both is and is not, or neither is nor is not"; the result: first speechlessness (*aphasia*), then freedom from worry (*ataraxia*); Aenesidemus says pleasure. [source: SEP-Anc §4.1]
- Metaphysical (things are indeterminate: Bett) vs epistemological (we cannot determine them: Svavarsson, Thorsrud) readings. Bett: Pyrrho "was arguably no Pyrrhonian skeptic", since he probably held a dogmatic view about reality. [source: SEP-Anc §4.1]
- Indian influence: Beckwith (2015, *Greek Buddha*) finds in Pyrrho "an authentic representative of early Buddhism"; Bett: "at least room for debate"; some think language barriers ruled out subtle exchange, which Bett thinks "overly pessimistic". Mills (2018) argues for an Indian "skepticism about philosophy" resembling Pyrrhonism. [source: SEP-Pyr; SEP-Anc §4.1] (Already treated in *Hellenistic Philosophy*: just cross-refer.)
- Aenesidemus (1st c. BCE) left the Academy to revive a more radical scepticism; "arguably, he is the first Pyrrhonian skeptic". Basic elements: oppose appearances and thoughts → equipollence (*isostheneia*, "of equal weight") → suspension → tranquillity → life according to appearances. [source: SEP-Anc §4.2]
- **The Ten Modes** (Sextus PH 1.36–163; also D.L. 9.78–88 and Philo of Alexandria), in Sextus' order: (1) differences between kinds of animals; (2) differences among human beings; (3) differences between the senses; (4) states (age, motion/rest, emotions); (5) positions, distances and places; (6) mixtures; (7) composition (quantity) of the object; (8) relativity (which comprises the others); (9) constancy or rarity of occurrence; (10) ways of life, customs, laws, myths and dogmatic assumptions. Examples: the tower that looks round from afar and square close up; honey sweet to most, bitter to the jaundiced. [source: SEP-Anc §4.2; SEP-Sext §3.5.1]
- Two readings of the modes: **Conflicting Appearances** (X appears F to A and F* to B; we cannot judge because we are a party to the dispute; there is "no 'view from nowhere'": Annas and Barnes 1985) vs **Causal Invariance** (if X were F by nature it would affect everyone as F: Woodruff, Bett). [source: SEP-Anc §4.2]
- Agrippa: "almost nothing is known" (1st–2nd c. CE); the Five Modes are at PH 1.164–177 and D.L. 9.88–89: (1) **disagreement** (*diaphônia*); (2) **infinite regress**; (3) **relativity**; (4) **hypothesis** (an assumption made without argument); (5) **circularity** (*diallêlos*, "reciprocal"). "Among the most famous arguments of ancient skepticism." [source: SEP-Anc §4.3]
- Subtleties to keep: *anepikriton* should be "undecided", not "undecidable" (claiming undecidability would be dogmatic: Barnes 1990); the regress mode speaks of anything that makes something credible, not narrowly of proof (Bury's Loeb translation obscures this); D.L. 9.90 says the Five Modes do away with "proof, criterion, sign, cause, movement, learning, coming into being, and that there is anything by nature good or bad". Morison (2018): the Ten and Five Modes serve the same purpose, producing equal and opposing arguments. [source: SEP-Anc §4.3–4.4]
- Sextus Empiricus: ca. 160–210 CE per SEP-Anc; "probably 2nd or 3rd century CE" per SEP-Carn (give a range). "Empiricus" marks the Empiricist medical school, though Sextus says scepticism is closer to Methodism. Works: *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* (PH, 3 books; PH 1 is "the only general account of Pyrrhonism that survives") and the works traditionally called *Against the Mathematicians* (M), better *Against the Learned*. [source: SEP-Anc §4.4]
- Scepticism is "an ability or skill (I 8), not a set of beliefs"; the sceptic keeps investigating, unlike dogmatists and "negative dogmatists" who claim things cannot be known (PH 1.1–4). [source: SEP-Sext §3.1; SEP-Anc §4.4]
- Tranquillity comes unexpectedly (Apelles' sponge, PH 1.28) and follows suspension "as a shadow follows a body" (PH 1.29). The end (*telos*): tranquillity in matters of belief and **moderate affection** (*metriopatheia*) in what is forced on us: the sceptic still feels cold, thirst and pain but suffers less, not adding the belief that pain is bad (PH 1.25–29). [source: SEP-Sext §3.3; SEP-Anc §4.4]
- Sceptical phrases "cancel themselves out", like fire burning itself out, or like a ladder one climbs and throws away (M 8.481; image later used by Wittgenstein, *Tractatus* 6.54). [source: SEP-Anc §4.4(iv)]

### Argument A (step by step): Agrippa's trilemma / the regress argument
Reconstruction from SEP-Sk §6 (Comesaña and Klein), suitable for the chapter:
1. If a belief is justified, it is either basically (non-inferentially) justified or inferentially justified.
2. There are no basic justified beliefs. [mode of hypothesis: an unsupported starting point is mere assertion]
3. So if a belief is justified, it is justified by belonging to an inferential chain. [from 1, 2]
4. Every inferential chain either (a) is infinite, (b) contains a circle, or (c) contains unjustified beliefs.
5. No belief is justified by an infinite chain. [mode of infinite regress]
6. No belief is justified by a circular chain. [mode of circularity]
7. No belief is justified by a chain containing unjustified beliefs.
8. So no belief is justified.
- Dialectic in practice: the sceptic asks the dogmatist for a reason for p1; refusal → hypothesis; p1 itself → circle; p2 → same question again; eventually either no reason or a repeated one. [source: SEP-Sk §6]
- Caveat: "somewhat misleading" to present Pyrrhonism as an argument, since Pyrrhonists suspend judgement on premises and conclusion; what matters is whether *we* accept the premises. Applied to epistemological theories themselves it becomes "the problem of the criterion" (Chisholm 1973). [source: SEP-Sk §6]
- **Replies (each rejects a premise):** **Foundationalism** rejects 2 (some beliefs are justified by experience, not by other beliefs; traditional foundationalists: basic beliefs are about experiences; moderate: experience can directly justify external-world beliefs); **infinitism** rejects 5 ("the answer … that has received the least attention"; Klein 1999, 2007; Aikin 2011); **coherentism** rejects 6 (BonJour 1985, Lehrer 1990; objection: a coherent system could be swapped wholesale between two people, preserving coherence but not justification); **"positism"** (Comesaña and Klein's own label, from Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* and Ortega y Gasset's *Ideas y Creencias*, 1940) rejects 7: chains end in posits that are neither justified nor unjustified; objection: it turns "a doxastic necessity into an epistemic virtue". Many now mix options (e.g. Haack's 1993 "foundherentism"). "It is a testament to the endurance of Pyrrhonian Skepticism that philosophers continue in this way to grapple with it." [source: SEP-Sk §§6.1–6.5]
- Name: also called the **Münchhausen trilemma**, a name coined by Hans Albert in 1968 (from a trilemma "dogmatism versus infinite regress versus psychologism" in Popper, who credited J. F. Fries). [source: WP-Münchhausen_trilemma]
- Indian parallel (see §4): Nāgārjuna's regress/circle/self-evidence argument against the *pramāṇas*.

### Argument B (step by step): the apraxia objection and Sextus' reply (living by appearances)
1. (Stoic objection) Action requires assent to an impression.
2. The sceptic withholds all assent.
3. So the sceptic cannot act (*apraxia*), or cannot live a human life.
- Reply (PH 1.21–24): the sceptic follows appearances "without belief" (*adoxastôs*) by a **fourfold observance**. Annas and Barnes's translation of PH 1.24: "By nature's guidance we are naturally capable of perceiving and thinking. By the necessitation of feelings, hunger conducts us to food and thirst to drink. By the handing down of customs and laws, we accept, from an everyday point of view, that piety is good and impiety bad. By teaching of kinds of expertise we are not inactive in those expertises which we accept." [source: SEP-Sext §3.4.1; SEP-Anc §4.4]
- Without making assertions, the sceptic reports "like a chronicler" what appears now (PH 1.4). [source: SEP-Anc §4.4]
- Earlier replies by the Academics: Arcesilaus, act on "the reasonable" (*to eulogon*: M 7.158); Carneades, on the "persuasive/probable" impression (*pithanon*, Latin *probabile*). [source: IEP-AGS; SEP-Carn §2.2]
- **Debate: can the Pyrrhonist really live it? (the "rustic vs urbane" debate).** Michael Frede, "The Sceptic's Beliefs" (German 1979; English in Frede 1987; repr. Burnyeat and Frede (eds), *The Original Sceptics*, 1997): the sceptic can hold ordinary, everyday beliefs, rejecting only "dogma" in the sense of assent to the "non-evident matters investigated by the sciences" (PH 1.13); later (1984) he distinguishes two kinds of assent, "having a view" vs "taking a position". Myles Burnyeat, "Can the Sceptic Live His Scepticism?" (1980, in *Doubt and Dogmatism*) and Jonathan Barnes, "The Beliefs of a Pyrrhonist" (1982): Pyrrhonism rejects all belief; Burnyeat: sceptical statements merely record how things appear to one and so are not true or false. **Barnes followed Galen in calling the all-belief-rejecting sceptic "rustic" and coined "urbane" for Frede's kind** (Barnes 1982: 61); Barnes argued the urbane reading fails because anything, even whether honey is sweet, can be treated as a non-evident matter. Morison concludes "the urbane interpretation is still standing". "In the past 40 years, scholars have paid attention to this question more than to any other interpretive issue." Recent work tries to get "Beyond Rustic and Urbane" (O'Donnell 2025). [source: SEP-Sext §3.4, §4.1 and bibliography; SEP-Anc §4.4(iii)]
- Translation point: Bury's Loeb renders *adoxastôs* "undogmatically", which suggests room for non-dogmatic beliefs; it means "without belief" (Burnyeat). [source: SEP-Anc §4.4(iii)]
- Hume's verdict (cross-ref *Berkeley and Hume*): in *Enquiry* 12 Hume rejects "antecedent" scepticism (which would make any epistemic progress impossible), explores "consequent" scepticism in an extreme form he calls "Pyrrhonian", rejects it "because it is harmful and psychologically impossible to sustain", and endorses "mitigated" or "Academic" scepticism, which lowers our degrees of confidence and limits the scope of inquiry. In the *Treatise* only "carelessness and inattention" save us from scepticism about the external world (T 1.4.2.57); the "Title Principle": "where reason is lively, and mixes itself with some propensity, it ought to be assented to" (T 1.4.7.11). [source: SEP-Hume §2.3]
- Hume, *Enquiry* 12 Part 2 (WS only, see Quotations): "Nature is always too strong for principle"; Pyrrhonian doubt is subverted by "action, and employment, and the occupations of common life"; the first and most trivial event in life puts the Pyrrhonist on the same footing as everyone else. [source: WS-1, WS-2]
- A recent paper, "Hume is the Enemy of Pyrrho" (*Philosophy*, 2021), argues Hume criticises Pyrrhonism rather than adopting it. [source: WS-2] (Optional.)

## 3. Academic scepticism (brief; cross-ref *Hellenistic Philosophy*)

- Arcesilaus of Pitane: 315/4–241/40 BCE (SEP-Arc; IEP-AGS gives c. 318–243); scholarch of the Academy from 268/7 (SEP-Arc; IEP-AGS: after Crates died c. 272) until his death. D.L. 4.28: "the first to argue on both sides of a question"; Cicero says he revived Socrates' practice (*De Finibus* 2.2). He attacked the Stoic "kataleptic" (cognitive) impression: for any true impression there could be an indistinguishable false one (twins, eggs, wax seals; dreams and madness: Cicero, *Lucullus* 84–91). He reportedly did not even claim to know that nothing can be known (*Academica*). [source: SEP-Arc; IEP-AGS §a]
- Dialectical interpretation (Couissin 1929): Arcesilaus only showed that the Stoics, by their own premises, should suspend judgement. [source: IEP-AGS]
- Carneades of Cyrene: 214–129/8 BCE; scholarch before 155 BCE, when he went to Rome on an Athenian embassy; the story of his speeches for and against justice on successive days is late and doubted. Said "If Chrysippus had not been, I would not have been" (D.L. 4.62). Best known for **probabilism**: action and inquiry can rest on "probable" (*probabilis*, Cicero's Latin for *pithanos*, "persuasive") impressions. Clitomachus' reading: the wise withhold assent; others (Metrodorus, Philo) took a fallibilist line; Carneades may have distinguished "assent" from "approval" (*Luc.* 104). [source: SEP-Carn §§1–2; IEP-AGS]
- Cicero (106–43 BCE), *Academica* ("On Academic Scepticism"), from his burst of writing in 45–44 BCE; survives in a "misshapen state" with parts of two editions; the *Lucullus* mounts the case against the sceptical Academy (e.g. the sceptic must surely grasp with certainty that nothing can be grasped with certainty). [source: SEP-Cic §6.1, §2]
- Academic vs Pyrrhonian: Pyrrhonism aims at tranquillity and gives pride of place to appearances; tranquillity "is not part of Academic skepticism". [source: SEP-Anc §4.1]
- Augustine's *Contra Academicos* refutes the Academics; *City of God* 11.26: "si enim fallor, sum" ("even if I err, I am"). [source: SEP-Anc §5.1] (Cross-ref *Augustine*.)

## 4. Indian scepticism (cross-ref *Mahāyāna Philosophy* and *Jains, Ājīvikas and Materialists*)

### Nāgārjuna (c. 150–200 CE per Mills; see WS-8)
- In the *Vigrahavyāvartanī* ("Dispeller of Disputes"/"End of Disputes") Nāgārjuna answers the Nyāya charge that "all things are empty" refutes itself. [source: IEP-Nag §4; SEP-Nag]
- **His argument against the means of knowledge (*pramāṇa*), which closely parallels Agrippa** (Berger's account): a putative fact is proved either (a) by something else or (b) as self-evident. (a) leads to infinite regress (Nyāya's own example: to trust a scale you must test it with a second scale, which needs testing too...). (b) is "a vacuous claim": if things were self-evident, proof would be superfluous. (c) Mutual corroboration of instruments and objects is "actually circular", like calling one man "father" because he has a son and another "son" because he has a father. [source: IEP-Nag §4]
- Westerhoff's version: three ways to establish the *pramāṇas* (mutual coherence; self-establishment; instruments and objects establishing each other); self-establishment would avoid a vicious regress but fails; mutual establishment is circular. Nāgārjuna lists four *pramāṇas*: perception, inference, recognition of likeness, testimony. [source: SEP-Nag §3.4]
- He used the logicians' own rejoinders: infinite regress (*anavasthā*), circularity, vacuous principle; he entered debate as a *vaitaṇḍika*, refuting without proposing a counter-thesis; Berger: "Nagarjuna has rightly merited the label of skeptic", and like the Greek sceptics his scepticism leads somewhere: to the insight that all things lack fixed essence. [source: IEP-Nag §2]
- **"I have no thesis" (VV 29)**, Berger's paraphrase: "If my statement (about emptiness) were a philosophical thesis, then it would indeed be flawed; but I assert no thesis, and so the flaw is not mine." Apart from his two major commentators, "this stance … satisfied no one in the Indian philosophical tradition"; such a debater was suspected to be "more likely a charlatan than a sage". [source: IEP-Nag §4]

### Jayarāśi Bhaṭṭa
- Flourished c. 800 (Franco: c. 770–830; Balcerowicz: c. 800–840), probably in southern India. Wrote the *Tattvopaplavasiṃha* ("The Lion that Upsets All Principles"; Balcerowicz: "The Lion [Destroying] the Delusion of Categories"). Manuscript discovered 1926, published 1940 (Saṁghavī and Pārīkh, Gaekwad Oriental Series 87, Baroda). [source: WP-Jayarāśi_Bhaṭṭa]
- Argument: one cannot reach knowledge by the *pramāṇas*, because one would first need valid criteria for valid cognition, which requires prior knowledge of reality to test them against; every means is either flawed or needs a further means, which stands in the same need. Perception cannot establish that it is not illusory; inference rests on generalisations that cannot be shown universal; testimony needs a reliable witness, established by another *pramāṇa*. [source: WP-Jayarāśi_Bhaṭṭa]
- Affiliation debated: Franco and Balcerowicz place him in the Cārvāka/Lokāyata (materialist) tradition (he pays homage to Bṛhaspati in the colophon; Franco: "a radical change from a rather primitive materialism to a highly sophisticated form of scepticism"); Chattopadhyaya, Werner and Bhattacharya deny it; Matilal, Solomon, Gokhale and Mills classify him as a sceptic. Balcerowicz himself argues he was not, strictly, a sceptic. [source: WP-Jayarāśi_Bhaṭṭa] (Already covered in *Jains, Ājīvikas and Materialists*: cross-refer.)

### Śrīharṣa
- Indian philosopher and poet, northern India, 12th century CE; only surviving philosophical work *The Sweets of Refutation* (*Khaṇḍanakhaṇḍakhādya*; SEP spells it *Khaṇḍanakhaṇḍanakhādya*). Whether he defended Advaita Vedānta is controversial (Phillips 1995, Ram-Prasad 2002 vs Granoff 1978). [source: SEP-Sri intro]
- Aim: "to demonstrate the instability of rational inquiry within philosophy. For any argument that a philosopher may offer for her view, there is always an equally persuasive counterargument" (an equipollence theme). He attacks the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika "method of definition", including definitions of knowledge, and Das says he "anticipates" twentieth-century difficulties in defining knowledge and causation. [source: SEP-Sri intro]
- Mills's dates: Śrī Harṣa c. 1125–1180. [source: WS-8]
- Ethan Mills, *Three Pillars of Skepticism in Classical India: Nāgārjuna, Jayarāśi, and Śrī Harṣa* (Lexington Books, 2018): argues India has a tradition of "skepticism about philosophy" itself and links it to Pyrrhonism; reviewers note Indologists do not all accept the three as sceptics. [source: WS-8; SEP-Anc §4.1 cites Mill(s) 2018]

## 5. Chinese: Zhuangzi (cross-ref *Mozi, the Daoists and the Legalists*)

- Butterfly dream, end of ch. 2 (Inner Chapters), Burton Watson's translation (*Chuang Tzu: Basic Writings*, 1964, p. 44): see Quotations. Hansen's SEP translation of the same (Zhuangzi 2:14) ends "This is called 'things change'". [source: WP-Zhuangzi_(book); SEP-Zh §4.8]
- Readings: Graham: the passage makes "the distinction between waking and dreaming … another false dichotomy. If [one] distinguishes them, how can [one] tell if [one] is now dreaming or awake?" (Graham 1981, pp. 21–22). Hansen: it illustrates "mild skepticism": the sense of being liberated from error by a gestalt shift "may go both ways". [source: WP-Zhuangzi_(book); SEP-Zh §4.8]
- The "great awakening" (Zhuangzi 2:12, Hansen's translation): "When we dream, we don't know it as a dream, and in our dreams, judge something else as a dream. On awakening, we know it was a dream, and there could be another greater awakening in which we know a greater dream." [source: SEP-Zh §4.8] (A near cousin of Descartes' and al-Ghazālī's dream doubts: good comparison.)
- Animal perspectives (Zhuangzi 2:11, Hansen's translation): Gaptooth asks Wang Ni ("Kingsley") whether he knows what all creatures agree on; "How would I know that?"… "How would I know that what I call 'knowing' is not 'not-knowing'?"; people who sleep in the damp get pains, would eels? Of the three (people, eels, monkeys), "does any know the correct place to live?" A Chinese parallel to the Pyrrhonists' first mode (differences between animals). [source: SEP-Zh §4.7]
- Hansen: Zhuangzi's scepticism concerns norms of word use (*shì-fēi*, "this/not-that"), "unlike Western skepticism of beliefs"; it is "broad but weak": it denies final certainty while allowing degrees of knowledge; Sturgeon (2015) calls it "positive" scepticism; other labels: "constructive skepticism" (Wong 2022), "epistemic modesty" (Hansen 2003), "fallibilism" (Coutinho 2015). It "should provoke curiosity, not paralysis". "Therapeutic" readings: Raphals, Kjellberg and Schwitzgebel in Kjellberg and Ivanhoe (eds) 1996. [source: SEP-Zh §4.7]
- Paul Kjellberg, "Skepticism, Truth, and the Good Life: A Comparison of Zhuangzi and Sextus Empiricus", *Philosophy East and West* 44(1), 1994; Kjellberg and Raphals compare Zhuangzi to Hellenistic scepticism; Coutinho: Zhuangzi "does not refrain from making judgments" but accepts a fallibilism, accepting changes "with equanimity". [source: SEP-Zh bibliography; IEP-Zh]
- Other readings: Guo Xiang (d. 312 CE) and Graham: relativist; Hansen: "linguistic skepticism" / "perspective relativism". [source: IEP-Zh; SEP-Zh §4.8]
- **Happy-fish debate**, ch. 17 ("Autumn Floods", an Outer Chapter, so possibly not by Zhuangzi), Watson's translation (Basic Writings pp. 188–89, via Nivison 1999): see Quotations. Graham: Huizi asks 安 *ān*, "whence?", not 何 *hé*, "how?"; Hansen: Huizi "cannot consistently insist that speakers can only use *zhī* (know) when they occupy the perspective of the object they are aware of"; Zhuangzi "uses perspective relativism to justify a way of claiming to know". As a reply to scepticism: the sceptic's challenge presupposes the very knowledge it denies. [source: WP-Zhuangzi_(book); SEP-Zh §§4.7–4.8] (Set out step by step in *Hundred Schools*: summarise and cross-refer; don't repeat the full reconstruction.)

## 6. Islamic: al-Ghazālī's *Deliverance from Error* (cross-ref *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes and the Quarrel over Reason*)

- *Deliverance from Error* (*al-Munqidh min al-ḍalāl*), a spiritual and intellectual autobiography; quoted here in R. J. McCarthy's translation (Fons Vitae, 1980, page numbers as "Deliverance: n"). [source: IEP-Gh §2; SEP-Gh bibliography]
- Motive: "The children of Christians always grew up embracing Christianity, and the children of Jews grew up adhering to Judaism, and the children of Muslims always grew up following the religion of Islam" (Deliverance: 55): belief from blind imitation (*taqlīd*). Standard: "Sure and certain knowledge is that in which the thing known is made so manifest that no doubt clings to it, nor is it accompanied by the possibility of error and deception." [source: IEP-Gh §2a]
- **Sense doubt**: sight sees a shadow as motionless, yet an hour later knows it has moved "so gradually and imperceptibly that it was never completely at rest" (Deliverance: 56). [source: IEP-Gh §2b]
- **Dream doubt (against reason's first truths, not the senses)**: in dreams we believe things and only on waking know them groundless; "what assurance have you that you may not suddenly experience a state which would have the same relation to your waking state as the latter has to your dreaming…?" (Deliverance: 57). He could not refute it, because a proof needs primary truths, the very thing in doubt. [source: IEP-Gh §2b]
- Crisis lasted about two months; cured "not … by constructing a proof or putting together an argument. On the contrary, it was the effect of a light which God Most High cast into my breast. And that light is the key to most knowledge." [source: IEP-Gh §2c]
- Comparison with Descartes: both use a sense doubt and a dream doubt, but al-Ghazālī's dream doubt targets reason's primary truths, Descartes' the external world; al-Ghazālī never doubts God, so no hyperbolic doubt; he is rescued by divine light, Descartes claims to use the natural light of reason. G. H. Lewes (1857): it "bears so remarkable a resemblance to the Discours sur la Méthode of Descartes, that had any translation of it existed in the days of Descartes, everyone would have cried out against the plagiarism". No translation existed until a French one in 1842; the theory that Golius orally translated it for Descartes rests on circumstantial evidence. [source: IEP-Gh §2d]

## 7. Descartes' First Meditation (cross-ref *Descartes*; give the arguments, not the biography)

- *Meditations on First Philosophy*, Latin, Paris 1641; French translation 1647 (Duc de Luynes). Translation used throughout: Cottingham, Stoothoff and Murdoch (CSM), *The Philosophical Writings of Descartes*. [source: SEP-DesEp; WS-3; Descartes notes]
- Aim: "it will be enough if I find in each of them at least some reason for doubt" (AT 7:18, CSM 2:12). [source: SEP-DesEp §2.2]
- Dream passage, AT 7:19, CSM 2:13 (see Quotations); "there are never any sure signs by means of which being awake can be distinguished from being asleep" (AT 7:19, CSM 2:13). Sixth Meditation restatement: "every sensory experience I have ever thought I was having while awake I can also think of myself as sometimes having while asleep" (AT 7:77, CSM 2:53). [source: SEP-DesEp §3.1]
- Arithmetic survives the dream: "whether I am awake or asleep, two and three added together are five, and a square has no more than four sides" (AT 7:20, CSM 2:14). [source: SEP-DesEp §3.2]
- Deceiving God (AT 7:21, CSM 2:14): "How do I know that he has not brought it about that there is no earth, no sky, no extended thing, no shape, no size, no place, while at the same time ensuring that all these things appear to me to exist just as they do now? … may I not similarly go wrong every time I add two and three or count the sides of a square…?" And the less powerful my origin, "the more likely it is that I am so imperfect as to be deceived all the time". [source: SEP-DesEp §3.2]
- The demon: "I will suppose therefore that not God, who is supremely good and the source of truth, but rather some malicious demon of the utmost power and cunning has employed all his energies in order to deceive me." (AT 7:21, CSM 2:15). Latin *genium aliquem malignum, eundemque summe potentem et callidum*; 1647 French "un certain mauvais génie". [source: SEP-DesEp §3.2 (gives "mauvais génie"); WS-3 for Latin and French] **"Malin génie" is a common label in French commentary but is not the 1647 wording: say "the evil demon (in the French version, *mauvais génie*)".**
- One deceiver or two (Gouhier 1937 two; Newman one); Newman: Descartes' official position is that the demon is "merely one among multiple hypotheses"; the deeper doubt is that our minds may be flawed. [source: SEP-DesEp §3.2]

### Argument C (step by step): the dream argument
Formal version (Ebbs, after Stroud 1984), for any belief p about the world around me:
1. If I know that p, then I know that I am not dreaming that p.
2. I do not know that I am not dreaming that p.
3. So I do not know that p.
Premise 2 is supported by **Dream**: "Anything that can be going on or that one can experience in one's waking life can also be dreamt about." (Stroud 1984: 18). [source: SEP-Ext §1]
- Newman distinguishes the **Now Dreaming Doubt** (for all I know I am dreaming now) from the **Always Dreaming Doubt** (perhaps even waking experience is no more produced by outer things than dreams are), the latter "by no means the standard interpretation". [source: SEP-DesEp §3.1]
- **Objection**: dream arguments are self-refuting; to say dreams resemble waking we must be able to tell them apart, which the conclusion denies. **Reply** (Newman): the Sixth Meditation premise speaks only of what we *think of* as waking and dreaming, which is compatible with the conclusion. [source: SEP-DesEp §3.1]
- Note: a dream need not make the belief false ("if I am right now dreaming that I have hands I do not thereby know that I have hands, even though I do have hands"), so the dream hypothesis attacks knowledge, not just truth. [source: SEP-Sk §4.2]
- Moore's own late concession: in "Certainty" (with "Four Forms of Scepticism", among his last writings) Moore accepted, "with reservations", that he cannot know for certain that he is not dreaming. [source: SEP-Moore §6]

## 8. Brains in vats, *The Matrix*, and the closure argument

- The scenario: one's brain "resides in a vat where it is electrically stimulated to have precisely the same sensory experiences that you now have". [source: SEP-Clo §2.1]
- Origin: Wikipedia credits Gilbert Harman, *Thought* (1973), p. 5, with the scenario ("a playful brain surgeon might be giving you these experiences by stimulating your cortex"); Putnam made it famous. [source: WP-Brain_in_a_vat; WP-Evil_demon] (WP only; say "often traced to".)
- IEP: the BIV "is usually taken to be a modern version" of Descartes' evil demon; "The hypothesis was the premise behind the 1999 movie *The Matrix*" (machines keep humans in pods fed a virtual reality). [source: IEP-BIV]
- *The Matrix*: film by the Wachowskis, released 31 March 1999; William Irwin suggests it alludes to Descartes' First Meditation and evil demon; Baudrillard's *Simulacra and Simulation* was required reading for most of the cast and crew. [source: WP-The_Matrix]
- A sceptical hypothesis SH (for p and S): if SH were true, S would not know p and could not distinguish SH from a situation in which S knows p. The evil demon is "a near-universal skeptical hypothesis". [source: SEP-Sk §4]

### Argument D (step by step): the closure argument for Cartesian scepticism
Let H = "I have hands", BIV = "I am a (handless) brain in a vat".
- Version 1 (SEP-Clo §2.1): CSA1. You don't know ¬BIV. CSA2. If you don't know ¬BIV, then you don't know H. CSA3. So you don't know H. Since any external-world claim can replace H, the result is scepticism about the external world.
- Version 2 (SEP-Sk §4, in terms of justification): CP1. If I am justified in believing h, I am justified in believing ¬SH. CP2. I am not justified in believing ¬SH. So I am not justified in believing h.
- CSA2/CP1 rests on the **closure principle**: if S knows p, and p entails q, then S knows q (with refinements: the entailment must be known or obvious, q graspable, etc.; David and Warfield 2008; Hawthorne 2014). Pritchard's form: "If an agent knows P, and knows that P entails Q, then that agent knows Q." Closure helps explain how we gain knowledge by deduction ("I know tomorrow is Saturday because I know today is Friday…"). [source: SEP-Sk §4.1; IEP-Skep §2; IEP-Ctx]
- Pritchard's framing as a paradox of three claims: I. I am unable to know the denials of sceptical hypotheses; II. if I do not, I do not know very much; III. a lot of what I believe, I know. One must go. [source: IEP-Skep §1]
- Options: deny CP1/closure (Dretske, Nozick); deny CP2/CSA1 (Moore, neo-Mooreans, Putnam, disjunctivists, Williamson); relativise to context (contextualism); accept the conclusion. [source: SEP-Sk §4; IEP-Skep §1]

## 9. The answers

### 9a. Moore: common sense (cross-ref *Frege, Russell and the Birth of Analytic Philosophy*)
- "A Defence of Common Sense" (1925): lists truisms, e.g. "the earth had existed also for many years before my body was born", which he says he knows for certain; their *analysis* is left open, and may be as radical as one likes. [source: SEP-Moore §6]
- "Proof of an External World", British Academy lecture, 1939: proof by holding up his hands (see Quotations); he called it "perfectly rigorous": premises known, different from and entailing the conclusion. "How absurd it would be to suggest that I did not know it, but only believed it…" [source: SEP-Moore §6]
- **Interpretation debate**: Baldwin: the common view that Moore meant to refute scepticism, and failed, is "incorrect": his avowed aim was to prove that external things exist, not that he *knows* they do; in "A Reply to my Critics" Moore said the proof answered "there are no material things", not "Nobody knows for certain that there are any material things". [source: SEP-Moore §6] Others (Pritchard) read Moore as answering the sceptic's modus ponens with a modus tollens: since I know I have hands, I know I am not a BIV. [source: IEP-Skep §4] (The "G. E. Moore shift", William Rowe's term, is in the published *Birth of Analytic Philosophy* chapter.)
- **Objection**: it begs the question; **Wright**: knowledge closes but warrant does not **transmit** across the inference: a "cogent argument is one whereby someone could be moved to rational conviction of the truth of its conclusion" (Wright 2000, 140), and Moore's is not, because his warrant for "here is a hand" presupposes that he is not a BIV. [source: IEP-Skep §4] Pryor (2000) defends Moore ("dogmatism": perceptual justification needs no antecedent justification for ¬SH). [source: SEP-Sk §4.2]

### 9b. Wittgenstein: hinges (cross-ref *Wittgenstein*)
- *On Certainty* (1969; ed. Anscombe and von Wright; trans. Denis Paul and G. E. M. Anscombe; Blackwell), notes from his last eighteen months, targeting Moore's 1925 and 1939 papers. [source: SEP-Witt bibliography; WP-On_Certainty; IEP-Skep §4]
- §§341–343: "the questions that we raise and our doubts depend upon the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn. … If I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put." §250: "My having two hands is, in normal circumstances, as certain as anything that I could produce in evidence for it. That is why I am not in a position to take the sight of my hand as evidence for it." §521: "Moore's mistake lies in this – countering the assertion that one cannot know that, by saying 'I do know it'." [source: IEP-Skep §4] §115: "The game of doubting itself presupposes certainty" (already in the published *Wittgenstein* chapter).
- Hinge propositions are those "about which doubt cannot be entertained", but whether because they are epistemically foundational, naturally certain or logically unavailable to doubt is still debated, as is whether Wittgenstein is unequivocally anti-sceptical; Moyal-Sharrock's "third Wittgenstein". [source: SEP-Witt]
- Uses: diagnostic (Strawson 1985, Putnam 1992, Stroll 1994); stronger anti-sceptical versions: Wright's "entitlement" (we are entitled to accept that we are not in a sceptical scenario without evidence, "because unless we were we would not be justified in believing any proposition"; Wright 2004; Coliva 2015 *Extended Rationality: A Hinge Epistemology*); Michael Williams's "inferential contextualism" (*Unnatural Doubts*, 1991): the sceptic discovers only that "knowledge of the world is impossible under the conditions of philosophical reflection". Objection to positism/hinges: it makes "a doxastic necessity into an epistemic virtue". [source: IEP-Skep §4; SEP-Sk §§4.2, 6.4]

### 9c. Denying closure: Dretske and Nozick
- Fred Dretske, "Epistemic Operators", *Journal of Philosophy* 67(24), 1970, 1007–1023: the zebra case (see Quotations); epistemic operators do not "penetrate" to a statement's presuppositions. Roots of the relevant-alternatives idea in J. L. Austin (1961). [source: SEP-Sk §4.1; IEP-Skep §2; SEP-Clo §2]
- Robert Nozick, *Philosophical Explanations* (Harvard UP, 1981), ch. 3 (pp. 172–187): S knows p iff (1) p is true, (2) S believes p, (3) if p were not true S would not believe p (**sensitivity**), (4) if p were true S would believe p; "tracking", like a guided missile; method must be held fixed (the grandmother case). Belief that I have hands is sensitive (if I had no hands, it would be because of e.g. an accident and I would know); belief that I am not a BIV is not (a BIV would still believe it). So I know H but not ¬BIV: closure fails, and that is how Nozick answers the sceptic. [source: SEP-Sk §4.1; SEP-Clo §2.1]
- **Objection: the "abominable conjunction"**, a name originating in DeRose 1995: closure-deniers must allow "I know that I have hands but I don't know that I'm not a (handless) BIV". Also: closure is hugely plausible; and counterexamples to sensitivity, e.g. Vogel's ice-cold lemonade left in the sun (Vogel 1987). [source: SEP-Clo §2.4 and note 22; SEP-Sk §4.1]
- **Reply** for closure-deniers: the oddness can be explained otherwise; Nozick denied one closure principle while affirming another. [source: SEP-Clo §2.4]
- **Safety** instead of sensitivity (Sosa 1999, "How to Defeat Opposition to Moore"; also Sainsbury, Williamson, Pritchard): in all nearby worlds where S believes p, p is true. If sceptical worlds are far off, my belief that I am not a BIV is safe though insensitive, so I know it and closure holds: a **neo-Moorean** answer. Stine (1976) anticipated it, admitting it "sound[s] odd". Objection: Comesaña's Halloween-party counterexample to safety; and neo-Mooreanism seems to make it a mystery why scepticism tempts us. [source: IEP-Skep §5; SEP-Sk §4.2]

### 9d. Contextualism
- Thesis (DeRose 1995, 4): "how strong an epistemic position S must be in with respect to P for A's assertion ['S knows that P'] to be true can vary according to features of A's conversational context". It is a thesis about the word "know" (knowledge *attributions*), like "tall" or "flat". Precursors: Stine 1976; Unger. [source: IEP-Skep §3; SEP-Sk §5; SEP-Ctx]
- Keith DeRose, "Solving the Skeptical Problem", *Philosophical Review* 104(1), 1995, 1–52: a solution must explain which premise is false and why it seemed true; his "Rule of Sensitivity": asserting that S knows p tends to raise the standards so that S's belief in p must be sensitive; mentioning the BIV raises standards so high that in that context we know neither ¬BIV nor H; in ordinary contexts both are known. Closure holds within each context. [source: SEP-Ctx §3; IEP-Skep §3; SEP-Clo bibliography]
- David Lewis, "Elusive Knowledge", *Australasian Journal of Philosophy* 74(4), 1996, 549–567: "S knows proposition P iff P holds in every possibility left uneliminated by S's evidence", where "every" is restricted to possibilities not "properly ignored"; the **Rule of Attention**: "a possibility that is not ignored is ipso facto not properly ignored"; so knowledge is "elusive": "Examine it, and straightway it vanishes". [source: SEP-Ctx §3.2]
- Stewart Cohen (1986, 1987, 1988, 1999…): an internalist version based on "justified", governed by a "rule of salience". Cohen's airport case (1999: 58): Smith "knows" from his itinerary that the flight stops in Chicago; Mary and John, who must make a vital contact in Chicago, say he doesn't "really know". [source: SEP-Ctx §§3.2, 3.3]
- DeRose's **bank cases** (DeRose 1992, "Contextualism and Knowledge Attributions", *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 52(4): 913–929): on a Friday a couple consider depositing their paychecks on Saturday; low stakes: "I know it'll be open; I was there two weeks ago on a Saturday"; high stakes (a bill depends on it, and the wife notes banks change their hours): the same speaker says he doesn't know. [bibliographic data: SEP-Sk bibliography; case outline: WS-4 only, not read in the original — **give only the outline, no quotation**]
- **Objections**: (1) it changes the subject: it is about "knowledge"-sentences, not knowledge; contextualism needs an independent argument that we have some justification for ¬SH (Feldman 2001; Conee 2014; SEP-Sk calls it "parasitic"); (2) its "error theory": Schiffer (1996): implausible that we get "bamboozled by our own words"; "speakers would know what they were saying if knowledge sentences were indexical"; (3) odd consequences like "I knew then that it was a zebra but I don't know now" (Kompa: "an unpleasant consequence"). Rival: subject-sensitive invariantism / "pragmatic encroachment" (Fantl and McGrath, Hawthorne 2003, Stanley 2005). [source: SEP-Ctx §§4.1–4.3; SEP-Sk §5]

### 9e. Externalism and reliabilism
- Alvin Goldman, "What Is Justified Belief?" (1979): justification depends on the reliability of the belief-forming process; Michael Williams called it "the Reliabilist Revolution". [source: SEP-Rel §1]
- Against scepticism: if knowledge needs only reliability, not reflective access to it, then a normal perceiver can know she has hands even though she cannot prove she is not a BIV. Pritchard: nearly all the anti-sceptical views above (Dretske, Nozick, DeRose, Lewis, Sosa) are externalist; internalism says some necessary condition on knowledge is knowable by reflection alone. [source: IEP-Skep §6]
- **Objection**: the "new evil demon problem" (Cohen 1984; Pollock 1984; Feldman 1985; Foley 1985): demon victims with experiences identical to ours seem justified, though their processes are unreliable. [source: SEP-Rel §2.2] And the meta-sceptics: Stroud (*The Significance of Philosophical Scepticism*, 1984), Nagel (*The View from Nowhere*, 1986), Fumerton: an externalist answer is not the answer we wanted; internalists think externalists "are no longer engaged in the same project … of determining 'from the inside'". Stroud: to avoid scepticism "we must deny platitudes we all accept". [source: IEP-Skep §6; SEP-Sk §6.1]

### 9f. Williamson: evidence and anti-luminosity
- Timothy Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits* (OUP, 2000): our evidence is what we know (E = K), so in the good case we have more evidence than the BIV; the good and bad cases are not symmetrical: the victim cannot tell he is in the bad case, but we in the good case can know we are not. [source: SEP-Sk §4.2]
- Anti-luminosity: a condition is "luminous" if, whenever one is in it, one is in a position to know one is in it; Williamson argues "no mental states are luminous". Case (ch. 4, c. pp. 94–98): one feels cold at dawn, warms very slowly, feels hot by noon; with a margin-for-error premise derived from safety (if one knows at t that one feels cold, one still feels cold a moment later), luminosity leads to contradiction. Critics (Weatherson 2004, Berker 2008, Duncan 2018 and others) target the margin-for-error premise. [source: SEP-SelfK; SEP-Ep §3 and bibliography; WS-6 for the cold-morning case] Relevance: it undermines the sceptic's assumption that we always have infallible access to our own experiences (the "Cartesian" common evidence). McDowell makes a similar point (SEP-Disj).

### 9g. Semantic externalism: Putnam
- Hilary Putnam (1926–2016), "Brains in a Vat", ch. 1 of *Reason, Truth and History* (Cambridge UP, 1981). [source: SEP-Ext §7; WP-Brain_in_a_vat; WP-Hilary_Putnam]
- Putnam's scenario differs from the usual: not a mad scientist, but all sentient beings are and always have been brains in vats, run by machinery that "came into existence by some kind of cosmic chance or coincidence" (1981: 12). [source: SEP-Ext §7.1; IEP-BIV]
- **Causal constraint**: "One cannot refer to certain kinds of things, e.g. trees, if one has no causal interaction at all with them, or with things in terms of which they can be described" (1981: 12). So a BIV's "brain" and "vat" refer to brains-in-the-image, electrical impulses or program features. [source: SEP-Ext §7.1]
- Putnam's conclusion: "if we are brains in a vat, then 'We are brains in a vat' is false" (1981: 15); the hypothesis is "self-refuting", like "all general statements are false". [source: SEP-Ext §7.1; IEP-BIV]

### Argument E (step by step): Putnam's BIV argument (simplest reconstruction, McIntyre 1984; Williams 1984; Brueckner 2011)
1. If I am a BIV, then my word "brain" does not refer to brains. [causal constraint]
2. My word "brain" refers to brains. [disquotation]
3. So I am not a BIV.
- **Objection** (Brueckner 1986; Wright 1994): if I don't already know whether I speak English or vat-English, I am not entitled to disquotation in premise 2, so the argument shows at most that my utterance "I am a BIV" is not true. **Reply** (Christensen 1993): disquotation is valid within whichever language I actually speak; "we don't need to know which of these languages we are speaking in order to know we are speaking the one we are speaking". Then premise 1 becomes the contested one. [source: SEP-Ext §7.2]
- **Limit**: the argument leaves untouched a recently envatted brain (taken from my skull last night; its words still refer to real things): Wright 1994. So it does not defeat Cartesian scepticism in general. [source: IEP-BIV; SEP-Ext §7.1]

### 9h. Epistemological disjunctivism (brief)
- Disjunctivism denies the "highest common factor" assumption that veridical perception and an indistinguishable hallucination share the same experience/epistemic significance. [source: IEP-Disj §1]
- John McDowell, "Criteria, Defeasibility and Knowledge" (*Proceedings of the British Academy* 68, 1982): "an appearance that such-and-such is the case can be either a mere appearance or the fact that such-and-such is the case making itself perceptually manifest to someone"; the move from indistinguishability to a common factor rests on an "illegitimate view of self-knowledge". McDowell (2008): "our knowledge that [the sceptical] possibilities do not obtain is sustained by the fact that we know a great deal about our environment". [source: SEP-Disj; IEP-Disj]
- Objection: since good and bad cases are indistinguishable, we still cannot tell which we are in; reply: disjunctivism rebuts an argument that we *cannot* know, it does not prove that we do. [source: IEP-Disj]
- Duncan Pritchard: "McDowellian Neo-Mooreanism" (2008); *Epistemological Disjunctivism* (2012, cited in SEP-Disj); *Epistemic Angst* (Princeton UP, 2015) combines a Wittgensteinian hinge view with epistemological disjunctivism, treating radical scepticism as two distinct problems. [source: SEP-Disj; IEP-Disj bibliography; WS-9]

### 9i. The Pyrrhonist's own answer: living without belief
- See Argument B. Pyrrhonism is the one tradition in which scepticism is itself offered as the answer: suspension brings tranquillity (*ataraxia*). Compare Zhuangzi's equanimity (IEP-Zh) and Nāgārjuna's claim that dismantling views leads to insight (IEP-Nag §2). Contrast Hume: unliveable (SEP-Hume).

## Debates
- **Do Pyrrhonists have beliefs? Can scepticism be lived?** Frede (urbane: ordinary beliefs allowed) vs Burnyeat and Barnes (rustic: no beliefs); Barnes coined "urbane" and took "rustic" from Galen; Morison: urbane reading "still standing". [SEP-Sext; SEP-Anc]
- **Was Pyrrho a Pyrrhonist?** Bett: probably not (a dogmatic metaphysics of indeterminacy); epistemological reading (Svavarsson, Thorsrud). Indian influence: Beckwith yes, others doubtful. [SEP-Anc §4.1; SEP-Pyr]
- **Did the ancients have external-world scepticism?** Burnyeat/Williams no (it needs a creator-God framework); Fine: Sextus' "external underlying things" suggest a version. [SEP-Anc §5.4]
- **Zhuangzi: sceptic, relativist, perspectivist, or therapist?** Kjellberg, Raphals (Pyrrhonian-style therapy), Hansen ("mild", "linguistic" scepticism, "perspective relativism"), Graham and Guo Xiang (relativism), Sturgeon ("positive" scepticism), Coutinho (fallibilism). [SEP-Zh; IEP-Zh]
- **Were Nāgārjuna, Jayarāśi and Śrīharṣa sceptics?** Mills: yes, "skepticism about philosophy"; Balcerowicz: Jayarāśi not strictly a sceptic but a Cārvāka; Śrīharṣa possibly an Advaitin; Nāgārjuna read as sceptic, metaphysician, or dialectician. [WS-8; WP-Jayarāśi; SEP-Sri; IEP-Nag]
- **Was Moore trying to refute scepticism?** Baldwin no (only "there are no material things"); the common/neo-Moorean reading yes; Wright: knowledge may be fine but the argument fails to transmit warrant. [SEP-Moore; IEP-Skep]
- **Closure: keep or deny?** Dretske/Nozick deny; DeRose's "abominable conjunction"; Sosa/safety, Williamson, contextualists keep it. [SEP-Clo; IEP-Skep]
- **Contextualism**: solution or change of subject? (Feldman, Conee; Schiffer's "bamboozled" objection; pragmatic-encroachment rivals). [SEP-Ctx; SEP-Sk §5]
- **Putnam**: sound? (disquotation, recent envatment). [SEP-Ext §7; IEP-BIV]
- **Hinges**: known, merely certain, or rules? Is Wittgenstein anti-sceptical at all? [SEP-Witt]
- **Al-Ghazālī and Descartes**: influence (Golius oral-translation theory, circumstantial) or parallel? [IEP-Gh §2d]
- **Is scepticism answerable at all?** The "new sceptics" (Stroud, Nagel, Fumerton): every answer is externalist and so unsatisfying. [IEP-Skep §6]

## Surprises (commonly believed, contradicted by sources)
- Moore's "here is one hand" was, on Baldwin's reading and Moore's own later statement, aimed at "there are no material things", not at the claim that nobody *knows* there are. [SEP-Moore]
- Pyrrho himself was probably not a Pyrrhonist (Bett). [SEP-Anc]
- Putnam's argument, even if sound, does not touch the "envatted last night" scenario, and the brain in a vat predates Putnam (Harman 1973, per WP). [IEP-BIV; WP]
- Contextualism is a thesis about the word "know", not about knowledge. [SEP-Ctx]
- The ancients arguably never posed external-world scepticism; the modern version depends on a creator who could make our faculties misfit the world. [SEP-Anc §5.4]
- Agrippa's trilemma is also the "Münchhausen trilemma" (Hans Albert, 1968), which Popper attributed to Fries without mentioning Sextus. [WP]
- Al-Ghazālī's dream doubt attacks the first truths of reason, not the senses; Descartes' attacks the senses. [IEP-Gh]
