## 1. What is race? The metaphysics debate

### The modern concept and its history
- SEP-RACE: "The dominant position is that race is a modern concept", though some scholars defend pre-modern race concepts. Benjamin Isaac (2004) and Denise McCoskey (2012) argue Greeks and Romans held "proto-racist" views; Geraldine Heng (2011, 2018) argues for a race concept applied to Jews in thirteenth-century England; Adam Hochman (2019) argues the concept is "late medieval", appearing in the Spanish statutes of blood purity (*limpieza de sangre*), "first issued in 1449", applied to *conversos*. [source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/race/ §1]
- First important articulation of the race concept: François Bernier (1625–1688), "A New Division of the Earth" (1684), dividing humanity into "four or five species or races of men". [source: SEP-RACE §1]
- Monogenesis (one human origin) vs polygenesis (separate origins). Kant's "Of the Different Human Races" (1775, rev. 1777) gave "a strong and clear defense of monogenesis": four races from a common "lineal root genus" whose "seeds" were triggered by climate; interfertility as proof of one species. [source: SEP-RACE §1] (Details and quotations: cross-ref *Kant*.)
- Blumenbach (1752–1840), "On the Natural Variety of Mankind" (1775): four varieties; a fifth added 1781; in 1795 he "first coined the term 'Caucasian'", justified by the supposed superior beauty of Georgians; also a monogenist. [source: SEP-RACE §1]
- Polygenesis persisted in the "American School" (Agassiz, Nott, Gliddon); Agassiz announced it at the 1850 AAAS meeting in Charleston. Gobineau's *Essay on the Inequality of the Human Races* (1853–1855). Darwin, *Descent of Man* (1871), predicted the monogenist–polygenist dispute would "die a silent and unobserved death". Galton coined "eugenics" in 1883; *Buck v. Bell* (1927) upheld sterilisation 8–1. [source: SEP-RACE §1]
- Challenges: Franz Boas (1858–1942) showed cranium size changed with environment among children of immigrants; Ashley Montagu (1905–1999) argued genes, not gross morphology, are the units of evolution; Montagu's efforts led to a UNESCO statement denying biological foundations of race in **1950**; the American Association of Physical Anthropologists published a similar statement only in **1996**. [source: SEP-RACE §1]
- Interpretive claim (SEP-RACE, citing Bernasconi and Lott 2000 and others): "arguably it was the conflict between the Enlightenment ideals of universal freedom and equality versus the fact of the European enslavement of Africans and Indigenous Americans that fostered the development of the idea of race." Present as an interpretation. [source: SEP-RACE §1]

### Mallon's map of positions (as in SEP-RACE §2)
- **Racial naturalism** (the old biological view): races have "biobehavioral essences" — heritable, shared by all and only members, explaining behaviour and culture (Mallon 2006). SEP: "philosophers and scientists have reached the consensus against racial naturalism." [source: SEP-RACE §2]
- Mallon's three metaphysical camps: **racial skepticism**, **racial constructivism**, **racial population naturalism**; two normative camps: **eliminativism** and **conservationism**. [source: SEP-RACE §2]
- **Skepticism / eliminativism**: Anthony Appiah (1995, 1996) and Naomi Zack (1993, 2002): since the only thing "race" could refer to — discrete, essentialist biological races — does not exist, races do not exist. Zack's summary: "Essences, geography, phenotypes, genotypes, and genealogy are the only known candidates for physical scientific bases of race. Each fails. Therefore, there is no physical scientific basis for the social racial taxonomy" (Zack 2002, 88). [source: SEP-RACE §2]
- The "mismatch" argument for elimination: the concept could only fit genetically isolated, inbred groups (Appiah's example: the Amish; Zack's: Irish Protestants), not the groups we call races, so the concept is incoherent (Mallon 2006). [source: SEP-RACE §2]
- Appiah later softened to a "nominalist" position: "human folk races" exist as social identities, constituted by ascription, identification and treatment, but the folk theory is false because it presumes a biological underpinning (Appiah 2006, "How to Decide If Races Exist", *Proc. Aristotelian Society* 106). [source: SEP-RACE §2]
- Wodak (2021/2022): eliminativism need not be tied to error theory; the dispute is "whether certain uses of racial terms are wrong"; proposes a continuum rather than a binary. [source: SEP-RACE §2]
- **Constructivism**: races exist through "human culture and human decisions" (Mallon 2007). Varieties: thin constructivism (Gooding-Williams, Outlaw, Mills); interactive-kind constructivism (being ascribed a race causes shared experiences; e.g. difficulty hailing cabs, police stops); institutional constructivism (race is society-specific — Root: someone Black in the US might not be Black in Brazil). [source: SEP-RACE §2]
- **Paul C. Taylor** (2000, "Appiah's Uncompleted Argument: DuBois and the Reality of Race", *Social Theory and Practice* 26(1)): races, even if biologically unreal, are real social objects; *Race: A Philosophical Introduction* (Polity 2004; 2nd ed. 2013). [source: SEP-RACE §2 and bibliography; WP-Paul_C._Taylor]
- **Sally Haslanger** (2000, "Gender and Race: (What) Are They? (What) Do We Want Them to Be?", *Noûs* 34: 31–55; also 2010, 2019): races are *racialized groups*: (1) members are "observed or imagined" to have bodily features taken as evidence of ancestry from certain regions; (2) having (or being imagined to have) those features marks them as subordinate or privileged; (3) satisfying (1)–(2) plays a role in their systematic subordination or privilege. She is *not* a conservationist: race in this sense should ultimately disappear, though "many forms of racial identity are important, valuable, and in some cases even inevitable responses to racial hierarchy" (2019, 29–30). [source: SEP-RACE §2]
- **Chike Jeffers**: distinguishes political constructivism (Haslanger: race made real "wholly or most importantly by hierarchical relations of power") from his cultural constructivism, which allows race to survive racism: "There is also joy in blackness, a joy shaped by culturally distinctive situations" (Jeffers 2013, 422; "The Cultural Theory of Race: Yet Another Look at Du Bois's 'The Conservation of Races'", *Ethics* 123). [source: SEP-RACE §2]
- Other views: Lionel McPherson's "socioancestry" (2015); Joshua Glasgow's "basic racial realism" (races = large groups disproportionately having certain visible traits; neither biologically nor socially real); Michael Hardimon's "deflationary realism" with four concepts — racialist (rejected), minimalist ("people differ in shape and color in ways that correspond to differences in their geographical ancestry. Essentially that is all it says", 2017, 6), populationist, and "socialrace" (*Rethinking Race*, Harvard UP 2017). [source: SEP-RACE §2]
- **Racial population naturalism**: non-essentialist biological races may exist. Philip Kitcher: discreteness/essentialism would also invalidate species: "there is no…genetic feature…that separates one species of mosquito or mushroom from another" (Kitcher 2007, "Does 'Race' Have a Future?", *Philosophy and Public Affairs* 35(4)). Three versions: cladistic race (Robin Andreasen: "ancestor-descendant sequences of breeding populations that share a common origin"); socially isolated race (Kitcher 1999: anti-miscegenation laws may have produced a genetically isolated African American population, "both biologically real and socially constructed"); genetically clustered race (Sesardic 2010; Kitcher 2007). [source: SEP-RACE §2]
- Kitcher's point about clustering: "If they program their computers to distinguish four genetic clusters, then European, Asian, Amerindian, and African groups will emerge; if only two clusters are sought, then only the African and Amerindian 'races' remain" — so clustering avoids mismatch "only through the decisions of the scientist". [source: SEP-RACE §2, paraphrasing Kitcher 2007, 304]
- **Quayshawn Spencer** (2012, 2014, 2019): rejects cladistic races but defends "biological racial realism": the US Office of Management and Budget (OMB) race categories map onto five "human continental populations" identified by population genetics; he is a pluralist about race talk (OMB race is one dominant meaning among several). Works: "What 'Biological Racial Realism' Should Mean" (*Philosophical Studies* 159, 2012); "A Radical Solution to the Race Problem" (*Philosophy of Science* 81, 2014); "How to Be a Biological Racial Realist" in *What Is Race? Four Philosophical Views* (OUP 2019). [source: SEP-RACE §2 and bibliography]
- *What Is Race? Four Philosophical Views* (Oxford UP, 2019) contains chapters by Joshua Glasgow, Sally Haslanger ("Tracing the Sociopolitical Reality of Race", pp. 4–37), Chike Jeffers ("Cultural Constructionism") and Quayshawn Spencer (pp. 73–110). [source: SEP-RACE bibliography]
- Medicine: Ian Hacking (2005) defends provisional use of racial categories (e.g. recruiting African American bone-marrow donors); Dorothy Roberts (2008) warns race-based medicine can revive biological racialism. [source: SEP-RACE §2]

### The genetics evidence (hedge carefully)
- Richard Lewontin, "The Apportionment of Human Diversity" (1972, in the series *Evolutionary Biology*, ed. Dobzhansky): using 17 markers (e.g. blood-group proteins) across seven classically defined "races", he found **85.4%** of human genetic variation within populations, **8.3%** between populations within a "race", and **6.3%** between "races". He concluded racial classification is "of virtually no genetic or taxonomic significance". [source: WP-The_Apportionment_of_Human_Diversity]
- The SEP gives a related figure: "only 7% of the differences between any two individuals regarding any one specific gene can be attributed to their membership in one of the commonly recognized racial categories", while aggregating many genes correlates statistically with a few continental groupings (Sesardic 2010; Kitcher 2007). [source: SEP-RACE §2] (Use "roughly 85% within groups" and "single-digit percentages between conventional races" as the safe summary.)
- A. W. F. Edwards, "Human Genetic Diversity: Lewontin's Fallacy" (2003): with enough loci, individuals can be assigned to groups with misclassification approaching zero (single locus ≈30%). Replies: Witherspoon et al. (2007, *Genetics*) found the two claims compatible; Winther (2014) says variance partitioning and clustering are "two sides of the same mathematics coin" and neither settles the reality of groups; Roseman (2021) argues Edwards himself erred. [source: WP-The_Apportionment_of_Human_Diversity]
- Rosenberg et al. (2002) and Bamshad et al. (2003): computer analyses of hundreds of loci reveal clustering "roughly … associated with groups that historically have occupied large continental and subcontinental regions"; Rosenberg et al. (2005) said their results "should not be taken as evidence of our support of any particular concept of biological race". [source: WP-Human_genetic_variation; WP-Race_(human_categorization)]
- Human genetic diversity is greatest within and among African populations and declines with distance from Africa, consistent with the Out of Africa model. Variation is often clinal (Livingstone: "there are no races, only clines"). [source: WP-Human_genetic_variation; WP-Race_(human_categorization)]
- The US National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine report *Using Population Descriptors in Genetics and Genomics Research* (released 14 March 2023) said "researchers should not use race as a proxy for describing human genetic variation". [source: WP-Race_(human_categorization)]
- Caution for the writer: the far right uses "Lewontin's fallacy" as a slogan (Carlson and Harris, per WP). Present the scientific situation as: most variation is within groups; aggregate data show continental-scale clustering whose number of clusters depends on modelling choices; whether that amounts to "race" is a *philosophical* question about what the word must refer to — which is exactly the Appiah/Spencer/Kitcher debate. [source: WP-The_Apportionment_of_Human_Diversity; SEP-RACE §2]

## 2. Enlightenment theories of race (compact; cross-ref)
- SEP-RACE situates Kant within two strands of scholarship (following Lu-Adler 2023, 2n4): critics — Emmanuel Eze (1995), Charles Mills (2005), Robert Bernasconi (2001, 2002); defenders of Kant's broader philosophy — Louden (2000), Hill and Boxill (2001), Kleingeld (2007), Allais (2016). [source: SEP-RACE §1]
- Three positions on how Kant's racism relates to his universalism (as framed by Kleingeld, per SEP-RACE): (a) the racism renders the moral theory non-universalist; (b) the theory is universal and Kant contradicted himself; (c) Kleingeld's own thesis: Kant changed his mind before *Toward Perpetual Peace* (1795), becoming "more egalitarian with regard to race" (2007, 586), rejecting colonialism, chattel slavery and the slave trade. [source: SEP-RACE §1]
- Lu-Adler ("Kant and Slavery—Or Why He Never Became a Racial Egalitarian", *Critical Philosophy of Race* 10(2), 2022: 263–94): Kleingeld's paper had a "narrative-shaping" effect; Kant's late remarks reflect concern that slavery and colonialism impede progress and "perpetual peace", treating racial slavery as a "disinterested philosopher of history"; *Kant, Race, and Racism: Views from Somewhere* (OUP 2023): a "consistent inegalitarian position" (2023, 18). [source: SEP-RACE §1]
- Bernasconi, "Who Invented the Concept of Race? Kant's Role in the Enlightenment Construction of Race", in Bernasconi (ed.), *Race* (Blackwell 2001), 11–36. Eze, "The Color of Reason: The Idea of 'Race' in Kant's Anthropology" (1995), in K. Faull (ed.), *Anthropology and the German Enlightenment*, 200–241. [source: SEP-RACE bibliography] Eze (18 Jan 1963 – 31 Dec 2007) also edited *Race and the Enlightenment: A Reader* (Blackwell 1997). [source: WP-Emmanuel_Chukwudi_Eze]
- Kleingeld, "Kant's Second Thoughts on Race", *The Philosophical Quarterly* 57(229) (2007): 573–92. [source: SEP-RACE bibliography]
- Hume: "Of National Characters" argues that national differences are due to moral (cultural/political) causes, not nature; amid this argument against crude naturalism he inserted a footnote (SEP-RACE: "in the 1754 edition"): "I am apt to suspect the negroes and in general all the other species of men (for there are four or five different kinds) to be naturally inferior to whites. There never was a civilized nation of any other complexion than white, nor even any individual eminent either in action or speculation" (as quoted from Zack 2002, 15). He later restricted it to "negroes": "scarcely ever was a civilized nation of that complexion". Popkin (1977) and Zack (2002) read the first version as assuming polygenesis; Andrew Valls (2005) denies either version espouses polygenesis. [source: SEP-RACE §1] (See source contradictions below on dates and wording; full debate — Beattie 1770, Immerwahr 1992, Garrett 2000, Eze 2000 — in *Berkeley and Hume* notes.)
- Kant's *Observations* (1764) explicitly draws on Hume's footnote ("Mr. Hume challenges anyone…") — cross-ref *Kant* notes.
- SEP-COL: "Enlightenment thinkers such as Kant, Smith and Diderot were critical of the barbarity of colonialism and challenged the idea that Europeans had the obligation to 'civilize' the rest of the world", but "universalism proved to be a relatively weak basis for criticizing colonialism". [source: SEP-COL §3]
- Liberal imperialism (SEP-COL, after Uday Mehta 1999): universalism combined with "stadial" developmental history (hunting → herding → farming → commerce; "savagery" → "barbarism" → "civilization") justified rule over peoples deemed not yet ready for self-government. J. S. Mill, "a life-long employee of the British East India Company", defended despotic government as a means to "improvement"; *Considerations on Representative Government* (1861) gives four reasons foreign peoples govern colonies badly. Tocqueville supported colonising Algeria for French national interest, wrote "Essay on Algeria" (1841): "we are now fighting far more barbarously than the Arabs themselves" and "I believe that the right of war authorizes us to ravage the country." [source: SEP-COL §3]
- Marx's 1850s *New York Daily Tribune* articles saw British rule in India as cruel yet an agent of progress; Lenin, *Imperialism: The Highest Stage of Capitalism* (1917). [source: SEP-COL §4] (Cross-ref *Marx*.)
- Spanish natural-law debate: Vitoria argued all humans share rationality and natural rights, so papal donation did not give Spain title, yet he still justified force when Indians violated the "Law of Nations" (e.g. peaceful travel and trade). Dominicans noted Hispaniola's indigenous population fell "from 250,000 to 15,000 in two decades" (SEP-COL's figure; an estimate). [source: SEP-COL §2] (Valladolid: cross-ref *Latin American Philosophy*.)

## 4. Fanon (political argument; bio in *Existentialism*)
- SEP-COL: *Wretched* (1961) "was the most influential philosophical account of the anti-colonial struggle and the challenges of post-colonial governance." Violence "is the foundation of the colonial regime, and therefore inevitably plays a role in its overthrow"; revolutionary violence can redirect internalised violence and is "a praxis that generates political agency, group cohesion and national identity". [source: SEP-COL §5]
- Opening of "Concerning Violence", Farrington translation (1963): "decolonization is always a violent phenomenon." [source: WS-11 (Farrington scan; Lewis Gordon interview, Verso)] Philcox (2004) titles the chapter "On Violence"; his wording of the sentence was NOT confirmed.
- Drabinski's reading (SEP-FA §4): violence as "precondition to liberation"; it shocks the colonizer "into awareness of the humanity of the colonized in the moment in which they are willing to risk their lives for another future", and shocks the colonized into awareness of their potential; "Violence is simultaneously a saying of no to colonialism and a saying of yes to the possibilities of post-colonial life"; Fanon's violence "is never nihilistic or random". Three groups of the colonized: workers, the colonized intellectual (a compromised go-between), and the lumpenproletariat (the most potent revolutionary force). [source: SEP-FA §4]
- Nicholls's reading (IEP-FA): critics who see an endorsement of violent insurgency "are often reading Fanon's words too simplistically"; "His argument is not that decolonizing natives are justified in using violent means to effect their ends"; rather "violence is a fundamental element of colonization, introduced by the colonizers", and the colonized choose between absorbing it (or displacing it onto other colonized people) and "throwing it back in the face of those who initiated it". [source: IEP-FA]
- National bourgeoisie: economic exploitation can continue after independence through "the comprador bourgeoisie", a segment of the indigenous managerial class allied with foreign interests; ethnic or national identity can "mask class differences in service of elite economic interests". Example in *Wretched*: Gabon's president Léon M'ba: "Gabon is independent, but between Gabon and France nothing has changed" (quoted in *Wretched*, p. 52 of the edition WP cites). [source: SEP-COL §5; WP-The_Wretched_of_the_Earth]
- Conclusion of *Wretched*: Europe is a "spirit built on strange foundations"; "[W]e must make a new start … develop a new way of thinking, and endeavor to create a new man" (Philcox 2005: 237, 239); a humanism motif "he shares with … Césaire's 1955 text *Discourse on Colonialism*". [source: SEP-FA §4]
- Sartre's preface (1961) "appears to endorse a counter-violence" (SEP-S, per *Existentialism* notes); WP: Sartre "supported Frantz Fanon's advocacy of violence by the colonized … as necessary for their mental health and political liberation". [source: WP-The_Wretched_of_the_Earth]
- Hannah Arendt, *On Violence* (1970): her main target was Sartre's preface, which she charged with an "irresponsible glorification of violence"; she treated Fanon more gently, noting he was more doubtful about violence than his admirers; her distinction between power (acting in concert) and violence underlies the critique. Frazer and Hutchings, "On Politics and Violence: Arendt Contra Fanon" (*Contemporary Political Theory*, 2008) judge Fanon's understanding of embodied violence more insightful. [source: WS-7 (secondary summaries only — present cautiously, no direct quotation of Arendt)]
- Legacy: Glen Coulthard, *Red Skin, White Masks: Rejecting the Colonial Politics of Recognition* (2014): following Fanon, recognition by the settler state legitimises it; Hamid Dabashi, *Brown Skin, White Masks* (2011). Henry Louis Gates Jr. called Fanon a kind of "Rorschach test". [source: SEP-FA §6; SEP-COL §6]
- Black radical tradition: Eric Williams, *Capitalism and Slavery* (sugar wealth financed the industrial revolution — Williams's thesis, contested); C. L. R. James, *The Black Jacobins*; "racial capitalism" (Cedric Robinson). [source: SEP-COL §5; SEP-CPR]

## 5. Césaire, *Discourse on Colonialism*
- Aimé Césaire: born 26 June 1913, Basse-Pointe, Martinique; died 17 April 2008. Co-founded *L'Étudiant noir* (1935) with Senghor and Damas; coined "négritude"; *Cahier d'un retour au pays natal* (first in *Volontés*, 1939; book 1947 with Breton's preface); founded *Tropiques* (1941) with Suzanne Roussi (Césaire); mayor of Fort-de-France 1945–2001; deputy in the French National Assembly 1945–1993; law on departmentalisation passed 19 March 1946; resigned from the French Communist Party in the *Letter to Maurice Thorez* (1956) after Hungary; founded the Parti progressiste martiniquais (1958). [source: WP-Aimé_Césaire; SEP-NEG]
- *Discours sur le colonialisme*: first published 1950, Paris, Éditions Réclame (a small publisher associated with the PCF); revised and republished 1955 in *Présence africaine*; English translation by Joan Pinkham, Monthly Review Press, 1972; reissued 2000 with an introduction by Robin D. G. Kelley. [source: WP-Discourse_on_Colonialism]
- Central theses: colonization is not civilization ("between colonization and civilization there is an infinite distance"); colonialism "decivilizes" the colonizer; Nazism was not an exception but the return to Europe of methods used on colonized peoples; the "civilizing mission" is a lie. [source: WP-Discourse_on_Colonialism]
- "My turn to state an equation: colonization = 'thingification.'" — Pinkham translation (1972); cited at pp. 33 and 42 of the 2000 edition by one reviewer; it replaces the "dishonest equations Christianity=civilization, paganism=savagery". [source: WS-4] (French original wording NOT checked; *chosification*.)
- Boomerang thesis (Pinkham): "And then one fine day the bourgeoisie is awakened by a terrific boomerang effect: the gestapos are busy, the prisons fill up, the torturers standing around the racks invent, refine, discuss. … that it is Nazism, yes, but that before they were its victims, they were its accomplices; that they tolerated that Nazism before it was inflicted on them, that they absolved it, shut their eyes to it, legitimized it, because, until then, it had been applied only to non-European peoples". In French Césaire wrote "un formidable choc en retour" ("a formidable shock in return"); older English: "terrific reverse shock". Arendt used a "boomerang effect" in *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951); also called "Foucault's boomerang". [source: WP-Discourse_on_Colonialism]
- Hitler "applied to Europe colonialist procedures which until then had been reserved exclusively for the Arabs of Algeria, the 'coolies' of India, and the 'n——s' of Africa" (writer should consider eliding the slur). [source: WP-Discourse_on_Colonialism]
- "No one colonizes innocently … a civilization which justifies colonization—and therefore force—is already a sick civilization … which … calls for its Hitler, I mean its punishment" (p. 42 of the edition WP cites). [source: WP-Discourse_on_Colonialism]
- Not a call to return to the past: "It is not a dead society that we want to revive … It is a new society that we must create, with the help of all our brother slaves". [source: WP-Discourse_on_Colonialism]
- Targets: Renan (*La Réforme intellectuelle et morale*), Mannoni, Caillois, Tempels (the missionary of *Bantu Philosophy* — cross-ref *African Philosophy*); torture in Algeria ("electricity," "the bathtub," "the bottleneck"). [source: WP-Discourse_on_Colonialism]

## 6. Mills, *The Racial Contract* (this chapter owns it)
- Charles Wade Mills: born 3 January 1951, London; grew up in Kingston, Jamaica; died 20 September 2021, Evanston, Illinois. BSc physics, University of the West Indies (1971); MA (1976) and PhD (1985) philosophy, University of Toronto (dissertation on ideology in Marx and Engels). Taught at Oklahoma (1987–90), University of Illinois at Chicago (1990–2007), Northwestern (John Evans Professor), CUNY Graduate Center (from August 2016). Tanner Lectures 2020. Described himself as "Caribbean-American". [source: WP-Charles_W._Mills]
- *The Racial Contract*: Cornell University Press, 1997; won a Gustavus Myers Outstanding Book Award; over 50,000 copies sold (per WP); 25th-anniversary edition 2022 with foreword by Tommie Shelby. [source: WP-The_Racial_Contract]
- Opening sentence: "White supremacy is the unnamed political system that has made the modern world what it is today." (p. 1) [source: WS-5]
- Inspired by Carole Pateman's *The Sexual Contract* (1988); non-whites stand to the social contract as women do in Pateman's account. [source: IEP-SC §4b]
- Mills: "what has usually been taken…as the racist 'exception' has really been the rule; what has been taken as the 'rule'…[racial equality]…has really been the exception." Racism/global white supremacy "is *itself* a political system, a particular power structure of formal or informal rule, socioeconomic privilege and norms for the differential distribution of material wealth and opportunities, benefits and burdens, rights and duties." [source: WP-The_Racial_Contract (quoting the book)]
- Terms of the contract (Cudd's summary): moral (the dominant group values its lives more highly), political (deprive the subordinate group of power), epistemological (the dominant see themselves as intellectually superior). [source: SEP-CON]
- The contract is "not hypothetical"; "an actual contract, or series of contracts, made by real men of history" — found in papal bulls, Locke's writings on Native Americans (land not farmed is not owned), voyages of discovery, colonisation. [source: IEP-SC §4b]
- "Epistemology of ignorance": "habits of knowing that the dominant consciously pursue in order to ensure that they can retain moral self-regard" (Mills 1997). [source: SEP-CPR]
- Later work: *Blackness Visible* (1998); "'Ideal Theory' as Ideology" (*Hypatia* 20(3), 2005); "Kant's *Untermenschen*" (2005); *Black Rights/White Wrongs: The Critique of Racial Liberalism* (OUP 2017), in which he came to endorse a reformed ("black radical") liberalism. [source: WP-Charles_W._Mills; SEP-AC; SEP-IDP]
- *Contract and Domination* (Pateman and Mills, Polity, 2007): Pateman adds the "settler contract"; Mills generalises to a "domination contract" and applies it normatively to reparations for slavery. [source: SEP-CON; SEP-RACE bibliography]

### ARGUMENT 1 (step by step): the Racial Contract
1. Classical contract theory (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Kant) presents political society as founded on agreement among free and equal persons, and is conventionally treated as race-neutral. [WP-The_Racial_Contract]
2. But the actual historical "agreements" that built the modern world — papal bulls, doctrines of discovery, Locke's denial that non-farming Native Americans owned land, colonisation and slavery — first fixed *who counts as a person* able to "contract in": white Europeans as full persons, non-whites as sub-persons and objects of contracts. [IEP-SC §4b]
3. This prior "racial contract" has moral, political and epistemological terms that let one group dominate and exploit another. [SEP-CON]
4. So white supremacy is not an accidental deviation from an otherwise just liberal order but "itself a political system"; the racist "exception" has been the rule. [WP-The_Racial_Contract]
5. Idealised contract theory, which abstracts from this history, therefore functions as ideology and is sustained by an "epistemology of ignorance". [SEP-CPR; SEP-RACE §4]
6. Conclusion (descriptive): political philosophy must start from the "naturalized", non-ideal contract — from where we actually are. (Normative, later: Mills's non-ideal contract in which parties know their society's history of racial exploitation but not their own race; Mills "hypothesizes that they will rationally agree to racial reparations as a form of corrective or rectificatory justice" — Pateman and Mills 2007, chs. 3, 4, 8.) [IEP-SC §4b; SEP-RACE §4]
- **Objection A (liberal / Rawlsian — Tommie Shelby 2013):** rectificatory justice "presupposes an ideal theory that can clarify when injustices have occurred"; reparations could leave Black people in a society "that nevertheless remains racially unjust in other ways"; so "ideal theory remains indispensable". Dale Matthew (2017): Rawls's framework may not prevent racial injustice but "does provide valuable resources for rectifying" it. [source: SEP-RACE §4]
- **Objection B (normative contractarian):** if non-whites can benefit from and reciprocate cooperation, a contract excluding them is irrational by contractarianism's own standard — so contractarianism properly understood condemns the racial contract rather than being implicated in it. [source: SEP-CON]
- **Objection C (consent — Bat-Ami BarOn 1999):** "how does one consent to this racial agreement?" If today's whites never agreed, the contract language misassigns responsibility. [source: WP-The_Racial_Contract]
- **Objection D (historiography — Andrew Valls 1998 review):** the claims about Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau are "either unclear or unsubstantiated". [source: WP-The_Racial_Contract] (WP flags this sentence "citation needed" — use cautiously.)
- **Objection E (from the left — Lucius Outlaw; Pateman; Lebron):** a "contract" cannot explain the racial worldviews that preceded any agreement (Outlaw); contract theory "essentially permits domination" and cannot be salvaged (Pateman); rehabilitating Rawls loses "the epistemological advantage of a non-white perspective" (Christopher Lebron 2013). [source: WP-The_Racial_Contract; SEP-CON; SEP-RACE §4]
- **Reply:** Mills's contract is offered as a *naturalized* explanatory device — "a story about who we actually are" — which, by exposing the gap, makes it possible "to someday actually live up to the norms and values that are at the heart of the Western political traditions" (IEP-SC); the consent is tacit and often implicit (SEP-CON: "sometimes explicit, though often only implicit"), so responsibility tracks benefit and acquiescence rather than signatures; and against Pateman, Mills holds contract theory "can be salvaged by beginning with a more realistic non-ideal starting point" — Cudd notes his positive theory is then contractualist rather than contractarian, "as it begins with the premise of moral equality". [source: IEP-SC §4b; SEP-CON]
- Gender (bridge to *Feminist Philosophy*): Kathryn Sophia Belle (writing as Kathryn T. Gines) argued the racial and sexual contracts had been treated as "parallel non-intersecting universes" and challenged Mills's claim that "race generally trumps gender". [source: WP-Charles_W._Mills]

## 7. Postcolonial theory and decolonial thought
- SEP-COL distinction: postcolonial theory (humanities, poststructuralist influence; "hybridity, diaspora, representation, narrative, and knowledge/power") vs theories of decolonisation ("revolution, economic inequality, violence, and political identity"). [source: SEP-COL §5]
- **Edward Said** (1 Nov 1935, Jerusalem – 24 Sep 2003), Columbia professor of literature. *Orientalism* (Pantheon Books, 1978). [source: WP-Edward_Said; WP-Orientalism_(book)] Applied Foucault's discourse analysis to Western knowledge of the Middle East: "knowing the Orient" was part of dominating it; discourse "is itself a form of power". [source: SEP-COL §5] Three senses (pp. 2–3): an academic field; "a style of thought based upon an ontological and epistemological distinction made between 'the Orient' and (most of the time) 'the Occident'"; and a political instrument of domination. [source: WP-Orientalism_(book)] Definition: Orientalism as "a Western style for dominating, restructuring, and having authority over the Orient" (Introduction; page given variously as 2 or 3). [source: WS-13] Afterword 1995; Preface 2003. Critics: Bernard Lewis ("The Question of Orientalism", 1993; called the thesis "anti-Western"), Robert Irwin (*For Lust of Knowing*, 2006), Sadiq Jalal al-Azm (1981: Said's account itself essentialises "the West"), Aijaz Ahmad. [source: WP-Orientalism_(book)]
- **Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak** (born 24 Feb 1942; Columbia). "Can the Subaltern Speak?" in Cary Nelson and Lawrence Grossberg (eds.), *Marxism and the Interpretation of Culture* (Urbana: University of Illinois Press, 1988), 271–313. Term "subaltern" from Gramsci. [source: WP-Subaltern_(postcolonialism); WP-Gayatri_Chakravorty_Spivak] Argument (SEP-COL): well-meaning scholars who want the subaltern to "speak for themselves" assume transparent speech, but "experience itself is constituted through representation"; "representation has not withered away", so interpretation is unavoidable. Case: British abolition of sati (1829 regulation — see *Age of Empire* notes) justified as "White men are saving brown women from brown men", against the Hindu claim "the women wanted to die"; the subaltern woman is silenced in both; essay ends with Bhubaneswari Bhaduri's 1926 suicide and the statement "The subaltern cannot speak." [source: SEP-COL §5; WS-14 (page numbers vary by edition)] She also coined "strategic essentialism" (later partly disavowed). [source: WP-Gayatri_Chakravorty_Spivak]
- Critique: Aijaz Ahmad (1997): despite Marxist claims, Spivak shows "contempt for materialism, rationalism, and progress"; Vivek Chibber (2013) defends universal categories (capitalism, class, rationality) against Dipesh Chakrabarty's *Provincializing Europe*. [source: SEP-COL §5]
- **Homi K. Bhabha** (born 1 Nov 1949, Bombay; Harvard): *Nation and Narration* (Routledge 1990); *The Location of Culture* (Routledge 1994). Concepts: hybridity, ambivalence, the Third Space, mimicry — the colonizer wants a reformed Other "almost the same, but not quite", and mimicry's "double vision" disrupts colonial authority. Criticised for obscurity (second prize in *Philosophy and Literature*'s 1998 "Bad Writing Competition"; Terry Eagleton's 1994 review). [source: WP-Homi_K._Bhabha]
- Self-critique of the field: "post-colonial" may smuggle in a progressive view of history (McClintock 1992) and implausibly unify very different colonial experiences. [source: SEP-COL §5]
- **Aníbal Quijano** (Peruvian sociologist; died 31 May 2018; WP gives birth as 17 Nov 1930 in the text but 1928 in the infobox — avoid the birth year): "coloniality of power" — colonial racial hierarchy (Spaniards at the top, ranked by phenotype and presumed culture) outlived formal colonialism and shapes postcolonial social and economic structures; race as the "naturalization of colonial relations between Europeans and non-Europeans"; Europe's hegemony concentrated "the control of subjectivity, culture, and especially knowledge". Key essay: "Coloniality of Power, Eurocentrism, and Latin America", *Nepantla: Views from South* 1(3) (2000): 533–580; Spanish "Colonialidad y modernidad/racionalidad" (1991/1992). Earlier he worked on dependency theory at ECLA/CEPAL (1966–71). [source: WP-Coloniality_of_power; WP-Aníbal_Quijano; WP-Decoloniality] María Lugones extended it to the "coloniality of gender". [source: WP-Coloniality_of_power]
- **Walter Mignolo** (born 1 May 1941, Argentina; Duke since 1993): *The Darker Side of the Renaissance* (1995); *Local Histories/Global Designs: Coloniality, Subaltern Knowledges, and Border Thinking* (Princeton UP, 2000); "Delinking" (2007); a founder of the modernity/coloniality group. Decoloniality claims to move "away and beyond the post-colonial", which it calls "a project of scholarly transformation within the academy" — a characterisation some postcolonial scholars dispute. [source: WP-Walter_Mignolo; WP-Decoloniality]
- Critic of decolonial talk: Frederick Cooper: "If colonialism is everywhere, it is nowhere. But it is somewhere." [source: WP-Decoloniality]
- **Wiredu's "conceptual decolonisation"** — cross-ref *African Philosophy* (definition, 1980 origin, argument and objection already verified there).
- Settler colonialism and Indigenous critique (SEP-COL §6): Patrick Wolfe (1999): settler colonialism is "a structure rather than an event"; Tuck and Yang, "Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor" (2012), call reconciliation "moves to innocence"; Dale Turner, *This Is Not a Peace Pipe* (2006); Coulthard (2014); Leanne Betasamosake Simpson; UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007). ## 8. Reparations
- Boxill and Corlett (SEP-BR) define the topic as reparations owed to US Black people for slavery "and its aftermath"; reparations are owed only for *wrongful* harm, unlike compensation (Nozick's definition: something fully compensates if it makes one "no worse off than he would otherwise have been", ASU 57) and unlike restitution (return of a thing). [source: SEP-BR §3]
- History: Lincoln at one point appeared ready to pay slaveholders $400,000,000 (compensated emancipation); Thaddeus Stevens proposed land confiscated from rebels for the freedpeople; James Forman's "Black Manifesto" (Detroit, 1969) demanded $500,000,000 from churches and synagogues; Boris Bittker, *The Case for Black Reparations* (1973); Civil Liberties Act 1988 (reparations to interned Japanese Americans); John Conyers introduced a study-commission bill (H.R. 40) in each Congress from 1989; Randall Robinson, *The Debt* (2000); California AB 3121 (Sept 2020) created a Task Force. [source: SEP-BR §1; WP-Reparations_for_slavery (Conyers "every year from 1989 until his resignation in 2017")]
- Locke as source: reparation is "the satisfaction due to any private Man, for the damage he has received", which the magistrate "cannot … remit" (Second Treatise §11); the conqueror's right to reparations is limited by the subsistence rights of the wrongdoers' innocent wives and children (§§180, 183). [source: SEP-BR §§2, 8]
- **Bernard R. Boxill**, "The Morality of Reparation", *Social Theory and Practice* 2(1) (1972): 113–123 — the classic philosophical defence, inspired by the Black Manifesto debate; also *Blacks and Social Justice* (1984; rev. 1992). [source: SEP-BR §1 and bibliography; WP-Bernard_Boxill]
- **Janna Thompson** (12 Nov 1942 – 24 Jun 2022), *Taking Responsibility for the Past: Reparation and Historical Injustice* (Polity, 2002); *Should Current Generations Make Reparation for Slavery?* (Polity, 2018). [source: WP-Janna_Thompson; SEP-BR bibliography]
- George Sher, "Ancient Wrongs and Modern Rights", *Philosophy and Public Affairs* 10(1) (1981): 3–17; "Transgenerational Compensation", *PPA* 33 (2005). Jeremy Waldron, "Superseding Historic Injustice", *Ethics* 103(1) (1992): 4–28. [source: SEP-BR bibliography; SEP-NI bibliography]
- **Ta-Nehisi Coates**, "The Case for Reparations", *The Atlantic*, June 2014 cover story; centred on housing discrimination, redlining and contract-buying in Chicago (through Clyde Ross); endorsed H.R. 40 as a starting point; Coates testified on reparations before a House panel five years later (2019). [source: WP-The_Case_for_Reparations; WS-12] Kevin D. Williamson's reply "The Case Against Reparations": "The people to whom reparations are owed are long dead." [source: WP-Reparations_for_slavery]
- Nozick's rectification principle: cross-ref *Justice and Equality* notes (ASU 230–231).
- Mills's non-ideal contract → reparations (Pateman and Mills 2007), see §6. [source: SEP-RACE §4]

### ARGUMENT 2 (step by step): the harm argument, the non-identity objection, and the reply
1. Slavery and post-emancipation injustice wrongfully harmed the enslaved and their descendants.
2. Wrongdoers (or responsible institutions) owe reparation for wrongful harms they cause.
3. **Harm argument:** an "unbroken chain of harms" runs from slavery to present-day Black Americans; so they are owed reparations. [SEP-BR §6]
- **Objection 1 (causation):** present disadvantages may stem from later economic changes, not slavery (Sher 1981: not the "automatic effects of slavery"). **Objection 2:** the slaveholders are dead. **Objection 3 (non-identity — Morris 1984, Levin 1980):** compensation means making someone no worse off than they would have been; but without slavery present-day Black Americans *would not exist*; so they cannot have been made worse off by it, and the argument "is incoherent". [SEP-BR §7] SEP-NI: if reparations "requires victims rather than apparent beneficiaries … we seem forced to conclude that reparations to later-conceived progeny are not owed". [SEP-NI]
- **Reply 1 (Fullinwider 2004; Bittker): shift the wrong** to post-1865 governments' failure to protect the freedpeople's rights. Boxill/Corlett add a second post-emancipation wrong — failing to pay reparations owed. [SEP-BR §7]
- **Reply 2 (the Tom, Beulah and Eulah case — Boxill/Corlett):** freed slaves Tom and Beulah are wronged by the government (rights denied; compensation withheld) both before and *after* their daughter Eulah is conceived. The wrongs after her conception harm *her* (poverty, lack of schooling, inherited disabilities of slavery). Her claim is for harms she herself suffered, from wrongs that occurred after she existed — so the non-identity objection does not apply. [SEP-BR §7] This parallels Sher's "subsequent-wrong" account (Sher 2005). [SEP-IJ]
- **Reply 3 (the inheritance argument — Boxill 1972; Kershnar 1999; Corlett 2010):** the freed people had unpaid rights to reparation; rights pass to heirs; so present descendants inherit them. This "avoids the objection that the present black population would not exist". Objections: it relies on counterfactuals about whether the money would have been kept; it seems to make innocents pay. Reply via Locke: the claims attach to the *estates* of those who "assisted, concurred, or consented" to the wrong (tacit consent of nearly all white citizens, by Boxill/Corlett's Lockean argument — itself contestable, since abolitionists dissented). Limit: inheritance yields only where honouring it would cause destitution or dangerous inequality. [SEP-BR §8]
- **Objection (supersession — Waldron 1992):** changed circumstances can "supersede" historic injustice (the water-hole example). **Reply (Boxill/Corlett):** at most this defeats *restitution* (returning the land), not *reparation* for the injury done. [SEP-BR §9]
- Other responses to non-identity in this context: threshold-based and identity-independent conceptions of harm (Waligore; Meyer), De-Shalit's transgenerational communities (SEP-IJ); Kumar's Scanlonian "types of persons" approach (SEP-NI). [source: SEP-IJ; SEP-NI]
- Practical proposal (SEP-BR §10, labelled as the authors' own): a Committee on Black Reparations elected by Black Americans and a 3% income tax — attribute clearly to Boxill and Corlett; do not present as a mainstream policy.

## Debates
- **Does race exist?** Skeptics/eliminativists (Appiah, Zack) vs social constructionists (Taylor, Haslanger — political; Jeffers — cultural; Mills — thin) vs population naturalists (Kitcher, Andreasen, Spencer) vs deflationary/basic realists (Hardimon, Glasgow) vs pluralists (McPherson). [source: SEP-RACE §2]
- **What the genetics shows:** Lewontin (1972) vs Edwards (2003); reconciliations (Witherspoon et al. 2007; Winther 2014; Roseman 2021). [source: WP-The_Apportionment_of_Human_Diversity]
- **Is race modern?** Dominant view yes; Isaac/McCoskey (ancient proto-racism), Heng (medieval England), Hochman (late-medieval Iberia). [source: SEP-RACE §1]
- **Kant's racism and his universalism:** Eze, Mills, Bernasconi vs Louden, Hill & Boxill, Kleingeld, Allais; Lu-Adler vs Kleingeld on the 1790s. [source: SEP-RACE §1] **Hume's footnote:** polygenist (Popkin, Zack) or not (Valls). [source: SEP-RACE §1]
- **Fanon and violence:** endorsement (Sartre's preface; read so by Arendt) vs violence as precondition/diagnosis (Drabinski; Nicholls); Bhabha against Sartre's framing. [source: SEP-FA §4; IEP-FA; WS-7; *Existentialism* notes]
- **Can contract theory be rescued?** Mills (yes, non-ideal contract) vs Pateman (contract inherently permits domination) vs Shelby (ideal theory indispensable) vs Lebron. [source: SEP-CON; SEP-RACE §4]
- **Postcolonial vs Marxist/universalist:** Said and Spivak vs Ahmad, Chibber; Chakrabarty vs Chibber; decoloniality (Quijano, Mignolo) vs postcolonialism; Cooper's caution. [source: SEP-COL §5; WP-Decoloniality]
- **Reparations:** harm vs inheritance arguments; non-identity objection; supersession (Waldron) vs Boxill/Corlett; Nozickian rectification; opponents (Williamson, Biggar). [source: SEP-BR; SEP-NI; WP-Reparations_for_slavery]
- **The canon:** Park, Garfield & Van Norden vs critics who define philosophy as the Greek-derived tradition. [source: WS-8; WP-Bryan_W._Van_Norden]

## Source contradictions found (record; do not propagate)
- Hume footnote date: SEP-RACE says inserted "in the 1754 edition" and softened "in the 1776 edition"; the *Berkeley and Hume* notes (WP, Immerwahr, Garrett) say added 1753(–54) and revised in the posthumous **1777** edition. Safest: "added in the early 1750s; revised in the edition published just after his death (1777)". SEP-RACE's quotation reads "all the other species of men … inferior to whites"; WP/Immerwahr's reads "all other species … to the whites". Use one source's wording consistently and name it.
- *Contract and Domination*: SEP-CON gives "Stanford: Polity Press", SEP-RACE "Malden, MA: Polity", SEP-IDP "Cambridge: Polity", all 2007; WP lists a 2013 Polity printing. Use "Polity, 2007".
- Quijano's birth year: WP lead 1930, WP infobox 1928.
- Farrington's *Wretched*: WP lists Présence Africaine 1963 and Grove 1965; *Existentialism* notes give 1963. Say "first English translation 1963".

- Lewontin percentages: WP gives 85.4/8.3/6.3; SEP-RACE gives a "7%" between-race figure (Sesardic/Kitcher framing). Both are approximate summaries; don't mix them in one sentence.
