## 1. Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (1971): the principles and their wording
- Publication: *A Theory of Justice*, Belknap/Harvard, 1971; revised edition 1999. Later: *Political Liberalism* (Columbia UP, 1993; expanded 2005); *The Law of Peoples* (Harvard UP, 1999; first a 1993 article/"sketch" in *Critical Inquiry*); *Justice as Fairness: A Restatement* (Belknap, 2001, edited by Erin Kelly from lecture material). [source: SEP-RA §1; WP-TJ; WP Political_Liberalism; WP The_Law_of_Peoples; WP Justice_as_Fairness:_A_Restatement]
- Rawls: born Baltimore 21 Feb 1921, died 24 Nov 2002. [source: WP John_Rawls] (Further biography is in the Analytic Philosophy chapter.)
- The phrase "justice as fairness" predates the book: Rawls's paper "Justice as Fairness", *Journal of Philosophy* 54 (24 Oct 1957) and a fuller version in *Philosophical Review* 67 (April 1958): 164–194. [source: WP John_Rawls bibliography]
- "Justice as fairness": Rawls's aim is an agreement situation fair among the parties; the fairness of the original agreement situation "transfers" to the principles agreed; the procedure is to "incorporate pure procedural justice at the highest level" (CP 310; cf. TJ 120/104 rev.). [source: SEP-OP §3]
- Rawls's aim against utilitarianism: a "workable and systematic moral conception to oppose" it (TJ xvii); classical utilitarianism "adopt[s] for society as a whole the principle of choice for one man" and so fails to "take seriously the distinction between persons" (TJ 24). [source: IEP-RA]
- **Final 1971 statement (TJ §46, 1971 p. 302; rev. 1999 p. 266), first principle**: "Each person is to have an equal right to the most extensive total system of equal basic liberties compatible with a similar system of liberty for all." [source: WP-TJ, citing rev. ed. p. 266; SEP-OP §6.1 quotes the same without "is to" — "Each person has an equal right…" (TJ 266); minor variant — check the book before quoting]
- **Final 1971 statement, second principle** (same place): "Social and economic inequalities are to be arranged so that they are both: (a) to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged, consistent with the just savings principle, and (b) attached to offices and positions open to all under conditions of fair equality of opportunity." [source: WP-TJ, citing rev. ed. p. 266]
- Note the order: in TJ the difference principle is (a) and fair equality of opportunity (b); but **lexically FEO comes before the difference principle**, so the priority order is 1, 2b, 2a. "A principle does not come into play until those previous to it are either fully met or do not apply" (TJ rev. 38). [source: WP-TJ]. In *Political Liberalism*/*Justice as Fairness* the parts are reordered so that FEO is (a) and the difference principle (b). [source: SEP-RA §4.3; SEP-DJ §4]
- **Revised wording (Political Liberalism 1993 / JF 2001)**: First principle: "Each person has an equal claim to a fully adequate scheme of equal basic rights and liberties, which scheme is compatible with the same scheme for all; and in this scheme the equal political liberties, and only those liberties, are to be guaranteed their fair value." Second: "Social and economic inequalities are to satisfy two conditions: (a) They are to be attached to positions and offices open to all under conditions of fair equality of opportunity; and (b), they are to be to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged members of society." (Rawls 1993, pp. 5–6) [source: SEP-DJ §4]. JF 42–43 version: "Each person has the same indefeasible claim to a fully adequate scheme of equal basic liberties, which scheme is compatible with the same scheme of liberties for all" [source: SEP-RA §4.3]
- The change from "most extensive" to "fully adequate scheme" was made in 1982 (PL 291). [source: SEP-OP §6.1]
- In *Political Liberalism* Rawls adds, "almost in passing", a principle of basic needs prior to the first principle: citizens' basic needs met at least so they can understand and fruitfully exercise their rights and liberties (PL 7; JF 79n). [source: SEP-OP §6.1]
- Basic liberties (list, TJ 53f., PL 291): liberty of conscience and freedom of association; freedom of thought and of speech and expression; integrity and freedom of the person and the right to hold personal property; equal political rights and their fair value; rule-of-law rights. Ownership of means of production and laissez-faire freedom of contract are **not** basic liberties (TJ 54 rev.). [source: SEP-OP §6.1; WP-TJ quoting TJ rev. 54]
- **Priority rules** (TJ 302/266 rev.): (a) basic liberties can only be restricted for the sake of liberty itself; (b) FEO is lexically prior to the difference principle; (c) the second principle is prior to efficiency and to maximizing the sum of advantages. [source: SEP-OP §6.1]
- Consequence: no sacrifice of basic liberties for greater equality of opportunity or material goods, "even for the worst off". Example (SEP-RA): a draft exemption for college students to boost productivity is disallowed; if there is a draft, all who are able must be equally subject to it. [source: SEP-DJ §4; SEP-RA §4.3]
- **General conception**: "All social goods—liberty and opportunity, income and wealth, and the bases of self-respect, are to be distributed equally unless an unequal distribution of any or all of these goods is to the advantage of the least favored" (TJ 1971, 302); applies in non-ideal conditions. [source: SEP-OP §6.1]
- **Just savings principle**: each generation should save for future generations at a rate they could rationally expect past generations to have saved for them (TJ §44; JF 159–160). [source: SEP-OP §6.1] (Intergenerational justice belongs to the Animals and Environment chapter.)
- **Fair equality of opportunity**: "In all parts of society there are to be roughly the same prospects of culture and achievement for those similarly motivated and endowed" (JF 44). SEP-RA's illustration: if talent is evenly spread across classes, roughly a quarter of people in each occupation should come from each income quartile. [source: SEP-RA §4.3]
- **Fair value of political liberties**: political liberties must be substantively, not just formally, equal (implications for election finance). [source: SEP-RA §4.3]
- **Primary goods**: rights and liberties, opportunities and powers, income and wealth, the social bases of self-respect; "things which a rational man wants whatever else he wants" (TJ 1971, 92). [source: WP-TJ; SEP-EG §3.2]

## 2. The difference principle, the original position, and maximin
- **Difference principle table** (SEP-RA §4.3; lifetime income for least / middle / most advantaged): A 10,000 / 10,000 / 10,000; B 12,000 / 30,000 / 80,000; C 30,000 / 90,000 / 150,000; D 20,000 / 100,000 / 500,000. The difference principle selects **C**: its inequalities benefit everyone relative to A and B, but it does not allow D, where the rich gain at the expense of the poor. [source: SEP-RA §4.3] (Good teaching example.)
- The difference principle embodies "equality-based reciprocity"; it rests partly on the "negative thesis" that natural endowments are undeserved; citizens "regard the distribution of natural endowments as a common asset"; "In justice as fairness … [citizens] agree to share one another's fate" (TJ 102). [source: SEP-RA §4.3]
- Rawls (TJ 1971, 101–102): "The naturally advantaged are [to gain] only to cover the costs of training and education and for using their endowments in ways that help the less fortunate as well." [source: SEP-NO §4.1]
- Technical refinements: the principle concerns representative members of social classes, not particular people; "close-knitness" and "chain-connection" assumptions let Rawls focus on the least-advantaged; a lexical ("leximin") version for those who reject the assumptions (TJ 72). [source: IEP-RA]
- Institutions: justice as fairness favours **property-owning democracy** or **liberal (democratic) socialism**, not welfare-state capitalism, laissez-faire or a command economy. "The least advantaged are not, if all goes well, the unfortunate and unlucky—objects of our charity and compassion, much less our pity—but those to whom reciprocity is owed as a matter of basic justice" (JF 139). [source: SEP-RA §4.9]
- **Veil of ignorance** quotation (TJ §3): "[N]o one knows his place in society, his class position or social status, nor does anyone know his fortune in the distribution of natural assets and abilities, his intelligence, strength, and the like. I shall even assume that the parties do not know their conceptions of the good or their special psychological propensities. The principles of justice are chosen behind a veil of ignorance." [source: WP-TJ (block quotation; page not given there — TJ 1971 p. 12 is the usual citation, not verified)]
- Precursors: the device had been described earlier by William Vickrey (1945) and John Harsanyi (1953, 1955), who used it to derive **utilitarianism**; Rawls coined the terms "original position" and "veil of ignorance". [source: WP-OP]
- **Maximin**: choose the alternative whose worst outcome is best; Rawls: choose "as if your enemy were to assign your social position" (SEP-OP paraphrase). Contrast "maximax" and Bayesian expected utility with the "principle of insufficient reason" (equal probability to each outcome). [source: SEP-OP §6.2]
- Why maximin here: the choice is a unique, irrevocable "superchoice" of the basic structure; not renegotiable or repeatable. [source: SEP-OP §6.2]
- **Three conditions** (TJ 154–55 / 134 rev.): (1) no, or a very insecure, basis for probability estimates; (2) the maximin option is acceptable, so one "cares relatively little" for gains above it; (3) the other options have outcomes "we could not accept and live with". Later (JF 99) the first plays "a minor role"; conditions 2 and 3 are crucial. [source: SEP-OP §6.2]
- **Harsanyi's objection**: under complete uncertainty use insufficient reason, assume equal probability of any position, and the parties choose **average utility** (Harsanyi, "Can the Maximin Principle Serve as a Basis for Morality? A Critique of John Rawls's Theory", *American Political Science Review* 69(2), 1975: 594–606). Kenneth Arrow also criticised maximin ("Some Ordinalist-Utilitarian Notes on Rawls's Theory of Justice", *Journal of Philosophy*, May 1973). [source: SEP-OP §6.2; WP-TJ]
- Bayesians' counterexample (Rawls's own, TJ 136 rev.): lotteries (0, n) vs (1/n, 1) — maximin picks (1/n, 1), "clearly irrational" for almost any n. Freeman's reply: maximin is irrational under most circumstances but not when you need 1/n to survive, or when outcomes cannot be recouped. [source: SEP-OP §6.2]
- **Risk-aversion objection and reply**: critics say the parties must be "risk-averse"; Rawls denies they have any psychological risk disposition (they don't know their attitudes to risk); it is rational to choose *as if* risk-averse when fundamental interests are at stake — like buying liability, health and home insurance; even a gambler should not gamble with his basic liberties, since in a repressive society he might never get to gamble at all. [source: SEP-OP §6.2]
- Nozick also called maximin "risk aversion taken to its extreme". [source: WP-OP] Rawls later stressed maximin is only "a useful heuristic rule of thumb" (JF 97) and the OP a "device of representation" (PL). [source: WP-TJ]
- **Strains of commitment** (TJ §29): parties must choose principles they can actually live with once the veil lifts; this favours justice as fairness over utilitarianism (TJ 176f./153f.). [source: SEP-OP §6.3]
- JF (2001) adds the comparison of the difference principle with "mixed conceptions" (e.g. restricted utility: average utility with a social minimum), arguing from reciprocity, stability and self-respect. [source: SEP-OP §6 (supplement headings)]
- Sen's critique of primary goods (Tanner Lecture 1979, 215–216): primary goods "seem to take little note of the diversity of human beings" — e.g. disability. [source: SEP-CA §6] Daniels on health care as a primary good (1979; *Just Health Care* 1985). [source: WP-TJ]
- G. A. Cohen's **incentives critique** ("Incentives, Inequality, and Community", Tanner Lectures 1992; later *Rescuing Justice and Equality*, 2008 — book title from SEP-EG citation "G. Cohen 2008"): inequalities paid to compensate unusually burdensome work are fine, but extra pay merely to induce the talented to work is not required by the difference principle, since in a well-ordered society citizens with an "egalitarian ethos" would do the work anyway; Cohen urges a stricter reading of the principle. [source: SEP-DJ §4]

## 3. Political Liberalism (1993) and the Law of Peoples (1999), briefly
- PL's problems: **legitimacy** (how can coercion under one law be legitimate given divergent worldviews?) and **stability**. Liberal principle of legitimacy: "Our exercise of political power is fully proper only when it is exercised in accordance with a constitution the essentials of which all citizens as free and equal may reasonably be expected to endorse in the light of principles and ideals acceptable to their common human reason" (PL 137). [source: SEP-RA §3.1]
- **Reasonable pluralism**: reasonable Catholicism, reasonable Islam, reasonable atheism etc. that accept toleration; **overlapping consensus**: citizens endorse a freestanding political conception ("module") each for reasons internal to their own comprehensive doctrine; SEP-RA's example is Vatican II's declaration on religious freedom. [source: SEP-RA §§3.3, 3.5]
- Law of Peoples: no stable world state (Rawls cites Kant: global despotism or civil strife); actors are **peoples**, not individuals; eight principles (LP 37), the last being a **duty to assist** "burdened societies". Liberal and "decent hierarchical" peoples; Rawls's imaginary decent Muslim society "Kazanistan". [source: SEP-RA §§5, 5.1, 5.2]
- The duty of assistance "has a target and a cutoff point": once all societies are well-ordered there is no further obligation; unlike the difference principle it does not apply continuously to primary goods and may consist more of know-how than money. Rawls rejected a global difference principle. [source: SEP-IJ]
- Rawls on the causes of wealth (LP 108): "I believe that the causes of the wealth of a people and the forms it takes lie in their political culture and in the religious, philosophical, and moral traditions that support the basic structure of their political and social institutions, as well as in the industriousness and cooperative talents of its members…" [source: SEP-GJ §2.1]

## 4. Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974)
- Nozick: born New York City 16 Nov 1938, died 23 Jan 2002. [source: WP Robert_Nozick] ASU: Basic Books, 1974; 1975 US National Book Award (Philosophy and Religion); named one of the "100 most influential books since the war" by the *TLS* (1995). [source: WP-ASU]
- Part II, ch. 7 "Distributive Justice" is the most discussed chapter; ch. 8 "Equality, Envy, Exploitation, Etc." includes a critique of Marxism (253–262). Aim of Part II: "no more extensive state can be justified" (xi). [source: SEP-NO §4]
- "Distributive justice" is not a neutral term: there is "no more a distributing or distribution of shares than there is a distributing of mates in a society in which persons choose whom they shall marry" (1974, 150); IEP-NO: wealth is not "manna from heaven". [source: IEP-DJ; IEP-NO]
- **Entitlement theory** (ASU 151): "If the world were wholly just, the following inductive definition would exhaustively cover the subject of justice in holdings. 1. A person who acquires a holding in accordance with the principle of justice in acquisition is entitled to that holding. 2. A person who acquires a holding in accordance with the principle of justice in transfer, from someone else entitled to the holding, is entitled to the holding. 3. No one is entitled to a holding except by (repeated) applications of 1 and 2." Past injustice "raises the third major topic under justice in holdings: the rectification of injustice in holdings" (152). "[T]he complete principle of distributive justice would say simply that a distribution is just if everyone is entitled to the holdings they possess under the distribution" (151). [source: IEP-DJ]
- Nozick never formulates the three principles: each is a "complicated truth" (150). [source: SEP-NO §4.1]
- Classification: **end-state** (current time-slice: only the matrix of who has what matters), **patterned** ("specifies that a distribution is to vary along with some natural dimension…", e.g. need, merit), vs **historical entitlement** (historical and unpatterned). "Almost every suggested principle of distributive justice is patterned" (156). [source: IEP-DJ; IEP-NO; SEP-NO §4.2]
- SEP-NO's slavery matrix against end-state views: D1 = (12, 10, 9), D2 = (4, 22, 13), D3 = (20, 8, 8); utilitarianism picks D2, equality and the difference principle D1; but if D2 is A enslaved to B with C as overseer and D3 is full emancipation, the matrix alone obviously can't settle justice. (Mack's illustration of Nozick's point, not Nozick's own numbers.) [source: SEP-NO §4.2]
- Nozick on Rawls: treating the talented's natural assets as a common asset treats them as means; only avoidable by holding their assets and choices "to develop their own natural assets" morally arbitrary (214). [source: SEP-NO §4.1]
- **Lockean proviso** (ASU 174–182): an acquisition is unjust if it leaves others without "enough, and as good" — Nozick reads this as: an acquisition is illegitimate only if it leaves others **worse off** (in welfare) than they would otherwise be; compensation can satisfy it; the baseline is low (roughly, how well off one would be had everything stayed in common); Nozick thinks free markets readily satisfy it. [source: SEP-NO §4.3; SEP-LIB §3]
- Nozick's jokes against Locke's labour-mixing: "if I own a can of tomato juice and spill it in the sea … do I thereby come to own the sea, or have I foolishly dissipated my tomato juice?"; and the astronaut who clears a place on Mars — does he own the planet, the universe, or a plot? (174–175) [source: IEP-DJ]
- **Rectification**: holdings are "shot through" with past injustice (murder, plunder, enslavement). Nozick speculated injustices might have something like a half-life; rectification might take the form of rough payments to classes likely worse off due to historical injustice; some end-state principle might guide it (though "it may well be implausible" to choose the difference principle, 231), and might in the short run require "a state more extensive than the minimal state" (230). [source: SEP-NO §4.4]
- **Taxation**: "Taxation of earnings from labor is on a par with forced labor" (169) [verified in Analytic chapter notes]; "Redistribution and Property Rights" (172): patterned principles involve "appropriating the actions of other persons. Seizing the results of someone's labor is equivalent to seizing hours from him … This process … makes [those who carry it out] a part-owner of you; it gives them a property right in you." [source: SEP-NO §4.2]
- "the socialist society would have to forbid capitalist acts between consenting adults" [source: IEP-NO] (page 163 usually cited — not verified)
- Part III "framework for utopia": the minimal state as a framework within which communities (including socialist ones) may form voluntarily; "inspiring, as well as right". [source: IEP-NO]
- Nozick says he gives no "precise theory of the moral basis of individual rights" (xiv). [source: SEP-NO §2.2]

### Featured argument (option A): Wilt Chamberlain, "How Liberty Upsets Patterns" (ASU 160–164), step by step
1. Take any distribution D1 (P1) that your favourite patterned principle (equality, the difference principle, desert…) declares just. Nozick lets the opponent choose it. [source: SEP-NO §4.2; IEP-NO]
2. Wilt Chamberlain signs a contract: 25 cents of each ticket goes to him. One million people come; he receives $250,000, far more than anyone else; this is D2. [source: SEP-NO; IEP-NO; IEP-DJ]
3. Each fan was entitled to his share under D1 and chose freely to pay; Chamberlain and the team chose freely; those who didn't pay still have their quarters; third parties' shares are unchanged. [source: IEP-NO; IEP-DJ]
4. Key question: "If the people were entitled to dispose of the resources to which they were entitled (under D1), didn't this include their being entitled to give it to, or exchange it with Wilt Chamberlain?" (161) And: "By what process could such a transfer among two persons give rise to a legitimate claim of distributive justice on a portion of what was transferred, by a third party who has no claim of justice on any holding of the others before the transfer?" (161–2) [source: SEP-NO §4.2]
5. Conclusion: D2 is just, though it breaks the pattern; so patterned principles are not necessary conditions of justice. Generalised: "no end-state principle or distributional patterned principle of justice can be continually realized without continuous interference with people's lives" (163); "Patterned distributional principles do not give people what entitlement principles do, only better distributed. They do not give the right to choose what to do with what one has…" (167). Hillel Steiner's quip: patterned theories "create rights to interfere with the rights that they have created" (Steiner 1977: 43). [source: SEP-NO §4.2]
- Interpretive note: Mack insists the argument is **internal** to the patterned theorist (how did injustice enter D2 if D1 and the transfers were just?), not a question-begging appeal to liberty as the standard — though Nozick's own wording (163) invites the latter reading. [source: SEP-NO §4.2] This is a debate about how to read Nozick.
- **Objection (the Rawlsian / institutional reply)**: Nozick misdescribes the patterned theorist as re-imposing the pattern daily. The difference principle governs the **basic structure** and aims at the least advantaged's *lifetime* expectations; what one is entitled to is the income that arises under known tax, regulatory and transfer rules; Chamberlain's claim is not violated when the tax authority takes, say, 70% of his windfall. Rawls: "There are no unannounced and unpredictable interferences with citizens' expectations and acquisitions. Entitlements are earned, and honored as the public system declares. Taxes and restrictions are all in principle foreseeable, and holdings are acquired on the known condition that certain transfers and redistributions will be made" (Rawls 1993: 283). [source: SEP-NO §4.2]
- **Reply (Mack, on Nozick's behalf)**: as preferences, needs and opportunities change unpredictably, sustaining any non-trivial pattern requires changing the public system in unannounced ways; Rawls himself: "even in a well-ordered society, adjustments in the basic structure are always necessary" (Rawls 1993: 284); so previously announced entitlements will be dishonoured. [source: SEP-NO §4.2 (Mack 2002a, 2018)]
- Cohen's well-known chapter on this argument: "Robert Nozick and Wilt Chamberlain: How Patterns Preserve Liberty", first in *Erkenntnis* 11 (1977), repr. as ch. 1 of *Self-Ownership, Freedom, and Equality* (Cambridge UP, 1995), pp. 19–37; its immediate aim is to refute Nozick's argument against socialism. [source: WP-ASU bibliography; WS-3] (Cohen's specific objections in that chapter were NOT verified — see NOT VERIFIED.)

### Other objections to Nozick
- **Nagel**, "Libertarianism without Foundations", *Yale Law Journal* 85 (1975): 136–149 (review of ASU): the commonplace charge that Nozick provides no foundation for his Lockean natural rights. [source: SEP-NO §2.2 and bibliography (Nagel 1975, repr. in Paul 1981: 191–205); WS-5 for journal volume and pages] WS-5 secondary summaries report Nagel's line that Nozick "starts from the unargued premise that individuals have certain inviolable rights" — wording not checked against the review.
- **Cohen, *Self-Ownership, Freedom, and Equality* (1995)**: Cohen took **self-ownership** to be the core of Nozick's view and made its link to libertarian economic justice the central problem; though Nozick mentions self-ownership only once, "self-ownership became synonymous with libertarianism". Cohen held (following Marx) that self-ownership explains why capitalist exploitation is unjust. [source: SEP-LIB §1]
- Cohen's **eye-lottery** passage (1995, p. 70): leftists who dismiss self-ownership "lose confidence … when they are asked to consider who has the right to decide what should happen, for example, to their own eyes. They do not immediately agree that, were eye transplants easy to achieve, it would then be acceptable for the state to conscript potential eye donors into a lottery whose losers must yield an eye to beneficiaries who would otherwise not be one-eyed but blind." [source: SEP-DJ §5]
- Cohen on the **proviso**: too weak — compensation only slightly above a low baseline suffices, so one person could appropriate everything; Cohen's beach example: someone "acquires" a beach and charges $1 admission while providing a clean-up or lifeguard service the users deem equally valuable; but a more efficient organiser among them could have charged 50 cents — an alternative Nozick's proviso never considers. [source: SEP-LIB §3; SEP-DJ §5]
- Cohen's "rolling pin" example (Cohen 1995: 39): D's rolling pin rolls into C's kitchen and C innocently mistakes it for his own; nothing unjust happened, so must Nozick say C is entitled? Mack: this misreads Nozick (just steps are not merely "not unjust" ones), but shows how little positive account Nozick gives. [source: SEP-NO §4.1]
- Kymlicka's reconstruction of the self-ownership argument (Kymlicka 1990, p. 112): (1) people own themselves; (2) the world is initially unowned; (3) you can acquire absolute rights over a disproportionate share of the world if you do not worsen others' condition; (4) it is relatively easy to do so without worsening others; therefore (5) once property is appropriated, a free market in capital and labour is morally required. The proviso difficulties put (3) and (4) in doubt. [source: SEP-DJ §5]
- **Left-libertarianism** (Steiner, Vallentyne, Otsuka): self-ownership plus egalitarian ownership of the external world. [source: SEP-LIB §1]
- Nozick and the levelling-down style objection: SEP-EQ cites Nozick 1974 p. 229 among sources of the levelling-down objection; Nozick on desert "all the way down" (225). [source: SEP-EQ]
- G. A. Cohen: born Montreal 14 Apr 1941, died 5 Aug 2009. [source: WP G._A._Cohen]

## 5. Luck egalitarianism and its critics
- The "currency" or "equality of what?" question (Sen 1979; Dworkin 1981a, 1981b; Arneson 1989; G. Cohen 1989) and the "responsibility challenge". [source: SEP-EG §3]
- Problems for **equality of welfare**: adaptive preferences, expensive tastes (Dworkin: giving more to those who splurge on oysters and fine wine seems unjust), offensive preferences. [source: SEP-EG §3.3]
- **Dworkin**, "What Is Equality? Part 1: Equality of Welfare", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 10(3) (1981): 185–246; "Part 2: Equality of Resources", 10(4) (1981): 283–345; reprinted in *Sovereign Virtue* (Harvard, 2000). [source: SEP-EG and SEP-EQ bibliographies] Dworkin: born Worcester, Massachusetts, 11 Dec 1931; died 14 Feb 2013. [source: WP Ronald_Dworkin]
- Dworkin: resources should be distributed "so that no further transfer would leave their shares of the total resources more equal" (1981a: 186). [source: SEP-EG §3.4]
- **Clamshell auction and envy test**: shipwreck survivors on an island receive equal numbers of clamshells and bid for unbundled resources; the result is equal when no one prefers anyone else's bundle (envy test); bundles are equal but not the same. [source: SEP-EG §3.4.1]
- **Brute vs option luck** (Dworkin 2000, 73): "Option luck is a matter of how deliberate and calculated gambles turn out—whether someone gains or loses through accepting an isolated risk he or she should have anticipated and might have declined." Brute luck is "a matter of how risks fall out that are not in that sense deliberate gambles." Example: going blind from a genetic condition = bad brute luck; winning a lottery = good option luck. Insurance links them: "the decision to buy or reject … insurance is a calculated gamble" (2000, 74). [source: SEP-BL]
- **Hypothetical insurance market** (1981b: 296): for brute luck, ask whether people would have insured against the misfortune had they had the chance; if yes, compensate. A distribution should be "ambition-sensitive" but "endowment-insensitive". [source: SEP-EG §3.4.2]
- SEP-EG's illustration: apple-grower vs pool-floater (inequality from choice, fair) vs two apple-growers one of whom is struck by codling moth (brute luck, unfair). [source: SEP-EG §3.4.2]
- **Arneson**, "Equality and Equal Opportunity for Welfare", *Philosophical Studies* 56 (1989): 77–93: preferences measured hypothetically, after ideal reflection; we owe each other opportunity for welfare, not welfare. [source: SEP-EQ §3.7 and bibliography; SEP-EG §3.3]
- **G. A. Cohen**, "On the Currency of Egalitarian Justice", *Ethics* 99 (1989): 906–944 — aims to eliminate involuntary disadvantage (916; Cohen's view usually called "equal access to advantage"); Dworkin's achievement was to incorporate within egalitarianism "the most powerful idea in the arsenal of the anti-egalitarian right: the idea of choice and responsibility" (1989, 933). [source: SEP-EQ; IEP-DJ; SEP-EG §3.6]
- Temkin's formula: it is bad/unfair for some to be worse off than others "through no fault or choice of their own" (Temkin 1993, 13). [source: SEP-EQ]
- Variants: Fleurbaey's helmetless motorcyclist (justice still requires helping him); Segall's "all-luck egalitarianism" (2010). [source: SEP-BL]

### Anderson's critique: relational / democratic equality
- **Elizabeth Anderson**, "What Is the Point of Equality?", *Ethics* 109 (1999): 287–337. Anderson b. 5 Dec 1959. [source: SEP-EQ bibliography; WP Elizabeth_S._Anderson]
- She asked provocatively whether contemporary egalitarianism would look much different if it had been secretly devised by conservatives. [source: SEP-EG §4]
- Point of equality: not an ideal distribution but a community whose members stand in relations of equality; egalitarians should want communities based on principles that "express equal respect and concern for all citizens" (1999, 289). Distribution matters only instrumentally; what is required is the ability of all to function as equals in civil society and in political decision-making ("democratic equality"), which yields a sufficientarian threshold. [source: SEP-BL; SEP-EG §§4, 6.2]
- Objections she pressed against luck egalitarianism: (i) **harshness** — those responsible for their own misery would be abandoned; (ii) **disrespect/stigma** — help for brute-luck victims is based on pity; (iii) intrusive state scrutiny of citizens' choices to classify their misfortunes; (iv) the surfer at Malibu who chooses frugality vs a person with a lifelong disability. [source: SEP-EQ §3.6; SEP-EG §§3.2, 4.2]
- The specific "abandonment of negligent victims" example (an uninsured driver who negligently makes an illegal turn, causes a crash, and on luck-egalitarian principles may be left without emergency care): reported in secondary sources (WS-6), not checked against the article. [source: WS-6]
- Relational egalitarianism's roots also in Iris Marion Young's *Justice and the Politics of Difference* (1990): distributive paradigm treats justice as "an ensemble of possessive relations" (16–17); five faces of oppression: exploitation, marginalization, powerlessness, violence, cultural imperialism. [source: SEP-EG §§4.1, 4.3]
- Luck-egalitarian replies: social standing can itself be treated as a good to be distributed; or the two camps answer different questions (what is a fair distribution vs what we owe each other); or they accept pluralism (luck neutralization balanced against respect). Objection to Anderson: once everyone is above the threshold of equal functioning, her view is indifferent between benefiting the worse off and the best off; and a high threshold makes others pay for the repeatedly reckless (Arneson 2000). [source: SEP-BL]
- Jonathan Wolff (1998): equality of opportunity would require "shameful revelations" (e.g. having to report one has no talent). [source: SEP-BL]

## 6. Sufficientarianism and prioritarianism
- **Frankfurt**, "Equality as a Moral Ideal", *Ethics* 98 (1987): 21–42. Quotations: "What is important from the moral point of view is not that everyone should have the same but that each should have enough. If everyone had enough, it would be of no moral consequence whether some had more than others" (p. 21); "The fundamental error of egalitarianism lies in supposing that it is morally important whether one person has less than another regardless of how much either of them has" (p. 34). [source: SEP-EQ] Frankfurt: b. 29 May 1929, d. 16 Jul 2023. [source: WP Harry_Frankfurt]
- Sufficiency's positive thesis (it is especially important to bring people above a threshold) and negative thesis (above it inequalities don't matter to justice); main objection: the threshold is arbitrary and the view implausibly indifferent above it. Crisp (2003): between rich and super-rich, neither has a claim of justice. [source: SEP-EG §6.2]
- **Parfit**, "Equality or Priority?", the **Lindley Lecture, University of Kansas, 1991**, published 1995 as a pamphlet and in Clayton & Williams (eds), *The Ideal of Equality* (Palgrave, 2000), pp. 81–125. SEP-PR calls it "the scholarly work that triggered intensive philosophical debate" on prioritarianism. [source: SEP-PR §2 and bibliography] (SEP-EG and SEP-EQ cite "Parfit 1997", the *Ratio* reprint "Equality and Priority".)
- Parfit's opening case (from Nagel 1979: 123–24): move to the city (child 1: 20, child 2 with a health condition: 10) or the suburb (25, 9); utilitarianism picks the suburb; the city is the "egalitarian" or prioritarian choice. [source: SEP-PR §2]
- Parfit distinguishes **Telic** egalitarianism (inequality is bad in itself) from **Deontic** egalitarianism (about wrongdoing/fairness). Telic pluralist egalitarianism: "It is in itself bad if some people are worse off than others" + "It is in itself better if people are better off" (p. 84). [source: SEP-PR §2]
- **Levelling Down Objection** (Parfit, p. 98): "If inequality is bad, its disappearance must be in one way a change for the better, however this change occurs. Suppose that those who are better off suffer some misfortune, so that they become as badly off as everyone else. Since these events would remove the inequality, they must be in one way welcome [for egalitarians], even though they would be worse for some people, and better for no one. This implication seems to many to be quite absurd. I call this the Levelling Down Objection." [source: SEP-PR §2]
- **The Priority View** (p. 101): "Benefitting people matters more the worse off these people are." "For Utilitarians, the moral importance of each benefit depends only on how great this benefit would be. For Prioritarians, it also depends on how well off the person is to whom this benefit comes." "[O]n the Priority View, we do not believe in equality … what is bad is not that these people are worse off than others. It is rather that they are worse off than they might have been." Altitude analogy: people high up find it hard to breathe, but would do so even if no one were lower down. [source: SEP-PR §2]

### Featured argument (option B): from levelling down to priority, step by step
1. Telic egalitarianism: inequality is in itself bad. [SEP-PR]
2. So any removal of inequality is in one respect good, however it occurs. [SEP-PR quoting Parfit p. 98]
3. Levelling down (making the better-off worse off, benefiting no one) removes inequality. [same]
4. So levelling down is in one respect good — but it is worse for some and better for no one, which "seems to many to be quite absurd". [same]
5. Therefore reject telic egalitarianism; the intuition behind helping the worse-off is better captured by priority: benefits matter more the worse off the recipient, an absolute (non-comparative) concern. [SEP-PR §2; SEP-EG §6.1]
- Classic illustration: making the sighted blind so the blind are no longer worse off would be "morally perverse" (SEP-EQ's example); Vonnegut's story "Harrison Bergeron" (1961 per standard bibliography; SEP-EQ says "1950" — see NOT VERIFIED). [source: SEP-EQ]
- **Replies**: (a) moderate pluralist egalitarians say levelling down is better only "in one respect", worse all things considered (but SEP-PR notes they remain subject to the objection in Parfit's sense); (b) Temkin (2000): levelling down can be reasonable; (c) for positional goods (votes, education, legal aid) levelling down loses no absolute value — if some have two votes and others one, giving everyone one vote is fine; (d) egalitarians equalize something worthwhile, so destroying value contradicts their commitments (Christiano & Braynen 2008); (e) prioritarianism has trouble with intrapersonal vs interpersonal risk (Otsuka & Voorhoeve 2009). [source: SEP-EG §6.1; SEP-PR §2]
- **Limitarianism** (Ingrid Robeyns 2017b, 2022, 2024): a ceiling on how much people may hold; a companion principle rather than a full theory. [source: SEP-EG §6.3]

## 7. The capabilities approach (Sen and Nussbaum)
- Sen, "Equality of What?", Tanner Lecture on Human Values delivered at **Stanford University, 22 May 1979**; published in McMurrin (ed.), *The Tanner Lectures on Human Values*, vol. 1 (Cambridge UP, 1980), pp. 197–220. [source: SEP-CA bibliography; WP Amartya_Sen]
- Sen: born 3 Nov 1933, Santiniketan, Bengal; Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences 1998. [source: WP Amartya_Sen]
- Core concepts: **functionings** = "doings and beings" (being nourished, literate, travelling); **capabilities** = real freedoms (substantive opportunities) to achieve functionings; **conversion factors** (personal, social, environmental) determine how resources become functionings — e.g. a bicycle and whether one was taught to ride, or whether women are socially allowed to ride. [source: SEP-CA intro, §§2.1, 2.3]
- Sen's disability argument (1979): two people with the same resources, one disabled — she can do less with them and has extra costs (wheelchair). Against utilitarian fixes: the disabled person may be a "pleasure wizard" who gets lots of utility from little, so utilitarianism would give her less; the deprived adapt their expectations (Kynch and Sen 1983). [source: SEP-CA §1.1]
- The approach is "a flexible and multi-purpose framework, rather than a precise theory"; "capabilities approach" often refers specifically to Nussbaum's partial theory of justice. [source: SEP-CA §1.2]
- **Nussbaum's ten central human capabilities**: life; bodily health; bodily integrity; senses, imagination and thought; emotions; practical reason; affiliation; other species; play; control over one's environment (political and material). [source: SEP-CA §3 (Nussbaum 2006: 76–78); WP-CA quoting *Creating Capabilities* (Harvard UP, 2011), pp. 33–34]
- Nussbaum's justification: each capability is needed so that a life is "not so impoverished that it is not worthy of the dignity of a human being" (2000: 72); the list should be constitutionally guaranteed, is open to revision, and can be the object of a Rawlsian overlapping consensus; it specifies **thresholds** — "a partial and minimal account of social justice" (2006: 71). [source: SEP-CA §§3, 4]
- Books: *Women and Human Development: The Capabilities Approach* (Cambridge UP, 2000); *Frontiers of Justice: Disability, Nationality, Species Membership* (Harvard UP, 2006); *Creating Capabilities: The Human Development Approach* (Harvard UP, 2011). Nussbaum b. New York City, 6 May 1947. [source: SEP-CA bibliography; WP-CA; WP Martha_Nussbaum]
- **Sen vs Nussbaum debate**: Sen refuses "one pre-determined canonical list of capabilities, chosen by theorists without any general social discussion or public reasoning" (Sen 2005a: 158); critics say Nussbaum's list leaves too little room for democratic deliberation (Crocker, Robeyns, Sen); Claassen (2011) defends her. Anderson (1999): for political justice the relevant capabilities are those needed to function as a citizen. [source: SEP-CA §3]
- Perfectionism/paternalism objection to the approach; replies via deliberation (Sen 1999) or a cross-culturally valid list (Nussbaum). [source: SEP-EG §3.5]
- Application: the UNDP **Human Development Reports**, launched by Mahbub ul Haq in **1990**, and the Human Development Index (life expectancy, education, income) are anchored in Sen's capabilities work. [source: WP-HDI; SEP-CA §6]
- Non-Western links: recent work relates the approach to Māori philosophy, ubuntu and buen vivir; Hoffmann and Metz (2017) argue ubuntu's relationality could be an "architectonic capability". [source: SEP-CA §5]
- Sen, *The Idea of Justice* (Belknap/Harvard, 2009): rejects "transcendental institutionalism" (identifying perfectly just institutions) in favour of comparative assessment of real lives; justice is a matter of degree. [source: WP The_Idea_of_Justice; WP-TJ]. Sen's Sanskrit distinction *nīti* (rules and institutions) vs *nyāya* (realised justice, what happens to people) and *matsyanyāya* ("justice of the fishes", the big fish eating the small) — from secondary sources only [source: WS-9]. Sen's three-children-and-a-flute example (Anne/Anna, Bob, Carla: one plays it, one is poorest, one made it) — setup only seen in WS-9; use with caution.

## 8. Global justice
- One-line link: Singer (1972) drowning-child principle (in Consequentialism chapter). SEP-GJ: "Singer emphasizes our capacity to assist with need satisfaction, Pogge emphasizes instead our contributions to the problem." [source: SEP-GJ §2.2]
- **Beitz**, *Political Theory and International Relations* (Princeton UP, 1979) and "Justice and International Relations", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 4(4) (1975): 360–389. Beitz (b. 1949). Argument: international trade and institutions form a scheme of cooperation akin to a basic structure, so the veil must cover nationality and Rawls's principles apply globally: "[I]f evidence of global economic and political interdependence shows the existence of a global scheme of social cooperation, we should not view national boundaries as having fundamental moral significance … The veil of ignorance must extend to all matters of national citizenship, and the principles chosen will therefore apply globally" (Beitz 1979b: 151, "Bounded Morality", *International Organization* 33(3)). Also an early **resource redistribution principle** (1975). [source: SEP-IJ; SEP-GJ; WP Charles_Beitz]
- Brian Barry's objection (1982: 233): trade "does not constitute a cooperative scheme of the relevant kind … we would hardly feel inclined to think of, say, the Beaker Folk as forming a single cooperative enterprise with their trading partners." [source: SEP-IJ]
- **Pogge**, *World Poverty and Human Rights: Cosmopolitan Responsibilities and Reforms* (Polity, 2002; 2nd ed. 2008); also *Realizing Rawls* (Cornell, 1989). Pogge b. 13 Aug 1953, Austria. Thesis: affluent countries impose a coercive global institutional order that foreseeably and avoidably produces massive human-rights deficits, so we **harm** the global poor (a negative duty), not merely fail to help. The **international resource privilege** and **borrowing privilege** (whoever holds effective power may sell the country's resources and borrow in its name) create incentives for coups and oppression. Proposal: a **Global Resources Dividend** — a small tax on resource extraction, paid by consumers, funding basic needs; also the Health Impact Fund. [source: SEP-GJ §§2.1, 2.2, 8; SEP-IJ bibliography; WP Thomas_Pogge]
- Pogge on institutional cosmopolitanism: those designing or administering global rules "ought to disregard their private and local, including national, commitments and loyalties to give equal consideration to the needs and interests of every human being on this planet" (Pogge 2013, 298). [source: SEP-GJ §2.3]
- **Nagel**, "The Problem of Global Justice", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 33(2) (2005): 113–147 — "extreme right institutionalism"/statism: justice, including egalitarian distributive justice, applies only within a sovereign coercive state whose institutions coerce "in the name of" those coerced and invoke their will; beyond the state there are only obligations of "humanitarianism" (preventing starvation, assault, murder). [source: SEP-IJ §3.1; SEP-GJ §2.3]
- Replies to Nagel: coercion is rampant globally, so it activates global duties (Cohen and Sabel 2006; Abizadeh 2007; Valentini 2012). [source: SEP-GJ §2.3]
- **David Miller**, *On Nationality* (Oxford, 1995); *National Responsibility and Global Justice* (OUP, 2007). Three claims constituting nationality (1995: 10–12): nations are real and valuable; ethically significant (special duties to compatriots); politically significant (claims to self-determination). Shared nationality explains why citizens accept redistribution beyond strict reciprocity (1995: 71–3). Nationalists hold that principles of distributive justice right within a nation may not be right globally (Miller 2007). [source: SEP-NAT §§1, 4; SEP-IJ]
- Debate summary: cosmopolitans (Beitz, Pogge, Caney, Moellendorf, Tan, Nussbaum 1996 "citizens of the world") vs statists (Nagel, Blake) vs nationalists (Miller, Tamir) vs Rawls's Law of Peoples (duty of assistance with cut-off). Diogenes is "widely credited" as first to call himself a "citizen of the world". [source: SEP-GJ §2.3; SEP-IJ]

## 9. Non-Western perspectives (as far as sources allow)
- **Mencius on "constant means of livelihood"** (*Mengzi* 1A7): if the people lack a constant means of livelihood they will lack a constant mind and fall into crime; to punish them then is to "entrap" them; so the king bears much of the responsibility; Mencius proposes light taxation, no close-mesh fishing nets, seasonal tree-felling, mulberry planting (1A3, 1A7). David Wong links this to Nagel's "circumstantial luck". Exception: the *shi* (gentry-scholar) class can keep a constant mind in poverty. [source: SEP-CE (David Wong)]
- **Well-field system** (*Mengzi* 3A3, "a detailed treatise on tax policy"): a village's land divided into nine equal squares like a tic-tac-toe grid resembling the character 井 (*jǐng*, "well"); eight families each keep the produce of an outer square; the central square is worked jointly and its produce goes to the government as tax. [source: SEP-MEN (Van Norden)] WP: attributed to Western Zhou; Mencius credits King Wen; historicity of the earliest examples doubtful ("could be mythological or imagined"); suspended in Qin by Shang Yang; Wang Mang's Xin dynasty briefly restored it as "King's Fields"; Song Neo-Confucians (Zhang Zai, Su Xun) wanted it restored. [source: WP-WF]
- Xunzi (via SEP-CE): critics of Confucian virtue-politics argued that order depends on the scarcity of goods relative to people and on a structure of roles — use only as context.
- **Zakat**: one of the Five Pillars of Islam; obligatory almsgiving by Muslims above a minimum (*nisab*) threshold; customary rate 2.5% on capital assets held a lunar year (2.5%–20% on agricultural produce, livestock, minerals); the Qur'an itself gives no percentages; scholars disagree on details. Qur'an 9:60 lists eight categories of recipients: the poor, the needy, zakat administrators, those whose hearts are to be reconciled, freeing slaves, debtors, "Allah's cause", stranded travellers. In six Muslim-majority countries (Libya, Malaysia, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Sudan, Yemen) zakat is state-collected (as of Marty & Appleby 1996). [source: WP-Z] (Translation of 9:60 on WP uses Khattab-style ˹brackets˺; translator not named — name a translation before quoting.)
- Twentieth-century "Islamic economics": Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr's *Iqtisaduna* ("Our Economics", 1961) and Mahmoud Taleghani: Islam as committed to "social justice, the equitable distribution of wealth, and the cause of the deprived classes", with a large role for the state in circulation and equitable distribution of wealth. [source: WP-IE]
- **Ubuntu** ("*Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu*", Zulu: "A person is a person through people"). [source: WP-UB] Thaddeus Metz (b. 1968; Cornell PhD 1997; Univ. of Pretoria) gives an analytic ubuntu-based moral theory: right action respects harmonious/communal relationships, understood as **identity** (sharing a way of life) plus **solidarity** (caring for others' good) — secondary summaries [source: WS-7, WS-10; WP-UB quotes Metz & Gaie 2010: "actions are right roughly insofar as they are a matter of living harmoniously with others or honouring communal relationships"]. Metz, "Relational Normative Economics: An African Approach to Justice" (*Ethical Perspectives*, 2020) and *A Relational Theory of Justice: African Economic, Political, and Legal Philosophy in and Beyond the Continent* (OUP, 2026), grounding economic/political justice on people's capacity to commune with others. [source: WP-METZ; WS-7]. Ubuntu justice is often linked with restorative justice; critics (Mboti 2015) call the normative definition "fuzzy", and some African feminists note patriarchal elements. [source: WP-UB]
- Sen's Sanskrit *nīti/nyāya* distinction (§7) can serve as the Indian thread.

## Debates
- **Maximin vs expected utility** (Rawls vs Harsanyi, Arrow): is maximin irrational, or rational for a unique, irrevocable choice of fundamental interests? Rawls later downplayed maximin as a heuristic and rested more on strains of commitment, stability and reciprocity. [SEP-OP §6; WP-TJ]
- **Reading Wilt Chamberlain**: internal argument about how injustice could enter D2 (Mack) vs question-begging appeal to liberty; Rawlsian basic-structure reply vs Mack's rejoinder about unpredictable adjustments. [SEP-NO §4.2]
- **Foundations of Nozick's rights**: Nagel's "libertarianism without foundations"; Mack argues Nozick shares Rawls's separateness-of-persons starting point and draws a more radical conclusion. [SEP-NO §§2.2, 4.1]
- **Self-ownership**: Cohen's challenge; left-libertarians; control vs income rights (Gibbard, Christman). [SEP-LIB §1]
- **Currency of equality**: welfare / resources / opportunity for welfare / access to advantage / capabilities / primary goods. [SEP-EG §3; SEP-EQ]
- **Luck vs relational egalitarianism** (Dworkin, Arneson, Cohen vs Anderson, Scheffler, Wolff, Young). [SEP-EG §4; SEP-BL]
- **Equality vs priority vs sufficiency vs limitarianism**; levelling down. [SEP-EG §6; SEP-PR]
- **Sen vs Nussbaum** on a fixed list; Rawls vs capabilities on primary goods. [SEP-CA]
- **Global justice**: cosmopolitan (Beitz, Pogge) vs statist (Nagel, Blake) vs nationalist (Miller) vs Rawls's duty of assistance; Rawls's domestic-culture account of national wealth vs Pogge's institutional account. [SEP-GJ; SEP-IJ]
- Interpretive debate whether Rawls's theory is distributive or relational (it contains "the seeds" of both). [SEP-EG §3]
