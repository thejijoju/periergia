## 1. The Vedas and their layers
- There are four Vedic collections: Ṛgveda, Yajurveda, Sāmaveda and Atharvaveda. Each has four layers: Saṃhitās (largely hymns praising gods), Brāhmaṇas (mostly describing and explaining rituals), Āraṇyakas and Upaniṣads. The last two are "firmly rooted in ritual", but with an "increasing emphasis on understanding the meaning of ritual". Some Upanishadic sections "move completely away from the ritual setting into naturalistic and philosophical inquiry". [source: IEP-Up §1a]
- The Upaniṣads are "the fourth and final section" of the Vedas. [source: IEP-Up §1a]
- Āraṇyaka means roughly "wilderness texts" or "forest treatises". [source: WP-Vedas]
- Vedānta means "end of the Vedas" and often refers specifically to the Upaniṣads. The Vedānta school divided the Vedas into a *karmakāṇḍa* (ritual section: Saṃhitās and Brāhmaṇas) and a *jñānakāṇḍa* (knowledge section: the Upaniṣads and, to some extent, the Āraṇyakas). [source: IEP-Up §8]
- The Vedas are *śruti* ("what is heard"), as distinguished from *smṛti* ("what is remembered"). Hindus regard them as *apauruṣeya*, "not of a man, superhuman", i.e. authorless. The epics, including the Bhagavad Gītā, are *smṛti*. [source: WP-Vedas]
- The Hindu *darśanas* Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta regard all the Vedic Upaniṣads as *śruti*, "a timeless revealed knowledge", and so do not accept a historical chronology of them. [source: IEP-Up §1a]
- **Ṛgveda date.** Usually dated to c. 1500–1000 BCE. Jamison and Brereton (2014 translation): the dating "has been and is likely to remain a matter of contention and reconsideration". Witzel places its codification c. 1200–1000 BCE in the early Kuru kingdom. A wider range of c. 1700–1100 BCE (Oberlies) has also been given. Mandalas 2–7 (the "family books") are the oldest; mandalas 1 and 10 are the youngest. [source: WP-Rigveda; WP-Vedas]
- IEP-Up gives "the Ṛgveda (c.1200 B.C.E.), the earliest textual source from ancient India". This is within, but at the late end of, the usual range. [source: IEP-Up §3]
- The Ṛgveda (Śākala recension) has 1,028 hymns. [source: WP-Rigveda]
- **Orality.** The Vedas were composed and transmitted orally with elaborate mnemonic techniques. They were written down only after 500 BCE, and the oldest surviving Ṛgveda manuscripts (from Nepal) date to c. 1040 CE. Only orally transmitted texts are regarded as authoritative. [source: WP-Vedas; WP-Rigveda]
- The Brāhmaṇas: the oldest date to about 900 BCE, and the youngest (such as the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa) were complete by about 700 BCE. [source: WP-Vedas]

## 2. Early speculation: the Nāsadīya hymn (Ṛgveda 10.129)
- The Nāsadīya Sūkta ("Hymn of Creation") is hymn 129 of the 10th mandala of the Ṛgveda. It is named after its opening words *ná ásat*, "not the non-existent". [source: WP-Nasadiya_Sukta]
- It belongs to mandala 10, which is "late within the Rigveda Samhita". It asks how and through whom the universe came to be, and gives no definite answer: the gods came after creation, and even the overseer "in the highest heaven" may or may not know. [source: WP-Nasadiya_Sukta]
- Structure: seven *triṣṭubh* verses. Line 7b is two syllables short, which Brereton (1999) argues is a deliberate device expressing puzzlement. [source: WP-Nasadiya_Sukta]
- Readings: Karel Werner reads it as rooted in a "visionary, mystical or Yogic experience put into words". Brereton (1999) argues the hymn re-enacts creation within the listener, equating poetic utterance with creation. An atheist or agnostic reading sees it as early sceptical inquiry, and Carl Sagan quoted it on India's "tradition of skeptical questioning". [source: WP-Nasadiya_Sukta]

## 3. The Upaniṣads: what they are, how many, what the word means
- IEP-Up: the Upaniṣads were "composed orally in Sanskrit between about 700 B.C.E. and 300 B.C.E.". There are "thirteen major Upaniṣads, many of which were likely composed by multiple authors". [source: IEP-Up intro]
- They contain some of the oldest discussions of ātman (self), brahman (ultimate reality), karma, yoga, saṃsāra, mokṣa, puruṣa (person) and prakṛti (nature). They explore means of knowing that include "deduction, comparison, introspection, and debate". [source: IEP-Up intro]
- Hundreds of later texts are also called "Upaniṣad": the Yoga, Saṃnyāsa (renunciation), Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava Upaniṣads. Most were composed between the 2nd and 15th centuries CE, and texts called "upaniṣad" have been composed up to the present. [source: IEP-Up §1b]
- The *Muktikā* Upaniṣad (which predates 1656 CE) lists a canon of 108 Upaniṣads. More than 200 texts called Upaniṣads are known. [source: WP-Upanishads]
- **Etymology (a debate).** The popular etymology upa + ni + sad, "sitting down near" a teacher, is not how the word is used in the texts or the commentaries. In its earliest contexts "upaniṣad" means something like *bandhu*, a hidden connection or equivalence. It often means "secret teaching" (e.g. CU 1.1.10, 1.13.4, 8.8.4). BU 2.2.20 equates *upaniṣad* with *satyasya satyam*, "the truth behind the truth". [source: IEP-Up §2]
- **Bandhus.** The Upaniṣads continue the ritual texts' search for hidden connections (*bandhus*) between the cosmos, the body of the sacrificer (*yajamāna*) and the ritual grounds. Example: BU 1.1 maps the parts of the sacrificial horse (*aśvamedha*) onto the cosmos. [source: IEP-Up §2]
- **Brahman.** In the oldest usage it meant a truthful or powerful utterance (linked to speech). In the Upaniṣads it also comes to mean "the underlying reality or the ontological foundation". [source: IEP-Up §2]
- **Ātman.** A reflexive pronoun, "likely derived from √an (to breathe)". Already in the Ṛgveda it meant "breath", "spirit" and "body". In the Upaniṣads it can mean the material body, but often an essence, life-force, consciousness or ultimate reality. [source: IEP-Up §3]
- **Authorship.** Most Upaniṣads are anonymous. Radhakrishnan: "almost all the early literature of India was anonymous, we do not know the names of the authors of the Upanishads". Teachings are attributed to sages such as Yājñavalkya, Uddālaka Āruṇi, Śvetaketu and Śāṇḍilya. Women such as Maitreyī and Gārgī take part in the dialogues. [source: WP-Upanishads]
- **Not a single system.** A recurring argument that the Upaniṣads are not philosophy is that they have no unified, systematic position. Black: this reflects their composite nature and their origin in "separate and often competing scholarly traditions or schools (śākhas)". Teachers competed for students and patronage and debated rivals in public contests. [source: IEP-Up §2]
- **As philosophy (a debate).** Black: the teachers "do not seem to make linear arguments that start with premises", and tend to argue by analogy and metaphor. Still, there are implicit methods: Yājñavalkya's reflective introspection and Uddālaka's observation of nature. The dialogue form helped shape debate, "the defining social practice of Indian philosophy". [source: IEP-Up §9]

## 4. Dates: how they are established and why they are disputed
- **Olivelle's chronology** (via IEP-Up and WP, citing Olivelle 1998, *The Early Upaniṣads*, pp. 12–13):
  - The Bṛhadāraṇyaka and Chāndogya are the two earliest. Both are edited texts whose sources differ in age. They are pre-Buddhist and "may be placed in the 7th to 6th centuries BCE, give or take a century or so". [source: WP-Upanishads; WP-Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad; WP-Chandogya_Upanishad]
  - Taittirīya, Aitareya and Kauṣītakī come next. They are probably pre-Buddhist and date to the 6th–5th centuries BCE. [source: WP-Upanishads]
  - The verse Upaniṣads (Kena, the oldest of them, then Kaṭha, Īśā, Śvetāśvatara and Muṇḍaka) were composed probably in the last few centuries BCE. Olivelle: "All exhibit strong theistic tendencies and are probably the earliest literary products of the theistic tradition, whose later literature includes the Bhagavad Gita and the Puranas." [source: WP-Upanishads]
  - IEP-Up gives this as two stages: first stage (BU, CU, Taittirīya, Aitareya, Kauṣītakī) "generally dated between 700 and 500 B.C.E.", before the Buddhists, Jains and Ājīvikas. The second stage (Kena, Kaṭha, Īśā, Śvetāśvatara, Praśna, Muṇḍaka, Māṇḍūkya, Maitrī) is dated by "scholarly consensus" to 300–100 BCE (citing Olivelle 1998: 12–13). The older texts are mostly prose, the later ones mostly verse. [source: IEP-Up §1a]
- **Olivelle's warning (quotable):** "in spite of claims made by some, in reality, any dating of these documents [early Upanishads] that attempts a precision closer than a few centuries is as stable as a house of cards". [source: WP-Upanishads; WP-Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad; WP-Chandogya_Upanishad, all citing Olivelle 1998]
- **Method of dating.** Stephen Phillips: the chronology is hard to resolve because "all opinions rest on scanty evidence and analysis of archaism, style and repetitions across texts", and on assumptions about how ideas evolved and which philosophy influenced which. Some scholars use parallels with Buddhist literature to date the Upaniṣads. [source: WP-Upanishads]
- The BU and CU are anthologies of material that existed as independent texts before they were compiled. [source: WP-Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad, citing Olivelle 1998 pp. 11–12]
- **Other ranges.** Gavin Flood: "Even the older texts were composed over a wide expanse of time from about 600 to 300 BCE." Phillips places the principal Upaniṣads at 800–300 BCE. Estimates for the BU range from 900 to 600 BCE. [source: WP-Upanishads; WP-Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad]
- **Bronkhorst's late dating.** Johannes Bronkhorst places even the BU as "possibly still being composed at 'a date close to Katyayana and Patañjali [the grammarian]'", i.e. c. 2nd century BCE. Alexander Wynne contests this late dating. [source: WP-Upanishads; WP-Greater_Magadha]
- **Kaṭha.** Dating is contested: King and Basham put it roughly in the 5th century BCE, while Olivelle puts it in the first centuries BCE. [source: WP-Katha_Upanishad]
- **BU.** It forms the last (14th) *kāṇḍa* of the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa of the Śukla (White) Yajurveda. It survives in two recensions, Mādhyandina and Kāṇva. It is traditionally credited to Yājñavalkya but "likely refined by a number of ancient Vedic scholars". [source: WP-Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad]
- **CU.** It belongs to the Sāmaveda (Tāṇḍya school) and has eight *prapāṭhakas*. [source: WP-Chandogya_Upanishad]
- **Theism.** Earlier Upaniṣads describe ultimate reality in "abstract and impersonal ways". The later ones, especially the Īśā and Śvetāśvatara, are "more theistic". [source: IEP-Up §1a]

## 5. Ātman: the teachers and their arguments
### Uddālaka Āruṇi and Śvetaketu (Chāndogya 6)
- CU 6.1–16: the brahmin Uddālaka Āruṇi instructs his son Śvetaketu. **Argument from material causes:** by knowing one thing made of clay you know clay; likewise copper and iron. Creation is not from nothing but "a process of transformation from an original being (sat)". This is often assumed to have influenced *satkāryavāda* (the effect exists in the cause), which Sāṃkhya, Yoga and Vedānta accepted. [source: IEP-Up §3]
- **Analogies and "experiments."** Nectar gathered by bees becomes an undifferentiated whole, and rivers merge in the ocean. Śvetaketu cuts open a banyan fruit and then a seed and sees nothing inside. Salt dissolved in water cannot be seen, but tasting shows it is everywhere. The conclusion is that the self is a fine, non-material essence pervading all living beings. [source: IEP-Up §3]
- Black's reading: Uddālaka offers "an organic understanding of ātman", the life force that animates all beings. [source: IEP-Up §3]
- Uddālaka's three forms of *sat* (fire = red, water = white, food = black; CU 6.2–5) closely resemble the later Sāṃkhya doctrine of *prakṛti* and the three *guṇas*. [source: IEP-Up §7]
- **Uddālaka never uses the word "brahman"**, neither in teaching Śvetaketu nor anywhere else in the Upaniṣads. Śaṅkara later read *tat tvam asi* as stating the identity of ātman and brahman. [source: IEP-Up §4]
- Uddālaka was a brahmin of the Gautama lineage from Kuru-Pañcāla. He is credited as the teacher of Yājñavalkya, and he lost to Yājñavalkya at Janaka's court. [source: WP-Uddālaka_Āruṇi; IEP-Up §3 ("by some accounts, his former teacher (BU 6.3.7; 6.5.3)")]
- Debiprasad Chattopadhyaya, in *Science and Society in Ancient India*, called Uddālaka the first "natural scientist" in intellectual history. Ben-Ami Scharfstein counts him among the first philosophers in recorded history. Present these as those scholars' claims. [source: WP-Uddālaka_Āruṇi]

### "Tat tvam asi" (CU 6.8.7) and the translation debate
- The phrase first appears at CU 6.8.7 and recurs as a refrain in the later sections of the teaching. [source: WP-Mahāvākyas]
- Olivelle's translation of the refrain: "The finest essence here—that constitutes the self of this whole world; that is the truth; that is the self (ātman). And that's how you are, Śvetaketu." (Olivelle, *Upaniṣads*, 1996/2008, p. 152) [source: WP-Mahāvākyas]
- Traditional renderings: "Thou art that", "That thou art", "You are that". [source: WP-Mahāvākyas]
- **Brereton's argument** (Joel P. Brereton, "'Tat Tvam Asi' in Context", *ZDMG* 136, 1986, pp. 98–109): the phrase should be rendered "In that way [= thus] are you, Śvetaketu", or "in that way you are". Brian Black gives the grammatical point: "the pronoun tat (that) is neuter, and therefor cannot correspond with the masculine tvam (you)… if 'you are that' was the intended meaning, then the passage should read sa tvam asi" (Black 2012 [*The Character of the Self in Ancient India*], p. 36). Brereton also argues that the refrain originally belonged to CU 6.12 and was copied into the other sections. Olivelle and Wendy Doniger follow Brereton. Doniger: "Joel Brereton and Patrick Olivelle have argued, fairly convincingly, that it should rather be translated, 'And that's how you are.'" [source: WP-Mahāvākyas]
- **Later Vedāntic readings of the same phrase:** Śaṅkara (Advaita), in the *Upadeśasāhasrī*: "Through such sentences as 'Thou art That' one knows one's own Atman". In Dvaita (Madhva's school) the sandhi is split as *atat tvam asi*, "you are not that", i.e. the self is not God. Rāmānuja (Viśiṣṭādvaita) takes ātman to be not identical with brahman but an aspect of it. [source: WP-Mahāvākyas; IEP-Up §8]

### Yājñavalkya (Bṛhadāraṇyaka)
- Yājñavalkya is the most prominent teacher in the BU. He characterises ātman "more in terms of consciousness than as a life-giving essence". [source: IEP-Up §3]
- **The inner controller** (*antaryāmin*, BU 3.7.23): present in all sensing and cognising, yet distinct. [source: IEP-Up §3]
- **Argument that the self cannot be an object.** We know the self "through what the self does, not through our senses". As consciousness it "cannot be an object of consciousness". Because it cannot be an object of knowledge it cannot have attributes, so it can be described only by negations. [source: IEP-Up §3]
- **Neti neti** ("not this, not this"; from *na iti*, "not so"): the doctrine of describing the self only by negation. It appears at BU 2.3.6 and 4.5.15 (both cited in WP-Neti_neti) and also in BU ch. 3 (9th brāhmaṇa) and ch. 4 (2nd brāhmaṇa) (WP-Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad). Later readings differ: Śaṅkara takes it as removing obstructions produced by ignorance. The Viśiṣṭādvaitin S. S. Raghavachar reads it as denying only that the listed forms exhaust brahman: "negative in the letter but embodies a super-abundance of affirmation in spirit". [source: WP-Neti_neti; WP-Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad]
- **The self as witness** (BU 3.7–3.8, as quoted in SEP-Pers, translator not named there): "The Self is the seer, Gargi, though unseen; the hearer, though unheard; the thinker, though unthought; the knower, though unknown." Chadha contrasts this "witnessing subject" picture with the BU's other picture of the person as agent (BU 4.4.5). [source: SEP-Pers §1.1]
- **Dreamless sleep.** Yājñavalkya calls dreamless sleep the "highest goal" and "highest bliss" (BU 4.3.32). In the CU, Prajāpati teaches Indra over more than a hundred years and treats the dreamless-sleep account of the self as false or incomplete. His final answer is that ātman is "the one who is aware" (CU 8.7–12). The Upaniṣads thus contain disagreements among their own teachers. [source: IEP-Up §3]
- **The court of Janaka.** BU ch. 3: King Janaka of Videha holds a sacrifice and offers a thousand cows to the most learned brahmin. Yājñavalkya tells his pupil to drive them home, and the other brahmins challenge him. He defeats Aśvala, Ārtabhāga, Bhujyu, Uṣasta, Uddālaka and others. Paul Deussen compared Yājñavalkya's portrayal here to Socrates in the dialogues. [source: WP-Yajnavalkya; WP-Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad; WP-Gārgī_Vāchaknavī]
- In the BU ch. 4 dialogues with Janaka, knowledge of the self is "beyond virtuous (kalyāṇa) and evil (pāpa)" (BU 4.4.22). In the world of brahman "a thief is not a thief… an outcaste not an outcaste… a renunciate (śramaṇa) is not a renunciate" (BU 4.3.22). Black: this does not license evil, since a knower "necessarily acts morally" and becomes "calm (śānta), restrained (dānta), withdrawn (uparata), patient (titikṣu), and composed (samāhita)" (BU 4.4.23). [source: IEP-Up §6]
- Yājñavalkya is also credited with the *Yājñavalkya Smṛti* (a law code) and the *Yoga Yājñavalkya*, both much later texts. [source: WP-Yajnavalkya]

### Women in the dialogues: Gārgī and Maitreyī, and how scholars read them
- **Gārgī Vācaknavī** questions Yājñavalkya twice at Janaka's court (BU 3.6 and 3.8). In 3.6 she asks what each layer of the world is "woven" on (water, then air, and so on, up to the worlds of brahman). Yājñavalkya stops her with a warning that her head will fall off if she asks too much, and she falls silent. In 3.8 she returns with two final questions and says that if he answers them no one will beat him. He answers with the doctrine of the imperishable (*akṣara*), across which space is woven, and she then declares him unbeatable. [source: WP-Yajnavalkya; WP-Gārgī_Vāchaknavī; WP-Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad (BU 3.6, 3.8 on "the imperishable")]
- WP-Yajnavalkya notes that Gārgī is the first in the debate to raise brahman itself. [source: WP-Yajnavalkya]
- **Maitreyī** (BU 2.4, repeated in modified form at 4.5) is one of Yājñavalkya's two wives. The other, Kātyāyanī, had only "woman's knowledge". When Yājñavalkya prepares to renounce and divide his property, Maitreyī asks whether the whole earth full of wealth would make her immortal, and he answers that it would not. Hume's translation: "What should I do with that through which I may not be immortal? What you know, Sir – that, indeed, tell me!" (BU 2.4.3, Hume 1921). [source: WP-Maitreyi]
- Yājñavalkya to Maitreyī (Hume, BU 2.4.5): "Lo, verily, not for love of a husband is a husband dear, but for the love of the Self a husband is dear." Olivelle's rendering of the conclusion: "it is one's self (atman) which one should see and hear, and on which one should reflect and concentrate. For by seeing and hearing one's self… one gains the knowledge of this whole world." (Olivelle 1998 p. 69) [source: WP-Maitreyi; WP-Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad]
- The dialogue survives in both recensions (Mādhyandina and Kāṇva), which differ in literary detail but share the philosophical theme (Brereton 2006). [source: WP-Maitreyi]
- **Scholarly readings (a debate):**
  - Black (IEP-Up §6): ātman is grammatically masculine, the teachings are directed at a male audience "in overtly androcentric metaphors", and Yājñavalkya assumes the knowers of the self will be brahmin men (Black 2007: 135–41). Yet "the Upaniṣads depict several women—such as Gārgī and Maitreyī—as participating in philosophical discussions and debates" (Black 2007: 48–67; Lindquist 2008). [source: IEP-Up §6]
  - Wendy Doniger: Maitreyī is not portrayed as an author but as part of a story about a brahmin with two wives who differ in intellect. Karen Pechilis: Maitreyī is portrayed as theologically minded, challenging Yājñavalkya and asking the right questions. Scholars differ on whether the dialogue shows that women in Vedic India were accepted as participants in philosophy. [source: WP-Maitreyi]
  - Steven Lindquist, "Gender at Janaka's Court: Women in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad Reconsidered", *Journal of Indian Philosophy* 36.3 (2008): 405–426. Per its abstract (second-hand): scholars have tended either to dismiss these women as literary fictions or to treat them as straightforward evidence about real women. Lindquist instead examines their literary function in the text. [source: IEP-Up references; WS-Lindquist (philpapers.org record, summary only)]
  - Other readings of Gārgī's silence are open: whether Yājñavalkya's threat is coercion or marks the limit of legitimate questioning (that one cannot ask what supports the support of everything). WP-Yajnavalkya asserts the latter reading without a named scholar, so present it as one interpretation. [source: WP-Yajnavalkya]

### Naciketas and Yama (Kaṭha Upaniṣad)
- Vājaśravasa (also called Aruṇi Auddālaki Gautama) gives away worn-out cows in a sacrifice. His son Naciketas keeps asking "To whom will you give me?", and the father says "To death." Naciketas goes to the house of Yama, who is absent, and waits three nights without food. Yama offers three boons. [source: WP-Katha_Upanishad; IEP-BG §5]
- The boons: (1) to return to a calm, unresentful father; (2) the fire ritual leading to heaven, thereafter called the "Naciketa fire"; (3) the question of whether a person exists after death (KaU 1.1.20). Yama tries to buy him off with wealth and pleasures, but Naciketas refuses. [source: WP-Katha_Upanishad]
- Yama distinguishes *preyas* (the pleasant) from *śreyas* (the good or beneficial) (KaU 1.2.1–2). WP-Katha notes a parallel with Plato's *Phaedrus*. [source: WP-Katha_Upanishad]
- **The chariot allegory** (KaU 1.3.3–4ff.): the self is the rider, the body the chariot, the intellect (*buddhi*) the charioteer, the mind (*manas*) the reins, the senses (*indriya*) the horses, and sense objects the roads. The wise rein in the senses, and this is explicitly called yoga. [source: IEP-BG §5; WP-Katha_Upanishad]
- Early descriptions of yoga practice (sense control, breathing, posture) appear in the Kaṭha (3.3–13; 6.7–11) and Śvetāśvatara (2.8–11). [source: IEP-Up §7]
- The Naciketas story is older than the Kaṭha: a version appears in the Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa 3.11. [source: WP-Katha_Upanishad]
- Ranganathan (IEP-BG) argues that the Gītā's authors "must have been quite conscious of the Kaṭha Upaniṣad (Jezic 2009)". On this reading the Gītā's chariot setting deliberately echoes Yama's allegory, with Kṛṣṇa as charioteer taking the role of the intellect. [source: IEP-BG §5]

## 6. Ātman = brahman, and its later readings
- **Śāṇḍilya** (CU 3.14) gives the most famous identification of ātman and brahman (CU 3.14.4): if one understands brahman as the whole world and the self as brahman, one becomes the whole world at death. Deussen considered CU 3.14, together with Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 10.6.3, perhaps the oldest full statement of Vedānta's basic premises. [source: IEP-Up §4; WP-Chandogya_Upanishad]
- Olivelle's CU 3.14 (p. 209): "This self (atman) of mine that lies deep within my heart—it is smaller than a grain of rice or barley… but it is larger than the earth… larger even than all these worlds put together." [source: WP-Chandogya_Upanishad]
- BU 1.4.10 (Olivelle 2008 p. 15): "In the beginning this world was only brahman, and it knew only itself (ātman), thinking: 'I am brahman.' As a result, it became the Whole." This is the source of *aham brahmāsmi*, "I am brahman". [source: WP-Mahāvākyas]
- Yājñavalkya also equates ātman with brahman (BU 4.4.5). [source: IEP-Up §4]
- **Caution (Black):** the identification "is often considered the central doctrine of the Upaniṣads", but it is "not the only characterization". Uddālaka does not make it. Even in Śāṇḍilya it is "often unclear" whether complete identity is meant or whether ātman is "an aspect or quality of brahman". [source: IEP-Up §4]
- Some passages are more materialist: brahman as space (BU 5.1), the world beginning from water (BU 5.5.1), and Raikva tracing everything to wind and breath (CU 4.3.1–2). [source: IEP-Up §4]
- In the Upaniṣads the self is often active and dynamic (inner controller, creator god), which contrasts with the "changeless and inactive" self of Sāṃkhya, Yoga and Advaita. [source: IEP-Up §4]
- **Vedānta** (cross-reference the Vedānta chapter, A2 no. 15). The Brahma Sūtra, attributed to Bādarāyaṇa (IEP: 200 BCE–200 CE; WP-Upanishads: c. 100 BCE), is the oldest surviving systematic interpretation. Vedānta's three core texts (*prasthānatraya*) are the Upaniṣads, the Brahma Sūtra and the Bhagavad Gītā. Śaṅkara (c. 700 CE; Advaita, non-dualism) holds that ātman and brahman are "one and the same". Rāmānuja (c. 1000 CE; Viśiṣṭādvaita, qualified non-dualism) holds that ātman is "an aspect of brahman" and identifies brahman with God. Madhva (c. 1200 CE; Dvaita, dualism) holds that God is infinite and independent and the self finite and dependent. All three wrote commentaries presenting the Upaniṣads as one consistent position. [source: IEP-Up §8]
- Śaṅkara's reading "continued to dominate readings of the texts throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries". [source: IEP-Up §8]
- **Other schools.** Before Vedānta (7th century CE), the Upaniṣads were "only cited occasionally". The Vaiśeṣika Sūtra (3.2.8) says the self's existence can be proven by inference, not by scripture alone. The Nyāya philosopher Jayanta Bhaṭṭa even found Lokāyata (materialist) doctrines in the Upaniṣads. [source: IEP-Up §7]
- **The Buddhist contrast** (cross-reference the Buddha chapter): the Buddhists rejected "any notion of an indivisible and unchanging self", introducing *anātman* (Pāli *anattā*) and explaining continuity by the five *skandhas*. [source: IEP-Up §4] SEP-Bud: within the Vedic canon the "comparatively late" Upaniṣads "likewise display disaffection with Brahmin ritualism". New ideas shared with the śramaṇas include rebirth, karma, the unsatisfactoriness of continual rebirth, liberation, and the need to overcome ignorance about one's true identity. In the Tevijja Sutta the Buddha rejects some Brahmins' claim to know the path to union with Brahman, "on the grounds that no one has actually observed this Brahman". [source: SEP-Bud §1–2]

## 7. Karma, rebirth, the five fires and mokṣa
- Karma literally means "action". It arose in a ritual context: correctly performed ritual action yields good results. "The Upaniṣads do not offer any explicit theory of karma", but they extend the notion beyond ritual to moral retribution and causality. [source: IEP-Up §5]
- BU 3.2.13: asked by Ārtabhāga what happens after death, Yājñavalkya says a person becomes good by good action and bad by bad action. They discuss it in private. Black: Yājñavalkya presents karma "more as a theory to promote good actions, than as a fatalistic doctrine". [source: IEP-Up §5; WP-Karma (quotes 3.2.13 as "Truly, one becomes good through good deeds, and evil through evil deeds", translator not named, citing Tull 1989)]
- BU 4.4.5, as quoted in SEP-Pers (translator not named): "What a man turns out to be depends on how he acts and on how he conducts himself. If his actions are good, he will turn into something good… A man resolves in accordance with his desire, acts in accordance with his resolve, and turns out to be in accordance with his action." [source: SEP-Pers §1.1]
- BU 4.4.3, Olivelle (1998): "As a caterpillar, when it comes to the tip of a blade of grass, reaches out to a new foothold and draws itself onto it, so the self (atman), after it has knocked down this body… reaches out to a new foothold." [source: WP-Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad]
- **The five fires** (*pañcāgnividyā*; CU 5.3–10, BU 6.2.9–16). King Pravāhaṇa Jaivali teaches Uddālaka Āruṇi (after Śvetaketu fails the king's questions). The dead go as smoke to the other world (first fire) and become soma; then rain cloud (second) and rain; then earth (third) and food; then man (fourth) and semen; then woman (fifth) and an embryo. Those who know this go by the path of the gods to the world of brahman, and those who do not go by the path of the ancestors and are reborn. [source: IEP-Up §5]
- CU 5.10.7: those of pleasant conduct enter a "pleasant womb" (brahmin, kṣatriya or vaiśya), while those of foul conduct enter the womb of "a dog, a pig, or an outcaste". Black: karma here is "an impersonal moral force" that is "not directed by a divine being". [source: IEP-Up §5]
- The same teaching appears in the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (14.9.1), BU 6.2 and Kauṣītakī ch. 1. Deussen took its presence in several texts to mean that the idea is older than all of them. [source: WP-Chandogya_Upanishad]
- **A king teaching brahmins.** In the CU, Pravāhaṇa (a kṣatriya king of Pañcāla) says this knowledge had not previously reached brahmins (CU 5.3.7). [source: WP-Pravahana_Jaivali ("knowledge had never been in possession of the Brahmins"); WS-Chandogya (Nikhilananda and Lokeswarananda translations: the teaching belonged to the kṣatriyas alone — second-hand)]
- **Saṃsāra and mokṣa come late.** The term *saṃsāra* ("that which turns around forever") first appears in the comparatively late Kaṭha (3.7) and Śvetāśvatara (6.16). *Mokṣa* takes on the sense of liberation only in later texts (e.g. ŚU 6.16). The early Upaniṣads "generally do not contain the assumption that life is suffering (duḥkha), or illusion (māyā), or ignorance (avidyā)". They "focus much more attention on securing wealth, status, and power in this lifetime" and present life as desirable. Their common goal is immortality (*amṛta*, "not dying"), understood variously: long life, surviving death in heaven, becoming one with the essence of the universe, being preserved in social memory. [source: IEP-Up §5]
- In the Upaniṣads the aim of self-knowledge is "primarily freedom from death". Sāṃkhya-Yoga later calls liberation *kaivalya*, and Advaita calls it freedom from ignorance (*avidyā*). [source: IEP-Up §4]
- Karma and rebirth became shared ground for Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and, to an extent, Ājīvikas. The Cārvāka/Lokāyata materialists rejected karma and rebirth. [source: IEP-Up §5; WP-Karma]
- **Origin debate**: see Debates below.

## 8. Social setting: varṇa, kings, renouncers
- The four *varṇas* were Brahmins (priests), Kṣatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaiśyas and Śūdras. Later Vedic texts fixed their roles and status, and the hierarchy emerged with the later Vedic monarchies. [source: WP-Vedic_period]
- BU 1.4.11ff (as summarised from Olivelle 1998 pp. 49–51): brahman created the ruling power among the gods and then all the castes (brahmin, kṣatriya, vaiśya, śūdra) and dharma. [source: WP-Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad]
- CU 5.10.7 ties rebirth to varṇa (see §7). [source: IEP-Up §5]
- Yājñavalkya claims that through knowledge of the self one can become a brahmin (BU 4.4.23), and he assumes the knowers will be brahmin men. Knowledge of the self also leads to the life of an ascetic sage (*muni*) (BU 4.4.22). [source: IEP-Up §6]
- The Upaniṣads also contain stories that complicate birth-status. In CU 4.1–3 the poor "man with the cart" Raikva has the knowledge and the rich king Jānaśruti does not (the king is called *śūdra*). In CU 4.4 the boy Satyakāma Jābāla does not know his father, and the teacher Hāridrumata Gautama accepts him for his honesty. Present these as stories in the text; the WP glosses on them are interpretive. [source: WP-Chandogya_Upanishad]
- Kings appear as teachers of brahmins (Pravāhaṇa Jaivali; Ajātaśatru of Kāśi; Aśvapati Kaikeya). [source: WP-Pravahana_Jaivali; WP-Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad (Ajātaśatru and Bālāki Gārgya, BU 2.1)]
- Black's book subtitle names the cast: *Priests, Kings, and Women in the Early Upaniṣads*. Related studies (cited by IEP-Up) include Olivelle 2012, "Kings, Ascetics and Brahmins: the Socio-Political Context of Ancient Indian Religions", and Romila Thapar 1993, "Sacrifice, Surplus, and the Soul" (*History of Religions* 33.4). These were not read; cite only as further reading. [source: IEP-Up references]
- **Second urbanisation.** From around 600 BCE cities grew in the Ganges plain, and renouncers (*śramaṇas*) appeared, including the Buddha and Mahāvīra (consistent with Periergia's Classical India chapter). Bronkhorst places the śramaṇa culture in "Greater Magadha", east of the Ganges–Yamuna confluence, where "Kshatriyas were placed higher than Brahmins". [source: WP-Greater_Magadha; WP-Śramaṇa]

## 9. The Bhagavad Gītā
- It forms part of the Mahābhārata, in the Bhīṣma Parva (book 6). It has 18 chapters and 700 verses, numbered as Mahābhārata 6.25–42 or 6.23–40 depending on the recension. [source: WP-Bhagavad_Gita; IEP-BG (start of book 6)]
- Śaṅkara's 8th-century commentary states that the Gītā has 700 verses, "likely a deliberate declaration to prevent further insertions", and 700 has been the standard since. A tradition of 745 verses exists, but no such manuscript has been found. [source: WP-Bhagavad_Gita]
- **Date.** "Generally dated to the second or first century BCE". Van Buitenen: "ca. 200 BC is a likely date". Later (1st century CE) and earlier (400–500 BCE, Upadhyaya) estimates exist, and 200 BCE may mark a major revision. Basham argued for a date in or after the 3rd century BCE, after Buddhism, Jainism and Aśoka. Linguistically it is "Epic-Puranic Sanskrit", after Pāṇini (Sargeant). IEP-BG: the epic was "formulated at the start of the Common Era". [source: WP-Bhagavad_Gita; IEP-BG intro]
- **Authorship.** It is traditionally attributed to Vyāsa, whom scholars regard as mythical or symbolic. Many scholars argue it is a composite work. Basham saw three or more authors or compilers. McLeod: "impossible to link the Bhagavad Gita to a single author". [source: WP-Bhagavad_Gita]
- **Interpolation or integral? (a debate).** Ranganathan says it "appears to be an interpolation in the wider epic". Van Buitenen holds that it is so well knit into the Mahābhārata that it did not "somehow wander into the epic", and that it was conceived to "bring to a climax and solution the dharmic dilemma of a war". Fowler: its dating depends on which view one takes. [source: IEP-BG §5; WP-Bhagavad_Gita]
- **Setting.** Before the Kurukṣetra war between the Pāṇḍavas and their cousins the Kauravas, Arjuna asks his charioteer Kṛṣṇa (an avatar of Viṣṇu) to drive between the armies. Seeing kin and teachers on the other side (e.g. his granduncle Bhīṣma), he refuses to fight. [source: IEP-BG §§1, 6; WP-Bhagavad_Gita]
- **Synthesis.** WP calls the Gītā "a synthesis of various strands of Indian religious thought": Vedic dharma, Sāṃkhya-based yoga and *jñāna*, and *bhakti*. It "reconcil[es] renunciation with action". Scheepers: it is "a Brahmanical text that uses Shramanic and Yogic terminology to propagate the Brahmanic idea of living according to one's duty or dharma, in contrast to the ascetic ideal". Flood and Martin: it "rejects the Buddhist and Jain path of non-action, emphasizing instead renunciation of the fruits of action". Hiltebeitel: it is "the sealing achievement of the consolidation of Hinduism". The three yogas: *jñāna* (knowledge), *karma* (action), *bhakti* (devotion). [source: WP-Bhagavad_Gita]
- SEP-Bud: the Gītā is "classified by some orthodox schools as an Upaniṣad" and discusses two views of the self: a plurality of distinct selves, and one self of pure consciousness identical with Brahman. [source: SEP-Bud §1]
- Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja and Madhva all wrote commentaries on the Gītā. Yāmunācārya (traditional dates 916–1041 CE, Śrī Vaiṣṇava) divided it into three hexads (karma and jñāna yoga; bhakti yoga; metaphysics). [source: IEP-BG §§1–2]
- **Modern prominence.** Robinson: "the extraordinary prominence of the Bhagavad Gita is a feature of modernity". Sharpe dates the change to the 1880s, prominent after 1900; Arvind Sharma to the 1920s. It was read by Bankim Chandra Chatterjee, Tilak (karma yoga as nationalist activism), Vivekananda (the four yogas, a modern systematisation from the 1890s), Aurobindo and Gandhi. Gandhi called it "The Gospel of Selfless Action" and its philosophy *anāsakti yoga*. [source: WP-Bhagavad_Gita]
- Ranganathan: since colonial rule the Gītā has increasingly been treated as a stand-alone religious book, the "Hindu equivalent to the Christian Bible". He argues that projecting this back is a colonial artefact (Ranganathan's view). [source: IEP-BG §4]
- **First English translation:** Charles Wilkins, 1785, with an introduction by Warren Hastings. Translations into French followed (1787), then German and Russian. Callewaert and Hemraj (1982) counted 1,891 translations in 75 languages, including 273 in English. [source: WP-Bhagavad_Gita]
- Oppenheimer: the Trinity test led him to think "I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds", from Gītā 11.32. *Kālo 'smi loka-kṣaya-kṛt* literally means "time I am, the destroyer of the worlds". He recited it in a 1965 documentary. [source: WP-Bhagavad_Gita]

### The Gītā's argument for acting without attachment (for the step-by-step reconstruction)
- **Arjuna's case against fighting** (Gītā ch. 1, as analysed by Ranganathan, IEP-BG §8):
  - (A1) The war will kill kin on both sides, so there can be no joy in victory (1.34–36): a consequentialist argument.
  - (A2) Fighting would make him no better than the wicked Kauravas (1.38–39): a virtue argument.
  - (A3) War destroys law and order and endangers women and children (1.41): consequentialist or duty-based.
  - The issue, in Arvind Sharma's words: "Is it morally proper to kill?" [source: IEP-BG §8; WP-Bhagavad_Gita]
- **Kṛṣṇa's reply**, in steps (composite of IEP-BG and WP; verse numbers as given there):
  1. **The self cannot be killed.** Selves are eternal, so "no one kills anyone" (2.11–32). This undercuts the worry about the deaths of loved ones. [IEP-BG §9]
  2. **Svadharma.** Arjuna's own duty (*sva-dharma*) is that of a kṣatriya, a warrior (Hacker). Shrinking from it is also dishonourable (2.2–3, 2.33–37), and Kṛṣṇa says paradise awaits those who die fighting (2.36–37). Ranganathan reads these appeals as showing that virtue and consequence considerations cut both ways. [IEP-BG §9; WP-Bhagavad_Gita "Duty (svadharma)"]
  3. **Action is unavoidable.** Even refusing to act is a choice and an action (ch. 3). [IEP-BG §2 (ch. 3 summary); WP-Bhagavad_Gita (ch. 3 summary)]
  4. **What binds is attachment to fruits, not action.** One has a right to the action, not to its fruits (2.47). Yoga is defined as evenness (2.48) and as "skill in action" (2.50). [IEP-BG §2]
  5. **Better one's own duty imperfectly done than another's done well** (3.35; 18.47). Duty is "suited to one's nature" (18.41). [IEP-BG §§2, 10]
  6. **Offer the fruits to God (bhakti).** Do everything as devotion to Kṛṣṇa (9.27–33), who himself acts for "the welfare of the world" (*lokasaṃgraha*, 3.20–24). [IEP-BG §9]
  7. **Conclusion.** Arjuna should fight, as duty, without desire for its results. That is karma yoga, and it is compatible with liberation (*mokṣa*). [IEP-BG §9; WP-Bhagavad_Gita]
- Gandhi took the central concept to be *niṣkāmakarma*, acting without desire, linked to *sthitaprajña* ("still knowing"). He held that one should not even be attached to good works (Gandhi, *Collected Works* vol. 37). *Niṣkāma karma* is not a term used in the Gītā, but it is equivalent to *karma-phala-tyāga* (renunciation of the fruits of action). [source: IEP-BG §10; WP-Bhagavad_Gita]
- **Objection (caste duty; Ambedkar):**
  - B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956; born into the Mahar (Dalit) caste, treated as untouchable) criticised the Gītā for its stance on caste and for "defending certain dogmas of religion on philosophical grounds" (Maitra 2018). [source: WP-Bhagavad_Gita; WP-B._R._Ambedkar]
  - Klausen's summary of Ambedkar's essay "Krishna and his Gita": the Gītā is a "tool" of Brahmanical Hinduism, offering "a defence of the Kshatriya duty to make war and kill, the assertion that varna derives from birth rather than worth or aptitude", and the injunction to perform karma. [source: WP-Bhagavad_Gita]
  - The chapter is titled "Philosophic Defence of Counter-Revolution: Krishna and His Gita", part of the unfinished *Revolution and Counter-Revolution in Ancient India*, printed in *Dr. Babasaheb Ambedkar: Writings and Speeches*, ed. Vasant Moon, vol. 3 (Government of Maharashtra, 1987), from p. 365. [source: WS-Ambedkar (second-hand; Brandeis *CASTE* journal article citation)]
  - Ambedkar's argument as reported there: the Gītā says *cāturvarṇya* (the four-varṇa order) is created by God, and it also gives it "a philosophic basis… by linking it to the theory of innate, inborn qualities" (the *guṇas*). He found that basis weak, partly because three guṇas cannot ground four varṇas. [source: WS-Ambedkar (second-hand)]
  - D. D. Kosambi (*Myth and Reality*): "practically anything can be read into the Gita by a determined person, without denying the validity of a class system". He quotes Kṛṣṇa: "The four-caste (class) division has been created by Me" (Gītā 4.13). [source: WP-Bhagavad_Gita]
  - The general form of the objection, for the chapter: if duty is fixed by birth-rank and must be done regardless of outcome, "acting without attachment" can be used to sanctify a hierarchy and silence the very moral doubts (Arjuna's) that started the dialogue.
- **Replies:**
  - (R1) Universalist readings: the Gītā's own verses on the equality of all selves (5.18; 13.27–28: one who sees "the Supreme Lord dwelling equally in all beings… sees verily"). Vivekananda took *svadharma* to mean the duty of one's life situation or profession, not caste. Aurobindo took it to mean one's own nature and capacities. Dnyaneshwar (1275–1296) held the Gītā's lessons are for everyone. [source: WP-Bhagavad_Gita]
  - (R2) Ranganathan (IEP-BG): karma yoga ties duty to one's nature and to a "world of diversity", while bhakti and jñāna yoga yield "an insight into the radical equality of all persons (Gītā 5.18)". [source: IEP-BG §9]
  - (R3) Counter-reply: Jacqueline Hirst holds that the universalist neo-Hindu readings are modernist, though she also says the Gītā should be read as a unitary pedagogic text, "not a list of norms". Paul Hacker calls Bankim's reading an attempt to "universalize Hinduism". Scholars have contested Kosambi's criticism on the basis of the karma, bhakti and jñāna yoga sections (Nadkarni 2016). So the objection that the text as written ties svadharma to varṇa still stands against the text, even if the principle of detached action can be detached from it. [source: WP-Bhagavad_Gita]
- Ambedkar also wrote *Annihilation of Caste*, an undelivered 1936 speech for the Jat-Pat Todak Mandal in Lahore, which he self-published (on 15 May 1936, per WP-B._R._Ambedkar). It argued that the real way to break up caste was "to destroy the religious notions upon which caste is founded". He converted to Buddhism at Nagpur on 14 October 1956. [source: WP-Annihilation_of_Caste; WP-B._R._Ambedkar]

## 10. Reception: Persian, Latin, Schopenhauer
- Akbar's reign saw the first Persian translations of Upaniṣads. [source: WP-Upanishads]
- **Dārā Shikoh** (20 March 1615 – 30 August 1659) was the eldest son of Shah Jahan. He completed a Persian translation of fifty Upaniṣads, the *Sirr-i-Akbar* ("The Greatest Secret"). Date: 1657 (WP-Dara_Shikoh; WP-Sirr-i-Akbar "c. 1657"), but WP-Upanishads says 1656, so give "1656–57" or "c. 1657". [source: WP-Dara_Shikoh; WP-Sirr-i-Akbar; WP-Upanishads]
- IEP-Up: the *Sirr-i Akbar* was the first known translation of the Upaniṣads into a non-Indian language. Besides the Vedic Upaniṣads it included many yoga, renunciate and devotional Upaniṣads and the Puruṣa Sūkta. Dārā "considered the Upaniṣads to be the sources of Indian monotheism". He held that the "hidden book" (*kitāb maknūn*) of the Qur'an (56:78) is the Upaniṣads. [source: IEP-Up §10; WP-Sirr-i-Akbar; WP-Dara_Shikoh]
- Dārā was defeated by his brother Aurangzeb at Samugarh (1658) and executed in 1659 after a convocation declared him an apostate. [source: WP-Dara_Shikoh]
- **Anquetil-Duperron** (7 Dec 1731 – 17 Jan 1805) received the Persian translation from India in 1775 and translated it into French and Latin; the French version was never published. The Latin *Oupnek'hat, id est, Secretum tegendum* appeared in two volumes at Strasbourg in **1801–1802** (WP; SEP-Sch says 1801). **IEP-Up says 1804, which conflicts with the other sources; use 1801–02.** It was the first European-language translation of a major Hindu sacred text. [source: WP-Abraham_Hyacinthe_Anquetil-Duperron; WP-Upanishads; SEP-Sch; IEP-Up §10]
- The first English rendering of an Upaniṣad was H. T. Colebrooke's Aitareya, 1805. Rammohan Roy's Bengali Kena (1816) was the first in a modern Indian language, and Roy used his translations to promote reform and to criticise idolatry and caste hierarchy. [source: IEP-Up §10]
- **Schopenhauer** was introduced to Indian thought in late 1813 by Friedrich Majer in Weimar. Library records show he began reading the Bhagavadgītā in December 1813 or soon after, and the Upaniṣads (the *Oupnek'hat*) in March 1814. He read the Latin version of a Persian translation, not the Sanskrit. [source: SEP-Sch; WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer]
- He named Kant, Plato and the Upaniṣads as his most important influences. [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer; IEP-Up §10]
- In the preface to *The World as Will and Representation* (1818/1819) he called access to the Vedas through the Upaniṣads "the greatest privilege which this still young century (1818) may claim before all previous centuries". [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer (English translation quoted there; translator not named)]
- The famous "solace" passage is from *Parerga and Paralipomena* (1851), vol. II. Its section number is given as **§184** (German numbering, Diogenes edition, pp. 436–437) in a Springer chapter (Pathak 2017). The commonly quoted §185 is not supported. [source: WS-Schopenhauer (second-hand)]
- He kept the *Oupnekhat* open on his table and read it before bed (WP). SEP-Sch: in his Frankfurt years he "frequently read inspirational texts such as the Upanishads before going to sleep". [source: WP-Arthur_Schopenhauer; SEP-Sch]
- Schrödinger: "Their multiplicity is only apparent, in truth there is only one mind. This is the doctrine of the Upanishads." W. B. Yeats and Shri Purohit Swami published *The Ten Principal Upanishads* in 1938. [source: WP-Upanishads]

## 11. Consistency with published Periergia texts
- **History of Philosophy overview** (`philosophy--history-of-philosophy--overview.sql`):
  - It dates the Upanishads "c. 800–300 BCE". IEP-Up gives 700–300 BCE, and the Olivelle chronology puts the earliest (BU, CU) in the 7th–6th centuries BCE "give or take a century" and the latest verse Upaniṣads in the last centuries BCE (IEP: 300–100 BCE). ## Debates
- **Dating of the Upaniṣads.** The mainstream view (Olivelle) puts the BU and CU in the 7th–6th centuries BCE ± a century, pre-Buddhist, and the verse Upaniṣads in the last centuries BCE. Wider ranges run from 900 to 300 BCE (Phillips 800–300; Flood 600–300). Bronkhorst argues the BU was still being composed c. 2nd century BCE, and Wynne disputes this. There is no consensus even on which Upaniṣads are pre- or post-Buddhist. The method is internal: archaism, style, cross-text repetition and assumptions about how ideas developed. [source: WP-Upanishads; IEP-Up; WP-Greater_Magadha]
- **Origin of karma and rebirth.**
  - (a) Vedic/Brahmanical development: Yuvraj Krishan, *The Vedic Origins of the Doctrine of Karma* (1988); Tull 1989. Alexander Wynne argues the ideas "developed within the Vedic tradition rather than being borrowed", perhaps among unorthodox brahmins in the east.
  - (b) Non-Vedic or śramaṇa origin: Flood (1996): "It is very possible that the karmas and reincarnation entered the mainstream brahaminical thought from the sramana or the renouncer traditions."
  - (c) Bronkhorst's "Greater Magadha" thesis (2007): a non-Vedic Indo-Aryan culture east of the Ganges–Yamuna confluence, home of the Buddha and Mahāvīra, was the source of karma, rebirth and liberation. Its critics (per Norelius) question its sharp east–west divide, its claim of lesser Brahmanisation in Magadha, its revised chronology, and its neglect of socio-economic factors.
  - (d) O'Flaherty: a "composite structure" shaped by several strands.
  - The fact that the five-fire teaching is given by a kṣatriya king who says brahmins lacked it (CU 5.3.7) is often cited in this debate, but no named scholar's use of it was verified (see NOT VERIFIED). [source: WP-Karma; WP-Greater_Magadha]
- **What *tat tvam asi* means.** Identity of self and absolute (Śaṅkara, Advaita) or "that's how you are": the self shares the nature of the subtle essence (Brereton 1986, Olivelle, Doniger, Black, on grammatical and contextual grounds). Dvaita reads "you are not that", and Viśiṣṭādvaita reads the self as a mode or aspect of brahman. [source: WP-Mahāvākyas; IEP-Up §§4, 8]
- **Is ātman = brahman *the* Upanishadic doctrine?** The traditional and Vedāntic view says yes. Black says it is one teaching among several; Uddālaka never uses "brahman", and the meaning of identity is unclear even in Śāṇḍilya. WP: older Upaniṣads treat ātman as part of brahman, and younger ones identify them (Koller). [source: IEP-Up §4; WP-Upanishads]
- **Are the Upaniṣads philosophy?** Some say no, because they lack a unified system and argue by analogy. Black: their diversity reflects competing schools, and they contain implicit methods (introspection, observation) and a debate culture. Cross-reference the Periergia overview's "philosophy, full stop" stance. [source: IEP-Up §§2, 9]
- **Women in the Upaniṣads.** Are Gārgī and Maitreyī evidence of women philosophers in Vedic India, or literary devices? Doniger and Pechilis differ on Maitreyī. Lindquist (2008) reframes the question around their literary role. Black notes both the androcentric framing and the women's real participation in the dialogues. [source: WP-Maitreyi; IEP-Up §6; WS-Lindquist]
- **The Gītā: date, unity and caste.** It is dated from the 5th century BCE to the 1st century CE, with most scholars placing it in the 2nd–1st centuries BCE. Scholars dispute whether it is an interpolation (Ranganathan) or integral to the epic (van Buitenen), and whether it is a composite (Basham, Minor). On caste, critics (Ambedkar, Kosambi, Narla) see a defence of birth-based varṇa, while universalist readers (Vivekananda, Aurobindo, Dnyaneshwar) disagree; Hirst calls the latter modernist. Ranganathan reads the Gītā as a philosophical argument for a "procedural" ethics, which is not the mainstream reading. [source: WP-Bhagavad_Gita; IEP-BG]
- **Etymology of "upaniṣad".** The popular gloss is "sitting down near" a teacher, but IEP (Black) and Doniger et al. say the original sense is "connection, equivalence", and in context often "secret teaching". [source: IEP-Up §2; WP-Upanishads]
