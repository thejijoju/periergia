## 1. Kinds of non-deductive (ampliative) inference
- WP-Inductive_reasoning lists the types of inductive reasoning as: generalization, prediction, statistical syllogism, argument from analogy, causal inference. [source: WP-Inductive_reasoning]
- **Inductive generalisation**: from a sample to a population: "The proportion Q of the sample has attribute A. Therefore, the proportion Q of the population has attribute A." Strength depends on sample size and representativeness (random sampling); weak example: "Six of the ten people in my book club are Libertarians. Therefore, about 60% of people are Libertarians." [source: WP-Inductive_reasoning, citing Govier 2013]
- **Simple enumerative induction** (Henderson's "inference I"): "All observed instances of A have been B. / The next instance of A will be B." Hume's own example: bread of a particular appearance has always nourished, so the next such bread will nourish. [source: SEP-IND §2]
- **Statistical syllogism** (also "proportional syllogism", "direct inference"): from a generalisation true for the most part to a particular case. Form: X proportion of F are G; I is an F; so I is a G. Example: "Almost all people are taller than 26 inches; Gareth is a person; therefore, Gareth is taller than 26 inches." Problem: the **reference class problem** — an individual belongs to many classes with different proportions; which to use? Henry Kyburg urged its importance. [source: WP-Statistical_syllogism]
- **Argument from analogy**: P and Q share properties a, b, c; P has x; so Q probably has x. Mill, *System of Logic*: "[t]here can be no doubt that every resemblance [not known to be irrelevant] affords some degree of probability, beyond what would otherwise exist, in favor of the conclusion." Requires checking the *relevance* of the shared features. [source: WP-Inductive_reasoning]
- **Abduction / IBE**: SEP's opening example: you know Tim and Harry had a terrible row that ended their friendship; someone saw them jogging together; the best explanation is that they made up; so you conclude they are friends again. [source: SEP-ABD §1]
- SEP notes "abduction" has two senses: Peirce's (generating hypotheses, context of discovery) and the modern one (justifying hypotheses = IBE). [source: SEP-ABD intro]
- Statistical data alone do not make an inference inductive: inferring "all elephants are gray" because that best explains observing only gray elephants is abductive in form. Harman (1965) "conceives induction as a special type of abduction". [source: SEP-ABD §1.1]
- Aristotle's word for the move from particular to universal was *epagōgē*, which Cicero translated as *inductio*. [source: WP-Inductive_reasoning, citing Gattei 2009]
- Consistency: Periergia's *Arguments* chapter says Govier attacked "the hallowed old distinction between inductive and deductive arguments" (1987) and Shanahan proposes dropping it, and promises that *this* chapter "takes up that side". The writer should briefly acknowledge the contested distinction (and that WP-Problem_of_induction warns that deduction/induction is not "general to specific vs specific to general"). [source: supabase/content/philosophy--logic-and-reasoning--arguments.sql; WP-Problem_of_induction]

## 2. Hume's problem (Treatise 1739, Enquiry 1748)
- Hume introduces the problem within his analysis of cause and effect; causation is the only relation by which "we can go beyond the evidence of our memory and senses" (E 4.1.4; T 1.3.2.3). Example: gunpowder and explosion. [source: SEP-IND §1]
- Inference schema Hume describes: "I have found that such an object has always been attended with such an effect, and I foresee, that other objects, which are, in appearance, similar, will be attended with similar effects." (E 4.2.16) [source: SEP-IND §1]
- Treatise statement of the principle: "if Reason determin'd us, it would proceed upon that principle that instances, of which we have had no experience, must resemble those, of which we have had experience, and that the course of nature continues always uniformly the same." (T 1.3.6.4) Henderson calls this the **Uniformity Principle (UP)**, also "Resemblance Principle" or "Principle of Uniformity of Nature". [source: SEP-IND §1]
- Hume's fork: "All reasonings may be divided into two kinds, namely, demonstrative reasoning, or that concerning relations of ideas, and moral reasoning, or that concerning matter of fact and existence." (E 4.2.18) [source: SEP-IND §1]
- First horn: "it implies no contradiction that the course of nature may change, and that an object seemingly like those which we have experienced, may be attended with different or contrary effects." (E 4.2.18) [source: SEP-IND §1]
- Second horn: probable reasoning "proceed[s] upon the supposition, that the future will be conformable to the past" (E 4.2.19); so using it would be "taking that for granted, which is the very point in question" (E 4.2.19; cf. T 1.3.6.7). [source: SEP-IND §1; SEP-HUME §2.3 gives EHU 4.19]
- Treatise conclusion: "even after experience has inform'd us of their constant conjunction, 'tis impossible for us to satisfy ourselves by our reason, why we shou'd extend that experience beyond those particular instances, which have fallen under our observation." (T 1.3.6.11) [source: SEP-IND §1]
- **Henderson's step-by-step reconstruction** (P = premise, C = conclusion): P1 only two kinds of arguments, demonstrative and probable (Hume's fork); P2 inference I presupposes the UP; P3 a demonstrative argument establishes a conclusion whose negation is a contradiction; P4 the negation of the UP is not a contradiction; C1 no demonstrative argument for UP; P5 any probable argument for UP presupposes UP; P6 an argument for a principle may not presuppose it (non-circularity); C2 no probable argument for UP; C3 no argument for UP; P7 no argument for UP → no chain of reasoning from premises to conclusion of any inference presupposing UP; C4 no chain of reasoning for inference I; P8 no chain of reasoning → not justified; C5 inference I is not justified. [source: SEP-IND §2]
- **Do not repeat** the 8-step version already printed in *Berkeley and Hume* verbatim; that chapter already gives an 8-step version of Henderson's argument, the "course of nature may change" quotation, custom/habit, Garrett/Owen vs Winkler/Millican, and short treatments of Popper, Strawson, Reichenbach and Goodman. This chapter should instead use Henderson's fuller P1–P8/C1–C5 form *or* a short recap with cross-reference, then go deeper on the answers. [source: supabase/content/philosophy--history-of-philosophy--early-modern--berkeley-and-hume.sql]
- Interpretive issue on "demonstrative/probable": Salmon (1966) equates them with deductive/inductive; Okasha (2001) with "deductively valid with a priori premises" vs "having an empirical premise"; Owen (1999) and Garrett (2002) argue the distinction in Hume's context has little to do with deductive form. Henderson adopts: first horn rules out a priori reasoning, second rules out empirical arguments. [source: SEP-IND §2]
- SEP-HUME's version: Hume considers four possible foundations (intuition, demonstration, sensation, probable argument) and rejects each (EHU 4.16, 4.18, 4.19). [source: SEP-HUME §2.3]
- Hume's "sceptical solution": custom or habit, "a propensity to renew the same act of operation without the intervention of any reasoning" (EHU 5.5); custom is among "a species of natural instincts, which no reasoning or process of the thought and understanding is able, either to produce, or to prevent" (E 5.1.8). He speaks of a "pre-established harmony" between nature's course and our ideas (E 5.2.21). [source: SEP-HUME §2.3; SEP-IND §1]
- Hume on living with it: "Nature will always maintain her rights, and prevail in the end over any abstract reasoning whatsoever." (E 5.1.2) [source: SEP-IND §6]
- Hume gave "rules by which to judge of causes and effects" (T 1.3.15), eight rules, e.g. cause and effect contiguous; cause prior; constant union; same cause always same effect. [source: IEP-IND §2a; SEP-IND §2]
- Publication: Treatise Books 1–2 anonymously 1739, Book 3 in 1740; the first Enquiry 1748, initially titled *Philosophical Essays concerning Human Understanding*, renamed in the 1758 edition. [source: SEP-HUME §1]
- Editions used by SEP: Treatise ed. D.F. & M.J. Norton (OUP 2000); Enquiry ed. T.L. Beauchamp (OUP 1999 per SEP-HUME); Selby-Bigge/Nidditch (1975) is the alternative standard edition. Citation formats differ: Henderson cites E 4.2.18 (section.part.paragraph); SEP-HUME cites EHU 4.18 (section.paragraph). Same passage. [source: SEP-HUME bibliography; SEP-IND bibliography]
- Naming: in Russell's *Problems of Philosophy* (1912) the chapter on induction makes no reference to Hume; Psillos says hardly anyone links Hume's name to "the Problem of Induction" before the Cambridge Apostles, except John Venn. (Consistent with *Berkeley and Hume*: Millican notes Hume never uses "induction" for this argument.) [source: IEP-IND §1]
- Popper distinguished Hume's **logical** problem (are we justified in reasoning from instances experienced to unexperienced ones?) and **psychological** problem (why do reasonable people nonetheless expect this?); Hume answered the first negatively and the second by custom (Popper, *Objective Knowledge*, 1972). [source: IEP-IND §5c]

## 3. Earlier versions (keep brief; cross-reference)
- **Sextus Empiricus, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* II.204** (trans. R. G. Bury, Loeb, 1933, p. 283): "It is also easy, I consider, to set aside the method of induction. For, when they propose to establish the universal from the particulars by means of induction, they will effect this by a review either of all or of some of the particular instances. But if they review some, the induction will be insecure, since some of the particulars omitted in the induction may contravene the universal; while if they are to review all, they will be toiling at the impossible, since the particulars are infinite and indefinite. Thus on both grounds, as I think, the consequence is that induction is invalidated." [source: WP-Problem_of_induction; SEP Sextus entry lists "PH II 204: Induction"]
- Sextus's worry concerns the gap between premises and conclusion, not Hume's circularity; R. Weintraub (1995, *Philosophical Quarterly*) argues Hume's strategy is an application of Sextus's criterion (regress) argument. Weintraub's thesis: "Hume's most important legacy is the supposition that the justification of induction is not analogous to that of deduction." [source: WP-Problem_of_induction]
- Sextus dated "second or third century CE" in *Hellenistic Philosophy* (published). [source: supabase/content/...hellenistic-philosophy.sql]
- **Cārvāka**: inference requires knowledge of *vyāpti* (invariable concomitance); perception cannot establish it; testimony "presupposes that a speaker would tell the truth" and fails likewise. The Nyāya/Gaṅgeśa pragmatic reply: "The skeptic himself relies on such generalizations when he opens his mouth to voice his skepticism, by using words with repeatable meanings." Phillips: "The Cārvāka argument identifying the problem of induction is turned by both Buddhist and Nyāya philosophers into an argument for fallibilism about inference." [source: SEP-EPI-IND §2] — Full step-by-step version (including *upādhi*, wet fuel, regress, revisionist reading via Purandara/Bhattacharya) is already published in *Jains, Ājīvikas and Materialists: India's Other Dissenters*: cross-reference, do not repeat. [source: supabase/content/...indian-dissenters.sql]
- **Jayarāśi Bhaṭṭa** (9th c.) attacked all means of knowledge including inference (cross-ref same chapter). [source: WP-Problem_of_induction]
- **al-Ghazālī**: published chapter *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes and the Quarrel over Reason* (exact title in the published SQL) covers the seventeenth discussion of the *Incoherence* (c. 1095, trans. Marmura): "The connection between what is habitually believed to be a cause and what is habitually believed to be an effect is not necessary, according to us." It already discusses the Hume comparison (similar on one point, differently motivated, no documented evidence Hume knew him). Cross-reference only. [source: supabase/content/...ghazali-and-averroes.sql]
- Medieval: al-Ghazālī and Ockham connected the problem with God's absolute power; Duns Scotus held induction justified by "a proposition reposing in the soul, 'Whatever occurs in a great many instances by a cause that is not free, is the natural effect of that cause.'" (trans. Wolter 1962; via Franklin, *The Science of Conjecture*, 2001). [source: WP-Problem_of_induction]
- **Russell's chicken** — *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912), ch. VI "On Induction": "Domestic animals expect food when they see the person who usually feeds them. We know that all these rather crude expectations of uniformity are liable to be misleading. The man who has fed the chicken every day throughout its life at last wrings its neck instead, showing that more refined views as to the uniformity of nature would have been useful to the chicken." [source: WP-Problem_of_induction (quotation); WS (ch. VI "On Induction", 1912)] Note: Russell's animal is a chicken, not a turkey (the "inductivist turkey" is a later variant — popularly attributed to Russell; not verified, do not use).
- Russell (1912) distinguished the causal fact that repetition makes us expect recurrence ("The frequent repetition of some uniform succession or coexistence has been a cause of our expecting the same succession or coexistence on the next occasion") from the question whether there is "any reasonable ground for giving weight" to such expectations. He stated a "Principle of Induction": (1) the more cases of A associated with B (no known failures), the more probable that A is always associated with B; (2) enough cases make it nearly certain; he held it can neither be proved nor disproved by experience and must be accepted on its intrinsic evidence. [source: IEP-IND §1, §4d.iii]

## 4. Attempted answers to Hume
### 4.1 Inductive justification of induction (Black, Braithwaite) and circularity
- Max Black, "Self-Supporting Inductive Arguments" (*Journal of Philosophy* 55, 1958): rule R = "Most instances of A's examined in a wide variety of conditions have been B; hence (probably) The next A to be encountered will be B"; argument: "In most instances of the use of R in arguments with true premises examined in a wide variety of conditions, R has been successful. Hence (probably): In the next instance to be encountered of the use of R in an argument with a true premise, R will be successful." (1958: 719–20) [source: IEP-IND §5b]
- R. B. Braithwaite, *Scientific Explanation* (Cambridge, 1953) distinguished **premise-circularity** (conclusion among the premises: vicious) from **rule-circularity**: "the circularity involved in the use of a principle of inference being justified by the truth of a proposition which can only be established by the use of the same principle of inference" (1953: 276). Others using such arguments: van Cleve (1984), Papineau (1992), Psillos (1999). [source: IEP-IND §5b.i]
- Defence: even modus ponens can be justified only rule-circularly (Dummett 1974; Lewis Carroll's Achilles and the Tortoise, 1895). Lange: "all that we can reasonably demand of a fundamental form of reasoning is that it endorse itself" (2011: 56). Ramsey noted that only via memory can we check memory. [source: SEP-IND §4.1; IEP-IND §5b]
- Objection (Salmon 1963): **counter-induction** ("Most observed As are Bs; therefore it is not the case that most As are Bs") supports itself the same way: most CI arguments have failed, so (by CI) many will succeed. Reply (Papineau): the argument is "not supposed to do very much"; it cannot persuade a sceptic or counterinductivist but is a consistency check. Psillos's reply: a "Fair-Treatment Principle" — rely on rules unless there are specific reasons to doubt them; there are many against counter-induction. [source: SEP-IND §4.1; IEP-IND §5b.ii]
- "No rules" / regress: Okasha (2001, 2005) and Norton (material theory of induction, 2003, 2021) deny a single UP; each induction rests on specific empirical presuppositions, turning circularity into a regress; Hume's argument charged with a "quantifier shift fallacy" (Sober 1988; Okasha 2005). Okasha: "Perhaps infinite regresses are less bad than vicious circles after all" (2005b: 253). Example of UP's vagueness: all my birthdays so far I have been under 40, which does not give reason to expect I will be under 40 on the next. [source: SEP-IND §4.2]
### 4.2 Reichenbach's pragmatic vindication
- Hans Reichenbach (1891–1953), *Experience and Prediction* (1938). He thought Hume's argument unassailable but offered a weaker "vindication" (term used by Feigl 1950, Salmon 1963). [source: SEP-IND §5.3; SEP-REICH; WP-Hans_Reichenbach]
- Analogy: physician: "I do not know whether an operation will save the man, but if there is any remedy, it is an operation" (1938 [2006: 349]). Aim of induction: "to find series of events whose frequency of occurrence converges towards a limit" (1938 [2006: 350]). The **straight rule**: posit that the limiting frequency is close to the observed frequency m/n. If a limit exists, the straight rule will eventually find it; if none exists, no method succeeds. Fisherman analogy: "Shall he cast his net? ... It is preferable to try even in uncertainty than not to try and be certain of getting nothing." (1938 [2006: 362–363]) [source: SEP-IND §5.3]
- Objections: infinitely many other rules (straight rule plus any correction c_n → 0) also converge (Salmon 1966: 53; Reichenbach acknowledged); Reichenbach replied the straight rule is simplest and least risky; it offers no short-run guidance (BonJour 1998; Salmon 1966); Lange: the fisherman argument "presumes that there is no cost to trying". [source: SEP-IND §5.3]
- Descendants: formal learning theory (Kelly, Schulte, Steel 2010) and Schurz's meta-induction (2008–2019). [source: SEP-IND §5.4–5.5]
### 4.3 Strawson's ordinary-language dissolution
- P. F. Strawson, *Introduction to Logical Theory* (London: Methuen, 1952), final section "The 'Justification' of Induction". Demanding a deductive justification is "a demand that induction shall be shown to be really a kind of deduction". [source: IEP-IND §5a; SEP-IND §5.2]
- Legal analogy: "it makes no sense to inquire in general whether the law of the land, the legal system as a whole, is or is not legal. For to what legal standards are we appealing?" (1952: 257) [source: SEP-IND §5.2]
- "It is an analytic proposition that it is reasonable to have a degree of belief in a statement which is proportional to the strength of the evidence in its favour; and it is an analytic proposition, though not a proposition of mathematics, that, other things being equal, the evidence for a generalisation is strong in proportion as the number of favourable instances, and the variety of circumstances in which they have been found, is great. ... Doing this is what 'being reasonable' means in such a context." (1952: 256–57) [source: SEP-IND §5.2]
- Psillos's reconstruction (IEP): (1) rational → degree of belief proportional to evidence; (2) → belief in a generalisation proportioned to number and variety of favourable instances; (3) → applying inductive method; (C) if someone is rational, they apply inductive methodology. [source: IEP-IND §5a]
- Objection (BonJour 1998: 198): we want to know whether inductive conclusions are reasonable "in the sense that ... there is reason to think that it is likely to be true"; conformity to standards does not show that. Strawson himself conceded whether "induction will continue to be successful" is a "contingent, factual matter" (1952: 262). [source: SEP-IND §5.2]
- Related: Wittgenstein (*PI* §481) and "hinges"; Crispin Wright (2004) "entitlement" to the UP; Russell (1948) five postulates; Salmon: unjustified postulates make scientific method "a matter of faith" (1966: 48). [source: SEP-IND §5.1]
### 4.4 Popper: abandoning induction (cross-ref *Logical Positivism and Its Critics*)
- Popper held the problem insoluble but denied science uses induction: bold conjectures, attempted falsification by modus tollens (*Logik der Forschung* 1935 [Eng. *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* 1959]). Objection: among unrefuted rival hypotheses, scientists prefer one as better supported; "corroboration" seems to take him away from pure deductivism (Salmon 1981). [source: SEP-IND §6] Popper: "Induction—the formation of a belief by repetition—is a myth" (*Objective Knowledge*, 1972). [source: IEP-IND §5c]
- Consistency: published *Logical Positivism* chapter dates *Logik der Forschung* "printed in 1934 with the imprint 1935" and already gives Salmon's and Urbach's corroboration objections; SEP-IND cites "1935 [1959]". Use "1934 (dated 1935)" for consistency. [source: supabase/content/...logical-positivism.sql]
### 4.5 Other answers (brief)
- Synthetic a priori (deny P3); nomological-explanatory solution via IBE (Armstrong 1983, BonJour 1998, Foster 2004): Armstrong: "To infer to the best explanation is part of what it is to be rational. If that is not rational, what is?" (1983: 59). Objection: Harman (1968) treats induction as a special case of IBE, so IBE lacks independence; and a time-restricted law could be an equally good explanation (Foster replies such laws are "more mysterious"). [source: SEP-IND §3.1–3.2]
- No-Free-Lunch theorems (Wolpert 1992–97) read as a version of Hume's first horn: with a uniform distribution over all possible sequences any learning algorithm expects generalisation error 1/2 (Schurz 2021b); machine learning requires "inductive bias" (Mitchell 1997). [source: SEP-IND §3.4]

## 5. Mill's methods (cross-ref *Bentham, Mill and Utilitarianism*)
- *A System of Logic* (1843). Mill held enumerative induction "the sole principle by which we are justified in inferring unobserved facts"; he rejected the independent validity of the "Hypothetical Method" (what is now IBE): a hypothesis "is not to be received probably true because it accounts for all the known phenomena; since this is a condition sometimes fulfilled tolerably well by two conflicting hypotheses." (System VII: 500) [source: SEP-MILL §3.2]
- SEP-MILL: four methods (System VII: 388–406): Agreement; Difference (with a parenthetical "Joint Method of Agreement and Difference"); Residues; Concomitant Variations. WP: "five methods" (Agreement, Difference, Joint, Residues, Concomitant Variation). Count varies 4/5 — consistent with the published Utilitarianism chapter's note. [source: SEP-MILL §3.3; WP-Mill's_methods]
- Method of Agreement (Mill's wording): "If two or more instances of the phenomenon under investigation have only one circumstance in common, the circumstance in which alone all the instances agree, is the cause (or effect) of the given phenomenon." (System, 1843, vol. 1, p. 454) Schema: A B C D with w x y z; A E F G with w t u v; so A is cause or effect of w. [source: WP-Mill's_methods]
- Mill's initiating validation of induction parallels his "proof" of utility: we adopt induction "spontaneously" and on inspection it seems reasonable; scientific induction corrects narrower generalisations by wider ones (System VII: 317–319). [source: SEP-MILL §3.2–3.3]
- Matilal (1998) compared the Indian anvaya/vyatireka (co-presence/co-absence) method with Mill's Method of Agreement and Difference. [source: SEP-DHK]

## 6. Hempel's raven paradox
- Carl Gustav Hempel (1905–1997); "Studies in the Logic of Confirmation" I and II, *Mind* 54 (1945), pp. 1–26 and 97–121 (also Hempel 1937, 1943). Built on Jean Nicod (1924). [source: WP-Raven_paradox; WP-Carl_Gustav_Hempel; SEP-CONF §1]
- Set-up: (1) "All ravens are black" is equivalent by contraposition to (2) "If something is not black, then it is not a raven." **Nicod's criterion**: an F that is G confirms "All F are G". **Equivalence condition**: evidence confirming a hypothesis confirms any logically equivalent one. A green apple (non-black non-raven) confirms (2), hence (1). Goodman called this the prospect of "indoor ornithology" (1955: 71). [source: WP-Raven_paradox; SEP-CONF §1.2]
- Hempel accepted the paradoxical conclusion: the appearance of paradox comes from tacitly using background information. His example: "All sodium salts burn yellow"; holding a piece of pure ice in a colourless flame that does not turn yellow confirms the hypothesis, but seems irrelevant only because we already know the substance is ice and ice contains no sodium salt. [source: WP-Raven_paradox, quoting Hempel 1945: 19–20]
- Bayesian reply (I. J. Good 1960, *BJPS*): a non-black non-raven gives positive but tiny support when non-black objects vastly outnumber ravens; a black raven gives more. SEP-CONF: under plausible conditions a black raven confirms, a non-black non-raven gives zero or negligible support (results by Fitelson and Hawthorne 2010). Chihara: "there is no such thing as *the* Bayesian solution". [source: WP-Raven_paradox; SEP-CONF §3]
- Good (1967) "The White Shoe is a Red Herring": with some background knowledge a black raven can *disconfirm* "all ravens are black"; Hempel (1967) "The White Shoe—No Red Herring" replied that confirmation must be judged without background information. [source: WP-Raven_paradox]

## 7. Goodman's new riddle
- Nelson Goodman (1906–1998). First grue-like example in "A Query on Confirmation" (1946, *Journal of Philosophy*), with "V-E day" as the time. *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast*: University of London, Athlone Press, **1954**; Harvard University Press **1955**; 4th ed. Harvard 1983. Most sources (and the brief) say 1955; give "1954/55" or "1955 (first published London 1954)". [source: WP-Nelson_Goodman; WP-New_riddle_of_induction; SEP-GOOD bibliography]
- Goodman's motivation: confirmation is not syntactic: "This piece of copper conducts electricity" confirms "All pieces of copper conduct electricity", but "This man in the room is a third son" does not confirm "All men in this room are third sons". Lawlike vs accidental generalisations. [source: SEP-GOOD §5.3; IEP-IND §5d]
- **Definition of "grue"** (SEP paraphrase of Goodman): the predicate "applies to all things examined before some future time t just in case they are green but to other things (observed at or after t) just in case they are blue": x is grue =df x is examined before t and green ∨ x is not so examined and blue. "Bleen": examined before t and blue, or not so examined and green. [source: SEP-GOOD §5.3; WS (Norton quotes "Grue applies to all thing examined before t just in case they are green")] CAUTION: the common gloss "green before t and blue after t" suggests emeralds change colour; Goodman's definition turns on *when an object is examined*, and no emerald changes colour.
- The riddle step by step: (1) every emerald examined before t was green; (2) each such emerald was therefore also grue; (3) the evidence confirms "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" to the same degree; (4) these yield incompatible predictions for emeralds first examined after t (green vs blue); (5) infinitely many grue-like predicates give further incompatible predictions; so the problem is to say which predicates are **projectible**. [source: SEP-GOOD §5.3]
- Objection 1: grue is positional/temporal/disjunctive (Carnap 1947 proposed purely qualitative predicates are projectible). Reply (Goodman): relative to language — with grue and bleen as primitives, green = examined before t and grue, or not so examined and bleen; "qualitativeness is an entirely relative matter" (1955: 80). [source: SEP-GOOD §5.3; IEP-IND §5d; WP-New_riddle_of_induction]
- Swinburne's reply: green can be checked without knowing the time; grue cannot (qualitative vs locational predicates). [source: WP-New_riddle_of_induction]
- Goodman's solution: **entrenchment** — "green" has been projected in far more hypotheses than "grue"; a hypothesis is projectible "iff it is supported, unviolated, and unexhausted, and all hypotheses conflicting with it are overridden" (FFF 108). Like Hume, a description of how we choose, not an ultimate justification. [source: SEP-GOOD §5.4]
- Goodman also called the "old" problem a pseudo-problem: rules and inferences are justified by mutual adjustment, a "virtuous" circle. [source: IEP-IND §5d]
- Objections to entrenchment: "a crude measure" (Teller 1969); conservative — "Science could never confirm anything new". [source: IEP-IND §5d]
- **Quine, "Natural Kinds"** (in *Ontological Relativity and Other Essays*, 1969; also in Rescher ed., *Essays in Honor of Carl G. Hempel*, 1970): asks "what tends to confirm an induction?"; answer: similarity/sameness of kind; green emeralds form a natural kind, grue ones do not; kind and similarity are "substantially one notion"; Darwinian evolution gives some reason to think our innate similarity space tracks natural similarities; mature science replaces the general notion with specific ones. He links Goodman's riddle to Hempel's ravens. [source: SEP-NK §1.2.1; WP-New_riddle_of_induction]

## 8. Probability: interpretations
- Russell epigraph (1929 lecture, cited in Bell 1945): "Probability is the most important concept in modern science, especially as nobody has the slightest notion what it means." [source: SEP-PROB epigraph] (second-hand; mark as "as quoted".)
- **Classical** (de Moivre, Laplace; inchoate in Pascal, Bernoulli, Huygens, Leibniz): Laplace (1814): "The theory of chances consists in reducing all events of the same kind to a certain number of equally possible cases ... The ratio of this number to that of all possible cases is the measure of this probability". Objection: "equally possible" is a category mistake or circular; finessed by the **principle of indifference** (Keynes's coinage, 1921); Bertrand's paradoxes (1889) show it gives inconsistent answers. [source: SEP-PROB §3.1]
- **Logical/evidential** (Keynes 1921, Johnson, Jeffreys, Carnap 1950). [source: SEP-PROB §3.2; SEP-IND §3.3]
- **Frequency**: finite frequentism (Venn 1876: "probability is nothing but that proportion"); limiting/hypothetical (Venn, Reichenbach 1949, von Mises 1957). Problem: a coin tossed once has frequency 0 or 1; the reference class problem. [source: SEP-PROB §3.4]
- **Propensity**: Popper (1957, 1959) for single-case probabilities in quantum mechanics (e.g. a radium atom), though Peirce anticipated it (1910): the die has a "would-be", "quite analogous to any habit that a man might have". [source: SEP-PROB §3.5]
- **Subjective/Bayesian**: De Morgan (1847): "By degree of probability, we really mean, or ought to mean, degree of belief". Ramsey: probability as "the logic of partial belief". [source: SEP-PROB §3.3.1]
- **Frank Ramsey** (born Cambridge 22 Feb 1903; died 19 Jan 1930, aged 26): "Truth and Probability" written 1926, **not published in his lifetime**; first printed in *The Foundations of Mathematics and other Logical Essays*, ed. R. B. Braithwaite (1931), ch. VII, pp. 156–198. It began from criticisms of Keynes, giving a subjective theory of probability and a pragmatic view of induction. [source: SEP-RAM §1; WP-Frank_P._Ramsey]
- **Bruno de Finetti** (13 Jun 1906, Innsbruck – 20 Jul 1985, Rome): classic statement "La prévision: ses lois logiques, ses sources subjectives" (1937); his representation theorem for **exchangeable** sequences; known in Anglo-American statistics from the 1950s through L. J. Savage. [source: WP-Bruno_de_Finetti; SEP-IND §3.3] Exchangeability "may be seen as a natural formalization of Hume's assumption that the past resembles the future". [source: SEP-IND §3.3]
- De Finetti's betting definition (1980 trans.): your degree of belief in E is p iff p units is the price at which you would buy or sell a bet paying 1 if E, 0 if not. [source: SEP-PROB §3.3.2]

## 9. Bayes's theorem, conditionalisation, Dutch books, priors
- Thomas Bayes (c. 1701 – 7 Apr 1761), Presbyterian minister at Tunbridge Wells, FRS 1742. "An Essay towards Solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances", communicated by Richard Price "in a Letter to John Canton", read to the Royal Society in 1763 and printed in *Philosophical Transactions* vol. 53 (the volume for 1763), pp. 370–418; WP-Thomas_Bayes says publication "the following year", and SEP cites it as "Bayes 1764". Say "read in 1763, published 1763/64". [source: WP-Thomas_Bayes; WP-An_Essay...; SEP-IND bibliography]
- Price prepared it for publication with amendments, wrote an introduction, and was elected FRS in 1765 partly for this. Contemporary reprints carried the title *A Method of Calculating the Exact Probability of All Conclusions Founded on Induction* (Stigler 2013). [source: WP-An_Essay...; WP-Richard_Price]
- Possibly written in response to Hume's *Enquiry* (1748): "it is possible" (Zabell 1989: 290–93). [source: SEP-IND §3.3] Keep hedged.
- Bayes's rule: p(H|E) = p(E|H) p(H) / p(E); p(H) prior, p(H|E) posterior, p(E|H) likelihood; updating by it is "conditionalisation". [source: SEP-IND §3.3]
- Conditionalization (Lin): after learning E (and nothing stronger): zero the possibilities incompatible with E; rescale the rest to sum to 1; "The essence of conditionalization is preservation of certain ratios of credences". Probabilism + Conditionalization = "the two core norms". [source: SEP-BAYES §1.2] Formal: P2(X) = P1(X|E), provided P1(E) > 0. [source: SEP-PROB §3.3.4]
- Laplace's **rule of succession** (1814): with a uniform prior, after n_w white in N draws, probability next is white = (n_w + 1)/(N + 2); 90 white of 100 → 91/102 ≈ 0.89. [source: SEP-IND §3.3] Laplace applied it to sunrise (sunrise problem); WP article flagged for original research — avoid details.
- **Dutch book**: "a series of bets bought and sold at prices that collectively guarantee loss, however the world turns out" (Hájek). Ramsey: if anyone's mental condition violated the probability laws, "his choice would depend on the precise form in which the options were offered him, which would be absurd". Converse theorem (Kemeny 1955): coherent credences cannot be Dutch-booked. Hájek's caveat: the theorem alone does not show incoherence is the only route to Dutch-book vulnerability. [source: SEP-PROB §3.3.2]
- Worked Dutch book (Lin): credence 0.75 in A and 0.30 in not-A; buy "win $100 if A" for $75 and "win $100 if not-A" for $30; net −$5 whatever happens. [source: SEP-BAYES §1.6]
- **Problem of the priors**: probabilism and conditionalization alone do not say whether, after 100 black ravens, credence that the next raven is black should rise; some coherent priors are inductive, some counter-inductive (Carnap 1955). Subjective Bayesians: any coherent prior permitted (de Finetti, Savage, Jeffrey, van Fraassen); objective Bayesians add a principle of indifference. Reply: merging-of-opinions theorems (Blackwell & Dubins 1962; Gaifman & Snir 1982) — opinions converge as shared evidence grows (idea traced to Peirce 1877). [source: SEP-BAYES §§1.5, 4.1]
- Bayesian answer to Hume assessed: the quest for an a priori prior "has been largely abandoned"; many see it as a "partial" or "logical" solution: Howson: "Inductive reasoning is justified to the extent that it is sound, given appropriate premises" (2000: 239); the prior is where the empirical assumption enters. [source: SEP-IND §3.3–3.4]

### Worked example A (medical test; base rates) — arithmetic by the researcher, numbers invented for illustration
- Suppose 1 in 100 people has a disease; the test detects 90% of those who have it; it falsely flags 9% of those who don't. Of 1,000 people: 10 have it, 9 test positive; 990 don't, about 89 test positive. 98 positives, 9 true: P(disease | positive) ≈ 9/98 ≈ 9%. By Bayes: (0.9 × 0.01)/(0.9 × 0.01 + 0.09 × 0.99) = 0.009/0.0981 ≈ 0.092. [arithmetic; method of natural frequencies: WP-Base_rate_fallacy (Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995)]
- Consistency: the *Fallacies* chapter already uses a drunk-driver example (1 in 1,000 drunk; test never misses; flags 50 of 999 sober; 1 in 51 ≈ 2%) and attributes counting-people formats to Gigerenzer and the "representativeness heuristic" to Kahneman and Tversky, plus Linda. Use a *different* example here (as above) and cross-reference; do not contradict. Base-rate neglect also called the prosecutor's fallacy (Thompson & Schumann 1987). [source: supabase/content/philosophy--logic-and-reasoning--fallacies.sql; WP-Base_rate_fallacy]

### Worked example B (Monty Hall)
- Origin: Steve Selvin, letters to *The American Statistician*, 1975; famous from Craig F. Whitaker's letter in Marilyn vos Savant's "Ask Marilyn" column, *Parade*, 9 September 1990. Question: "Suppose you're on a game show, and you're given the choice of three doors: Behind one door is a car; behind the others, goats. You pick a door, say No. 1, and the host, who knows what's behind the doors, opens another door, say No. 3, which has a goat. He then says to you, 'Do you want to pick door No. 2?' Is it to your advantage to switch your choice?" She answered: switch (2/3 vs 1/3). [source: WP-Monty_Hall_problem]
- About 10,000 readers wrote, nearly 1,000 with PhDs, most saying she was wrong; Paul Erdős remained unconvinced until shown a computer simulation. Equivalent to Gardner's three prisoners problem (1959); related to Bertrand's box paradox (1889). [source: WP-Monty_Hall_problem]
- Standard assumptions: host always opens an unchosen door, always reveals a goat, always offers the switch; chooses randomly when both unchosen doors hide goats. Bayes: P(car 2 | host opens 3) = (1/3 × 1)/(1/3 × 1/2 + 1/3 × 1 + 1/3 × 0) = 2/3. Vos Savant's million-door version. If the host opens a door at random and happens to reveal a goat ("Monty Fall"/"Ignorant Monty"), switching wins 1/2. Lesson: what the evidence is depends on the process that produced it. [source: WP-Monty_Hall_problem; arithmetic checked]
- Vos Savant born 11 Aug 1946. [source: WP-Marilyn_vos_Savant]

## 10. Inference to the best explanation (cross-ref *American Pragmatism*)
- Peirce coined "abduction" (also "hypothesis", "retroduction"); around 1865 he began to distinguish two kinds of probable inference, induction and abduction, by permuting the Barbara syllogism (urn and beans/balls: deduction rule→case→result; induction from sample to population; abduction: all balls in this urn are red, these are red, so these come from this urn). [source: SEP-PEIRCE §3]
- Peirce: "[a]bduction is the process of forming explanatory hypotheses. It is the only logical operation which introduces any new idea" (CP 5.172). 1903 schema (Harvard Lectures on Pragmatism, CP 5.189): "The surprising fact, C, is observed. / But if A were true, C would be a matter of course. / Hence, there is reason to suspect that A is true." For Peirce abduction belongs to discovery; deduction derives consequences; induction tests. No reference in Peirce to *best* explanation. Frankfurt (1958) objected the schema introduces no new idea since A is already in a premise. [source: SEP-ABD-P; WP-Abductive_reasoning (1903 date)] Consistent with published *American Pragmatism*.
- Gilbert Harman (26 May 1938 – 13 Nov 2021), "The Inference to the Best Explanation", *Philosophical Review* 74 (1965): 88–95; generally credited with coining the term; Harman said it corresponds approximately to what others called "abduction". [source: WP-Gilbert_Harman; WP-Abductive_reasoning; WS]
- Peter Lipton (9 Oct 1954 – 25 Nov 2007), Hans Rausing Professor, Cambridge; *Inference to the Best Explanation* (Routledge 1991; expanded 2nd ed. 2004). [source: WP-Peter_Lipton]
- Lipton's distinction: the **likeliest** explanation (best supported by evidence) vs the **loveliest** (would, if true, provide the most understanding); "best" read as "likeliest" makes IBE uninformative, so he reads IBE as inference to the loveliest explanation, holding that loveliness is a guide to likeliness. [source: WS (NDPR review of 2nd ed.; encyclopedia summaries); WP-Peter_Lipton lists "Inference to the loveliest explanation"]
- Lipton (2004, ch. 7): Bayesians should be "explanationists": explanatory considerations should guide priors and likelihoods. Others: Okasha 2000, McGrew 2003, Weisberg 2009; critics Roche & Sober 2013. [source: SEP-ABD §4]
- **Van Fraassen's "bad lot"**: IBE compares only hypotheses we have thought of, so may lead us to believe "the best of a bad lot" (*Laws and Symmetry*, 1989, 143); it is a priori implausible we are privileged to hit on the true one (1989, 144). Replies: require the best to be "good enough" (Lipton 1993) or "satisfactory" (Musgrave 1988); comparative-conclusion versions (Kuipers). [source: SEP-ABD §3.1]
- Van Fraassen's second objection (1989 ch. 6): a probabilistic IBE rule either equals Bayes's rule (redundant) or departs from it and is Dutch-bookable (Lewis's dynamic Dutch book, via Teller 1973). Replies: Maher (1992), Skyrms (1993); Douven's simulations: some explanationist updating rules reach high probability for the truth faster than Bayes's rule. [source: SEP-ABD §3.1]
- Historical counterpoint: Mill rejected the "Hypothetical Method" (§5 above); Vācaspati Miśra appealed to IBE in defending Nyāya's argument for God (published *Classical Indian Schools*). [source: SEP-MILL; supabase/content/...classical-indian-schools.sql]

## 11. Induction in other traditions (brief)
- **Nyāya**: inference (*anumāna*) rests on *vyāpti* ("pervasion"; also *a-vinā-bhāva*, "indispensability"): "one superstratum pervades another just in case wherever the second occurs the first occurs". Stock five-member inference: fire on the hill because smoke; "Wherever there is smoke, there is fire; like a kitchen hearth and unlike a lake". [source: SEP-LOG-IND; IEP-NY]
- Pervasion "is known typically through generalization from cases (although in some cases a single observation, some say, will suffice)", using positive (*sapakṣa*) and negative (*vipakṣa*) examples. [source: SEP-EPI-IND §7.1; IEP-NY]
- Nyāya's "response to the problem of induction" (IEP): extraordinary perception of a universal through an individual — seeing smoke qua smokiness lets one grasp the tie between the universals smokiness and fieriness; "Nyāya thus solves the problem of induction by appeal to extraordinary perception." [source: IEP-NY §1b]
- *Upādhi* (hidden/adventitious condition, e.g. wet fuel for smoke) — already explained in the *Dissenters* chapter (sourced there to WP-Charvaka and a WebSearch). Not independently verified this session from SEP/IEP; IEP-NY uses *upādhi* in a different sense (pseudo-universals). Use the *Dissenters* wording and cross-reference.
- **Dharmakīrti** (dates debated: 6th or 7th c. CE; Frauwallner's 600–660 contested; Krasser 2012 argues mid-6th c.). Against mere non-observation of counterexamples (*Pramāṇavārttika*, inference chapter, vv. 13–25), he grounded the universal premise in two relations: **causation** (*tadutpatti*) and **identity** (*tādātmya*). Gillon (1991) argues this does not work; Steinkellner (2015) dissents. [source: SEP-LOG-IND; SEP-DHK]
- Dharmakīrti on smoke and fire: whatever is smoke must come from fire or it is "pseudo-smoke"; connection "by essential natures" (*svabhāvapratibandha*). He also used the anvaya/vyatireka method of observation and non-observation (*upalabdhi/anupalabdhi*), compared to Hume and to Mill (Matilal 1998). Tillemans: a charitable reading separates how we discover causal connections (a posteriori, fallibly) from what causation is. [source: SEP-DHK]
- Published *Mahāyāna* chapter: Dharmakīrti's "triple-mark" reason; *Dissenters* chapter: Buddhists grounded vyāpti "in identity of nature or in causation" (śiṃśapā is necessarily a tree) — consistent.
- **Avicenna** (Ibn Sīnā): in *al-Burhān* (Demonstration) of *The Book of Healing*, contrasted Aristotelian induction (*istiqrāʾ*), which "does not lead to the absolute, universal, and certain premises that it purports to provide", with experience/experimentation (*tajriba*), yielding conditional but certain first principles (McGinnis, "Scientific Methodologies in Medieval Islam", *J. Hist. Phil.* 41, 2003, 307–27). [source: WP-Avicenna; WS]
- **Mohists** (cross-ref *Hundred Schools* and *Logic across Traditions*): "extending kinds" (*tuī lèi* 推類); "Accept and propose on the basis of kinds (lèi)"; the "difficulty of extending kinds" (Canon B2); the *Lesser Selection* (*Xiǎo Qǔ*), "probably the most important text in the history of Chinese logic", names four techniques — analogy (*pì*), paralleling (*móu*), pulling (*yuán*), pushing (*tuī*) — all "procedures for analogical argumentation". Fraser: their dialectics is "the study of fair or unbiased analogical persuasion", not deductive logic; early Chinese mathematics and science likewise based on analogy and taxonomy. [source: SEP-MOH §7]

- **Does Popper escape induction?** Corroboration seems inductive (Salmon 1981). [source: SEP-IND §6]

- **Raven paradox**: accept the conclusion (Hempel), Bayesian degree-of-support solutions (Good, Fitelson & Hawthorne), deny Nicod's criterion (Good 1967), hypothetico-deductive accounts (SEP-CONF §2.2). [source: WP-Raven_paradox; SEP-CONF]
