## 1. Context: after 1277 (cross-ref *Thomas Aquinas*, *Early Scholasticism*, *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes*)
- On 7 March 1277 Bishop Stephen Tempier of Paris prohibited 219 philosophical and theological theses. By condemning articles 16–26 (divine omnipotence) and others, "Tempier endorsed God's absolute power to do whatever he wills." [source: SEP-1277 §1, §5]
- Pierre Duhem held that Tempier, by insisting on God's absolute power, freed Christian thought from dogmatic Aristotelianism and so "marked the birth of modern science"; "no one in the field any longer endorses his view that modern science started in 1277"; Edward Grant comes closest to Duhem today. [source: SEP-1277 §5]
- Among the 1277 condemned views: that there could not be more than one angel in a species, a view held by Aquinas and tied to individuation by matter. This is one origin of the late medieval debate on individuation. [source: SEP-Hae intro]
- Henry of Ghent (d. 1293), a member of the commission that drew up the condemnations, was the most influential Paris theologian of the next generation; "But it was John Duns Scotus … who transformed the subject." [source: SEP-Med §2.7]
- Marenbon's one-line summary: Ockham "followed Scotus's line on God's will, but substituted for his metaphysics a very sparse ontology in which the distinctions Scotus had thrust on reality are made through a mental language." [source: SEP-Med §2.7]

## 2. Duns Scotus: life and works
- Name: "Scotus" means "the Scot"; family name Duns, also the name of the Scottish village of his birth "just a few miles from the English border." [source: SEP-Sco §1.1]
- Birth date is disputed. Traditional 1265/1266 (inferred from ordination on 17 March 1291 at the earliest canonical age of 25). Stephen Dumont (2023) argues the ordination records point to a birth year of 1263 or 1264, which SEP calls "likely". IEP still gives the first quarter of 1266. Say "c. 1265 (perhaps 1263/64)". [sources: SEP-Sco §1.1; IEP-Sco §1a]
- Ordained priest 17 March 1291 (WP adds: at St Andrew's Priory, Northampton). [sources: SEP-Sco §1.1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duns_Scotus]
- Career per SEP (following Dumont 2023): two years teaching philosophy at the Franciscan studium in Cambridge, the Paris lectorate in the mid to late 1280s, then Oxford theology from probably 1290–91. Oxford lectures on the *Sentences* (the *Lectura*) 1298–99. He had begun revising them into the *Ordinatio* by June 1300 and worked on it until his death, leaving it unfinished. Paris *Sentences* lectures from October 1302. [source: SEP-Sco §1.1]
- Expulsion from Paris: on 24–25 June 1303 royal agents of Philip IV took a census of the Franciscans in his quarrel with Boniface VIII. Scotus sided with the pope, and non-adherents had three days to leave France. He returned for 1304–05. Magister regens 1306–07, then transferred to Cologne, where he died on 8 November 1308. (IEP dates the census to June 1301; SEP's dating follows Dumont 2023 and is preferred.) [sources: SEP-Sco §1.1; IEP-Sco §1a]
- On 18 November 1304 the Franciscan Minister General Gonsalvus of Spain named Scotus the next Franciscan master at Paris, praising his "laudable life, superb knowledge, most subtle intellect". [source: SEP-Sco §1.1, quoting Dumont 2023, 35]
- Works: early questions on the Old Logic; questions on Aristotle's *Metaphysics* and *De anima*; *Lectura*; *Ordinatio* ("generally taken to be Scotus's premier work; the critical edition was at last completed in 2013"); Paris *Reportationes* (student reports); *Collationes*; *De primo principio* (late work of natural theology); *Quodlibetal Questions* (Advent 1306 or Lent 1307); *Theoremata* (authenticity once doubted, accepted by the recent critical edition). [source: SEP-Sco §1.2]
- Epithet "Subtle Doctor" (*Doctor Subtilis*). Ockham "would praise his judgment as excelling all others in its subtlety." IEP calls him, with Bonaventure, Aquinas and Ockham, "one of the four great philosophers of High Scholasticism." [sources: IEP-Sco intro; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duns_Scotus]
- Beatified by Pope John Paul II on 20 March 1993. [sources: IEP-Sco §1a; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duns_Scotus]
- Influence: the Scotist school "flourished well into the seventeenth century", where his influence appears in Descartes and Bramhall. Interest dwindled in the 18th century, and 19th-century neo-scholastics turned to Aquinas. [source: IEP-Sco §1b]
- "Dunce": the OED (3rd ed.) cites mid-16th-century uses of *dunce* for a follower of Duns Scotus, a pedant, or a fool. When 16th-century Scotists opposed Renaissance humanism, "Dunsmen", "duns" or "dunce" became a term of abuse among humanists and reformers. The etymology is also given by Richard Stanyhurst. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunce]

## 3. Scotus: univocity of being (against Aquinas's analogy). The main argument
- The disagreement: Aquinas and Scotus agree that knowledge of God starts from creatures and that we cannot know God's essence in this life. Scotus holds that some predicates apply *univocally* (with exactly the same meaning) to God and creatures. Aquinas holds this impossible and allows only analogical predication. [source: SEP-Sco §2.1]
- Scotus's two tests for univocity: (1) affirming and denying the concept of one and the same subject is enough for a contradiction; (2) it can serve as the middle term of a syllogism. [source: IEP-Sco §7a; SEP-Ana §7 confirms the contradiction criterion]
- Why it matters to him: if "being" were not univocal, metaphysics (the science of being qua being) would lack a unified subject, and no a posteriori argument from creatures to God would be possible. "In Scotus's view, analogy is just a form of equivocity." [source: IEP-Sco §7a–b]
- **Argument from certain and doubtful concepts** (Ordinatio 1 d.3 p.1 q.1–2 n.27), "perhaps his most influential", reconstructed by IEP:
  1. A concept of which one is certain is different from concepts of which one is doubtful (one concept cannot be both certain and doubtful for the same person).
  2. A person can be certain that God is a being while doubting whether God is finite or infinite, created or uncreated.
  3. So the concept of being of which they are certain differs from the concepts "finite being", "infinite being", "created being" and "uncreated being".
  4. Yet it is included in all of them. Therefore it is a single concept common to God and creatures: being is univocal.
  [source: IEP-Sco §7c]
- Supporting argument from Aquinas's own premise: if all our concepts come from creatures, any concept we apply to God must be the very same concept we got from creatures, or we could not speak of God at all, "which is false." [source: SEP-Sco §2.1]
- Supporting argument from Anselm's "pure perfections" (Ordinatio's fourth argument): to test whether, e.g., "good" is a perfection it is better in every respect to have, we must use the same concept for creatures and God. If the concept fit creatures only, God would not be the most perfect being. If it fit God only, we would have to know God's features before identifying the perfections, which reverses the test. [sources: SEP-Sco §2.1; IEP-Sco §7b]
- **Objection** (divine transcendence): univocity seems to give God and creatures something in common, against the doctrine that God is wholly other. **Reply**: univocity is a logical, not a metaphysical, doctrine. God and creatures share no reality. By removing the modes "finite" and "infinite" we form "incomplete" concepts that apply to both. Finite and infinite are "intrinsic modes" of being, not differences dividing a genus, so being is not a genus and the categories are not its species. Analogy remains possible but rests on a univocal core. [source: IEP-Sco §7c]
- Henry of Ghent's middle position (two distinct concepts of being that the mind conflates into one "fruitful error") was rejected by Scotus: if the mind naturally conflates close concepts, the unity of every concept (even "human being") would be in doubt. [source: IEP-Sco §7b]
- Divine infinity as positive and intrinsic, "a measure of intrinsic excellence that is not finite". "Infinite being" is the simplest concept of God available to us and "virtually contains" the other perfections. Williams: infinity is for Scotus what simplicity is for Aquinas. [source: SEP-Sco §2.3]
- Scotus's development: in early logical commentaries he called "being" a chance equivocal, and in later theological works he made it univocal. Later Scotists divided over whether the univocity is purely logical or implies a real community between God and creatures. [source: SEP-Ana §7]
- IEP: before Scotus "the prevailing mediaeval view … was that philosophy and theology would come to ruin if the concept of being was univocal"; Scotus argued the reverse. [source: IEP-Sco §7b]

## 4. Scotus: common nature, haecceity, formal distinction
- Scotus was a realist about universals. The extra-mental "common nature" (*natura communis*) is "indifferent" to existing in any number of individuals. Outside the mind it exists only in particulars, "contracted" in each by the haecceity (*haecceitas*), the principle of individuation. [source: SEP-Sco §3.3 (Ordinatio 2 d.3 p.1 qq.1–6, tr. in Spade 1994, 57–113)]
- Common natures have a real but "less than numerical unity". Argument: if all real unity were numerical, Socrates would be as different from Plato as from a line, and the concept "human" would be a fiction. A second argument: fire generates fire whether or not any mind observes it, so the two fires share a mind-independent nature. [source: IEP-Sco §4]
- Common nature is not itself a universal. Only a concept is predicable of many as a universal (except the divine essence). Inspiration: Avicenna's "horseness is just horseness." Unlike Plato's Forms, common natures exist only in individuals or as concepts and have a "diminished" being. [source: IEP-Sco §4]
- Individuation: against Henry of Ghent's "double negation" and against individuation by existence, quantity or matter (Aquinas, Giles of Rome, Godfrey of Fontaines). The individuator must be positive, intrinsic, non-common and in the category of substance. Scotus calls it "the individual entity", "individual form", or "haecceity". The last term dominates today "perhaps because of its use by C. S. Peirce." [source: IEP-Sco §5]
- Haecceity = "thisness" (from Latin *haec*, "this"), as opposed to quiddity, "whatness". It is a non-qualitative property, not a bare particular. Scotus does not use the word in the *Ordinatio* (where he says "individual difference/entity") but does elsewhere. Cross calls it "a term of Scotus's own invention". Robert Adams (1979) revived "thisness" in modern metaphysics. [source: SEP-Hae intro, §3]
- Formal distinction: between the real distinction (separable things) and the conceptual distinction. x and y are formally distinct iff they are really the same but have different, mutually non-inclusive *rationes*. Examples: will and intellect in the soul; common nature and individuator; genus and difference; the divine attributes; each divine Person and the divine essence. Scotus also has a weaker "modal distinction" (a thing and its intrinsic degree, e.g. finite/infinite being). [source: IEP-Sco §3]
- The *Logica Scoti* (disputed question on the formal distinction, 1305–07). [sources: SEP-Sco §1.2; IEP-Sco §1b]

## 5. Scotus: proof of God's existence (brief)
- Versions in *Lectura* 1 d.2 q.1, *Ordinatio* 1 d.2 q.1, *Reportatio* 1 d.2 q.1 and *De primo principio*. Williams: "rightly regarded as one of the most outstanding contributions ever made to natural theology." IEP: "perhaps the most complicated of any ever written." [sources: SEP-Sco §2.2; IEP-Sco intro]
- Key distinction: accidentally ordered causes (grandfather → father → son; the father's causing does not depend on the grandfather now) vs essentially ordered causes (shoulders move arm moves golf club). No infinite regress in an essentially ordered series. A modal sub-argument runs: it is possible that something is a first agent, and if a first agent is possible, one exists. [source: SEP-Sco §2.2, numbered steps (1)–(7)]
- Structure: "triple primacy" (first efficient cause, first final cause, most eminent being); the three primacies coincide; such a being has intellect and will, is infinite and is unique. [sources: SEP-Sco §2.2; IEP-Sco §6]

## 6. Scotus: will, freedom, natural law
- **Synchronic contingency**: for a volition to be free, the agent must, "at the very moment of willing, have the power to will otherwise." Aquinas's account gives only diachronic alternatives (now x, later y). [source: SEP-Sco §5.2]
- Freedom is rooted in the will, not the intellect. Aquinas: freedom is in the will as subject but in reason as cause. Scotus: "in any moment of free choice the will can either accept or disregard the intellect's judgment." [source: SEP-Sco §5.2]
- Only the will is "a self-determining power for opposites", and it alone "transcends nature" (Boler 1993). IEP labels Scotus's position "moderate voluntarism", opposed to Aquinas and Godfrey of Fontaines, and "influential into the modern period." [source: IEP-Sco intro, §10]
- Anselm's two affections (for advantage and for justice). Scotus uses them, but became "progressively less Anselmian" (King 2010). By the *Reportatio* they are "not distinct from the will". [sources: SEP-Sco §5.2; IEP-Sco §10]
- Modality: to explain how anything is contingent if God brings everything about by knowing it, Scotus argued that God wills contingently. He "made explicit a different understanding of modality, in which possibility and necessity are not reducible to temporal conditions" (*Lectura* I 39). [source: SEP-Med §3.2]
- Natural law in the strict sense = propositions *per se notae ex terminis* (self-evident from their terms) and what follows from them (Ordinatio 3 d.37). Even God cannot make them false. In the broad sense = propositions "highly consonant" (IEP: "exceptionally harmonious") with these, which are contingent and depend on God's will. [sources: SEP-Sco §5.1; IEP-Sco §9]
- Which commandments are strict natural law: the first table of the Decalogue (duties to God). The second table (honour parents, do not kill, steal, commit adultery, lie, covet) belongs only to the broad sense and admits divine dispensation (Abraham and Isaac; Jacob's two wives; the Hebrew midwives who lied). SEP-Sco: after analysis only negative propositions remain strict ("God is not to be hated", no other gods, no irreverence), and even the Sabbath commandment is not strict. IEP-Sco: the first *two* commandments are strict. Say "the first table, or part of it". [sources: SEP-Sco §5.1; IEP-Sco §9]
- Why God willed this law and not another: "God's will with respect to contingent propositions is unqualifiedly free". There is no total explanation beyond his willing it. [source: SEP-Sco §5.1]

## 7. Ockham: life
- Born probably late 1287 or early 1288 in the village of Ockham ("Oak Hamlet"), Surrey. As a boy (between 7 and 13) he was "given" to the Franciscans (an oblate) and sent to London Greyfriars. [source: SEP-Ock §1.1 and n.3] IEP gives c. 1280–c. 1349 (born 1280–85, from an ordination record of 1306; died 1347–49). SEP's dates are the more recent scholarship. [source: IEP-Ock §1]
- Oxford: lectured on the *Sentences* 1317–19, but never completed the mastership. Nickname *Venerabilis Inceptor*, "Venerable Beginner". From c. 1321 at London Greyfriars with Walter Chatton and Adam Wodeham, "both sharp critics of Ockham's views." [source: SEP-Ock §1.1]
- 1323: called before the Franciscan provincial chapter at Bristol. Someone (perhaps John Lutterell, former Oxford chancellor) charged him with heresy at Avignon. Summoned to Avignon in May 1324, he never returned to England. [source: SEP-Ock §1.1, n.4; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Ockham]
- Avignon 1324–28: "house arrest" is "an exaggeration" (SEP). IEP says "a loose form of house arrest". Finished the *Quodlibets* there. "Although some stern pronouncements came out of the investigation, his views were never officially condemned as heretical." [source: SEP-Ock §1.2] WP: the committee's reports were critical "but the process was never brought to a conclusion." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Ockham]
- The papacy sat at Avignon 1309–1376/77. [sources: SEP-Ock n.4; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avignon_Papacy (1309 Clement V moved there; Gregory XI left 1376, reaching Rome January 1377)]
- Poverty controversy: Pope John XXII rejected the Franciscan doctrine that Christ and the Apostles owned nothing, individually or in common. In *Cum inter nonnullos* he condemned it as heresy. Michael of Cesena, Franciscan minister general, came to Avignon in 1327 and asked Ockham to study the papal statements. Ockham concluded that John was a heretic and so had effectively abdicated. [sources: SEP-Ock §1.2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Ockham]
- Flight: on the night of 26 May 1328 Michael, Ockham and others fled to Ludwig of Bavaria (then at Pisa). Ockham was excommunicated on 6 June 1328 for leaving without permission. To Munich c. 1329. Wrote only political works thereafter. Died on the night of 9/10 April 1347, at about 60. [source: SEP-Ock §1.3] Michael of Cesena: c. 1270–29 November 1342. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_of_Cesena]
- WP: Ockham died "just before the outbreak in Munich of the Black Death". The plague reached Europe in 1347–48 (Periergia *Black Death*). Do not say he died of the plague. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Ockham]
- Second nickname "More than Subtle Doctor" (thought to surpass Scotus). [source: IEP-Ock §1]
- Main works and dates: *Sentences* commentary (1317–18; Book I an *ordinatio*, II–IV a *reportatio*); *Seven Quodlibets* (London disputations 1322–24, edited in Avignon 1324–25); *Summa of Logic* (c. 1323–25); *Treatise on Predestination* (1321–24); *Work of Ninety Days* (1332–34); *Dialogue* (c. 1334–46, unfinished); *Eight Questions on the Power of the Pope* (1340–41); *Short Discourse* (1341–42). [source: SEP-Ock §2]
- Influence on him of Peter John Olivi (1248–1298), "the single most important influence" per IEP, unacknowledged because Olivi was condemned. This is Kaye's interpretive claim. [source: IEP-Ock §1]

## 8. Ockham: logic, mental language, supposition (brief)
- Logic is "the most useful tool of all the arts" (*Summa of Logic*, prefatory letter, tr. Spade). [source: SEP-Ock §3]
- SEP caution: his logical "originality and influence should not be exaggerated"; much of his supposition machinery derives from Walter Burley. [source: SEP-Ock §3 and n.7]
- Mental language: "Ockham was the first philosopher to develop in some detail the notion of mental language". Written words are subordinated to spoken words, spoken words to concepts. Spoken/written signification is conventional ("dog"/"chien"), while concepts are natural signs. [source: SEP-Ock §3.3]
- Debate: whether Ockham's mental language is a lean "ideal" language with no synonymy or equivocation (Trentman 1970, Spade 1980) or contains redundancies (Panaccio, Tweedale, Chalmers). [source: SEP-Ock §3.3, nn.16–17]
- Supposition (roughly "reference"): personal (the term stands for things: "the cat is on the mat"), simple (for a concept, in Ockham's version: "horse is a species"), material (for the word itself: "'stop'"). Realists analysed simple supposition differently (for a universal). [source: IEP-Ock §5b; SEP-Ock n.12–13]
- Absolute vs connotative terms: "human being" signifies its objects absolutely; "parent", "white", "similar" signify one thing directly and another indirectly, and have nominal definitions. This is the tool of his ontological reduction. [sources: SEP-Ock §4.3; IEP-Ock §5b]

## 9. Ockham: nominalism, against real universals. The main argument
- Thesis: everything that exists is singular. The only universals are concepts (and the spoken/written terms subordinated to them), which are "universal" only as predicable of many. [source: SEP-Ock §4.2]
- Method: Ockham does **not** reject universals by the razor. He holds the realist theories of his day "outright incoherent". [sources: SEP-Ock §4.2; IEP-Ock §3]
- **Argument 1 (one and many)**: a real universal would be one thing and yet many things, existing whole in each individual. Ockham: "There is no universal outside the mind really existing in individual substances or in the essences of things…. The reason is that everything that is not many things is necessarily one thing in number and consequently a singular thing." (OPh II, pp. 11–12; translator unnamed in IEP) Steps: (1) whatever exists is either many things or not many things; (2) what is not many things is one thing in number, i.e. singular; (3) a universal in re is supposed to be neither a mere plurality nor a mere singular; (4) so there is no such thing. [source: IEP-Ock §3]
- **Argument 2 (annihilation)**: if humanity were a real constituent of each human, God could not annihilate one human without destroying the universal in it and so all other humans (OPh I, p. 51). **Realist reply**: destroying one human destroys only part of the universal. **Ockham's rejoinder**: then the universal is not one single shared essence after all, contradicting the realist's own claim. [source: IEP-Ock §3]
- Full text: Ockham's discussion of universals (*Ordinatio* I d.2 q.6 and related) is translated in Spade, *Five Texts* (1994). [source: SEP-Ock bibliography]
- Ockham's positive account: no common nature is needed. Two humans are simply more similar to each other than to a brute, and the mind's concept represents each indifferently. [source: SEP-UM §9]
- His view of concepts changed: first the *fictum* theory (concepts as purely intentional objects), then, by the *Summa of Logic* and *Quodlibets*, the *intellectio* theory (a universal concept is just the act of thinking, a singular quality of a mind). [source: SEP-Ock §4.2; Boehner 1946 per n.26]
- Klima: Ockham's charge that the *via antiqua* multiplies entities ("a column is to the right by to-the-rightness … someone blind is blind by blindness", *Summa Logicae* I c.51) is "not quite justified", since several realists identified the significates of terms in different categories. [source: SEP-UM §9, n.46–48]
- "Nominalism" = *nomina*, "names". IEP calls Ockham's version "conceptualism", contrasted with Roscelin's earlier view. [source: IEP-Ock §3] Buridan entry: most 14th-century philosophers were "nominalists" in the bare sense of denying real universals; by Buridan's time nominalism was "a practice, or way of doing philosophy". [source: SEP-Bur §5]

## 10. Ockham's razor: what he actually said
- SEP: the slogan that one "must not multiply entities beyond necessity" — "Although the sentiment is certainly Ockham's, this particular formulation is nowhere to be found in his texts." [source: SEP-Ock §4.1]
- Ockham's fullest formulation names three conditions under which positing something is justified: reason (self-evident premises), experience, or infallible religious authority (Scripture) (e.g. *Treatise on the Body of Christ* ch. 29; *Sent.* I d.30 q.1). [source: SEP-Ock §4.1, n.25]
- Attested formulations: *Numquam ponenda est pluralitas sine necessitate*, "Plurality must never be posited without necessity" (*Sentences* commentary, I d.27 q.2, per WP); *Frustra fit per plura quod potest fieri per pauciora*, "It is futile to do with more things that which can be done with fewer" (*Summa Logicae* i.12, per WP citing Thorburn 1918 and Kneale and Kneale 1962). IEP favours the latter as Ockham's own formulation. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor; IEP-Ock §2]
- Not Ockham's invention: phrases like "a plurality is not to be posited without necessity" were commonplace in 13th-century scholastic writing. Antecedents in Aristotle, Grosseteste (c. 1217–20), Aquinas. WP shows the words *Pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate* in a manuscript of Scotus's *Oxford Commentary*. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor; IEP-Ock §2]
- The famous wording "Entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity" (*Entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem*) is absent from his extant works. WP attributes the popular wording to the Irish Franciscan John Punch (Johannes Poncius) in his 1639 commentary on Scotus (Wadding edition, *Opus Oxoniense* III d.34 q.1), where it is called a "common axiom" (*axioma vulgare*). WP's lead quotes it as *Non sunt multiplicanda entia sine necessitate*. W. M. Thorburn, "The Myth of Occam's Razor", *Mind* 27 (1918), traced the formula to the 1639 Wadding edition. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor; WS-2] Caveat: the Punch attribution rests on WP and secondary reports. Safe wording: "the famous phrasing is not Ockham's. It appears to come from a 1639 commentary on Scotus by John Punch."
- Name "razor": IEP says the first known use of "Occam's razor" is 1852, by Sir William Hamilton. WP says Libert Froidmont in 1649 claimed to have coined *novacula occami*. The sources disagree. Safe wording: "the name came centuries later". [sources: IEP-Ock §2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor]
- How he used it: it is "merely a cautionary methodological principle"; used against extra categories (quantity, relation) and against intelligible species, not against universals. [sources: SEP-Ock §4.1–4.3; IEP-Ock §2, §4b]
- Ockham himself restricted it where God's power and miracles are concerned (e.g. the Eucharist). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor]
- Walter Chatton's "anti-razor": if three things are not enough to verify an affirmative proposition about things, a fourth must be added, and so on. [sources: IEP-Ock §2; SEP Walter Chatton entry read]

## 11. Ockham: reduction of the categories
- Of Aristotle's ten categories, only substance and quality name kinds of real thing. Ten semantic categories, two ontological ones. Exceptions on theological grounds: certain relational entities for the Trinity, Incarnation and Eucharist. [source: SEP-Ock §4.3]
- Example (SL II, 11, tr. Spade): "from the very fact that Socrates is white and Plato is white, Socrates is similar to Plato and conversely" — no extra entity "similarity". A body is extended by having "parts outside of parts", not by an extra entity "quantity". [source: SEP-Ock §4.3]
- Why keep quality? Debated. IEP suggests the Eucharist (accidents of bread remaining without their substance) may have stayed his razor. IEP also links his identification of quantity with substance to his Eucharistic theory and the Avignon summons. [source: IEP-Ock §5c] (Kaye's interpretation)
- SEP: the paraphrase strategy helped open the way to mathematising nature, one factor in 14th-century mathematical physics. [source: SEP-Ock §5]

## 12. Ockham: intuitive cognition, divine power, the "non-existent" problem
- Scotus originated the intuitive/abstractive distinction that "structured much of the discussion of cognition for the rest of the scholastic period". Intuitive = of a thing as existing and present; abstractive = abstracts from existence. Scholars disagree whether Scotus's statements on intellectual intuition are consistent (Day 1947: yes; Wolter 1990: changed; Pasnau 2003: inconsistent). [sources: IEP-Sco intro, §8a; SEP-Sco §4.2]
- Ockham's definition (Sent. I Prol. q.1, OTh I 31–32, tr. Spade): "intuitive cognition of a thing is a cognition such that by virtue of it, it can be known whether the thing exists or not, in such a way that if the thing does exist, the intellect at once judges it to exist and evidently knows it to exist … Abstractive cognition, however, is that by virtue of which it cannot be evidently known of the thing whether it exists or does not exist." [source: SEP-Ock §6.2]
- He rejected intelligible species (representational intermediaries) by the razor. [sources: SEP-Ock §6.1 heading; IEP-Ock §4b]
- Intuitive cognition of a non-existent: Ockham held that God can supernaturally cause an intuitive cognition of a non-existent thing, and then it causes the true judgment that the thing does not exist (Panaccio and Piché 2010). God could also cancel its natural effect and directly cause a false judgment. [source: SEP-Ock §6.2 and n.38]
- SEP-Skep summary: for Ockham perception via intuitive cognition is infallible in this sense "even given the possibility that God is deceiving us". Adam Wodeham disagreed (no mark distinguishes true from false perception if God deceives). Peter Auriol's cases (dreams, the bent stick in water, the whirled firebrand) were the challenge. [source: SEP-Skep §4.2.2]
- Absolute vs ordained power (*potentia absoluta/ordinata*): the 1277 condemnation endorsed God's absolute power to do whatever he wills. [source: SEP-1277] Ockham, Quodlibet VI q.1: "Some things God can do by his ordained power, some by his absolute power. This distinction must not be understood to mean that there are really two powers in God." Courtenay reads absolute power as the whole range of possibilities initially open to God. Later "Ockhamists" equated absolute power with miracles and ordained power with the common course of nature, arguably a misreading. God can do whatever involves no contradiction (Quodl. VI q.6). [source: WS-1 (secondary summary; weaker)] Ockham's thought experiments "involving God's power" appear in his arguments against quantitative and relational entities. [source: SEP-Ock n.29]
- IEP calls Ockham a "fideist" who rejected all the proofs of God's existence. This is Kaye's strong reading; treat it as interpretation (see Debates).

## 13. Ockham: will and ethics
- "Will-based ethics in which intentions … count for everything"; external acts are morally neutral in themselves. [source: SEP-Ock §7]
- Divine command element: the Israelites' "spoiling the Egyptians" was permissible and even obligatory because God commanded it. Yet he allows a "science of morals" through experience and natural reason (Quodl. II q.14), and the "virtuous pagan". "Scholars continue to debate how to best characterize his ethics." [source: SEP-Ock §7]
- IEP quotation (OTh V, p. 352; translator unnamed): "hatred, theft, adultery, and the like may involve evil according to the common law, in so far as they are done by someone who is obligated by a divine command to perform the opposite act. As far as everything absolute in these actions is concerned, however, God can perform them without involving any evil." [source: IEP-Ock §7a]

## 14. Ockham: political thought (brief)
- Concern: the threat of a heretical pope; defence of individual liberty; the fallibility of institutions. Against papal "fullness of power", one argument is that it would make all Christians the pope's slaves (*Breviloquium* II c.1). Spiritual and temporal powers are distinct and independent ("dualist"). Like Marsilius of Padua, a fellow exile at Ludwig's court. [source: SEP-Ock §8, §8.3]
- On John XXII's claims (tr. Kilcullen): "heretical, erroneous, silly, ridiculous, fantastic, insane, and defamatory, contrary and likewise plainly adverse to orthodox faith." (*Letter to the Friars Minor*, 1334). [source: SEP-Ock §8.2]
- The *Dialogue* (master and student) was written anonymously, and Ockham never states his own view in it. [source: SEP-Ock §8.2]
- WP: increasingly seen as a contributor to Western constitutional ideas of limited government. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Ockham]

## 15. John Buridan
- Born around 1300 in or near Béthune, Picardy. Educated at the Collège du Cardinal Lemoine and the University of Paris. Master of Arts by the mid-1320s. University rector twice (1328, 1340). Last documented alive in 1358. By 1361 one of his benefices had passed to another, so he died c. 1358–61. [source: SEP-Bur §1] (SEP-UM gives "ca. 1300–1358"; SEP-Skep gives "ca. 1295–1361"; Periergia economics chapter "d. c. 1361" — all compatible with "d. c. 1360".)
- He spent his whole career in the arts faculty and never took a theology degree, so he wrote no *Sentences* commentary. [source: SEP-Bur §1]
- Legends are unverified: thrown into the Seine in a sack over an affair with the queen; founding the University of Vienna; hitting the future Clement VI with a shoe. [source: SEP-Bur §1]
- *Summulae de dialectica* renovated Aristotelian logic through the *via moderna*. The *via Buridani* shaped teaching at Paris, Heidelberg, Prague, Vienna and northern Italy "well into the Renaissance". [source: SEP-Bur §2]
- Impetus: an internal motive force transmitted from mover to projectile, replacing Aristotle's air explanation. It probably did not originate with Buridan, but he considered it might last indefinitely: "would be of infinite duration were it not diminished and corrupted by some contrary force" (QM XII.9; translation unattributed, presumably Zupko's). Impetus is proportional to speed and quantity of matter. But "it is too much to claim, as Duhem did, that he was a forerunner of Galileo." [source: SEP-Bur §6]
- Buridan answered Autrecourt's causal scepticism: there is a "virtual infinity of self-evident principles through the senses, or through experience" (Questions on Metaphysics II.1, Klima trans.), with "conditional evidentness" for natural knowledge. [source: SEP-Skep §4.2.4]
- **Buridan's ass** (a donkey starving between two equal haystacks) is "nowhere to be found in Buridan's writings". Versions go back to Aristotle (*De caelo* 295b32: a man equally hungry and thirsty). Al-Ghazālī used two equal dates. Zupko's explanation: it began as a parody of Buridan's theory that the will can defer choice. [sources: SEP-Bur §7; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buridan%27s_ass] Cross-ref *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes*.
- His moral psychology uses voluntarist terminology for an intellectualist view, perhaps "to dispel the cloud of heterodoxy" around intellectualism since 1277 (Zupko). [source: SEP-Bur §7]

## 16. Nicholas of Autrecourt (cross-ref *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes*)
- Born between 1295 and 1298 at Autrécourt (diocese of Verdun). Master of arts c. 1318–20. Sorbonne in the 1330s. Summoned to Avignon by Benedict XII on 21 November 1340. Convicted in 1346 (66 articles). Recanted at Avignon before 19 May 1346. In Paris on 25 November 1347 he publicly recanted and burned his articles and a treatise (probably *Exigit ordo*). Later canon, then dean, of Metz. Died 16 or 17 July 1369. [source: SEP-Aut §1–2; WP agrees: c. 1299–1369]
- Writings: letters to Bernard of Arezzo (two of nine survive, dated 1335–36), a letter from Master Giles, the *Exigit ordo* (1333–35, also misnamed *Tractatus universalis*), a theological question. [source: SEP-Aut §3]
- Core thesis: all evident knowledge (apart from faith) reduces to the principle of non-contradiction. So from the existence of one thing one cannot evidently infer the existence of another: no certain inference from cause to effect or from accidents to substance. Second Letter: "From the fact that some thing is known to be, it cannot be inferred evidently … that there is some other thing." [sources: SEP-Aut §4; SEP-Skep §4.2.4]
- "The medieval Hume" is Hastings Rashdall's epithet (1906–07 paper). But "it is now generally agreed that he is not a sceptic at all when it comes to defending the reliability of sense-perception". His sceptical attack targets rationalist claims about causality and substance. He also defended atomism and the eternity of things "as a natural philosopher". [sources: SEP-Aut §4–6; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_of_Autrecourt]
- SEP-Skep (Bolyard), by contrast, says he "espoused the most radical form of skepticism found at any point during the Middle Ages". This is a debate. [source: SEP-Skep §4.2.4]

## 17. Nicole Oresme (brief)
- Born c. 1320 near Caen, Normandy. MA Paris by 1341/42. Grand Master of the College of Navarre 1356–62. Served Charles V. Bishop of Lisieux (elected 1377). Died 11 July 1382. [source: SEP-Ore §1]
- Translated Aristotle's *Ethics*, *Politics* and *On the Heavens* into French for Charles V. "Generally considered the greatest medieval economist" (*De moneta*). [source: SEP-Ore intro]
- In *Le livre du ciel et du monde* he argued that no proof establishes a stationary Earth and a rotating heaven, showing the possibility of the Earth's daily rotation, "he finished by affirming his belief in a stationary Earth". [source: SEP-Ore §2.5]
- Graphing qualities and motions (*latitudo*/*longitudo*; uniform acceleration as a right triangle); the first proof of the Merton mean-speed theorem; an opponent of astrology. [source: SEP-Ore §2.5–2.6]

## 18. Via moderna vs via antiqua; the end of scholasticism
- Klima: the "nominalist" *via moderna*, initiated by Ockham's semantics and systematised by Buridan, was distinguished from the "realist" *via antiqua* from the second half of the 14th century. By the 15th century nominalism had a strong following "in all major universities of Europe". [source: SEP-UM intro, §10]
- Domingo de Soto (1494–1560), "born among nominalists and raised by realists": the realist doctrine was harder to understand, the nominalist harder to believe. [source: SEP-UM §10]
- Klima: the struggle of the *viae* bred "an ever growing dissatisfaction, even contempt, in a new, lay, humanist intelligentsia" and ended with the demise of both frameworks. [source: SEP-UM §10]
- Marenbon: "Although most accounts of Latin university philosophy tail off in the mid-fourteenth century, this style of philosophizing continued vigorously for at least three centuries." Examples: Wyclif (d. 1384); Paul of Venice (d. 1429); Pomponazzi (1462–1525); Zabarella (d. 1589); Salamanca (Vitoria, Soto, Molina, Suárez d. 1617). Pasnau's *Metaphysical Themes 1274–1671* (2011) deliberately crosses the divide. [source: SEP-Med §2.7, §5.2]

## 19. Women philosophers of the period (only as SEP supports)
- Marenbon: women were excluded from universities. Candidates "all well-known writers with an accepted body of work" who were not until recently recognised as philosophers: Hildegard of Bingen, Hadewijch, Mechthild of Magdeburg, Margaret (Marguerite) Porete (d. 1310), Catherine of Siena (1347–80), Julian of Norwich (1342/3–c. 1416). [source: SEP-Med §2.9]
- Porete: "burnt at the stake by the Inquisition, for her refusal to cooperate in the process against her book, *The Mirror of the Simple Souls Who Are Annihilated and Remain Only in Will and Desire of Love*". The book "circulated anonymously quite widely". She "stands out for her argumentative manner": Reason is "roundly defeated by Love". Plausible links to Theology Faculty debates (King 2018, "Marguerite Porete and Godfrey of Fontaines"). [source: SEP-Med §2.9] Burned in Paris on 1 June 1310. Her book had earlier been burned at Valenciennes by the bishop of Cambrai (between 1296 and 1306). Written in Old French. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marguerite_Porete]
- Christine de Pizan (1364–c. 1430): not a religious; a married woman educated by her father, the French king's astrologer; supported her family by writing after her husband's death. Her writing "became philosophical, with Boethius but also Aquinas as points of reference, informed by a strong consciousness both of the restrictions placed on women and of their capabilities" (König-Pralong 2012). [source: SEP-Med §2.9] Born Venice, September 1364. *The Book of the City of Ladies* finished by c. 1405. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christine_de_Pizan; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Book_of_the_City_of_Ladies]
- Van Dyke (2022) argues philosophers can learn from these contemplatives on self-knowledge, reason, love, will and immortality. [source: SEP-Med §2.9]

## 20. On the eve of the Renaissance (cross-ref *Renaissance Philosophy*)
- Petrarch (Francesco Petrarca, 20 July 1304 – 19 July 1374). In *On His Own Ignorance and That of Many Others* (*De sui ipsius et multorum ignorantia*) he attacked contemporaries who followed Aristotle "blindly". He mocked useless questions such as the crocodile's moving upper jaw or the number of hairs on a lion's head, and said Aristotle's ethics "lacks the words that sting and set afire and urge toward love of virtue and hatred of vice". Behind this was a clash of method: humanists "prized eloquence and saw it as key to the production of virtue." [sources: SEP-ArR §2 (quoting the translation in Cassirer, Kristeller and Randall (eds.), *The Renaissance Philosophy of Man*, 1948, p. 103); dates https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrarch]
- Petrarch is credited with creating the idea of the "Dark Ages", which "most modern scholars now find inaccurate and misleading". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrarch]
- Nicholas of Cusa (1401–1464), born at Kues on the Moselle. Cardinal, reformer, bishop of Brixen (named 1450). "Arguably the most important German thinker of fifteenth century". The insight for *De docta ignorantia* (*On Learned Ignorance*, 1440) came to him on the voyage home from Constantinople (1437–38). God as the absolute maximum and the "coincidence of opposites", beyond the domain "where the principle of contradiction holds sway". "There is no proportion between finite and infinite." Steeped in Neoplatonism, aware of humanist and scholastic learning, "mostly self-taught". Died at Todi in 1464. [source: SEP-Cus intro, §1, §2.1]
- Marenbon: non-university 15th-century thinkers included Cusa, Lorenzo Valla (1407–57), Ficino (1433–99) and Pico (1463–94). Ficino and Pico are often called "Renaissance", yet neither lived until 1500, so they "do not fit into a chronology that places Renaissance Philosophy after Medieval Philosophy". [source: SEP-Med §2.8, §5.2]

## Debates
- **Univocity vs analogy.** Scotus: analogy collapses into equivocation and would ruin metaphysics and natural theology. Thomists: univocity threatens transcendence. Scotists split over whether univocity is purely logical or implies a real community of being. Cross reads analogy as resting on a univocal core. [sources: IEP-Sco §7; SEP-Ana §7]
- **Was Scotus a divine command theorist?** On one reading (Williams 1997/1998), second-table morality is simply what God wills. On another, it is naturalistic (Quodlibet 18), grounded in the natures of agent and act. Recent critics of the strong voluntarist reading: Borland and Hillman 2017, Ward 2019. [sources: SEP-Sco §5.1; IEP-Sco §9]
- **How to characterise Ockham's ethics.** Divine command vs a reason-based "science of morals" with natural-law elements. "Scholars continue to debate". [source: SEP-Ock §7] IEP (Kaye) reads him as a straightforward divine command theorist and a "fideist" who rejected all theistic proofs, and calls the "destroyer of the medieval synthesis" his reputation. SEP's treatment is more cautious. Present Kaye's as one interpretation. [sources: IEP-Ock intro, §6–7; SEP-Ock §7]
- **Did Ockham use the razor against universals?** No: both SEP and IEP say he argued realism is incoherent, not merely unnecessary. A common popular misunderstanding. [sources: SEP-Ock §4.2; IEP-Ock §3]
- **Was Ockham fair to the realists?** Klima: his charge that the *via antiqua* multiplies entities with terms is "not quite justified". [source: SEP-UM §9]
- **Mental language: ideal or natural?** Trentman/Spade vs Panaccio/Tweedale/Chalmers. [source: SEP-Ock §3.3]
- **Absolute/ordained power.** Courtenay (absolute power = the range of possibilities initially open to God) vs the later "Ockhamist" equation of absolute power with miracles. [source: WS-1, weaker]
- **Autrecourt: sceptic or not?** Rashdall's "medieval Hume" and Bolyard's "most radical form of skepticism" vs Thijssen: "now generally agreed" he trusted sense-perception and targeted only rationalist claims about cause and substance. [sources: SEP-Aut §4; SEP-Skep §4.2.4]
- **1277 and modern science.** Duhem's thesis (1277 started modern science) is abandoned. Grant holds a refined version. [source: SEP-1277 §5] Buridan as forerunner of Galileo: "too much to claim" (Zupko). [source: SEP-Bur §6]
- **"Decline of scholasticism".** The older narrative has university philosophy tailing off after c. 1350 and humanists sweeping it aside (Klima describes a breakdown of scholastic discourse and humanist "contempt"). Marenbon stresses vigorous continuation for three more centuries (Paul of Venice, Pomponazzi, Zabarella, Salamanca, Suárez) and calls the 1500 boundary questionable. [sources: SEP-UM §10; SEP-Med §2.7, §5.2]
- **Women as philosophers.** Whether "mystics" such as Porete count as philosophers. Marenbon and Van Dyke argue yes, with Porete distinctive for argument. [source: SEP-Med §2.9]

- Ockham "condemned by the Catholic Church" (IEP intro) or "condemned as a heretic": SEP says his views were never officially condemned as heretical. He was excommunicated in 1328 for leaving Avignon.
- Ockham born c. 1280–85 / died c. 1349 (IEP): SEP and WP give 1287/88 and 9/10 April 1347.
- Ockham died of the Black Death: contradicted by WP (died just before it reached Munich).
- Buridan's ass as an example in Buridan's writings: it is not there (SEP-Bur).
- Buridan legends (Seine sack, founding Vienna, shoe blow to Clement VI): unverifiable (SEP-Bur).
- Duhem's claim that Buridan was Galileo's forerunner or that 1277 began modern science: rejected (SEP-Bur, SEP-1277).
- Autrecourt "condemned 1347": conviction 1346; public recantation and burning, Paris, 25 November 1347.
- Autrecourt "1300–after 1350" (sibling Ghazālī notes): superseded by SEP-Aut (1295/98–1369).
- The translator of the IEP-Ock quotations (OPh/OTh pages) and the SEP-Sco/SEP-Bur/SEP-Cus quotations: not named. The translator of Petrarch's *On His Own Ignorance* in the 1948 CKR volume is not checked (often given as Hans Nachod, from memory only).
- Christine de Pizan as "the first professional woman writer in Europe": WP and the History chapter say "one of the first"; SEP doesn't settle it. Keep "one of the first".
- Exact Latin of Punch's 1639 wording: WP gives both *Non sunt multiplicanda entia sine necessitate* and *Entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem*. Thorburn 1918 was not read directly (wikisource blocked). Do not quote the Latin as Punch's exact words.
- The "Radical Orthodoxy" thesis that Scotus's univocity began secular modernity: commonly discussed, but not found in the sources read. Omit or research separately.
- Marenbon, *Medieval Philosophy: An Historical and Philosophical Introduction* (2007): the SEP bibliography line was garbled ("Philosophy: An Historical…"), so the exact title is not confirmed. Left off the reading list.
