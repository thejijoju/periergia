## 1. Background: Philo of Alexandria (brief only)
- Philo lived in Alexandria; "the consensus is that he lived between the end of the first century BCE and the middle of the first century CE"; precise birth date impossible. [source: SEP-Philo §1.1] WP gives c. 20 BCE – c. 50 CE. [source: WP-Philo] CAUTION: SEP-Philo's own opening sentence says "first half of the first century BCE", which contradicts its §1.1 — a slip; use "c. 20 BCE – c. 50 CE".
- He went on the embassy of the Alexandrian Jews to the emperor Caligula after the anti-Jewish riots of 38 CE (his *Legatio*); he knew very little Hebrew and read the Bible in the Greek Septuagint. [source: SEP-Philo §1.1]
- "Philo's name has almost become a byword for allegory." [source: SEP-Philo §4.1] He "is also considered the founder of negative theology in the history of monotheistic faith." [source: SEP-Philo §4.2]
- Whether Philo "really was a philosopher is a question still debated at length." [source: SEP-Philo intro]
- WRITER: keep Philo to a paragraph; cross-ref *Plotinus and Neoplatonism*. Medieval Jewish philosophers did not read Philo (he wrote in Greek and was preserved by Christians) — NOT VERIFIED in a source read today, so do not assert it; just say the medieval tradition began afresh in Arabic.

## 2. The Judaeo-Arabic context
- Saadia "is the first important rabbinic figure to write extensively in Judeo-Arabic". [source: WP-Saadia_Gaon] Judeo-Arabic = Arabic written in Hebrew characters, interspersed with words from Jewish tradition. [source: SEP-Hal intro]
- Saadia's milieu: 10th-century Iraq; Muʿtazilite *kalām* theologians tried to show by rational argument that revelation agrees with reason (ʿ*aql*); in public debates followers of other religions felt obliged to argue in the same style; Saadia's *Book of Beliefs and Opinions* is "a summa ... of Rabbinic Judaism, written in the rationalist style of the kalām". [source: SEP-Saad §1.1]
- SEP-Saad distinguishes *falsafa* (the school that followed Greek texts) from philosophy in general: Saadia "did not belong to the falāsifa school" though he used arguments of Greek origin. [source: SEP-Saad intro] (So: kalām as Saadia's model; falsafa as the model of Ibn Daud and Maimonides.)
- Maimonides' *Guide* was written in Judeo-Arabic; it is "heavily influenced by the Neo-Platonized Aristotelianism that had taken root in Islamic circles". [source: SEP-Maim intro] Its Islamic background includes the *Theology of Aristotle*, al-Fārābī, Avicenna, al-Ghazālī and Averroes. [source: SEP-MaimIsl intro]
- Maimonides is "in step with those Kalam theologians ... who stress, above all, the absolute unity of God and His lack of attributes", though he is "extremely critical of the dialectical methods of the Kalam". [source: SEP-MaimIsl §3.1] Stroumsa argues for the influence of Almohad theology on him. [sources: SEP-MaimIsl §3.1; SEP-Maim §1]
- Medieval Jews in Latin Europe who philosophised in Hebrew relied on Arabic works: the *Guide* (translated into Hebrew twice soon after it was written) and an almost complete Aristotle curriculum, mostly via Averroes' paraphrases. [source: SEP-Med §1.3.4] The *Guide* was translated into Latin in the 13th century from a Hebrew version. [source: SEP-Med §1.3.7]
- Gersonides wrote all his works in Hebrew; Provençal Jews knew Aristotle through Averroes' commentaries, translated into Hebrew by the end of the 13th century. [source: SEP-Gers §2]
- WRITER: cross-ref *Falsafa: al-Kindī, al-Fārābī and Avicenna* and *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes and the Quarrel over Reason*. History cross-ref: *The Rise of Islam and the Caliphates* (al-Andalus, Córdoba, the *convivencia* debate) and *The Islamic Commercial Revolution* (the Cairo Geniza in Fustat).

## 3. Saadia Gaon
- Names: Saʿadya ben Yosef; Arabic Saʿīd ibn Yūsuf al-Fayyūmī. [sources: SEP-Saad; WP-Saadia_Gaon]
- **Dates (sources disagree on birth):** SEP-Saad: born in Ḍilāẓ in the Fayyūm, Egypt, "probably in 882"; died 942. SEP-SaadOld: born 882 in Fayyūm, died 942 in Baghdad. WP-Saadia_Gaon: born c. 892 (infobox "c. July 892"), died 16 May 942 at Sura. WRITER: "882 (some sources 892) – 942". Death place: SEP-SaadOld says Baghdad, WP says Sura — say "in Iraq".
- Left Egypt in 915; Palestine, then Aleppo; settled in Iraq (Baghdad) in 922; appointed Gaon of Sura in 928 (expertise in astronomy and the calendar); not from one of the prestigious Babylonian families; expelled in 932 after conflict with the Exilarch; reinstated 936 (SEP) / reconciled four years after 933, i.e. c. 937 (WP); died 942. [sources: SEP-Saad §1.1; WP-Saadia_Gaon] WP: first foreigner ever to serve as Gaon at Sura. [WP-Saadia_Gaon]
- Calendar dispute with Aaron ben Meïr (922) in which Saadia prevailed. [source: WP-Saadia_Gaon] Had corresponded with Isaac Israeli before leaving Egypt. [source: SEP-Saad §1.1]
- Other work: Arabic translation of the Torah (the *Tafsīr*), Hebrew dictionary (*Agron*), prayer book, liturgical poems. [sources: SEP-SaadOld §1; WP-Saadia_Gaon]
- ***Book of Beliefs and Opinions*** (Arabic *Kitāb al-amānāt wa-l-iʿtiqādāt*; Hebrew *Sefer ha-emunot ve-ha-deʿot*), written in Judeo-Arabic, completed **933**; an introduction plus ten treatises; Hebrew translation by Judah ibn Tibbon 1186; unabridged English translation by Samuel Rosenblatt, 1948. [sources: SEP-Saad §2; WP-The_Book_of_Beliefs_and_Opinions] Also *Commentary on Sefer Yeẓira* (931), in a Neoplatonising Ismaili style for a smaller mystical readership. [source: SEP-Saad intro, §1.1]
- "The greatest achievement in Jewish kalam." [source: SEP-Saad §2] Aims: show Judaism meets the highest intellectual standards; defend the Oral Tradition (Mishna, Talmud) against the Karaites; equip believers troubled by doubt. [source: SEP-Saad §2]
- **The Karaites**: a scripturalist movement that sought to base Jewish law on the Hebrew Bible alone and to replace the post-biblical "oral tradition"; Saadia "fiercely defended the Rabbinic version of Judaism and the importance of the Talmud". [source: SEP-Saad §1.1, §2] At 23 he wrote a polemic against followers of Anan ben David (founder figure of Karaism). [source: WP-Saadia_Gaon]
- **The four sources of knowledge**: "four modes of proper knowing, viz. knowledge from sense perception, knowledge from reason, knowledge from inference and knowledge from tradition." [source: SEP-SaadOld §2] WP: three natural sources (sense perception, "the light of reason", logical necessity) plus a fourth, "veritable revelation"/reliable tradition. [source: WP-The_Book_of_Beliefs_and_Opinions] Tradition (Arabic *al-khabar*, "report") gives knowledge all at once and is immediately certain to its recipient; God "prepared in our minds a place for the acceptance of reliable tradition" (Altmann tr.). [source: SEP-SaadOld §3] Reason's intuitive knowledge includes "approbation of truthfulness and disapproval of mendacity" (p. 13). [source: SEP-Saad §2.1]
- Commandments divided into "rational" (e.g. honouring parents) and "revealed" (e.g. food purity laws); revealed laws may later be found to have reasons. [source: SEP-Saad §2.4]
- Free will: "Man cannot be considered as the agent of an act unless he exercises freedom of choice in performing it" (pp. 151–152); God's foreknowledge is outside time and does not cause human acts — echoing the Muʿtazilites. [source: SEP-Saad §2.5]
- God's unity: numbers apply only to material things; against dualists and the Christian Trinity (fault of Christians: holding "God's vitality and omniscience are distinct from His essence", p. 87); anthropomorphic biblical terms are figurative; he runs God through the ten Aristotelian categories by *via negativa*. [source: SEP-Saad §2.3]

### Argument 2 (option A): Saadia's case for creation ex nihilo
- Saadia offers **four rational proofs** of creation ex nihilo, "borrowed from John Philoponus" (Davidson 1969, 1987), without naming him: [source: SEP-Saad §2.2]
  1. **Finitude**: heaven and earth are finite (earth at the centre, heaven revolving round it); a finite body can have only finite power, so the world cannot be eternal. [SEP-Saad §2.2 gives the premise as "finitude of heaven and earth"; the step "finite power" is the standard reading but is not spelled out in SEP-Saad — keep the writer's wording to "the world is finite, so it had a beginning".]
  2. **Composition**: things everywhere are composed of parts, "signs of the handiwork of a Maker".
  3. **Accidents**: the force that attaches accidents to things lies outside them.
  4. **Time**: "If time was endless, the present would not be able to exist since the actual moment would not have been able to traverse infinity" (pp. 32–37).
- He then refutes **twelve** rival cosmogonies (incl. Platonic co-eternal matter, emanation from God's own substance, dualism, the four elements, chance, eternity, the Sophists, scepticism). Against the eternalists: they rely on the senses alone and forget reason and inference. [source: SEP-Saad §2.2]
- Conclusion links creation to God: "there is no means of proving the existence of a Creator other than that of creation" (p. 81). [source: SEP-Saad §2.3]
- **Objection/reply (for the writer)**: Maimonides rejected the kalām method of first proving creation and then inferring a Creator (Guide 1.71–73), holding that creation cannot be demonstrated; he proved God's existence even on the assumption of an eternal world. [source: SEP-Maim §§4–5] Saadia's time argument is the ancestor of the modern "kalām cosmological argument" — NOT VERIFIED for Saadia specifically (Falsafa notes verify Craig revived the kalām argument from Islamic sources); do not draw the line to Craig.

## 4. Isaac Israeli (brief)
- Isaac ben Solomon Israeli: SEP ca. 855–955; WP c. 832 – c. 932 (WP notes other dates) — **sources disagree**: say "tenth century" / "d. c. 932 or later". Physician to the founder of the Fatimid dynasty in North Africa (WP: died at Kairouan). [sources: SEP-Isr intro; WP-Isaac_Israeli_ben_Solomon]
- "One of the earliest medieval Jewish Neoplatonist writers"; influenced by al-Kindī and by the *Theology of Aristotle* or a pseudepigraphic Neoplatonist treatise (S. M. Stern's hypothesis); his medical works were read for centuries in Latin (Constantine the African translated some in the 11th c.). [sources: SEP-Isr intro; WP-Isaac_Israeli]

## 5. Solomon ibn Gabirol (Avicebron)
- Born Málaga 1021/2 (Guttmann: 1026); died most likely in Valencia, most likely 1057/8 (other proposals: 1050, 1054–8, 1070). [source: SEP-Gab §1] Latin names Avicebron/Avicembron/Avencebrol.
- Poet and philosopher; his best-known poem *Keter Malkhut* ("Kingdom's Crown"/"Royal Crown") is in many prayer books for Yom Kippur. [source: SEP-Gab §1]
- *Fons Vitae* ("Fountain of Life"; Arabic *Yanbūʿ al-ḥayāh*, Hebrew *Meqor ḥayyim*): written in Arabic in the 11th c. as a teacher–student dialogue; Arabic original lost except fragments; translated into Latin in the 12th c. by Dominicus Gundissalinus and John of Spain; abridged Hebrew version by Shem Tov ibn Falaquera in the 13th c. [source: SEP-Gab §1] Also *On the Improvement of the Moral Qualities* (Arabic). [same]
- **Universal hylomorphism**: "all things — including soul and intellect — are comprised of matter and form", with an emphasis on the divine Will; a "modified Plotinian universe". [source: SEP-Gab intro] SEP-Med: insisted the universe was produced at God's will, not by necessary emanation, and that matter is present at every level, even in intellectual creatures. [source: SEP-Med §2.6] (SEP-Gab §2.2 keeps open whether Will rules out emanation — a debate.)
- **Latin reception**: medieval Christian scholars took the author to be a Muslim or a Christian; Franciscans read him as an Augustinian and used him against Dominicans such as Aquinas. [source: SEP-Gab §1]
- **Identification**: "In 1846, Solomon Munk discovered among the Hebrew manuscripts in the French National Library in Paris a work by Shem-Tov ibn Falaquera" and, comparing it with Avicebron's Latin *Fons Vitae*, showed they were the same work. [source: WP-Solomon_ibn_Gabirol] SEP-Gab says "the middle of the 19th century". Salomon Munk (1803–1867), German-born French Orientalist, also worked on the *Guide* from the Arabic. [source: WP-Salomon_Munk]
- Abraham ibn Daud criticised Gabirol's philosophy as repetitive and said it "led the Jewish people into error". [source: SEP-Gab §1]

## 6. Bahya ibn Paquda (brief)
- Author of *Guidance to the Duties of the Heart* (Arabic, c. 1080; Hebrew *Ḥovot ha-Levavot*, tr. Judah ibn Tibbon 1161–80); held the post of *dayyan* (rabbinic judge), probably in Zaragoza; argued many Jews attended to outward observance ("duties of the limbs") and neglected inner "duties of the heart". [source: WP-Bahya_ibn_Paquda] Diana Lobel's study is titled *A Sufi-Jewish Dialogue* (cited in WP-Bahya). [same]

## 7. Judah Halevi and the *Kuzari*
- Died 1141; birth year unknown (circumstantial evidence: 1070s or first half of 1080s); likely born in Tudela (WP: Tudela or Toledo, c. 1075); grew up in the Christian north of Iberia; poet, physician, public figure; "one of the greatest Hebrew poets of all time". [sources: SEP-Hal §1, intro; WP-Judah_Halevi]
- Arrived in Egypt (Alexandria) early Sept. 1140 (WP: 8 Sept. 1140), stayed about eight months; sailed from Alexandria in May 1141 for the Land of Israel; probably died there summer 1141 (inferred from Geniza letters). [source: SEP-Hal §1] The legend that he was trampled by an Arab horseman at Jerusalem is first found c. 450 years later. [source: WP-Judah_Halevi] WRITER: give the legend only as legend, or omit.
- Recent Geniza research (Friedman 2013, 2016) suggests the *Kuzari* was written in a short period shortly before 1140, not over many years as Goitein, Gil and Fleischer held. [source: SEP-Hal §1]
- Full title: *Book of Refutation and Proof on Behalf of the Despised Religion* (*Kitāb al-radd waʾl-dalīl fīʾl-dīn al-dhalīl*); written in Judeo-Arabic; Hebrew translation by Judah ibn Tibbon (d. c. 1190). [source: SEP-Hal intro] English translation from the Arabic by Hartwig Hirschfeld, 1905. [source: WP-Kuzari; WS-2]
- **Frame story**: set in the court of a Khazar king in the Jewish year 4500 (= 739–740 CE), four hundred years before writing; after a repeated dream the king summons a philosopher, a Christian and a Muslim, then a Jewish sage (the *Ḥaver*); dialogue with the Jewish sage fills most of the five treatises. The story draws on reports that the Khazar king and people converted to Judaism; the philosopher figure is Halevi's addition. The book ends not with the conversion but with the sage leaving for the Land of Israel; they part as friends without agreement (5:22–28). [source: SEP-Hal §2]
- Krinis reads the frame as a "cover story" aimed at the Jewish-Andalusian courtly elite and its attraction to philosophy. [source: SEP-Hal §2] (Interpretation — attribute to Krinis.)
- Halevi = "the premier formulator of Jewish fideism"; uses sceptical arguments (philosophers' disagreements, equipollence of counterarguments) against philosophy. [source: SEP-Hal intro, §5]
- Although only Greek philosophers are named, the doctrines attacked are those of al-Fārābī, Avicenna and Ibn Bājja, and the formulations of al-Ghazālī; e.g. his critique of the emanation of separate intellects is based on al-Ghazālī (Wolfson). [source: SEP-Hal §3, §5.4.1]
- **Historical revelation**: at Sinai God introduced himself not as "Creator of the world" but as "I am God, whom you worship, who brought you out of the land of Egypt" — a historical event witnessed by the senses and transmitted by multiple chains of transmitters ("concurrent tradition"), not a universal truth proved by argument (1:25). [source: SEP-Hal §6.2.1]
- **God of Abraham vs God of Aristotle** (4:15–16): Aristotle reached by intellect only the name *Elohim*; Abraham, through prophecy, the personal name; "Aristotle took the way of philosophy, and Abraham took the way of prophecy". [source: SEP-Hal §6.2.2]
- Prophecy: against al-Fārābī's theory of prophecy as conjunction with the Active Intellect; Halevi stresses an "internal eye"/"taste" — terms from Sufi literature. [source: SEP-Hal §§3, 6.2.2]

## 8. Abraham ibn Daud (brief)
- c. 1110 – c. 1180; associated with Toledo (WP lead says born in Córdoba; infobox Toledo — say "Andalusian, worked in Toledo"); "considered the first Aristotelian in medieval Jewish philosophy"; philosophical work *al-ʿAqīda al-rafīʿa* (Hebrew *Ha-Emunah ha-Ramah*, "The Exalted Faith"); also the chronicle *Sefer ha-Qabbalah* (1161). Maimonides follows him in many ways on prophecy. [source: WP-Abraham_ibn_Daud] The Arabic original is lost; it survives in Hebrew translations, the first commissioned by Hasdai Crescas. [source: SEP-Cresc §1]

## 9. Maimonides: life and works
- Moses ben Maimon (Rambam), **1138–1204**; birth "often given as 1135, research now supports the later date" (Davidson 2005; Kraemer). [source: SEP-Maim §1 and note 1] WP: 1138 is the date Maimonides gives himself in the *Commentary on the Mishnah*. [WP-Maimonides]
- Born in Córdoba (then under Muslim rule; also birthplace of Averroes). Almohads took Córdoba in **1148** and offered non-Muslims conversion, exile or death; the family wandered and reached **Fez in 1160**. [source: SEP-Maim §1] WP: about ten years in southern Spain and North Africa before settling in Fez.
- Then to the Land of Israel: WP: in Acre with father and brother from 16 May 1165 [WP-Maimonides]; IEP: visited the Holy Land briefly [IEP-Maim §1]. Egypt: SEP "arrived in Egypt in 1166 and eventually settled in Fustat"; WP: settled in Fustat by 1168, possibly Alexandria first and Fustat only in 1171. [sources: SEP-Maim §1; WP-Maimonides]
- Brother David, a merchant who supported the family, was lost at sea on a voyage to India; Maimonides then made his living as a physician. [sources: IEP-Maim §1; WP-Maimonides]
- **Court physician**: physician to al-Qāḍī al-Fāḍil, Saladin's chief secretary, then (WP) to Saladin himself, and after Saladin's death to the Ayyubids. [source: WP-Maimonides] IEP: "physician to a Grand Vizier who was ruling Egypt for the Sultan Saladin". [IEP-Maim §1] WRITER: safest wording "physician at the court of Saladin's Egypt, in the service of his vizier al-Qāḍī al-Fāḍil".
- Nagid (head) of the Egyptian Jewish community from c. 1171 (replaced 1173; later again). [source: WP-Maimonides]
- Died in Fustat, 12 Dec. 1204 (SEP: "Fostat in 1204"); tradition says buried in Tiberias, "though there is no contemporary evidence for his removal from Egypt". [sources: SEP-Maim §1; WP-Maimonides]
- Saying: "from Moses to Moses, there was none like Moses". [source: SEP-Maim §1]
- **Works**: *Treatise on the Art of Logic*; *Commentary on the Mishnah* (finished 1168) with the 13 principles; ***Mishneh Torah*** (14 volumes, in Hebrew; WP: 1170–1180), systematising all the commandments of the Talmud and arguing every law has a rational purpose; ***Guide of the Perplexed*** completed **1190** (WP: 1185–1190), in Judeo-Arabic; *Treatise on Resurrection* 1191; *Letter on Astrology* 1195 (rejects astrology). [sources: SEP-Maim §1; WP-Maimonides] *Epistle to Yemen* 1172–74. [WP-Maimonides]
- The *Guide* is "ostensibly a letter written to an advanced student who cannot decide whether to follow philosophy or the teachings of his religion"; it is a commentary on biblical terms that seem to make God corporeal, a defence of negative theology, a critique of kalām, a treatment of creation, prophecy, providence and law. [source: SEP-Maim §1] Translated into Hebrew by Samuel ibn Tibbon (IEP-Maim §1); standard English translation Shlomo Pines, University of Chicago Press, **1963** (SEP-Maim bibliography; IEP-Maim refs); new translation Goodman & Lieberman, Stanford UP, 2024 (SEP-Maim bibliography).
- Literal reading of the Bible leads to a material God, "which, in Maimonides' opinion, amounts to idolatry". [source: SEP-Maim §1] If an interpretation ascribes a demonstrably false doctrine to the Bible (e.g. God's corporeality), the interpretation is wrong; if science demonstrated the eternity of the world, scripture would have to be reinterpreted (SEP-Maim cites GP 2.24 for this principle). [source: SEP-Maim §3]
- **The 13 principles** (Commentary on Mishnah Sanhedrin ch. 10): existence of God; absolute unity; incorporeality; eternity; God alone to be worshipped; prophecy; Moses the greatest prophet; Torah given by God; Torah immutable; divine providence (God knows human deeds); reward and punishment; the Messiah; resurrection of the dead. "The first attempt to introduce articles of faith to Judaism and set off a controversy that persists to this day (Kellner 1986, 1999)." [source: SEP-Maim §1] Crescas later built his *Or Hashem* on a rival hierarchy of dogmas opposed to the 13 principles. [source: SEP-Cresc §2.4] Debate whether he genuinely accepted bodily resurrection continues. [sources: IEP-Maim §6; SEP-Maim §1]

## 10. Maimonides' negative theology (MAIN ARGUMENT, step by step)
Sources: SEP-Maim §4; SEP-MaimIsl §3.1; IEP-Maim §§4–5.
- **Starting point**: proofs of God's existence (Guide 2.1) are versions of the cosmological argument, e.g. from the eternal motion of the heavens to an infinite power that is "neither a body nor a force in a body". They show *that* God is, not *what* God is. [SEP-Maim §4]
- **P1 (absolute unity)**: God is "one in all respects; no multiplicity should be posited in Him; there is no notion that is superadded to His essence" (Guide 1.52, Pines tr., as quoted in SEP-MaimIsl §3.1, cited there as "P 378" — probably a misprint for p. 118; give the chapter only). In God, essence and existence are identical (Guide 1.57, Pines p. 132). [SEP-MaimIsl §3.1; SEP-DDS]
- **P2 (why plurality is impossible)**: if God were a composite of F and G, something would have to bring and keep them together, i.e. a cause prior to God, which is absurd (Guide 2 Intro, premise 21). Nor can God fall under a genus (as man falls under animal), since then something would be prior to or wider than God (1.52). So God cannot be defined. [SEP-Maim §4]
- **P3 (no likeness)**: God's power, knowledge, life are not bigger versions of ours; God cannot be put "on the same scale" as anything created (1.56). Terms used of God and us are "completely equivocal" (purely homonymous) — neither univocal nor analogical. [SEP-Maim §4; IEP-Maim §5]
- **P4 (positive attributes add plurality and comparison)**: "God is wise, powerful, living" taken as separate attributes would introduce plurality; and affirmation implies a likeness. "Know that when you make an affirmation ascribing another thing to Him, you become more remote from Him in two respects: one of them is that everything you affirm is a perfection only with reference to us, and the other is that He does not possess a thing other than His essence" (Guide 1.59, Pines p. 139). [SEP-MaimIsl §3.1]
- **C1 (essential attributes are impossible)**: God "cannot have an affirmative attribute in any respect" (1.58, Pines p. 135). [SEP-MaimIsl §3.1]
- **C2 (two permitted kinds of language)**:
  (a) **Attributes of action**: "Every attribute that is found in the books of the deity ... is therefore an attribute of His action and not an attribute of His essence" (Guide 1.53, Pines p. 121) [IEP-Maim §5]. "God is merciful" describes the effects of God's activity (e.g. the well-ordered care of the embryo), not an emotion in God; fire analogy: fire softens wax, hardens clay, blackens and whitens, without being soft, hard, black or white. Maimonides links these to God's "goodness" shown to Moses at Exodus 33: Moses sees God's "back" (effects), not his "face" (essence). [SEP-Maim §4; SEP-MaimIsl §3.1]
  (b) **Negations**: "God is powerful" = "God is not lacking in power" — and, more radically, does not possess it in any way comparable to other things (1.58). Negations give no false picture of the essence; "the more you can prove inapplicable to God, the better" (1.59, Goodman–Lieberman tr. as quoted in SEP-Maim). [SEP-Maim §4]
- **C3 (silence)**: even negation is imperfect; theological language is a heuristic that removes error; citing Psalm 65, the highest praise is silence. "When the tongues aspire to magnify Him by means of attributive qualifications, all eloquence turns into weariness and incapacity!" (1.58, Pines p. 137). [SEP-Maim §4; SEP-MaimIsl §3.1]
- Practical upshot: daily prayer still obligatory; its descriptions are read as negations or attributes of action. [SEP-Maim §4]
- **Objection 1 (emptiness)**: if "wise", "powerful", "lives" are completely equivocal, the conception left "is too thin for the average worshipper"; from an atheist's side, removing all anthropomorphic content removes "all content of any kind". [source: SEP-Maim §8] IEP: it "may seem to cut us off from any grounding of semantic meaning". [IEP-Maim §4]
- **Objection 2 (Gersonides, then Crescas)**: Gersonides rejected Maimonides' *via negativa* [WS-4/5: Feldman's review of his *Gersonides* book; weaker source], holding that divine and human attributes share a meaning but differ in degree. SEP-Cresc §3.2 (strong source): Crescas first defended Maimonides "against the arguments of Gersonides", but later held that human and divine attributes are "common in essence but divergent in quantity" — most probably from Gersonides — and infinite in God. WRITER: present the objection as "if 'knows' said of God shares nothing with 'knows' said of us, we cannot even say God knows rather than does not know; attributes must be the same in kind, perfect and infinite in God" — attribute the idea to Gersonides and Crescas in that general form only; do not quote Wars III.3 (not read).
- **Reply (Maimonides)**: (i) we still know *that* God is, and can speak meaningfully of what God causes (attributes of action) — "speaking of what God causes is a meaningful way to speak of God" [IEP-Maim §4]; (ii) negations progressively narrow what God is not and so increase knowledge (the more negations, the closer the apprehension) [SEP-Maim §4, 1.59]; (iii) on divine knowledge specifically: "between our knowledge and His knowledge there is nothing in common, as there is nothing in common between our essence and His essence ... there is a community only in the terms" (Guide 3.20, Pines p. 482) — so the paradoxes we generate (e.g. foreknowledge vs freedom) come from treating the word univocally. [IEP-Maim §4] Seeskin's sympathetic gloss: the insight preserved is Socratic — the wisest person "recoils in awe and humility" before what he cannot fathom. [SEP-Maim §8]

## 11. Creation vs eternity
- Four accounts considered: kalām, Moses, Plato, Aristotle. He rejects kalām's method of proving creation first (1.71–73). From 2.13 three options: **Moses** — created de novo and ex nihilo; **Plato** — created de novo from pre-existing matter; **Aristotle** — eternal. [source: SEP-Maim §5] Maimonides' characterisations "are neither precise nor historically accurate" (Seeskin). [same]
- **Neither is demonstrable**: like Aquinas, he holds it impossible to show by logic alone that the world is created or eternal; one can only "tip the scales". [source: SEP-Maim §5] IEP: he analysed the arguments for eternity and concluded it "could be neither proved nor disproved". [IEP-Maim §3]
- Why Aristotle's arguments fail: (1) from nature — change is always from something, but the origin of a thing need not follow the laws of its later development (2.17); (2) from God — a perfect being can will something new without changing, if it was part of his original intention: "changing one's will is not the same as willing change" (2.18). [source: SEP-Maim §5]
- Positive tilt to creation: features of the heavens (speeds and order of spheres, distribution of stars; 2.19–24) look chosen, not necessary; and how could complexity arise from an utterly simple first cause by necessary emanation (2.22)? Not a demonstration (2.24). Practical reason: a God without free will could not issue commandments (2.25); textual reason: creation "does less violence to scripture". [source: SEP-Maim §5]
- Prefers Moses, allows Plato as acceptable. [source: SEP-Maim §5]
- Problem: divine simplicity makes God's will identical with God's wisdom, which pushes toward necessity (Harvey 1981); "the question of how to think about the relation between will and wisdom in God is never settled". [source: SEP-Maim §5]

## 12. Prophecy
- Prophecy is "an overflow overflowing from God ... through the intermediation of the active intellect" (Guide 2.36, Pines p. 369); "This is the highest degree of man and the ultimate term of perfection that can exist for his species" (2.36, Pines p. 369). [source: SEP-MaimIsl §6.5]
- **Three opinions** (Guide 2.32, which compares them with the three opinions on creation, Pines p. 361): (1) the vulgar view — God can make anyone a prophet; (2) the philosophers' view — prophecy is a natural perfection of intellect, imagination and moral character, so a perfected person necessarily prophesies; (3) "the opinion of our Law" — as the philosophers say, "except in one thing ... it may happen that one who is fit for prophecy and prepared for it should not become a prophet, namely, on account of the divine will" (the "divine veto"). [sources: IEP-Maim §6; SEP-MaimIsl §6.5]
- Intellect alone affected → speculative thinker; intellect and imagination → prophet; imagination only → (IEP) "lawgiver" [IEP-Maim §6] (WRITER: safer to say "statesmen, diviners and dreamers" is NOT verified; use IEP's wording or omit the third case).
- Moses' prophecy is a unique exception, entirely intellectual. [source: IEP-Maim §6] How naturalistic the view really is remains "open to scholarly debate". [source: SEP-MaimIsl §6.5]

## 13. Esotericism and contradictions
- Introduction to the *Guide*: seven causes of contradictions in books; "Divergences that are to be found in this Treatise are due to the fifth cause and the seventh" (Pines p. 17). Fifth = necessities of teaching; seventh = very obscure matters where parts are concealed and others disclosed. [source: SEP-MaimIsl §7 area]
- On the seventh: "In such cases the vulgar must in no way be aware of the contradiction; the author accordingly uses some device to conceal it by all means." [source: SEP-Maim §7; wording also attributed to Pines's translation in WS-5 — treat as Pines, p. 18, with modest confidence]
- Jewish law forbids public teaching of the "Account of the Beginning" (= physics) and "Account of the Chariot" (= metaphysics). [source: SEP-Maim §§2, 7]

## 14. The Maimonidean controversy (brief)
- The *Guide* "in some rabbinic circles was originally banned." [source: SEP-Maim §1]
- 1232: rabbis led by Jonah Gerondi and Solomon ben Abraham of Montpellier banned the study of philosophy including the *Guide* and the *Sefer ha-Madda*; "in 1232 Maimonides' books were confiscated and burnt by Dominicans (although there are stories about this being initiated by anti-Maimonideans ... the historical situation is more than unclear)". Later phases: Abraham Maimonides' defence; 1305 ban by Solomon ibn Adret of Barcelona on studying physics and metaphysics before age 25 (WP: local ban against extreme allegory and Greek physics/metaphysics); ended with the expulsion of Jews from France in 1306. [source: WP-Maimonidean_Controversy] WRITER: say "in 1232 (some accounts 1233) Dominicans burned copies, reportedly at the instigation of anti-Maimonideans — the details are disputed"; place (Montpellier/Paris) NOT VERIFIED.

## 15. Gersonides
- Levi ben Gershom (acronym Ralbag; Latin Leo Hebraeus), born **1288** in Provence (possibly Bagnols-sur-Cèze), died **20 April 1344**; probably lived mostly in Orange; wrote in Hebrew; may have read Latin. [sources: SEP-Gers §2; WP-Gersonides]
- ***Milḥamot ha-Shem* (Wars of the Lord)**, begun 1317 as an essay on creation, completed **1329**; six books: immortality of the soul; prophecy; God's knowledge of particulars; providence; astronomy; creation. An opponent called it "Wars against the Lord". [source: SEP-Gers intro, §§3–4]
- First Jewish philosopher to use the analytic, scholastic method; plain style (Husik: "monotonous and wearisome"). [source: SEP-Gers §3]
- Reason: "the Law cannot prevent us from considering to be true that which our reason urges us to believe." [source: SEP-Gers §2] Feldman calls him "a most vigorous and consistent defender of human reason in religion". [SEP-Gers §1]
- Creation: against Maimonides, holds creation demonstrable; "unambiguously Platonic": created by a free agent from an eternal formless matter (ex nihilo judged incompatible with physics). [source: SEP-Gers §4]
- **Science**: astronomy in Wars V.1 (136 chapters); invented an instrument he called *Megalle ʿamuqqot* ("Revealer of Profundities"), called **Jacob's staff** (*baculus Jacobi*) by Christians, to measure star heights; the astronomical part was translated into Latin in his lifetime; lunar crater "Rabbi Levi". [source: SEP-Gers §3] WP: generally accepted that he first described the Jacob's staff, though Needham suggests Shen Kuo (1088) described one. [WP-Jacob's_staff] Preferred his own observations over Ptolemy's. [SEP-Gers §3] Also *Maʿaseh Ḥoshev* (1321, arithmetic), commentary on Job (1325), logical treatise (1319). [SEP-Gers §3]

### Argument 2 (option B): Gersonides on divine knowledge of future contingents
Source: SEP-Gers §4.
- P1: If God knows future contingents before they occur, they are not contingent (divine knowledge "precludes contingency") — Gersonides rejects Maimonides' claim that God's knowledge does not necessitate its objects.
- P2: There is real contingency (human choice); the Torah's commands presuppose it.
- P3: God's knowledge is perfect; not knowing something in the way it really is would not be a defect.
- C: God knows particulars "only insofar as they are ordered": he knows the general order (e.g. individuals through the species), and knows that certain states of affairs may or may not be actualised, but not which alternative will occur.
- Corollaries: prophecy gives general knowledge which the prophet applies to particulars; providence is general, attaching to species and only incidentally to individuals ("God ... does not know the particular individual Levi ben Gerson"). He presents this as a middle way between Aristotle (God does not know particulars) and Maimonides (he does).
- Objection: this limits God's knowledge and seems to undercut individual providence and prophecy of specific events (critics called his book "Wars against the Lord"). Reply (Gersonides): God knows contingents perfectly *as contingents*; the order of things he knows is the whole intelligible content of particulars, and providence reaches individuals in proportion as they belong to well-ordered groups/perfect their intellect. [SEP-Gers §4; the last clause is SEP's account of providence via species and "the species philosopher"]
- Maimonides' rival reply on the same problem: the word "knowledge" is equivocal (Guide 3.20, Pines p. 482) — see §10.

## 16. Hasdai Crescas (transition)
- c. 1340 – 1410/11; Barcelona, then rabbi of Saragossa (1389); chief judge of the Jews of the Crown of Aragon; lost a son in the pogroms of 1391. [source: SEP-Cresc §1]
- *Or Hashem* (The Light of the Lord) was planned as an attack on the *Guide* (with a planned legal work against the *Mishneh Torah*). He criticised 14 of Maimonides' 26 Aristotelian premises and his proofs. [source: SEP-Cresc §§2.4, 3.1]
- **Infinite**: an actual infinite is possible; one infinite can be larger than another; infinite void with possibly infinite worlds. [source: SEP-Cresc §4]
- **Free will**: an "uncompromising determinism" compatible with choice; humans like bronze shaped by external causes; reward and punishment as natural consequences. [source: SEP-Cresc §5]
- Philosophy can prove a simple first cause but not the unique God of the Bible; revelation stands above philosophy. [source: SEP-Cresc §3.1]
- Influenced Pico della Mirandola and Spinoza. [source: SEP-Cresc intro, §4]

## 17. Reception
- Maimonides influenced "thinkers as diverse as Aquinas, Spinoza, Leibniz, and Newton". [source: SEP-Maim intro] WP adds Albertus Magnus and Duns Scotus. [WP-Maimonides]
- Gersonides' ideas, via Crescas and others, reached Leibniz and Spinoza. [source: SEP-Gers intro]
- For a naturalist like Spinoza, Maimonides was too ready to fall back on creation and divine will. [source: SEP-Maim §8]

## Debates
- **Esotericism / Strauss**: Leo Strauss (1952) argued contradictions are central and "the more evidence Maimonides presents for a particular view, the less likely it is that he held it"; "Of late the esotericist reading appears to be losing favor" (Davidson 2005, Ivry 1991, Manekin 2005, Ravitzky 2005, Seeskin 2000). There is general agreement he is esoteric at least in scattering topics. [source: SEP-Maim §7] IEP frames it as whether he was ultimately "loyal" to philosophy or to Judaism. [IEP-Maim §2]
- **His true view on creation**: explicit view = Moses (Feldman, Hyman, Wolfson, Seeskin); secretly Aristotle (Harvey 1981; Straussians); really Plato (Davidson 1979); combination Plato/Aristotle (Ivry 2016). [source: SEP-Maim §5] Others read the *Guide*'s conclusions as sceptical (SEP-Med §2.6; Stern 2013; Ivry: Maimonides is himself one of the perplexed, SEP-Maim §8).
- **Prophecy**: how naturalistic, and what the "divine veto" means. [source: SEP-MaimIsl §6.5]
- **Halevi and al-Ghazālī**: Halevi's critique of emanation is "based on al-Ghazali" (Wolfson); the doctrines he attacks follow al-Ghazālī's formulations (Kaufmann 1877, Baneth, Kogan 2002); WP compares their aims ("Like al-Ghazali, Judah Halevi attempted to liberate religion from the bondage of philosophical systems"). But SEP-Hal also stresses Sufi vocabulary and Halevi's own fideism; how far he depends on al-Ghazālī is a matter of degree, discussed by Kogan ("Al-Ghazali and Halevi on Philosophy", 2002). [sources: SEP-Hal §§3, 5.4.1, bibliography; WP-Jewish_philosophy] WRITER: present as parallel and influence, not identity.
- **Kuzari frame**: literal apologetic vs Krinis's "cover story" aimed at the Andalusian elite; dating (Goitein/Gil/Fleischer vs Friedman). [source: SEP-Hal §§1–2]
- **Maimonides' birth year** 1135 vs 1138. [SEP-Maim n.1]
- **Saadia's sources**: kalām vs Greek; his creation proofs from Philoponus (Davidson). [SEP-Saad §2.2]
- **Pines on sources**: Pines's "Translator's Introduction" is classic but debated; more scholars now find direct influence of Averroes and al-Ghazālī in the *Guide*. [source: SEP-MaimIsl intro]

## Consistency with published Periergia texts
- *History of Philosophy* overview: "Maimonides (d. 1204) ... pioneering negative theology (of God we can say only what He is not) and a hermeneutics of religious language that Aquinas read closely." — "pioneering" is an OVERSTATEMENT: SEP-Philo calls Philo "the founder of negative theology in ... monotheistic faith", and Saadia already used a *via negativa* (SEP-Saad §2.3). Also Maimonides allows attributes of action, not only negations. Record as contradiction; the new chapter should say Maimonides gave negative theology its most rigorous medieval form. "Aquinas read closely": consistent with SEP-Maim (influenced Aquinas).
- *Aristotle* chapter: Maimonides 1138–1204, *Guide* in Judeo-Arabic, neither creation nor eternity demonstrable, preferred Moses — CONSISTENT (but the new chapter must add that his true view is debated).
- *Plotinus and Neoplatonism*: Israeli in the 10th century drew on the Arabic Plotinus; Gabirol's *Fountain of Life* in Arabic, "a modified Plotinian universe"; taken for Muslim or Christian "until the nineteenth century"; Maimonides received Neoplatonism via the *Theology* — CONSISTENT (SEP-Gab, SEP-Isr, WP 1846).
- *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes*: Maimonides acquired Averroes' commentaries (c. 1190) — consistent; no conflict.
- *Falsafa*: Maimonides took up Avicenna's necessary-existent argument — consistent (Guide 1.57 echoes Avicenna, SEP-MaimIsl §3.1).
- *What Is Philosophy?*: Philo "harmonised scripture with Greek philosophy", Saadia → Maimonides — consistent.
- History: *The Rise of Islam and the Caliphates* (Córdoba caliphate 929; *convivencia* debate) and *Islamic Commercial Revolution* (Cairo Geniza in Fustat) — consistent; Halevi's last years are known from Geniza letters (SEP-Hal). *Crusades* (Saladin) — consistent.
