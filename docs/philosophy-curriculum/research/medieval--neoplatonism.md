## 1. The label "Neoplatonism"
- "Neoplatonic" is a modern coinage and "to some extent a misnomer": the late-antique philosophers called themselves "Platonists"; their project is better described as "a grand synthesis" of almost the entire Hellenic tradition, except Epicureanism and Stoic corporealism, which they rejected. [source: SEP-Neo intro]
- IEP: "The term 'Neoplatonism' is a modern construction. Plotinus ... would not have considered himself a 'new' Platonist in any sense, but simply an expositor of the doctrines of Plato." [source: IEP-Neo §1]
- Wikipedia cites an older SEP version: the term is "an invention of early 19th century European scholarship", meant to mark Plotinus as starting a new phase; etymonline dates first appearance to 1827. [source: WP-Neoplatonism, "Origins of the term"] (1827 is etymonline via WP only: say "early nineteenth century".)
- Neoplatonism is "not an ancient philosophical school, but a modern historical category" whose reliability some scholars question (Catana 2013); there was "no unitary Neoplatonic school", but several schools over some three centuries with sometimes close, sometimes tenuous relations; no institutional continuity between Plotinus and Iamblichus despite later claims of a "golden chain". [source: SEP-Iamb §2.1]
- Time span: SEP-Neo gives roughly mid-3rd to mid-7th century (from the Imperial Crisis to the Arab conquest). IEP-Neo instead defines it as running from Plotinus to Justinian's closing of the Academy in 529. [sources: SEP-Neo intro; IEP-Neo intro] WRITER: note that the end-date is a convention.
- Through much of history Neoplatonic readings were taken as Plato's own doctrine (Ficino, for instance, thought so). In a 1929 essay E. R. Dodds traced key Neoplatonic ideas back through Plato, Speusippus and the Neopythagoreans, calling Plotinus "not the starting-point of neoplatonism but its intellectual culmination". [source: WP-Neoplatonism; WP-Plotinus]
- For several centuries after the Reformation, Neoplatonism was condemned as a "decadent and 'oriental' distortion of Platonism". [source: WP-Plotinus]

## 2. Plotinus: life (from Porphyry's *Life of Plotinus*)
- Almost all of Plotinus' work survives, thanks to Porphyry's edition, which he prefaced with his *Life of Plotinus* (*Vita Plotini*, VP), "a sufficiently reliable account". [source: SEP-Plot §1] Porphyry probably composed the *Life* in 301 CE. [source: SEP-Porph §1]
- Birth: Porphyry says Plotinus died aged 66 at the end of the second year of Claudius II (269/70), so he computes a birth in the 13th year of Septimius Severus (204/5). [source: WP-Plotinus] SEP-Plot: "we may infer that he was born in 204 CE"; SEP-Neo: 204/5–270; IEP-Plot: 204–270.
- Birthplace: Porphyry says only that he was "a native of Egypt" [SEP-Plot §1]; IEP: "in Egypt, the exact location of which is unknown" [IEP-Plot §1]. The later writer Eunapius says "Lyco", which could be Asyut (Lycopolis) in Upper Egypt or a Delta Lycopolis. [source: WP-Plotinus] WRITER: say "born in Egypt, perhaps at Lycopolis (Asyut), according to a later source".
- His ethnicity (Hellenised Egyptian, Greek, or Romanised) is speculation; Plotinus never discussed his ancestry, childhood or birth date and refused to have his portrait painted. [source: WP-Plotinus]
- At 28 (c. 232) he turned to philosophy in Alexandria; dissatisfied with every teacher until a friend sent him to Ammonius Saccas, on whom he said "this is the man I was looking for"; he stayed with Ammonius eleven years. [sources: SEP-Plot §1; WP-Plotinus; WP-Ammonius_Saccas quotes the passage]
- Ammonius Saccas: Alexandrian, self-taught, reportedly published nothing, "one of the most enigmatic philosophers of all antiquity" (SEP-Neo §1). The Christian Origen may also have been his pupil (SEP-Neo §1: "may also have been"). [sources: SEP-Neo §1; WP-Ammonius_Saccas] Hierocles (5th c.) reports that Ammonius taught that Plato and Aristotle agreed. [source: WP-Ammonius_Saccas] "Saccas" is often read as "sack-carrier/porter" (Theodoret), but the meaning is disputed; claims of an Indian origin for Ammonius are contested. [source: WP-Ammonius_Saccas] Porphyry says Ammonius' parents were Christians and he turned pagan; Eusebius and Jerome deny it; possibly there were two men named Ammonius. [source: WP-Ammonius_Saccas]
- Persian expedition: Plotinus joined Gordian III's campaign against Persia in 243 (SEP-Plot; WP: 242–243; IEP: he left Ammonius in 242) because he wanted to become "acquainted with the wisdom of the Persians and the Indians" (SEP-Plot's rendering; WP quotes Armstrong's Loeb: "he became eager to make acquaintance with the Persian philosophical discipline and that prevailing among the Indians"). Gordian was murdered by his troops [SEP-Plot]; Plotinus escaped with difficulty to Antioch [WP-Plotinus, citing Britannica]. Gordian III died c. 11 February 244, aged 19. [source: WP-Gordian_III] (WP-Gordian_III says ancient accounts of his death differ; "murdered by his troops" is SEP-Plot's wording. WRITER: "died on campaign, probably murdered".)
- Rome: settled there late in 244 (SEP-Plot), "around 245, at the age of 40" (SEP-Neo), during the reign of Philip the Arab (WP); taught for more than 25 years until his death in 270. Lectures open to the public, with a core of trusted pupils. [sources: SEP-Plot §1; SEP-Neo §1; WP-Plotinus]
- Teaching method: sessions began by reading out commentaries on Plato and Aristotle (by e.g. contemporary exegetes), then discussing the "difficulties" (aporiai), then Plotinus' own analysis (VP 14). [source: SEP-Plot §1] Porphyry: his writings are "full of concealed Stoic and Peripatetic doctrines: Aristotle's Metaphysics, in particular, is concentrated in them" (VP 14.4–6). [source: SEP-Plot §1, translator not named]
- Circle: Porphyry; Amelius Gentilianus of Tuscany; the senator Castricius Firmus; the doctor Eustochius of Alexandria; Zethos, "an Arab by ancestry", who left him land in Campania; several senators; women including Gemina, in whose house he lived, her daughter Gemina, and Amphiclea (wife of Iamblichus' son Ariston) (VP 9). [source: WP-Plotinus]
- Writing began about ten years after he reached Rome (c. 253/4), i.e. in roughly the last sixteen years of his life, from age about fifty; the treatises were "reminders" of classroom discussions. [source: SEP-Plot §1; WP-Plotinus: "from c. 253"] His eyesight was poor, his handwriting bad, and he would not revise; Porphyry edited. [source: IEP-Plot §1; WP-Plotinus]
- Gallienus (emperor Sept 253 – Sept 268 [WP-Gallienus]) and his wife Salonina honoured Plotinus; Plotinus asked them to rebuild a ruined "city of philosophers" in Campania to be governed by Plato's *Laws* (VP 12). [source: WP-Plotinus] The English translation of VP 12 gives the planned name "Platonopolis". [source: WS-13] Why it failed: WP says "for reasons unknown to Porphyry"; search summaries of the text say Porphyry blamed jealousy or spite among the emperor's associates. [WP-Plotinus; WS-13] WRITER: say "courtiers blocked it, Porphyry says".
- Death: in 270, in seclusion on an estate in Campania (IEP: "died in solitude at Campania"); Eustochius attended him. [sources: IEP-Plot §1; WP-Plotinus] Porphyry was in Sicily when he died (he had left in 268). [source: SEP-Porph §1] (WP-Plotinus says that Plotinus "went to live in Sicily", which is a confusion with Porphyry: see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Porphyry tells that a snake slipped under the bed and out through a hole in the wall at the moment Plotinus died. [source: WP-Plotinus]

## 3. The *Enneads* and their arrangement
- Porphyry divided the work into 54 treatises, splitting some and joining others to reach that number, and grouped them into six sets of nine (Greek *ennea*, "nine"). [sources: WP-Enneads; IEP-Plot §1; SEP-Neo §1] He published them in three volumes (I–III; IV–V; VI). [source: WP-Enneads]
- The order runs from ethics (I) through the physical world (II–III) to Soul (IV), Intellect (V) and the One (VI): from easy to hard, as Porphyry intended (VP 24–26). [sources: SEP-Plot §1; WP-Enneads] The arrangement has often been criticised as "utterly artificial and arbitrary". [source: SEP-Plot §1]
- Porphyry also recorded the chronological order (VP 4–6), which some modern editors follow; e.g. I.6 "On Beauty" was the first treatise written, and I.7 the last (54th). [sources: SEP-Plot §1; WP-Enneads] II.9 (no. 33), traditionally "Against the Gnostics", has the full title "Against those who say that the maker of the world is evil and the world is evil". [source: WP-Enneads]
- Citation form: Ennead, treatise, chapter, line (e.g. IV.8.1), based on the Henry–Schwyzer critical edition (1951–73; Oxford editio minor 1964–84). [sources: WP-Enneads; IEP-Plot §1]
- Standard English translations: Stephen MacKenna (1917–30, revised by B. S. Page 1956); A. H. Armstrong, Loeb, 7 vols (1966–88); Lloyd P. Gerson (ed.), Cambridge (2018). [sources: WP-Plotinus bibliography; SEP-Plot bibliography] (WP gives Gerson's as 2017; Cambridge's edition year needs checking: use "2018" only if confirmed, otherwise "recent Cambridge translation edited by Lloyd Gerson".)

## 4. The system: the One, Intellect, Soul
- Three supra-sensible "hypostases" (realities): the One, Intellect (*nous*) and Soul; *Ennead* IV treats soul, V–VI Intellect, VI the One. [source: SEP-Plot §1] "Hypostasis" comes from a verb meaning "to place oneself under", with the sense of "standing one's ground"; it later acquired new meanings in Christian Trinitarian theology. [source: SEP-Neo §3]
- The One, also called the Good, is "beyond being", absolute unity; Plotinus identifies it with the One of the first hypothesis of Plato's *Parmenides* and the Form of the Good "beyond being" of *Republic* VI. [source: SEP-Plot §7]
- No predicate can be attributed to the One, since all predicates refer to beings; it is known only negatively, and any talk of it uses metaphor. [source: SEP-Plot §7] Plotinus denies the One self-awareness or any activity (*ergon*) in the ordinary sense (V.6.6). [source: WP-Plotinus]
- Plotinus calls the One "the (productive) power of all things" (*dunamis pantōn*), cause even of itself (VI.8.14), and identifies it with its own unconstrained "will" (*boulēsis*, VI.8.21). [source: SEP-Plot §7]
- Intellect is a "multiple unity" (*hen polla*): the Forms are not inert abstractions but living intellects; Intellect is identical with its objects, so its thinking is complete self-thinking, non-discursive, grasping everything at once (like seeing a face before analysing it). [source: SEP-Plot §6] SEP-Porph §3: Intellect "is the sphere of real being, identified with the Platonic Forms, which are also the thoughts of a universal intellect".
- The five "greatest kinds" of Plato's *Sophist* (Being, Motion, Rest, Sameness, Difference) are the basic categories of Intellect. [source: SEP-Plot §6]
- Soul is the outer activity of Intellect; it carries images of the Forms into the lower world; the whole sensible world "rests in Soul, not the other way round". Below Soul in the hierarchy is Nature (*phusis*), a lower, silently contemplative aspect of soul that produces the natural world. [source: SEP-Neo §4] Plotinus' treatise III.8 is titled "On Nature, Contemplation and the One". [source: WP-Enneads]
- Plotinus held that individual souls do not wholly descend: something of the soul always remains "undescended" in the intelligible world, and the body is animated by an image of soul. [sources: SEP-Plot §2; SEP-Iamb §6.1]
- The pagan Neoplatonists were not creationists: the emergence of the world from the One goes on eternally, without a beginning in time; this became a contested point when Christians, Muslims and Jews adapted the system. [source: SEP-Neo §2]
- Against the Gnostics (II.9), Plotinus defended the goodness and beauty of the cosmos against the view that it was the work of a foolish or malevolent creator. [source: SEP-Plot §4] Armstrong saw the treatise as vehement; Gerson's translation team reads it as less vitriolic. [source: WP-Plotinus]

## 5. Procession and return ("emanation")
- The "theory of two activities": each thing has an internal activity that makes it what it is, and an accompanying external activity that flows from it, like heat from fire; the external activity depends on the internal but does not diminish it. This is the core of so-called "emanation". [source: SEP-Plot §5 (FP2, FP3, SP1); SEP-Porph §3]
- Each lower level is formed in two phases: "procession" (*proodos*), an indeterminate outflow, then "reversion/return" (*epistrophē*), turning back to its source, which shapes it into a new hypostasis. With "abiding" (*monē*) this gives the triad abiding–procession–reversion (Hadot 1960). [source: SEP-Plot §5 SP3, §8]
- Wildberg warns that "emanation" is a misleading word, with "obviously materialist connotations"; the Neoplatonists said "procession and return" (*prohodos* and *epistrophê*); nothing spatial, material or craftsman-like happens. [source: SEP-Neo §3] WRITER: the published overview says "a metaphysics of emanation"; keep the word but gloss it.
- Love (*erōs*) drives the return: every level strives towards the Good; even Intellect, transcending itself, enters "inspired inebriation" (V.1.6, VI.7.35). [source: SEP-Plot §8]
- Principle of plenitude (FP4): any real possibility must be realised, so the One's power necessarily overflows. [source: SEP-Plot §5]

## 6. Matter and evil
- Matter is the last product, where the chain of activity exhausts itself, like darkness at the limit of light; it is "no thing", formless and passive, but not nothing. [source: SEP-Neo §5]
- Plotinus (I.8, "On the Nature and Source of Evil") makes matter the primary cause of evil because it is a complete privation of form; yet as non-being it has no power to act, and is only the precondition of wickedness in the soul, which is distracted by the reflections of the Good appearing in matter. [sources: SEP-Plot §4; WP-Enneads for the title]
- Evil therefore does not come "from above"; matter is not an independent Gnostic or Manichaean evil principle. [source: SEP-Neo §5]
- Proclus wrote a whole treatise (*On the Existence of Evils*) rejecting Plotinus' identification of evil with matter: evil has only a "parasitic existence" (*parhypostasis*), an unintended by-product of actions aimed at good (De mal. §50, trans. Opsomer and Steel 2003). Pseudo-Dionysius adopted this view in *Divine Names* 4, and through him it dominated medieval discussion of evil. [sources: SEP-Procl §3.4; SEP-Neo §5]

## 7. The return of the soul: virtue, contemplation, union
- Plotinus' ladder of virtues: "political" (civic) virtues regulating the body's impulses; "cathartic" (purificatory) virtues freeing the soul from concern with the body; "theoretical" virtues; and the "paradigmatic" virtues, the Forms of the virtues in Intellect (I.2). Conforming to them makes a person "resemble God" (I.2.6). [source: SEP-Plot §4] (Porphyry systematised this grading in the *Sentences*; later Neoplatonists extended it: Marinus lists natural, ethical, political, purifying, intellectual and theurgic virtues. [SEP-Procl §1.1])
- Union: through "cathartic" practices humans can reach a complete experiential "union" or "contact" with the One (V.3.17, VI.7.34–36). [source: SEP-Plot §7] The term used in scholarship is *henōsis*. [source: WP-Plotinus]
- Porphyry reports that Plotinus attained union four times while Porphyry knew him (VP 23), and that Porphyry himself did so once, in his 68th year. [sources: WP-Plotinus; WS-14 quoting MacKenna's translation: "Four times, during the period I passed with him, he achieved this Term"]
- Life's purpose, for the Neoplatonists, is deification, not ordinary civic virtue; they would protest against the Christian claim that salvation had been won vicariously by another. [source: SEP-Neo §6]
- Plotinus' last words (VP 2), in several translations: "to bring back the god in us to the divine in the All" (SEP-Neo §6); "Strive to bring back the god in yourselves to the God in the All" (IEP-Neo §2c, Moore's own translation); "Try to raise the divine in yourselves to the divine in the all" (WP-Plotinus, citing Mark Edwards 2000). [sources as listed] WRITER: say the wording varies by translator.

## 8. Porphyry (c. 234 – c. 305)
- Born probably 234 at Tyre in Phoenicia; his Semitic name was Malchos ("king"), which he rendered "Basileus". Studied with Longinus in Athens; with Plotinus in Rome 263–268; left for Sicily in 268 on Plotinus' advice to recover from depression ("melancholy"); probably died around 305. [source: SEP-Porph intro, §1]
- At first he wrote a refutation of Plotinus (that the intelligibles exist outside Intellect), then recanted in a "palinode" read to the class (VP 18). [source: SEP-Porph §1]
- He met the Christian Origen "while still quite young", probably at Caesarea (fragment preserved by Eusebius). [source: SEP-Porph §1]
- Late in life he married Marcella, a widow with seven children, justifying it by her philosophical nature (*Letter to Marcella*). [source: SEP-Porph §1]
- He wrote over sixty works (Goulet's catalogue), most lost. Extant: *Life of Plotinus*, *Life of Pythagoras*, *Letter to Marcella*, *On Abstinence from Animal Food* (a defence of vegetarianism, partly on grounds of justice to animals), the *Sentences* (*Sententiae*), the *Isagoge*, *On the Cave of the Nymphs*, and commentaries. [source: SEP-Porph §2, §3.2.2]
- He was the first Platonist to comment on Aristotle and held that Plato and Aristotle agreed on essentials. [source: SEP-Porph intro]
- *Isagoge* ("Introduction"), addressed to the senator Chrysaorius: treats the "five items" genus, species, differentia, property (*proprium*) and accident; the first extant text to give this list, reworking Aristotle's four "predicables" in the *Topics*. The "Porphyrian tree" (substance → body → animate body → animal → rational animal → man) is a later diagram drawn from his worked example. [source: SEP-Porph §3.4.4] WP dates its composition to Sicily, 268–270. [source: WP-Isagoge]
- In the preface Porphyry declines, as too deep for an introduction, three questions: whether genera and species subsist or are only in concepts; if they subsist, whether corporeal or incorporeal; and whether separate from sensibles or in them (Isagoge 1.9–16). These questions "fixed the terms in which the medieval problem of universals would be debated". [source: SEP-Porph §3.4.4; SEP-Boe §2]
- Marius Victorinus made the first (lost) Latin translation of the *Isagoge* in the 4th century; Boethius' translation became the standard medieval textbook; a 7th-century Syriac translation also exists, and the work circulated in Arabic. [source: WP-Isagoge]
- *Against the Christians*: 15 books according to the Suda; lost; known only through Christian refuters (Methodius, Eusebius, Apollinaris) and repeatedly ordered destroyed by imperial decree. Porphyry rejected allegorical reading of the Old Testament, attacked Origen, and dated the Book of Daniel to the time of Antiochus IV Epiphanes, i.e. after the events it "predicted". Its date is contested (only after 270 is secure), and one scholar (Beatrice) denies it was a separate work at all, deriving the fragments from the *Philosophy from Oracles*. [source: SEP-Porph §3.1]
- Later Neoplatonists preferred "the divine Iamblichus" to "the philosopher Porphyry"; an alleged oracle said "the Syrian is inspired, the Phoenician is (only) learned". [source: SEP-Porph §2]

## 9. Iamblichus and theurgy
- Iamblichus (c. 242 – c. 325), Syrian, from the Sampsigeramid family of Emesa; probably born at Chalcis; pupil of Anatolius and (probably) Porphyry, though even that has been questioned; founded a school at Apamea in Syria. [source: SEP-Iamb §1.1; SEP-Porph §1]
- Karl Praechter (1910) called him the "second founder" of Neoplatonism, comparable to Chrysippus in Stoicism; SEP adds that this needs qualification. [source: SEP-Iamb §2.1]
- His main surviving work is the *Reply of Master Abamon to Porphyry's Letter to Anebo*, known since Ficino's Latin translation as *On the Mysteries of the Egyptians* (*De mysteriis*): a defence of sacrifice, prayer and divination against Porphyry's objections. [source: SEP-Iamb §1.2]
- He rejected Plotinus' undescended soul: the human soul descends entirely and is changed even in its essence, hence needs help from the gods. [source: SEP-Iamb §6.1–6.2]
- Theurgy (*theourgia*, "godlike action", working with the gods): ritual (prayer, offerings, sacrifice) given precedence over discursive philosophy for the soul's salvation; the gods cannot be compelled (it is not magic); SEP calls it "a rationalization of the irrational, rather than the converse". [source: SEP-Iamb §6.3]
- He posited a "wholly unspeakable" principle above the One that is the first cause; Damascius took this up. [source: SEP-Iamb §3.1]
- He fixed a curriculum of twelve Platonic dialogues, beginning with the *Alcibiades*, and required each work to be read for its single "target" (*skopos*). [source: SEP-Iamb §2.2]
- Women in the 4th-century circles: Sosipatra of Pergamon, philosopher and seer, known from Eunapius. [source: WP-Sosipatra] (WP only.)
- Emperor Julian (died 363) was himself a Neoplatonist; after his death the pagan revival could not withstand Christianity, as SEP-Neo puts it. [source: SEP-Neo §7]

## 10. Hypatia of Alexandria (born c. 350–370; murdered March 415)
- Neoplatonist philosopher, astronomer and mathematician; daughter of the mathematician Theon of Alexandria; she taught a Plotinian (not Iamblichean) Neoplatonism; all her known students were Christians, including Synesius of Cyrene, later bishop of Ptolemais (410). [source: WP-Hypatia, citing Watts 2008, Cameron, Dzielska]
- Socrates Scholasticus (a contemporary) says she "succeeded to the school of Plato and Plotinus" and lectured to auditors from afar. [source: WP-Hypatia, quoting the *Ecclesiastical History*; translator not named]
- Works: she edited the text of Book III of Ptolemy's *Almagest* (an older view said she only revised Theon's commentary); commentaries on Diophantus' *Arithmetica* and Apollonius' *Conics* are attributed to her by the Suda. No independent philosophical writing survives or is known. She did not invent the astrolabe or hydrometer. [source: WP-Hypatia]
- Death: in Lent, March 415, a Christian mob led by a lector named Peter dragged her from her carriage into the Kaisarion church and killed her with *ostraka* (roof tiles, oyster shells or potsherds). Background: the feud between the prefect Orestes, whom she advised, and Bishop Cyril. [source: WP-Hypatia] Socrates presents the murder as political and condemns it; no concrete evidence links Cyril directly, though many believed he was responsible. [source: WP-Hypatia]
- Legend vs evidence: the Library of Alexandria "had already ceased to exist in any recognizable form centuries prior to Hypatia's birth", so linking her death to its burning (as in Carl Sagan's *Cosmos*, 1980) is false; the Serapeum was destroyed in 391 under Bishop Theophilus. John Toland (1720) invented details for anti-Catholic polemic; Charles Kingsley's novel *Hypatia* (1853) cast her as "the last of the Hellenes"; Amenábar's film *Agora* (2009) is "heavily fictionalized". Mathematician Ari Belenkiy's theory linking her death to an Easter-date dispute is dismissed by Alan Cameron and Edward Watts. [source: WP-Hypatia]
- After her death she became a "martyr for philosophy" for later Neoplatonists such as Damascius; in the Middle Ages she was probably a source of the legend of St Catherine of Alexandria. [source: WP-Hypatia]

## 11. Athens: Plutarch, Syrianus, Proclus
- A revived Platonist school in Athens dates from the early 5th century (c. 410), under Plutarch of Athens; its heads called themselves "successors" (*diadochoi*) of Plato, but there was no institutional, geographical or economic continuity with Plato's Academy, which had ended in the 1st century BCE. [source: WP-Platonic_Academy, citing Alan Cameron 1969]
- Proclus: born 412 (8 Feb, WP) in Constantinople to a Lycian family; raised in Xanthos; studied in Alexandria; came to Athens in 430/431 aged 18; studied under Plutarch (d. 432) and Syrianus; head of the school from 437 for almost fifty years until his death in 485 (17 April, WP). [sources: SEP-Procl §1.1; WP-Proclus]
- His day began with a prayer to the sun; he practised theurgy; he had to go into exile in Lydia for about a year to avoid anti-pagan trouble. He wrote about 700 lines a day; about two-thirds of his output is lost. [source: SEP-Procl §1.1–1.2] He learned theurgy from Asclepigenia, daughter of Plutarch of Athens (Marinus). [source: WP-Asclepigenia] (WP only.)
- Main source for his life: the eulogy *Proclus, or On Happiness* by his pupil and successor Marinus. [source: SEP-Procl §1]
- *Elements of Theology*: a "concatenated demonstration" of propositions in geometric style (like Euclid); first half establishes the One as ultimate cause and basic concepts (causality, participation, wholes and parts, infinity, eternity); second half treats gods (henads), intellects and souls. Best starting point for Neoplatonic metaphysics, with E. R. Dodds' edition and commentary (1933; 2nd ed. 1963). [source: SEP-Procl intro, §3.1] Number of propositions: SEP-Procl says 217; WP-Elements_of_Theology says 211 (citing Dodds' edition). WRITER: say "some two hundred propositions".
- Proclus accepted Plotinus' three hypostases but criticised him: rejected the One as cause of itself, the undescended soul, and the identification of evil with matter; and gave theurgy and the *Chaldaean Oracles* great weight. [source: SEP-Procl §3]
- The "triad of triads": every effect remains in its cause, proceeds from it, and reverts upon it (Elem. Theol. §35, Dodds trans., quoted in Quotations). [source: SEP-Procl §3.1]
- Henads ("unities"): participated unities that bridge the gap between the unparticipated One and everything else; a way of integrating the traditional gods into the metaphysics of the One. Whether Iamblichus or Syrianus introduced them is disputed. [sources: SEP-Procl §3.1; SEP-Iamb §3.1]
- Hegel: "in Proclus we have the culminating point of the Neo-Platonic philosophy". [source: SEP-Procl §4, quoting Hegel's *Lectures on the History of Philosophy*]

## 12. Damascius and the end of the Athenian school (529)
- Damascius: born in Damascus c. 462 (WP; WP-Hypatia gives c. 458); studied rhetoric in Alexandria under Horapollo; fled with Isidore to Athens after Horapollo's arrest (489); head of the Athenian school by 515; the last head (scholarch). Main work: *Problems and Solutions Concerning First Principles*, an "intensely aporetic" examination asking whether an absolutely transcendent principle can coherently be called a cause, a One, or anything at all; he distinguishes the causal One from a wholly Ineffable beyond it. His *Philosophical History* (*Life of Isidore*) survives in fragments (Photius, Suda), translated by Polymnia Athanassiadi (1999). [source: WP-Damascius]
- In 529 Justinian, according to the chronicler Malalas, ordered "that no one was any longer allowed to teach philosophy and astronomy" (SEP-Simpl §1's rendering); this is usually called the closing of the Academy; it probably targeted non-Christians such as Neoplatonists and Manichaeans. [source: SEP-Simpl §1] A related law of Justinian in 529 (Codex Justinianus 1.5.18.4), extending an order of Justin I, decreed that only the orthodox might teach and receive a public stipend. [source: SEP-Amm §1] Justinian reigned 527–565. [source: SEP-Olymp]
- The closure involved "an apparent expropriation of its assets". [source: SEP-Olymp §1] Justinian also ended stipends for teachers; the Platonist school's property was worth more than 1,000 gold pieces in Proclus' day (Damascius via Photius). [source: WP-Simplicius_of_Cilicia]
- Seven philosophers led by Damascius, including Simplicius and Priscian of Lydia, went to the court of the Persian king Khosrow I (Chosroes, came to power 531), whom they had heard was a philosopher-king. Disappointed (Agathias), they left; the Roman–Persian peace treaty of 532 guaranteed that "these men should be allowed to return to their own country and live there henceforth in safety, without being forced to adopt opinions which they did not hold, or to change their own faith" (trans. Alan Cameron). [sources: SEP-Simpl §1; WP-Platonic_Academy; WP-Damascius] (WP-Simplicius says the treaty was c. 533; SEP and WP-Platonic_Academy say 532.)
- Damascius was back in Syria by 538 (an epigram at Emesa). [sources: SEP-Simpl §1; WP-Damascius]
- Philosophy did not die in 529: Hellenic philosophy went on being taught in Alexandria and Constantinople to the end of the 6th century, by people who had embraced or come to terms with Christianity [SEP-Neo §7]; under Justinian philosophy "flourished — in Alexandria especially ... in a manner unparalleled in late antiquity", because Christian theology needed logic and ontology (Olympiodorus, a pagan, taught Christian students) [SEP-Olymp §1]. By the end of the 5th century the Alexandrian audience was mostly Christian. [source: SEP-Neo §1]
- Some historians have treated 529 as "the end of Antiquity". [source: SEP-Simpl §1]

## 13. Transmission: Christian thought
- Origen may have heard Ammonius Saccas; the Cappadocians Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus studied in Athens in the 4th century; Augustine (354–430) knew Plotinus and Porphyry intimately; Trinitarian and Christological debates could not be followed without Greek philosophical training. [source: SEP-Neo §1]
- Augustine read "the books of the Platonists" (Confessions 7.13), translated into Latin by the Christian Neoplatonist Marius Victorinus, in Milan in 386; this precipitated his conversion. Whether Plotinus or Porphyry was the main source was hotly debated in the 20th century; most now accept a compromise: some treatises of Plotinus (e.g. I.6, I.2, V.1, VI.4–5) plus some Porphyry. [sources: SEP-Aug §4; SEP-Porph §2]
- Neoplatonic features in Augustine: God's transcendence and immateriality; hierarchy God–soul–body; Forms as ideas in God's mind; turning inward to find God; evil as privation of good; ascent of the mind. Whether his ascents (Confessions 7.16, 7.23, 9.24–26) report mystical experiences is "difficult to determine". [source: SEP-Aug §4]
- Augustine is said to have died with words of Plotinus on his lips (Possidius, *Life of Augustine* 28.11, after Enneads I.4.7.23–24). [source: SEP-Aug §1]
- Pseudo-Dionysius: wrote between 485 (Proclus' death) and 518–528 (first clear citation, by Severus of Antioch), posing as Dionysius the Areopagite, Paul's Athenian convert (Acts 17:34); probably a pupil of Proclus, perhaps Syrian. Works: *Divine Names*, *Celestial Hierarchy* (nine choirs of angels), *Ecclesiastical Hierarchy*, *Mystical Theology*, and ten letters. Doubted by Hypatius of Ephesus (6th c.), Lorenzo Valla (1457), John Grocyn (1501), Erasmus (from 1504); in 1895 Hugo Koch and Josef Stiglmayr independently showed his treatment of evil copies Proclus. [sources: SEP-PsD §1–2; WP-Pseudo-Dionysius; IEP-PsD]
- Dionysius in the West: the Byzantine emperor Michael II sent the corpus to Louis the Pious in 827; Hilduin's Latin version (c. 838) was so poor that Charles the Bald had John Scottus Eriugena retranslate it (862, revised 875); later commentators included Albert and Aquinas; the abiding–procession–return triad may be said to structure Aquinas' *Summa*. [source: SEP-PsD §5]
- E. R. Dodds: "Proclus was [...] conquering Europe in the guise of an early Christian." [source: SEP-Procl §4, quoting Dodds 1963, xxviii]
- Boethius (c. 475/7 – probably 526, executed by Theoderic's regime): translated Aristotle's logic and Porphyry's *Isagoge*, wrote two commentaries on the *Isagoge*, and drew extensively on Porphyry and Iamblichus; wrote the *Consolation of Philosophy* in prison. [source: SEP-Boe §1] (Many reference works give 524 for his death; SEP-Boe says "probably in 526".)
- Medieval Christian Neoplatonists named by SEP-Neo §8: Eriugena, Aquinas, Duns Scotus, Meister Eckhart. Byzantium: Michael Psellos studied Proclus (11th c.); his pupil Ioane Petritsi translated the *Elements* into Georgian; Nicholas of Methone wrote a refutation (12th c.). [source: SEP-Procl §4]

## 14. Transmission: the Islamic world
- The Arabic Plotinus was produced in the circle of al-Kindī (c. 800–870, "philosopher of the Arabs"), probably in the 830s–840s: the preface of the *Theology of Aristotle* says it is "the book of Aristotle the philosopher, called in Greek 'Theologia' ... The exposition of Porphyry of Syria, translated into Arabic by Ibn Na'ima al-Himsi, and corrected for Ahmad ibn al-Mu'tasim by al-Kindi". Ahmad was the son of the caliph al-Mu'tasim (r. 833–842). [sources: SEP-ThArist §2; SEP-Kindi §1]
- The extant Arabic Plotinus (the *Theology*, ten sections called *mimar*; the *Letter on Divine Science* falsely ascribed to al-Fārābī; sayings of the "Greek sage") draws only on Enneads IV, V and VI, out of order; it is often a free paraphrase with interpolations, so "an interpretation or reworking of Plotinus, and not just a translation". [source: SEP-ThArist §1]
- The translator al-Himsi was a Syrian Christian; whether he used a Syriac intermediary is disputed. The Greek exemplar was older than our oldest Greek manuscripts and preserves a passage (IV.7.8) missing from them. [source: SEP-ThArist §2]
- Whether the Kindī circle really believed it was by Aristotle is doubted: a plausible reading is that they saw Plotinus as completing Aristotle. [source: SEP-ThArist §2]
- Changes: the One becomes a creating God ("originator", "creator") with no felt tension with emanation (*fayḍ*); God is "pure being" (*anniyya faqaṭ*), an idea traced forward to Aquinas. [source: SEP-ThArist §4.2]
- The *Liber de causis* (*Book of Causes*; Arabic *Kitāb al-Īḍāḥ fī al-khayr al-maḥḍ*, "Book of the Explanation of the Pure Good"): a 9th-century Arabic adaptation of Proclus' *Elements of Theology*, from the Kindī circle; it simplifies Proclus to the hierarchy God–Intellect–Soul–material world. Not very influential in Arabic, but in Latin (translated by Gerard of Cremona, 1167–1187) it circulated as Aristotle's and was studied at the universities until Aquinas, using William of Moerbeke's 1268 Latin *Elements*, recognised its source in Proclus. [sources: SEP-ThArist §3; SEP-Procl §4; WP-Liber_de_causis; WP-Elements_of_Theology]
- Al-Kindī's *On First Philosophy* (dedicated to al-Mu'tasim) argues that the world is not eternal and that God is a simple "true One"; the proof draws on Proclus and the Arabic Plotinus, though the biggest single influence on the eternity question is the Christian Neoplatonist John Philoponus. [source: SEP-Kindi intro, §2.1]
- Later Arabic reception of the *Theology*: al-Fārābī (or the work ascribed to him, *Harmony of the Two Sages*) cites it to reconcile Plato and Aristotle; 10th-century Ismāʿīlī thinkers al-Sijistānī and al-Kirmānī adapt its hierarchy; Miskawayh and al-ʿĀmirī; Avicenna doubted its authenticity yet wrote notes on it; Suhrawardī ascribed its material to Plato; Safavid philosophers including Mullā Ṣadrā commented on it. [source: SEP-ThArist §5]
- A "Longer Version" in fourteen chapters, probably from Fāṭimid Egypt, was the basis of the Latin translation printed in 1519 (Pier Nicola Castellani; revised 1572 by Jacques Charpentier). [source: SEP-ThArist §5]
- SEP-Neo names al-Kindī, al-Fārābī and Avicenna as integrating Greek philosophy into Islam partly through the *Theology*. [source: SEP-Neo §8]

## 15. Transmission: Jewish thought
- Isaac Israeli (10th c.) drew on the Arabic Plotinus and al-Kindī. [source: SEP-ThArist §5]
- Solomon ibn Gabirol (Latin Avicebron/Avencebrol): born in Málaga 1021/2, died probably in Valencia 1057/8 (other dates proposed: 1050, 1054–8, 1070). Poet and philosopher; *Fons Vitae* ("Fountain of Life"), written in Arabic as a teacher–student dialogue, translated into Latin in the 12th century by Dominicus Gundissalinus and John of Spain; abridged in Hebrew by Shem Tov ibn Falaquera (13th c.). Doctrine of "universal hylomorphism" (even soul and intellect are composed of matter and form) and of the divine Will; a "modified Plotinian universe" with the ideal of Neoplatonic return. [source: SEP-Gab §1]
- Medieval Christians took Avicebron for a Muslim or a Christian Augustinian; Franciscans used him against Dominicans; only in the mid-19th century did Solomon Munk, via Falaquera's Hebrew summary, identify the author as Ibn Gabirol. His poem *Keter Malkhut* ("Royal Crown") is in many prayer books for Yom Kippur. [source: SEP-Gab §1]
- Maimonides is named by SEP-Neo §8 as receiving Neoplatonic thought through the *Theology*. [source: SEP-Neo §8] (The published Aristotle chapter already describes Maimonides' "Neoplatonised Aristotelianism".)

## 16. Renaissance and after
- Gemistos Plethon (1355–1452), Bessarion (1403–1472) and above all Marsilio Ficino (1433–1499) revived Neoplatonism; the Cambridge Platonists, Leibniz, Hegel, Schelling, Fichte, Bergson and Teilhard de Chardin show its influence. [source: SEP-Neo §8]
- Ficino: born 19 October 1433 at Figline Valdarno; Cosimo de' Medici gave him a property at Careggi in 1463 (the "Platonic Academy" was not a formal institution); his Latin Plato printed October 1484; *Platonic Theology* written by 1474, printed 1482, in imitation of Proclus' work of that name; in the late 1480s he made "the first full Greek to Latin translation of Plotinus's works along with commentary". [sources: SEP-Fic §1–2; IEP-Fic] The Latin Plotinus was printed in Florence in 1492 (printer Antonio Miscomini). [source: WS-12 (booksellers' and editors' descriptions); not in SEP] He also translated Iamblichus' *De mysteriis* (title from Ficino's version). [source: SEP-Iamb §1.2]
- Comparisons of Plotinus with Advaita Vedānta were made by J. F. Staal (*Advaita and Neoplatonism*, 1961); Radhakrishnan and Coomaraswamy used Plotinus in expounding Indian monism. [source: WP-Plotinus] These are comparisons, not evidence of influence.

## ARGUMENTS (step by step)

### A. Why the first principle must be absolutely simple (Plotinus)
Source: SEP-Plot §5 ("FP1: the Principle of the (logical) Priority of the Simple vis-à-vis the Multiple", citing V.3.12, V.3.16, V.4.1.5–15, V.6.3, V.9.8) and §7 (V.1.8, V.3.15–16).
1. Whatever is ontologically and explanatorily prior is simpler than what depends on it. Reason: a cause more complex than its effect "would fail to account for that effect, for it would lead to even greater compositeness" (i.e. the cause would itself need explaining).
2. Every multiplicity is made of constituents, each of which is one; and a multiplicity is gathered into a whole by some single unifying factor. So every many presupposes unity.
3. Hence unity is prior to multiplicity, logically and so ontologically.
4. If the first principle were composite in any way, it would presuppose its components and their unifier, and so would not be first.
5. Therefore the first principle is "utterly uncompounded, that is, the absolute One".
6. Corollaries: it is self-sufficient and lacks nothing, so it is the Good that all else seeks; and since every predicate names something a being is, no predicate applies to it: it is "beyond being" and ineffable, approachable only by negation and analogy (SEP-Plot §7).
Proclus' parallel proof (Elements of Theology Prop. 1): "Every manifold in some way participates unity." Reductio: if a manifold in no way participated in unity, neither it nor any of its parts would be one; each part would be a manifold of manifolds without end, leading either to infinitely many infinites or to nothing. Both are absurd. [source: WS-15 (summaries of Dodds' and Johnson's translations; wording "Every manifold in some way participates unity" attributed to Dodds, likely but not confirmed from the book itself)]
Main objections and replies:
- Objection 1 (how can many come from the simple?): if the One is perfectly simple and does nothing, how does a diverse world arise from it? SEP-Neo calls this "the fundamental challenge all Neoplatonists struggled to meet". Reply: the theory of two activities and the principle of plenitude: perfect things necessarily "overflow" with an external activity that does not diminish them, like heat from fire or light from the sun; what proceeds is other than the One and so multiple, becoming Intellect, a "one-many", by turning back to its source (SEP-Plot §5, §7; SEP-Neo §2–3). Proclus refines the reply with the "law of mean terms" (every cause produces things like itself before unlike) and the henads, so there are no "leaps" in the chain (SEP-Procl §3.1).
- Objection 2 (the ineffability paradox): calling it "the One", "the Good", "cause" or even "beyond being" already says something of it. Plotinus himself grapples repeatedly with "the limits of intellection and of the verbal formulation of our thoughts" (SEP-Plot §7; e.g. III.8.11: once you say "the Good" add nothing [WP-Plotinus quoting]); his reply is that such names are pointers, and reason can only approach while contact is experiential. Iamblichus responded by positing a "wholly unspeakable" principle above the causal One (SEP-Iamb §3.1); Damascius pushed the objection furthest, asking whether such a principle can coherently be called a cause or a One at all (WP-Damascius).

### B. Evil as privation (Plotinus, and Proclus' objection)
Sources: SEP-Plot §4; SEP-Neo §5; SEP-Procl §3.4.
1. Everything real comes from the Good, and what proceeds from the Good cannot be the source of evil.
2. Matter, the lowest limit of procession, has no form at all: it is pure privation, and as non-being it has no power to act.
3. Moral evil arises when the soul turns downward and is misled by the reflections of goodness appearing in matter.
4. So evil does not come "from above", and there is no independent evil principle (against Gnostic and Manichaean dualism).
Objection (Proclus, *On the Existence of Evils*): if matter is evil and matter is itself produced (ultimately from the Good, via soul: SEP-Plot §8 notes soul's image "produces" matter), the Good causes evil; and making matter evil does not explain wickedness in souls. Proclus' reply: evil has no cause or proper existence, only a "parasitic existence" (*parhypostasis*), an unintended by-product of agents aiming at good. Augustine independently held evil to be privation of good (SEP-Aug §4), and Dionysius transmitted Proclus' version to the Middle Ages.

## Debates
- **Is "Neoplatonism" a useful label?** Modern coinage; SEP-Neo calls it "to some extent a misnomer"; SEP-Iamb notes doubts about the category (Catana 2013) and the absence of a single school. Dodds (1929) argued its ideas grow out of Plato's own school. [sources: SEP-Neo; SEP-Iamb §2.1; WP-Neoplatonism]
- **"Emanation"**: Wildberg (SEP-Neo §3) calls the word misleading; Kalligas (SEP-Plot) uses it in scare quotes for the "theory of two activities". [sources as given]
- **Was Plotinus a "mystic"?** IEP-Plot (Moore): "often referred to as a 'mystical' thinker, but even this designation fails to express the philosophical rigor of his thought". SEP-Plot (Kalligas) treats union as a real experiential "contact" prepared by purification but not reachable by intellection. Corrigan (SEP-PsD): Plotinus' mysticism is not "solitary"; and "mystical" in Dionysius' *Mystical Theology* meant "hidden", not a private experience in the modern sense (Bouyer, Vanneste, McGinn). Search results show the topic is "highly controversial" in Plotinus scholarship, with no agreement on what "mysticism" means (Hadot and Beierwaltes most influential). [sources: IEP-Plot; SEP-Plot §7; SEP-PsD §2, §4.1; WS-16]
- **Plotinus' sources and Eastern influence**: which predecessors inspired him "is still unclear"; candidates are Numenius, Alexander of Aphrodisias, the Stoics, possibly Gnostic and Hermetic writings (SEP-Neo §1). His wish to learn Persian and Indian wisdom is attested, but influence from Indian thought is not established; modern scholars compare (Staal 1961). [sources: SEP-Neo §1; SEP-Plot §1; WP-Plotinus]
- **Porphyry's originality and development**: Bidez (1913) saw a superstitious young Porphyry, rationalised by Plotinus, who later relapsed; recent work (Johnson 2013) argues for a single project of "philosophical translation" of religion into Platonism. Hadot's (1968) attribution of the anonymous *Parmenides* commentary to Porphyry is widely accepted but challenged. Why Porphyry left Rome in 268 (depression, politics around Gallienus' murder, or a doctrinal rift) is disputed. [source: SEP-Porph §1–2]
- **Augustine's Neoplatonic source**: Plotinus or Porphyry; compromise view now dominant. [source: SEP-Aug §4]
- **Theurgy**: Iamblichus vs Porphyry; SEP-Iamb rejects reading it as simple "religion vs philosophy": two modes of access to the divine. [source: SEP-Iamb §6.3]
- **Who was Pseudo-Dionysius?** A Christian pupil of Proclus (SEP-PsD); Lankila (2011) reads him as a crypto-pagan; Carlo Maria Mazzucchi (2006, 2013) argued Damascius wrote the corpus as the "last counter-offensive of Paganism", a thesis criticised by others (e.g. Curiello 2013). Corrigan: "forgery" is a modern notion; adopting an ancient persona was a recognised device. [sources: SEP-PsD §1; WP-Damascius]
- **What happened in 529?** The usual story ("Justinian closed the Academy") rests chiefly on Malalas; Edward Watts ("Justinian, Malalas, and the End of Athenian Philosophical Teaching in A.D. 529", *Journal of Roman Studies* 94, 2004) argues the evidence is thin, other sources are silent, and the edict was probably not aimed specifically at the Athenian school; contemporaries did not treat it as a dramatic event. [source: WS-8, abstract and summaries; Watts 2004 not read] What the school was: not Plato's Academy, but a 5th-century Neoplatonist foundation claiming succession (WP-Platonic_Academy, citing Cameron 1969).
- **The Harran story**: Michel Tardieu (1986, 1987), followed by Pierre and Ilsetraut Hadot, argued that Simplicius and others settled at Harran (Carrhae) and that a Neoplatonic school survived there, perhaps (Sorabji) long enough to feed the 9th-century Baghdad translation movement; others dispute it. SEP-Simpl: where they went after 532 "has not been satisfactorily answered" (Goulet & Coda 2016; Watts 2005). Context: the pagans of Harran called themselves "Sabians" to gain protection, according to the story, when al-Ma'mun passed through in 830, naming Hermes Trismegistus as their prophet; Thābit ibn Qurra (d. 901) was a Harranian Sabian; scholars have called the Harranians Neoplatonists, Hermeticists or Gnostics "but there is no firm evidence for any of these identifications" (Van Bladel 2009). [sources: WP-Damascius; WP-Platonic_Academy; SEP-Simpl §1; WP-Harran; WP-Sabians]
- **Hypatia's murder**: political (Socrates Scholasticus) or religious; Cyril's responsibility unproven; modern legend has added the Library and her "martyrdom for science". [source: WP-Hypatia]
- **Arabic Plotinus**: who reworked it (al-Himsi, per Zimmermann and Adamson, or more al-Kindī, per D'Ancona); was the order deliberate; did the Kindī circle think it was Aristotle's. [source: SEP-ThArist §2]
- **Ibn Gabirol's "Will"**: does it rule out emanation (as with Augustine) or not? SEP-Gab keeps both options open. [source: SEP-Gab §2.2]

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use
- "Plotinus subsequently went to live in Sicily" (WP-Plotinus, "Death"): contradicted by SEP-Porph, which says it was Porphyry who went to Sicily in 268; Plotinus died in Campania. Do not repeat.
- That Plotinus was born in Lycopolis as a fact: only a late report (Eunapius) and ambiguous between two towns. Use "perhaps".
- That Plotinus was influenced by Indian philosophy: no evidence found; only his stated wish to learn it and modern comparisons.
- That Ammonius Saccas was Indian, or that "Saccas" certainly means "sack-carrier": both disputed.
- That Iamblichus was certainly Porphyry's pupil: "questioned (Goulet 2012a)" (SEP-Porph).
- That Porphyry's *Against the Christians* was written to prepare Diocletian's persecution of 303: SEP-Porph says this identification "has been judged unpersuasive".
- That Porphyry formally succeeded Plotinus as head of a school: "no evidence" (SEP-Porph).
- That Hypatia's death involved the burning of the Library of Alexandria, that she invented the astrolabe, or that she was killed for her science or over an Easter-date dispute: false or unsupported (WP-Hypatia).
- Exact wording of Armstrong's translation of VP 1 and VP 23, and the exact Dodds wording of *Elements* Prop. 1: seen only in search summaries.
- The 1827 first use of "Neoplatonism" (etymonline via WP only); and who coined the term (not found).
- Ficino's Plotinus printing date 1492 rests on search results (booksellers, editors' notes), not SEP; it is widely attested but not read in a scholarly source this session. Usable as "printed in 1492" with low risk; prefer "published in the early 1490s" if caution is wanted.
- The "Platonopolis" name and Porphyry's reason for the project's failure: from search summaries of VP 12 (WP says the reason was unknown to Porphyry). Use "a city to be called Platonopolis, Porphyry says" with care.
- That the Athenian Neoplatonists of 529 were the "last pagans" or that philosophy ended in 529: contradicted by SEP-Neo, SEP-Olymp (teaching continued in Alexandria and Constantinople).
- Sosipatra and Asclepigenia details: Wikipedia only (Asclepigenia's article calls Plutarch of Athens founder of the "New Academy", which is a confusion with the Hellenistic sceptical New Academy). Usable with care only for the bare facts: Asclepigenia taught Proclus theurgy (Marinus); Sosipatra known from Eunapius.
- Lloyd Gerson's Cambridge translation year (WP: 2017; probably 2018): not confirmed.
