## 1. What metaethics is; the map of positions

- Metaethics: "the attempt to understand the metaphysical, epistemological, semantic, and psychological, presuppositions and commitments of moral thought, talk, and practice." It steps back from first-order questions (is abortion wrong?) to ask about the status of moral claims themselves; whether it can be fully neutral on first-order morality is "dubious". [source: SEP-ME intro, §1]
- Ancient precedent: Plato's *Republic* — Thrasymachus (Book I) says justice is the interest of the stronger, a human creation by the powerful; Glaucon (Book II) sees justice as a human creation that solves a real problem (a mutual agreement for peace); Socrates argues justice is not a human invention but an independent, eternal standard. SEP-ME uses this as the first metaethical debate. [source: SEP-ME §1]
- **Moral realism, minimal definition**: moral claims "do purport to report facts and are true if they get the facts right. Moreover, they hold, at least some moral claims actually are true." Some accounts add independence from human thought or objectivity. [source: SEP-MR intro]
- **The two questions (the map)**: (1) Do moral claims purport to report facts (are they truth-apt beliefs)? (2) Are some of them true? Realists: yes/yes. Non-cognitivists: no (and so no). Error theorists: yes/no. [source: SEP-MR intro]
- Cognitivism = moral judgments express beliefs and are truth-apt; non-cognitivism = they express some non-cognitive attitude (emotion, approval, prescription, plan) and are not (robustly) true or false. [source: SEP-ME §4; SEP-COG §1]
- Joyce's three-way characterisation of anti-realism: deny cognitivism (non-cognitivism), deny "success" (error theory), or deny objectivity/mind-independence (non-objectivism, which includes relativism and some constructivism). "Robust" realism = cognitivism + success theory + objectivism. The objectivity condition makes the realist/anti-realist line "obscure"; Gideon Rosen doubts the notion of objectivity can be sharpened at all. [source: SEP-MAR §3.3]
- Realists themselves divide: **naturalists** (moral facts are natural facts, investigable broadly scientifically: Cornell realism, Railton, Copp, neo-Aristotelians Foot 2001 and Hursthouse 1999) vs **non-naturalists** (moral facts are *sui generis*, not natural: Moore; today Shafer-Landau 2003, Enoch 2011, Parfit 2011, Scanlon 2014). [source: SEP-MR §3; SEP-NAT §6; SEP-NN intro]
- **Constructivism** is presented as "an important apparent alternative to both naturalism and non-naturalism": moral truths are what a privileged point of view (fully rational agents, impartial spectators, ideal observers) would construct (Hume 1740, Smith 1759, Kant 1785, Firth 1952, Rawls 1980, Smith 1994, Korsgaard 1996). Whether it counts as realism is disputed: Parfit, Shafer-Landau and Enoch say making the facts depend on us "change[s] the subject". [source: SEP-MR §3]
- Theological voluntarism ("divine command theory"): the family of views holding that "what God wills is relevant to determining the moral status" of acts, etc.; the label "theological voluntarism" follows Quinn 1990, because not all such views appeal to *commanding*. A standard objection is arbitrariness (cf. the Euthyphro). [source: SEP-TV intro, §3.2]
- Burden of proof: many hold realism is the default — Dancy (1986: 172): "we take moral value to be part of the fabric of the world"; Brink (1989): "We begin as (tacit) cognitivists and realists about ethics… Moral Realism should be our metaethical starting point". [source: SEP-MAR §2]

## 2. Hume's is–ought passage (Treatise 3.1.1.27)

- Location: *A Treatise of Human Nature*, Book III ("Of Morals"), Part I, Section I, closing paragraph (T 3.1.1.27; SBN 469–70). Book III published 1740 (Books I–II 1739). [source: WP-Is–ought_problem; SEP-HM §5]
- Full passage (modernised spelling as printed by WP; SEP-HM prints the original "perceiv'd"): "In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remarked, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning, and establishes the being of a God, or makes observations concerning human affairs; when of a sudden I am surprised to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, *is*, and *is not*, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an *ought*, or an *ought not*. This change is imperceptible; but is, however, of the last consequence. For as this *ought*, or *ought not*, expresses some new relation or affirmation, it's necessary that it should be observed and explained; and at the same time that a reason should be given, for what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it. But as authors do not commonly use this precaution, I shall presume to recommend it to the readers; and am persuaded, that this small attention would subvert all the vulgar systems of morality, and let us see, that the distinction of vice and virtue is not founded merely on the relations of objects, nor is perceived by reason." [source: WP-Is–ought_problem; SEP-HM §5 for "altogether inconceivable" and the closing clause]
- SEP-HM: "Few passages in Hume's work have generated more interpretive controversy." [source: SEP-HM §5]
- The logical reading (dominant in the 20th century): no ought-conclusion validly follows from purely is-premises; generalised to all evaluative conclusions; R.M. Hare calls this "Hume's Law". On this reading Hume must simply assume that no factual proposition is itself evaluative (Francis Snare's point). [source: SEP-HM §5] Also called "Hume's guillotine" (Max Black 1964). [source: WP-Is–ought_problem]
- Other readings: (a) noncognitivist (if moral judgments express feelings without propositional content, they trivially cannot be inferred from propositions); (b) anti-realist (values excluded from facts); (c) cognitivist readings: it only says moral properties are not discoverable by *demonstrative* reason, or that the *original* discovery of virtue and vice requires sentiment; Hume himself often reasons from facts to moral conclusions. [source: SEP-HM §5]
- SEP-ME's reconstruction of the gap: "If Hume is right, every valid argument for an evaluative conclusion either includes or presupposes some evaluative premise" — e.g. inferring "something bad is happening" from "someone is in pain" presupposes "pain is bad". [source: SEP-ME §4]
- A technical wrinkle: "The Sun is yellow" entails "Either the Sun is yellow, or it is wrong to murder", but such formally valid bridges are "vacuous"; formulations of Hume's Law that avoid this exist (Campbell Brown 2017; Guevara 2008). [source: WP-Is–ought_problem]
- Non-cognitivism "fit[s] well" with Hume's gap: between beliefs and other attitudes "no entailment relations hold at all". [source: SEP-ME §4]

## 3. Moore's open question argument (Principia Ethica, 1903)

- G.E. Moore (4 Nov 1873 – 24 Oct 1958). *Principia Ethica* first published October 1903, Cambridge University Press. [source: WP-G._E._Moore; WP-Principia_Ethica]
- The argument is in §§5–15, "particularly 13–14"; Moore's original statement is "famously unclear", and he admitted flaws in an unfinished preface to a planned second edition (Moore 1993: 2–27) while holding that its "general thrust and conclusion were still correct". [source: SEP-NAT §4.1; SEP-MM §2]
- Moore called the error of defining good in natural (or metaphysical) terms "the naturalistic fallacy" (1903: 10); he found it in hedonists (Bentham), evolutionary ethicists (Herbert Spencer) and metaphysical ethicists (T.H. Green). Sidgwick had used a form of the argument "in passing". [source: SEP-MM §2]
- Moore quotation (§6): "If I am right, then nobody can foist upon us such an axiom as that 'Pleasure is the only good' or that 'The good is the desired' on the pretense that 'this is the very meaning of the word.'" [source: SEP-NAT §4.1]
- **Argument, step by step** (the version "most contemporary metaethicists would recognize"; SEP-NAT, with SEP-ME and SEP-MM):
  1. Take any proposed naturalistic definition: "good" means N (e.g. pleasant; what we desire to desire; more evolved).
  2. If that were a correct definition, the question "x is N, but is x good?" would be *closed*: anyone who understood the words would see the answer must be yes (compare "x is a bachelor, but is x unmarried?").
  3. But the question is *open*: a competent speaker can understand it and still sensibly doubt the answer, without conceptual confusion. Equivalently, "pleasure is good" says something substantive, not the tautology "pleasure is pleasure"; and "x is pleasant but not good" is not self-contradictory.
  4. So good is not (by definition) N.
  5. The same holds for every natural property proposed; so good is not identical to any natural property — it is "simple", "indefinable" and "non-natural".
  Moore allowed that everything pleasant might in fact be good; his claim was that this is not settled by meaning. [source: SEP-NAT §4.1; SEP-ME §4; SEP-MM §2; WP-Open-question_argument (modus tollens form)]
- Impact: "striking most people at the time as decisive". Baldwin (1990): "twentieth century British ethical theory is unintelligible without reference to Principia Ethica"; until about 1960 Moore was taken to have refuted naturalism, but his own non-naturalism seemed to make "unacceptable metaphysical and epistemological demands", so many turned to emotivism, prescriptivism or other anti-realism. [source: SEP-ME §4; SEP-NN §1 quoting Baldwin]
- Moore's metaphysics was more modest than often said: goodness has "being" but does not "exist", like numbers; there is no "supersensible reality" (1903: 110–12, 123–25). Some read him as anticipating today's "non-metaphysical" realism (Nagel, Scanlon, Parfit). [source: SEP-MM §3]
- **Main objection (the water/H2O reply)**: the argument at most shows that *thinking* something good differs from thinking it pleasant, not that *being* good differs from being pleasant. "Thinking that some liquid is H2O is different from thinking it is water", and one can intelligibly ask whether water is H2O; yet water *is* H2O. "A difference between the thoughts does not establish a difference in the properties." [source: SEP-ME §4]
- In current terms the OQA refutes only **analytic** naturalism (moral terms are *definable* in natural terms), not **synthetic** naturalism (moral properties are identical with natural ones as an a posteriori discovery, like water = H2O: Brink 1989 ch. 6; Brink 2001) or "one-term" naturalism (Sturgeon 2003). "The common verdict today among metaethicists is that – unless it can be substantially modified or extended in some way – the OQA has no force against naturalism." In Moore's defence, these views did not exist yet and were developed partly in response to him. [source: SEP-NAT §4.1]
- Older objections: Frankena (1939): the argument begs the question (a convinced analytic naturalist will find the question closed); the paradox of analysis (Langford 1942; Smith 1994): correct analyses can be unobvious; and the argument is an induction from a few failed definitions. [source: SEP-NAT §4.1; SEP-MM §2]
- Replies for Moore: the more naturalist proposals fail the test, and the more the failure reflects "a general sense of a gap between the moral and non-moral", the stronger the argument; and with a genuine analysis we eventually come to see it as analytic, but no amount of reflection does this for "good". [source: SEP-MM §2]
- Modern successors: the **Normativity Objection** (Parfit 2011; Scanlon 2014): natural facts are not normative, moral facts are about what *matters*; Parfit's **Triviality Objection**: if naturalism were true, moral–natural identity claims would be trivial, but they are substantive. Naturalists answer both like the OQA: synthetic identities are informative, as water = H2O is (Copp 2017). [source: SEP-NAT §4.2]
- Two lines of response to Moore: non-cognitivists say his mistake was thinking moral claims attribute *any* property (the question is open because we express attitudes, not beliefs); naturalist realists say his mistake was inferring the nature of a property from how we think of it. [source: SEP-ME §4]

## 4. Naturalist realism: Cornell realism

- Developed in the 1980s by Richard Boyd (1988), David Brink (1986), Nicholas Sturgeon (1985) and Peter Railton (1986a, 1986b); named because Boyd, Brink and Sturgeon were working or studying at Cornell. It "deliberately mirrors scientific methodology in ethics as closely as possible". [source: SEP-NAT §6] (Railton is grouped with them by SEP-NAT but was not at Cornell — say "Boyd, Brink and Sturgeon, with Railton".)
- Key works: Boyd, "How to Be a Moral Realist" (1988, in Sayre-McCord ed., *Essays on Moral Realism*: 181–228); Brink, *Moral Realism and the Foundations of Ethics* (Cambridge UP, 1989); Sturgeon, "Moral Explanations" (1985, in Copp and Zimmerman eds., *Morality, Reason, and Truth*: 49–78). [source: SEP-ME bibliography]
- The health analogy (Boyd 1988): goodness is like healthiness — a complex natural property, not directly observable, multiply realisable, known by its causes and effects; a "homeostatic cluster property". So it can enter into explanations. [source: SEP-NAT §6]
- Harman's challenge (*The Nature of Morality*, 1977, ch. 1): scientific beliefs are best explained partly by the facts they are about (a proton in a cloud chamber), but our moral beliefs seem explicable by upbringing and psychology alone; his example: seeing hoodlums setting a cat on fire. [source: SEP-NN §4; SEP-EPI (hoodlums example); SEP-NAT §6]
- Sturgeon's reply: moral facts do explain things — "we think Hitler was evil because Hitler was evil" (Sturgeon 1988); the oppressiveness of slavery figures in the best explanation of the growth of the anti-slavery movement in the US between the Revolutionary War and the Civil War (Sturgeon 1988: 245). [source: SEP-NAT §3; SEP-NN §4]
- Semantics: Cornell realists use causal-regulation semantics (moral terms refer to whatever property causally regulates their use, as "water" does), which makes them synthetic naturalists and lets them evade the OQA (Brink 2001). [source: SEP-NAT §6]
- **Main objection: Moral Twin Earth** (Horgan and Timmons 1991). Imagine people whose use of "right" is causally regulated by a different property. By Putnam's Twin Earth logic, their "right" would mean something else and there would be no real disagreement; but intuitively we and they genuinely *disagree* about what is right. So moral terms do not simply refer to whatever regulates their use. Replies: Copp (2000), Dowell (2016), Dunaway and McPherson (2016), Väyrynen (2018); Wisdom (2021) says the intuitions are too unstable to settle anything. Relativists Prinz (2007) and Wong (2006) argue that Cornell semantics actually leads to relativism. [source: SEP-NAT §6]

## 5. Mackie's error theory (Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong, 1977)

- John Leslie Mackie (25 Aug 1917, Sydney – 12 Dec 1981, Oxford); Australian; studied under John Anderson at Sydney; professor at Otago 1955–59, Challis Professor at Sydney 1959–63, first professor of philosophy at York 1963–67, then fellow of University College, Oxford. [source: WP-J._L._Mackie]
- *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*, 1977, Penguin (Pelican Books), Harmondsworth. Chapter 1, "The Subjectivity of Values", opens: "There are no objective values." (p. 15 of the 1977/78 Penguin edition). [source: WP-Ethics:_Inventing_Right_and_Wrong; SEP-ME bibliography]
- Mackie "coined the term 'error theory' in 1977". Error theory: moral judgments are beliefs/assertions (cognitivism) but none is true, because the properties needed (objective wrongness, goodness) do not exist or are never instantiated. Defenders: Mackie 1977, Hinckfuss 1987, Joyce 2001 (*The Myth of Morality*), Olson 2014 (*Moral Error Theory: History, Critique, Defence*). [source: SEP-MAR §3.2]
- Analogy: like an atheist about religious claims (SEP-ME), or someone who thinks talk of unicorns is false because there are none (SEP-MAR). The error theorist can still assert "Moral wrongness does not exist" truly, and does not think torturing innocents is morally *permissible* either — criticisms should not equivocate. [source: SEP-ME §4; SEP-MAR §3.2]
- Two-step structure of error theory: a *conceptual* step (moral discourse is committed to something — for Mackie, objective values and objective prescriptions) and an *ontological* step (there is no such thing). So there are two places to object: deny the commitment, or deny that it is strange. "We are moral realists" conceptually, Mackie holds, but the world does not cooperate. [source: SEP-MAR §3.2]
- **Argument from relativity** (better called "from disagreement"): (1) there is enormous variation in moral views, and moral disagreements are unusually intractable; (2) the best explanation is that moral judgments "reflect adherence to and participation in different ways of life" (1977: 36), not that some cultures have better access to objective moral facts; Mackie's example is monogamy vs other marital arrangements. Objections: deny the empirical premise (disagreement masks deep agreement on principles — Mackie replies briefly at 1977: 37), or deny that error theory is the best explanation (Brink 1984; Shafer-Landau 1994; and others). [source: SEP-MAR-Mackie; IEP-ME (inference to the best explanation)]
- **Argument from queerness**, two strands: metaphysical — objective values would be "qualities or relations of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe" (1977: 38); epistemological — to know them we would need "some special faculty of moral perception or intuition, utterly different from our ordinary ways of knowing everything else" (ibid.). The epistemological strand depends on the metaphysical one. [source: SEP-MAR-Mackie]
- What is queer: "objective prescriptions"; Mackie denies that any "categorically imperative element is objectively valid" (1977: 29). Illustrations: Plato's Form of the Good (knowing it automatically motivates) and Samuel Clarke's "necessary relations of fitness between situations and actions, so that a situation would have a demand for such-and-such an action somehow built into it" (1977: 40). Metaphor: moral properties would have "to-be-pursuedness" (1977: 40). Later: such prescriptions would give reasons "independent of that agent's desires or purposes" (Mackie 1982: 115). [source: SEP-MAR-Mackie; SEP-MAR §3.2]
- Mackie allows institutional reasons (chess rules, promising): requirements "constituted by human thought, behaviour, feelings, and attitudes" (1977: 81); what he rejects are "requirements which simply are there, in the nature of things" (1977: 59). [source: SEP-MAR-Mackie]
- **Objectification / projection**: Mackie explains why we believe in objective values anyway — partly "a natural human projectivist tendency" (1977: 42), partly institutional thinking. He borrows Hume's image of the mind "spreading itself" on external objects (more fully developed in his *Hume's Moral Theory*, 1980). [source: SEP-MAR-Mackie (1977: 42); WS-3 for the Hume link and the 1980 book — use cautiously, paraphrase]
- The book's title states his positive aim: since there are no objective values, ethics must be invented rather than discovered; Mackie goes on (in later chapters) to propose a morality. [source: WP-J._L._Mackie]
- Replies to queerness (from SEP-ME, SEP-NN): "companions in guilt" — if moral facts are queer, so are other normative facts, e.g. epistemic reasons or the standards of good explanation that the naturalist herself relies on (Sayre-McCord 1988b); or deny morality is committed to anything so strange. Korsgaard is reported (WS-1) to accept that such entities would be queer but deny this counts against them — NOT checked in a source read; omit. [source: SEP-NN §4; SEP-ME §4]
- David Lewis on the impasse: "Strictly speaking, Mackie is right: genuine values would have to meet an impossible condition, so it is an error to think there are any. Loosely speaking, the name may go to a claimant that deserves it imperfectly … What to make of the situation is mainly a matter of temperament" (Lewis [1989] 2000: 93). [source: SEP-MAR §3.2]
- After error theory: fictionalists keep moral talk as a useful fiction (Kalderon 2005; Joyce 2017); abolitionists/eliminativists would give it up (debate: Garner & Joyce 2019). [source: SEP-MAR §3.2]
- Ayer himself later considered Mackie's error theory "closer to the truth" than his own emotivism (Ayer 1984). [source: SEP-AY §8]

## 6. Emotivism and expressivism; the Frege–Geach problem

- A.J. Ayer (1910–1989), *Language, Truth and Logic* (London: Gollancz, 1936), written when he was 24; chapter 6, "Critique of Ethics and Theology". [source: SEP-AY intro, §8; WP-Emotivism]
- Ayer's emotivism: moral utterances express feelings and are not truth-evaluable; Ayer insisted this is *not* subjectivism (which says moral claims *describe* the speaker's feelings, and so are true or false). He later added a prescriptive element (encouraging others to share the feeling). [source: SEP-AY §8; SEP-COG §1]
- Ayer's own wording (Dover ed. 1952, p. 107, per WP): "The presence of an ethical symbol in a proposition adds nothing to its factual content. Thus if I say to someone, 'You acted wrongly in stealing that money,' I am not stating anything more than if I had simply said, 'You stole that money.' In adding that this action is wrong I am not making any further statement about it. I am simply evincing my moral disapproval of it. It is as if I had said, 'You stole that money,' in a peculiar tone of horror, or written it with the addition of some special exclamation marks." [source: WP-Emotivism]
- **"Boo/Hooray" caveat**: Ayer's text does not say "boo" or "hooray"; it speaks of "a peculiar tone of horror" and "special exclamation marks". "Boo-hurrah theory" is a later nickname for emotivism. SEP-COG says only that "the claim that moral terms function much like 'boo' and 'hurrah'" is expressivist in a broad sense, without attributing the words to Ayer. [source: WP-Emotivism (quotation); SEP-COG §2.4; WS-4]
- Precursors: Ogden and Richards, *The Meaning of Meaning* (1923); W.H.F. Barnes and A. Duncan-Jones (1934). [source: WP-Emotivism]
- Ayer said his emotivism would survive the demise of his positivism; "emotivism could be seen as separable from verificationism". [source: SEP-AY §8]
- Charles Leslie Stevenson (27 Jun 1908 – 14 Mar 1979), American; "The Emotive Meaning of Ethical Terms", *Mind* 46 (1937): 14–31; *Ethics and Language* (Yale University Press, 1944); developed his ideas independently of Ayer. [source: WP-Charles_Stevenson_(philosopher); SEP-ME bibliography; SEP-AY §8]
- Stevenson: distinguished disagreement in belief from "disagreement in attitude"; moral disagreements involve both. First pattern of analysis: "This is good" means "I approve of this; do so as well" (*Ethics and Language*, p. 21). Moral language has "emotive meaning" and works partly by persuasion. [source: SEP-COG §3; WP-Emotivism]
- R.M. Hare's prescriptivism (*The Language of Morals*, 1952) is a related non-cognitivism; Hare objected that Stevenson made moral argument look like propaganda. [source: WP-Emotivism; SEP-COG]
- Arguments *for* non-cognitivism: tight link between moral judgment and motivation; persistent, vehement disagreement looks like a clash of attitudes (Stevenson 1948; Gibbard 1992; Blackburn 1998); we are uncomfortable with deferring to moral "experts" (McGrath; Hills). [source: SEP-MAR §3.1]
- **Expressivism** (narrow sense): a systematic semantics pairing moral sentences with the states of mind they express. **Allan Gibbard**: *Wise Choices, Apt Feelings* (Harvard UP, 1990) — normative judgments express acceptance of systems of norms; *Thinking How to Live* (2003) — a planning attitude akin to intending. [source: SEP-COG §2.4–2.5; SEP-ME bibliography]
- **Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism**: start from an anti-realist (projectivist) picture and "earn the right" to all the realist-sounding talk — "true", "fact", "mind-independent". Two moves: talk of truth and facts "comes cheap" (saying "it is true that lying is wrong" is just another way of saying lying is wrong); and whether realist-sounding talk is legitimate is itself an evaluative question. Works: *Spreading the Word* (1984), *Essays in Quasi-Realism* (1993), *Ruling Passions* (1998). Blackburn prefers "projectivism" to "noncognitivism" for his view. Worry: if it mimics all the realist's assertions, why is it not simply realism (Wright 1988; Dreier 2018)? [source: SEP-ME §4; SEP-MAR §3.1; SEP-ME bibliography]
- **The Frege–Geach problem**, stated (Geach, "Assertion", *Philosophical Review* 74 (1965): 449–465; also Geach 1960 "Ascriptivism"; Searle 1962 independently; some credit W.D. Ross 1939):
  - Geach's example (1965: 463): (P1) If tormenting the cat is bad, getting your little brother to do it is bad. (P2) Tormenting the cat is bad. Therefore, getting your little brother to torment the cat is bad.
  - The argument is valid (modus ponens). Validity requires that "tormenting the cat is bad" mean the same in P1 and P2.
  - In P2 the expressivist says the sentence expresses disapproval. In P1, inside an "if", it expresses no disapproval (one could accept P1 while approving of cat-tormenting).
  - So on the expressivist account the phrase changes meaning, and the argument commits a fallacy of equivocation — which it plainly does not.
  - "The Frege Point": "A thought may have just the same content whether you assent to its truth or not; a proposition may occur in discourse now asserted, now unasserted, and yet be recognizably the same proposition" (Geach 1965: 449). Named after Geach's invocation of Frege. [source: SEP-COG §4.1; IEP-ME (Geach 1960, 1965)]
  - SEP-AY's version: "(1) If John killed Jane, he did something wrong. (2) John killed Jane. So (3) John did something wrong." [source: SEP-AY §8]
- **Blackburn's reply**: the conditional expresses a higher-order attitude — approval of making disapproval of getting little brother to lie "follow upon" disapproval of lying; someone who holds the premises but not the conclusion has a clash of attitudes: "He has a fractured sensibility which cannot itself be an object of approval" (Blackburn 1984: 195). Counter-objection: this turns logical inconsistency into mere pragmatic incoherence (Hale 1986; Schueler 1988; van Roojen 1996 and others). More sophisticated replies: Gibbard 1990, 2003; hybrid theories (Ridge 2006, 2014). The problem is still considered unresolved/"generates much controversy". [source: SEP-COG §4.1, §4.2; IEP-ME]

## 7. Constructivism

- The term entered moral theory with John Rawls's Dewey Lectures, "Kantian Constructivism in Moral Theory", *Journal of Philosophy* 77(9) (1980): 515–572. Rawls reinterpreted Kant: objectivity is a *practical* problem for people who disagree and must agree on what to do; nobody has privileged access to moral truth, all have equal standing in reasoning; Kant's theory thus offers an alternative to both realism and scepticism. [source: SEP-CON §1, bibliography]
- Christine Korsgaard (born 9 Apr 1952; PhD Harvard under Rawls; Harvard professor since 1991). *The Sources of Normativity* (Cambridge UP, 1996), revised from her Tanner Lectures, ed. Onora O'Neill. [source: WP-Christine_Korsgaard; SEP-CON bibliography]
- Korsgaard's "procedural realism" vs "substantive realism": procedural realism holds "there are answers to moral questions because there are correct procedures for arriving at them"; substantive realism holds there are correct procedures "because there are moral truths or facts, which exist independently of those procedures, and which those procedures track" (Korsgaard 1996a: 36–37). [source: SEP-CON §2.2]
- Her case: substantive realism cannot answer the sceptic about why moral facts *bind* us; reflective agents can step back from their desires; practical identities (Camille as artist, French citizen, lover) bind only if reflectively endorsed; a transcendental argument: valuing humanity (rational nature) is the condition of valuing anything at all, so we must value humanity in ourselves and others "on pain of incoherence" (1996a: 121–123). [source: SEP-CON §2.2]
- Humean constructivism (Sharon Street; Velleman; Lenman): normative truth is relative to an agent's contingent evaluative starting points; an "ideally coherent Caligula" who values maximising suffering is conceivable; Street: "truth and falsity in the normative domain must always be relativized to a particular practical point of view" (Street 2008a: 224). [source: SEP-CON §3]
- Main objection (a Euthyphro-style dilemma, Shafer-Landau 2003: 42): if the constructive reasoning is unconstrained, its results are arbitrary; if constrained, the constraints are not constructed, which means realism. So constructivism is "either arbitrary or collapses into realism". Also: it may presuppose the moral views it claims to construct (Raz 2003; Timmons 2003). [source: SEP-CON §6]

## 8. Relativism

- Distinctions (Gowans): **Descriptive moral relativism** (DMR): "As a matter of empirical fact, there are deep and widespread moral disagreements across different societies, and these disagreements are much more significant than whatever agreements there may be." **Metaethical moral relativism** (MMR): "The truth or falsity of moral judgments, or their justification, is not absolute or universal, but is relative to the traditions, convictions, or practices of a group of persons." **Normative relativism**: we ought to tolerate those we disagree with — "most philosophers believe" it does not follow from DMR or MMR. [source: SEP-REL §2]
- DMR does not establish MMR, but the main rationales for MMR depend on something like DMR. Wong argues the world contains two broad approaches: relationship-centred moralities and rights-centred moralities. [source: SEP-REL §4]
- Ancient origins: Herodotus and Protagoras in Greece; Zhuangzi's nonobjectivism "sometimes interpreted as a kind of relativism". Ancient Greeks more often drew scepticism (Sextus Empiricus) than relativism from diversity. Modern relativism prominent from the 20th century: W.G. Sumner (1906), anthropologists Ruth Benedict (*Patterns of Culture*, 1934), Herskovits, Margaret Mead; Edward Westermarck. [source: SEP-REL §1]
- **Herodotus, *Histories* 3.38 (the Callatiae)**: King Darius asked Greeks at his court what price would make them eat their dead fathers; they said nothing would. He then asked Callatiae (Godley's translation calls them "Indians … who eat their parents") what would make them burn their dead fathers; they "cried aloud" that he should not speak of so horrid an act. Herodotus concludes Pindar was right that "custom is lord of all" (Godley; the Greek *nomos ho pantōn basileus* is also rendered "custom is king of all"). The Pindar line is also quoted in Plato's *Gorgias*. [source: IEP-REL §1 (paraphrase of the story); WS-6 (Godley wording, Perseus/Perseids text of 3.38); WP-Endocannibalism (citation 3.38)]
- **Gilbert Harman**, "Moral Relativism Defended", *Philosophical Review* 84 (1975): 3–22: "inner judgments" (that someone ought to do X) imply the agent has motivating reasons, which plausibly arise only from implicit agreements; so such judgments are valid only relative to groups who share an agreement. Relies on internalism, which many reject. [source: SEP-REL §6; SEP-ME bibliography]
- **David B. Wong**: *Moral Relativity* (1984); "Pluralistic Relativism" (1996); *Natural Moralities: A Defense of Pluralistic Relativism* (Oxford UP, 2006); *Moral Relativism and Pluralism* (Cambridge UP, 2023). "More than one morality may be true, but there are limits": universal constraints from human nature and the need for cooperation (e.g. any adequate morality includes reciprocity; must foster agency and identity in personal contexts like the family) underdetermine one true morality, so several are true. Motivated by "moral ambivalence" — disagreeing with someone while recognising they are reasonable. SEP: "In many respects, his position is the most sophisticated form of relativism developed to date". [source: SEP-REL §6, bibliography]
- Objections to MMR: (a) it cannot say the Holocaust was objectively wrong (relativists call this question-begging, but it may reflect that we have more reason to trust such intuitions than any relativist argument); (b) moral progress — abolition of slavery would be mere change, not progress (reply: progress by drawing out values already held, e.g. antebellum arguments against slavery); (c) which group? People belong to many groups; (d) the reformer/dissident problem: if "wrong" just meant "what my community disapproves of", "My community disapproves of this, but they shouldn't" would be self-contradictory, and it is not (Schroeder 2010). [source: SEP-REL §6; SEP-NAT §6]
- Empirical note: people who accept relativism tend to be somewhat more tolerant (Collier-Spruel et al.; Wright et al. 2008), but that does not show relativism *justifies* tolerance. [source: SEP-REL §4]

## 9. Non-naturalist realism today (Parfit, Enoch, Shafer-Landau)

- Contemporary non-naturalists distinguish themselves from Moore; examples Shafer-Landau 2003, Enoch 2011, Parfit 2011. [source: SEP-NN §1]
- Russ Shafer-Landau (born 1963; University of Wisconsin–Madison), *Moral Realism: A Defence* (Oxford: Clarendon, 2003). Defines the objectivity at issue as "stance-independence": moral truths hold independently of all our evaluative attitudes, even idealised ones (Shafer-Landau 2003: 15; adopted by Street). His metaphysics: moral properties are constituted by natural properties without being identical to them. [source: WP-Russ_Shafer-Landau; SEP-EPI §4.2; SEP-NN §6]
- David Enoch, *Taking Morality Seriously: A Defense of Robust Realism* (Oxford UP, 2011). Arguments: normative facts are **deliberatively** indispensable (we cannot deliberate without them), and that should count as much as explanatory indispensability; Moore-style non-naturalism is presented as more "robust" than the quietist versions. [source: SEP-NN §4; SEP-MM §6; SEP-ME bibliography]
- Derek Parfit (11 Dec 1942, Chengdu, China – 2 Jan 2017, London). *On What Matters*: vols 1–2 2011, vol. 3 2017; grew from his 2002 Tanner Lectures at Berkeley; introduction by Samuel Scheffler; manuscript circulated as *Climbing the Mountain*. Vol. 1's "Triple Theory" claims Kantian, consequentialist and contractualist theories converge ("climbing the same mountain on different sides"). Metaethics in vol. 2 (pp. 464–87): an ontologically minimalist ("non-metaphysical") non-naturalism; the Normativity and Triviality objections to naturalism. Peter Singer said Parfit's arguments "put those who reject objectivism in ethics on the defensive". [source: WP-Derek_Parfit; WP-On_What_Matters; SEP-MM §6; SEP-NAT §4.2]
- Classic objections to non-naturalism: metaphysical (properties "outside the causal nexus"), epistemological (how could we know them? intuitionism makes reliable tracking a "cosmic coincidence" — Bedke 2009), supervenience (why must moral properties covary with natural ones?), semantic access (Eklund 2017), and explanatory impotence (Harman). [source: SEP-ME §4; SEP-NN §3–4]

## 10. The evolutionary debunking challenge

- Sharon Street (born 1973; BA Amherst 1995, PhD Harvard 2003 under Korsgaard; NYU from 2002), "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value", *Philosophical Studies* 127 (2006): 109–166. She defends "Humean constructivism". [source: WP-Sharon_Street; SEP-ME bibliography]
- Richard Joyce (born 1966, England; raised in New Zealand; PhD Princeton 1998), *The Myth of Morality* (Cambridge UP, 2001); *The Evolution of Morality* (MIT Press, 2006). [source: WP-Richard_Joyce_(philosopher); SEP-BIO bibliography]
- Precursor: Darwin, *Descent of Man* (1871: 73): if "men were reared under precisely the same conditions as hive-bees, there can hardly be a doubt that our unmarried females would, like the worker-bees, think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and mothers would strive to kill their fertile daughters; and no one would think of interfering." [source: SEP-BIO §4.1]
- **Street's argument, step by step** (SEP-BIO's reconstruction, with SEP-MR and SEP-EPI):
  1. "Our system of evaluative judgments is thoroughly saturated with evolutionary influence" (Street 2006: 114).
  2. Natural selection favoured evaluative dispositions for their effect on reproductive success, not for tracking independent moral truths.
  3. Realism holds moral truths are "stance-independent" — not a function of what helped hunter-gatherers reproduce.
  4. Dilemma: either the realist denies any relation between evolutionary pressures and the independent truths — then it would be "a sheer coincidence" if our evolved values matched them, so we should expect them to be largely off track (scepticism); or the realist asserts a relation — that tracking moral truth was itself adaptive (the "tracking account"). Street argues the tracking account is scientifically worse than the "adaptive link account" (we have these values because they linked circumstances to fitness-enhancing responses, true or not).
  5. Reflection cannot rescue us: we would be assessing "thoroughly contaminated" judgments with "tools of rational reflection [that are] equally contaminated" (2006: 124).
  6. So realism leads to scepticism; better to reject realism for an anti-realist (constructivist) view on which evolved values can still be justified.
  [source: SEP-BIO §4.1; SEP-MR §2 ("either the alignment … is a miraculous coincidence, or there are no moral truths to track"); SEP-EPI §4.2; WS-7 for the labels "tracking account"/"adaptive link account" — terminology from secondary sources, accurate but not read in Street's text]
- **Joyce's version** (2006, ch. 6): we have a complete non-moral genealogy of moral beliefs (evolution of concepts like fairness and guilt + psychology and sociology); this makes moral truths "explanatorily superfluous", like truths about witches; so we should be agnostic about moral truth (moral scepticism). Joyce's argument targets moral knowledge as such; Street's targets realism. [source: SEP-BIO §4.1; SEP-NAT §3]
- **Replies**:
  - *Naturalist reply* (Copp 2008): if moral truths are about what enables societies to meet their needs (stable cooperation etc.), evolution would tend to make our beliefs track them. Street's rejoinder (2008): Copp's theory is itself the product of evolved intuitions, so the match is unsurprising. Many debunkers therefore aim only at non-naturalists (Joyce 2006; Bedke 2009; Lutz 2018). [source: SEP-BIO §4.1; SEP-NAT §3]
  - *Third-factor reply* (Wielenberg 2010; Enoch 2011): some third factor explains both the moral truth and our belief. Enoch: survival and reproduction (or what promotes them) are in fact good, so evolution, in aiming at them, gave us mostly accurate moral beliefs — a non-theological "pre-established harmony". Objection: the reply relies on evolved intuitions (that survival is good) and leaves the match resting on a lucky accident (Bedke 2009; FitzPatrick 2014b). [source: SEP-BIO §4.1; SEP-NN §3]
  - *Autonomy of reflection* (FitzPatrick 2014–2017; Nagel 1997): we grasp truths in physics and philosophy that played no role in our evolution; many moral beliefs may come from reflection (we believe trafficking is wrong because of the reasons that make it wrong); the "saturation" premise begs the question against realism. [source: SEP-BIO §4.1]
  - *Overgeneralisation / companions in guilt*: debunking may spread to other domains, leading to global scepticism (Vavova 2015). [source: SEP-NN §3; WS-8]
  - *Parfit's reply* (OWM vol. 2): Street demands that the realist show evolution led us to true normative beliefs *without assuming any normative truths*; Parfit says this cannot be done but does not count against those beliefs being true; he also argued Street's constructivism leads to nihilism. [source: WS-8 (Katzke, citing OWM vol. 2 p. 533) — secondary only; state in paraphrase and with a hedge]

## 11. Non-Western perspectives

- **Mencius vs Xunzi as realism vs constructivism (an interpretation)**: Mencius (372–289 BCE?) held human *xing* (nature) is good — all have moral "sprouts"; Xunzi (born c. 310 BCE, alive in 238 BCE), ch. 23 "Human Nature Is Evil/Bad": "Human xing is evil; what is good is artifice (*wei*)" (23.1a, Goldin's translation). [source: SEP-XUN §1–2]
- David Wong (SEP-CHE): on one plausible reading, for Mencius morality is part of the order imparted by *tian* (heaven), with innate moral intuitions, while Xunzi says the sage kings *generated* (created/invented) ritual and moral precepts; "the contrast between Mencius and Xunzi exemplifies the contrast between a robust moral realism that has moral properties such as rightness existing independently of human invention and a constructivist position that makes moral properties dependent on human invention." But Xunzi as constructivist can still be an objectivist (Nivison 1991): morality is invented to perform a function (harmonising interests, transforming short-term desire), and human nature constrains which inventions work (e.g. mourning rites suit human love for parents). The readings are disputed (Ames vs Bloom on Mencius; Eno, Machle on Xunzi's *tian*). [source: SEP-CHE (Mencius/Xunzi section)]
- Goldin's caveat: both agreed all humans *can* become good; they used *xing* differently (Xunzi: "What is so by birth is called xing", 22.1b); Guodian manuscripts (c. 300 BCE) suggest Xunzi's usage, not Mencius's, may have been the ordinary one. [source: SEP-XUN §2]
- **Mīmāṃsā: dharma known only through the Veda**: Jaimini's *Mīmāṃsā Sūtra* (dated from c. 3rd–2nd c. BCE to the early 1st millennium CE; sources differ) opens with "the desire to know dharma"; sūtra 1.1.2: "dharma is a goal defined by [Vedic] injunction (codanā)" (Arnold's translation). Dharma is always "yet to be" (*bhaviṣyat*), hence "essentially inaccessible to perception, and only language can be thought to represent a way of knowing it"; Vedic injunction is "the only valid way of knowing dharma". The Vedas are held to be authorless (*apauruṣeya*), the word–meaning relation primordial (MS 1.1.5). [source: SEP-KUM §1.2–1.3, §2.1; WP-Mīmāṃsā (dates, dharma not known by perception or inference)]
  - Metaethical gloss (for the writer to frame as a comparison, not as the school's own terms): dharma is objective and not human-made, yet known neither by perception nor inference — only through authorless scripture. Unlike divine command theory there is no divine author: Mīmāṃsakas argued no maker is needed for the world or the Vedas. [source: WP-Mīmāṃsā; SEP-KUM]
- **Islamic debate: Muʿtazila vs Ashʿarites**:
  - Muʿtazila (8th–10th c., Basra and Baghdad): human intellect can know "the very basics of morality" before revelation; God, being just and wise, "cannot command what is contrary to reason"; ʿAbd al-Jabbār (935–1025, *Sharḥ al-Uṣūl al-Khamsa*): God never chooses the wrong because he knows its immorality and is self-sufficient; acting badly stems from ignorance or need. Al-Ashʿarī attributes to al-Naẓẓām (d. 845) a distinction between acts wrong only because forbidden and acts "wrong by themselves". [source: WP-Mu'tazilism] (WP-Mu'tazilism is partisan in tone — previous research notes flag this; use only these points.)
  - Ashʿarites (founded by al-Ashʿarī, d. 935/6): "Good is what God commands … evil is what God forbids"; right and wrong "are not objective realities" knowable intuitively or naturally (WP citing Esposito and J.A.C. Brown). Al-Ghazālī "maintained in Islamic law (fiqh) the Ashʿarite position that human rationality is misleading with regard to normative judgments about human actions and cannot decide whether an action is 'good' or 'bad'"; we confuse moral value with benefit; "'Good' actions are those that are rewarded in the afterlife and 'bad' actions are those that are punished". [source: WP-Ash'arism; SEP-GHA (Griffel)]
  - Framing: Muʿtazilite rational ethical objectivism vs Ashʿarite theological voluntarism, a version of the Euthyphro question; Muʿtazilis focused on divine justice, Ashʿaris on divine omnipotence. Recommended scholarship: George F. Hourani, *Reason and Tradition in Islamic Ethics* (Cambridge UP, 1985); Sophia Vasalou, *Moral Agents and Their Deserts: The Character of Muʿtazilite Ethics* (Princeton UP, 2008). [source: WP-Mu'tazilism; SEP-GHA bibliography (Hourani)]

## Debates
- Is Hume's is–ought paragraph a logical thesis ("Hume's Law", Hare), a corollary of noncognitivism, an anti-realist thesis, or a narrower claim about demonstrative reason or about the role of sentiment in first discovering virtue? [source: SEP-HM §5]
- Does the open question argument refute naturalism? Moore and many contemporaries said yes; today's "common verdict" is that it refutes only analytic naturalism; Parfit and Scanlon revive it as the Normativity/Triviality objection. [source: SEP-NAT §4]
- Cornell realism vs Moral Twin Earth (Horgan and Timmons vs Copp, Dowell, Dunaway & McPherson, Väyrynen). [source: SEP-NAT §6]
- Is error theory's conceptual premise true? Does morality really claim objective, desire-independent prescriptivity (Mackie, Joyce), or not? Lewis thinks the question may be undecidable. [source: SEP-MAR §3.2]

## Surprises (for the writer)
- The "naturalistic fallacy" is Moore's name for defining good in natural terms; it is not the same thing as Hume's is–ought gap, though the two are often run together.
- Most metaethicists now think Moore's open question argument fails against naturalism as such (only against *analytic* naturalism) — the argument that launched modern metaethics is widely held to have "crippling flaws".
- Mackie coined "error theory" (1977); and Ayer late in life thought Mackie's view closer to the truth than his own emotivism.
- Herodotus's line is "custom is lord of all" in Godley's standard translation (a quotation from Pindar); the Callatiae are described as Indians.
- Mencius and Xunzi agreed more than the slogans suggest: both held everyone can become good; they defined *xing* differently.
- About 62% of surveyed philosophers in 2020 accepted or leaned towards moral realism — up from 56% in 2009, but the surveys are not directly comparable.
