## 1. Moral status: what it is and the candidate criteria
- "To say that a being deserves moral consideration is to say that there is a moral claim that this being can make on those who can recognize such claims. A morally considerable being is a being who can be wronged." The class of beings able to *recognise* moral claims and the class able to *suffer moral wrongs* "are not co-extensive". [source: SEP-MA §1]
- Candidate criteria surveyed by SEP-MA: species membership (speciesism), human exceptionalism (distinctive capacities), personhood (rational/Kantian, legal), sentience, agency. SEP-MA (Gruen): attempts to find sharp behavioural or cognitive boundaries "remain controversial"; most capacities thought distinctive of humans "have been observed, often in less elaborate form, in the non-human world". [source: SEP-MA §§1.1–1.5]
- Moral considerability vs moral significance: "Being morally considerable is like showing up on a moral radar screen—how strong the signal is or where it is located on the screen are separate questions." [source: SEP-MA §2.2]
- IEP's threefold map: (1) **indirect theories** (animals matter only via humans: Aristotle, Aquinas, Descartes, Kant, Carruthers, some religious views); (2) **direct but unequal** (sentience gives animals direct status, but rationality/autonomy/self-consciousness give human interests more weight — "most people" accept this); (3) **moral equality** theories (Singer, Regan). [source: IEP-AE intro, §2]
- Simple argument for direct status (IEP): (1) If a being is sentient, it has direct moral status; (2) (most) animals are sentient; (3) so (most) animals have direct moral status. "Sentience" = capacity for positively or negatively valenced awareness (pleasure, joy, contentment; pain, suffering, depression, anxiety). Premise 2 is usually supported by the argument from analogy (behaviour, physiology, evolutionary closeness). [source: IEP-AE §2a]
- Agency as a criterion: Sebo (2017) distinguishes propositional from "perceptual" agency, widespread in animals; Wilcox (2020) makes agency necessary and sufficient for moral status; Lessard (2024) argues agency better protects animals in law than sentience. [source: SEP-MA §1.5]
- Sentience beyond mammals and birds: recent sentience-based defences of the moral status of fishes, octopuses, arthropods and decapod crustaceans (Crump et al. 2022); because sentience is "a non-observable capacity", several authors defend **precautionary principles** (Fischer 2016; Birch 2017; Knutsson & Munthe 2017). [source: SEP-MA §1.4]
- UK Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022: royal assent 28 April 2022; recognises animal sentience in UK law and covers all vertebrates plus some invertebrates (octopuses, lobsters). Jonathan Birch was Principal Investigator (2021) of the review that led the UK to recognise cephalopods and decapod crustaceans as sentient. [source: WP Animal_Welfare_(Sentience)_Act_2022; WP Jonathan_Birch_(philosopher)]

## 2. Bentham: "Can they suffer?"
- Location: a footnote in Jeremy Bentham, *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (printed 1780, published 1789), chapter XVII ("Of the Limits of the Penal Branch of Jurisprudence"); SEP-MA cites it as "Bentham 1780/1789: chapter xvii, paragraph 6"; the utilitarianism notes cite Collected Works 1970, 283n (Kniess: 282–3n). [source: SEP-MA §1.4; research/nineteenth--utilitarianism.md]
- Context of the footnote (SEP-MA's quotation, with its ellipses): animals, "on account of their interests having been neglected by the insensibility of the ancient jurists, stand degraded into the class of things"; "The day has been, I grieve it to say in many places it is not yet past, in which the greater part of the species, under the denomination of slaves, have been treated … upon the same footing as … animals are still. The day may come, when the rest of the animal creation may acquire those rights which never could have been withholden from them but by the hand of tyranny. The French have already discovered that the blackness of skin is no reason why a human being should be abandoned without redress to the caprice of a tormentor. It may come one day to be recognized, that the number of legs, the villosity of the skin, or the termination of the os sacrum, are reasons equally insufficient for abandoning a sensitive being to the same fate. What else is it that should trace the insuperable line? Is it the faculty of reason, or perhaps, the faculty for discourse?…the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" [source: SEP-MA §1.4] (SEP prints "ossacrum" as one word; WP and other editions print "the blackness *of the* skin"; minor variants between editions.)
- The same footnote also argues that a full-grown horse or dog is "beyond comparison a more rational, as well as a more conversable animal, than an infant of a day, or a week, or even a month, old" — an early form of the argument from marginal cases (wording as given in the utilitarianism notes; the clause "an infant of a day, a week or a month" is verified there). [source: research/nineteenth--utilitarianism.md]
- Bentham nevertheless accepted killing animals for food or defence provided there was no unnecessary suffering, and in a March 1825 letter to the *Morning Chronicle* accepted experiments with "a determinate object, beneficial to mankind". [source: research/nineteenth--utilitarianism.md, citing WP Jeremy_Bentham]

## 3. Speciesism and Singer's *Animal Liberation*
- **Coinage:** "speciesism" first appeared in **1970** in a privately printed pamphlet/leaflet by British psychologist **Richard D. Ryder** (b. 3 July 1940), distributed in Oxford to protest against animal experimentation; Ryder says he thought of the word while lying in the bath at the Old Manor House, Sunningwell, Oxfordshire. He used it again in "Experiments on Animals" in *Animals, Men and Morals* (1971), ed. Stanley and Roslind Godlovitch and John Harris (members of the "Oxford Group"). [source: WP Speciesism; WP Richard_D._Ryder] (SEP-MA says only "in the 1970s"; 1970 is the specific year in WP.)
- Ryder's pamphlet: "[s]ince Darwin, scientists have agreed that there is no 'magical' essential difference between humans and other animals, biologically-speaking. Why then do we make an almost total distinction morally?" [source: WP Speciesism]
- Ryder later coined "painism" (1990); chaired the RSPCA Council 1977–79; helped organise the first academic animal-rights conference, Trinity College, Cambridge, August 1977. [source: WP Richard_D._Ryder]
- **Animal Liberation: A New Ethics for Our Treatment of Animals**, Peter Singer, **1975** (New York Review/Random House); 2nd edition 1990; revised edition **Animal Liberation Now: The Definitive Classic Renewed**, released **23 May 2023**, foreword by Yuval Noah Harari, about two-thirds new material (including meat's climate impact). Widely considered the founding philosophical statement of the animal liberation movement. Singer credited Ryder with the term; chapter 5 is titled "Man's Dominion … a short history of speciesism". [source: WP Animal_Liberation_(book); WP Speciesism]
- Origin story: a 1970 lunch at Oxford with fellow graduate student Richard Keshen, who avoided meat; Singer then read Ruth Harrison's *Animal Machines* and a paper by Roslind Godlovitch. [source: WP Animal_Liberation_(book)]
- Singer's definition of speciesism: "a prejudice or attitude of bias in favour of the interests of members of one's own species and against those of members of other species" (Singer 1990, pp. 6, 9). [source: WP Speciesism]
- Singer is **not** a rights theorist: he "rejected the use of the theoretical framework of rights"; the utilitarian position is "popularly, though erroneously, referred to as an animal rights position". [source: WP Animal_Liberation_(book); SEP-MA §2.2]
- Principle of Equal Consideration of Interests: "The essence of the Principle of Equal Consideration of Interests is that we give equal weight in our moral deliberations to the like interests of all those affected by our actions" (Singer, *Practical Ethics*, 2nd ed. 1993, p. 21). [source: IEP-AE §3a]
- Singer on species overlap: "any such characteristic that is possessed by all human beings will not be possessed only by human beings. For example, all human beings, but not only human beings, are capable of feeling pain; and while only human beings are capable of solving complex mathematical problems, not all humans can do this." [source: WP Argument_from_marginal_cases, citing *Animal Liberation*, HarperCollins 2002]
- Practical conclusions (IEP): factory farming must end; Singer "does not unequivocally claim that we must not eat animals" — whether painless killing wrongs an animal depends on whether it is self-conscious and so has an interest in continued existence; the **replaceability argument** might permit killing non-self-conscious animals who lived good lives and died painlessly, if replaced, but Singer doubts the conditions are met and says factory farms do not meet them. He condemns cosmetic testing and challenges medical experiments: if the benefits justify using animals, consistency would permit using orphaned, severely cognitively disabled infants; recoiling only at the human case is speciesism. [source: IEP-AE §3a.iii] WP: Singer "condemns vivisection except where the benefit … outweighs the harm". [source: WP Animal_Liberation_(book)]
- Singer's well-being theory: *Animal Liberation* defended preference-satisfaction, but Singer "now endorses hedonism" (de Lazari-Radek & Singer 2016). [source: SEP-MA §2.1]
- Singer on the book's impact (to Michael Specter, *The New Yorker*, 6 Sept 1999): "All you have to do is walk around the corner to McDonald's to see how successful I have been." [source: WP Animal_Liberation_(book)]
- Scale: SEP-MA: "An estimated 8 billion animals in the United States are born, confined, biologically manipulated, transported and ultimately slaughtered each year so that humans can consume them" (date-hedge: SEP 2024 revision, figure unsourced in the entry; US only). [source: SEP-MA §2.2]
- Utilitarian argument is "not an argument for vegetarianism" as such: where people would otherwise go hungry, painlessly killing a happy animal may be justified; so utilitarianism can avoid charges of "cultural chauvinism" that rights views face (e.g. Arctic hunting). Objection: that would equally license painlessly killing humans for food. Gruen's reply: a "multi-factor" view — non-persons may lack future-directed interests in continued existence; interests are scalar (crucial > important > replaceable > trivial). [source: SEP-MA §2.2]

## 4. The argument from marginal cases (ARGUMENT FOR STEP-BY-STEP RECONSTRUCTION)
Name: also "argument from species overlap" (Horta; Animal Ethics). "Marginal cases" is described by SEP-MA as "unfortunately" named. [source: WP Argument_from_marginal_cases; SEP-MA §1.3.1]

**Version A (IEP's reductio against indirect theories):**
1. If we are justified in denying direct moral status to animals, then we are justified in denying direct moral status to the marginal cases (infants, people with severe cognitive disabilities, the senile, the comatose).
2. We are not justified in denying direct moral status to the marginal cases.
3. Therefore we are not justified in denying direct moral status to animals.
Support for (1): whatever capacity is cited (reason, autonomy, language), some humans lack it to a degree no greater than some animals. The one property immune to this is *being human*, but then one must claim species membership is itself morally relevant, which "few in recent times are willing" to say. [source: IEP-AE §1f.i]

**Version B (Singer's, as reconstructed by IEP):**
1. To hold that all and only humans have full and equal moral status, there must be some property P that all and only humans have and that grounds this status.
2. Any P that *only* humans have (rationality, autonomy, moral agency, complex language) is a property some humans lack.
3. Any P that *all* humans have (e.g. sentience) is a property many animals have too.
4. So no property grounds the claim that all and only humans have full and equal status. Either not all humans are equal, or not only humans are. Singer judges the first option too counter-intuitive, so we must extend equal consideration to animals. [source: IEP-AE §3a.i]
- Supporting "sophisticated inegalitarian" argument: if racism were wrong only because racists are mistaken about how intelligence is distributed, then weighting interests by intelligence would be acceptable; since that "sophisticated inegalitarianism" is as objectionable as racism, intelligence and rationality cannot be the basis of moral status. Works only for properties that admit of degrees. [source: IEP-AE §3a.ii]
- History: Dombrowski traces the argument to Porphyry's 3rd-century *On Abstinence from Eating Animals*; versions in Singer, Regan (1979), Pluhar (1995), Horta (2014). Frey called it "one of the most common arguments in support of an equal value" of animal lives. [source: WP Argument_from_marginal_cases]

**Objections:**
- *Deny premise 2* (Frey 1980; Francis & Norman 1978): few humans are truly marginal (infants are potentially rational; the senile had rational desires), and it is not counter-intuitive to deny status to the remainder. IEP: "few are willing to accept that conclusion". [source: IEP-AE §1f.i]
- *Kind/species-norm objection* — **Carl Cohen**, "The Case for the Use of Animals in Biomedical Research", *New England Journal of Medicine* 315 (2 Oct 1986): 865–870. Rights arise only among beings who can make moral claims; animals are not beings *of a kind* capable of moral judgment, so they have no rights (though we may have other obligations to them); humans who lack the capacities keep rights because of the kind they belong to (secondary summaries; Cohen's own wording not read). Cohen: "Speciesism is not merely plausible; it is essential for right conduct, because those who will not make the morally relevant distinctions among species are almost certain, in consequence, to misapprehend their true obligations." [source: WP Speciesism (quotation, citing NEJM 1986); WS-6 (summary of the kind argument)] Cohen (30 Apr 1931 – 26 Aug 2023), University of Michigan; co-author with Regan of *The Animal Rights Debate* (2001). [source: WP Carl_Cohen_(philosopher)]
- Related "argument from species normality" (term David Graham; proposed by Tibor Machan's "broken chair" analogy, 2004): a broken chair is still a chair. Rachels's reply: by that logic a chimpanzee who could read and write should not be admitted to university because reading is not normal for chimpanzees. [source: WP Argument_from_marginal_cases]
- McMahan's reply to species-norm accounts: "intelligent and morally sensitive Martians would be required to treat severely retarded human beings in the same way they would be required to treat us" (McMahan 2002; SEP's quotation, wording of the period); species membership is not a focus of collective identity. [source: SEP-CD]
- **Bernard Williams, "The Human Prejudice"** (in *Philosophy as a Humanistic Discipline*, Princeton UP, **2006**, pp. 135–152; published posthumously; WP cites a 2009 reprint in *Peter Singer Under Fire*): speciesism is unlike racism and sexism because "[h]uman beings do not have to deal with any other creature that, in terms of argument, principle, worldview, or whatever, can answer back" (Williams 2006, 148); to grade the world's inhabitants in absolute terms treats the universe as having a point of view (a deity or a utilitarian Ideal Observer). Williams calls his view "humanism". [source: SEP-CD; WP Speciesism] Christopher Grau supports him: rationality etc. are valued because humans have them, so they are as "questionable" as species membership. [source: WP Argument_from_marginal_cases]
- **Cora Diamond**, "Eating Meat and Eating People", *Philosophy* 53 (1978): 465–79: we do not first find a capacity and then conclude humans must be treated well; the requirement is part of the concept "human being"; the case against eating animals is made by exposing tensions in our practices, not by citing shared attributes. [source: SEP-CD; IEP-AE bibliography]
- **Eva Feder Kittay** (disability critique): philosophers who compare humans with severe cognitive disabilities to animals (McMahan 1996, 2002, 2009; Singer 1993, 2009) are criticised as making comparisons that are "unnecessary and offensive" (Carlson 2009; Carlson and Kittay 2009); Kittay ("At the Margins of Personhood", *Ethics* 116 (2005): 100–131) argues there is no reason to assume any human is radically cognitively disabled in the sense used, doubts that evidence about animal cognition transfers to humans, and insists terms like "marginal cases" refer to actual people (her daughter Sesha is the subject of *Learning from My Daughter*, OUP 2020). McMahan (2009) replies that such humans very likely exist. [source: SEP-CD; WP Eva_Kittay] (Name of daughter "Sesha": not in fetched text — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- **Reply on behalf of the argument:** the objectors grant premise 2 (we do owe the most cognitively limited humans direct concern) and so must find a morally relevant property that is not a mere species label; Rachels's chimpanzee and McMahan's Martian press the point that species-norm views make an individual's treatment depend on facts about *other* individuals ("moral alchemy", McMahan 2008, 85). Some versions (Horta 2014) say the argument is not meant to lower the status of humans but to raise that of animals. [source: SEP-CD; WP Argument_from_marginal_cases]

## 5. Regan: *The Case for Animal Rights* and the lifeboat
- Tom Regan (28 Nov 1938 – 17 Feb 2017), American philosopher. *The Case for Animal Rights*, University of California Press, **1983**; 2004 edition with a long new preface answering critics. [source: WP Tom_Regan; WP The_Case_for_Animal_Rights]
- Subject-of-a-life (Regan 1983: 243, as quoted by IEP): beings who "have beliefs and desires; perception, memory, and a sense of the future, including their own future; an emotional life together with feelings of pleasure and pain; preference- and welfare-interests; the ability to initiate action in pursuit of their desires and goals; a psychological identity over time; and an individual welfare in the sense that their experiential life fares well or ill for them, logically independently of their utility for others, and logically independently of their being the object of anyone else's interests." (WP's quotation reads "psychophysical identity over time".) [source: IEP-AE §3b; WP The_Case_for_Animal_Rights]
- Being a subject-of-a-life is **sufficient, not necessary**, for inherent value; Regan held that "normally mental mammals over a year old" qualify (and perhaps some birds and fish). [source: WP The_Case_for_Animal_Rights; SEP-EE §4]
- Popular formulation (Regan 1985, "The Case for Animal Rights", in Singer ed. *In Defence of Animals*, p. 24): subjects of a life "want and prefer things, believe and feel things, recall and expect things… they too must be viewed as the experiencing subjects of a life, with inherent value of their own." [source: SEP-MA §1.3.1]
- Against Singer: what matters is the *individual* with the interest, not the interest; utilitarian aggregation could license experimenting on humans. Rights are not absolute, but may not be overridden merely to make everyone better off ("sacrificing rights for utility"). [source: IEP-AE §3b]
- Practical conclusions: raising animals for food (however treated or killed), experimentation, rodeos and hunting all use animals merely as means; abolition, not reform. [source: IEP-AE §3b; SEP-MA §2.2]
- Conflict principles: when the many and the few would be harmed comparably, override the rights of the few ("miniride"); when harms are not comparable, the **worse-off principle**: avoid making the worse-off worse off. [source: WP The_Case_for_Animal_Rights]
- **Lifeboat case:** four normal adult humans and a dog in a lifeboat that can carry only four; Regan concludes the dog should go overboard, because death is a greater harm to any of the humans than to the dog (worse-off principle); he also discusses a variant weighing one human against a million dogs. Critics say this sits uneasily with equal inherent value. [source: WS-1 (secondary summaries and paper abstracts: Kary; Abbate 2015); Regan's own wording and page not seen]
- Regan called Callicott's holistic land ethic "environmental fascism" (Regan 1983, p. 362). [source: SEP-EE §4]
- Mary Midgley's review (LRB) asked to "move the controversy in the direction of asking what we mean by rights and by equality". [source: WP The_Case_for_Animal_Rights]

## 6. Kantian views: Kant, Korsgaard
- Kant, indirect duties (*Lectures on Ethics*, 1784–5, Ak 27:459): "If a man shoots his dog because the animal is no longer capable of service, he does not fail in his duty to the dog, for the dog cannot judge, but his act is inhuman and damages in himself that humanity which it is his duty to show towards mankind… he who is cruel to animals becomes hard also in his dealings with men." [source: SEP-MA §1.3.1 (Cambridge translation 1997: 212)]
- Objection to indirect views: they miss "the independent wrong that is being done" to the animal (SEP-MA); Nozick's "moral spillover" question (*Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, 1974, p. 36): if it is perfectly all right in itself to do anything to animals, why should it brutalise a person who keeps the line between animals and persons clear? [source: SEP-MA §1.3.1; IEP-AE §1f.ii]
- Christine M. Korsgaard (b. 9 April 1952), Harvard; vegetarian for over 40 years, now vegan. Earlier (1996, *Sources of Normativity*, p. 153): "When you pity a suffering animal, it is because you are perceiving a reason… Another animal can obligate you in exactly the same way another person can… So of course we have obligations to animals." Also "it is a pain to be in pain. And that is not a trivial fact" (1996: 154). [source: SEP-MA §1.4; WP Christine_Korsgaard]
- Korsgaard (2007: 7): what we demand when we demand recognition is that our "natural concerns" be accorded the status of values, and many of those, like the desire to avoid pain, "spring from our animal nature, not from our rational nature". [source: SEP-MA §1.3.1]
- ***Fellow Creatures: Our Obligations to the Other Animals***, Oxford University Press, **2018** (hardback 5 July 2018): argues that Kantian ethics supports obligations to animals; draws on Kant's argument for the value of humanity to argue that rationality commits us to treating every sentient creature as an end in itself, in the sense that its good is absolutely important; begins from an Aristotelian theory of the good — animals are beings for whom things go well or badly; value is "tethered" to creatures for whom things are good. Also 2014 Uehiro Lectures, "The Moral and Legal Standing of Animals". [source: WP Christine_Korsgaard; WS-3 (publisher listings, PhilArchive review)]

## 7. Nussbaum: capabilities for animals
- Martha C. Nussbaum (b. 6 May 1947), University of Chicago. *Frontiers of Justice: Disability, Nationality, and Species Membership* (Harvard/Belknap, 2006) extended the capabilities approach to animals, with a list including life; bodily health; bodily integrity; senses, imagination and thought; emotions; practical reason; affiliation; play; control over one's environment. [source: SEP-MA §2.1; WP Martha_Nussbaum]
- Critique of utilitarianism (Nussbaum, in Sunstein & Nussbaum eds., *Animal Rights*, 2004, 299–320): it ignores adaptive preferences, elides the separateness of persons, misidentifies valuable emotions such as grief, and uses "sum-rankings" instead of inviolable entitlements. [source: WP Animal_Liberation_(book)]
- ***Justice for Animals: Our Collective Responsibility***, Simon & Schuster, New York, **2023** (US edition dated 3 January 2023; a later hardcover date of June 2023 is listed in another market): calls on humanity to secure for all sentient beings the central capabilities they need to flourish. [source: WS-2 (LSE review record; Chicago Review of Books; Publishers Weekly)]
- Objection to species-norm objective lists (SEP-MA): risk of a naturalistic fallacy — it may be natural for stags to fight, but fighting probably does not make a stag's life go well; danger of projecting "an anthropocentric ideal". [source: SEP-MA §2.1]

## 8. Contractualist exclusion (Carruthers) and relational views
- Peter Carruthers (b. 16 June 1952), *The Animals Issue: Moral Theory in Practice*, Cambridge University Press, **1992** (IEP lists subtitle as "Morality in Practice"; see NOT VERIFIED). Extending Rawls's contract to all of morality: self-interested contractors behind the veil know they are not animals, so they adopt rules protecting rational agents only; animals get no direct moral standing. [source: IEP-AE §1d; WP Peter_Carruthers_(philosopher)]
- Carruthers on cruelty: "Such acts [as torturing a cat for fun] are wrong because they are cruel. They betray an indifference to suffering that may manifest itself…with that person's dealings with other rational agents" (1992: 153–54). [source: IEP-AE §1e]
- Carruthers also argued (1989, "Brute Experience", *J. Phil.* 86) that conscious experience requires higher-order thought, which we have no reason to ascribe to animals. (Covered in *Consciousness* as the "animals objection" to HOT theories — cross-reference only.) [source: IEP-AE §1c]
- Regan's reply to contract theory: young children cannot sign contracts yet have rights; on contract views, kicking a dog is wrong only because it upsets its owner. [source: WP The_Case_for_Animal_Rights]
- Feinberg (1974): to have rights a being need only be capable of being *represented* as pursuing its interests; requiring self-representation excludes infants (a marginal-cases move). [source: IEP-AE §2b.i]
- **Zoopolis** (Sue Donaldson & Will Kymlicka, 2011): all sentient animals have basic negative rights; domesticated animals are owed **citizenship**; wild animals **sovereignty** over their territories; liminal animals (squirrels, deer, raccoons) **denizenship**. [source: SEP-MA §2.2]
- Feminist/relational views: Elizabeth Anderson (2004: 289): "Moral considerability is not an intrinsic property of any creature… It depends, deeply, on the kind of relations they can have with us." Gruen's "entangled empathy" (2015); care-ethics critiques of purely rational argument (Adams & Donovan). [source: SEP-MA §3]
- Virtue approach: Rosalind Hursthouse (2000: 165–6) on coming to see meat-eating as "unnecessary, greedy, self-indulgent" — "Without thinking animals had rights, I began to see both the wild ones and the ones we usually eat as having lives of their own". Clark (1977), Diamond (2001), Crary (*Inside Ethics*, 2016). [source: SEP-MA §3]
- Legal personhood: the Nonhuman Rights Project, founded by the late Steven Wise, has filed habeas-corpus-style cases in New York courts for particular chimpanzees. [source: SEP-MA §1.3.2]

## 9. Indian traditions: Jain ahiṃsā, Buddhist non-harm, Aśoka
**Jainism (brief; full treatment in the history chapter).**
- Jain diet: prohibits meat, fish, eggs, alcohol and honey (honey because collecting it harms bees); strict Jains also avoid root vegetables (potatoes, onions, garlic) because they are held to contain infinitely many one-sensed souls (*anant-kāy*); beings are classed by number of senses (five-sensed: humans, animals, birds, fish; one-sensed: plants etc.); lay diet permits only one-sensed plant-bodied beings. [source: WP Jain_vegetarianism]
- Jainism distinguishes physical violence (*dravya-hiṃsā*) from intention (*bhāva-hiṃsā*); practices include not eating after sunset and filtering water. [source: WP Jain_vegetarianism]
- Ahiṃsā predates Mahāvīra: Pārśvanātha (c. 8th century BCE; regarded by modern historians as historical) founded a community whose members vowed ahiṃsā. [source: WP Jain_vegetarianism, citing Dundas 2002]
- SEP-EIB credits the Buddha "along with" Mahāvīra with making ideals of nonviolence more prevalent in India, so that "animal sacrifice declined rapidly in frequency and prestige". [source: SEP-EIB §5]

**Buddhism.**
- First lay precept, as recited: "I undertake the training-precept to abstain from onslaught on breathing beings" (Pāli *Pāṇātipātā veramaṇī sikkhāpadaṃ samādiyāmi*) — translation as given by WP citing Harvey 2000, p. 67. It prohibits killing humans and all animals; violated by intentionally and successfully killing a being one understands to be sentient. [source: WP Five_precepts]
- Buddhism "does not regard humans as fundamentally different from other animals"; through rebirth the same continuum can be human now and animal before or after; an animal you meet "may even have been your own mother in a previous life"; animals suffer and are objects of compassion, lovingkindness and equanimity. But human lives are more precious because "only in a human body can one attain awakening", so killing a human is worse. [source: SEP-EIB §5]
- Dhammapada X.1–2 (Maitreya trans. 1995, p. 37): "Everybody fears being struck by a rod. Everybody fears death. Therefore, knowing this, feeling for others as for yourself, Do not kill others or cause others to kill." The Pāli *attānaṃ upamaṃ katvā* = "having made an analogy with oneself" (Goodman compares the Golden Rule). [source: SEP-EIB §6]
- Hunting and fishing are "wrong livelihood"; the meat trade is one of five wrong livelihoods for laity (*Vaṇijja Sutta*, AN 5.177); in majority-Buddhist countries butchers are often from non-Buddhist minorities. Most Buddhist texts hold plants are not sentient. In Southeast Asia some monks have campaigned against logging. [source: SEP-EIB §5; WP Buddhist_vegetarianism]
- **Vegetarianism debate within Buddhism:**
  - Theravāda: monks must accept what is put in the alms bowl, including meat, unless they have **seen, heard or suspected** that the animal was killed specifically for them (the "threefold purity" rule; *Jīvaka Sutta*, MN 55). The Buddha refused Devadatta's proposal to make vegetarianism compulsory for monks. [source: SEP-EIB §5; WP Buddhist_vegetarianism]
  - Some sources hold it is worse to kill an animal yourself than to eat meat someone else has killed. [source: SEP-EIB §5]
  - Mahāyāna sūtras (e.g. the *Mahāyāna Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra*: "the eating of meat extinguishes the seed of Great Kindness"; the *Aṅgulimālīya Sūtra*) vigorously condemn meat-eating as incompatible with the bodhisattva's compassion. [source: WP Buddhist_vegetarianism]
  - In China, Korea, Vietnam and Taiwan monks and nuns are expected to abstain from meat; Emperor Wu of Liang's ban on meat (and alcohol) for the Chinese saṅgha, in the early sixth century, standardised monastic vegetarianism, backed by the *Brahmajāla* (Fanwang) bodhisattva precepts — dates in sources range from 511 to 517/518 CE. Tibetan Buddhism, where vegetables were scarce, eats meat, though teachers such as Shabkar urged vegetarianism; in Japan Emperor Tenmu's edict of 675 banned eating cows, horses, dogs, monkeys and roosters (*Nihon Shoki*). [source: WP Buddhist_vegetarianism; WS-8]
- Many contemporary Buddhists, "especially in the West", connect Buddhist interdependence to ecological concern. [source: SEP-EIB §5]

**Aśoka's edicts on animals** (translations: E. Hultzsch, *Inscriptions of Asoka*, 1925, public domain):
- Dating: Major Rock Edicts c. 257 BCE (12th–13th regnal year, Sugandhi); Major Pillar Edicts in the 26th–27th regnal years (WP gives "237 to 236 BC" on its chronology; on a coronation of c. 268 the 26th year is c. 243/242 BCE — hedge as "late in his reign, c. 240s–230s BCE"). The edicts are issued in the name "Devānampiya Piyadasi" ("Beloved of the Gods"), not "Aśoka". [source: WP Major_Rock_Edicts; WP Major_Pillar_Edicts; research/ancient--early-buddhism.md]
- **Rock Edict 1:** "Here no living being must be killed and sacrificed… Formerly in the kitchen of King Beloved of the Gods Priyadarsin many hundred thousands of animals were killed daily for the sake of curry. But now… only three animals are being killed (daily), (viz.) two peacocks (and) one deer, but even this deer not regularly. But even these three animals shall not be killed (in future)." (Hultzsch 1925, p. 27) [source: WP Major_Rock_Edicts]
- **Rock Edict 2:** provision of medical care for humans and animals, including in neighbouring kingdoms (Cholas, Pandyas, the Greek king Antiochus II). Rock Edict 3: "Abstention from killing animals is meritorious." Rock Edict 4: previously "for many hundreds of years… the killing of animals and the hurting of living beings" had been promoted, now "abstention from killing animals, abstention from hurting living beings" is promoted. [source: WP Major_Rock_Edicts]
- **Pillar Edict 5** ("anointed twenty-six years"): declares inviolable "parrots, mainas, the aruna, ruddy geese, wild geese… bats, queen-ants, terrapins, boneless fish… tortoises and porcupines, squirrels (?)… the rhinoceros, white doves, domestic doves, (and) all the quadrupeds which are neither useful nor edible"; she-goats, ewes and sows with young or in milk protected; "Husks containing living animals must not be burnt. Forests must not be burnt either uselessly or in order to destroy (living beings). Living animals must not be fed with (other) living animals"; fish not to be killed or sold on certain fast days; castration and branding restricted on holy days. Note it is a list of protected species and days, not a general ban on killing. [source: WP Major_Pillar_Edicts]
- **Pillar Edict 7**: morality is promoted "by moral restrictions and by conversion"; the restrictions, e.g. "that certain animals are inviolable", are "of little consequence"; conversion matters more "because it leads to abstention from hurting living beings and to abstention from killing animals." (Hultzsch p. 137) [source: WP Edicts_of_Ashoka]

## 10. From animal ethics to environmental ethics
- Environmental ethics became a distinct discipline in the **1970s**, prompted by Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962) and Paul Ehrlich's *The Population Bomb* (1968). [source: IEP-EE intro]
- Lynn White Jr., "The Historical Roots of Our Ecologic Crisis" (*Science* 1967): Judeo-Christian thought encouraged exploitation by holding humans superior and nature created for human use; modern science inherited "orthodox Christian arrogance toward nature" (White 1967: 1207); White saw minority traditions (St Francis) as alternatives. John Passmore (1974) replied the tradition also contains "stewardship". [source: SEP-EE §1]
- Intrinsic vs instrumental value is central; "anthropocentric" views in a strong sense give intrinsic value to humans alone, in a weak sense far more to humans. [source: SEP-EE §1]
- **Individualism vs holism / "triangular affair":** animal liberation and biocentrism are individualistic; species, ecosystems are not sentient or subjects-of-a-life; ecosystem integrity may require culling feral animals; disputes whether animal liberation is environmental ethics at all (Callicott 1980; Sagoff 1984; Jamieson 1998). Sagoff's reductio: must we stop predators killing prey? [source: SEP-EE §4; IEP-EE §1b]
- Rolston argued it would be wrong to make a rare butterfly species extinct simply to increase the value of collectors' specimens (Rolston 1975); for endangered species vs individual animals, "priority must be given to the endangered species" (Rolston 1985). [source: SEP-EE §2; IEP-EE]
- Nozick: if species membership is irrelevant, endangered animals would have no special claim (Socratic Puzzles 1997, p. 309). [source: WP Speciesism]

## 11. Leopold's land ethic
- Aldo Leopold (11 Jan 1887 – 21 Apr 1948), American forester, ecologist, University of Wisconsin; died of a heart attack helping neighbours fight a grass fire. [source: WP Aldo_Leopold]
- ***A Sand County Almanac: And Sketches Here and There***, Oxford University Press, **1949**, published posthumously a year after his death, edited by his son Luna; essays about his farm (the "shack") near Baraboo, Sauk County, Wisconsin ("there is, in fact, no 'Sand County'"); includes "Thinking Like a Mountain" and "The Land Ethic". [source: WP A_Sand_County_Almanac; WP Land_ethic]
- Exact maxim: **"A thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community. It is wrong when it tends otherwise."** — Leopold, "The Land Ethic", *A Sand County Almanac* (1949). Page numbers vary by edition: WP cites p. 189 (OUP 1949); SEP cites 224–5; IEP cites the 1949/1989 commemorative edition, pp. 218–225. [source: WP Land_ethic; SEP-EE §2; IEP-EE §1d]
- "The land ethic simply enlarges the boundaries of the community to include soils, waters, plants, and animals, or collectively: the land… [A] land ethic changes the role of *Homo sapiens* from conqueror of the land community to plain member and citizen of it. It implies respect for his fellow-members, and also respect for the community as such." (1949, p. 171 per WP) [source: WP Land_ethic]
- "That land is a community is the basic concept of ecology, but that land is to be loved and respected is an extension of ethics." (Leopold 1949: vii–ix, foreword) [source: SEP-EE §2]
- Leopold "provided no systematic ethical theory"; critics say he moves too quickly from how the land is to what we ought to do (is/ought); Callicott replies that Leopold grounds moral standing in sentiment and affection (Humean/Darwinian), not a property. [source: SEP-EE §2; IEP-EE §1d]
- Callicott (1980) read the maxim as the supreme principle, the biotic community the sole locus of intrinsic value: if culling a white-tailed deer protects the community, the land ethic requires it — and by consistency the same would apply to humans. Charge of misanthropy; Regan: "environmental fascism". Callicott later (1989, 1999) gave individuals intrinsic value too and added "second-order principles": obligations from "more venerable and intimate communities take precedence"; stronger interests generate duties that take precedence over weaker ones (1999: 76). Debate: some read Leopold as a pluralist (Dixon 2017). [source: SEP-EE §4]

## 12. Routley's last man (STEP-BY-STEP CANDIDATE)
- Richard Routley (later Sylvan; 13 Dec 1935 – 16 Jun 1996), New Zealand-born, at the ANU from 1971; married to Val Routley (later Val Plumwood) until their separation in 1982; adopted the name "Sylvan" after remarrying in 1983. [source: WP Richard_Sylvan]
- Paper: "Is There a Need for a New, an Environmental, Ethic?", *Proceedings of the XVth World Congress of Philosophy*, vol. 1 (Sofia: Sophia Press, 1973), pp. 205–210; the congress was held in Bulgaria (WP says Varna; one secondary source says Sofia — say "in Bulgaria"). [source: WP Richard_Sylvan; WS-5]
- Reconstruction (from SEP-EE):
  1. The "dominant western view" (the "western superethic") holds that only human interests matter morally — Routley calls this "human chauvinism", a form of class chauvinism. 
  2. Imagine the last person, surviving a world catastrophe, who acts to ensure the elimination of all other living things (variant: the "last people" destroying forests and ecosystems after their own demise).
  3. On the human-chauvinist view this act harms no human interests (no humans will remain), so it is not wrong.
  4. But we have a firm intuition that the act *is* wrong.
  5. The best explanation is that the non-human things destroyed have intrinsic value, independent of their usefulness to humans.
  6. So the dominant western ethic is inadequate and needs "overhaul of a significant kind": a new, environmental ethic. [source: SEP-EE §2]
- SEP notes the example echoes "the plot of a popular movie some three years earlier" (Lo and Brennan 2013). [source: SEP-EE §2]
- Routley was "very critical" of much deep ecology ("A Critique of Deep Ecology", 1985). [source: WP Richard_Sylvan]
- Objections/replies: the sources read do **not** state a specific named objection to the last-man argument. Usable, sourced material for an objection: virtue-ethical readings explain such wrongness by the agent's character, not the object's value (SEP-EE §4 on virtue approaches, e.g. Hill 1983); and Feinberg's interest principle (only things with interests can have moral/legal standing). Present these as general lines, not as published replies to Routley, or prefer the nonidentity or marginal-cases arguments for the full objection-and-reply. [source: SEP-EE §§2, 4]

## 13. Deep ecology (Næss), Rolston, Taylor
- Arne Næss (27 Jan 1912 – 12 Jan 2009), Norwegian philosopher and mountaineer; led the first ascent of Tirich Mir (1950); founded the journal *Inquiry* (1958); wrote on Spinoza and Gandhi; in 1970 chained himself with protesters below the Mardalsfossen waterfall against a dam. [source: WP Arne_Næss]
- "Deep ecology" was born from discussions between Næss, Sigmund Kvaløy and Nils Faarlund; impressed by Sherpa guides who would not climb mountains they held sacred. [source: SEP-EE §3.1]
- Næss, "The Shallow and the Deep, Long-Range Ecology Movement. A Summary", *Inquiry* 16 (1973): 95–100 (from a 1972 lecture). Shallow ecology = "fight against pollution and resource depletion", whose central objective is "the health and affluence of people in the developed countries"; deep ecology endorses "biospheric egalitarianism". [source: SEP-EE §3.1; IEP-EE; WP Arne_Næss]
- "Ecosophy T" (after his mountain hut Tvergastein): one norm, "Self-realization!" — identification of a widened Self with nature; Spinoza-inspired rejection of atomistic individualism. [source: SEP-EE §3.1; IEP-EE §2a; WP Arne_Næss]
- **Eight-point platform**, drafted by Næss and George Sessions in **April 1984** while camping in Death Valley, California (WS-4: Britannica; reproduced text); published in Devall & Sessions, *Deep Ecology* (1985, p. 70) and Næss 1986. IEP's text (Naess 1986): (1) The well-being and flourishing of human and non-human life on Earth have value in themselves (intrinsic value, inherent worth), independent of usefulness for human purposes. (2) Richness and diversity of life forms contribute to these values and are values in themselves. (3) Humans have no right to reduce this richness and diversity except to satisfy vital needs. (4) The flourishing of human life and cultures is compatible with a substantially smaller population; the flourishing of non-human life requires a smaller human population. (5) Present human interference with the non-human world is excessive, and the situation is rapidly worsening. (6) Policies must therefore be changed (basic economic, technological, ideological structures). (7) The ideological change is mainly appreciating life quality "rather than adhering to an increasingly higher standard of living"; "a profound awareness of the difference between bigness and greatness". (8) Those who subscribe have an obligation directly or indirectly to try to implement the necessary changes. [source: IEP-EE §2a; WP Deep_ecology; WS-4]
- The platform was meant as a middle ground compatible with many "ultimate" orientations: "indigenous, Christian, Buddhist, Daoist, process philosophy". [source: SEP-EE §3.1]
- Population: in a 1982 interview Næss said 100 million would be desirable. [source: WP Deep_ecology]
- **Critiques:** Ramachandra Guha, "Radical American Environmentalism and Wilderness Preservation: A Third World Critique", *Environmental Ethics* 11(1) (Spring 1989): 71–83 — wilderness-centred deep ecology is distinctively American and, exported, a form of cultural imperialism ("green missionaries") that can dispossess the poor; "Putting deep ecology in its place… is to recognize that the trends it derides as 'shallow' ecology might in fact be varieties of environmentalism that are more apposite, more representative and more popular in the countries of the South." Næss repudiated imperialism. Feminists (Plumwood) call the "expanded self" a disguised colonialism that fails to respect nature as genuinely other. Bookchin called it misanthropic (citing Earth First!'s Dave Foreman). [source: SEP-EE §3.1; WP Deep_ecology; WS-9]
- **Holmes Rolston III** (19 Nov 1932 – 12 Feb 2025), Colorado State University; ousted as a Presbyterian minister in 1965 over his environmental interests; 2003 Templeton Prize; *Environmental Ethics: Duties to and Values in the Natural World* (Temple UP, 1988). Species intrinsically valuable, usually more than individual specimens; nature (or God) intrinsically valuable; no duty to intervene in natural suffering. Controversially (1996) said feeding people without attention to social results "we could be feeding a kind of cancer" — criticised as misanthropic (Attfield, Brennan; Martinez-Alier: the poor are often good environmental managers). [source: WP Holmes_Rolston_III; SEP-EE §§2, 5]
- **Paul W. Taylor** (19 Nov 1923 – 14 Oct 2015), Brooklyn College, CUNY; ***Respect for Nature: A Theory of Environmental Ethics***, Princeton University Press, **1986** (25th-anniversary ed. 2011, foreword by Dale Jamieson). Biocentrism: every organism is a "teleological-center-of-life" with a good of its own; all have equal "inherent worth"; denies human superiority. Priority principles (1986, pp. 264–304): self-defence permitted; basic interests of non-humans outweigh non-basic human interests; in clashes of basic interests humans need not sacrifice themselves. Objection: too demanding (one may not pull a weed for a pretty garden). Hierarchical alternatives: Attfield (1983/1987), Varner (1998). [source: WP Paul_W._Taylor; SEP-EE §4; IEP-EE §1c]
- Albert Schweitzer as the "most prominent philosophical influence" on life-centred ethics (reverence for life). [source: IEP-EE §1c]
- Christopher Stone, "Should Trees Have Standing?" (1972): natural objects should have legal standing like corporations; prompted by Sierra Club v. Morton (Disney's Mineral King resort); the Supreme Court majority denied standing, but Justices Douglas, Blackmun and Brennan dissented citing Stone. Stone's work was later cited in arguments for river personhood. [source: SEP-EE §2]

## 15. Indigenous and non-Western views; rights of nature
- Ecuador's **2008** constitution (approved by referendum in September 2008, 63.93% to 28.10%) is "the first in the world to recognize legally enforceable rights of nature"; Articles 71–74; framed with reference to **Pachamama**. [source: WP 2008_Constitution_of_Ecuador; WP Rights_of_nature]
- Universal Declaration of the Rights of Mother Earth, adopted 2010 at the World People's Conference on Climate Change and the Rights of Mother Earth in Bolivia (Cochabamba). [source: WP Rights_of_nature]
- **Te Urewera** (New Zealand, Tūhoe): the former national park became a legal entity with legal personhood in **2014** (Tūhoe Claims Settlement Act 2014), "the first natural resource in the world" given the rights of a person. [source: WP Te_Urewera]
- **Whanganui River** (*Te Awa Tupua*): agreement 30 August 2012; **Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act 2017** passed **15 March 2017** — the world's second natural resource after Te Urewera to receive legal identity, "with all the corresponding rights, duties and liabilities of a legal person" (minister Chris Finlayson: "no stranger than family trusts, or companies"). Iwi first petitioned Parliament in the 1870s; often called NZ's longest-running legal case; represented by two guardians, one from Māori and one from the Crown. [source: WP Whanganui_River]
- Reconciling the "firsts": Ecuador (2008) is the first *constitution* recognising rights of nature; Te Urewera (2014) the first specific natural feature given legal personhood; Whanganui (2017) the first river. [synthesis of the three WP sources above]
- SEP-EE: Stone's and Næss's arguments "have been said to provide analogues to indigenous understandings of the intrinsic value of the land" (Morris and Ruru 2010; Kramm 2020); similar claims about Leopold "need to be interpreted with caution" (White 2015). [source: SEP-EE §2]
- New animism: inspired by how some Indigenous peoples relate to animals, plants and things through ritual (Kimmerer 2020 as an example); Freya Mathews's panpsychism. [source: SEP-EE §3.3]
- Debate: "several scholars have denounced the Indigenous aspects of rights of nature to be a myth", and some argue one-sided implementations could harm Indigenous communities. [source: WP Rights_of_nature]
- Buddhist/Hindu framing used by some rights-of-nature advocates: Indra's Net, dependent arising (Macy 1991; Thiele 2011). [source: WP Rights_of_nature]

## 16. Climate ethics: Gardiner's perfect moral storm
- Stephen M. Gardiner (b. 1967), University of Washington. "A Perfect Moral Storm: Climate Change, Intergenerational Ethics and the Problem of Moral Corruption", *Environmental Values* 15 (2006): 397–413; book *A Perfect Moral Storm: The Ethical Tragedy of Climate Change* (Oxford University Press, **2011**). [source: SEP-EE §6 bibliography; WP Stephen_M._Gardiner]
- Thesis: even if the hard ethical questions (non-identity, historic injustice) were answered, it would still be close to politically impossible to act, because of a convergence of (a) a **global storm** — dispersion of causes and effects, many uncoordinated agents, no effective institutions (a Prisoner's Dilemma / tragedy of the commons); (b) an **intergenerational storm** — the brunt falls on future (and younger) generations, so the present lacks incentive; (c) a **theoretical storm** — mainstream ethical, political and economic theories are not up to the task. The convergence encourages "buck-passing, weakness of will, distraction and procrastination", making us "extremely vulnerable to moral corruption" (2006: 397). Named by analogy with the convergent storms that sank the fishing boat *Andrea Gail* ("the perfect storm"). [source: SEP-EE §6; WS-7]
- Gardiner is pessimistic about geoengineering as an antidote (2011, ch. 11). [source: SEP-EE §6]
- Gardiner's precautionary principle (2006 "A Core Precautionary Principle", *J. Political Philosophy* 14): draws on Rawls's maximin; prioritise avoiding very bad outcomes under uncertainty when gains above the guaranteed minimum matter little and the alternatives include unacceptable outcomes. [source: SEP-CJ §4]

## 17. Intergenerational justice and the non-identity problem (STEP-BY-STEP CANDIDATE)
- Derek Parfit named the "non-identity problem" (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984, ch. 16). [source: IEP-EE §1a; SEP-CJ §3.3]
- Person-affecting intuition: "what is bad must be bad for someone" (Parfit 1987, 363); Narveson: we are in favour of "making people happy" but "neutral" about "making happy people" (1976, 73). Kavka on the "precariousness" of existence (1982): had our forebears acted even slightly differently, a different sperm and egg would have produced a different person. [source: SEP-NI §1]
- **Depletion case** (Parfit 1987, 361–364):
  1. A community chooses to deplete rather than conserve resources; for about two centuries life is "slightly" better, after which quality of life is much lower.
  2. The choice changes the timing and manner of conceptions, so the distant future people under Depletion are different people from those who would exist under Conservation.
  3. Their lives are still worth living, so Depletion is not worse *for* any of them — without it they would not exist.
  4. Person-affecting intuition: an act is wrong only if it is bad for someone.
  5. So Depletion is not wrong.
  6. But surely the "great lowering of the quality of life must provide some moral reason not to choose Depletion". Contradiction. [source: SEP-NI §2.1]
- Applied to climate: any significant climate policy will change who is born, so on the nonidentity logic it "very probably will not make any particular person or collection of future people better off"; Broome (2012: 61–64) uses this to question duties of justice to remote generations. [source: SEP-NI §1; SEP-CJ §3.3]
- **Responses:** (a) **impersonal** views (total utilitarianism: wrongness need not be bad for anyone) — but these lead to Parfit's "repugnant conclusion"; (b) **bite the bullet**: Boonin (2008, 2014) says the intuition confuses atypical with ordinary cases; widely resisted (Parfit 2017); (c) rights violated without harm (Woodward 1986: an airline refusing a Black man a ticket wrongs him even if the plane crashes; Kumar 2003); (d) the **threshold conception of harm** (Hanser, McMahan, Shiffrin, Meyer, Harman): one harms a person if one causes her to come into existence in a sub-threshold state, worse off "than she ought to be"; (e) **sufficientarian** justice: a generation acts unjustly if those in the future fall below a sufficiency threshold, regardless of identity (Caney; Reiman 2007 rejects the "narrow person-affecting" view). [source: SEP-NI §§1, 3; SEP-IJ §3.1; SEP-CJ §3.3; IEP-EE §1a]
- Reciprocity objection: future people cannot reciprocate, so lie outside the moral community (Golding 1972); replies: duties to the dead (Kavka 1978), chain of transgenerational reciprocity (Gewirth 2001). Brian Barry (1999): whatever future people's conception of the good, they need basic resources — food, water, health. [source: IEP-EE §1a]
- Brundtland Report, *Our Common Future* (WCED, 1987), chaired by Gro Harlem Brundtland. [source: SEP-EE §6]

## 18. Discounting: Stern vs Nordhaus
- **Stern Review on the Economics of Climate Change**, ~700 pages, released **30 October 2006** for the UK government by Nicholas Stern (commissioned by Chancellor Gordon Brown, 19 July 2005). Without action, costs equivalent to losing at least **5% of global GDP each year, "now and forever"**, possibly 20% or more with wider risks; stabilisation at 500–550 ppm CO₂e would cost around 1% of global GDP a year if strong action starts now. [source: WP Stern_Review]
- Social discount rate (Ramsey formula) s = δ + ηg (pure time preference + elasticity of marginal utility × consumption growth). Stern used a pure rate of time preference of about **0.1%** (Dasgupta accepted 0.1% but criticised η = 1) and an average discount rate for climate damages of about **1.4%**; earlier studies by Nordhaus and others used pure time preference rates of up to **3%**, which halves the value of a cost or benefit 25 years away. Stern: applying a pure time-preference rate "of anything much more than zero" to social policy is ethically inappropriate; he cited Ramsey, Solow, Sen, Keynes. [source: WP Stern_Review]
- **William Nordhaus** (Yale; developer of the DICE/RICE integrated assessment models; Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics **2018**): the Review's "unambiguous conclusions about the need for extreme immediate action will not survive the substitution of assumptions that are more consistent with today's marketplace real interest rates and savings rates" (Nordhaus 2007, *J. Economic Literature*). The difference between their estimates is largely, though not entirely, explained by the pure time-preference rate. [source: WP Stern_Review; WP William_Nordhaus]
- **Date note:** WP reports that Nordhaus died in New Haven on **30 October 2026**, aged 85 (citing *The New York Times*, 2 Nov 2026). Very recent; if used, say "Nordhaus (1941–2026)". [source: WP William_Nordhaus]
- Philosophers' verdict on **time discounting**: many philosophers object to discounting well-being merely because it comes later — it penalises people "for a property that lacks any moral significance" (Caney 2014; Parfit 1984: 480–486; Rawls 1999: 259–262); communitarian defenders (de Shalit 1995). **Growth discounting** (future people likely richer — Stern 2008: "This reason for discounting is, and should be, part of most models") is more defensible on egalitarian grounds, but depends on the assumption of growth. [source: SEP-CJ §3.2]
- Nordhaus has argued that his "optimal" policy would mean 3.5°C warming in 2100 relative to 1900 (Nordhaus 2018: 348). [source: SEP-CJ §2]

## 19. Who should pay? Burden-sharing (Caney)
- Simon Caney (b. 1966), University of Warwick; author of SEP "Climate Justice" (2020). Distinguishes **isolationism** (treat climate separately from poverty, trade etc.) from **integrationism** (treat it within a general theory of justice), favouring the latter. [source: WP Simon_Caney; SEP-CJ §1]
- **Polluter Pays Principle (PPP):** burdens in proportion to emissions (Shue 2014: 182–186). Objections: (1) excusable ignorance of past emitters — replies: no plausible ignorance "for several decades now" (Singer 2002: 34); beneficiaries of non-culpable emissions can fairly pay; (2) many past emitters are dead — replies: collective agents (states), or benefit; (3) the very poor need to emit to reach a decent standard; if so, rich countries' own early emissions while poor might also be excused. [source: SEP-CJ §5.2.1]
- **Beneficiary Pays Principle (BPP):** pay because and to the extent one has benefited from emitting activities (Page 2012); questions: does it only fill PPP's gaps (Duus-Otterström 2014)? Is benefiting sufficient, if the beneficiary is still poor? [source: SEP-CJ §5.2.2]
- **Ability to Pay Principle (APP):** the greater the ability, the greater the share (Shue 2014: 186–189; Moellendorf 2014); criticised for divorcing payment from causation and benefit and for presupposing a positive duty of assistance. [source: SEP-CJ §5.2.3]
- Caney's sources treat these as combinable ("hybrid" approaches are standard in the literature; SEP's framing is that PPP "should be supplemented by other principles"). [source: SEP-CJ §5.2.2]

## 20. Shue: subsistence vs luxury emissions; equal per capita
- Henry Shue (b. 24 Mar 1940), Merton College, Oxford; *Basic Rights: Subsistence, Affluence, and U.S. Foreign Policy* (Princeton, 1980; 2nd ed. 1996); *Climate Justice: Vulnerability and Protection* (OUP, 2014); *The Pivotal Generation: Why We Have a Moral Responsibility to Slow Climate Change Right Now* (Princeton, 2021). [source: WP Henry_Shue]
- "Subsistence Emissions and Luxury Emissions", *Law & Policy* 15(1) (**1993**): emissions needed to secure the basic right to subsistence should take priority over "luxury" emissions beyond a minimally adequate level; it is unacceptable to ask the poor to give up subsistence emissions so the affluent can keep luxury emissions. [source: SEP-CJ §6.1; WS-10]
- **Equal per capita** alternative: Anil Agarwal and Sunita Narain (Centre for Science and Environment, New Delhi), *Global Warming in an Unequal World: A Case of Environmental Colonialism* (**1991**): the atmosphere's carbon and methane sinks are a "global common" that "should be shared equally on a per capita basis" (p. 9); similar views: Jamieson (2010), Vanderheiden (2008), Singer (2002, *One World*). Versions differ on whether quotas go to countries or individuals and whether past emissions are debited (Neumayer 2000). [source: SEP-CJ §6.2]
- Carbon budgets (date-hedged): AR5-based figure in SEP: ~420 GtCO₂ for a 66–67% chance of 1.5°C (Rogelj et al. 2019); AR6 WGI (2021): from the start of 2020, ~500 GtCO₂ for a 50% chance of staying below 1.5°C, 400 for 67%, 300 for 83%. Paris Agreement (2015): "well below 2°C… pursuing efforts to limit the temperature increase to 1.5°C". [source: SEP-CJ §§2, 6; WP IPCC_Sixth_Assessment_Report]

## Debates
- **Utility vs rights (Singer vs Regan):** equal consideration of interests and aggregation vs inherent value and abolition; Regan says utilitarianism would license using humans; utilitarians say rights views are culturally chauvinist about subsistence hunting. [source: SEP-MA §2.2; IEP-AE §3]
- **Marginal cases:** Singer/Regan/Pluhar/Horta vs Cohen (kind), Machan (species norm), Williams and Diamond (humanism), Kittay and Carlson (disability critique: the comparisons are offensive and the "radically disabled human" may be a philosophers' fiction) vs McMahan (such humans exist; species norm is "moral alchemy"). [source: SEP-CD; IEP-AE; WP]
- **Basis of status:** sentience (Bentham, Singer) vs subject-of-a-life (Regan) vs Kantian reconstruction (Korsgaard) vs capabilities (Nussbaum) vs agency (Sebo, Wilcox) vs relational views (Anderson, Gruen, Donaldson & Kymlicka). [source: SEP-MA]
- **Animal ethics vs environmental ethics** (Callicott's 1980 holism; Sagoff 1984 "bad marriage, quick divorce"-type objections on predation; whether species can matter more than individuals). [source: SEP-EE §4; IEP-EE] (Sagoff's article title not verified.)
- **Holism and "environmental fascism":** Callicott's land ethic vs Regan; Callicott's revision with second-order principles; Leopold as monist or pluralist (Dixon 2017). [source: SEP-EE §4]
- **Deep ecology:** Næss's biospheric egalitarianism vs critics: Guha (wilderness imperialism, Third World critique), Plumwood (expanded self as colonialism), Bookchin (misanthropy), Sylvan (critique 1985); "too vague" to guide action (IEP). [source: SEP-EE §3.1; IEP-EE §2a; WP]
- **Ecofeminism:** Plumwood (critique of rationalism/dualism) vs Warren (dualism harmful only within a logic of domination) vs cultural ecofeminists who stress women's closeness to nature (rejected by Plumwood). [source: IEP-EE §2c]
- **Rights of nature and Indigenous thought:** proponents treat them as aligned with Indigenous worldviews; critics call the Indigenous framing partly a "myth" and warn of harms. [source: WP Rights_of_nature; SEP-EE §2]
- **Buddhist meat-eating:** Theravāda threefold purity and Devadatta episode vs Mahāyāna sūtras' prohibition; East Asian monastic vegetarianism vs Tibetan practice. [source: SEP-EIB; WP Buddhist_vegetarianism]
- **Non-identity problem:** impersonal (total) views → repugnant conclusion; Boonin bites the bullet; rights-based (Woodward, Kumar); threshold harm (Shiffrin, Harman, Meyer); sufficientarianism. Broome uses NIP against duties of justice to remote generations; Caney and others reject the narrow person-affecting premise. [source: SEP-NI; SEP-IJ; SEP-CJ]
- **Discounting:** Stern (near-zero pure time preference, ethical) vs Nordhaus (market-based rates); Dasgupta on η and inequality; Tol: low-rate results "arbitrary". Philosophers largely reject pure time discounting (Parfit, Rawls, Caney). [source: WP Stern_Review; SEP-CJ §3.2]
- **Burden sharing:** PPP vs BPP vs APP; subsistence (Shue) vs equal per capita (Agarwal & Narain, Singer, Jamieson); isolationism vs integrationism (Caney). [source: SEP-CJ]
- **Animal consciousness evidence:** New York Declaration (2024) vs Lau (2024) — handled in *Consciousness*; this chapter cites only the precautionary clause. [source: research/mind--consciousness.md §12]
