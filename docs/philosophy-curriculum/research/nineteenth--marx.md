## Life
- Karl Marx was born on 5 May 1818 at Brückengasse 664 in Trier (Kingdom of Prussia) and died in London on 14 March 1883, aged 64, a stateless person (Prussian citizenship ended 1845) [source: WP-Karl_Marx]
- Family: his father Heinrich Marx, a lawyer and Enlightenment liberal, came from a line that had supplied Trier's rabbis since 1723; his mother Henriette Pressburg was a Dutch Jew; the family had converted, and Karl and his siblings were baptised Lutheran on 28 August 1824 [source: WP-Karl_Marx]
- Studied at Bonn from October 1835 (law, at his father's insistence), then from October 1836 at the University of Berlin, where he heard Eduard Gans (progressive Hegelian) and Savigny; joined the "Doctors' Club" and, through it, the Young Hegelians around Bruno Bauer (and Feuerbach) in 1837 [source: WP-Karl_Marx]
- Doctoral dissertation *The Difference Between the Democritean and Epicurean Philosophy of Nature* (completed 1841), submitted to the more liberal University of Jena, which awarded the doctorate in April 1841 (certificate dated 15 April 1841) [source: WP-Karl_Marx]. SEP-M: "trained academically as a philosopher (with a doctorate in ancient philosophy)" [source: SEP-M intro]
- An academic career was barred by the government's hostility to the Young Hegelians; Marx moved to Cologne in 1842 to write for the *Rheinische Zeitung* (launched 1 Jan 1842); he joined the editorial board on 15 October 1842 and resigned as editor on 17 March 1843; the paper was banned by Prussia in 1843 after pressure from Tsar Nicholas I [source: WP-Rheinische_Zeitung; WP-Karl_Marx]
- Married Jenny von Westphalen (born 12 February 1814, Salzwedel; died 2 December 1881), daughter of the liberal aristocrat Ludwig von Westphalen, on 19 June 1843 at Kreuznach, after a seven-year engagement; Marx dedicated his dissertation to her father [source: WP-Karl_Marx; WP-Jenny_von_Westphalen]
- Paris, October 1843 – early 1845: co-editor with Arnold Ruge of the *Deutsch-Französische Jahrbücher* (one issue), in which Marx published "On the Jewish Question" and the "Introduction" to a Contribution to the Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right; wrote the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts* (April–August 1844); met Engels at the Café de la Régence on 28 August 1844; expelled from France (Guizot) and moved to Brussels in February 1845 [source: WP-Karl_Marx]
- Brussels 1845–48: wrote the *Theses on Feuerbach* (1845) and, with Engels, *The German Ideology* (1845–46); *The Poverty of Philosophy* (1847) against Proudhon; the League of the Just became the Communist League in June 1847 [source: WP-Karl_Marx; SEP-M §2.1]
- *Manifesto of the Communist Party*: commissioned by the Communist League, written December 1847–January 1848, published in German in London (23 pages, anonymously) on/around 21 February 1848 [source: WP-The_Communist_Manifesto]. Authorship: SEP-E says "drafted first by Engels and then revised by Marx"; WP says the final text was "penned exclusively by Marx", drawing on Engels's catechism *Principles of Communism* (late October 1847), and quotes Engels's 1883 preface: "The basic thought running through the *Manifesto* … belongs solely and exclusively to Marx" [sources: SEP-E §1.1; WP-The_Communist_Manifesto]
- 1848–49: Cologne, editor of the *Neue Rheinische Zeitung* (from 1 June 1848); acquitted at several trials; ordered to leave Prussia 16 May 1849; moved to London in (early June / August) 1849 and stayed there for the rest of his life — WP gives both dates in different paragraphs [source: WP-Karl_Marx]
- London years: severe poverty, supported chiefly by Engels; European correspondent of the *New-York Daily Tribune* 1852–62 (first article 21 August 1852; the editor and his contact was Charles Dana); many articles were in fact written by Engels [source: WP-Karl_Marx]
- First International (International Workingmen's Association): Marx was elected to its General Council at its founding in 1864; struggle with Bakunin's anarchist wing; *The Civil War in France* (1871) defended the Paris Commune [source: WP-Karl_Marx]
- *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859), whose Preface became the canonical statement of his theory of history [source: WP-Karl_Marx; SEP-M §2.1]. The *Grundrisse* (notebooks of 1857–58) first appeared in print in 1939 [source: WP-Karl_Marx]
- *Capital*, vol. 1: manuscript delivered to Otto Meissner in Hamburg in April 1867, published September 1867; the first edition of 1,000 copies took four years to sell; second German edition 1872–73; Russian translation 1872. Vols 2 and 3 were edited from Marx's manuscripts by Engels and published in 1885 and 1894 [source: WP-Das_Kapital; WP-Karl_Marx]
- English translations of *Capital* vol. 1: Samuel Moore and Edward Aveling (Eleanor Marx's partner; WP calls him Marx's son-in-law), edited by Engels, 1887; Ben Fowkes (Penguin / New Left Review, 1976); vols 2–3 by David Fernbach (1978, 1981) [source: WP-Das_Kapital]
- Children: seven, of whom three daughters survived to adulthood — Jenny Caroline (Longuet, 1844–1883), Laura (Lafargue, 1845–1911), Eleanor (1855–1898). The claim (made in 1962) that he fathered a son, Frederick ("Freddy") Demuth, with the housekeeper Helene Demuth is disputed for lack of documentary evidence [source: WP-Karl_Marx]
- Eleanor Marx ("Tussy", 16 January 1855 – 31 March 1898): socialist activist and translator of her father's work; translated Flaubert's *Madame Bovary* (1886) and Ibsen; poisoned herself in March 1898 after discovering that her partner Edward Aveling had secretly married [source: WP-Eleanor_Marx]
- Death: after Jenny's death (December 1881) Marx was ill; died 14 March 1883 at home in London; buried in Highgate Cemetery 17 March 1883; Wheen counts 9–11 mourners. Engels's graveside speech: "the greatest living thinker ceased to think" [source: WP-Karl_Marx]. Reburied 1954; the present tomb (unveiled 14 March 1956, erected by the Communist Party of Great Britain) carries "Workers of All Lands Unite" and Thesis XI in Engels's edited wording [source: WP-Karl_Marx; WP-Theses_on_Feuerbach]
- Late writings: *Critique of the Gotha Programme* (1875); correspondence with Vera Zasulich (1881); ethnological notebooks (on Lewis H. Morgan, used by Engels for *Origin of the Family*) [source: WP-Karl_Marx; SEP-E §5.3]

## Engels
- Friedrich Engels: born 28 November 1820 in Barmen (now Wuppertal), son of a textile manufacturer (firm of Ermen & Engels); died of cancer in London on 5 August 1895; ashes scattered off Beachy Head [source: WP-Friedrich_Engels]. SEP-E dates 1820–1895.
- Before collaboration he was "the more well-established figure" among German radicals; anonymous pamphlets against Schelling's Berlin lectures (1842); "Outlines of a Critique of Political Economy" (1844), which "made a strong impression on Marx … and it is usually seen as having given direction to Marx's own turn toward questions of political economy" [source: SEP-E §1.1]
- *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (Leipzig, 1845; English 1887, translated by Florence Kelley Wischnewetzky), written after his 1842–44 stay in Salford/Manchester; SEP-E calls it "still widely regarded as Engels's masterpiece" [source: SEP-E §1.1; WP-The_Condition_of_the_Working_Class_in_England]
- Joint works: *The Holy Family* (1845, first joint book, mostly by Marx); *The German Ideology* (1845–46; "most scholars now argue that this text was intended as a series of articles, not as a single book" — Carver 2010, Johnson 2022); the *Manifesto* (1848) [source: SEP-E §1.1]
- From November 1850 Engels returned to the family firm in Manchester to support Marx for two decades; partner Mary Burns (d. 1863), then her sister Lizzie (married 1878 the day before her death) [source: WP-Friedrich_Engels]
- "Division of labour" (Engels's own phrase, 1887): Marx concentrated on the critique of political economy, Engels wrote for wider audiences [source: SEP-E §1.2]. Engels said he willingly played "second fiddle" [source: SEP-E intro]
- *Anti-Dühring* (*Herr Eugen Dühring's Revolution in Science*, 1878; written 1876–78; Marx drafted one chapter); three chapters reissued as *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific* (1880), "for many decades … by far the most influential popular introduction"; *Dialectics of Nature* (1873–82, unfinished, published 1925); *Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy* (1886; its 1888 pamphlet edition first printed Marx's Theses on Feuerbach); *The Origin of the Family, Private Property, and the State* (1884) [source: SEP-E §§1.2, 2]
- Engels's "laws" of dialectics: in *Anti-Dühring* "transformation of quantity into quality" and "negation of the negation"; in *Dialectics of Nature* drafts also "interpenetration of opposites" [source: SEP-E §3.2]
- **"Dialectical materialism" is not Marx's or Engels's term.** SEP-E: Engels "did not use the compound term … The term … may have multiple origins, and is commonly attributed both to … G. V. Plekhanov and to … Joseph Dietzgen; it was widely popularized only after Engels's death", and editorial headings in posthumous editions created "the mistaken impression that it was his own term" [source: SEP-E §3]. WP says Dietzgen (1828–1888) coined it in 1887 and Plekhanov introduced it into Marxist literature in the 1890s [source: WP-Dialectical_materialism]; WS reports Plekhanov's use in 1891 writings on Hegel and doubts about whether Dietzgen used the exact phrase [source: WS-6]. Safe wording: "coined by the 1880s–90s, usually credited to Dietzgen and Plekhanov". Stalin codified it in "Dialectical and Historical Materialism" (1938) [source: WP-Dialectical_materialism]
- Engels's 1884 *Origin* places "the production and reproduction of immediate life" — both subsistence and "the propagation of the species" — in the material base; "the first class antithesis which appears in history coincides with the development of the antagonism between man and woman in monogamous marriage"; built on Marx's notes on Lewis Morgan, but "the arguments … are distinctively Engels's own"; taken up in second-wave and Marxist-feminist (social reproduction) theory [source: SEP-E §5.3, quoting MECW 26: 131–32, 173–74]

## The 1844 Manuscripts and alienation
- Written April–August 1844 in Paris; unpublished in Marx's lifetime; first published in 1932 (MEGA, Moscow; D. Ryazanov edited, Lukács helped decipher them); standard English translation Martin Milligan [source: WP-Economic_and_Philosophic_Manuscripts_of_1844]
- Source of the idea: Feuerbach's *The Essence of Christianity* (1841): humans invent God in their own image and project their powers onto him, alienating their "species essence". Marx accepted this but said Feuerbach could not explain why people fall into religious alienation; religion is a response to alienation in material life and will wither only when material life is emancipated [source: SEP-M §1.2]
- Alienation in general = "a separation between a subject and an object that properly belong together", problematic by reference to a baseline unity [source: SEP-M §1.1]
- The four aspects (MECW 3: 270–282), as SEP-M lists them: (1) from the product, which workers "neither own nor control, indeed, which comes to dominate them"; (2) from productive activity, work that is "mentally and/or physically debilitating"; (3) from other individuals, seen "as merely means to their own particular ends"; (4) from their own human nature (species-being), "the human capacities for community and for free, conscious, and creative, work" [source: SEP-M §1.2]. NB: Marx's own order in the manuscript, followed by WP, is product → activity → species-being (*Gattungswesen*) → other people [source: WP-Marx's_theory_of_alienation]. Either order may be used; the WP order matches the request.
- Key move: "it is the implicit claim about human nature (the fourth separation) which identifies the other three separations as dysfunctional" [source: SEP-M §1.2]
- Alienation ≠ low pay: alienated labour "could—even if it is typically not—be highly remunerated, limited in duration, and relatively secure" [source: SEP-M §1.2]
- Unalienated work (Kandiyali 2020): freely chosen; self-realising; intended to satisfy others' needs; appropriately appreciated by them [source: SEP-M §1.2; SEP-AL §3]
- Objectification (*Vergegenständlichung*) is not alienation: all production objectifies, but only some is alienated; the "Christian" view of work as a necessary evil confuses the two [source: SEP-AL §3]
- Alienation comes from capitalist social relations, not technology; Marx rejected the "romantic critique of capitalism" and celebrated the bourgeoisie's destruction of feudalism [source: SEP-M §1.3]
- Subjective vs objective alienation: in *The Holy Family* capitalists are objectively alienated but feel "at ease" in it (MECW 4: 36) [source: SEP-M §1.5]
- Political vs human emancipation ("On the Jewish Question", 1843, against Bruno Bauer): liberal rights are "rights of separation"; political emancipation is an improvement on feudalism but insufficient for human emancipation [source: SEP-M §1.4]. WP notes a debate: some (Paul Johnson, Bernard Lewis, Hyam Maccoby, Robert Wistrich) read the essay as antisemitic; others (Wendy Brown, Robert Fine, David McLellan, Francis Wheen) dispute this [source: WP-Karl_Marx]

## Theses on Feuerbach and The German Ideology
- Eleven short notes written in Brussels in 1845 as an outline connected with *The German Ideology*; first published (edited) by Engels in February 1888 as an appendix to *Ludwig Feuerbach*; Marx's unedited text first published 1924 (Marx–Engels Archives, Moscow) [source: WP-Theses_on_Feuerbach]
- Content: old materialism understood physical reality but ignored the active subject; idealism (Hegel) grasped activity but only as thought; Marx's materialism locates world-transforming activity in material practice [source: SEP-M §2.2]
- Thesis XI — see Quotations; Engels's 1888 edition added "however" ("aber"); the tomb carries the Engels version [source: WP-Karl_Marx; WS-1]
- *The German Ideology* (Marx and Engels, 1845–46): unpublished in their lives ("the gnawing criticism of the mice", Marx's 1859 Preface); parts published 1921 (Gustav Mayer) and 1924/1926 (Ryazanov, "Feuerbach" chapter); first complete edition 1932 (MEGA, Moscow, under Adoratsky), an editorial "construction" from manuscripts [source: WP-The_German_Ideology]
- Its "premises": start from "real human beings", who must produce their means of subsistence; material life determines, or at least "conditions", social life and then consciousness [source: SEP-M §2.2]
- Ideology as "inversion", the "camera obscura" image (MECW 5: 36) [source: SEP-M §5.1]

## Historical materialism: the 1859 Preface and Cohen's reconstruction
- Preface key text (Ryazanskaya trans.) — see Quotations; core terms: relations of production, material productive forces, economic structure = "real foundation", legal and political superstructure, forms of social consciousness; at a certain stage relations "turn into their fetters. Then begins an era of social revolution" [source: WP-A_Contribution_to_the_Critique_of_Political_Economy; WS-10]
- Cohen's reconstruction (*Karl Marx's Theory of History: A Defence*, 1978; 2nd ed. 2001), the argument to present step by step [source: SEP-M §§2.3–2.5]:
  1. Development Thesis (presupposed, not stated in the Preface): productive forces (means of production + labour power) *tend* to develop over time.
  2. Primacy Thesis (a): the economic structure is explained by the level of development of the productive forces.
  3. Primacy Thesis (b): the superstructure (legal and political institutions) — and ideology — is explained by the economic structure.
  4. When an economic structure ceases to develop the forces it "fetters" them and is "burst asunder" (Manifesto, MECW 6: 489), replaced by one better suited.
  5. Apparent contradiction: the *Manifesto* also says "The bourgeoisie cannot exist without constantly revolutionising the instruments of production" (MECW 6: 487), giving the structure primacy.
  6. Resolution by **functional explanation**: capitalism does develop the forces, and that is *why* it exists; if it stopped, it would disappear. Fettering = dysfunction.
- Cohen's development thesis rests on humans being "somewhat rational", facing scarcity, and having intelligence to improve their situation [source: WP-Karl_Marx's_Theory_of_History]
- Objection (Elster, *Making Sense of Marx*, 1985: 27–35): functional explanation needs a mechanism; without a guiding agent it appeals to "purposes" in history that are no one's purposes. Simone Weil anticipated this: Marx attributed "to matter what is the very essence of mind—an unceasing aspiration towards the best" (Weil 1955, trans. Wills & Petrie 1958: 43) [source: SEP-M §2.4]
- Cohen's reply: biology offered justified functional explanations (birds' hollow bones) before Darwin supplied the mechanism; functional explanations need "elaborations", but so do causal ones; enough inductive evidence can justify one without a known mechanism [source: SEP-M §2.4]
- Counter-reply: (a) empirically, long periods of stagnation and regression without revolution; (b) a Darwinian-style elaboration (chance variation, selection of structures) would make history contingent and undercut Marx's prediction of communism — "the best model for developing the theory makes predictions based on the theory unsound" [source: SEP-M §2.4]
- Further objections: technology's development is driven by profit, not scarcity; ruling classes may prefer stagnation; Cohen himself ("Reconsidering Historical Materialism", 1988) worried that the emphasis on production is one-sided [source: SEP-M §2.5]. Cohen's book "has been criticised as a form of technological determinism" and won the Isaac Deutscher memorial prize [source: WP-Karl_Marx's_Theory_of_History]
- Class struggle has a small role in Cohen's account because the 1859 Preface does not mention it explicitly; but the Preface implies it ("then begins an era of social revolution", "the ideological forms in which men become conscious of this conflict and fight it out"), and Marx may have written with the censor in mind [source: SEP-M §2.1]
- Alternatives: Marx had no general theory, only analysis of particular episodes (e.g. *Eighteenth Brumaire*, 1852); a looser theory (Richard Miller 1984); dialectical critics reject Cohen (Sean Sayers 1984) [source: SEP-M §2.6]
- Before Cohen, H. B. Acton (*The Illusion of the Epoch*, 1955: 271): "Marxism is a philosophical farrago" [source: SEP-M §2.4]
- Kohei Saito (2017, 2024): after *Capital* vol. 1 Marx moved towards eco-socialism, and in his last years (e.g. the Zasulich correspondence) towards "degrowth communism" — a contested reading [source: SEP-M §2.6]

## Ideology and "false consciousness"
- Ideology as non-repressive source of class-society "stability": dominant ideas are "false or misleading in a fashion that works to the advantage of the economically dominant class"; it is part, not all, of the explanation (also "dull economic pressure", collective-action problems, doubts about alternatives) [source: SEP-M §5.2]
- Marx has "no clear and sustained discussion of ideology"; commentators find two to five models (Geuss 1981: descriptive, positive, critical) [source: SEP-M §5.1]
- Typical falsehoods: presenting ruling-class interests as the general interest (MECW 5: 60); presenting historical arrangements as natural (MECW 35: 605); the "wage form" hides surplus labour [source: SEP-M §5.3]
- Objection (Elster 1985: 473): Marx never explains why the ruling ideas should be those of the ruling class (functionalism again). Reply: mechanisms are mentioned — control of "means of mental production" (MECW 5: 59), and psychological need for consolation (religion, MECW 3: 175) [source: SEP-M §5.3]
- Ricoeur called Marx, with Nietzsche and Freud, a "master of suspicion" (Ricoeur 1970: 32–33) [source: SEP-M §5.1]
- **"False consciousness" is not Marx's phrase.** Engels used it in a letter to Franz Mehring of 14 July 1893 (MECW 50: 164): ideology is a process carried out consciously "but with a false consciousness"; the real motive forces remain unknown to the thinker [sources: SEP-ID §1.3.1; WP-False_consciousness; WS-3]. SEP-E: the phrase "was not a keyword of Engels's writing … though he did use it casually in correspondence" [source: SEP-E §5.2]. Lukács (1923) developed it [source: WP-False_consciousness]
- Surprise: the phrase predates Marxism — Destutt de Tracy, who coined "ideology", wrote of feelings that "form within us a false and blind consciousness [une fausse et aveugle conscience]" (1817) and conceived ideology as a *remedy* for false consciousness [source: SEP-ID §1.1]. Marx read Destutt de Tracy in 1844 and again for *Capital* [source: SEP-ID §1.3.1]

## Religion
- "Opium of the people" is in the "Introduction" to *A Contribution to the Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right*, written 1843 (late 1843–January 1844), published February 1844 in the *Deutsch-Französische Jahrbücher* (print run c. 1,000); the book itself was published only after Marx's death [source: WP-Opium_of_the_people]
- SEP-M: religion as "a harmful, illusion-generating painkiller" (MECW 3: 175) [source: SEP-M §1.2]; Avineri reads the passage as expressing "empathy, not scorn" for religious feeling [source: WP-Karl_Marx]
- The common quotation splices "bits of two sentences"; context: "Religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature…" [source: WP-Opium_of_the_people]

## Class struggle, the Manifesto and the state
- Opening of chapter 1: "The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles" (Samuel Moore's 1888 English translation, approved by Engels and standard since) [source: WP-The_Communist_Manifesto]
- First English translation: Helen Macfarlane, serialised in Harney's *Red Republican*, November 1850 ("A frightful hobgoblin stalks throughout Europe") [source: WP-The_Communist_Manifesto]
- Three models of the state (Elster 1985: 409–437): instrumental ("the executive of the modern state is but a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie", MECW 6: 486), largely abandoned from the early 1850s; class balance (Bonapartism after December 1851); abdication (bourgeoisie gives up the "crown" to save its "purse", MECW 11: 143) — closest to Marx's considered view [source: SEP-M §6.1]
- Marx never directly says capitalism is unjust; Wood (1972) says he did not think it unjust; Husami (1978) says he did; Cohen (1983): Marx "believed that capitalism was unjust, but did not believe that he believed it was unjust" [source: SEP-M §4.2]

## Capital: value, surplus value, exploitation (argument)
- Commodity = useful external object produced for exchange; has use-value and exchange-value [source: SEP-M §3.2]
- Labour theory of value: value determined by "socially necessary" labour time (average intensity and productivity). Two-stage argument: (1) if commodities can be equated there must be a "third thing of identical magnitude in both of them"; (2) labour is the only plausible common element. "Both steps … highly contestable" [source: SEP-M §3.2]
- Surplus-value argument step by step [source: SEP-M §3.2; SEP-EX §1.2]:
  1. The capitalist buys labour power (the capacity to work) for a day, at its value.
  2. Its value = the value of the commodities needed to keep the worker going (e.g. 4 hours' labour).
  3. The worker works longer (e.g. 8–12 hours); hours beyond "necessary labour" are "surplus labour" creating surplus value.
  4. Labour power is the only commodity producing more value than it costs ("variable capital"); means of production only pass on their value ("constant capital").
  5. So surplus value is the source of all profit; workers, lacking means of production, are forced to sell labour power: exploitation.
- Like a feudal serf's corvée, but hidden: under capitalism workers "appear to work entirely for the benefit of themselves" [source: SEP-EX §1.2]
- Formulas: rate of exploitation s/v; organic composition c/v; rate of profit s/(c+v) [source: SEP-EX §1.2]
- Objection 1, transformation problem: the theory implies labour-intensive industries earn higher profit rates, which is empirically false and inconsistent with competition equalising profit rates; Marx's own solution in vol. 3 (prices deviating systematically from values) "suffers from technical difficulties" [source: SEP-M §3.2; SEP-EX §1.2]. Classic statement: Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk (1851–1914), *Zum Abschluss des Marxschen Systems* (1896; English *Karl Marx and the Close of His System*, trans. Alice M. Macdonald, 1898) — Vol. 3 contradicts Vol. 1; Hilferding replied for Marx [source: WP-Karl_Marx_and_the_Close_of_His_System; WP-Transformation_problem]. Bortkiewicz (1906) showed no general solution; later Steedman (*Marx after Sraffa*); Samuelson (1971); defenders (the "temporal single-system interpretation", Kliman and Freeman) deny any inconsistency [source: WP-Transformation_problem]
- Objection 2, marginalism: the labour theory of value "has largely been abandoned by economists in the wake of the marginalist revolution of the 1870s"; heterogeneous labour, value of unproduced land and resources [source: SEP-EX §1.2]
- Objection 3: why labour? A formally identical "corn theory of value" could be built (Roemer 1982; R. P. Wolff 1981) [source: SEP-M §3.2; SEP-EX §1.2]
- Nozick (1974): if the labour theory of value fails, Marx's theory of exploitation collapses [source: SEP-M §3.3]
- Replies: Cohen (1979): exploitation is independent of (even incompatible with) the labour theory of value — "Labor may not produce value, but it is the only thing that produces what has value"; Roemer (1985) defines exploitation as unequal exchange of labour for goods; others add unfreedom or domination (Vrousalis 2013) [source: SEP-EX §1.2; SEP-M §3.3]. Joan Robinson (1942) kept Marx's class conflict and denial of a tendency to equilibrium [source: SEP-M §3.2]
- Falling rate of profit predicted in the vol. 3 manuscripts; Sweezy (1942) found the analysis problematic [source: SEP-M §3.2]
- Readings: orthodox (economic theory) vs Roberts (2017: *Capital* as political theory) vs Heinrich (2012) [source: SEP-M §3.1]
- Commodity fetishism (*Capital* vol. 1, ch. 1 §4; Fowkes p. 165): see Quotations. SEP-M: fetishism — "human creations escape our control, achieve the appearance of independence, and come to oppress us" — is one form of alienation, not identical to it [source: WP-Commodity_fetishism; SEP-M §1.2]
- Consistency: Periergia's Capitalism chapter says Marx "rarely or never used the noun" capitalism, speaking of the "capitalist mode of production" — SEP-M also uses "capitalist mode of production"; no contradiction.

## Communism, the dictatorship of the proletariat, the Gotha slogan
- Marx refused "recipes" for the "restaurants" of the future (MECW 35: 17); anti-utopian arguments (blueprints undemocratic, impossible, redundant) [source: SEP-M §7]
- *Critique of the Gotha Programme* (1875): marginal notes to the Eisenach leaders against Lassallean influence before the Gotha unity congress (May 1875); suppressed, published by Engels in *Die Neue Zeit* in 1891; two phases of communism (lower: according to labour contributed; higher: needs); Lenin's *State and Revolution* builds on it [source: WP-Critique_of_the_Gotha_Programme]
- Origin of the slogan: it predates Marx — attributed to Louis Blanc (in later editions of *L'Organisation du travail*, dating disputed; Engels credited Blanc in 1851), with precursors in Saint-Simonian formulas, Cabet, Morelly's *Code of Nature* (1755) and Acts 4:35 [source: WP-From_each…; WS-5]
- Justice: Gilabert (2015) reads the needs principle as a principle of justice; Lukes (1987) reads communism as transcending justice (Humean circumstances of justice: abundance or full fellow-feeling) [source: SEP-M §4.3]
- "Dictatorship of the proletariat": Marx's uses are "infrequent" (Draper 1986; Hunt 1974); "dictatorship" then meant emergency rule (Roman *dictatura*), not totalitarianism; Marx's usage implies a democratic, temporary, extra-legal transition [source: SEP-M §6.2]. First uses: "Dictatorship of the working class!" in *The Class Struggles in France* (1850); letter to Weydemeyer, 5 March 1852; the phrase was likely first used by Blanqui (1837); Engels (1891): "Look at the Paris Commune. That was the dictatorship of the proletariat" [source: WP-Dictatorship_of_the_proletariat]
- Paris Commune as model: elections, recall, officials paid workers' wages — "democracy without professionals" (Hunt); "Freedom consists in converting the state from an organ superimposed upon society into one completely subordinate to it" (MECW 24: 94) [source: SEP-M §6.2]

## Colonialism, India, late Marx
- 1853 *New-York Daily Tribune* articles: "The British Rule in India" (dated London 10 June 1853, published 25 June) and "The Future Results of British Rule in India" (written 22 July, published 8 August 1853) [source: WS-8]. Marx condemned British devastation ("misery … infinitely more intensive") yet called village communities "the solid foundation of Oriental despotism" and England "the unconscious tool of history" in bringing a social revolution to Asia [source: WP-Karl_Marx (quotations); WS-8]
- Said's *Orientalism* (WS cites the 1979 Vintage edition; first-edition year not checked here) uses as epigraph Marx's "They cannot represent themselves; they must be represented" (from *The Eighteenth Brumaire*, about French smallholding peasants — the peasant context is my knowledge only), beside Disraeli's "The East is a career"; Said reads the 1853 India articles as reproducing Orientalist assumptions; Aijaz Ahmad objected that Said relied on two passages and ignored Marx's other writings on India [source: WS-7, WS-9]
- Kevin B. Anderson, *Marx at the Margins: On Nationalism, Ethnicity, and Non-Western Societies* (Chicago, 2010): Marx moved from a unilinear to a "multilinear and nonreductionist" theory of history (India, Russia, Ireland, Poland) [source: SEP-M bibliography; WS-11]
- Zasulich: Vera Zasulich wrote on 16 (28) February 1881 asking whether the Russian peasant commune (*obshchina*/*mir*) could pass directly to socialism; Marx's reply of 8 March 1881 (after several long drafts) says the "historical inevitability" of the process in *Capital* is "expressly restricted to the countries of Western Europe" and that his studies convinced him the commune is "the fulcrum for social regeneration in Russia" (Shanin ed. translation; Progress renders "fulcrum of Russia's social revival"), if harmful influences are removed [source: WP-Vera_Zasulich; WP-Karl_Marx; WS-11b]. Shanin (ed.), *Late Marx and the Russian Road* (1983) [source: SEP-M bibliography]
- Algeria 1882: Marx observed colonial torture of Arabs and settler arrogance [source: WP-Karl_Marx]

## Early vs mature Marx
- Althusser (1918–1990), *For Marx* and *Reading Capital* (both 1965): an "epistemological break" (term from Bachelard, 1938) in 1845 (*The German Ideology*) separates the humanist, Feuerbachian young Marx from the scientific Marx; "theoretical anti-humanism" rejects "species-being" as bourgeois ideology [source: WP-Louis_Althusser; WP-Epistemological_rupture]
- Continuity readings: the claim that Marx wrote about alienation only early "is not sustained by the textual evidence" (its role may shift from explanatory to diagnostic: Wood 1981); the mature Marx still holds a universal human nature with constant and variable elements (Geras 1983; McMurtry 1978) [source: SEP-M §1.5]. Many analysts see commodity fetishism as a deeper elaboration of alienation [source: WP-Marx's_theory_of_alienation]
- The 1932 publication of the Manuscripts and *German Ideology* fuelled humanist readings; Landshut's 1953 West German edition stressed early humanism [source: WP-The_German_Ideology; WP-Economic_and_Philosophic_Manuscripts_of_1844]

## Marx and the Marxisms
- "I am not a Marxist": Marx's remark, reported by Engels, said to Paul Lafargue about French "Marxists"; Engels reported it in letters to Eduard Bernstein (2–3 November 1882) and Conrad Schmidt (5 August 1890); French wording varies ("ce qu'il y a de certain c'est que moi, je ne suis pas marxiste" / "tout ce que je sais, c'est que moi, je ne suis pas marxiste") [source: WS-4, MECW notes as summarised]. Use as "Engels reported Marx saying…".
- Second International (1889–1914); "orthodox Marxism" = Kautsky's codification, history as science proving capitalism's collapse inevitable; after 1914 Lenin's orthodoxy on the left and reformism on the right [source: WP-Second_International; WP-Orthodox_Marxism]
- Engels's role: as early as the 1890s critics spoke of "Engels's distortion of Marx"; Lukács (1923) blamed Engels for extending dialectics to nature; Norman Levine, *The Tragic Deception: Marx Contra Engels* (1975), said Engels misrepresented Marx and laid foundations for Stalinism; Terrell Carver, *Marx and Engels: The Intellectual Relationship* (1983), took an intermediate position; Cold War politics shaped the split (Blackledge 2019); MEGA now moves the debate to Engels's editing of *Capital* vols 2–3, which has "both fervent defenders and incisive critics" [source: SEP-E §2]
- Western Marxism: Lukács, Korsch, Gramsci, Frankfurt School; term traced to Comintern attacks of 1923, popularised by Merleau-Ponty (*Adventures of the Dialectic*, 1955); Perry Anderson, *Considerations on Western Marxism* (1976): its "structural divorce from political practice" [source: WP-Western_Marxism]. SEP-M points to Anderson 1976 and Kołakowski for the legacy [source: SEP-M §8]
- Stalin's "Dialectical and Historical Materialism" (1938) became mandatory Soviet doctrine [source: WP-Dialectical_materialism]
- SEP-M's verdict: Marx's thought is "less systematic than sometimes thought"; it is "a mistake to think that all the various threads … form a 'system' which has to be swallowed or rejected in its entirety" [source: SEP-M §8]
- Analytical Marxism: September Group, from London meetings on exploitation convened by Elster with Cohen's support in September 1979 and 1980; initially also called the "Non-Bullshit Marxism Group"; members by 2000 included Bardhan, Bowles, Brenner, G. A. Cohen, Joshua Cohen, Van Parijs, Roemer, Steiner, van der Veen, Wright; Elster and Przeworski left in 1993 ("not much of Marxism is left") [source: SEP-AM §§1.3, 2.2]

## Debates
- Engels vs Marx: distorter (Lukács 1923; Levine 1975) vs faithful collaborator vs intermediate (Carver 1983, 2020); now focused on Engels's editing of *Capital* vols 2–3 [source: SEP-E §2]
- Early vs mature Marx: Althusser's 1845 epistemological break vs continuity (Wood, Geras, alienation → fetishism) [source: WP-Louis_Althusser; SEP-M §1.5]
- Theory of history: Cohen's functionalist technological reading vs Elster's critique vs dialectical (Sayers) and "no general theory" readings [source: SEP-M §§2.4–2.6]
- Did Marx think capitalism unjust? Wood vs Husami vs Cohen [source: SEP-M §4.2]
- Labour theory of value and exploitation: transformation problem, marginalism; exploitation reconstructed without LTV (Cohen, Roemer, Vrousalis) [source: SEP-M §3; SEP-EX §1.2]
- Marx on colonialism: Eurocentric/Orientalist (Said) vs multilinear late Marx (Anderson; Zasulich letter) [source: WS-7; WS-11]
- Ecological Marx (Saito) vs "Promethean" productivism [source: SEP-M §2.6]
- Marx and the 20th-century states: "dictatorship" meant temporary emergency rule (Draper, Hunt; SEP-M) vs Soviet usage; SEP-M declines to survey legacy [source: SEP-M §§6.2, 8]
