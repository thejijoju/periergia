## 1. The problem of perception: the Crane–French framing (SEP-PP)

- Crane and French cite A.D. Smith: the Problem of Perception is the "question of whether we can ever directly perceive the physical world", where the physical world has "an existence that is not in any way dependent upon its being... perceived or thought about" (Smith 2002: 1). [source: SEP-PP §1]
- "Our ordinary conception of perceptual experience" is built from phenomenological reflection, not science (Broad 1952: "purely phenomenological point of view"). Strawson (1979: 97): mature sensible experience "presents itself as, in Kantian phrase, an immediate consciousness of the existence of things outside us"; McDowell (1994: 111): experience is "openness to the world". [source: SEP-PP §1.1]
- Strawson's sample description of an experience: "I see the red light of the setting sun filtering through the black and thickly clustered branches of the elms; I see the dappled deer grazing in groups on the vivid green grass…" (1979: 97). Usable as an opening illustration. [source: SEP-PP §1.2]
- The ordinary conception's components as SEP-PP labels them: **Ordinary Objects** (experiences are directly of ordinary mind-independent objects); **Presentation** (experiences are direct perceptual presentations of their objects); together **Direct Realist Presentation**, from which **Direct Realism** ("we can directly perceive ordinary objects") follows; **Direct Realist Character** (phenomenal character is determined at least partly by the direct presentation of ordinary objects); **Common Kind Claim** (veridical, illusory and hallucinatory experiences "(as) of an F are fundamentally the same; they form a common kind"). [source: SEP-PP §§1.2–1.6]
- Definitions used: veridical = an ordinary object is perceived and appears as it is; illusion = an ordinary object is perceived but appears other than it is; hallucination = an experience that seems to the subject exactly like a veridical perception but where there is no such perceived object. Running example: a "snow-covered churchyard". [source: SEP-PP §1.6]
- The Problem stated: "if illusions and hallucinations are possible, then perceptual experience, as we ordinarily understand it, is impossible." [source: SEP-PP §2]
- Generic structure of both arguments: (A) in an illusory/hallucinatory experience a subject is not directly presented with an ordinary object; (B) the same account must apply to veridical experiences as to illusory/hallucinatory ones; therefore (C) subjects are never directly presented with ordinary objects. [source: SEP-PP §2]
- Paul Snowdon (1992, 2005) named the two movements: the **base case** (A) and the **spreading step** (B), which generalises the result; background assumptions (a1) and (a2) carry it from veridical cases to all cases. "The main burden on the arguer from illusion is in supporting... (A), whereas the main burden on the arguer from hallucination is in defending... (B)." [source: SEP-PP §2]
- Classic readings listed by SEP-PP: Moore (1905, 1910); Russell (1912); Price (1932); Broad (1965); Ayer (1940); collection Swartz (1965). [source: SEP-PP §2]

### 1a. The argument from illusion, step by step (SEP-PP §2.1)
- (i) In an illusion it seems to S that something has a sensible quality F which the ordinary object supposedly perceived does not have. (ii) When it seems to S that something is F, there is something directly presented to S which is F. (iii) So in an illusion S is not directly presented with the ordinary object. (iv) The same account applies to veridical and illusory experiences. (v) So in veridical experience S is not directly presented with the ordinary object. (vi) If not in veridical experience, then never. Conclusion: we are never directly presented with ordinary objects. Example given: a white wall that looks yellow in peculiar lighting (Smith 2002: 25). [source: SEP-PP §2.1]
- Premise (ii) is "the most controversial premise"; it is Howard Robinson's **Phenomenal Principle**: "If there sensibly appears to a subject to be something which possesses a particular sensible quality then there is something of which the subject is aware which does possess that sensible quality" (Robinson 1994: 32). [source: SEP-PP §2.1]
- Support for (ii): Broad, explanatory: "If, in fact, nothing elliptical is before my mind, it is very hard to understand why the penny should seem elliptical rather than of any other shape" (Broad 1923: 240). Price takes it as obvious: "When I say 'this table appears brown to me' it is quite plain that I am acquainted with an actual instance of brownness" (1932: 63). [source: SEP-PP §2.1]
- Support for (iv): continuity — veridical and illusory experiences form a "continuous series" in which they "shade into one another", differences being of "degree and not of kind" (Ayer 1940: 8–9); also Price 1932: 32, Broad 1952: 9. [source: SEP-PP §2.1]
- Validity objection (French and Walters 2018): (i), (ii) and Leibniz's Law yield only that S is presented with an F thing distinct from the object, not that S is NOT presented with the object; one could be presented with both. Repair requires the **Exclusion Assumption** (cf. Snowdon 1992: 74), whose defensibility is open. [source: SEP-PP §2.1]

### 1b. The argument from hallucination (SEP-PP §2.2)
- The relevant hallucinations are "philosophers' hallucinations": merely possible experiences subjectively indistinguishable "from the inside" from a veridical perception, with no object; that they are possible "is widely accepted but not indisputable" (Austin 1962; Masrour 2020). [source: SEP-PP §2.2]
- Unlike the illusion argument, the base case does NOT need the Phenomenal Principle: (A) "simply falls out of what hallucinations are supposed to be." The spreading step is read as the Common Kind Claim. Hence "the very intelligibility of our ordinary conception of perceptual experience is threatened." Rejecting the Common Kind Claim is "central to the disjunctivist response". [source: SEP-PP §2.2]
- Score card (SEP-PP §§3.2.4, 3.3.4, 3.4.2): adverbialists and intentionalists reject the illusion argument (they deny the Phenomenal Principle) but must accept the hallucination argument (they accept (A) and the Common Kind Claim); naive realist disjunctivists accept (A) but reject the spreading step (B). [source: SEP-PP]
- Conclusion of SEP-PP: the live debate is now between intentionalists and naive-realist disjunctivists, "arguably 'the greatest chasm' in the philosophy of perception (Crane (2006))"; "The question, now, is not so much whether to be a direct realist, but how to be one." [source: SEP-PP §4]

### 1c. Other arguments for intermediaries
- **Argument from Perceptual Variation** (the earliest; Hatfield's name): Russell 1912: in looking at a table, what we see varies in shape as we move; the real table does not vary; hence what we see is not the real table. Hatfield calls Hume its first standard proponent: *Enquiry* XII.I.9: naive realism is "destroyed by the slightest philosophy, which teaches us, that nothing can ever be present to the mind but an image or perception"; "The table, which we see, seems to diminish, as we move farther from it: But the real table, which exists independent of us, suffers no alteration: It was, therefore, nothing but its image, which was present to the mind." [source: SEP-SD §§1, 2.1.1, 2.2.2]
- Reply to Hume's table argument: Reid (*Intellectual Powers*, 1785, Essay 2 ch. 14) — if "seems" refers to the table's appearance, there is no contradiction: appearances can vary while the thing does not. [source: SEP-SD §2.1.1]
- Hatfield's historical point: Russell, Moore, Broad and others did not treat the elliptical-looking penny as an *illusion*; their basic argument was from Perceptual Variation, not Illusion. The **bent stick partially submerged in water** is mentioned as a stock illusion, but "this was not the basic argument for sense data". Price's soccer-ball version made the Argument from Illusion central. [source: SEP-SD §2.2.3]
- Price's version (Price 1932: ch. 2, esp. 27–33), as Hatfield reconstructs it: viewing a distant soccer ball we are acquainted with a flat circle; the ball's surface is spherical; so we are acquainted with a sense datum; walking closer we have a continuous series with no phenomenal break to a veridical bulgy appearance; it is implausible that qualitatively similar perceptions have radically different objects; so the direct object is always a sense datum. Price extended this to hallucination (1932: 28–29). [source: SEP-SD §2.2.3]
- Stick in water as worked example: IEP-Obj sets out the argument with a pencil partly submerged in a glass of water: it appears bent; there is a bent shape in my visual field; the pencil is not bent; so the bent shape is not the pencil; it is not a physical object on the cornea; so it is mental, a sense datum; veridical cases are qualitatively identical, so sense data are present there too. IEP-Obj marks the "qualitatively identical... so there must be something in common" step as "a key assumption". [source: IEP-Obj §2a]
- **Time-lag argument**: light from stars may be thousands of years old and the star may no longer exist; if the object of veridical perception must exist simultaneously, we perceive something else; since all perception is a causal process taking time, this generalises (Robinson 1994: ch. 3). IEP-Obj gives it with the coffee cup ("perceiving the cup as it was a fraction of a millisecond ago") and calls it "not a persuasive line of argument": reject the assumption that the object must exist when we become aware of it; "all the events we perceive are to some extent in the past." [source: SEP-SD §4; IEP-Obj §2]
- Robinson extends the time-lag argument to a **Causal Argument** (1994: ch. 6) and argues that if sense-data arguments succeed they favour phenomenalistic idealism over physical realism (1994 chs 8–9; *Perception and Idealism* 2022). [source: SEP-SD §4]
- Martin's reply to the causal argument (2004: 56–57): on naive realism the objects of perception are "constituents of the experiential episode... its absence is sufficient for the non-occurrence of such an event"; the debate continues with the "reverse causal argument" and the "screening-off problem", answered by Martin's purely negative epistemic account of hallucination ("not introspectively knowably not a veridical perception"). Good for an advanced aside only. [source: SEP-PP §3.4.3]
- **Double vision** (pressing an eyeball): see NOT VERIFIED for the exact Hume wording; a scholarly article (WS) calls the *Treatise* 1.4.2 perceptual-relativity argument the "Double Vision Argument". [source: WS: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/365208]

## 2. Naive and direct realism

- IEP-Obj distinguishes **naive direct realism** (unperceived objects keep all the properties we perceive, including colour) from **scientific direct realism** (only primary qualities persist unperceived); both hold that we directly attend to perceiver-independent objects. [source: IEP-Obj §1]
- Naive realism in SEP-PP: veridical experiences are at least partly direct presentations of ordinary objects; things look white "because you are directly presented with some white snow"; the presentation relation is typically described as non-representational. Many naive realists extend this to illusion (e.g., Brewer 2011: white chalk under abnormal lighting has "visually relevant similarities" with paradigm red chalk, so it objectively looks red). [source: SEP-PP §§3.4.1–3.4.2]
- Moore's 1903 "Refutation of Idealism" introduced the act/object distinction and endorsed naive realism: we are directly aware of "the existence of a table in space" (1903: 453). Later Moore favoured either representative realism (sense data in "relation R" to objects) or the view that a sense datum is part of the object's surface (direct realism); in his final publication (1957) he endorsed the former. [source: SEP-SD §2.2.1]
- J.L. Austin's Oxford lectures (from 1947), published 1962 as *Sense and Sensibilia*, attacked the doctrine "that we never see or otherwise perceive (or 'sense'), or anyhow we never directly perceive or sense, material objects (or material things), but only sense-data" (1962: 2); he promised to show "that our ordinary words are much subtler in their uses, and mark many more distinctions, than philosophers have realized" (1962: 3); he objected to the continuity premise (1962: 52). Hatfield notes Austin does not engage Price's reasons for distinguishing senses of "see". [source: SEP-SD §3]

## 3. Sense-datum theory

- Term: "sense data" means "what is given to sense"; made prominent "during the early decades of the twentieth century by G. E. Moore and Bertrand Russell, followed by intense elaboration and modification of the concept by C. D. Broad, H. H. Price, and A. J. Ayer". Scattered earlier uses: Sidgwick 1883, James 1887. [source: SEP-SD intro, §1; notes 11]
- Moore introduced the TERM in a paper to the Aristotelian Society in **December 1909**, "The Subject-Matter of Psychology" (1909–10: 57): "By sense-data I understand a class of entities of which we are very often directly conscious... the colours... the sounds... the pain..." He had introduced the concept without the term in "The Nature and Reality of Objects of Perception" (1905–06). [source: SEP-SD §2.2.1; notes 13]
- Hatfield's eight-point "Classical Notion": directly aware of sense data; by acquaintance; they have the properties they appear to have; these are determinate; awareness involves no affirmation of an object beyond the datum; known with certainty (perhaps infallibly); private; distinct from the act of sensing. [source: SEP-SD §1]
- The early (classical) theorists mostly held sense data to be NON-mental (a *tertium quid*); later (second half of the 20th century) they were standardly treated as mind-dependent mental items. Present-day textbook contrast of naive realism vs sense-data theory reflects the later mental reading. [source: SEP-SD intro, §1; SEP-PP §3.1.2]
- **Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912), ch. 1 ("Appearance and Reality")**: opening sentence "Is there any knowledge in the world which is so certain that no reasonable man could doubt it?" (1912 [1997: 7]). The table's colour varies with viewpoint and light (brown, but white where light reflects from a window); its shape appears as a series of quadrilaterals, parallel sides converging. Russell then names "sense-data" the things "immediately known in sensation: such things as colours, sounds, smells, hardnesses, roughnesses, and so on" (1912 [1997: 12]). In 1912 he treated sense data as caused by and "representing" a real material table (representative realism); by 1914 (*Our Knowledge of the External World*; "The Relation of Sense-data to Physics") he treated material objects as logical constructions from sense data and "sensibilia". [source: SEP-SD §2.2.2; WS for the "Let us give the name of 'sense-data'..." wording]
- *The Problems of Philosophy* is a 1912 book Russell called his "shilling shocker", written for a general audience; long his most-read book. [source: WP-The_Problems_of_Philosophy]
- Broad, *Scientific Thought* (1923), used the term "sensa"; held sensa are not mental and that belief in physical objects is not reached by inference: we "pass automatically" from sensa to judgements (1923: 246–248). [source: SEP-SD §2.2.3; notes 1]
- Price, *Perception* (London: Methuen, 1932): the "bulgy patch of red" seen when viewing a tomato cannot be doubted even if one can doubt that there is a tomato (1932: 3). [source: SEP-SD §2.2.3; SEP-PP bibliography]
- Ayer, *The Foundations of Empirical Knowledge* (1940): whether sense data exist is a matter of linguistic convention; the argument from illusion supports choosing sense-datum language; he developed a phenomenalist language in which material-object statements are about actual and possible sense data (1940: ch. 5). (IEP-Obj cites a 1947 London printing; first publication 1940 per SEP-SD.) [source: SEP-SD §2.2.3]
- Objections: (a) the "veil of perception" — sense data interpose between perceivers and objects (reply: they are the medium, no more a veil than words are a "veil of words"); (b) the Phenomenal Principle is fallacious (Barnes 1944–5; Anscombe 1965) — Chisholm's "sense-datum fallacy" (1957: 151–152); (c) naturalism — mind-dependent sense data hard to fit into a physical world; (d) the "speckled hen" — a sense datum with indeterminately many speckles. [source: SEP-PP §3.1.5; SEP-SD §3]
- Sellars, "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind" (1956), attacked the "myth" of the given: "instead of coming to have a concept of something because we have noticed that sort of thing, to have the ability to notice a sort of thing is already to have the concept of that sort of thing" (§45). [source: SEP-SD §3]
- The sense-datum theory was "widely rejected in the second half of the 20th century" but had champions: Jackson (*Perception: A Representative Theory*, 1977; later abandoned per IEP-Obj), Robinson (*Perception*, 1994), Foster (2000). [source: SEP-PP §3.1.5; SEP-SD §4; IEP-Obj §2a]

## 4. Indirect (representative) realism and Locke (cross-reference *Locke*)

- A sense-datum theorist who holds that we perceive ordinary objects indirectly, by being directly presented with sense data, is "an indirect realist or representative realist". [source: SEP-PP §3.1.4]
- Locke's secondary qualities: "Such qualities which in truth are nothing in the objects themselves, but powers to produce various sensations in us by their primary qualities" (*Essay* 2.8.10, as quoted by IEP-Obj); Locke's primary qualities: shape, size, position, number, motion-or-rest, solidity (IEP-Obj's list). "The mind… perceives nothing but its own ideas" (*Essay* 4.4.3) is the basis of the "orthodox interpretation" of Locke as an indirect realist. [source: IEP-Obj §§1, 2a] — The published *Locke* chapter already gives the distinction and the Boyle source, and promises that the "veil of perception" (Bennett's phrase) is "taken up in *Perception and the External World*"; this chapter should honour that promise briefly and not repeat the distinction in detail.
- Democritus as precursor: "By convention sweet and by convention bitter, by convention hot, by convention cold, by convention colour; in reality atoms and void" (quoted by Sextus; Barnes 1987, pp. 252–3). Already quoted in the published *Presocratics* chapter. [source: IEP-Obj §1]
- Galileo, *The Assayer* (1623), tr. Stillman Drake (*Discoveries and Opinions of Galileo*, 1957, p. 275): "I think that tastes, odors, colors, and so on are no more than mere names so far as the object in which we locate them are concerned, and that they reside only in the consciousness. Hence if the living creature were removed, all these qualities would be wiped away and annihilated". [source: WP-Primary–secondary_quality_distinction (secondary; check page if quoted)]

## 5. Adverbialism

- Defended by C.J. Ducasse (1942, "Moore's Refutation of Idealism", in Schilpp ed., *The Philosophy of G.E. Moore*) and Roderick Chisholm (*Perceiving: A Philosophical Study*, 1957). The experience is modified in a way: not "visually sensing a white sphere" but "visually sensing whitely and spherely". [source: SEP-PP §3.2.1]
- IEP-Obj's analogies: a "beautiful free kick" is not an object one owns; one gives a "mean-eye" by looking meanly; so to perceive yellow is to sense "yellowly". [source: IEP-Obj §2b.ii]
- **Many-Property Problem** (Frank Jackson 1975, "On the Adverbial Analysis of Visual Experience", *Metaphilosophy* 6): "sensing brownly and squarely and greenly and triangularly" fits both a brown square + green triangle and a brown triangle + green square. Responses: Tye 1984, D'Ambrosio 2019. [source: SEP-PP §3.2.3]
- Butchvarov's objection (1980: 272): adverbialism is "incapable of doing justice to... its object-directness". [source: SEP-PP §3.2.3]

## 6. Intentionalism / representationalism

- Intentionalists hold we directly experience ordinary objects by directly *representing* them; also called "the representationalist theory of experience"; the common element between veridical experience and indistinguishable hallucination is intentional content. Sources listed: Anscombe 1965, Armstrong 1968, Pitcher 1970, Peacocke 1983, Harman 1990, Tye 1992/1995, Dretske 1995, Lycan 1996, Byrne 2001, Siegel 2010. [source: SEP-PP §§3.3.1–3.3.3]
- Belief theory (Armstrong 1968; Pitcher 1970: perception as acquisition of belief) is undermined by the **Müller-Lyer illusion**: you can see the lines as unequal while knowing they are equal; Armstrong retreated to "potential belief". [source: SEP-PP §3.3.2]
- **Harman, "The Intrinsic Quality of Experience" (1990, *Philosophical Perspectives* 4: 31–52)**: transparency argument: "Look at a tree and try to turn your attention to intrinsic features of your visual experience. I predict you will find that the only features there to turn your attention to will be features of the presented tree, including relational features of the tree 'from here'" (p. 39). Harman denies "mental paint"; Block (1996, 2004) defends it. [source: SEP-RepC §3.3; SEP-PP §3.2.2 & bibliography]
- Transparency definition: introspecting what it is like to have an experience reveals only its mind-independent objects, not features of the experience itself; objection: blurry vision when you take off your glasses seems a difference in *how* objects are experienced, not in the objects. [source: SEP-PP §1.2; SEP-RepC §3.3]
- Michael Tye: *Ten Problems of Consciousness* (1995), *Consciousness, Color and Content* (2000); requires representation to be "nonconceptual and 'poised'". Fred Dretske: *Naturalizing the Mind* (1995), appealing to sensory and "systemic" representation; Dretske (2003) endorses a radical transparency. [source: SEP-RepC §§1, 3.3; SEP-PP bibliography]
- Intentionalists reject the illusion argument (an illusion of a white wall as yellow is a white wall "directly represented as being yellow") but accept the hallucination argument; they recover direct realism via a causal theory: experience directly represents the churchyard and is non-deviantly caused by it (cf. Grice 1961). [source: SEP-PP §§3.3.4–3.3.5]
- Non-conceptual content idea "derives from Evans (1982)"; arguments: fine grain of experience (more shades than concepts), animal perception (IEP). Opponents: McDowell 1994, Brewer 1999. [source: SEP-PP §3.3.3; IEP-Obj §4a.i]
- Peacocke's objection (two trucks on the motorway, one near one at the horizon: represented as same size, but the nearer "seems bigger"): "concepts of sensation are indispensable to the description of the nature of any experience" (1983: 4). [source: IEP-Obj §4a.ii]

## 7. Disjunctivism

- First proposed by J.M. Hinton ("Visual Experiences", *Mind* 1967; *Experiences: An Inquiry into Some Ambiguities*, Oxford: Clarendon, 1973); developed by Paul Snowdon (1979–80/1980–81, 1990), John McDowell (1982, "Criteria, Defeasibility and Knowledge"; 1987) and M.G.F. Martin (2002, 2004, 2006). [source: SEP-PP §3.4.2; SEP-Dis §1; IEP-Dis references]
- Hinton rejects "the doctrine of the 'experience' as the common element in a given perception" and an indistinguishable hallucination (1973: 71); the most fundamental common description is disjunctive: "either a genuine perception of an F or a mere hallucination as of an F". [source: SEP-PP §3.4.2]
- Caution: Putnam's gloss that there is "nothing literally in common" (1999: 152) is NOT the best construal — both are subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical perception; what is denied is that the same fundamental kind of mental state makes this true. [source: SEP-PP §3.4.2]
- Martin's formulation of what is rejected, the "common kind assumption" (CKA): "whatever fundamental kind of mental event occurs when you veridically perceive, the very same kind of event could occur were you undergoing a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination." [source: SEP-PP §3.4.3]
- McDowell (1986, as quoted in IEP-Dis/IEP-Obj): "Of facts to the effect that things seem thus and so to one, we might say, some are cases of things being thus and so within the reach of one's subjective access to the external world, whereas others are mere appearances." McDowell is an epistemological disjunctivist; whether this commits him to metaphysical disjunctivism is debated (Snowdon 2005a; Byrne and Logue 2008). [source: IEP-Obj §5; SEP-Dis §2.4]
- Objection: two physically identical brains (one seeing a tin, one with the tin removed and the brain stimulated identically) would on disjunctivism be in different perceptual states, violating local supervenience; disjunctivists accept externalism instead. [source: IEP-Obj §5a]
- Disjunctivist readings have been proposed for earlier philosophers, e.g., Husserl (Overgaard 2013–2023; contested by Bower 2020, Salice 2024). [source: SEP-Dis §1]

## 8. Idealism and phenomenalism (cross-reference *Berkeley and Hume*, *Logical Positivism*)

- A theorist who denies we perceive mind-independent objects at all, only sense data construed as mental, is "a phenomenalist or an idealist" (Foster 2000). [source: SEP-PP §3.1.4]
- Berkeley (1710, *Principles* §6): "all the furniture of the earth... have not any subsistence without a mind... their being is to be perceived or known". God sustains unperceived objects. [source: IEP-Obj §3] — The published *Berkeley and Hume* chapter already gives *esse est percipi*, the Master Argument, the First Dialogue relativity arguments (hot to one hand, cold to the other; microscopes), and the "desk when I leave the room" answer; do NOT repeat.
- Phenomenalism: physical-object statements are statements about actual and possible experiences; "as Mill (1867) claims, material objects are nothing but 'permanent possibilities of sensation'" (*An Examination of Sir William Hamilton's Philosophy*; first edition 1865, IEP cites the 1867 edition). Example: to say the paper clip is in my drawer is to say I would see it on opening the drawer. [source: IEP-Obj §3; SEP-SD bibliography for 1865]
- Chisholm's (1948) objection from perceptual relativity: no phenomenalist translation succeeds, because what appears depends on observation conditions (gloves, colour blindness, lights out) that can only be specified in physical terms: "it is the thing perceived and the observation conditions working jointly which determine what is to appear" (Chisholm 1948, p. 513). Also the charge of solipsism. [source: IEP-Obj §3a]
- Ayer's phenomenalist language (1940: ch. 5) — see §3. [source: SEP-SD §2.2.3]

## 9. Molyneux's question (worked example)

- William Molyneux (1656–1698), Irish scientist and politician, author of *Dioptrica Nova* (1692); his wife lost her sight in the first year of their marriage (Simms 1982). [source: SEP-Mol §1]
- He first sent the problem in a letter dated **Dublin, 7 July 1688** (Letter 1064 in De Beer's *Correspondence*), after reading a French extract of Locke's *Essay* in the *Bibliothèque Universelle & Historique* (1688). Locke did not reply. Text: "A Man, being born blind, and having a Globe and a Cube, nigh of the same bignes, Committed into his Hands... Whether he Could, by his Sight, and before he touch them, know which is the Globe and which the Cube?" — it also asked whether he could tell by sight whether he could reach them "tho they were Removed 20 or 1000 feet from Him". [source: SEP-Mol §1; IEP-Mol references]
- A simplified version came in a letter of **2 March 1693**; Locke: "Your ingenious problem will deserve to be published to the world." Printed in the **second edition (1694)** of the *Essay*, II.ix.8, "Sensations often changed by the judgment": "Suppose a Man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a Cube, and a Sphere of the same metal... Quaere, Whether by his sight, before he touch'd them, he could now distinguish, and tell, which is the Globe, which the Cube." [source: SEP-Mol §1]
- Molyneux's answer, quoted by Locke: "Not. For though he has obtain'd the experience of, how a Globe, how a Cube affects his touch; yet he has not yet attained the Experience, that what affects his touch so or so, must affect his sight so or so". Locke agreed: the blind man "at first sight, would not be able with certainty to say, which was the Globe, which the Cube, whilst he only saw them" (II.ix.8). [source: SEP-Mol §1]
- Cassirer (1955) called it "the fundamental question of the 18th Century for epistemology and psychology". Berkeley (1709) gave a more radical "no" (ideas of sight and touch bear no similarity); Synge (1693), Leibniz (1704), Hutcheson (1727) answered "yes"; Leibniz insisted time be allowed for sight to recover. [source: SEP-Mol §§1–2]
- Step-by-step structure for the chapter (SEP-Mol, IEP-Mol): Premise: the subject can discriminate the shapes by touch. Question: does the tactile idea of "cube" carry over to sight without learning? "No" (Molyneux, Locke, Berkeley): ideas are individuated by sense, the link is learned from experience. "Yes" (Leibniz etc.): shape is common to the senses and can be reasoned out or is innately linked. Objection to any experimental test: the newly sighted have degraded vision and need time, and giving time lets learning (including from touch) contaminate the test. [source: SEP-Mol §§2, 4; IEP-Mol]
- **Cheselden (1728)**: the English surgeon William Cheselden (1688–1752) published the first experimental account of a congenitally blind person given sight by cataract surgery. Patient: a boy of 13 (WP; named in 2021 as Daniel Dolins); partially blind (could tell night from day, IEP). "When he first saw, he was so far from making any judgment of distances, that he thought all objects whatever touched his eyes (as he expressed it) as what he felt did his skin." Berkeley (1733) took it as confirming his "no"; Voltaire (1738), Diderot (1749), Condillac, Reid (1764) and others thought it ambiguous. [source: SEP-Mol §2; IEP-Mol; WP-William_Cheselden]
- **Held et al. (2011)**, "The newly sighted fail to match seen with felt", *Nature Neuroscience* 14: 551–553 (Richard Held, Yuri Ostrovsky, Beatrice de Gelder, Tapan Gandhi, Suma Ganesh, Umang Mathur, Pawan Sinha), through Project Prakash (India, set up 2003): five congenitally blind subjects aged 8–17, treated 2007–2010; tested within 48 hours; they passed touch-to-touch and vision-to-vision matching but failed touch-to-vision ("barely better than if the subjects had guessed"). Three of the five, retested later (5 days, 7 days, 5 months), improved markedly (WP: 80–90%). Reading: "initially no but subsequently yes"; neural cross-modal substrates "might already be in place" (Held 2011: 552). [source: SEP-Mol §4; IEP-Mol; WP-Molyneux's_problem]
- Caveats: robustness of newly sighted vision questioned (Spence et al. 2024; Van Cleve 2014; Schwenkler 2013); newborn infants (Meltzoff et al. 1979; Streri 2012) and dark-reared chicks (Versace et al. 2024) suggest "yes" to an "action" version. SEP-Mol: "there is no problem in the history of the philosophy of perception that has provoked more reflection, refinement, and experimentation." [source: SEP-Mol §§4–5]

## 10. Indian debates (cross-reference *The Classical Indian Schools*, *Mahāyāna Philosophy*)

- *Nyāya-sūtra* attributed to Gautama, c. 200 CE (IEP); published *Classical Indian Schools* says "usually dated to around the second century CE" — consistent. Commentators: Vātsyāyana (c. 450), Uddyotakara (c. 600), Vācaspati Miśra (c. 900); Gaṅgeśa (c. 1325) founds Navya-Nyāya. [source: IEP-Nya §1]
- **Nyāya-sūtra 1.1.4**: "A perceptual cognition arises by means of the connection between sense faculty and object, is not dependent on words, is non-deviating, and is determinate" (IEP's translation). IEP: the sense–object connection condition "evinces Nyāya's direct realism"; "Perception" has "success grammar" (an illusion like shell taken for silver is a pseudo-pramāṇa). Uddyotakara lists six kinds of sense–object connection (sannikarṣa). [source: IEP-Nya §2a]
- The "not dependent on words" clause does NOT endorse Dignāga's view that genuine perception is non-conceptual (*kalpanā-apoḍha*); later Naiyāyikas, notably Vācaspati Miśra, read "not dependent on words" and "determinate" disjunctively to yield non-propositional and propositional perception. [source: IEP-Nya §2a]
- **Nirvikalpaka / savikalpaka**: since Vācaspati Miśra, Naiyāyikas hold two stages: *nirvikalpaka-pratyakṣa* (non-qualificative, non-conceptual, indeterminate; sense–object contact, *indriya sannikarṣa*) and *savikalpaka-pratyakṣa* (qualificative, conceptual, a fact with truth-evaluable content). Kumārila (Mīmāṃsā) treats them as stages, citing the infant; Bhartṛhari held all cognition is "shot through with words". [source: SEP-EI]
- Gaṅgeśa's argument for indeterminate perception: if every grasp of a qualifier needed a prior grasp of another, there would be an infinite regress, so concept-free perception must be posited at the start (Phillips 2001). Arindam Chakrabarti (2000) proposes dropping indeterminate perception to keep Nyāya direct realism. [source: SEP-EI]
- Nyāya on illusion: rope seen as snake; shell (mother-of-pearl) as silver; illusion is a "misplacement" of a qualifier from memory; "the concept of illusion is parasitic on that of veridical experience (not all coins can be counterfeit)" (Nyāya-sūtra 4.2.26–36); SEP-EI: "Ontologically, Nyāya takes a disjunctivist position: an illusion is a different kind of critter than a genuine perception." Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā holds a "no-illusion"/"omission" theory. [source: SEP-EI]
- **Dignāga (c. 480–c. 540) and Dharmakīrti (c. 600–660, Frauwallner's dating; SEP-EI "early seventh century")**: two pramāṇas, perception and inference, matching two objects, real particulars (*svalakṣaṇa*) and fictional universals; "Perception is always purely non-conceptual and non-linguistic"; conceptual thought is "distorted" (*bhrānta*) because it superimposes universals. Dignāga's *Pramāṇasamuccaya*. [source: SEP-Dh §§1–2]
- Dignāga and Dharmakīrti restrict perception to cognition free from conceptual determination (*kalpanāpoḍha*); Dharmakīrti adds "non-erroneous" (*abhrānta*) (*Pramāṇaviniścaya* I). [source: SEP-MIB]
- Consequence noted by Tillemans: for Dignāga "one and the same object could never be both perceived and thought about"; Naiyāyikas and Mīmāṃsakas did not have this problem. [source: SEP-Dh §2]
- Both were in their final positions Yogācāra idealists; Dharmakīrti argues (*Pramāṇavārttika* III, after v. 194) that the Sautrāntika's own epistemology makes external objects explanatorily redundant (a "redundancy argument", Tillemans; Dan Arnold 2008 on epistemic idealism). [source: SEP-Dh §1]
- **Vasubandhu (4th–5th c. CE), *Twenty Verses* (*Viṃśikā*)**: "Everything in the three realms is only appearance (vijñaptimātra)", like hairs seen by someone with eye disease (Gold's translation). Four objections (restriction in place, in time, intersubjective agreement, causal efficacy) answered by dreams (place and time), hell-guardians seen by all hell-beings (shared experience), wet dream (causal efficacy); then the atom argument. [source: SEP-Vas §4] — The published *Mahāyāna Philosophy* chapter already covers this and the Nyāya reply ("barren woman's son"; Uddyotakara), and says "The debate continues in Perception and the External World"; cross-reference, don't repeat.
- Hungry ghosts (*pretas*) seeing a river as filled with pus (and blood) where humans see water: an example used in Yogācāra discussions of intersubjectivity (Vasubandhu, Asaṅga, Sthiramati per the 2017 article "Imagine Being a Preta: Early Indian Yogācāra Approaches to Intersubjectivity"; author not recorded). [source: WS: https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/imagine-being-a-preta-early-indian-yog%C4%81c%C4%81ra-approaches-to-intersu/ ; https://www.lionsroar.com/can-we-know-others-worlds/] — verse number not verified, see NOT VERIFIED.
- B.K. Matilal, *Perception: An Essay on Classical Indian Theories of Knowledge* (Oxford: Clarendon, 1986). The published *Modern Chinese and Indian Philosophy* chapter says it "defends a realism close to Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika... against Buddhist phenomenalism and idealism, while engaging modern sense-data theorists" — consistent. [source: SEP-PP bibliography]

## 11. Optics: extramission, intromission and Ibn al-Haytham

- Ancient theories: extramission/emission (rays from the eye) held by Euclid (*Optics*, c. 300 BCE) and Ptolemy (*Optics*, 2nd c. CE), and Plato (WP); Empedocles posited emanations both ways; intromission (something representative of the object enters the eye) with Aristotle (*De Sensu*) "its main propagator". [source: WP-Visual_perception; WP-Emission_theory_(vision)]
- Epicurean intromission: "sensations occur as a result of thin films emitted by objects that enter the appropriate sense organ" (eidola/simulacra). [source: SEP-Epi]
- **Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen)**, b. c. 965 Basra, d. c. 1040 Cairo (WP); SEP-SkM gives 965–1039. *Kitāb al-Manāẓir* (*Book of Optics*), seven books, traditionally dated **1011–1021** (WP); SEP-SD cites the Optics as "ca. 1030" (date given for Sabra's translation entry). Latin *De aspectibus* / *Perspectiva* (late 12th or early 13th c.); printed by Friedrich Risner 1572. [source: WP-Ibn_al-Haytham; WP-Book_of_Optics; SEP-SkM §3.1.1; SEP-SD]
- He argued against extramission (e.g., looking at the sun damages the eye; implausible that the eye fills all space the instant the eyelids open to the night sky) and held that from each point of an illuminated coloured body light and colour issue along every straight line (following al-Kindī, breaking with Aristotle); he combined Euclid's/Ptolemy's ray geometry, Galen's eye anatomy and Aristotelian intromission. [source: WP-Book_of_Optics; WP-Ibn_al-Haytham]
- Philosophically central: *Optics* II.3 — many perceptions are inferential, through sudden "imperceptible" inferences so rapid they seem immediate; even "the whole is greater than its part" is inferential; *Optics* III.7 catalogues illusions (e.g., the moon looking larger near the horizon). Not a sceptic, but influential on later medieval scepticism and on Roger Bacon. [source: SEP-SkM §3.1.1]
- He distinguished "pure sensation" (light, colour, visual direction) from what depends on recognition, judgement and reason (*Optics* II.3.25; tr. A.I. Sabra, Warburg Institute, 1989); the optical tradition framed Descartes's and Berkeley's theories of vision (Hatfield & Epstein 1979). [source: SEP-SD §2.1.1]
- The house-arrest/feigned-madness story under Caliph al-Ḥākim (d. 1021) is LEGEND (WP: "Legend has it..."). [source: WP-Ibn_al-Haytham]
- Published *Falsafa* chapter does not discuss Ibn al-Haytham (only Avicenna's internal senses), so no clash.

## 12. The science of perception (keep hedged)

- **Helmholtz** (1821–1894): "unconscious inference" (*unbewusster Schluss*), term WP says was coined in 1867 in vol. 3 of *Handbuch der physiologischen Optik* (1856–1867; English *Treatise on Physiological Optics*, Optical Society of America, 1924–25). WP credits Ibn al-Haytham (ca. 1030) with an early form (colour constancy). [source: WP-Unconscious_inference; WP-Hermann_von_Helmholtz; WP-Ibn_al-Haytham]
- **Gestalt psychology**: founded by Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka, Wolfgang Köhler; Wertheimer's 1912 publication on the phi phenomenon (apparent motion) "marks the beginning"; von Ehrenfels introduced "Gestalt" in 1890. Gestaltists (Koffka 1935; Köhler 1929) held the world is given in three dimensions, not built from 2-D sensations; size/shape constancy. [source: WP-Gestalt_psychology; SEP-SD §3]
- **J.J. Gibson** (1904–1979): agreed with the Gestaltists that 2-D sense-datum-like experience requires a special act of attention (1950); a plate at a slant "looks circular, not elliptical"; *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception* (1979) defines: "The affordances of the environment are what it offers the animal, what it provides or furnishes, either for good or ill." Term coined in *The Senses Considered as Perceptual Systems* (1966). [source: SEP-SD §3; WP-Affordance; WP-James_J._Gibson]
- **Predictive processing**: the brain as a hierarchical engine of prediction-error minimisation (Clark 2013, "Whatever next?", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 36: 181–204; Hohwy, *The Predictive Mind*, OUP 2013). Clark: "[t]o perceive the world just is to use what you know to explain away the sensory signal" (2013: 190); perception and cognition "profoundly unified and, in important respects, continuous" (2013: 187). Hohwy (2013: 122): "no theoretical or anatomical border preventing top-down projections". Computational precursor: Rao and Ballard (1999). Clark's book: *Surfing Uncertainty: Prediction, Action, and the Embodied Mind* (2016). Hedge: whether PP entails cognitive penetration is disputed (Macpherson 2015; Hohwy 2013; Drayson 2017). [source: SEP-Mod §4.2; WP-Predictive_coding; WP-Andy_Clark_(philosopher)]
- **Cognitive penetration**: notion introduced by Pylyshyn (1984); "A perceptual system is cognitively penetrable if and only if its operations are directly causally sensitive, in a semantically coherent way, to the agent's beliefs, desires, intentions, or other nonperceptual states." Evidence claimed since New Look (Bruner and Goodman 1947): desirable objects look closer (Balcetis & Dunning 2010), etc. Skeptics (Firestone & Scholl 2016, "Cognition does not affect perception", *BBS* 39) say effects are on judgement, memory or attention (e.g., heavy-ball distance effect disappears when subjects judge appearance only, Woods et al. 2009). Fodor (*The Modularity of Mind*, 1983): the persistence of the Müller-Lyer illusion after measuring shows perception is informationally encapsulated. [source: SEP-Mod §§1, 2.1]

## 13. Illusions: Müller-Lyer and the carpentered world

- Devised by Franz Carl Müller-Lyer (1857–1916) in **1889**: shafts with outward fins look longer than shafts with inward arrowheads. [source: WP-Müller-Lyer_illusion]
- Segall, Campbell and Herskovits, *The Influence of Culture on Visual Perception* (Indianapolis/New York: Bobbs-Merrill, 1966): tested more than 1,300 people in 17 groups; Europeans/Americans most susceptible; Kalahari San foragers least ("nearly immune" per SEP-Mod); pre-adolescent children more susceptible than adults within cultures; "carpentered world" hypothesis: environments of straight lines and right angles. Earlier report 1963. Rivers (c. 1900) had found Murray Islanders less susceptible. [source: SEP-Mod §2.1; WP-Müller-Lyer_illusion; WS]
- Critics: Jahoda found no difference between rural and urban members of the same African group and proposed retinal pigmentation (supported by Pollack 1970); Ahluwalia (1978) found urban Zambians more susceptible; WP also asserts susceptibility is universal and earlier cultural studies "suffered from poor methodology" (claim contested). McCauley and Henrich (2006, *Philosophical Psychology* 19: 79–101) read the data as showing "diachronic" penetrability during a critical developmental period, not synchronic penetration. Pigeons and a grey parrot perceive the illusion. [source: WP-Müller-Lyer_illusion; SEP-Mod §2.1]

## 14. Colour

- Physicists who held colours are not in objects: Galileo, Boyle, Descartes, Newton, Thomas Young, Maxwell, Helmholtz. Hume: "Sounds, colors, heat and cold, according to modern philosophy are not qualities in objects, but perceptions in the mind." Maxwell (1871): "It seems almost a truism to say that color is a sensation". Palmer (1999: 95): "Neither objects nor lights are actually 'colored' in anything like the way we experience them." [source: SEP-Col §1.1]
- Taxonomy: primitivism, reductive physicalism (Byrne & Hilbert), eliminativism/irrealism (Hardin, *Color for Philosophers*, 1988), subjectivism, dispositionalism (Locke usually so read), relationalism (Cohen 2009), action-based. [source: SEP-Col contents & §§1.3, 2]
- **The dress (2015)**: photograph taken February 2015 by Cecilia Bleasdale of a dress at Cheshire Oaks before her daughter's wedding on Colonsay; posted on Tumblr by Caitlin McNeill on 26 February 2015; went viral via BuzzFeed. The dress was royal blue with black lace (Roman Originals); many saw white and gold. Explanation (no consensus): differing assumptions about illumination / colour constancy (Bevil Conway, Jay Neitz, Anya Hurlbert, Pascal Wallisch). First large study (*Current Biology*, 1,400 respondents): 57% blue/black, 30% white/gold, 11% blue/brown, 2% other. *Journal of Vision* special issue "A Dress Rehearsal for Vision Science". [source: WP-The_dress]

## 15. Perceptual knowledge vs perceptual experience

- Central epistemological problem: how perception could give knowledge or justified belief about an external world. Lyons's reconstruction "PEW": (1) Indirectness Principle: nothing is directly present to the mind except perceptual appearances; (2) Metaevidential Principle: without good reason to think appearances veridical, perceptual beliefs are unjustified; (3) No-Good-Reason Claim; (4) so perceptual beliefs unjustified. (2) and (3) do the work; (1) motivates (2). [source: SEP-EP §1] — The *Scepticism* chapter is the place for scepticism proper; use this only to link.
- Two families of response: metaphysical (about what perception is: direct realism, idealism, etc.) and epistemological (about what justification requires). [source: SEP-EP intro]
- Modest foundationalism: Pryor's "dogmatism" ("The Skeptic and the Dogmatist", *Noûs* 34, 2000), Huemer's "phenomenal conservatism" (*Skepticism and the Veil of Perception*, 2001; 2007), Pollock's "direct realism": if it perceptually seems to S that p, S is prima facie justified in believing p, defeasibly. [source: SEP-EP §3.1]
- Russell 1912 distinguished knowledge by acquaintance (with sense data; asserts no truths) from knowledge by description; "When we see a white patch, we are acquainted, in the first instance, with the particular patch..." (1912 [1997: 101]). [source: SEP-SD §2.2.2]
- Seeing without noticing: **inattentional blindness** — term from Arien Mack and Irvin Rock (1992; book *Inattentional Blindness*, MIT Press, 1998). Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris, "Gorillas in Our Midst: Sustained Inattentional Blindness for Dynamic Events", *Perception* 28(9): 1059–1074 (1999): subjects counting passes between basketball players; in most groups about 50% failed to report a person in a gorilla suit (or with an umbrella); based on Neisser and Becklen (1975). Ig Nobel Prize 2004. Book *The Invisible Gorilla* (2010). [source: WP-Inattentional_blindness; WP-Daniel_Simons; WP-The_Invisible_Gorilla]
- Memmert's eye-tracking replication with children: non-noticers fixated the gorilla for about a second on average (25 frames) yet did not see it. [source: WP-Inattentional_blindness]

## Debates
- **Does the argument from illusion even get going?** Sense-datum theorists accept the Phenomenal Principle (Broad, Price, Robinson); adverbialists, intentionalists and naive realists reject it (Barnes, Anscombe; Chisholm's "sense-datum fallacy"); defenders reply that it rests on phenomenology, not on the grammar of "appears" (Smith 2002). French and Walters (2018) argue the standard argument is invalid without an "Exclusion Assumption". [source: SEP-PP §§2.1, 3.1.5; SEP-SD §3]
- **The spreading step / Common Kind Claim**: disputed since Austin (continuity premise, 1962: 52); disjunctivists (Hinton, Snowdon, McDowell, Martin) reject it; Robinson's causal argument and Martin's screening-off dialectic. [source: SEP-PP §3.4; SEP-SD §3]
- **Intentionalism vs naive-realist disjunctivism**: "arguably 'the greatest chasm'" (Crane 2006); intentionalists split over qualia (Block, Shoemaker, Peacocke vs Harman, Tye); transparency contested (blurry vision; Block's "mental paint"). [source: SEP-PP §§1.2, 3.3.1, 4; SEP-RepC §3.3]
- **Is Locke an indirect realist?** "Orthodox interpretation" yes (IEP-Obj); published *Locke* chapter notes a 2004 PPQ symposium mostly read him as a representationalist but not a sceptic, and Berkeley's report is often mistaken for Locke's view. Robinson (1994) traces sense-data to Locke, Berkeley and Hume; Hatfield disagrees about Locke and Berkeley. [source: IEP-Obj §2a; SEP-SD §4]
- **Was Yogācāra idealism?** Contested; SEP-Vas: "it seems safe to say that the Twenty Verses defends at least some form of idealism"; for Dharmakīrti, epistemic vs metaphysical idealism (Arnold 2008; Tillemans' redundancy-argument reading). [source: SEP-Vas §4.1; SEP-Dh §1]
- **Concept-free perception**: Dignāga/Dharmakīrti (all genuine perception non-conceptual) vs Nyāya/Mīmāṃsā (foundational perception is concept-laden, with a concept-free first stage) vs Bhartṛhari (all cognition linguistic); modern parallel: the conceptual/non-conceptual content debate (McDowell vs Peacocke, Evans); Chakrabarti (2000) would drop nirvikalpaka. [source: SEP-EI; SEP-PP §3.3.3; IEP-Obj §4a]
- **Molyneux's answer**: Held et al. (2011) as "no", or "initially no but subsequently yes" (IEP); whether cataract patients are fair test subjects (Spence et al. 2024; Van Cleve 2014; Schwenkler 2013); whether Locke's wider views fit a "yes" (Mackie 1976; Bruno and Mandelbaum 2010; Glenney 2024). [source: SEP-Mol §4; IEP-Mol]
- **Carpentered world**: Segall, Campbell and Herskovits (1966) vs Jahoda (pigmentation), and later claims that susceptibility is universal; McCauley and Henrich (2006) on diachronic penetrability. [source: WP-Müller-Lyer_illusion; SEP-Mod §2.1]
- **Cognitive penetration**: New Look and recent top-down studies vs Firestone and Scholl (2016); whether predictive processing implies penetration (Clark 2013; Vetter & Newen 2014; Lupyan 2015) or not (Macpherson 2015; Hohwy 2013; Drayson 2017). [source: SEP-Mod §§2.1, 4.2]
- **Colour realism vs eliminativism**: the scientific tradition (Galileo to Maxwell; Palmer, Hardin) vs primitivists, physicalists (Byrne & Hilbert), dispositionalists, relationalists (Cohen). [source: SEP-Col]
- **The dress**: no consensus on why perceptions diverge (illumination priors / colour constancy; Wallisch notes unusually stable, non-switching perception). [source: WP-The_dress]
- **Ancient optics**: Galen is listed among extramissionists by WP-Emission_theory but described as part of the intromissionist medical tradition by WP-Book_of_Optics ("followers of Aristotle and Galen"); do not class Galen either way without a better source.
