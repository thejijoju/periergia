## The problem stated; kinds of evil
- The problem, in SEP-CE's words: "the problem of accounting for evil in a world created by an all-powerful, all-knowing, all-good God. It seems that if the creator has these attributes, there would be no evil in the world. But there is evil in the world." [source: SEP-CE intro]
- Broad vs narrow concept of evil: the broad concept "picks out any bad state of affairs, wrongful action, or character flaw" (a toothache, a harmless lie) and is the sense used in the problem of evil; the narrow concept picks out "only the most morally despicable" actions and characters. [source: SEP-CE intro]
- **Natural vs moral evil**: natural evils "do not result from the intentions or negligence of moral agents" (hurricanes, toothaches); moral evils do (murder, lying). [source: SEP-CE] IEP-Log's definitions: moral evil = "evil or suffering that results from the immoral choices of free creatures"; natural evil = "evil or suffering that results from the operations of nature or nature gone awry". [source: IEP-Log §6]
- Leibniz's threefold division (metaphysical, physical, moral) is in *Leibniz* — cross-ref, don't repeat. [cf. Leibniz chapter]
- Three standard senses of the problem: **logical** (God and evil are inconsistent), **evidential/probabilistic** (evil, consistent with God, lowers the probability of theism), and the **experiential/existential** problem (difficulty of believing in a loving God when confronted by suffering). [source: WP-Problem_of_evil "Formulation"; IEP-Evi]
- SEP-Ev further divides formulations into incompatibility vs inductive, abstract (the mere existence of some evil) vs concrete (specific evils: animals dying in forest fires, children dying of cancer), and axiological vs deontological. [source: SEP-Ev §1]

## The "Epicurean" trilemma: attribution (recap of *Hellenistic Philosophy*)
- Reported only by the Christian writer **Lactantius** (c. 250 – c. 325), *De ira Dei* (On the Wrath of God) **13.20–21**, a work aimed against Stoics and Epicureans; Lactantius attributes it to Epicurus but gives no source. [source: WP-Epicurean_paradox; WP-Lactantius]
- WP's English of Lactantius: "God," says Epicurus, "either wants to remove evils, but cannot; or he can, but does not want to; or he does not want to and cannot; or he wants to and can. If he wants to, but cannot, he is impotent… If he can, but does not want to, he is envious… If he wants to and can, which alone is proper to God, then where do evils come from? Or why does he not remove them?" (WP renders from an Italian edition; translator not named — paraphrase, don't quote as authoritative.) [source: WP-Epicurean_paradox]
- No text of Epicurus contains it; Reinhold Glei (*Vigiliae Christianae* 42, 1988) argues it comes from a non- or anti-Epicurean (Academic) source; WP's image caption suggests Carneades. The oldest preserved version of such a trilemma (unattributed) is in **Sextus Empiricus**, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* III.175. Epicurus believed in gods but denied they care for human affairs. [source: WP-Epicurean_paradox] Consistent with *Hellenistic Philosophy*.
- Transmission: Lactantius → Pierre Bayle's *Dictionnaire historique et critique* → Hume, *Dialogues concerning Natural Religion* (posthumous, **1779**), Part X, spoken by **Philo**. [source: WP-Epicurean_paradox; SEP-HR §]
- Hume's wording (Part X; SEP-HR cites D 10.25/198): "Epicurus's old questions are yet unanswered. Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?" [source: WP-Problem_of_evil (citing Gutenberg text); SEP-HR ("Epicurus's old questions" … "unanswered", D 10.25/198); WS-11 — one online source prints "still unanswered"; "yet" is the majority reading]
- In Part XI Philo lists four hypotheses about the first causes (perfect goodness, perfect malice, both, neither) and concludes "Mixed phenomena can never prove the two former unmixed principles… The fourth, therefore, seems by far the most probable" — the ancestor of Draper's argument. [source: SEP-Ev §3.3 quoting Dialogues 1779 Part XI, 212; SEP-HR]

## Scripture and early responses (brief)
- **Job**: composed in the Persian period (540–330 BCE) on linguistic grounds; prose prologue and epilogue frame poetic dialogues: three cycles of debate with friends (Eliphaz, Bildad, Zophar), the Poem to Wisdom (ch. 28), Elihu's speeches, and God's two speeches from the whirlwind. In the prologue God allows "the adversary" (*ha-sāṭān*) to test Job. Maimonides read the story as a parable; Aquinas as true history. [source: WP-Book_of_Job]
- Zachary Braiterman coined "anti-theodicy" in *(God) After Auschwitz* (1998); anti-theodicy has been likened to Job's protests. [source: WP-Theodicy]
- **Augustine** (cross-ref; do not repeat): evil as privation of good (from Plotinus); evil will has a "deficient" cause; free will an "intermediate" good. *De natura boni* (399) is "a concise anti-Manichean argument for the doctrine that evil is a privation of goodness rather than an independent substance". Hick named this family the "Augustinian theodicy" (1966). [source: SEP-Aug; Augustine chapter]
- **Boethius**, *Consolation of Philosophy* IV.1–4: drawing on Plato's *Gorgias*, Philosophy argues that the wicked do not really prosper and are in fact powerless (everyone wants happiness, which is the good; the wicked lack it); in IV.5 on, apparently unjust fortunes "always serve a good, though to us hidden, purpose: for instance, exercising good people to increase their virtue, helping the wicked to repent". [source: SEP-Boe] (Brief — the Augustine chapter ends with Boethius.)
- **Plotinus/Proclus** privation debate is in *Plotinus and Neoplatonism*. [cf.]

## The logical problem: Mackie, "Evil and Omnipotence" (1955)
- J. L. Mackie (1917–1981), Australian; "Evil and Omnipotence", *Mind* n.s. 64 (254), April 1955, 200–212. [source: SEP-Ev bibliography; WP-J._L._Mackie]
- Mackie's charge: "Here it can be shown, not that religious beliefs lack rational support, but that they are positively irrational, that several parts of the essential theological doctrine are inconsistent with one another." (Mackie 1955, 200) [source: IEP-Log §8]
- His "additional premises" (p. 201): "good is opposed to evil, in such a way that a good thing always eliminates evil as far as it can, and that there are no limits to what an omnipotent thing can do". [source: WS-3]
- **Step by step (IEP-Log's reconstruction):** (1) God is omnipotent. (2) God is omniscient. (3) God is perfectly good. (4) Evil exists. (6) If omnipotent, God could prevent all evil. (7) If omniscient, God would know of all evil and how to prevent it. (8) If perfectly good, God would want to prevent all evil. Hence (12) if evil exists, God is not omnipotent, or not omniscient, or not perfectly good; so (13) contradicts the conjunction of (1)–(3). [source: IEP-Log §1]
- **Objection (morally sufficient reason):** premise (8) is false if God could have a "morally sufficient reason" for allowing evil — like Mrs Jones, who lets her infant suffer a polio injection for a greater good. [source: IEP-Log §3]
- Mackie anticipated the free-will reply (1955, 209): "why could he not have made men such that they always freely choose the good? … there was open to him the obviously better possibility of making beings who would act freely but always go right." Antony Flew (1955) said the same. [source: IEP-Log §4]
- Mackie also discussed the "paradox of omnipotence" (can an omnipotent being make things it cannot control?) — NOT checked in a source this session; omit or verify.

## Plantinga's Free Will Defence (God, Freedom, and Evil)
- Alvin Plantinga (b. 15 Nov 1932). Versions in *God and Other Minds* (1967), *The Nature of Necessity* (Oxford, 1974) and *God, Freedom, and Evil* (1974; IEP and WP-FWD cite the Eerdmans edition of **1977**; WP-Alvin_Plantinga dates the book 1974). Say "1974". [source: WP-Alvin_Plantinga; IEP-Evi bibliography; SEP-Ev §1.3]
- **Defence vs theodicy**: a defence only shows that God and evil are logically consistent, by telling a possible story; it need not be true or even plausible. A theodicy tries to give a plausible explanation of why God permits evil (IEP-Evi: a defence answers the logical problem, a theodicy the evidential). Plantinga and R. M. Adams used "theodicy" in the strong sense of giving God's actual reasons; Swinburne and Hasker call that an unnecessarily ambitious programme. Peter van Inwagen (*The Problem of Evil*, 2006) defines a defence as a story containing God and all actual evils, "put forward not as true but as 'true for all anyone knows'". [source: IEP-Evi §4b; SEP-Ev §4; WP-Alvin_Plantinga's_free-will_defense]
- Plantinga's summary (1974, 166–7, quoted by IEP-Log): "A world containing creatures who are sometimes significantly free (and freely perform more good than evil actions) is more valuable, all else being equal, than a world containing no free creatures at all. Now God can create free creatures, but he cannot cause or determine them to do only what is right… he could have forestalled the occurrence of moral evil only by excising the possibility of moral good." (WP-FWD's version of the same passage, from GFE 1977 p. 30, reads "He can't… removing the possibility of moral good" — two printings, slightly different wording.) [source: IEP-Log §4; WP-FWD]
- "The essential point of the Free Will Defense is that the creation of a world containing moral good is a cooperative venture; it requires the uncoerced concurrence of significantly free creatures." (Plantinga 1974, 190) [source: IEP-Log §4]
- **Omnipotence** = power to do anything logically possible; God cannot make a round square or "make significantly free creatures and… causally determine that they will always choose what is right". [source: IEP-Log §5]
- **Strong vs weak actualisation; "Leibniz's Lapse"**: Plantinga calls the assumption that an omnipotent God can actualise any possible world "Leibniz's lapse". God *strongly* actualises what he causes; he can only *weakly* actualise a world whose contents depend on creatures' free choices. [source: WP-FWD]
- **Transworld depravity** (GFE 1977 p. 48, as given by WP-FWD): a person suffers from it iff for every world W in which that person is significantly free and does only what is right, there is an action A and a maximal world segment S′ such that S′ includes A's being morally significant for the person and the person's being free with respect to A, S′ is included in W but includes neither performing nor refraining from A, and "if S′ were actual, P would go wrong with respect to A". Plantinga: "if a person suffers from it, then it wasn't within God's power to actualize any world in which that person is significantly free but does no wrong". It is *possible* that every creaturely essence suffers from it (GFE p. 53). [source: WP-FWD]
- **Curley Smith example** (counterfactuals of freedom): Plantinga imagines a mayor, Curley Smith, who accepted a **$35,000** bribe to drop his opposition; then if he had been offered $36,000 he would (still) freely have accepted. Robert Adams (1977, middle-knowledge paper) accepted the example while holding the counterfactual made true by Smith's actual choice. [source: WS-2 (secondary summaries); city and exact wording NOT checked — see NOT VERIFIED]
- **Step by step (for the writer):** (1) Morally significant (libertarian) freedom is very valuable. (2) God cannot cause a creature to do only right *freely* (W3 in IEP-Log is impossible). (3) A world where free creatures always choose right (W4) is possible, but whether God can actualise it depends on what the creatures would freely do. (4) Possibly every creaturely essence suffers transworld depravity. (5) Then possibly God could not create a world with moral good but no moral evil. (6) So "God exists and is omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good" is consistent with "moral evil exists." [source: IEP-Log §§4–7; WP-FWD]
- **Natural evil**: Plantinga suggests natural evil could be due to the free actions of non-human persons (Satan; following Augustine) — only its possibility is needed (Plantinga 1974a, 58; C. S. Lewis made a similar suggestion). IEP-Log substitutes a Fall-based story (MSR2) and notes many find these implausible but possible. [source: SEP-Ev §7.2; WP-FWD; IEP-Log §6]
- **Objections:** (a) compatibilism (Flew, Mackie): God could have determined us to do only good freely; Plantinga: "One might as well claim that being in jail doesn't really limit one's freedom" (GFE 1977, 32). (b) Mackie, *The Miracle of Theism* (1982): an omnipotent God choosing among all possible essences could have created beings "such that they would always freely choose the good" (p. 174). (c) Heaven: if the redeemed cannot sin yet are free and valuable, why not create that condition now? (d) Critics (Howard-Snyder and O'Leary-Hawthorne; Pruss) dispute that universal transworld depravity is possible; Graham Oppy (*Arguing About Gods*, 2006) doubts the logical argument is dead. [source: WP-FWD; IEP-Log §10]
- **Reception:** Mackie conceded (1982, 154) that "the problem of evil does not, after all, show that the central doctrines of theism are logically inconsistent with one another. But whether this offers a real solution of the problem is another question." Rowe (1979, 335): "granted incompatibilism, there is a fairly compelling argument for the view that the existence of evil is logically consistent with the existence of the theistic God"; R. M. Adams: "it is fair to say that Plantinga has solved this problem". Discussion has since shifted to the evidential problem. [source: IEP-Log §8; WP-FWD]

## The evidential problem I: Rowe (1979)
- William L. Rowe (26 Jul 1931 – 22 Aug 2015); "The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism", *American Philosophical Quarterly* 16 (1979): 335–41. [source: WP-William_L._Rowe; IEP-Evi bibliography]
- **The argument (Rowe 1979, 336):** (1) There exist instances of intense suffering which an omnipotent, omniscient being could have prevented without thereby losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse. (2) An omniscient, wholly good being would prevent the occurrence of any intense suffering it could, unless it could not do so without thereby losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse. (3) Therefore there does not exist an omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being. Valid; premise (1) is the "factual premise" (gratuitous suffering exists), (2) the "theological premise", widely granted. [source: IEP-Evi §2]
- **The fawn (E1):** "In some distant forest lightning strikes a dead tree, resulting in a forest fire. In the fire a fawn is trapped, horribly burned, and lies in terrible agony for several days before death relieves its suffering" (Rowe 1979, 337). Hypothetical but "a familiar sort of tragedy, played not infrequently on the stage of nature" (Rowe 1988, 119). [source: IEP-Evi §2]
- **E2 ("Sue")**: a five-year-old girl in Flint, Michigan, beaten, raped and strangled early on New Year's Day 1986, introduced by Bruce Russell (1989). **The names "Bambi" and "Sue" are William Alston's (1991, 32), not Rowe's.** [source: IEP-Evi §2]
- **The inductive step:** (P) "No good state of affairs that we know of is such that an omnipotent, omniscient being's obtaining it would morally justify that being's permitting E1 or E2" → probably (Q) no good state of affairs is such (Rowe 1991, "Ruminations about Evil", 72). Rowe defends the inference as ordinary induction from the known to the unknown; denying it "is simply to encourage radical skepticism concerning inductive reasoning in general" (1991, 73). [source: SEP-Ev §3.2.2]
- **Theist reply 1 — the "G. E. Moore shift"** (Rowe's name, 1979, 339, where he calls it "the theist's best response"): start from "God exists" and premise (2) and infer the denial of (1). [source: IEP-Evi §2]
- Tooley's objection to Rowe's induction: projecting "no known good has J" to *all* goods is like a long conjunction; it needs a law, not mere regularity. [source: SEP-Ev §3.2.3]

## The evidential problem II: Draper's Humean argument (1989)
- Paul Draper (b. 1957, Purdue); "Pain and Pleasure: An Evidential Problem for Theists", *Noûs* 23 (3), June 1989: 331–350. Inspired by Hume (Dialogues XI). [source: WP-Paul_Draper; SEP-Ev §3.3]
- **Hypothesis of Indifference (HI):** "neither the nature nor the condition of sentient beings on earth is the result of benevolent or malevolent actions performed by nonhuman persons" (1989, 13). [source: SEP-Ev §3.3]
- **Step by step:** O = the observed facts about pain and pleasure: (a) biologically useful pain and pleasure in moral agents, (b) in sentient non-moral-agents, (c) pain and pleasure not known to be biologically useful. (i) Pr(HI) ≥ Pr(T). (ii) Pr(O|HI) > Pr(O|T). (iii) So, by Bayes' theorem, Pr(HI|O) > Pr(T|O). (iv) HI entails not-T. (v) So given O, theism is more likely false than true. An inference to the best explanation rather than Rowe's direct induction. [source: SEP-Ev §3.3]
- **Objections (Tooley's list):** HI may not entail ¬T (a God who never intervenes after creating a chancy evolutionary world); the priors claim (i) is questionable (HI is a sweeping universal negative); the argument says nothing about *how far* below 0.5 theism falls; van Inwagen attacks premise (ii); and the total evidence may include other facts (O*) favouring theism. Draper's alternative "Indifferent Deity Hypothesis" (1989, 26) avoids the first two. [source: SEP-Ev §3.3]
- Rowe also gave a Bayesian version, and Tooley an inductive-logic version. [source: SEP-Ev §3.1]

## Skeptical theism and its costs
- Stephen J. Wykstra, "The Humean Obstacle to Evidential Arguments from Suffering: On Avoiding the Evils of 'Appearance'", *International Journal for Philosophy of Religion* 16 (1984): 73–93. [source: IEP-Evi bibliography; SEP-ST bibliography] (IEP-Evi's prose calls it "…from Evil"; the bibliography title is "from Suffering".)
- **CORNEA** = "Condition Of ReasoNable Epistemic Access" (SEP-ST: "an unprincipled acronym"). IEP-Evi's simplified form: one may infer "There is no x" from "So far as I can tell, there is no x" only if it is reasonable to believe that if there were an x, one would likely perceive it. Wykstra (1996) called such inferences "noseeum inferences" ("we no see 'um, so they ain't there"). [source: IEP-Evi §3; SEP-ST §1.3]
- **Parent/infant analogy**: Wykstra (1984, 87–91) compares the gap between God's mind and ours to that between a parent and her one-month-old infant: the infant cannot be expected to discern the purposes of the pains its parents allow. [source: IEP-Evi §3; SEP-ST §1.3]
- Plantinga's rhetorical question: "Why suppose that if God does have a reason for permitting evil, the theist would be the first to know?" (1974, 10). [source: IEP-Evi §3]
- Alston's analogies (1996): the physics student and a quantum theory, a novice and Picasso's *Guernica*, a beginner and Kasparov. Alston (1991, 61) concluded "the inductive argument from evil is in no better shape than its late lamented deductive cousin". [source: IEP-Evi §3]
- **Reply/objection (Rowe):** disanalogies with the infant (Rowe 1996, 275); and Rowe: "God, if he exists, isn't playing chess with our lives" — a loving parent would comfort or explain. Divine hiddenness is connected. [source: IEP-Evi §3]
- **Moral paralysis objection** (Almeida and Oppy 2003; Piper 2007; Rancourt 2013): if seemingly unjustified evils may have justifying reasons beyond our ken, the skeptical theist should reason that she should never intervene to prevent them — clearly unreasonable and immoral; combined with believing God commands us to prevent suffering, this produces "a kind of moral paralysis or aporia" (Piper 2007, 72–3). Reply: theists can act on God's commands, and our obligations depend on what we know, not on all-things-considered value. [source: SEP-ST §6.1–6.3]
- Other costs: does skeptical theism lead to wider (external-world, religious) scepticism? [source: SEP-ST §5]

## Soul-making: Irenaeus and Hick
- **Irenaeus** of Lyon (c. 130 – c. 202 CE per IEP-Evi), *Against Heresies* (c. 180): humans made first in the "image" and then to grow into the "likeness" of God; "epistemic distance" is Hick's term drawing on him. Hick identified this "Irenaean" type in *Evil and the God of Love* (1966); Mark Scott argues Origen rather than Irenaeus is its father; Schleiermacher is Hick's modern precursor. [source: WP-Irenaean_theodicy; IEP-Evi §4; IEP-Hick]
- John Hick (20 Jan 1922 – 9 Feb 2012); *Evil and the God of Love* (London: Macmillan, **1966**; 2nd ed. 1977; reissue 2007 with a foreword by Marilyn Adams). [source: WP-John_Hick; IEP-Evi bibliography; IEP-Hick]
- Hick: the world is a "**vale of soul-making**"; "it is an ethically reasonable judgment… that human goodness slowly built up through personal histories of moral effort has a value in the eyes of the Creator which justifies even the long travail of the soul-making process" (EGL 256); "Men are not to be thought of on the analogy of animal pets, whose life is to be made as agreeable as possible, but rather on the analogy of human children…" (EGL 258); a soul-making world needs "obstacles to be overcome, tasks to be performed, goals to be achieved, setbacks to be endured, problems to be solved, dangers to be met" (Hick 1966, 362). [source: IEP-Hick; IEP-Evi §4]
- Hick's value judgement: one "who has attained to goodness by meeting and eventually mastering temptations… is good in a richer and more valuable sense than would be one created ab initio in a state either of innocence or of virtue" (1977, 255–6). [source: SEP-Ev §7.1; IEP-Log §9]
- **The phrase is Keats's**: letter to George and Georgiana Keats (journal-letter of Feb–May 1819; passage dated **21 April 1819** in Rollins's edition): "Call the world if you Please 'The vale of Soul-making'"; Keats asks whether one can see "how necessary a World of Pains and troubles is to school an Intelligence and make it a soul". [source: WP-Irenaean_theodicy (phrase "taken from the poet John Keats"); WS-4 (one blog gives 12 April)]
- Hick's universalism: the hope "that God will eventually succeed in His purpose of winning all men to Himself" (EGL 342); later he moved from a soul-making theodicy to a soul-making soteriology (Adams). [source: IEP-Hick]
- **Step by step:** (1) A good God aims at persons who freely grow into moral and spiritual maturity. (2) Goodness won through overcoming real challenges is more valuable than goodness created ready-made. (3) Such growth needs a world of real dangers, pain and possible failure, and "epistemic distance" from God. (4) Our world is such an environment. (5) So the evils it contains are justified by the soul-making they make possible, completed beyond death. [source: IEP-Hick; IEP-Evi §4; SEP-Ev §7.1]
- **Objections:** (a) horrendous and **dysteleological** suffering (Hick's term for suffering that seems unjust, undeserved, unnecessary or excessive and destroys rather than builds character; Hick's reply is that such mystery is itself conducive to soul-making and the process continues after death) [source: WS-13]; Stump's "spiritual chemotherapy" view fails because terminal illness does not track spiritual health [source: SEP-Ev §7.1]; (b) **animal suffering** — soul-making gives no justification for animal pain or for predation over millions of years (Tooley calls Hick's discussion, 1977, 309–17, weak); (c) young children; (d) the distribution is poor: some die young, some suffer too much to grow, some live in ease. [source: SEP-Ev §7.1] Soul-making may benefit perpetrators or witnesses rather than victims ("jailhouse conversions"). [source: IEP-Evi §4]
- Swinburne's variant: free will is more valuable the greater the range of good and evil open to us; otherwise a "toy world". Objection: still no account of natural evils. [source: SEP-Ev §7.3; WP-Irenaean_theodicy]
- General objections to free-will theodicy (Tooley): we think we should prevent rape and murder when we can; freedom need not include power to inflict great harm; natural evils are untouched. [source: SEP-Ev §7.2]

## Leibniz, Bayle, Lisbon, Voltaire, Rousseau, Kant (recap; the Leibniz chapter has the step-by-step)
- "Theodicy" coined by Leibniz (Greek *theos* + *dikē*), *Essais de Théodicée sur la bonté de Dieu, la liberté de l'homme et l'origine du mal* (1710). [source: WP-Theodicy; Leibniz chapter]
- **Bayle**: his best-known thesis is "the skeptical claim that there is no rational solution to the problem of evil"; centrepiece the *Dictionary* articles "Manicheans" and "Paulicians" (1st ed., 1697) and the "Clarification on the Manicheans" (2nd ed.). These were "the occasional cause of Leibniz's *Theodicy* (1710)", and inspired Voltaire's *Candide*, several chapters of Hume's *Dialogues* and possibly Kant's 1791 essay. [source: SEP-Bay §6]
- **Lisbon earthquake**: Saturday **1 November 1755**, All Saints' Day, c. 09:40; quake, then a tsunami about 40 minutes later, then fires (candles lit for the feast) burned for hours; magnitude est. 7.7–9.0. **Deaths**: in Lisbon an estimated **30,000–40,000** of c. 200,000 inhabitants (Álvaro Pereira); perhaps 10,000 more in Morocco; total in Portugal, Spain and Morocco **40,000–50,000**; 85% of Lisbon's buildings destroyed. WP's poem article gives "between 30,000 and 50,000" (Scott 1988). [source: WP-1755_Lisbon_earthquake; WP-Poème] Consistent with *Leibniz* (30,000–40,000 in the city).
- **Voltaire, *Poème sur le désastre de Lisbonne***: 180 lines in rhyming couplets, composed December 1755, published **1756**; attacks Pope's "Whatever is, is right" and Leibnizian optimism. Lines (WP's English, translator not named): "And can you then impute a sinful deed / To babes who on their mothers' bosoms bleed? / Was then more vice in fallen Lisbon found, / Than Paris, where voluptuous joys abound?" [source: WP-Poème]
- ***Candide, ou l'Optimisme***: published January 1759; Pangloss's formula "all is for the best in the best of all possible worlds" (*Tout est pour le mieux dans le meilleur des mondes possibles*) is Voltaire's caricature, not Leibniz's wording. [source: WP-Candide; Leibniz chapter]
- **Rousseau's letter to Voltaire** ("Lettre sur la Providence"), dated **18 August 1756** (WS-5); WP-Poème says Voltaire *received* it on 18 August 1756 — sources differ on whether that is the date of writing or receipt; say "dated 18 August 1756". Rousseau argued that particular evils serve the general good and that Voltaire must either renounce Providence or find it beneficial; he believed *Candide* was Voltaire's reply. [source: WP-Poème; WS-5]
- **Natural evil and urban planning**: Rousseau held that the severity of the disaster was due to too many people living in close quarters in the city, and used the earthquake as an argument against cities. [source: WP-1755_Lisbon_earthquake] (His famous line about "twenty thousand houses of six or seven storeys" was NOT verified — see below.)
- **Kant** published three texts on the earthquake in **1756** ("On the causes of the earthquakes…", "History and natural description of the most remarkable occurrences of the earthquake…", "Continued observations…"), with a theory of shifting underground caverns of hot gases — wrong, but among the first naturalistic explanations. [source: WP-1755_Lisbon_earthquake] Later, "**On the Miscarriage of All Philosophical Trials in Theodicy**" (1791; AK 8:253–271; trans. George di Giovanni) concludes that all theodicies fail and that the doubts raised by evil "can neither be conclusively answered nor conclusively refute God's infinite moral wisdom": theodicy is a matter of faith, not knowledge. [source: SEP-KR bibliography; IEP-KR §5]
- Adorno, *Negative Dialectics*: "The earthquake of Lisbon sufficed to cure Voltaire of the theodicy of Leibniz." [source: WP-1755_Lisbon_earthquake] (Already quoted in *Leibniz* — don't repeat at length.)
- The earthquake prompted the first large-scale scientific survey of an earthquake under the Marquis of Pombal (questionnaires to parish priests) and earthquake-resistant "Pombaline" rebuilding. [source: WP-1755_Lisbon_earthquake]

## Horrendous evils, protest, the Holocaust
- **Marilyn McCord Adams** (12 Oct 1943 – 22 Mar 2017), *Horrendous Evils and the Goodness of God* (Cornell UP, **1999**). [source: WP-Marilyn_McCord_Adams]
- Horrendous evils: those "the participation in which (that is, the doing or suffering of which) constitutes prima facie reason to doubt whether the participant's life could (given their inclusion in it) be a great good to him/her on the whole" (1999, 26). Her examples include "the rape of a woman and axing off of her arms, psycho-physical torture whose ultimate goal is the disintegration of personality, betrayal of one's deepest loyalties, child abuse of the sort described by Ivan Karamazov… the explosion of nuclear bombs over populated areas". [source: IEP-Evi §1]
- Adams's answer: face-to-face intimacy with God is a good *incommensurable* with any temporal evil, so for each participant "strictly speaking, there will be no balance to be struck" (1986, 263); she was a Christian universalist. [source: IEP-Evi §4; WP-Marilyn_McCord_Adams]
- **Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*** (serialised 1879–80; book 1880): Book V "Pro and Contra", chapter 4 "Rebellion": Ivan cannot accept a world built on the suffering of children. Garnett's translation (1912): "It's not God that I don't accept, Alyosha, only I most respectfully return Him the ticket." (Editions vary "him/Him".) Followed by "The Grand Inquisitor". Dostoevsky meant Book VI (Zosima) as the refutation (letter of 10 May 1879). Camus centred *The Rebel* (1951) on Ivan's revolt. [source: WP-The_Brothers_Karamazov; WS-6]
- **Protest theodicy**: John K. Roth, "A Theodicy of Protest", in Stephen T. Davis (ed.), *Encountering Evil: Live Options in Theodicy* (1981/1982; 2nd ed. 2001, pp. 1–20). IEP-Evi groups Roth (2001) and David Blumenthal (*Facing the Abusing God*, 1993; "worship of God through protest") with theists who posit a "dark side" in God. [source: IEP-Evi §2; WP-Holocaust_theology] (WP dates the first edition 1982; IEP-Evi gives Hick's essay in the same volume as 1981 — say "first edition early 1980s".)
- **Elie Wiesel**: *Night* (Auschwitz, Buna, Buchenwald); his 1979 play *The Trial of God*, in which God is the defendant, reportedly based on events he witnessed as a teenager in Auschwitz. [source: WP-Holocaust_theology]
- Other post-Holocaust Jewish positions: Richard Rubenstein, *After Auschwitz* (rejecting the God of history; later modified); Eliezer Berkovits (God's hiddenness preserves freedom; *tzimtzum*). [source: WP-Holocaust_theology]
- **Hans Jonas**, "Der Gottesbegriff nach Auschwitz. Eine jüdische Stimme": lecture on receiving the Dr. Leopold-Lucas Prize from Tübingen's Protestant theology faculty, **1984**, printed 1984 in O. Hofius (ed.), *Reflexionen finsterer Zeit*; English "The Concept of God after Auschwitz: A Jewish Voice", *Journal of Religion* 67 (1) (1987): 1–13. Commentators read it as giving up divine omnipotence to keep God's goodness and intelligibility (interpretive gloss, not Jonas's wording). Jonas's mother was murdered in Auschwitz (1942). [source: WS-10; WP-Hans_Jonas]
- Adorno on Auschwitz is in *Critical Theory* — cross-ref.

There seems to me too much misery in the world. ## Islamic thought
- For medieval Muslim thinkers "the existence of God was never in question"; the problem was "What is evil?", "Is our world the best possible world?", and which divine attribute, justice or omnipotence, takes priority. [source: SEP-AIR §5]
- **Ashʿarite voluntarism**: al-Ashʿarī early on stressed God's absolute control: suffering needs no further justification, "since the universe is God's property and God can treat creatures the way he pleases. He can punish an innocent person and can hold people responsible to do that which they cannot do" (Ashʿarī, EIF 111–13). [source: SEP-AIR §5] Al-Ghazālī kept the Ashʿarite view in law that human reason cannot decide whether an act is good or bad. [source: SEP-Gh]
- **Muʿtazilite justice (ʿadl)**: one standard of goodness and justice for God and humans; suffering God causes must be justified by reasons such as compensation (*ʿiwaḍ*), admonition (*ʿibr*), punishment, benefit (*maṣlaḥa*) or grace (*luṭf*) (ʿAbd al-Jabbār); hence God must do what is best (*aṣlaḥ*) — first in religious matters, ultimately in everything, so "for them this world is the best possible world" (implied, not stated). [source: SEP-AIR §5] (Five principles incl. ʿadl: see *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes*.)
- **Avicenna**: evil is non-being or imperfection; real suffering is confined to the sphere of generation and corruption and is a "necessary evil": fire that burns a poor man's house is good in being hot; removing it would remove a whole class of goods (*Metaphysics* of the *Healing* 9.6). [source: SEP-AIR §5]
- **Al-Ghazālī**: "there is in possibility nothing more wondrous than what is" (*laysa fī l-imkān abdaʿ mimmā kān*) (SEP-Gh, citing the *Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn*, Ghazālī 1937–38, 13:2515–18 = Burrell trans. 2001, 47–50); fuller form: "In the realm of the possible, there is nothing at all more wondrously beautiful than [this world] nor more complete nor more perfect" (*laysa fī-l-imkān abdaʿ aṣlan aḥsana minhu…*); popular rhyming form "In the realm of all the possible, nothing than what is is more wonderful" (SEP-AIR, citing *Faith in Divine Unity* 45–6). Argument: if a better world were possible and God could but did not create it, he would be miserly; if he could not, impotent; both impugn God; imperfections reflect the limits of our knowledge and help us appreciate the good. [source: SEP-Gh; SEP-AIR §5] Consistent with *Possibility and Necessity*'s rendering.
- **Interpretive difference**: SEP-Gh says al-Ghazālī "followed Avicenna" in holding this the best of all possible worlds; SEP-AIR says neither Avicenna nor the Muʿtazila explicitly claimed it and al-Ghazālī was the one who "explicitly argued" it. Also SEP-Gh stresses (against Frank 1992) that al-Ghazālī held God chooses freely between alternatives. [source: SEP-Gh; SEP-AIR]
- The dictum "led to a long-lasting debate among later Muslim theologians" — Eric L. Ormsby, *Theodicy in Islamic Thought: The Dispute over al-Ghazālī's 'Best of All Possible Worlds'* (Princeton, 1984). Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī argued that Avicenna's analysis sidesteps the real problem, a world with more pain than pleasure (WP, citing Shihadeh). [source: SEP-Gh; WP-Theodicy]

## Jewish thought (Maimonides; cross-ref *Jewish Philosophy*)
- Maimonides, *Guide of the Perplexed*: evil is privation — God creates good, evil is the absence of good (Guide 3:10); good is far more common than evil if one looks at the whole universe rather than humanity (3:12); **three kinds of evil** (3:12): (1) evils from nature (the rarest, and necessary to a world of generation and decay), (2) evils people inflict on one another, (3) evils people bring on themselves through excess and bodily desire — the most common. [source: WP-Maimonides (secondary summary; no translator named)]
- Providence in proportion to intellectual perfection; "If man knows this, every misfortune will be borne lightly by him" (on Job's understanding). [source: IEP-Mai]

## Indian approaches: karma, beginninglessness, Buddhist and Jain critiques
- Most Indian schools posit no omnipotent, omnibenevolent creator (Sāṃkhya, early Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, Buddhism, Jainism), so the classical problem takes a different form; karma explains suffering. [source: WP-Problem_of_evil_in_Hinduism] (Caveat: this WP article is of mixed quality; use only for framing.)
- **Brahmasūtra 2.1.34–36** (the *Brahmasūtras* probably composed 200 BCE–200 CE): 2.1.34 — "partiality and cruelty" (*vaiṣamya*, *nairghṛṇya*) cannot be ascribed to the Lord, because he takes other factors into account, i.e. dispenses according to each soul's merit and demerit (like rain, which makes each seed grow according to its own kind — the rain image NOT confirmed this session); 2.1.35 — objection: before creation there were no distinctions, so no karma to explain unequal creation; reply: transmigration is **beginningless** (*anāditva*), like seed and sprout; a body requires karma and karma requires a body; 2.1.36 — beginninglessness is reasonable and scripturally attested. Numbering differs slightly between editions/translations (Vireśvarānanda vs Thibaut). [source: WS-7; WP-Problem_of_evil_in_Hinduism]
- Śaṅkara's statement of the objection (trans. Arvind Sharma, via WP): "…on account of the possibility of partiality and cruelty, God is not an agent" (i.e. not an agent *independently* of karma). [source: WP-Problem_of_evil_in_Hinduism] WARNING: that WP article also says "Shankara develops the argument that God is not the Brahman, and that 'a loving and good God could not have created the universe'" — this garbles the passage (Śaṅkara's conclusion is that the Lord is not *unjust*, because creation depends on karma). Don't use that sentence.
- **Step by step (Śaṅkara's karma reply):** (1) The world is unequal and full of suffering. (2) If the Lord created it unequal by his own choice, he would be partial and cruel. (3) But the Lord creates in dependence on the merit and demerit (karma) of beings. (4) So inequality reflects beings' own past actions, not partiality. (5) Objection: at the first creation there was no karma. (6) Reply: saṃsāra has no beginning, so there was no first creation without prior karma. [source: WS-7]
- **Objection (Jain, Jinasena's *Mahāpurāṇa*, 9th c.)**: "If he created because of the karma of embodied beings… He is not the Almighty Lord, but subordinate to something else"; "If out of love for living beings and need of them he made the world, why did he not make creation wholly blissful free from misfortune?"; "God commits great sin in slaying the children whom he himself created." (WP's English; translator not named — attribute as "in one English translation".) [source: WP-Jainism_and_non-creationism]
- Jains reject a creator: atoms are eternal and combine by natural laws; Jains criticise views that do not favour ethical responsibility; karma is the agent's own. [source: SEP-Jain]
- **Madhva**: B. N. K. Sharma (1962): the tripartite classification of souls "makes it unnecessary to answer the problem of evil"; Buchta objects (see *Vedānta*). [source: WP-Problem_of_evil_in_Hinduism; Vedānta chapter]
- **Buddhist**: *Devadaha Sutta* (Majjhima Nikāya 101): "If the pleasure and pain that beings feel are caused by the creative act of a Supreme God, then the Nigaṇṭhas [Jains] surely must have been created by an evil Supreme God, since they now feel such painful, racking, piercing feelings." (WP's translation unnamed.) Richard Hayes: no systematic disproof of God in the early texts; the Buddha is a sceptic about speculation. [source: WP-Creator_in_Buddhism]
- Jātaka (VI.208 in the PTS numbering; the Bhūridatta Jātaka per common identification — NOT confirmed): "If Brahma is lord of the whole world and Creator of the multitude of beings, then why has he ordained misfortune in the world without making the whole world happy… or is the lord of beings evil in that he ordained injustice when there could have been justice?" (quoted from Netland and Yandell 2009). [source: WP-Creator_in_Buddhism]
- Medieval Buddhists (Vasubandhu and others) refuted Hindu theism; Matthew Kapstein says they used the argument from evil alongside formal objections; Nāgārjuna's *Twelve Gate Treatise* argues against Īśvara. Detailed Buddhist and Mīmāṃsā critiques of Īśvara belong to *God: Arguments For and Against*. [source: WP-Creator_in_Buddhism]
- Classical Sāṃkhya starts from threefold suffering; early Buddhism's first noble truth is suffering without a creator (see *Buddha*, *Classical Indian Schools*). [cf. research notes]

## The "karma and theodicy" debate
- **Max Weber** (1916; *The Religion of India*, trans. Gerth and Martindale, 1958, p. 121) called the karma doctrine "the most consistent theodicy ever produced by history". [source: WS-8]
- **Gananath Obeyesekere**, "Theodicy, Sin and Salvation in a Sociology of Buddhism", in E. R. Leach (ed.), *Dialectic in Practical Religion* (Cambridge, **1968**), 7–40; he accused Weber of confusion in his use of "theodicy" and distinguished karma's eschatology; returned to it in *Imagining Karma* (2002). [source: WS-12]
- **Arthur L. Herman**, *The Problem of Evil and Indian Thought* (Motilal Banarsidass, **1976**; 2nd ed. later): reduces some 21 historical Western solutions to eight, judges them failures, and argues (p. 287) that the Indian "doctrine of rebirth solves the problem of evil". [source: WS-8; WP-Problem_of_evil_in_Hinduism]
- Critics: Whitley R. P. Kaufman, "Karma, Rebirth, and the Problem of Evil" (doubts karma works as a complete theodicy; it is hard to treat it as one on its own); Elisa Freschi, "Is the Theory of Karman the Solution to the Problem of Evil? Some Thoughts from Viśiṣṭādvaita Vedānta", *Religions* 12 (2021). [source: WS-8; WP-Theodicy ref]

## Debates
- **Did Plantinga defeat the logical problem?** Majority yes (Mackie conceded consistency; Adams, Alston, Rowe agree); dissent from Oppy (2006) and Weisberger (1999); critics of transworld depravity (Howard-Snyder & O'Leary-Hawthorne; Pruss). [WP-FWD; IEP-Log]
- **Defence vs theodicy** — what a defence must show against an *evidential* argument (true for all we know? a real possibility? likely?): van Inwagen vs Tooley. [SEP-Ev §4]
- **Rowe's inductive step** vs skeptical theism (Wykstra, Alston, Bergmann); Rowe's disanalogy replies; costs of skeptical theism (moral paralysis; wider scepticism; hiddenness). [IEP-Evi §3; SEP-ST]
- **Draper's priors and HI** — Tooley's objections; van Inwagen against premise (ii). [SEP-Ev §3.3]
