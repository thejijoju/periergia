## 1. The problem and key distinctions
- Interpretation = "the attribution of meaning to a work"; the recurring dispute is "whether the artist's or author's intention is relevant to the interpretation of the work". In analytic philosophy of art the views split into intentionalism and anti-intentionalism, with an initial focus on literature. [source: IEP-Int, intro]
- IEP's opening puzzles (good for the chapter's opening): the point of Kafka's *Metamorphosis* or Duchamp's *Fountain*; whether the end of Nolan's *Inception* is reality or dream; whether the ghosts in Henry James's *The Turn of the Screw* are real; whether the woman in white in Raphael's *School of Athens* is Hypatia; whether the conch in Golding's *Lord of the Flies* symbolises civilisation and democracy. [source: IEP-Int §1]
- Global vs local meaning: global = the work's theme, thesis or point; local = what a part conveys. "Attribute" can mean "recover" (retrieval view: meaning already in the work) or "impose" (impositional view). [source: IEP-Int §1]
- Evidence for intention: external (interviews, diaries, letters, autobiography) vs internal (the work itself, regarded by intentionalists as the best evidence, e.g. for anonymous works). [source: IEP-Int §1]
- SEP-Beard frames the whole debate as the relation between (1) "The artist intended x to mean p in work w" and (2) "x means p in work w": Hirsch holds (1) entails (2); Beardsley holds (1) gives "no direct evidential support" for (2); "many philosophers, probably the majority" hold intermediate positions (intentions have some weight, not infinite weight). [source: SEP-Beard §9]
- Ontological contextualism: a work's identity and content are partly fixed by its context of production (e.g. whether it was revolutionary relative to its tradition); hence the work/text distinction (Currie 1991): a text is context-independent, a work is not. Most late-20th and 21st-century participants accept contextualism, traced to Danto's "The Artworld" (1964). [source: IEP-Int §2c, §7]

## 2. Anti-intentionalism: Wimsatt and Beardsley
- William K. Wimsatt Jr. (17 Nov 1907, Washington DC – 17 Dec 1975), Yale professor of English; Monroe C. Beardsley (10 Dec 1915, Bridgeport, Connecticut – 18 Sep 1985), philosopher of art, taught mostly at Swarthmore (22 years) and Temple (16 years). [source: WP William_K._Wimsatt; WP Monroe_Beardsley]
- "The Intentional Fallacy", *Sewanee Review* 54 (1946): 468–88; revised and reprinted in Wimsatt's *The Verbal Icon: Studies in the Meaning of Poetry* (University of Kentucky Press, 1954), 3–18. IEP calls it "the first thorough presentation of anti-intentionalism, commonly regarded as starting point of the intention debate". It was a reaction against biographical criticism and is associated with the New Criticism. [source: IEP-Int §2 and refs; WP-DotA ref]
- Companion paper "The Affective Fallacy" (1949), also Wimsatt and Beardsley: a reader's affective responses are irrelevant to a work's descriptive, interpretive and evaluative properties. [source: SEP-Beard §9]
- Beardsley later extended anti-intentionalism across the arts in *Aesthetics: Problems in the Philosophy of Criticism* (1958; 2nd ed. 1981) and in *The Possibility of Criticism* (1970). [source: IEP-Int §2 and refs]
- Beardsley's Principle of Autonomy: critical statements must in the end be tested against the work itself. Anti-intentionalism is also called **conventionalism**: linguistic and literary conventions are necessary and sufficient for work-meaning. [source: IEP-Int §2a]
- The "pudding" passage (IF: 469, as quoted by SEP): "Judging a poem is like judging a pudding or a machine. One demands that it work. It is only because an artifact works that we infer the intention of the artificer…." Reconstructed by Wreen as: works, like puddings or washing machines, are independent of their makers once made. Wreen's objection: not all artefacts "leave their creators behind"; linguistic artefacts are tied to "the intention-laden conditions of their origin". Beardsley's rejoinder (as Wreen gives it): this is special pleading unless one shows why art differs from other artefacts; "pure music" is not meaning-laden, and newspapers are meaning-laden but not art. [source: SEP-Beard §9]
- The "Human Destiny" sculpture (Beardsley, *Aesthetics* 18–21): a "large, twisted, cruller-shaped object of polished teak" whose maker says it symbolises "Human Destiny"; Beardsley appeals to Alice against Humpty Dumpty: the question is whether the object *can be made* to mean that, otherwise anything could symbolise anything by fiat (another copy could be labelled "Spirit of Palm Beach, 1938"). Wreen: this shows the artist's say-so is not absolute, not that intention is irrelevant. [source: SEP-Beard §9]
- Sentence meaning vs speaker meaning: what a sentence means depends on "public conventions of usage"; an author can be wrong about his own work — Beardsley thought A. E. Housman probably wrong in denying that his poem "1887" is ironic. [source: SEP-Beard §9]
- Three arguments in *The Possibility of Criticism* (PC 18–20): (i) texts produced with no author can still be interpreted — printer's error "Jensen argued like a man filled with righteous indigestion" (for "indignation"); (ii) textual meaning can change after the author's death (1744 line "He raised his plastic arm"); (iii) texts can have meanings the author was unaware of. Wreen's replies: (i) shows intention is not identical to meaning, not that it is irrelevant, and such texts are parasitic on intended ones; (ii) licenses "unbridled anachronism" (compare "Now we don our gay apparel"); Beardsley seems later to have renounced (ii); (iii) again shows non-identity only. [source: SEP-Beard §9]
- Beardsley himself in *Aesthetics* (p. 20) concedes intention and object "are causally connected", so each is indirect evidence for the other. [source: SEP-Beard §10]
- Beardsley's later speech-act theory (1981 "Fiction as Representation", *Synthese* 46; 1982 *The Aesthetic Point of View*): fictional works are *imitations* of illocutionary acts ("address without access", non-referring names), so what is represented is fixed by convention, as with a mime. [source: IEP-Int §2b]

## 3. ARGUMENT (step by step): the Intentional Fallacy dilemma
- Reconstruction (IEP-Int §2a, "the famous argument offered by Wimsatt and Beardsley"):
  1. Either the artist's intention is successfully realised in the work, or it is not.
  2. If it is realised, appeal to external evidence of intention is unnecessary: we can detect the intention from the work.
  3. If it is not realised, appeal to external evidence is insufficient: the intention is extraneous to the work, and convention alone fixes what the work means.
  4. So appeal to external evidence of intention is either unnecessary or insufficient; treating it as relevant commits "the intentional fallacy".
  - Second formulation: since artists do not always realise their intentions, the inference "the artist intended w to mean p, so w means p" is invalid. "Intentional fallacy" thus has a normative layer (a bad principle of interpretation) and an ontological layer (a fallacious inference). [source: IEP-Int §2a]
- Objection 1 (Hirsch 1967): convention alone often cannot pick out a single meaning; works like *Ulysses* sustain several readings, so we need intention to disambiguate. Beardsley's reply (1970): in most cases appeal to the work's coherence eventually leaves a single correct interpretation. [source: IEP-Int §2c]
- Objection 2 (Hirsch 1976, *The Aims of Interpretation* 24–5): irony. Daniel Defoe's pamphlet *The Shortest Way with the Dissenters* was taken by many at publication as genuinely anti-Dissenter; the only ground for calling it ironic seems to be Defoe's intention. Beardsley's reply (1982: 203–7): irony must offer the possibility of understanding; if the artist cannot imagine anyone taking it ironically, there is no reason to think it ironic. [source: IEP-Int §2c]
- Objection 3 (contextualism): features like being revolutionary depend on context, so a purely textual interpretation misidentifies the work. Reply: Stephen Davies (2005, "Beardsley and the Autonomy of the Work of Art", *JAAC* 63) argues Beardsley's position actually allows contextualism. [source: IEP-Int §2c]

## 4. Value-maximising theory (anti-intentionalism's revival)
- Core claim: the primary aim of interpretation is to offer readings that maximise (or enhance) the work's value within limits set by convention (Goldman 2013, *Philosophy and the Novel*) or convention plus context (Stephen Davies 2007, *Philosophical Perspectives on Art*). It is pluralist, not monist: several value-enhancing readings (e.g. of *Metamorphosis*) can be legitimate. [source: IEP-Int §3a]
- Main objection: it would turn a mediocre work into a masterpiece — Ed Wood's *Plan 9 from Outer Space*, read as postmodern satire. Davies's reply (2007: 187): that reading is anachronistic; postmodern views were not available to Wood, so it misidentifies the work. [source: IEP-Int §3b]

## 5. Actual intentionalism
- E. D. Hirsch Jr. (born 22 Mar 1928, Memphis), *Validity in Interpretation* (Yale University Press, 1967), defends "the sensible belief that a text means what its author meant"; distinguishes a text's "meaning" (fixed) from its "significance" (changes over time); Sherri Irvin calls it "the seminal statement of actual intentionalism". [source: WP E._D._Hirsch; IEP-Int refs]
- IEP distinguishes three versions:
  - **Absolute**: a work means whatever its creator intends; intention necessary and sufficient. Dubbed "Humpty-Dumptyism" after *Through the Looking-Glass*. Supporting argument: marks without an intentional agent cannot mean (marks caused by accident in sand are not a poem); objection: this shows intention necessary for meaningfulness, not that it infallibly fixes what is meant. Knapp and Michaels's absolute intentionalism is in Iseminger (ed.), *Intention and Interpretation* (1992); Kathleen Stock (2017, *Only Imagine*) defends what she calls "extreme" intentionalism (IEP classes it as absolute). [source: IEP-Int §4a and refs]
  - **Extreme** (IEP's classification of Hirsch 1967): convention fixes the range of possible meanings; intention selects one; if intention matches none, the work is meaningless. Objection (Nathan, in Iseminger 1992): a dilemma over irony. [source: IEP-Int §4b]
  - **Moderate** (Carroll 2001 *Beyond Aesthetics*; Stecker 2003 *Interpretation and Construction*; Livingston 2005 *Art and Intention*): when intention succeeds it fixes meaning; when it fails, convention plus (perhaps) context do. Success conditions: weak "meshing" (sufficient coherence between intention and the work's patterns) or strong (the intended meaning is the one most likely to secure uptake from a well-backgrounded audience — Stecker 2003; Davies 2007: 170–1). Test case: if Agatha Christie announced Hercule Poirot was a smart Martian in disguise, bare "compatibility" would wrongly admit it; meshing rules it out. [source: IEP-Int §4c]
- Objections to actual intentionalism: Trivedi's (2001) epistemic dilemma (redundancy or indeterminacy) — reply: success is not judged by comparing independently found meaning with intention (Stecker 2010: 154–5); the epistemic worry (minds unknowable) — Carroll (2009: 71–5): we grasp intentions all the time in conversation and history; Nathan's (2006) "publicity paradox" — reply (Stecker 2010: 153–4): not all artists have the second-order intention, and the argument conflates two intentions. [source: IEP-Int §4c–d]
- Carroll's **conversation argument**: interpretation is like conversation, whose standard aim is to grasp what the speaker means. Objection (Dickie 2006; Nathan 2006; Huddleston 2012): art is more like a monologue. Carroll's example of a constrained "hermeneutics of suspicion": a racist reading of Jules Verne's *Mysterious Island* (the slave Neb portrayed as docile and superstitious) presupposes the portrayal is not ironic. [source: IEP-Int §4d]
- IEP's verdict on the state of the field: "actual intentionalism remains the most popular position among all", probably due to Grice's influence; monographs Stecker 2003, Livingston 2005, Carroll 2009, Stock 2017. [source: IEP-Int §7]

## 6. Hypothetical intentionalism
- Core claim: correct meaning = the best hypothesis about the artist's intention formed by an appropriate audience. Founding paper: William Tolhurst, "On What a Text Is and How It Means", *British Journal of Aesthetics* 19 (1979): 3–14; main defender Jerrold Levinson (*The Pleasures of Aesthetics*, 1996, ch. 10; replies in *Contemplating Art*, 2006; *Aesthetic Pursuits*, 2016). [source: IEP-Int §5 and refs]
- Tolhurst: the relevant audience is the *intended* audience; e.g. the best hypothesis for Swift's *A Modest Proposal*, given his intended readers' background, is satire, not a real proposal to sell poor children as food. Problem: an esoteric intended audience makes meaning private, close to Humpty-Dumptyism. Levinson substitutes an *ideal/appropriate* audience who knows the public facts about artist and work. Aesthetic tie-breaker: among equally good hypotheses, prefer the one that makes the work artistically better. [source: IEP-Int §5a]
- Levinson's semantic vs **categorial** intention (1996: 188–9): categorial intention fixes what kind of thing the work is (artwork, genre) — a text taken as a grocery list says nothing beyond the items. [source: IEP-Int §5a]
- Objections: (a) Carroll (2001: 208–9): why keep a hypothesis when a found diary proves it false? Levinson (2006: 308): the best hypothesis constitutes *utterance* meaning, not *utterer's* meaning. (b) Davies (2007: 183–4): it collapses into value-maximising. (c) Carroll (2001: 212): public/private evidence line is blurry; Levinson (2006: 310): public context = what the artist appears to have wanted the audience to know. (d) Stecker (2010: 159–60) counterexamples, e.g. Dr Watson's war wound placed in his arm on one occasion and his thigh on another. [source: IEP-Int §5b]
- Variant: fictionalist intentionalism / "postulated author" (Nehamas 1981, "The Postulated Author: Critical Monism as a Regulative Ideal", *Critical Inquiry* 8; Currie 1990; Walton's "apparent artist"), descending from Wayne Booth's "implied author" (*The Rhetoric of Fiction*). [source: IEP-Int §6]

## 7. The "Humpty Dumpty" problem
- Lewis Carroll, *Through the Looking-Glass* (1871), ch. 6: Humpty Dumpty: "When I use a word … it means just what I choose it to mean" (wording confirmed in search summaries of the chapter; the rest of the sentence — "neither more nor less" — not confirmed this session). [source: WP Humpty_Dumpty (date, chapter context); search]
- In the debate "Humpty-Dumptyism" names absolute intentionalism; Beardsley used Alice-vs-Humpty Dumpty against the Human Destiny sculpture (calling Humpty's position "extreme semantic conventionalism", which Wreen glosses as intentionalism); hypothetical intentionalism's "intended audience" risks sliding back into it. [source: IEP-Int §4a, §5a; SEP-Beard §9]

## 8. Barthes and Foucault (interpretive argument only; history chapter has background)
- "The Death of the Author" ("La mort de l'auteur"), 1967; first published in English in the American magazine *Aspen*, nos. 5–6 (1967); French debut in *Manteia* no. 5 (1968); collected in *Image-Music-Text* (1977), which also contains "From Work to Text". [source: WP-DotA]
- Argument (WP's summary): against criticism that relies on the author's intentions and biography to fix the "ultimate meaning" of a text; "To give a text an Author" and assign a single interpretation "is to impose a limit on that text"; a text is "a tissue [or fabric] of quotations" drawn from "innumerable centres of culture"; the author becomes a "scriptor" who "is born simultaneously with the text"; refusing a final meaning is "an anti-theological activity" because "to refuse meaning is, in the end, to refuse God and his hypostases—reason, science, law". Epigraph/example: the castrato passage in Balzac's *Sarrasine* — "Who is speaking?... We can never know." Precursors cited by Barthes: Mallarmé ("it is language that speaks"), Proust, Surrealist automatic writing, linguistics. [source: WP-DotA; translation of these fragments not identified by WP]
- Closing line, two translations (search-level verification):
  - Stephen Heath (in *Image-Music-Text*, 1977): "...the birth of the reader must be at the cost of the death of the Author." [source: search]
  - *Aspen* (1967) version, which a Wikipedia talk-page editor attributes to Richard Howard: "...the birth of the reader must be ransomed by the death of the Author." [source: search; WP Talk:The_Death_of_the_Author (weak)]
- Contrast with New Criticism (WP): New Criticism anticipated Barthes but sought more authoritative readings; Wimsatt and Beardsley's poem "is detached from the author at birth and goes about the world beyond his power to intend about it or control it. The poem belongs to the public." [source: WP-DotA quoting Wimsatt & Beardsley 1946]
- Critics: Seán Burke, *The Death and Return of the Author: Criticism and Subjectivity in Barthes, Foucault, and Derrida* (3rd ed., Edinburgh UP, 2010); Derrida's "The Deaths of Roland Barthes"; a satirical essay by "J. C. Carlier" (pseudonym of Cedric Watts), *Cambridge Quarterly* 29:4 (2000). Peter Lamarque, *The Philosophy of Literature* (2009) chs 3–4, gives an analytic critical assessment of the "author-is-dead" claim. [source: WP-DotA; IEP-Int refs]
- IEP: the analytic dominance of intentionalism "presents an interesting contrast" with post-structuralism's "author-is-dead thesis". [source: IEP-Int §7]
- Foucault, "What Is an Author?" ("Qu'est-ce qu'un auteur?"), lecture to the Société française de philosophie, 22 February 1969; *Bulletin de la Société française de philosophie* 63(3): 73–104. English translations: Donald F. Bouchard and Sherry Simon in *Language, Counter-Memory, Practice* (Cornell, 1977), 113–38; Josué V. Harari in *Textual Strategies* (Cornell, 1979), 141–60. [source: WP-WIAA]
- Author-function: the author as a function of discourse, "a classifying principle within a particular discursive formation"; it does not apply to all texts equally (a science textbook vs a novel); Foucault ties the rise of the author to legal liability for transgressive texts (punishment). Foucault did not mention Barthes, but the lecture is widely read as a response/challenge to him. [source: WP Author_function; WP-DotA; WP-WIAA]
- Quotation (Harari tr., p. 159): "The author is therefore the ideological figure by which one marks the manner in which we fear the proliferation of meaning." Also: the author "is a certain functional principle by which, in our culture, one limits, excludes, and chooses". [source: WP-WIAA]

## 9. Critical pluralism vs monism
- Michael Krausz's terms: **singularism** (an object of interpretation answers to one and only one ideal interpretation) vs **multiplism** (it may answer to more than one *opposed* interpretation); both require the rival readings to address the same object; where they address different objects one has "innocuous pluralism". Krausz argues this contest is "logically detachable" from realism vs constructivism. Anthology: Krausz (ed.), *Is There a Single Right Interpretation?* (Penn State UP, 2002). [source: WP Michael_Krausz; IEP-Int refs]
- Joseph Margolis (16 May 1924 – 8 June 2021), "radical historicist" who "elaborated a robust form of relativism" ("robust relativism"); artworks are "physically embodied, culturally emergent entities"; book *Interpretation Radical But Not Unruly* (University of California Press, 1995). [source: WP Joseph_Margolis] (His use of many-valued "plausibility" in place of truth for interpretations is NOT verified here.)
- Monism in the analytic camp: Beardsley (single correct interpretation via coherence); Nehamas's "critical monism as a regulative ideal" (1981). Pluralism: value-maximisers (Davies 2007; Goldman 2013) explicitly allow several legitimate readings; Wimsatt allowed some variation. [source: IEP-Int §2c, §3a, refs; WP William_K._Wimsatt]
- Robert Stecker's *Interpretation and Construction* (2003) addresses "incompatible interpretations". [source: IEP-Int refs]

## 10. Music: formalism vs expressiveness
- The puzzle: music is "standardly said" to be happy or sad, but "the paradigm expressers of emotions are psychological agents"; pieces and performances are not. [source: SEP-Mus §3.1]
- Eliminativism/formalism: emotional descriptions are shorthand for sonic features (Urmson 1973), "basic dynamic features" (Hanslick 1854), purely musical features (Sharpe 1982) or aesthetic properties (Zangwill 2007). Objections: no workable reduction scheme (Budd 1985); paraphrase loses what matters (Davies 1994: 153–4). [source: SEP-Mus §3.1]
- Hanslick's formula *tönend bewegte Formen*: Geoffrey Payzant (Hackett, 1986) "tonally moving forms"; Lee Rothfarb and Christoph Landerer's 2018 translation "sonically moved forms" (IEP-Hans quotes it: the content of music consists entirely of "sonically moved forms"). IEP-Hans reports an interpretive DEBATE: some (Zangwill 2004; Lippman 1992) read Hanslick as severing music from emotion; others cite his preface, where beauty rests on "the direct evidence of feeling" and his protest targets only the "mistaken intrusion of feelings in the domain of science". [source: IEP-Hans; IEP-Mus refs; search]
- Other rejected views: conventionalism (slow tempo = sad by mere convention; implausible for dirges); "language of the emotions" (Cooke 1959, Meyer 1956; Langer 1953) — a sad face, not the sentence "I am sad", is the right model; expression theory (Tolstoy, Dewey, Collingwood) — composers need not feel what they express and can fail to express what they feel; arousalism — some competent listeners are unmoved, fear needs a threatening object, circularity (defended by Matravers 1998). [source: SEP-Mus §3.1]
- **Contour/resemblance theory** — Peter Kivy (22 Oct 1934 – 6 May 2017), *The Corded Shell: Reflections on Musical Expression* (Princeton, 1980): we recognise emotion in music through similarity between musical shape and the shape of human utterance or gesture, as we see sadness in a St Bernard's face; we are "psychologically determined to animate what we perceive" (Kivy 1980: 57–9); music possesses expressive properties without expressing or arousing emotion; some cases (major/minor) explained by convention. Later "enhanced formalism". Objections: DeBellis (restates the problem); Scruton (*The Aesthetics of Music*) — confuses expression with its means. [source: IEP-Mus; SEP-Mus §3.1; WP Peter_Kivy]
- **Persona theory** — Jerrold Levinson (born 11 Jul 1948): everything resembles everything in some respect, so resemblance cannot be fundamental; music is expressive because it is "readily hearable as" an expression of emotion; the imaginative experience must include "some agent whose expression the music literally is" — an indeterminate persona (Levinson 1996a; 2006b "Musical Expressiveness as Hearability-as-expression"). Aaron Ridley similar. [source: SEP-Mus §3.1; IEP-Mus; WP Jerrold_Levinson]
- **Appearance emotionalism** — Stephen Davies (*Musical Meaning and Expression*, Cornell 1994): music is expressive in a "literal albeit secondary sense", as a weeping willow is sad (Davies 2006: 183) — "no more metaphorical than a claim that a chair has arms"; music "presents an appearance of sadness"; the listener's response is an experience of resemblance, not imagined expression; listeners may "mirror" the emotion (contagion). The label is used in Ravasio, "Appearance Emotionalism in Music: Analysis and Criticism" (2019b), who argues Davies's view predicts weaker responses than Levinson's. [source: SEP-Mus §3.1–3.2; IEP-Mus]
- Responses in the listener: Kivy (1999) says listeners confuse pleasure in beauty with felt emotion; Robinson (*Deeper than Reason*, 2005) stresses non-cognitive responses; the musical analogue of the paradox of tragedy (why seek sad music?): Levinson 1982, Walton 1990 (sadness not in itself negative), Gaut 2007. [source: SEP-Mus §3.2]

## 11. Depiction (pictures)
- **Resemblance** theories go back to Plato's *Republic* X. Goodman's attack (*Languages of Art: An Approach to a Theory of Symbols*, 1968): of "A depicts B iff A appreciably resembles B", "more error could hardly be compressed into so short a formula" (1968: 3–4); resemblance is reflexive and symmetric, representation is neither; "resemblance in any degree is no sufficient condition for representation" (1968: 4); "there is no innocent eye" (after Gombrich). SEP notes the simplistic formula "was never proposed" by Peirce or anyone else. [source: SEP-Dep §1.1; WP Languages_of_Art]
- **Goodman's conventionalism**: "A picture that represents—like a passage that describes—an object refers to and, more particularly, denotes it. Denotation is the core of representation and is independent of resemblance" (1968: 5); pictorial systems are syntactically and semantically dense and "relatively replete" (1968: 229); "Realism is relative, determined by the system of representation standard for a given culture or person at a given time" (1968: 37). [source: SEP-Dep §2; SEP-Good]
- **Seeing-in and twofoldness** (Richard Wollheim, 5 May 1923 – 2003; *Art and Its Objects*, 2nd ed. 1980; *Painting as an Art*, 1987): seeing a picture is one experience with two inseparable aspects, configurational (the marked surface) and recognitional (the depicted thing). Quotation: "when seeing-in occurs, two things happen: I am visually aware of the surface I look at, and I discern something standing out in front of, or (in certain cases) receding behind, something else" (1987: 46). Leonardo's stained wall. The bison parable: a surface represents a bison when marked with the intention that a bison be seen in it, and the intention succeeds (1987: 46). Consequences: trompe l'oeil and purely abstract paintings are not depictions. Objections listed by SEP (trompe l'oeil; standard of correctness oversimplifies intention vs content, cf. Grice; no account of point of view, Hopkins 1998). [source: SEP-Dep §3.1.1; WP Richard_Wollheim]
- **Experienced resemblance** — Christopher Peacocke (born 22 May 1950), "Depiction", *Philosophical Review* 96 (1987): 383–410: marks depict a φ iff they are designed to occupy a region of the visual field experienced as similar in shape to a region in which a φ could be presented; Robert Hopkins (1998) develops this as experienced resemblance in *outline shape*. [source: SEP-Dep §3.1.2; WP Christopher_Peacocke]
- **Make-believe** — Kendall Walton (born 1939), *Mimesis as Make-Believe: On the Foundations of the Representational Arts* (1990): pictures are props in visual games of make-believe; one imagines seeing the depicted objects and imagines one's actual seeing of the surface to be seeing them (Walton 2011: 396–7). Criticised by Wollheim (1998) and others for failing to integrate the two aspects of seeing-in. [source: SEP-Dep §3.1.2; WP Kendall_Walton]
- **Photographic transparency** — Walton, "Transparent Pictures: On the Nature of Photographic Realism", *Critical Inquiry* 11(2) (1984): 246–77: "Photographs are *transparent*. We see the world *through* them" (p. 86), as through mirrors or telescopes. SEP calls it "controversial"; Dominic Lopes (1996) extends seeing-through to all pictures. [source: SEP-Dep §3.2; WP Kendall_Walton]
- Roger Scruton, "Photography and Representation", *Critical Inquiry* 7(3) (Spring 1981): 577–603 — the "ideal photograph" stands in a merely causal (not intentional) relation to its subject, so it is not a representation in the way a painting is; critics say he neglects intentionality in photography. NOTE: Scruton's paper PRECEDES Walton's (1981 vs 1984), so present it as a related sceptical challenge, not as a reply to Walton. [source: search]

## 12. Fiction and emotion: the paradox of fiction
- Colin Radford (1935–2001), "How Can We Be Moved by the Fate of Anna Karenina?", *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society, Supplementary Volume* 49 (1975): 67–94 (part I of a symposium). Radford concludes that being moved by fiction is "irrational, incoherent, and inconsistent" (1975: 75) and that it "involves us in inconsistency and so incoherence" (1975: 78); he compares cheering at a televised game and fearing death while believing it a dreamless sleep (1975: 79). [source: SEP-Fic §4; SEP-FER §2.1; WP Colin_Radford]
- SEP's standard triad (note: SEP-FER says "this formulation is not found in Radford's original 1975 piece"):
  - (A) People experience emotions for fictional objects and situations, knowing them to be fictional.
  - (B) People do not believe that fictional objects and situations exist.
  - (C) In order to experience an emotion for an object or situation, one must believe that it exists. [source: SEP-Fic §4; SEP-FER §2]
- Map of solutions (SEP-Fic, following Levinson 1997): Irrationalist (Radford: keep all three; we are irrational); deny (A): Non-Intentionalist (moods/reflexes, Charlton 1970), Surrogate-Object (Lamarque 1981; Charlton 1984), Make-Believe (Walton); deny (B): Suspension of Disbelief (Coleridge 1817) — few accept it because we don't behave as believers; illusion theses (Carroll 2008 classifies cognitive vs perceptual); deny (C): Surrogate-Belief (Neill 1993) and the Anti-Judgmentalist/"thought theory", "possibly the most widely accepted of all". [source: SEP-Fic §4; SEP-FER §2.3]
- **Walton's quasi-emotions** — "Fearing Fictions", *Journal of Philosophy* 75(1) (1978): 5–27: Charles watches a horror film in which the Green Slime turns towards the camera; he shows the physiological marks of fear but doesn't run from the theatre. "Charles does not believe that he is in danger; so he is not afraid" (1978: 7); "fear must be accompanied by, or must involve, a belief that one is in danger" (1978: 6–7). It is fictional in his game of make-believe that he fears the slime; his actual state is "quasi-fear"; a reader likewise has quasi-pity for Anna Karenina. Walton 1997 ("Spelunking, Simulation, and Slime") clarifies. Objection: our fear at horror films feels real (racing heart, screams). [source: SEP-FER §2.2; SEP-Fic §4]
- **Thought theory** — Peter Lamarque, "How Can We Fear and Pity Fictions?", *British Journal of Aesthetics* 21(4) (1981): 291–304; Noël Carroll, *The Philosophy of Horror* (1990): emotions need only that we "mentally represent" (Lamarque) or entertain in thought (Carroll) their objects, not believe them real. Objection (Walton 1990 vs Lamarque): the objects of emotion become thought contents or nonexistent objects. Non-cognitivist route (James, Prinz, Robinson 2005): feelings/bodily changes suffice. [source: SEP-Fic §4; SEP-FER §2.4]
- Carroll's paradox of horror: "the imagery of horror fiction seems to be necessarily repulsive and, yet, the genre has no lack of consumers" (1990: 160); his answer: fascination with the imagery and plot. [source: SEP-FER §3.3]

## 13. ARGUMENT (step by step): the paradox of fiction with objection and reply
- Premises (A)–(C) above are each plausible but jointly inconsistent; Radford's conclusion: our emotional response is irrational. [source: SEP-Fic §4]
- Walton's objection (reject A): what we have are quasi-emotions; the decisive evidence is behavioural — Charles doesn't flee; real pity would prompt attempts to console. [source: SEP-Fic §4; SEP-FER §2.2]
- Reply (thought theorists, reject C instead): (C) is "a piece of articulated theory (the so-called cognitive theory of emotion) rather than a commonsense claim" (SEP-Fic, cf. Gendler 2008); one can be frightened by merely entertaining a thought (Carroll's example: thinking about the demon in *The Conjuring*); and Walton's own lack-of-behaviour criterion also explains why few accept the suspension-of-disbelief view. [source: SEP-Fic §4; SEP-FER §2.4]

## 14. The paradox of tragedy
- David Hume, "Of Tragedy", in *Four Dissertations* (London: A. Millar, 1757) (the volume also contains "Of the Standard of Taste"). [source: WP Four_Dissertations]
- Statement: "It seems an unaccountable pleasure, which the spectators of a well-written tragedy receive from sorrow, terror, anxiety, and other passions, that are in themselves disagreeable and uneasy. The more they are touched and affected, the more are they delighted with the spectacle; and as soon as the uneasy passions cease to operate, the piece is at an end." (1757 [Liberty Fund 1987: 216]); audiences are "pleased in proportion as they are afflicted" (217). [source: SEP-Trag intro, §1; SEP-FER §3.3]
- The puzzle is older (Aristotle's *Poetics*) and recurs in Burke 1757, Batteux 1746, Kant, Schopenhauer, Nietzsche; contemporary framing comes from Hume. [source: SEP-Trag intro]
- Hume's answer, "conversion": the melancholy passions are "converted into pleasure" by eloquence and artistry (1757 [1987: 220]); example Cicero's eloquent account of the crucified Sicilian captains (Verres). Hume also considers and adapts Dubos (relief from "indolence"); principle borrowed from the *Treatise* (T 419): a subordinate passion is converted into the predominant one. Objection (SEP-Trag §4): there are not two stages; we only ever respond to "content-as-represented", so nothing is "converted". [source: SEP-Trag §4; SEP-HumeAes]
- Other solutions: compensation (pleasure outweighs pain); Feagin (1983) meta-response — pleasure in being the kind of person who is moved; Walton — sadness is not itself negative, the situation is; Gaut 2007. [source: SEP-FER §3.3; SEP-Mus §3.2; SEP-Trag contents]

## 15. Imaginative resistance
- Hume's original remark ("Of the Standard of Taste", 1757, paras. 32–33): "where vicious manners are described, without being marked with the proper characters of blame and disapprobation; this must be allowed to disfigure the poem, and to be a real deformity. I cannot, nor is it proper that I should, enter into such sentiments..." Speculative errors in old works "detract but little" from their value, but "a very violent effort is requisite to change our judgment of manners". Contrast: we easily accept "princesses carrying water from the spring, and kings and heroes dressing their own victuals". Walton credits Hume as the first to diagnose the phenomenon. [source: SEP-FER §3.1; SEP-IR §1]
- Tamar Szabó Gendler (born 1965), "The Puzzle of Imaginative Resistance", *Journal of Philosophy* 97(2) (2000): 55–81: "the puzzle of explaining our comparative difficulty in imagining fictional worlds that we take to be morally deviant" (2000: 56). Authors may make cars fly and mice talk, but not make female infanticide or slavery right. [source: SEP-IR §1; WP Tamar_Gendler]
- Standard examples: Walton (1994): "In killing her baby, Giselda did the right thing; after all, it was a girl"; Moran (1994: 95): an alternative *Macbeth* in which Duncan's murder is unfortunate only for interfering with Macbeth's sleep. [source: SEP-IR intro, §2.1.1]
- Puzzles distinguished (Walton 2006; Weatherson 2004): aesthetic, fictionality, imaginative (and phenomenological). [source: SEP-FER §3.1; SEP-IR §1]
- Rival explanations: "cantian" (we can't — Walton 1994: moral facts depend on natural facts and we cannot imagine the dependence otherwise), "wontian" (we won't — Gendler 2000: impossibilities are imaginable, cf. her story "The Tower of Goldbach" in which 12 both is and isn't 5+7; resistance arises in realist, "non-distorting" fictions where we import and export truths), and eliminativist. Gendler was criticised for conflating the imaginative and fictionality puzzles and revised her view (2006). [source: SEP-IR §2]

## 16. Art and morality
- Taxonomy (IEP-Eth): **radical moralism** (aesthetic value determined by/reduced to moral value — Tolstoy; Bourdieu's "popular aesthetic"); **radical autonomism**/aestheticism ("art for art's sake"; Oscar Wilde's Preface to *The Picture of Dorian Gray*: "Life is the solvent that breaks up art, the enemy that lays waste her house"); **moderate autonomism** (J. Anderson and J. Dean, "Moderate Autonomism", *BJA* 38(2), 1998: moral evaluation of art is legitimate but moral merits/defects are never aesthetic ones); **moderate moralism** (Carroll); **ethicism** (Gaut). Revived partly by the *Philosophy and Literature* symposium of 1997–8 (Posner, "Against Ethical Criticism" 1997; Nussbaum; Booth). [source: IEP-Eth §§1–3]
- **Moderate moralism**: Noël Carroll, "Moderate Moralism", *British Journal of Aesthetics* 36(3) (1996): "some works of art may be evaluated morally (contra radical autonomism) and that sometimes the moral defects and/or merits of a work may figure in the aesthetic evaluation of the work" (p. 236). Narrative art is incomplete and relies on "activating the moral powers of readers, viewers and listeners" (p. 228). The "common reason" argument: sometimes the same feature that makes a work morally flawed blocks the uptake (pity, amusement) the work aims at, so is also an aesthetic flaw. Carroll's image: the sensitive viewer "can't depress the accelerator because it is jammed" (2000: 378). [source: IEP-Eth §3]
- **Triumph of the Will** example (handle carefully): Mary Devereaux, "Beauty and Evil: The Case of Leni Riefenstahl's *Triumph of the Will*", in Levinson (ed.), *Aesthetics and Ethics* (CUP, 1998): the film is morally problematic because it presents the Nazi regime as appealing; a morally sensitive viewer can admire its formal features (innovative camera work) but cannot fully engage with its central vision, the glorification of Hitler — so on Carroll's view the moral and aesthetic defects share a common reason. [source: IEP-Eth §3c]
  - Facts: *Triumph des Willens*, 1935, directed, produced, edited and co-written by Leni Riefenstahl (22 Aug 1902 – 8 Sep 2003); commissioned by Hitler; records the 1934 Nazi Party Congress at Nuremberg (attended by more than 700,000 supporters); premiered 28 March 1935, Ufa-Palast am Zoo, Berlin; won a Gold Medal at the 1935 Venice festival and awards in Germany, France and Italy; known for moving cameras, aerial photography, long lenses. Riefenstahl later claimed "I just observed and tried to film it well". Roger Ebert first called it "by general consent [one] of the best documentaries ever made" posing "a classic question of the contest between art and morality", and later reversed himself ("a terrible film, paralyzingly dull"). [source: WP-TotW; WP Leni_Riefenstahl]
- **Ethicism**: Berys Gaut, "The Ethical Criticism of Art", in Levinson (ed.), *Aesthetics and Ethics* (1998): "the ethical assessment of attitudes manifested by works of art is a legitimate aspect of the aesthetic evaluation of those works, such that, if a work manifests ethically reprehensible attitudes, it is to that extent aesthetically defective, and if a work manifest[s] ethically commendable attitudes, it is to that extent aesthetically meritorious" (p. 182). It is a *pro tanto* principle; manifesting good attitudes is neither necessary nor sufficient for aesthetic goodness; there are "a plurality of aesthetic values"; it does not entail the causal thesis that good art improves people (p. 184). Gaut later wrote *Art, Emotion and Ethics* (OUP, 2007). [source: IEP-Eth §4a; WP Berys_Gaut]
- Difference: Carroll says "sometimes"; Gaut always (pro tanto). Carroll (1998a: 419): "my case is more limited in scope than Gaut's". Conolly (2000, *BJA* 40:3) argues that moderate moralism, relying on an ideal sensitive audience, collapses into ethicism. [source: IEP-Eth §4]
- **Immoralism**: moral flaws can sometimes be aesthetic/artistic merits. Daniel Jacobson, "In Praise of Immoral Art", *Philosophical Topics* 25 (1997): 155–99 (an anti-theoretical, case-by-case view); Matthew Kieran (born 19 Dec 1968), "Forbidden Knowledge: The Challenge of Immoralism", in Bermúdez and Gardner (eds), *Art and Morality* (Routledge, 2003) — "cognitive immoralism": a moral defect can be a merit when it yields understanding (e.g. of a morally repugnant perspective); A. W. Eaton, "Robust Immoralism", *JAAC* 70(3) (2012): a lack of imaginative resistance to morally deviant scenarios can be an aesthetic achievement. [source: search (Jacobson, Kieran); SEP-IR §1 (Eaton); WP Matthew_Kieran] Kieran earlier proposed "most moderate moralism" ("In Defence of the Ethical Evaluation of Art", *BJA* 41(1), 2001). [source: IEP-Eth refs]
- SEP-Erot lists the field's positions as "autonomism, ethicism, immoralism, or contextualism". [source: SEP-Erot]
- Plato (cross-ref): the *Republic*'s first discussion of poetry "censors poems that corrupt the young" (377b–398b): stories that gods quarrel (378c) or that Cronus castrated his father; gods are good and should not be said to cause harm (379b). [source: SEP-PlAes]
- Tolstoy (cross-ref): IEP-Eth classes him as a radical moralist: "The inaccuracy of all these definitions arises from the fact that in them all … the object considered is the pleasure art may give, and not the purpose it may serve in the life of man and of humanity." [source: IEP-Eth §2]

## 17. ARGUMENT (step by step): Gaut's merited-response argument for ethicism
- As numbered by IEP-Eth from Gaut (in Levinson 2000 reprint, pp. 195–6):
  1. A work's manifestation of an attitude is a matter of the work's prescribing certain responses toward the events described.
  2. If those responses are unmerited, because unethical, we have reason not to respond in the way prescribed.
  3. Our having reason not to respond in the way prescribed is a failure of the work.
  4. What responses the work prescribes is of aesthetic relevance.
  5. So the fact that we have reason not to respond in the way prescribed is an aesthetic failure of the work, that is to say, is an aesthetic defect.
  6. So a work's manifestation of ethically bad attitudes is an aesthetic defect in it.
  7. Mutatis mutandis for merits. 8. So ethicism is true. [source: IEP-Eth §4a]
- Objection (Carroll 2000: 375–6, by analogy with immoral jokes): if "unmerited" means "unwarranted", it is false that every immoral prescribed response is unwarranted — a joke's structure can warrant amusement that is immoral; if it means "morally unmerited", the argument begs the question; so "not all ethically unmerited responses to artworks are unmerited aesthetically". [source: IEP-Eth §4b]
- Reply (Peek, IEP): an ideally moral audience would not find a racist joke funny any more than it would find *Triumph of the Will* engaging; and laughing at a joke is not judging an artwork aesthetically good (we can enjoy a "bad film" knowing it is bad). [source: IEP-Eth §4b]

## 18. Non-Western interpretive tradition: *dhvani* (suggestion)
- Ānandavardhana (c. 820 – 890 CE per Wikipedia; SEP "ca 9th century CE"), Kashmiri court poet and critic in the reign of King Avantivarman, author of the *Dhvanyāloka* (*Light on Suggestion*). Abhinavagupta (c. 950 – 1016 CE) wrote the commentary *Locana* (*The Eye*). [source: WP-Anand; SEP-LitInd §1, §3]
- Before the *Dhvanyāloka* Sanskrit poetics (*alaṅkāra*, "ornamentation") mostly classified figures of speech; after it, drawing on Mīmāṃsā, it theorised meaning itself. [source: SEP-LitInd §1]
- Thesis: a new linguistic capacity, suggestion (*dhvani* or *vyañjanā*), beyond primary (denoted) meaning and secondary meaning (indication); its ultimate aim in poetry is to suggest *rasa* (rasa-dhvani). Wikipedia: *dhvani* is the "soul" (*ātman*) of poetry. Ānandavardhana divides suggested content into implied meanings, implied figures, and moods (*rasa*). [source: SEP-LitInd §3, §3.1; WP-Anand]
- Example: "The village is on the Ganges": secondary meaning repairs the absurd literal reading (on the *bank*); suggestion adds purity and coolness. Key difference: secondary meaning requires a failure of semantic fit; suggestion does not. Primary meaning is a fixed "natural relation"; suggestion an "artificial relation", not one-to-one. [source: SEP-LitInd §3.1]
- Intention: Ānandavardhana subdivides suggestion by the speaker's intention (intending primary meaning plus suggestion, e.g. a love poem by Dharmakīrti; or only the suggested meaning, e.g. a *Rāmāyaṇa* line where the moon is "like a mirror blinded by breath") and by the hearer's experience (instantaneous vs after a "reverberation"). Abhinavagupta counts 35 types; Ānandavardhana says combinations are endless. SEP compares Pound's "In a Station of the Metro". [source: SEP-LitInd §3.2]
- For Ānandavardhana *rasa* is in the text (meaning), not the reader; after Abhinavagupta the emphasis shifts to the reader's experience. Critics: Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka (c. 900) — rasa is an experiential event, not meaning; Mahima Bhaṭṭa (c. 1025) — suggestion reduces to inference. The Nyāya philosopher Jayanta Bhaṭṭa disparaged suggestion in passing. [source: SEP-LitInd §3, §3.3]
- Translation: *The Dhvanyāloka of Ānandavardhana with the Locana of Abhinavagupta*, tr. Daniel H. H. Ingalls (with J. M. Masson and M. V. Patwardhan per search), Harvard Oriental Series 49 (Harvard UP, 1990). P. V. Kane: "an epoch-making work in the history of Alaṅkāra literature", comparable to Pāṇini in grammar. [source: SEP-LitInd bibliography; WP-Anand; search for co-translators]
