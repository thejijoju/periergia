## 0. Definitions to keep IDENTICAL with *Arguments: Validity, Soundness and Form*

- Validity (IEP "Validity and Soundness", as quoted in the published Arguments chapter): "A deductive argument is said to be valid if and only if it takes a form that makes it impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion nevertheless to be false." Soundness: "A deductive argument is sound if and only if it is both valid, and all of its premises are actually true." [source: published chapter `supabase/content/philosophy--logic-and-reasoning--arguments.sql`; IEP https://iep.utm.edu/val-snd/]
- Model-theoretic version, already quoted in Arguments and attributed to "Stewart Shapiro and Teresa Kouri Kissel": "An argument is valid if there is no interpretation (in the semantics) in which its premises are all true and its conclusion false. This reflects the longstanding view that a valid argument is truth-preserving." [source: SEP-CL §1]
- Propositional (truth-table) version, consistent with the above: "a wff β is said to be a logical consequence of a set of wffs α1, α2, ..., αn, if and only if there is no truth-value assignment to the statement letters making up these wffs that makes all of α1, α2, ..., αn true but does not make β true. An argument is logically valid if and only if its conclusion is a logical consequence of its premises." IEP-PL itself points to the IEP "Validity and Soundness" article for the general definition. [source: IEP-PL §III]
- The Arguments chapter already covers: modus ponens, modus tollens, hypothetical and disjunctive syllogism, the dilemmas, affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent, the counterexample method, the Stoic indemonstrables, Philo's conditional, the "paradoxes" of material implication, explosion, relevance logic, the Wason task. This chapter should USE these names without redefining them at length and should not repeat the paradoxes (green cheese/Ecuador, MacColl, Lewis's derivation of explosion). Its own material-conditional section should instead take up the Hook-versus-Arrow debate (§3 below), which the Arguments chapter does not cover.

## 1. Propositional logic: atoms, connectives, truth tables

- Propositional logic, "also known as sentential logic and statement logic, is the branch of logic that studies ways of joining and/or modifying entire propositions, statements or sentences to form more complicated propositions"; it "treats simple statements as indivisible wholes", represented by capital letters ('P', 'Q'). [source: IEP-PL intro, §II]
- Truth-functionality: "A logical operator is said to be truth-functional if the truth-values ... of the statements it is used to construct always depend entirely on the truth or falsity of the statements from which they are constructed." "and", "or", "not" are "(at least arguably) truth-functional". Non-example: "Someone is reading an article in a philosophy encyclopedia" is true but "Necessarily, someone is reading an article in a philosophy encyclopedia" is false, so "necessarily" is not truth-functional. [source: IEP-PL §I]
- Classical ("bivalent") truth-functional propositional logic assumes only two truth values, true and false. [source: IEP-PL §I]
- The five connectives of IEP-PL's language: ∧ (and), ∨ (or), → (if... then...), ↔ (if and only if), ¬ (not); they correspond "respectively, to the truth-functions of conjunction, disjunction, material implication, material equivalence, and negation". "the correspondence is really only rough, because the operators of PL are considered to be entirely truth-functional, whereas their English counterparts are not always used truth-functionally." [source: IEP-PL §III]
- Truth conditions (IEP-PL): conjunction true iff both conjuncts true; disjunction "true if either α is true or β is true, or both ... and is false only if both α and β are false"; material implication "false if α is true and β is false, and is true if either α is false or β is true (or both)"; biconditional true iff both sides have the same truth value; negation reverses the value. Terms: conjuncts, disjuncts, antecedent, consequent. [source: IEP-PL §III]
- Standard truth tables (constructed from those clauses; T = true, F = false):
  - ¬P: P=T → F; P=F → T.
  - Rows P,Q = TT / TF / FT / FF: P∧Q = T F F F; P∨Q = T T T F; P→Q = T F T T; P↔Q = T F F T.
  [source: IEP-PL §III (clauses quoted above); SEP-Cond §2.1 gives the → column in the same order]
- Biconditional oddity: "we would not normally regard the statement 'Al Gore is the President of the United States in 2004 if and only if Bob Dole is the President of the United States in 2004' as true simply because both simpler statements happen to be false." [source: IEP-PL §III]
- Number of rows: a formula with n atoms has a truth table "with exactly 2^n rows"; completing it "is therefore a finite determinate task". [source: SEP-PL §2.1.5] IEP-PL: an argument with six letters needs 64 rows; its example table "would have 22 columns, thereby requiring 1,408 distinct T/F calculations". [source: IEP-PL §IV]
- Main connective / scope via parentheses: IEP-PL's example contrasts "Paris is the most important city in France if and only if Paris is the capital of France and Paris has a population of over two million" read two ways (↔ main vs ∧ main): "The difference between the two is subtle, but important logically." [source: IEP-PL §II]
- Expressive completeness: {∨, ¬} and {∧, ¬} are each "truth-functionally complete" (e.g., A∧B is equivalent to ¬(¬A∨¬B)). "In 1913, Henry Sheffer rediscovered an unpublished observation of Charles Sanders Peirce" that "not both ... and" and "neither ... nor" each alone suffice. [source: SEP-PL §2.1.2] IEP-PL: single-connective definability "was first published by American logician H. M. Sheffer in 1913, though American logician C. S. Peirce (1839-1914) seems to have discovered this decades earlier"; Nicod (1917) axiomatised propositional logic with the Sheffer stroke, a single axiom schema and a single rule. [source: IEP-PL §I]
- De Morgan's laws: "not (A or B) = (not A) and (not B)" (and dually). Named after Augustus De Morgan, whose first explicit statement is in *Formal Logic* (1847); "a similar observation was made by Aristotle, and was known to Greek and Medieval logicians", e.g. Ockham and Buridan. [source: WP-De_Morgan's_laws]

## 2. Tautology, contradiction, contingency; consistency, equivalence; the truth-table test

- "Definition: a wff is a tautology if and only if it is true for all possible truth-value assignments to the statement letters making it up." Example: P ∨ ¬P. A less obvious one: R → ((P → Q) ∨ ¬(R → Q)). [source: IEP-PL §IV]
- "Definition: a wff is a self-contradiction if and only if it is false for all possible truth-value assignments". Examples: P ∧ ¬P; less obvious: ¬(P → Q) ∧ ¬(Q → P). [source: IEP-PL §IV]
- "A statement that is neither self-contradictory nor tautological is called a contingent statement." [source: IEP-PL §IV]
- Two wffs are consistent iff at least one assignment makes both true, inconsistent iff none does; logically equivalent iff they agree on every assignment (example: ¬P → ¬Q and ¬(Q ∧ ¬P)). [source: IEP-PL §IV]
- Truth-table validity test (IEP-PL worked example): premises P → Q, ¬Q → P; conclusion Q. Both premises are true only in the rows (P true, Q true) and (P false, Q true), and Q is true in both; "It is possible for the conclusion to be false, but only if one of the premises is false as well" — valid. Invalid example: P → Q; therefore ¬Q ∨ ¬P: on the row P, Q both true the premise is true and the conclusion false. [source: IEP-PL §IV]
- Writer's worked example (derived; uses only the above truth conditions): modus ponens is valid because the only row in which both P→Q and P are true is P=T, Q=T, where Q is true; affirming the consequent (P→Q, Q, so P) is invalid because the row P=F, Q=T makes both premises true and the conclusion false. [derived from IEP-PL truth conditions; the invalidity of affirming the consequent is in the published Arguments chapter]
- Connection: in classical propositional logic, S ∪ {A} ⊨ B iff S ⊨ A ⊃ B ("readily verifiable with a truth table"); so A ⊨ B exactly when A ⊃ B is a tautology. Franks: "A⊃B does not express any implication relation between A and B, but the claim that A⊃B is a classical propositional validity is such an expression of implication." [source: SEP-PL §2.1.4] (Use this to explain why an argument is valid iff its "corresponding conditional" is a tautology.)
- Decidability: "Classical propositional logic is readily seen to be decidable": enumerate the 2^n rows. [source: SEP-PL §2.1.5] IEP-PL: truth tables "provide an effective procedure for determining the logical truth, or tautologyhood of any single wff, and for determining the logical validity of any argument"; "In sum, classical propositional logic is decidable." [source: IEP-PL §IV]
- But not efficiently: the satisfiability problem (SAT) is NP-complete; no known procedure answers in a number of steps polynomially bounded by formula size. [source: SEP-PL §2.1.6]

## 3. The material conditional (cross-ref Arguments; do not repeat the paradoxes)

- "If A, B" on the truth-functional account "is false when A is true and B is false. In all other cases, 'If A, B' is true. It is thus equivalent to '~(A & ~B)' and to '~A or B'." Edgington: "It is the first theory of conditionals which students encounter. Typically, it does not strike students as obviously correct. It is logic's first surprise." [source: SEP-Cond §2.1]
- History: the truth-functional conditional "was integral to Frege's new logic (1879). It was taken up enthusiastically by Russell (who called it 'material implication'), Wittgenstein in the Tractatus, and the logical positivists, and it is now found in every logic text." [source: SEP-Cond §2.1] SEP-PL: the claim is "apparently ancient, attributed to Philo" (SEP-PL's text says "Philo of Melaga" — evidently a typo for Philo of Megara/the Dialectician; the *Hellenistic Philosophy* chapter and SEP logic-ancient call him Diodorus's pupil Philo) and "defended in a modern context by Charles Peirce, H. P. Grice, and Frank Jackson. But its detractors have been many." [source: SEP-PL §2.1.4]
- Why this truth function if any: "of the sixteen possible truth-functions of A and B, it is the only serious candidate": the TF row must be false (else modus ponens would be invalid), and each of TT, FT, FF is sometimes true ("If it's a square, it has four sides", said of an unseen figure, true whether it is a square, rectangle or triangle), so if "if" is truth-functional these rows must always be true. [source: SEP-Cond §2.1]
- IEP-PL's illustration that English "if" is not fully truth-functional: "If Bob Dole is president of the United States in 2004, then the president ... is a member of the Republican party" (tempting: true) vs the same with Al Gore (tempting: false) — both have false antecedent and true consequent. [source: IEP-PL §III]
- Frege's caution: he used the connective but rejected the idea that it expressed the natural-language indicative: "the causal connection implicit in the word 'if' ... is not expressed by our symbols" (Frege 1972: 116, i.e. Bynum's translation *Conceptual Notation*). "It is a matter of ongoing debate" whether Frege thought it failed even to capture truth conditions. [source: SEP-PL §2.1.4]
- Ramsey / Lewis / Edgington: Frank Ramsey suggested "If A, then B" be understood via conditional probability; "In 1976 David Lewis proved that the conditional expressions, so understood, not only fail to be truth-functional but in fact are not even propositions"; Edgington (1995) gave a simple argument for Lewis's result. [source: SEP-PL §2.1.4] Ernest Adams (1965, 1966, 1975) developed a probabilistic theory of validity for conditionals. [source: SEP-Cond]
- Grice's defence (in "Logic and Conversation", William James lectures 1967): the conditional, like the disjunction "He is either in the pub or in the library" said by someone who knows he is in the pub, can be true but misleadingly asserted. Lewis (1976) example: "You won't eat those and live", said of wholesome mushrooms. [source: SEP-Cond §2.4]
- Material implication vs strict implication: C. I. Lewis's entailment/strict implication says A and ¬B cannot occur together, ¬◇(A ∧ ¬B), stronger than material ¬(A ∧ ¬B). [source: IEP-Mod §1; WP-C._I._Lewis (definition from Lewis & Langford 1932, p. 124)]

## 4. Translation from English

- "only if": SEP-CL: (θ → ψ) "can be read 'if θ then ψ' or 'θ only if ψ'". [source: SEP-CL §2] "P only if Q" says Q is a necessary condition for P; it is equivalent to "if P then Q" and to "P cannot be true unless Q is true". [source: WP-Necessity_and_sufficiency]
- "if and only if" ↔; some authors define (θ ↔ ψ) as ((θ → ψ) & (ψ → θ)). [source: SEP-CL §2]
- "unless": textbook practice renders "p unless q" as "p if not q" (¬q → p), or equivalently as the disjunction p ∨ q; "a disjunction will always capture the truth functional meaning of 'unless'". [source: WS-1 (course handouts, e.g. https://davidsanson.com/logic/supplements/2-english_equivalents.html; weaker source); the equivalence ¬q → p ≡ q ∨ p follows from IEP-PL's truth conditions]
- Inclusive vs exclusive "or": "the sign '∨' is used for disjunction in the inclusive sense". Exclusive example: a parent to a child: "Either we can buy the toy robot, or we can buy the toy truck; you must choose!"; inclusive example: "either she studied hard, or she is extremely bright". Exclusive "or" is expressible: ¬(α ↔ β) "is true if either α or β is true but not both". [source: IEP-PL §III]
- Linguistics: natural-language disjunction is "often understood exclusively" ("Mary is eating an apple or a pear"); Tarski suggested an ambiguity; "More recent work in pragmatics has shown that this inference can be derived as a conversational implicature on the basis of a semantic denotation which behaves classically"; Hungarian *vagy... vagy* and French *soit... soit* have been argued to be inherently exclusive. [source: WP-Logical_disjunction §Semantics] ⟨and, or⟩ is listed among Gricean scalar pairs. [source: WP-Implicature]
- "but": "Donovan is poor *but* happy" has the same truth conditions as "Donovan is poor *and* happy"; "but" adds a contrast — a *conventional implicature* (Grice). Words said to trigger conventional implicatures: "but, although, however, nevertheless, moreover, ... even, yet, still". [source: WP-Implicature] (So "but" translates as ∧.)
- "and" with time order: "He drank a bottle of vodka and fell into a stupor" implicates "and consequently"; ∧ itself is commutative. [source: WP-Implicature]
- Historical note: the Stoic "or" was exclusive; Theophrastus recognised an inclusive "or". (Already in Arguments and Hellenistic; brief mention only.) [source: Arguments research notes citing SEP logic-ancient]
- Boole's addition x + y was defined only for disjoint classes (exclusive aggregation); "Jevons, and practically all mathematical logicians after him" adopted inclusive union. [source: SEP-Boole §3.3; WP-Logical_disjunction] Peirce's first logic papers (1867) "re-interpret union or logical addition A+B so that it applies also when A and B are not disjoint". [source: SEP-FOL §2]

## 5. Notation and its variants

- IEP-PL uses ∧, ∨, →, ↔, ¬ and notes variants: "some other logical works use the signs '&' or '•' for conjunction"; "The sign '⊃' is sometimes used instead of '→' for material implication"; "The sign '≡' is sometimes used instead of '↔' for material equivalence"; "the signs '∼' or '-' are sometimes used in place of '¬'". [source: IEP-PL §III]
- SEP-CL uses "&" for conjunction and "→"; SEP-Cond and SEP-PL use "⊃" for the material conditional (Edgington: "'A ⊃ B' has, by stipulation, these truth conditions"); SEP-Mod uses "~" for negation. So the notation is not standardised even within SEP. [source: SEP-CL §2; SEP-Cond §2.1; SEP-PL §2.1.4; SEP-Mod §2]
- Origins (WP, citing Roegel's survey and primary sources): ¬ and ∧ appear in Heyting 1930; ∨ in Russell 1908; → in Hilbert 1918 (Göttingen lecture notes); ≡ in Frege 1879; Peano (1889) wrote "If A then B" as A Ɔ B (reversed C); Russell followed Peano in *Principia Mathematica* (1910–13) with A ⊃ B, and Gentzen followed Russell; Heyting first used ⊃ and later →; Bourbaki used ⇒ (1954). [source: WP-Logical_connective "History of notations"; WP-Material_conditional §History]
- Quantifier symbols: Peirce (1883, 1885) used Π and Σ and was "the first to call" it the "quantifier"; Peano (1889) introduced ∃ for the existential quantifier but no parallel universal symbol; "It seems to have been Whitehead who introduced the (x) notation in Principia, and Gentzen, in 1935, who first used the symbol ∀ in print." [source: SEP-FOL §§2, 5]
- Frege's *Begriffsschrift* used "an idiosyncratic two-dimensional notation, based on negation, material conditional and universal quantification", other connectives being defined. [source: WP-Begriffsschrift]
- Polish (prefix, Łukasiewicz) notation: operators precede operands, so no brackets are needed. Łukasiewicz (1931): "I came upon the idea of a parenthesis-free notation in 1924." In his 1951 *Aristotle's Syllogistic* he said he had used it in his logical papers since 1929. (WP's Łukasiewicz article says "around 1920" — conflicts with his own 1924; prefer 1924.) It underlies the computer "stack" idea; Burroughs B5000 (1960s) used it; reverse Polish notation is the postfix variant. [source: WP-Polish_notation; WP-Jan_Łukasiewicz] (Letters in Polish notation such as C for implication, K for conjunction: NOT verified in sources read — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Turnstiles: Γ ⊢ φ "to indicate that φ is deducible from Γ"; ⊨ for semantic consequence/satisfaction. [source: SEP-CL §3, §4]

## 6. Predicate (first-order) logic

- Why it is needed: propositional logic treats simple statements as indivisible; "Aristotle's sophisticated writings on logic dealt with the logic of categories and quantifiers such as 'all', and 'some', which are not treated in propositional logic." [source: IEP-PL §I]
- The two augmentations that produced modern logic from Boole's: "(a) the introduction, in addition to one-placed predicates ('x is mortal'), of many-placed relations ('x is the brother of y'; 'x lies between y and z'); and (b) the introduction of a notation for universal and existential quantification." De Morgan (1864) partly did (a); Peirce (1885) did (b); "Working entirely independently, Gottlob Frege carried out both steps simultaneously in his Begriffsschrift of 1879." [source: SEP-FOL §1]
- Vocabulary (SEP-CL): individual constants (like proper names), variables, predicate letters (n-place), identity sign "="; ¬, &, ∨, →; "The symbol '∀' is called a universal quantifier, and is an analogue of 'for all'"; "'∃' is called an existential quantifier, and is an analogue of 'there exists' or 'there is'". Some authors add function letters ("the father of x"). [source: SEP-CL §2]
- Free and bound variables, scope: in ∃xPxy the x is bound; θ(v|t) is "the result of substituting t for each free occurrence of v in θ". If θ is (Qx & ∃xPxy), θ(x|c) is (Qc & ∃xPxy): "The last occurrence of x is not free." [source: SEP-CL §3]
- Standard translations (SEP-Sq: "Most contemporary logic texts symbolize the traditional forms as follows"): Every S is P: ∀x(Sx → Px); No S is P: ∀x(Sx → ¬Px); Some S is P: ∃x(Sx & Px); Some S is not P: ∃x(Sx & ¬Px). [source: SEP-Sq §1.1]
- Pitfall (writer's gloss, derivable from the truth conditions): ∀x(Sx & Px) says everything is both S and P; ∃x(Sx → Px) is true if anything at all fails to be S. [derived; consistent with SEP-Sq translations]
- Existential import debate: with these translations "the relations embodied in the traditional square mostly disappear"; "Every modern logic text must address the apparent implausibility of letting 'Every S is P' be true when there are no Ss." Defence: the notation "does not claim to capture every nuance"; one can add a conjunct, ∀x(Sx → Px) & ∃xSx. According to Alonzo Church this modern view "probably originated sometime in the late nineteenth century". [source: SEP-Sq §1.1] (Cross-ref *Aristotle*: A/E/I/O.)
- Over an empty range a universally quantified formula is "vacuously true"; an existential one false. For a finite domain {a1,...,an}, ∀xPx is equivalent to the conjunction Pa1 ∧ ... ∧ Pan. [source: WP-Quantifier_(logic)] Peirce read his Π as a "(possibly infinite) conjunction" and Σ as a "(possibly infinite) sum" (disjunction). [source: SEP-FOL §2]
- "some" in logic means "at least one" and does not rule out "all"; in conversation "John ate some of the cookies" implicates (does not entail) "not all" — a scalar implicature. [source: WP-Implicature]
- Multiple quantifiers, order matters: Peirce's example: with l_ij for "i is a lover of j", Σ_i Σ_j l_ij says "somebody loves somebody", Π_i Σ_j l_ij that "everybody loves somebody". [source: SEP-FOL §2] Modern: ∀x∃yLxy ("everyone loves someone") vs ∃y∀xLxy ("someone is loved by everyone"). The quantifier-shift fallacy: ∀x∃yRxy ⊬ ∃y∀xRxy ("Every person has a woman that is their mother. Therefore, there is a woman that is the mother of every person"); the converse ∃y∀xRxy ⊢ ∀x∃yRxy is valid. [source: WP-Quantifier_shift] (The published *Aquinas* chapter uses "everyone has a mother" as the quantifier-shift charge against the Third Way — cross-reference; consistent.)
- Frege's analysis (published chapter *Frege, Russell and the Birth of Analytic Philosophy*): function and argument; "John loves Mary" to "Something loves Mary" and "John loves something" are the same move with quantifiers. Do not repeat at length; cross-ref. [source: published SQL]
- Identity: "=" treated as logical; rules (=I) "Γ ⊢ t=t" ("everything is identical to itself") and (=E): if t1 = t2, anything true of t1 is true of t2 (substitution). Opaque contexts break =E: Harry = Donald, but "Dick knows that Harry is wicked" does not yield "Dick knows that Donald is wicked"; SEP-CL's language is assumed to have no opaque contexts. [source: SEP-CL §3] (Cross-ref *Meaning and Reference* / Frege's Hesperus-Phosphorus in the Birth chapter.)
- Definite descriptions (Russell 1905, "On Denoting"; also 1919): "An F is G" = ∃x(Fx & Gx); "The F is G" = ∃x(Fx & ∀y(Fy → x=y) & Gx), i.e. (a) there is an F, (b) at most one thing is F, (c) something that is F is G (Neale's variant: everything that is F is G). [source: SEP-Desc §2] Scope: "The present king of France does not exist" has two readings — negation with wide scope (true) vs the description with wide scope (false). [source: SEP-Desc §3.1] (The Birth chapter already sets out existence/uniqueness/predication and Strawson's objection — cross-ref, don't repeat.)

## 7. Proofs: natural deduction

- Natural deduction "designates a type of logical system described initially in Gentzen (1934) and Jaśkowski (1934)"; what sets it apart is "the notion of a 'subproof' — parts of a proof in which the argumentation depends on temporary premises (hypotheses 'assumed for the sake of argument')". [source: SEP-ND intro]
- IEP-ND: ND systems are "composed of simple and self-evident inference rules based upon methods of proof and traditional ways of reasoning"; "The first formal ND systems were independently constructed in the 1930s by G. Gentzen and S. Jaśkowski and proposed as an alternative to Hilbert-style axiomatic systems." [source: IEP-ND intro]
- Dates (Gentzen): SEP-ND and IEP-ND cite Gentzen (1934), "Untersuchungen über das logische Schließen. I & II", *Mathematische Zeitschrift* 39: 176–210 and 405–431; WP cites the same parts as 1935a/1935b (volume 39 issues 2–3) and says the system was proposed "in 1933, in a dissertation delivered to the faculty of mathematical sciences of the University of Göttingen". Safe wording: "Gentzen's 'Investigations into Logical Deduction', written as his Göttingen dissertation of 1933 and published in 1934–35". English translation in *American Philosophical Quarterly* 1 (1964) and 2 (1965). [source: SEP-ND bibliography; WP-Gerhard_Gentzen; WP-Natural_deduction]
- Gentzen coined the term ("natürliches Schließen"): "Ich wollte nun zunächst einmal einen Formalismus aufstellen, der dem wirklichen Schließen möglichst nahe kommt. So ergab sich ein 'Kalkül des natürlichen Schließens'." — "First I wished to construct a formalism that comes as close as possible to actual reasoning. Thus arose a 'calculus of natural deduction'." (Gentzen 1935a p. 176, translation as given by WP). [source: WP-Natural_deduction]
- Gentzen: b. 24 Nov 1909; student of Paul Bernays at Göttingen (Weyl formal supervisor after Bernays was dismissed in 1933); assistant to Hilbert Nov 1935–1939; joined the SA (Nov 1933) and the Nazi Party (1937); arrested in Prague in May 1945, died of starvation in a Soviet-run prison camp, 4 Aug 1945. Also invented the sequent calculus (to prove the Hauptsatz/cut elimination) and gave a consistency proof for arithmetic. [source: WP-Gerhard_Gentzen; WP-Natural_deduction]
- Jaśkowski: Stanisław Jaśkowski (22 Apr 1906 Warsaw – 16 Nov 1965 Warsaw), doctoral student of Jan Łukasiewicz. "Jaśkowski was strongly influenced by Łukasiewicz, who posed on his Warsaw seminar in 1926 the following problem: how to describe, in a formally proper way, proof methods applied in practice by mathematicians. In response to this challenge Jaśkowski presented his first formulation of ND in 1927, at the First Polish Mathematical Congress in Lvov, mentioned in the Proceedings (Jaśkowski 1929)." His paper: "On the Rules of Suppositions in Formal Logic", *Studia Logica* 1 (1934): 5–32. [source: IEP-ND §1; WP-Stanisław_Jaśkowski; SEP-ND bibliography]
- Formats: Gentzen's proofs are trees (assumptions at the leaves, conclusion at the root; numerals mark discharged assumptions); Jaśkowski used nested "boxes" (in a long footnote) and a second, linear method; Fitch (1952) popularised a simplified version with vertical lines; Suppes (1957) and Lemmon (1965) introduced line-number dependency notation (Suppes–Lemmon). "Gentzen's presentation tends to be preferred by writers on technical proof theory, but Fitch's presentation by writers of elementary textbooks." Popularity came in "the 1950s and 1960s when a huge number of elementary logic textbooks were published"; Gödel and Bernays also lectured on ND in the late 1930s. Quine (1950) and some editions of Copi (1954) had flawed quantifier rules. [source: SEP-ND §§1–2, notes 1, 6; IEP-ND §4; WP-Natural_deduction] (IEP-PL gives Copi 1953 for the textbook; SEP-ND cites Copi 1954 — edition-date discrepancy, avoid the year.)
- Intro/elim pattern: Gentzen characterised "every constant by a pair of rules, in which one is the rule for introduction a formula with that constant into a proof, and the other is the rule of elimination". For classical logic Gentzen added excluded middle as an axiom; double-negation elimination works too. [source: IEP-ND §3]
- SEP-CL's rules (as stated there): (As) each premise follows from itself; (&I) from θ and ψ infer (θ & ψ); (&E) from (θ & ψ) infer θ, and ψ; (∨I) from θ infer (θ ∨ ψ); (∨E) from θ ∨ ψ, with φ derivable from θ and from ψ, infer φ; (→I) "if one deduces ψ from an assumption θ, then one can conclude that (θ → ψ)" (in some texts proved as a "deduction theorem"); (→E) from θ → ψ and θ infer ψ — "sometimes called 'modus ponens'"; (¬I) if an assumption θ yields both ψ and ¬ψ, conclude ¬θ; (DNE) from ¬¬θ infer θ — "rejected by philosophers and mathematicians who do not hold that each meaningful sentence is either true or not true. Intuitionistic logic does not sanction the inference"; (∀E) from ∀vθ infer θ(v|t) for any constant t; (∀I) from θ(v|t) infer ∀vθ "provided that t is not in Γ or θ" — the mathematician's "let n be a natural number" ... "arbitrary"; (∃I) from θ(v|t) infer ∃vθ; (∃E) from ∃vθ and a derivation of φ from θ(v|t), infer φ, provided t does not occur in φ, Γ2 or θ ("let n be such a natural number"); (=I), (=E) as above. [source: SEP-CL §3]
- SEP-CL's sample derivation of the law of excluded middle ⊢ (A ∨ ¬A) in ten steps using As, ∨I, ¬I and DNE; "It is not valid in intuitionistic logic." Explosion {θ, ¬θ} ⊢ ψ is derivable via ¬I and DNE ("ex falso quodlibet or, more colorfully, explosion"). [source: SEP-CL §3]
- IEP-PL's worked deduction ("Macavity"): premises (1) Either cat fur or dog fur was found at the scene; (2) If dog fur was found, officer Thompson had an allergy attack; (3) If cat fur was found, Macavity is responsible; (4) Thompson did not have an allergy attack. (5) Dog fur was not found (2, 4, modus tollens). (6) Cat fur was found (1, 5, disjunctive syllogism). (7) Macavity is responsible (3, 6, modus ponens). (IEP-PL's text mislabels the DS step as "step 5"; it is step 6.) IEP-PL's system follows Copi's textbook (nine rules of inference plus replacement rules, conditional and indirect proof). A truth table for its six-letter example would need 64 rows. [source: IEP-PL §§IV–V]
- Writer's worked natural-deduction proof (derived from SEP-CL's rules; suitable for the chapter): to show P → Q, ¬Q ⊢ ¬P (modus tollens from more basic rules): 1. P → Q (premise); 2. ¬Q (premise); 3. P (assumption for ¬I); 4. Q (→E, 1, 3); 5. ¬Q (reiterate 2); 6. ¬P (¬I, 3–5: assumption P led to Q and ¬Q, discharge). A second, for the conditional: P → Q, Q → R ⊢ P → R: assume P; Q by →E; R by →E; discharge to P → R by →I (hypothetical syllogism). [derived; rules from SEP-CL §3]
- What the rules "mean" (debate): Gentzen "preferred introduction rules as a kind of definition of a constant"; elimination rules are their "inversion" (Prawitz 1965's inversion principle). Links to Wittgenstein's meaning-as-use and Dummett's anti-realism (truth replaced by proof); Brandom's inferentialism. "Proof-theoretic semantics" first named 1991 (Schroeder-Heister). Prior's "tonk" (1960): tonk-I: from φ infer φ tonk ψ; tonk-E: from φ tonk ψ infer ψ — "any formula is deducible from any formula" once added. Belnap (1962): rules must be "noncreative", giving a conservative extension; "harmony" is the general name for adequacy conditions; still debated. [source: IEP-ND §10]

## 8. Truth trees / semantic tableaux (brief)

- A tableau (truth tree) applies fixed rules that break formulas down along branches; a branch "closes" when it contains a formula and its negation; if every branch closes, the tree is closed, showing the starting set is unsatisfiable (so to test an argument one starts from the premises plus the negated conclusion). [source: WP-Method_of_analytic_tableaux]
- History: Lewis Carroll's *Symbolic Logic Part II* introduced a "Method of Trees", which WP calls "the earliest modern use of a truth tree"; semantic tableaux were "invented independently by the Dutch logician Evert Willem Beth (Beth 1955), the Finnish logician and philosopher Jaakko Hintikka, and the Swedish philosopher Stig Kanger, and simplified, for classical logic, by Raymond Smullyan (Smullyan 1968, 1995)" — Smullyan's "one-sided tableaux"; Carnielli (1987) generalised to many-valued logics. Beth 1955: "Semantic entailment and formal derivability", *Mededelingen van de Koninklijke Nederlandse Akademie van Wetenschappen*. Smullyan, *First-Order Logic* (Springer, 1968). Tableaux can be seen as sequent systems upside down. [source: WP-Method_of_analytic_tableaux; WP-Raymond_Smullyan]
- Beth: 7 Jul 1908 (Almelo) – 12 Apr 1964; professor of logic in Amsterdam from 1946. Smullyan: 25 May 1919 – 6 Feb 2017. [source: WP-Evert_Willem_Beth; WP-Raymond_Smullyan] Beth also gave a semantics for intuitionistic logic (1956), with Kripke (1965). [source: SEP-Int intro]
- Peter Smith's *An Introduction to Formal Logic* first edition (2003) developed formal systems "based on the 'tree' method"; the 2nd edition (2020) switched to natural deduction. [source: WS-5]

## 9. Soundness and completeness

- Soundness: "an argument is derivable only if it is valid ... no deduction takes one from true premises to a false conclusion." Completeness: "an argument is valid only if it is derivable ... the deductive system is rich enough to provide a deduction for every valid argument. So there are enough deductions: all and only valid arguments are derivable." [source: SEP-CL §1] "one should not automatically expect the converse to soundness ... to hold"; the converses "are among the most important and influential results in mathematical logic." SEP-CL labels the completeness theorem "Gödel [1930]". [source: SEP-CL §5]
- Warning on terminology: IEP-VS (quoted in Arguments notes) notes that in mathematical logic a calculus is called "sound" if all its theorems are semantically valid — a different use from "sound argument". [source: Arguments research notes, IEP val-snd]
- Propositional completeness: Emil Post's 1921 doctoral work ("Introduction to a General Theory of Elementary Propositions", *American Journal of Mathematics* 43: 163–185) proved, among other things, that the propositional calculus of *Principia Mathematica* was complete. [source: WP-Emil_Leon_Post; IEP-PL bibliography] SEP-PL: completeness for propositional logic "describes an exact correspondence between the truth-functional and deductive frameworks"; its value is that "two vastly different conceptions of logicality—formal provability and truth-functional validity—coincide". "Post-completeness" (maximality) is a different property. [source: SEP-PL §2.2.2] Hilbert and Bernays knew a normal-form decision procedure for the propositional calculus (Bernays 1918). [source: SEP-CT §1.7]
- First-order completeness: Hilbert & Ackermann's *Grundzüge der theoretischen Logik* (1928) posed completeness of first-order logic as an open question. [source: SEP-FOL §8] Gödel proved it in his 1929 doctoral dissertation ("Über die Vollständigkeit des Logikkalküls"), published 1930 as "Die Vollständigkeit der Axiome des logischen Funktionenkalküls", *Monatshefte für Mathematik*. Henkin's simpler proof (PhD thesis; published 1949). [source: WP-Gödel's_completeness_theorem] (SEP-FOL says "1929" for the completeness theorem; SEP-CL cites "Gödel [1930]" — both right: 1929 proof, 1930 publication.)
- Consequences: compactness (a set is satisfiable if every finite subset is) and the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems (Löwenheim 1915; Skolem sharpened 1920); Lindström (1969): no logic with compactness and Löwenheim–Skolem is more expressive than first-order logic. The price: first-order logic cannot pin down a unique model even of the natural numbers; notions like finitude, countability, "natural number" cannot be expressed. [source: SEP-FOL §§7, 10; SEP-CL §§5–6]
- Gödel: b. 28 Apr 1906, Brünn (Brno); d. 14 Jan 1978, Princeton; a regular of the Vienna Circle (per published *Logical Positivism* chapter). [source: WP-Kurt_Gödel]
- Completeness vs incompleteness (key teaching point): the completeness theorem is about first-order LOGIC (every valid argument is provable); the 1931 incompleteness theorems are about formal THEORIES containing enough arithmetic. First theorem: "Any consistent formal system F within which a certain amount of elementary arithmetic can be carried out is incomplete; i.e., there are statements of the language of F which can neither be proved nor disproved in F." Second: "the consistency of F cannot be proved in F itself." (Statement incorporates Rosser's 1936 improvement.) [source: SEP-Göd §1.1]
- SEP-Göd warns: "A common misunderstanding is to interpret Gödel's first theorem as showing that there are truths that cannot be proved. This is, however, incorrect, for the incompleteness theorem does not deal with provability in any absolute sense, but only concerns derivability in some particular formal system or another." [source: SEP-Göd §1.1]
- Timeline: Gödel announced the first theorem at a roundtable on the third day of the Königsberg conference (1930) — "The announcement drew little attention apart from that of von Neumann"; the manuscript was received by *Monatshefte* on 17 Nov 1930; published 1931 as "Über formal unentscheidbare Sätze der Principia Mathematica und verwandter Systeme I". Hilbert's retirement address at the same conference ended "Wir müssen wissen. Wir werden wissen!" [source: WP-Gödel's_incompleteness_theorems] SEP-Göd: Gödel reached incompleteness while trying to advance Hilbert's programme, "and not to undermine it". [source: SEP-Göd §5]
- Cross-ref: the published Birth chapter already states the 1931 result and logicism's decline; keep consistent ("a true statement of arithmetic can be neither proved nor refuted in Peano arithmetic" is compatible, since it is relative to a system).

## 10. Decidability

- "A logic is said to be decidable if there is a reliable method for determining whether a formula is satisfiable"; propositional logic is (truth tables). [source: SEP-PL §2.1.5]
- The Entscheidungsproblem ("decision problem"): "the problem of devising an effective method for deciding whether or not a given formula—any formula—is provable in the calculus". Hilbert and Ackermann in 1928 called it for the first-order functional calculus "das Hauptproblem der mathematischen Logik" ("the main problem of mathematical logic"). Church ("A Note on the Entscheidungsproblem") and Turing ("On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem") independently showed in 1936 (Church a few months earlier) that it is unsolvable for first-order logic; "Church's proof, which made no reference to computing machines, is for that reason sometimes considered to be of less interest than Turing's." [source: SEP-CT §1.7] Turing's paper is in *Proc. London Math. Soc.* ser. 2, 42 (1936–7). [source: WP-Entscheidungsproblem]
- Fragments: the monadic fragment (one-place predicates only) is decidable (Löwenheim 1915). [source: SEP-CT §1.7]
- Semi-decidability: "first-order classical quantification theory, which Church and Turing proved to be undecidable in 1936. Here, the Completeness Theorem of Gödel 1930 is especially insightful: the set of quantificational validities is not recursive, but it is at least recursively enumerable." I.e. if an argument is valid a search will eventually find a proof, but no procedure can always say "invalid". [source: SEP-PL §2.2.2.4]
- Leibniz's dream of a calculating machine for truth is often cited as an ancestor of the problem. [source: WP-Entscheidungsproblem] (Cross-ref *Leibniz*: the published chapter discusses his 1679 number-assignment calculus and Lenzen's result.)

## 11. History: Boole, De Morgan, Peirce, Frege, Peano–Russell, the Poles

- Boole: b. 2 Nov 1815, Lincoln; shoemaker's son; schoolmaster; from 1849 first professor of mathematics at Queen's (College/University), Cork; married Mary Everest (niece of George Everest) 1855; d. 8 Dec 1864 (pneumonia), Cork. Works: *The Mathematical Analysis of Logic* (1847); *An Investigation of The Laws of Thought on Which are Founded the Mathematical Theories of Logic and Probabilities* (1854). "He revolutionized logic by applying methods from the then-emerging field of symbolic algebra to logic"; where traditional logic catalogued syllogisms, his method "provided general algorithms in an algebraic language which applied to an infinite variety of arguments of arbitrary complexity." [source: SEP-Boole intro, §1 chronology; WP-George_Boole]
- "The modern study of logic is commonly dated to 1847, with the appearance of Boole's Mathematical Analysis of Logic." [source: SEP-FOL §1]
- Boole's algebra: classes x, y, z; 1 = universe, 0 = "Nothing"; xy = intersection; x + y only for disjoint classes; the law x² = x ("idempotent law", so named by Benjamin Peirce in 1870); "No x is y" as xy = 0; reading x = 1 as "x is true" and x = 0 as "x is false" gives a propositional logic. [source: SEP-Boole §3.3; IEP-PL §I]
- "Boolean algebra" is not quite Boole's: C. S. Peirce coined "Boolian algebra" for the algebra without Boole's arithmetical scaffolding; the modern version is essentially Jevons's of 1864, "a version that Boole had rejected"; Burris and Jackson therefore call Boole's own system "Boole's algebra". The term spread via Royce and his students (Wiener, Sheffer, C. I. Lewis). [source: SEP-Boole §2]
- Stimulus: early 1847, a public dispute between De Morgan and the Scottish philosopher Sir William Hamilton (not the Irish mathematician) over "quantifying the predicate". "Some say" Boole's monograph and De Morgan's *Formal Logic* "appeared on the same day in November 1847" (SEP hedges; WP states it flatly). [source: SEP-Boole §3; WP-Augustus_De_Morgan]
- De Morgan: b. 27 Jun 1806, Madurai (Madras Presidency, India); d. 18 Mar 1871; *Formal Logic* (1847); "On the structure of the syllogism" (1846); began the logic of relations (1864 per SEP-FOL); friend of Boole from 1842 and, after Boole's death, worked to secure a pension for his family. [source: WP-Augustus_De_Morgan; SEP-FOL §1; SEP-Boole chronology]
- Peirce: first logic papers 1867; "Description of a Notation for the Logic of Relatives" (1870); 1883 note and "On the Algebra of Logic" (1885) introduced quantifier notation; §2 of the 1885 paper contains "the first explicit use of two truth values" and a decision procedure: "[T]o find whether a formula is necessarily true substitute f and v for the letters and see whether it can be supposed false by any such assignment of values" (1885: 191); he defends material implication. Ewald: Peirce in 1885 "came closer ... to isolating" first-order logic than his contemporaries. [source: SEP-FOL §2] (Published Birth chapter: Peirce and his student O. H. Mitchell independently developed quantification 1883–85; SEP: "Peirce and Frege, independently of each other, took us from the traditional Aristotelian logic to modern logic" — consistent.)
- Truth tables' history: "at least implicit" in Peirce, Jevons, Lewis Carroll, Venn and Marquand; explicit in Eugen Müller "as early as 1909"; popularised in the early 1920s "perhaps due to the combined influence of the work of Emil Post, whose 1921 work makes liberal use of them, and Ludwig Wittgenstein's 1921 Tractatus". [source: IEP-PL §I] Irving Anellis argued Peirce devised a truth-table matrix (manuscripts of 1883–84 and 1893); John Shosky in 1997 found truth-table matrices (one in Wittgenstein's hand) on the back of a typed transcript of a Russell lecture. [source: WP-Truth_table] (Consistent with *Wittgenstein* chapter: "truth-tables (of which Wittgenstein gave an early presentation)".)
- Frege: *Begriffsschrift* (1879) — the IEP-PL says "the first modern axiomatic calculus for logic"; "from Frege's axiomatization it is possible to distill the first complete axiomatization of classical truth-functional propositional logic"; he was "the first to systematically argue that all truth-functional connectives could be defined in terms of negation and the material conditional". [source: IEP-PL §I] The *Begriffsschrift* system "is essentially classical bivalent second-order logic with identity". [source: WP-Begriffsschrift]
- Schröder's three-volume *Vorlesungen über die Algebra der Logik* (from 1890) gave the Peirce notation wide circulation; Löwenheim credited it. [source: SEP-FOL §§4, 7]
- Peano (1889), "independently of Peirce and Frege", a notation for universal quantification fused with implication; he added "a range of new symbols ... influential on the work of Whitehead and Russell in Principia Mathematica", including ∃. [source: SEP-FOL §5] Russell gave an axiomatisation in "The Theory of Implication" (1906); *Principia* (1910) used disjunction and negation as primitives. [source: IEP-PL §I]
- Hilbert & Ackermann 1928 is the textbook in which first-order logic first appears as a separately studied system; first-order logic's dominance was "natural only in retrospect"; well into the 1930s Gödel, Carnap, Tarski, Church and Hilbert–Bernays still used higher-order systems. [source: SEP-FOL intro, §§8, 10]
- Łukasiewicz: b. 21 Dec 1878 Lemberg (Lviv); Twardowski's pupil; Lwów–Warsaw school; Polish minister of education 1919–20; professor at Warsaw 1920–39, twice rector; from 1946 in Dublin at de Valera's invitation; d. 13 Feb 1956 Dublin; reburied in Warsaw 22 Nov 2022. [source: WP-Jan_Łukasiewicz]
- Polish logic: IEP-PL lists Hilbert, Bernays, Tarski, Łukasiewicz, Gödel, Church in the 1920s–40s metatheory of propositional logic. [source: IEP-PL §I] Jaśkowski was Łukasiewicz's student (§7). [source: WP-Stanisław_Jaśkowski]
- Women in this story (where they actually were): Christine Ladd(-Franklin) (1847–1930) studied logic with Peirce at Johns Hopkins, published "On the Algebra of Logic" in Peirce's *Studies in Logic* (1883), completed all PhD requirements but was refused the degree as a woman until 1926 (already in the Birth and Pragmatism chapters); Olga Hahn-Neurath worked on Boolean algebra (Logical Positivism chapter); Ruth Barcan (Marcus) (1921–2012) published the first axiomatic systems of quantified modal logic, *Journal of Symbolic Logic* 1946 and 1947 (the "Barcan formula"); C. I. Lewis recognised her as the first to extend propositional modal logic to a higher-order intensional logic. [source: WP-Christine_Ladd-Franklin; WP-Ruth_Barcan_Marcus; published SQL] Contemporary: Dorothy Edgington (conditionals; SEP-Cond author), Joan Moschovakis (SEP-Int author), Susan Haack, *Deviant Logic* (CUP 1974) and *Philosophy of Logics* (CUP 1978). [source: SEP-Cond; SEP-Int; WP-Susan_Haack]

## 12. Shannon and switching circuits (global: Nakashima, Shestakov)

- Claude Shannon (30 Apr 1916 – 24 Feb 2001), BS (EE and mathematics) Michigan 1936; MIT master's thesis "A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits", written 1937, published 1938 (*Transactions of the AIEE* 57(12): 713–723); won the 1939 Alfred Noble Prize. It showed Boolean algebra could simplify relay arrangements in telephone exchanges and that relay arrangements could solve Boolean problems. [source: WP-A_Symbolic_Analysis_of_Relay_and_Switching_Circuits; WP-Claude_Shannon] (SEP-PL cites "Shannon 1940" — the MIT degree/thesis record date; say "1937 thesis, published 1938".)
- Quotations about it: Herman Goldstine (1972): "surely ... one of the most important master's theses ever written ... It helped to change digital circuit design from an art to a science." Howard Gardner: "possibly the most important, and also the most famous, master's thesis of the century" (WP gives the year as 1985 in one article and 1987 in another — omit the year). [source: WP-A_Symbolic_Analysis...; WP-Claude_Shannon]
- SEP-PL's account: sentence letters stand for circuits; conjunction for series and disjunction for parallel connection (on the convention where "true" = closed); "Shannon's thesis greatly facilitated circuit design". (SEP-PL's paragraph mixes conventions — it says 0 = closed and later writes "connect A and B in series (A∨B)", which reflects Shannon's original "hindrance" convention in which series = addition. Do not reproduce the details; say only that and/or correspond to series/parallel switches.) [source: SEP-PL §2.1.3]
- Independent work: Akira Nakashima (1908–1970, NEC engineer) "independently introduced switching circuit theory in papers from 1934 to 1936"; his approach was more grounded in existing circuit theory than Shannon's abstract one. Victor Shestakov (1907–1987), Moscow State University, discovered the Boolean interpretation of relay circuits in 1935 (Soviet logicians credit him with priority), but defended his thesis in 1938 and first published in 1941 (in Russian). [source: WP-Akira_Nakashima; WP-Victor_Shestakov; WP-A_Symbolic_Analysis...]

## 13. What classical logic does not capture (brief tour; cross-refs)

- Non-truth-functional operators: "necessarily" (§1); IEP-PL lists modal, deontic (Ernst Mally 1926), epistemic (Hintikka, early 1960s), three-valued (Łukasiewicz), relevance (Anderson and Belnap, mid-1970s), paraconsistent (da Costa, Priest and others, 1970s–80s). [source: IEP-PL §I]
- MODAL: "Modal logic is, strictly speaking, the study of the deductive behavior of the expressions 'it is necessary that' and 'it is possible that'", more broadly including logics of belief, tense and obligation. Notation □ (necessarily), ◇ (possibly), ◇A = ~□~A; system K "after Saul Kripke". [source: SEP-Mod §§1–2] C. I. Lewis (12 Apr 1883 – 3 Feb 1964) began publishing on implication in 1912 ("Implication and the Algebra of Logic", *Mind*); *A Survey of Symbolic Logic* (1918); *Symbolic Logic* with C. H. Langford (1932); systems S1–S5, S4 and S5 still central. IEP-PL dates "the first systematic inquiry into this modal propositional logic" to "the work of C. I. Lewis in 1912 and 1913". [source: WP-C._I._Lewis; IEP-PL §I]
- Possible-worlds (Kripke) semantics (gloss, not a quotation: □φ is true at a world when φ is true at every world accessible from it; IEP-Mod says the "quantifier view" of modality "has been dominant since the 1950s"); IEP-Mod: "There is a continuing historical discussion about the origins of this semantics. Often-quoted papers are Kripke 1959, 1963, but there were predecessors on the other side of the Atlantic, of which we mention Kanger 1957"; accessibility added "in the 1950s ... by Kanger, Hintikka, Kripke, and Montague". Kripke 1959: "A Completeness Theorem in Modal Logic", *JSL* 24; 1963: "Semantical Considerations on Modal Logic", *Acta Philosophica Fennica* 16. Carnap had the idea of indexing valuations to Leibnizian possible worlds; Prior and Meredith, McKinsey–Tarski and Jónsson–Tarski came close. [source: IEP-Mod §§1–2, bibliography; WP-Kripke_semantics "History"] (Cross-ref *Analytic Philosophy since 1950* — Kripke 1940–2022 — and *Possibility and Necessity*.)
- TENSE: temporal logic reads accessibility as "earlier than"; Arthur Prior's work on temporal logic (Prior 1967). [source: IEP-Mod §§1, 3]
- MANY-VALUED: rejects bivalence. Around 1910 Łukasiewicz questioned excluded middle (because of future contingents) and non-contradiction (his 1910 critique of Aristotle). "The first explicit systems of many-valued logics were published by Łukasiewicz in a short note on three-valued logic (1920 ...), in which Łukasiewicz offered to use the third value to represent modal possibility, and then by Emil Post" (1921). Peirce had earlier proposed trivalent tables in unpublished notes; MacColl (1906) and Vasiliev also challenged bivalence. [source: SEP-MV §1] DATE DISCREPANCY: WP and IEP-PL date Łukasiewicz's three-valued logic to 1917 ("introduced in 1917", "began in the work of Jan Łukasiewicz in 1917"); SEP-MV dates the first published system to 1920. Safe: "worked out from about 1917, published 1920". Motivation: Aristotle's sea battle / future contingents. [source: WP-Jan_Łukasiewicz; WP-Łukasiewicz_logic; IEP-PL §I; SEP-MV]
- Kleene's (1938, 1952) three-valued tables (truth-value gap; used by Kripke for the Liar); applications: presupposition ("Mary has stopped smoking"), vagueness/sorites ("John is old"; a range of borderline cases or degrees of truth as in fuzzy logic, Zadeh 1975). [source: SEP-MV §§1–2] (Cross-ref *Paradoxes: the Liar, the Heap and Zeno* for vagueness and the Liar.)
- INTUITIONISTIC: from Brouwer's mathematics (beginning 1907, 1908; Brouwer 1881–1966); "can be succinctly described as classical logic without the Aristotelian law of excluded middle (LEM) A ∨ ¬A or the classical law of double negation elimination ¬¬A → A", but keeping ex falso. Formalised by Heyting (1930), Gentzen (1935), Kleene (1952); Gödel (1933) proved equiconsistency with classical theories; semantics by Beth (1956) and Kripke (1965). Brouwer (1908): LEM "was abstracted from finite situations, then extended without justification to statements about infinite collections." Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov: "A ∨ B asserts that either a proof of A, or a proof of B, has been constructed." Heyting: 1898–1980. [source: SEP-Int intro, §§1–2; WP-L._E._J._Brouwer; WP-Arend_Heyting]
- PARACONSISTENT: a consequence relation is explosive if a contradiction entails anything (ECQ); "paraconsistent if it is not explosive"; classical and intuitionistic logic are explosive. Term coined by Francisco Miró Quesada at the Third Latin American Conference on Mathematical Logic, 1976. Vasil'év (from c. 1910) and Orlov (1929) were early; "The first (formal) logician to have developed paraconsistent logic was Jaśkowski in Poland" (discussive logic, 1948); independently in South America Florencio Asenjo (dissertation 1954) and Newton da Costa (1963), with a continuing school at Campinas and São Paulo; relevant logics (Smiley 1959; Anderson and Belnap); in Australia the Routleys and Priest (dialetheism). Asian logic: Dignāga (5th c.) and Dharmakīrti (7th c.) "do not embrace ECQ", though "it is difficult to make categorical statements about formal principles such as ECQ in this context". Example motivation: Bohr's inconsistent atom theory. [source: SEP-Para §§1–2] Da Costa: 16 Sep 1929 Curitiba – 16 Apr 2024. [source: WP-Newton_da_Costa] (Cross-ref Arguments chapter on relevance logic; *Logic across Traditions*.)
- NATURAL-LANGUAGE MEANING beyond truth conditions: Grice's implicature — conventional ("but") and conversational/scalar ("some" +> "not all"; "or" +> "not both"); "Logic and Conversation", William James Lectures, Harvard, 1967, published 1975 (Cole and Morgan eds., *Syntax and Semantics* 3) and in *Studies in the Way of Words* (1989). Grice: 13 Mar 1913 Birmingham – 28 Aug 1988. [source: WP-Paul_Grice; WP-Implicature; SEP-Cond §2.4] (Published *Analytic Philosophy since 1950* already has Grice 1913–1988, Harvard 1967, Cooperative Principle, "I'm low on gas" — consistent; cross-ref, don't repeat the gas example.)
- Expressive limits of first-order logic itself (§9): Barwise (1985): "As logicians, we do our subject a disservice by convincing others that logic is first-order and then convincing them that almost none of the concepts of modern mathematics can really be captured in first-order logic." [source: SEP-CL §6]

## 14. Arguments to reconstruct (step by step)

A. Is "if" the material conditional? (Hook vs Arrow; SEP-Cond §§2.1–2.4)
 1. "If A, B" is false when A is true and B false (else modus ponens fails).
 2. "If A, B" is sometimes true in each of the other three combinations (the unseen-square example).
 3. Suppose "if" is truth-functional. Then the truth value of "If A, B" is fixed by those of A and B.
 4. So it is true in all three other rows: it is the material conditional (the "only serious candidate" of 16 truth functions).
 Supporting argument (the "or-to-if" / two-balls case): knowing only that at least one of two balls is red is "enough to know that if x isn't red, y is red"; only the truth-functional reading licenses this. Second support: Conditional Proof seems sound, and "for no reading of 'if' which is stronger than the truth-functional reading is CP valid".
 Objection: on Hook, the falsity of A suffices for "If A, B": does "She didn't touch the wire" entail "If she touched the wire she got a shock"? Also IEP-PL's Dole/Gore pair; Frege's own "causal connection" caveat; and with uncertain beliefs (conditional probability, Adams; Lewis 1976 triviality) the account fits badly. Edgington argues the oddities are tolerable in mathematics but "harder to tolerate" for empirical conditionals.
 Reply: (i) when we know ¬A we have no use for "If A ..." thoughts, so the intuition is "silent"; (ii) Grice: such conditionals are true but misleading to assert (pub/library; Lewis's mushrooms); (iii) Frege's attitude: replace the "elusive" "if" by its "neat, close relative" for serious reasoning. Edgington herself judges Grice right about disjunctions but thinks compounds of conditionals tell against Hook. Status: open. [source: SEP-Cond; SEP-PL §2.1.4; IEP-PL §III]

B. Brouwer's argument against the universal validity of excluded middle (SEP-Int §1)
 1. (BHK) To assert A ∨ B is to have a proof of A or a proof of B (or a method to get one).
 2. Let A = the twin primes conjecture (∀x there is a twin-prime pair beyond x). We have neither a proof nor a refutation, and no effective method is known.
 3. So we cannot now assert A ∨ ¬A.
 4. So LEM is not a universally assertible law; it was "abstracted from finite situations, then extended without justification to ... infinite collections".
 Objection: this confuses truth with knowledge; on the classical (realist) view each sentence is true or false whether or not we can prove it (SEP-CL: DNE is rejected only by those "who do not hold that each meaningful sentence is either true or not true"); also the example depends on an unsolved problem (SEP-Int raises this objection).
 Reply: Brouwer had further, weak counterexamples not tied to a particular open problem (SEP-Int: "A number of Brouwer's original 'counterexamples' depended on..." — writer should keep to SEP's general point); Dummett's anti-realism replaces truth with proof as the key notion of meaning (IEP-ND §10). [source: SEP-Int §§1–2; SEP-CL §3; IEP-ND §10]

C. Do inference rules define the logical words? (IEP-ND §10)
 1. Gentzen: introduction rules work as definitions of a connective; elimination rules invert them.
 2. Generalised (inferentialism; meaning as use): a connective's meaning is fixed by its rules.
 Objection: Prior's "tonk" (1960): tonk-I (φ ⊢ φ tonk ψ) and tonk-E (φ tonk ψ ⊢ ψ) let anything be derived from anything; so not every rule-set defines a meaning.
 Reply: Belnap (1962): rules must be "noncreative" — adding the connective must give a conservative extension; more generally "harmony" between intro and elim rules; "Belnap's solution is not sufficient", the search for the right harmony condition continues (Schroeder-Heister). [source: IEP-ND §10]

## Debates
- Is the indicative "if" the material conditional? Hook (Frege's pragmatism, Grice, Jackson, Peirce) vs Arrow (Stalnaker; Ramsey–Adams probabilistic accounts; Lewis 1976 triviality; Edgington). [source: SEP-Cond; SEP-PL]
- Did Frege think ⊃ failed to capture the truth conditions of "if", or only its non-truth-conditional aspects? "a matter of ongoing debate". [source: SEP-PL §2.1.4]
- Existential import of "All S are P": modern translation vs traditional square; defence as convenience of notation. [source: SEP-Sq]
- Is "or" ambiguous (Tarski) or inclusive with an exclusive implicature (Gricean pragmatics)? Some constructions (Hungarian *vagy... vagy*, French *soit... soit*) argued inherently exclusive. [source: WP-Logical_disjunction]
- Excluded middle: classical vs intuitionistic (Brouwer, Heyting, Dummett). [source: SEP-Int; SEP-CL]
- Explosion: classical vs paraconsistent and relevant logics; dialetheism (true contradictions) a "small minority". [source: SEP-CL; SEP-Para]
- One true logic or many? Classical first-order as "One True Logic" (Quine 1986, Resnik, Rumfitt, Williamson) vs another single logic (Priest) vs logical pluralism (Beall and Restall 2006; Shapiro 2014) vs logical nihilism. [source: SEP-CL §6]
- Do rules define the connectives? Gentzen/Prawitz/Dummett vs tonk; Belnap; harmony. [source: IEP-ND §10]
- Origins of possible-worlds semantics: Kripke (1959, 1963) vs Kanger (1957), Hintikka, Carnap, Prior. [source: IEP-Mod; WP-Kripke_semantics]
- Priority in switching theory: Shannon vs Nakashima vs Shestakov. [source: WP articles §12]
- Who first had truth tables: Peirce (Anellis), Ladd-Franklin (WP footnote claim), Müller 1909, Post and Wittgenstein 1921. [source: IEP-PL; WP-Truth_table]
- Is first-order logic the "right" logic? "natural only in retrospect" (Ewald); Lindström's theorem vs higher-order expressive power. [source: SEP-FOL; SEP-CL §6]
