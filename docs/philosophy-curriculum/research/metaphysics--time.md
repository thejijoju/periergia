## 1. Framing the problem
- SEP-T organises the field into: fatalism; reductionism vs Platonism (relationism vs substantivalism); topology of time; McTaggart's argument; A-theory vs B-theory; presentism, eternalism, growing block; 3D/4D persistence; dynamic vs static theory; moving spotlight; time travel; time and physics; time and rationality. [source: SEP-T intro]
- SEP-T says time "is a very expansive topic that appears in many different historical and contemporary philosophical traditions", and that its own entry "largely focuses on time as discussed in the Western philosophical tradition", pointing to Mahāyāna (Nāgārjuna) and Japanese Zen (Dōgen) literatures as "another area where the reader should expect ongoing work to change the field". [source: SEP-T §13.2]
- SEP-T names women in the history of philosophy of time: Émilie du Châtelet, Anne Conway, Margaret Cavendish, and Hilda Oakeley (Oakeley 1930, "Time and the Self in McTaggart's System", *Mind* 39: 175–93, a critique of McTaggart). Emily Thomas (2015) wrote on Oakeley against McTaggart; Thomas (2023) on "Mary Calkins, Victoria Welby, and the Spatialisation of Time". [source: SEP-T §13.1, bibliography; SEP-McT bibliography]
- IEP-T: Mary Calkins (with Shadworth Hodgson and William James) believed in a "thick" present; Plato, Aristotle, Reid and Meinong in a "thin" one; the thick view "is now the more favored one by experts". [source: IEP-T §17]
- Worked example for relationism (SEP-T): could all change in the universe stop for a year ("birds froze in mid-flight")? If time without change is possible, time is independent of events. Aristotle and Leibniz: no ("reductionism/relationism"); Plato and Newton: yes ("Platonism/substantivalism/absolutism"). [source: SEP-T §2]

## 2. Augustine's puzzle (cross-ref *Augustine and Early Christian Philosophy*, which already sets out the 8-step argument; recap, do not repeat)
- The meditation on time is *Confessions* 11.17–41 in paragraph numbering (= 11.14.17–11.31.41); its context is an exegesis of Genesis and the distinction of time and eternity. [source: SEP-Aug §10]
- "What then is time? If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that asketh, I know not." — *Confessions* 11.14.17, trans. E. B. Pusey. This is the wording the published Augustine chapter uses (verified there via WS). Latin as given by a WS result: "Si nemo ex me quaerat, scio; si quaerenti explicare velim, nescio." [source: WS-1; Augustine research notes WS-4] **Use Pusey; Chadwick's wording was NOT seen.**
- Wittgenstein refers to this passage in *Philosophical Investigations* §89. [source: WS-1 (logicmuseum summary) — weak; do not lean on it]
- Time is part of creation; "there is no time when God has not yet created" (11.13.15); God is outside time (11.11.13). [source: IEP-Aug §2d]
- Pusey, 11.12.14: Augustine refuses the joking answer that before creation God "was preparing hell (saith he) for pryers into mysteries"; 11.13.15: "For there was no 'then,' when there was no time." [source: WS-2 (Pusey text reprinted at reformedperspectives.org / sacred-texts) — wording flagged as from reprints]
- The present has no extension: the present year has months past and future; the present day hours past and future; and so on (11.15.18–20). [source: IEP-Aug §2d]
- Past, future and present "exist" for us as present memory, present attention and present expectation (11.26 = 11.20.26); "phenomenal proof": measuring times, and reciting a poem known by heart, the words passing from expectation through attention to memory (11.38 = 11.28.38); time is "a distention of the soul" (11.33 = 11.26.33; 38–39), "perhaps echoing Plotinus, Enneads III.7.11.41"; "this is the closest Confessiones 11 comes to a definition of time". [source: SEP-Aug §10]
- Interpretive point: Augustine "has no intention of giving a definition of time"; outside the *Confessions* he discusses cosmic/physical time; here "the purpose of the book is ethical rather than cosmological"; it ends with Paul's "straining forward" (Phil. 3:12–14). [source: SEP-Aug §10]
- IEP-T classes Augustine as "clearly" a subjectivist ("time is nothing in reality but exists only in the mind's apprehension of that reality"). This is exactly the reading SEP-Aug and the published Augustine chapter resist. **Name it as a debate.** [source: IEP-T §10; SEP-Aug §10]

## 3. Aristotle (*Physics* IV.10–14)
- Definition: time is "just this — number of motion in respect of 'before' and 'after'" (*Physics* IV.11, 219b1–2), trans. R. P. Hardie and R. K. Gaye (Oxford *Works of Aristotle*, ed. W. D. Ross, vol. II, 1930). Hardie & Gaye also: time "is not movement, but only movement in so far as it admits of enumeration". [source: WS-3 (Elpenor text crediting Hardie & Gaye)] Wording from an online reprint; check a print copy if possible.
- IEP-T quotes (translator not named): "there is no time apart from change" (Physics ch. 11); "time is not change [itself]" because a change "may be faster or slower, but not time" (ch. 10); "time is the measure of change" (ch. 12); "neither does time exist without change" (IV.11, 218b) — "often called Aristotle's Principle". [source: IEP-T §§3, 6a]
- Aristotle asked whether time requires a mind (time as the actual counting of movement, or only its countability) and left it open. [source: IEP-T §10]
- Aristotle's argument that time has no beginning (SEP-T's reconstruction): a first moment would have to be a moment, and every moment lies between an earlier and a later period; so there is no first moment (likewise no last). [source: SEP-T §3]
- The *Physics* IV.10 chapter is headed in Hardie & Gaye's contents "Doubts about the existence of time" (218a31 per WS). [source: WS-7] The exact wording of the aporia ("one part has been and is not...") was NOT verified — see NOT VERIFIED.
- Arabic-language philosophers following Aristotle mostly defined time "as the measure of motion with respect to priority and posteriority". [source: SEP-AINP §2.5]

## 5. McTaggart's argument
- J. M. E. McTaggart (born 3 Sep 1866, London; died 1925), Trinity College Cambridge, lecturer in Moral Sciences 1897–1923; British idealist and Hegel interpreter; *The Nature of Existence* vol. 1 (1921), vol. 2 (1927, edited posthumously by C. D. Broad). [source: SEP-McT §1]
- "The Unreality of Time", *Mind* 17 (1908): 457–73 (WP) / 457–74 (IEP-Et) — page range differs by one between sources; a restated version is chapter 33 of *Nature of Existence* vol. 2 (1927). An earlier, different argument appears in *Studies in the Hegelian Dialectic* (1896) §§141–142; he wrote to Roger Fry in June 1889 of ideas about "the elimination of time". [source: SEP-McT §3; WP-The_Unreality_of_Time]
- Opening sentence: "It doubtless seems highly paradoxical to assert that Time is unreal, and that all statements which involve its reality are erroneous." [source: WS-6] (search-result level; no transcription opened)
- Definitions (*Nature of Existence* 1927, p. 10, as quoted in IEP-T): "For the sake of brevity, I shall give the name of the A series to that series of positions which runs from the far past through the near past to the present, and then from the present through the near future to the far future, or conversely. The series of positions which runs from earlier to later, or conversely, I shall call the B series." [source: IEP-T §12]
- A-properties: being past, present, future (two days future, etc.); B-relations: earlier than, simultaneous with, later than. B-relations are "fixed"; A-positions constantly change. [source: SEP-T §4; SEP-McT §3]
- **Argument (SEP-McT reconstruction):** (1) Time is real only if real change occurs. (2) Real change occurs only if the A-series exists. (3) The A-series does not exist. (4) So time is not real. [source: SEP-McT §3]
  - For (2): the only way an event can change is by moving from future to present to past; a poker hot then cold is no "real change", since it is always true that its earlier part is hotter than its later part. So McTaggart rejects Russell's account of change (*Principles of Mathematics* §442). [source: SEP-McT §3]
  - For (3): past, present and future are incompatible; yet every event has all three. Objection McTaggart anticipates: an event is not past, present and future at once, but *was* future, *is* present, *will be* past. McTaggart's reply: the extra times invoked must themselves have all three A-properties, "and so on ad infinitum": "we never resolve the original contradiction ... but, instead, merely generate an infinite regress of more and more contradictions" (SEP-T's paraphrase). [source: SEP-T §4]
  - In 1908 the attack had two prongs, circularity and vicious regress; in the 1927 version McTaggart dropped the circularity charge (treating tense as simple and indefinable) and kept the contradiction. [source: WP-The_Unreality_of_Time]
  - How best to reconstruct premise (3) "is highly contentious" (McDaniel). [source: SEP-McT §3]
- C-series: a real, non-temporal ordering underlying apparent time; "Were there to be an A-series, the conjunction of it with the C-series would yield a B-series." McTaggart called his denial of time "Hegelian rather than Kantian" because Hegel, unlike Kant, held there is an underlying reality to which apparent time corresponds. In *Nature of Existence* (chs 44–50) he made the C-series an "inclusion series". [source: SEP-McT §3]
- McTaggart's view perhaps grew from mystical experiences (SEP-McT says "perhaps"). [source: SEP-McT §3]
- **Responses:**
  - B-theorists accept McTaggart's case against the A-series (tense is contradictory) but reject premise (2), allowing change as objects having different properties at different times (Braithwaite 1928, Smart 1949, Mellor 1981 & 1998, Oaklander 1984, Le Poidevin, Dyke 2002). Dummett (1960) is listed among those who find the argument against the A-series compelling. [source: WP-The_Unreality_of_Time]
  - A-theorists reply that McTaggart misreads tensed talk: "M is present, has been future, and will be past" does not attribute all three at once (Marhenke 1935; **Broad 1938**; Mink 1960; **Prior 1967**; Christensen 1974; Lloyd 1977; Lowe 1987); and that he misdescribes how time appears (Broad 1938: 313; Prior 1967). [source: WP-The_Unreality_of_Time]
  - Ingthorsson (1998, 2016) argues the argument is not stand-alone but draws on McTaggart's earlier metaphysics (existence and reality coincide and have no degrees), which explains why A- and B-theorists talk past each other. [source: WP-The_Unreality_of_Time]
  - SEP-T: the A/B distinction "has stuck around as a way of classifying theories of change", "Though not particularly creative as names". [source: SEP-T §5]
- Dummett, "A Defense of McTaggart's Proof of the Unreality of Time", *Philosophical Review* 69 (1960): 497–504. Broad, *An Examination of McTaggart's Philosophy*, vol. I (1933), vol. II (1938), Cambridge UP. Mellor, *Real Time* (Cambridge UP, 1981); *Real Time II* (Routledge, 1998). [source: WP-The_Unreality_of_Time bibliography; SEP-T bibliography]
- Parallel worth drawing (editorial, not attributed to any scholar in the sources): the Sautrāntika regress against Sarvāstivāda "activity" (§10 below) has the same shape as McTaggart's regress. If used, present it as the chapter's own comparison.

## 6. A-theory and B-theory; Prior's "Thank goodness that's over"
- B-theory: all change can be described in before–after terms, e.g. "The leaf is red at October 7, 2019"; A-theory: some change requires tensed propositions. A-theorists cite the felt flow of time, thermodynamics, and the "temporal value asymmetry" (we care whether a horrible event is past or still future); B-theorists cite special relativity. [source: SEP-T §5]
- IEP-T lists the camps: A-theorists include A. N. Prior, E. J. Lowe, Quentin Smith; B-theorists Russell, Quine, D. H. Mellor, Nathan Oaklander; "Bertrand Russell offered the original B-theory." [source: IEP-T §12]
- Arthur Norman Prior (4 Dec 1914, Masterton, New Zealand – 6 Oct 1969) founded tense logic (1957). [source: WP-Arthur_Prior; IEP-T §16]
- Prior, "Thank Goodness That's Over", *Philosophy* 34 (128) (1959): 12–17; reprinted in *Papers in Logic and Ethics* (1976), 78–84. [source: SEP-T bibliography]
- **Prior's argument set out** (IEP-T reconstruction): after a painful event one says "Thank goodness that's over"; this cannot mean "Thank goodness the date of the conclusion of that thing is Friday, June 15, 1954", nor "...is contemporaneous with this utterance" ("Why should anyone thank goodness for that?"); the only way to make sense of the relief is that we are thankful for the A-fact that the pain is past; so the A-theory is correct. [source: IEP-T §14, quoting Prior 1959]
- **Objection/replies (B-theory):** (a) it is not improper to thank goodness for the B-fact that the pain ends before the utterance; (b) a four-dimensionalist can say it is proper to care more about later time-slices; (c) Mellor/Smart "new tenseless theory": tensed sentences cannot be translated into tenseless ones, but their truth conditions can be given tenselessly ("It is now midnight" is true iff uttered at midnight), so tensed facts are not needed (Ockham's razor). **A-theorist rejoinder:** what I am glad about is that the event has pastness; Quentin Smith says the new tenseless theory "is faced with insurmountable problems". [source: IEP-T §14]
- SEP-Pres cites Prior 1959 as "the classic statement of the 'thank goodness that's over' argument, in favor of A theories of time", and gives a related headache argument for presentism: headaches must hurt if they exist; past headaches don't hurt; so past headaches don't exist (Zimmerman 1998). [source: SEP-Pres §2]
- Indexical point: Chisholm and Prior hold "It is now midnight" has no adequate tenseless translation; John Perry, "The Problem of the Essential Indexical" (1979). [source: IEP-T §14]

## 7. Presentism, eternalism, growing block, moving spotlight
- Definitions: presentism = necessarily, only present objects exist (Socrates and future Martian outposts are not on the list of what exists); eternalism = past and future objects exist too; growing block = past and present exist, the future does not; both presentism and the growing block are A-theories. [source: SEP-T §6]
- Four combinations (SEP-TT): block universe = eternalism + B-theory; moving spotlight = eternalism + A-theory; presentism = "nowism" + A-theory; growing block = "now-and-then-ism" + A-theory. [source: SEP-TT §4]
- Two senses of "exists now": temporal-location sense ("is present") vs ontological sense (in the domain of our most unrestricted quantifiers). [source: SEP-T §6]
- Name "presentism": Robert Adams first used it in print (1986); Emily Thomas (2024) argues G. E. Moore gave the first substantial Anglophone defence in his 1910–11 lectures; Prior wrote "the present simply is the real considered in relation to two species of unreality, namely the past and the future" (1970: 245), though whether his complete view is presentism is disputed. [source: SEP-Pres §1]
- Presentism is often said to be the "intuitive"/"common sense" view (Bigelow 1996; Sider 2001; Markosian 2004...). [source: SEP-Pres §2]
- **Problems for presentism** (SEP-T): reference to non-present things ("Socrates", "the year 3000"); cross-temporal relations (Lincoln taller than Napoleon; WWII a cause of the end of the Depression); truthmakers for "there were dinosaurs"; conflict with special relativity. [source: SEP-T §6]
- Bigelow's argument from relations (1996: 37): "in order for a relation to hold between two things, both of those two things will have to exist ... relations sometimes hold between a present thing and something else which is not present. The conclusion follows ineluctably, that some things exist which are not present." Bigelow traces it to the Stoics and Epicureans. [source: SEP-Pres §4]
- **Truthmaker objection set out:** a tension between (i) presentism, (ii) there are objective truths about the past, (iii) truthmaker theory (truths are made true by some existent). Replies: (a) locate present truthmakers — Bigelow's "Lucretianism": <Curie existed> is made true by the world now instantiating the property of *having contained Marie Curie*; or abstract "ersatz times" (ersatz presentism: Bourne 2006, Crisp 2007); (b) deny truths about the past need truthmakers (Tallant 2009; Ingthorsson 2017) — "hasn't received a huge amount of support"; (c) deny that truthmaking is existence-entailing. Objection to Lucretian properties: they are primitive past-tensed properties of the present world, and SEP-Pres reports that some find such properties "wholly objectionable"; the green-banana case (the world's Lucretian properties must change as the banana ripens) is SEP-Pres's illustration of the difficulty. [source: SEP-Pres §6]
- Growing block: C. D. Broad, *Scientific Thought* (1923). Quotation: "Nothing has happened to the present by becoming past except that fresh slices of existence have been added to the total history of the world. The past is thus as real as the present. On the other hand, the essence of a present event is, not that it precedes future events, but that there is quite literally *nothing* to which it has the relation of precedence. The sum total of existence is always increasing..." (Broad, *Scientific Thought*, Routledge 2002 reprint, pp. 66–67). [source: WP-Growing_block_universe] Defenders: Michael Tooley, *Time, Tense, and Causation* (OUP 1997); Peter Forrest; George Ellis, Richard Jeffrey. [source: WP-Growing_block_universe; IEP-T §14]
- SEP-T: the "growing block" name is misleading, as the view involves no commitment to four-dimensionalism; "probably better described as the growing universe theory". [source: SEP-T §6]
- **Epistemic objection to the growing block** (Braddon-Mitchell, "How do we know it is now now?", *Analysis* 64 (2004): 199–203; also Craig Bourne, Trenton Merricks): past people in the block think their thoughts are at the edge of being and are wrong; nothing distinguishes us from them, so we cannot know it is now now. Reply: Forrest (2004, "The real but dead past", *Analysis* 64: 358–62) and Forbes (2015): the past exists but is "lifeless", consciousness exists only at the edge. [source: WP-Growing_block_universe]
- Moving spotlight: eternalist A-theory — the whole manifold exists but one region is objectively present, and which region changes; "The term 'moving spotlight theory' was coined by C.D. Broad—himself a growing blocker" (policeman's "bull's eye" metaphor). Versions: Cameron 2015 (*The Moving Spotlight*); Fine's "fragmentalism" (2005). Cost: inherits eternalism's "dinosaurs still exist" and the A-theory's complex tensed logic. [source: SEP-T §9]
- Static vs dynamic packages (SEP-T §8): Static = 4D manifold, temporal parts, no irreducible A-properties, fixed ontology, tenseless semantics, no passage ("Static Theorists ... insist that this is just a feature of consciousness"). Dynamic = the opposite. [source: SEP-T §8]
- Tim Maudlin, asked if he believes in a block universe: "In a sense I absolutely do, and in a sense I absolutely do not. I do if it means past, present, and future are equally real. But not if it means, as (Huw) Price believes, there is no objective direction of time, or if it means, say, that Einstein is not really dead now." [source: IEP-T §14]
- Link to persistence (keep consistent with *Identity, Change and Persons*): four-dimensionalism (temporal parts; you are a "spacetime worm"; film-strip image) tends to go with B-theory/eternalism; three-dimensionalism (wholly present at each moment) with A-theory/presentism; Lewis's problem of temporary intrinsics; the 3D reply uses tensed semantics. [source: SEP-T §7] IEP-T: eternalism is distinct from four-dimensionalism, though most four-dimensionalists are eternalists. [source: IEP-T §14]

## 8. Relativity against presentism (Rietdijk–Putnam–Penrose)
- C. W. Rietdijk, "A Rigorous Proof of Determinism Derived from the Special Theory of Relativity", *Philosophy of Science* 33 (Dec 1966): 341–344; Hilary Putnam, "Time and Physical Geometry", *Journal of Philosophy* 64 (1967): 240–247. Also called the Rietdijk–Putnam–Penrose argument. [source: WP-Rietdijk–Putnam_argument; SEP-BB §3]
- Relativity of simultaneity = (i) whether distant events are simultaneous depends on reference frame (empirical, e.g. Michelson–Morley) + (ii) no frame is privileged (an extra-empirical principle). A-theorists can reject (ii) "without any conflict with empirical results". [source: SEP-T §11]
- **Argument set out (IEP-Et's "classical argument"):** events A, B, C; A and B spacelike separated and simultaneous in frame 1 (AsB); A and C simultaneous in frame 2 (AsC); B and C timelike separated (one later than the other). Premise: simultaneous events are equally real (AsB → ArB; AsC → ArC). "Equally real" is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. So BrC: a later event C is as real as B. Generalising, every event is real: the block universe. [source: IEP-Et §2b]
- **Andromeda version (Penrose, *The Emperor's New Mind*, 1989, pp. 303–4):** Alice walks toward Andromeda, Bob away, at ~4 km/h; their planes of simultaneity cut Andromeda's history about 5¾ days apart (SEP-BB; IEP-Et says "about five days"); an Andromedean invasion fleet launched in between is in Alice's past and Bob's future. Penrose: "If to *either* person the decision has already been made, then surely there *cannot* be any uncertainty." [source: SEP-BB §3; WP-Rietdijk–Putnam_argument] Andromeda distance: SEP-BB "about two million light years or 2×10^19 km"; WP "over 2.5 million" light years for the light-travel time. Use "about 2.5 million light years" or avoid a figure.
- **Objections/replies:** (a) Transitivity of "equally real" is "the most controversial" premise; conventionality of simultaneity (Poincaré; Einstein; Reichenbach's ε, 1958) means distant simultaneity may be conventional, so the argument "does not even get off the ground" (Ben-Yami 2006; Dieks 2012; Rovelli 2019). [source: IEP-Et §4a] (b) Howard Stein (1968, 1991): define "has become" relative to a spacetime point as the point's past light cone; this is "a formal refutation" of the argument, but then "in Einstein-Minkowski space-time an event's present is constituted by itself alone" (Stein 1968: 15); Callender (2000) finds this too thin for genuine becoming. [source: SEP-BB §4] (c) Neo-Lorentzian interpretations (Tooley 1997; Craig 2001) are empirically equivalent but, Balashov & Janssen (2003) argue, leave Lorentz invariance an "unexplained coincidence". [source: SEP-Pres §8] (d) Prior (1970): physics shows only that "in some cases we can never know ... whether something is actually happening or merely has happened or will happen". [source: SEP-BB §3]
- Simon Saunders (2002): on presentism "special relativity is radically deficient as a description of reality". Hawley (2006): two strategies, "Undermining" and "Counterargument". [source: SEP-Pres §8]
- Gödel (1949): "The concept of existence … cannot be relativized without destroying its meaning completely"; "The existence of an objective lapse of time … means (or, at least, is equivalent to the fact) that reality consists of an infinity of layers of 'now' which come into existence successively" (Schilpp volume, p. 558). [source: SEP-BB §§2, 4]
- Einstein's letter to Besso's family (21 Mar 1955; Besso died 15 Mar 1955): "For those of us who believe in physics, the distinction between past, present, and future is only a stubbornly persistent illusion." IEP-Et warns that calling this eternalism is "to read a personal, poetic letter out of its context". [source: IEP-Et §2a]
- N. David Mermin: "That no inherent meaning can be assigned to the simultaneity of distant events is the single most important lesson to be learned from relativity." (*It's About Time*, Princeton 2021 per WP). [source: WP-Rietdijk–Putnam_argument]

## 9. Time in physics
- Newton, *Principia* (1687), Scholium after the Definitions: "Absolute, true and mathematical time, of itself, and from its own nature flows equably without regard to anything external, and by another name is called duration: relative, apparent and common time, is some sensible and external (whether accurate or unequable) measure of duration by the means of motion, which is commonly used instead of true time ..." — this is the Motte-based American edition (N. W. Chittenden, 1846) quoted in WP; SEP-Newt gives only a paraphrase ("passes equably without relation to anything external"). Name the translation (Motte 1729, as revised) if quoting. [source: WP-Absolute_space_and_time; SEP-Newt §1]
- Newton's bucket argument used effects of acceleration to argue for absolute motion (hence absolute space and time). [source: SEP-T §§2, 11]
- Leibniz–Clarke correspondence 1715–16: began with Leibniz's letter to Caroline of Ansbach, Princess of Wales; ended with Leibniz's death in 1716; Clarke published it in 1717 (*A Collection of Papers ...*, London: Knapton). Leibniz's argument (SEP-T): a world identical except shifted one second later in absolute time would violate the PSR (no reason for one over the other) and the identity of indiscernibles. [source: WP-Leibniz–Clarke_correspondence; SEP-T §2] (The published *Leibniz* chapter already quotes the Third Paper §4: "an order of coexistences, as time is an order of successions" — cross-refer.)
- Al-Rāzī (Abū Bakr, c. 864–925 or 930) posited five eternals: Creator, Universal Soul, prime matter, absolute time and absolute space; he distinguished time (relative) from duration (absolute) — an Islamic-world precursor of absolutism. [source: SEP-AINP §2.6]
- Special relativity: Einstein 1905 "On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies"; Minkowski's 1908 Cologne lecture "Space and Time" introduced four-dimensional spacetime/light-cone diagrams. [source: IEP-Et §2a]
- **Muons:** mean lifetime about 2.2 μs (2.197 μs); without time dilation, cosmic-ray muons would have a half-survival distance of only about 456 m, yet they reach the ground; at 98% of light speed their lifetime is about five times longer. Rossi and Hall (1941) compared muon counts at a mountain top and sea level — the first observation of time dilation with muons. CERN muon storage ring: γ = 29.327, lifetime dilated to 64.378 μs. [source: WP-Muon; WP-Time_dilation]
- **Hafele–Keating (October 1971):** Joseph Hafele (physicist) and Richard Keating (astronomer) flew four caesium-beam atomic clocks twice round the world on commercial airliners, eastward then westward, compared with clocks at the US Naval Observatory; published in *Science* 1972. Predicted vs measured (ns): eastward −40 ± 23 predicted, −59 ± 10 measured; westward +275 ± 21 predicted, +273 ± 7 measured. Agreement within the ~10% precision expected. Both special-relativistic (velocity) and general-relativistic (altitude) effects contributed. Funding: $8,000 from the Office of Naval Research. Repeated at higher accuracy (Maryland 1975–76; NPL 1996). GPS routinely corrects for both effects. [source: WP-Hafele–Keating_experiment]
- General relativity sometimes (not always) allows a preferred "foliation" into instants; some interpretations of quantum mechanics seem to need absolute simultaneity. [source: SEP-T §11]
- **Problem of time (hedge heavily):** quantum mechanics treats time as an external background parameter; in canonical quantum gravity the Wheeler–DeWitt equation yields a "frozen" wave function of the universe that does not evolve ("frozen formalism problem"). [source: WP-Problem_of_time] SEP-T: in some quantum-gravity approaches "there does not seem to be anything like temporal structure at the fundamental level"; "Work in this area is nascent". [source: SEP-T §11]
- Julian Barbour (born 1937), *The End of Time* (1999): "timeless physics", the "controversial view that time ... does not exist as anything other than an illusion"; criticised by Lee Smolin and Sean Carroll. [source: WP-Julian_Barbour] Carlo Rovelli, *L'ordine del tempo* (2017; English *The Order of Time*, Penguin 2018); Rovelli is "a central architect" of loop quantum gravity. [source: WP-The_Order_of_Time_(book); SEP-QG]
- Bergson vs Einstein: on 6 April 1922 Einstein, at the Société française de philosophie, replied briefly to Bergson; Bergson published *Durée et simultanéité* (1922); later reluctant to reprint it (Le Roy's report), yet in 1924 wrote that relativity "represents a very great advance, not only from the viewpoint of physics, but also from the viewpoint of philosophy". Merleau-Ponty accused Einstein of missing Bergson's point. [source: SEP-Berg §1; WP-Henri_Bergson] Book: Jimena Canales, *The Physicist and the Philosopher* (Princeton 2015). [source: WP-Henri_Bergson]

## 10. The direction of time
- Arthur Eddington coined "time's arrow" (1927; popularised in *The Nature of the Physical World*, 1928): "I shall use the phrase 'time's arrow' to express this one-way property of time which has no analogue in space." [source: WP-Arrow_of_time]
- Everyday asymmetries: "Heat flows from hot to cold. The smell of coffee spreads throughout its available volume ... The temporally reversed order of these processes never happen." SEP-Thermo also uses milk mixing in coffee. [source: SEP-Thermo intro, §1]
- Second Law first discovered by Carnot, 1824. [source: SEP-Thermo §1]
- **The puzzle:** fundamental laws are (near enough) time-reversal invariant — "no asymmetry in, no asymmetry out" — so where does thermodynamic asymmetry come from? (Weak-interaction violations of time symmetry don't appear responsible.) [source: SEP-Thermo §3]
- Boltzmann (1844–1906): entropy S = k log |Γ_M|; there are overwhelmingly more microstates corresponding to equilibrium, so a system starting in a small region of phase space almost certainly moves to larger ones. Loschmidt (1876/77; reversibility) and Zermelo (1896; recurrence) objected to his earlier H-theorem: for every entropy-increasing solution there is a mirror entropy-decreasing one. [source: SEP-Thermo §3; WP-Ludwig_Boltzmann]
- **Past Hypothesis:** the universe began in an extremely low-entropy state; Boltzmann, Einstein, Feynman and Schrödinger saw such a hypothesis was needed; David Albert, *Time and Chance* (Harvard 2000), named it the "Past Hypothesis". Boltzmann himself explained the low entropy as a fluctuation in a vastly larger universe. [source: SEP-Thermo §3.1]
- **Objections:** Penrose (1989): its probability on the standard measure is astronomically small — reply: treat it as a law (then its probability is one), or deny it needs explanation (Callender) vs Price's demand for explanation; the "subsystem" worry (Winsberg); Earman (2006): it "isn't even coherent enough to be false" (can't be stated in general relativity). [source: SEP-Thermo §3.1]
- Huw Price, *Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point* (OUP 1996): the "view from nowhen"; insists on explaining the low-entropy past. [source: SEP-Thermo §§3.1, 3.3; SEP-T bibliography]
- **Why we remember the past, not the future (records):** "our way of knowing the past is different than our way of knowing the future" (Albert 2000). A scale's reading is a record only if the scale was in its "ready state" before; "the Past Hypothesis effectively is the world's Ready State", causing macroscopic traces of the past, not the future (Albert 2000, 2015; Loewer 2012). Footprint-on-the-beach example. Uffink: "going inside a refrigerator doesn't cause us to remember the future" (the connections are subtle). Other arrows: mutability, psychological, causal/explanatory/counterfactual; Lewis (1979) grounded counterfactual asymmetry in "an alleged over-determination of traces"; Horwich, *Asymmetries in Time* (1987). [source: SEP-Thermo §4, §4.2]
- SEP-T: reducing earlier/later to entropy has the odd consequence that entropy could not have decreased over time. [source: SEP-T §11]
- IEP-T lists "mini-arrows": knowledge, thermodynamic, causal, cosmological, electromagnetic, memory, action. [source: IEP-T §15]

## 11. Time travel
- David Lewis (1941–2001), "The Paradoxes of Time Travel", *American Philosophical Quarterly* 13 (1976): 145–52. [source: SEP-TT bibliography; WP-David_Lewis_(philosopher)]
- Lewis's definition: time travel "involves a discrepancy between time and time"; distinguish "external time" from the traveller's "personal time" ("roughly, that which is measured by his wristwatch") (1976: 145–6). It has trouble with Gödel-type spacetimes, where external time "loses its grip". Alternative definition: existence of closed timelike curves (CTCs). [source: SEP-TT §1]
- **Grandfather paradox set out** (SEP-T §10): (1) If you could travel back, you could kill your grandfather before your father was conceived. (2) You could not do that (you would ensure you never existed). (3) So you cannot travel back in time. [source: SEP-T §10] Asimov (1995) and Hawking (1992, chronology protection conjecture) took such paradoxes as reason to think backward travel impossible. [source: SEP-TT §2]
- **First reply (Lewis):** the traveller fails "for some commonplace reason" (1976: 150) — the gun jams, a noise distracts her, she slips on a banana peel. So time travel does not entail impossible events. [source: SEP-TT §2]
- **Second version: "can"** — Tim, a crack shot who hates Grandfather, faces him with a loaded gun (SEP's example year: 1921). He can kill him (skills, weapon, motive) and he can't (Grandfather didn't die then). Lewis: "If a time traveler visiting the past both could and couldn't do something that would change it, then there cannot possibly be such a time traveler." (1976: 149). **Lewis's solution:** "can" means *compossible with certain facts*, and context selects which facts; relative to Tim's weapon, training and state of mind, killing is compossible; relative to the further fact that Grandfather survived 1921, it is not; "no contradiction here—merely an equivocation". Widely accepted. [source: SEP-TT §2.1]
- **Objection (Vihvelin 1996, "What time travelers cannot do", *Phil. Studies* 81):** "Tim can kill Grandfather" is simply false, since the counterfactual "If Tim had tried, he would or might have succeeded" is false at any world like ours. [source: SEP-TT §2.1]
- **Horwich's improbability objection** (*Asymmetries in Time*, 1987): consistent time travel requires extensive uncaused correlations (banana peels always present when needed); these are extremely unlikely; so time travel is extremely unlikely — not impossible. [source: SEP-TT §2.2]
- Causal loops (e.g. the time machine stolen from a museum and donated back; information with no origin): Lewis (1976: 149) accepts a loop as a whole is inexplicable but "merely strange, not impossible", like the Big Bang. Mellor (1998, ch. 12) objects that chances would not match frequencies. [source: SEP-TT §3.2]
- Presentism-based objection: if neither past nor future exists, there is nowhere to travel to. [source: SEP-T §10]
- SEP-T: consistent time-travel stories (e.g. the film *Los Cronocrímenes* / *Timecrimes*) lack the "glitchy feeling" of impossible stories. [source: SEP-T §10]
- **Gödel's universe:** Kurt Gödel, "An Example of a New Type of Cosmological Solutions of Einstein's Field Equations of Gravitation", *Reviews of Modern Physics* 21 (1949): 447–450 — a rotating, non-expanding dust universe with CTCs (so not our universe); companion paper "A Remark about the Relationship between Relativity Theory and Idealistic Philosophy" in Schilpp (ed.), *Albert Einstein: Philosopher-Scientist* (1949). Gödel and Einstein were close friends; Einstein replied that in such a case "the distinction 'earlier-later' is abandoned for world-points which lie far apart in a cosmological sense". Gödel 1949 "can well be regarded as initiating the modern academic literature on time travel". [source: SEP-TT §4; WP-Gödel_metric; SEP-TM]
- Wormhole time machines: Morris, Thorne and Yurtsever, "Wormholes, Time Machines, and the Weak Energy Condition", *Physical Review Letters* 61 (1988). [source: WP-Novikov_self-consistency_principle; SEP-TM]
- **Novikov self-consistency principle** (Igor Novikov, mid-1980s; stated in a 1990 paper with Thorne, Morris, Yurtsever and others): "the only solutions to the laws of physics that can occur locally in the real Universe are those which are globally self-consistent"; also called Larry Niven's "law of conservation of history". [source: WP-Novikov_self-consistency_principle]
- Hawking's chronology protection conjecture (*Physical Review D* 46 (1992): 603–11): "The laws of physics prevent closed timelike curves from appearing." [source: SEP-TT §2 and bibliography]

## 12. India
- **Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika:** time (*kāla*) is one of nine substances (earth, water, fire, air, ether, time, space/direction, self, mind) (already in *The Classical Indian Schools*); ether, time, space and self are substances without activity (*karma*). Qualities include *paratva* (priority) and *aparatva* (posteriority). [source: WP-Vaisheshika] Praśastapāda (5th or 6th c. CE; sources differ) gave priority/posteriority etc. as the inferential mark (*liṅga*) of *kāla*; later Vyomaśiva and Udayana formalised it; grounds of inference listed in reference works also include simultaneity/succession and quickness/slowness; time, like ether and space, is one, eternal and all-pervading (*vibhu*). [source: WS-8 — search summaries of a PhilPapers abstract, a Sage encyclopedia entry and the *Sarva-darśana-saṅgraha*; weak, flag in text or keep to one sentence]
- Nyāya's recognition argument for an enduring self ("that is the very man I saw a week ago", NS 1.1.10) — relevant as a time-based argument; already in *Classical Indian Schools* and *Identity, Change and Persons*. [source: IEP-Nyaya §5]
- **Buddhist momentariness:** Sarvāstivāda-Vaibhāṣika held a "fully-fledged doctrine of momentariness"; a moment (*kṣaṇa*) is "the smallest, definite unit of time that cannot be subdivided"; texts suggest 0.13 to 13 milliseconds in modern terms (Gethin 1998; von Rospatt 1995) — an "atomistic conception of time". [source: SEP-Abh §4]
- In the Sūtras the "three times" mean past, present and future lives; Abhidharma makes them phases each dharma undergoes every moment; time "is seen as an inherent feature of the operation of dharmas" (Ronkin compares this to a "somewhat Bergsonian sense"). [source: SEP-Abh §4]
- **"All exists" (sarvam asti):** Sarvāstivāda = "advocates of the doctrine that all things exist": dharmas exist as real entities in past, present and future. Theravāda rejected this: dhammas exist only in the present. [source: SEP-Abh §§2, 4; IEP-Sarv §2c]
- **Four arguments (Vasubandhu, *Abhidharmakośa*):** (i) scripture: the Buddha said monks could not consider the past or delight in the future if they did not exist; (ii) cognition requires a sense-object, and past/future can be objects; (iii) consciousness always has a real object; we think of last week's cake, so past dharmas exist; (iv) past actions have present results (karma), so past dharmas must exist to act. [source: IEP-Sarv §2c]
- **How do the times differ if dharmas are permanent? Four Sarvāstivāda answers** (*Mahāvibhāṣā* and *Abhidharmakośa*): Dharmatrāta — difference of *being* (gold vessel remelted); Ghoṣaka — difference of *characteristic*; Buddhadeva — difference of *relation* (a woman is daughter and mother); Vasumitra — difference of *position* according to *activity* (*kāritra*): a dharma not yet exercising its activity is future, exercising it present, having exercised it past (chess piece on a board). Vasubandhu judged Vasumitra's "the most convincing", then criticised it. Note the parallel: Buddhadeva's view resembles a B-theory and Vasubandhu's objection resembles McTaggart's (an editorial comparison). [source: IEP-Sarv §2c]
- **Sautrāntika regress objection:** either activity is separate from the dharma, in which case the activity is itself temporal and needs a further activity to fix its time, "leading to an infinite regress" (Vasubandhu, Pruden trans. p. 811); or it is not separate, in which case an ever-existing dharma always acts and the times collapse (p. 812). Sautrāntikas instead hold that basic elements (*svalakṣaṇa*) exist only momentarily and arise and cease — a Buddhist presentism. [source: IEP-Sarv §3a]
- Vasubandhu wrote the *Abhidharmakośa* verses presenting Sarvāstivāda views and a commentary often favouring Sautrāntika criticisms (consistent with *Mahāyāna Philosophy* chapter). [source: IEP-Sarv §§1, 3a]
- **Cosmic cycles (brief, hedged; this is cosmology, not metaphysics of time):** in Hindu texts (Purāṇas such as the *Viṣṇu* and *Bhāgavata*) a *kalpa* = 4.32 billion years = one day of Brahmā = 1,000 cycles of four *yuga*s (Satya/Kṛta, Tretā, Dvāpara, Kali); a night of equal length (*pralaya*); Brahmā lives 100 "years" = 311.04 trillion years. In the Rigveda *yuga* meant a generation or "yoke". [source: WP-Kalpa_(time); WP-Yuga] The Buddha gave no number but similes: a mountain of rock a league each way, rubbed once a century with Kāsi cloth, would wear away before an aeon ends (SN 15.5, trans. Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu). [source: WP-Kalpa_(time)]
- SEP-T notes "some authors have argued that thinking of time linearly is specific to the Western tradition, and perhaps even a fairly recent development within that tradition" (citing Burkhart 2025, Gorham 2012, Thomas 2023/2025, Whyte 2021 on Indigenous perspectives). Present as a claim some authors make. [source: SEP-T §3]

## 14. Islamic world
- Kalām: al-Ashʿarī lists three accounts of the moment and time: (1) time an aggregate of moments, the moment "a certain duration between one action and the next action" (possibly atomic); (2) the moment an accident (Avicenna reported this view: time a collection of such accidents); (3) most popular: the moment is a conventionally specified event ("someone's entering a house"), "'time' is just another name for the motion of the heavens". If motion is atomic, time is too. **Maimonides' claim (*Guide* I.73, premise 3) that the theologians had a single atomic theory of time "seems to be gainsaid by earlier sources"**; it is "not clear whether Muslim theologians explicitly espoused a theory of atomic time", but occasionalism (God recreates the world at each moment) "at least implicitly endorses such a view". [source: SEP-AINP §§1.2–1.3]
- Falsafa: al-Kindī "virtually alone" in affirming time's finitude; Avicenna argued time has no beginning (any first moment would be preceded by the possibility of earlier motion — a rectilinear conception); Averroes thought of time as circular, like the heavens' motion. Averroes on how we sense time: through awareness of our own change (Plato's cave prisoners still sense time). [source: SEP-AINP §2.5]
- Al-Ghazālī, *Incoherence*, First Discussion (longest): attacks Avicenna's and al-Fārābī's arguments for the world's pre-eternity; argues an eternal world is impossible, drawing on John Philoponus (c. 490–c. 570) against an infinite number of past events; condemns "the world has no beginning in the past and is not created in time" as one of three teachings amounting to unbelief. [source: SEP-Gh (section on the Incoherence)] That he held time to be created with the world (no time "before" it) is reported in WS-5 and WP-The_Incoherence_of_the_Philosophers (uncited line) — keep to "time, on his view, begins with the world" only if the cross-ref chapter supports it; better to cross-refer.

## 15. Kant, Bergson, phenomenology (brief; cross-ref)
- Kant: time and space as forms of intuition (*Kant* chapter). IEP-T: Kant said time is "the form of inner sense" and "an a priori condition of all appearance whatsoever"; IEP-T calls the "mind projects time on things" reading "a slight misinterpretation". [source: IEP-T §3] First antinomy (beginning in time vs none) is in the Kant chapter.
- Bergson: *Essai sur les données immédiates de la conscience* (1889; English *Time and Free Will*) is an attack on Kant: Kant "confused space and time"; Bergson "un-mixes" them; consciousness is duration (*la durée*), a "qualitative multiplicity" in which "several conscious states are organized into a whole, permeate one another, [and] gradually gain a richer content" (*Time and Free Will*, p. 122); quantitative multiplicity = a flock of sheep counted in homogeneous space (pp. 76–77). Sartre said this book drew him to philosophy. Nobel Prize in Literature 1927. [source: SEP-Berg §2; WP-Henri_Bergson]
- William James (*Principles of Psychology*, 1890, ch. 15) popularised "the specious present", crediting E. R. Clay; "the specious present, the intuited duration, stands permanent, like the rainbow on the waterfall". Husserl's retention/protention (already in *Phenomenology* chapter). [source: SEP-TC §2.1]
- Heidegger: *Being and Time* analyses existence in terms of temporality (already in *Phenomenology*); Watsuji's *Fūdo* (1935) said he overplayed time and underplayed space (already in *Kyoto School* / *Phenomenology*). Cross-refer only.

## Debates
- **Is McTaggart's argument against the A-series sound?** B-theorists (Dummett 1960, Mellor 1981/1998, Oaklander, Le Poidevin) accept it; A-theorists (Broad 1938, Prior 1967, Lowe 1987) say it misreads tense; Ingthorsson says it depends on McTaggart's idealist system; reconstruction is "highly contentious" (McDaniel). [source: WP-The_Unreality_of_Time; SEP-McT]
- **A vs B; presentism vs eternalism vs growing block vs spotlight** — open; SEP-T: "it is pretty clear that there are some major problems facing presentism", but presentists claim common sense. [source: SEP-T §6]
- **Does relativity refute presentism?** Putnam/Rietdijk/Penrose and Saunders yes; Stein, conventionality of simultaneity, neo-Lorentzians, Prior no; Callender: Stein's notion of becoming too thin. [source: SEP-BB; SEP-Pres §8; IEP-Et §4]
- **Is the Past Hypothesis a law, a brute fact, or in need of explanation?** Callender vs Price; Earman doubts it is coherent. [source: SEP-Thermo §3.1]
- **Is Augustine a subjectivist about time?** IEP-T says yes; SEP-Aug and the Augustine chapter say the book is ethical/first-personal. [source: IEP-T §10; SEP-Aug §10]
- **Can Tim kill Grandfather?** Lewis (contextual "can") vs Vihvelin (no). [source: SEP-TT §2.1]
- **Did the kalām theologians hold atomic time?** Maimonides said yes; McGinnis says earlier sources show three different theories. [source: SEP-AINP §1.2]
- **How do the three times differ, if dharmas always exist?** Four Sarvāstivāda answers; Sautrāntika regress. [source: IEP-Sarv]
- **Is time fundamental?** Barbour/Rovelli-style emergence vs realists like Smolin; "nascent". [source: WP-Julian_Barbour; SEP-T §11]
