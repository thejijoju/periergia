## Precursors
- Utilitarian ideas were stated before Bentham "most notably" by John Gay, Francis Hutcheson, David Hume, Claude-Adrien Helvétius and Cesare Beccaria; Bentham "rendered the theory in its recognisably secular and systematic form" [source: SEP-B]
- Francis Hutcheson (1694–1746), Irish-born professor at Glasgow, published his *Inquiry into the Original of our Ideas of Beauty and Virtue* (two treatises) in 1725 [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Hutcheson_(philosopher)]. In the *Inquiry Concerning Moral Good and Evil* he wrote that "that action is best, which procures the greatest happiness for the greatest numbers; and that worst, which, in like manner, occasions misery", adding that "the dignity, or moral importance of persons, may compensate numbers" [source: SEP-HU]. SEP-B cites the work as the 1729 edition; 1725 is the first edition (WP). SEP-HU calls Hutcheson "explicitly utilitarian when it comes to action choice" and reads the dignity caveat as a deontological constraint.
- Bentham was "unaware" of Hutcheson's formula and sometimes (by "faulty memory") thought he had found it in Priestley's *Essay on the First Principles of Government* (1768); "more plausible" are his claims to have found it in Beccaria's *Dei delitti e delle pene* (1764): "la massima felicità divisa nel maggior numero" (SEP prints "messima"), "the greatest happiness of the greatest number" [source: SEP-B; Beccaria 1764 and Priestley 1768 dates also in https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesare_Beccaria and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Priestley]
- Bentham "discovered the utility principle" in 1769 in Hume, Helvétius and Beccaria; reading Hume's *Treatise* (1739–40), which equated virtue with utility, he "felt as if scales had fallen from my eyes"; he took the word "utility" from Hume and the link between happiness and pleasure/pain from Helvétius's *De l'esprit* (1758) [source: SEP-B]
- "Theological" utilitarians: Richard Cumberland (1631–1718), Susanna Newcome (1685–1763), John Gay (1699–1745) and Joseph Priestley (1733–1804) held that promoting happiness is obligatory because God wills it; Gay: the happiness of mankind is "the criterion of virtue, but once removed" [source: SEP-HU]
- William Paley (1743–1805), *The Principles of Moral and Political Philosophy* (1785), was "one of the most frequently re-printed and well read books of moral thought" of the period; religious utilitarianism "dominated in the universities until the 1830's"; to Mill's dismay Bentham's utilitarianism was often conflated with Paley's; for Paley God's eternal sanctions make self-interest and virtue coincide [source: IEP-JSM]. Paley's *Principles* remained a set text at Cambridge into the Victorian era; Darwin had to read it [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Paley]
- Mozi (c. 470–c. 391 BCE) and his followers are "considered proto-utilitarian" (Van Norden 2007), with commitments to impartiality, production of the good and maximization; scholars also find proto-utilitarian theory in the Indian Buddhist Śāntideva (Goodman 2009) [source: SEP-HU]. CONSISTENCY: the published Hundred Schools chapter gives Mozi "about 470 to about 390 BCE" with Fraser's c. 430 BCE, calls Mohism (Fraser) "the world's earliest form of consequentialism" and says that, unlike Bentham and Mill, the Mohists sought collective welfare and judged practices rather than single acts. No contradiction.

## Bentham: life
- Jeremy Bentham was born in London on 15 February 1748 (4 February 1747/8 Old Style, Houndsditch) and died there on 6 June 1832 [source: SEP-B; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Bentham]
- Educated at Westminster School (1755–60) and Queen's College, Oxford, which he entered aged twelve. Degree dates differ: SEP-B gives BA 1763, MA 1766; WP gives BA 1764, MA 1767 [source: SEP-B; WP Jeremy_Bentham]
- He heard Blackstone's lectures at Oxford (1763–64) and rejected his natural-law reasoning; called to the Bar 1769 but abandoned practice for "analytic jurisprudence, law reform, and social and political improvement" [source: SEP-B]
- *A Fragment on Government* (1776, anonymous) stated the "fundamental axiom" that "it is the greatest happiness of the greatest number that is the measure of right and wrong" [source: SEP-B, quoting CW 1977, 393]
- *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (IPML) was printed 1780 and published, with additions, 1789 [source: SEP-B; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Introduction_to_the_Principles_of_Morals_and_Legislation]
- In 1829 ("Article on Utilitarianism") Bentham replaced "greatest happiness of the greatest number" with the "greatest happiness principle", because the older formula could justify sacrificing a minority to a majority [source: SEP-B §4.2]
- He financed the *Westminster Review* (funds December 1823; first issue 1824) [source: SEP-B; IEP-JSM]. The "philosophic radicals" (a coinage of J. S. Mill's) around him included the Mills, Francis Place, George Grote, Edwin Chadwick and John Bowring; the group was "far less cohesive than is sometimes thought" [source: SEP-B]
- He was made an honorary citizen of France in September 1792 [source: SEP-B]
- He never married and died "on the eve of the signing of the Great Reform Act" [source: SEP-B]
- Auto-icon: by will he directed public dissection of his body; Thomas Southwood Smith gave the eulogy over the dissected remains. His pamphlet *Auto-Icon; or Farther Uses of the Dead to the Living* (printed 1842, not then published) proposed displaying preserved bodies for public instruction. His skeleton, dressed in his clothes, is still displayed at University College London, with a wax head replacing the mummified one [source: SEP-B]. UCL accepted the auto-icon in 1850 (papers donated 1849); since 2020 it is in a new glass case at the entrance of UCL's Student Centre; the real head was eventually locked away after student pranks [source: WP Jeremy_Bentham]. SEP-B reads auto-iconism as an attempt at a secular substitute for religious ritual.
- Bentham is often called UCL's "spiritual founder", but his "direct involvement in the university's founding was limited"; Henry Tonks's painting (1922) of him approving the plans is "entirely imaginary" [source: WP Jeremy_Bentham]

## Bentham: ideas and arguments
- IPML opens: "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do." (full passage in Quotations) [source: SEP-B, CW 1970, 11; IEP-B (Ch. 1)]
- The passage combines (1) psychological hedonism (all motives are pleasure/pain) and (2) ethical hedonism (pleasure the only good; acts right as they tend to produce pleasure) [source: SEP-B]
- The principle of utility: "that principle which approves or disapproves of every action whatsoever, according to the tendency which it appears to have to augment or diminish the happiness of the party whose interest is in question" (IPML I 2) [source: SEP-B; SEP-MMP]. Bentham admitted it is not capable of direct proof ("all explanation must start somewhere") [source: IEP-B]
- The "felicific calculus": Bentham did NOT use this term. The value of a pleasure or pain to an individual depends on its intensity, duration, certainty or uncertainty, and propinquity or remoteness; for the tendency of an act add fecundity ("the chance it has of being followed by sensations of the same kind") and purity ("the chance it has of not being followed by sensations of the opposite kind"); and for several persons, extent (the number affected) (IPML ch. IV; CW 1970, 38–39). Seven dimensions in all. Bentham presented it as a model of ideal calculation that neither individual nor legislator could follow strictly [source: SEP-B]
- Push-pin: "Prejudice apart, the game of push-pin is of equal value with the arts and sciences of music and poetry. If the game of push-pin furnish more pleasure, it is more valuable than either" — *The Rationale of Reward* (1825) [source: WP Jeremy_Bentham, citing Bentham 1825]. The common "push-pin is as good as poetry" is a later paraphrase (WP John_Stuart_Mill gives that wording, with a Bronfenbrenner 1977 citation).
- Animals: the penal code was to include a section on cruelty to animals; footnote in IPML ch. XVII ("Of the Limits of the Penal Branch of Jurisprudence"), CW 1970, 283n (Kniess cites 282–3n): "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" The footnote also compares the legal treatment of slaves to that of animals ("The French have already discovered that the blackness of the skin is no reason why a human being should be abandoned without redress to the caprice of a tormentor"), asks what should "trace the insuperable line", and notes that a full-grown horse or dog is more rational than an infant of a day, a week or a month [source: SEP-B; WP IPML; WP Jeremy_Bentham; WebSearch on footnote location: https://transcribe-bentham.ucl.ac.uk/td/Animal_Welfare]. SEP-B: "lies at the beginnings of utilitarian arguments for the ethical treatment of animals (see Singer 1975)". Bentham accepted killing animals for food or defence provided no unnecessary suffering [source: WP Jeremy_Bentham]
- In a March 1825 letter to the *Morning Chronicle* he accepted medical experiments on animals with "a determinate object, beneficial to mankind" but had "a decided and insuperable objection" to pain inflicted without such a view [source: WP Jeremy_Bentham]
- Natural rights: in *Anarchical Fallacies* (written 1791–95 against the French declarations of rights; first published 1816, in French) Bentham held that rights are created by law, which is the command of the sovereign; "natural rights" are a "perversion of language", ambiguous, figurative and "anarchical"; the social contract is unhistorical [source: IEP-B]. Exact line: "Natural rights is simple nonsense: natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense,—nonsense upon stilts" [source: IEP-B; Bowring Works vol. II; modern critical edition title CW 2002 *Rights, Representation, and Reform: Nonsense upon Stilts…* per SEP-B bibliography]. SEP-B paraphrases as "nonsense on stilts" (1795 [2002, 330]); use "upon".
- Schofield (Utilitas 2003) showed that in Bowring's edition the passage was misplaced at the start of the work as commentary on the Preamble [source: WebSearch summary of https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0953820800003745 — not read directly]
- Legal positivism: Bentham's distinction between "the law as it is and the law as it ought to be" "stands at the foundation of the doctrine of legal positivism"; John Austin (who tutored J. S. Mill in Roman law from 1821; first Chair of Jurisprudence at the new University of London, 1826; *Lectures on Jurisprudence*, posthumous 1863) carried it into jurisprudence [source: SEP-B §10; SEP-LP]. For Bentham law "is simply a command expressing the will of the sovereign", so an evil law is still law [source: IEP-B]. Caveat: Rosen argues Bentham's theory of sovereignty is "contrary to" the simple Hobbesian command-obedience model usually associated with his positivism ("split concept" of sovereignty) [source: SEP-B §9.2]
- Panopticon: developed from an idea of his brother Samuel's while visiting him in Krichev, Russian Empire (1785–87); set out in *Panopticon; Or, The Inspection House* (1791); a circular building for any institution needing supervision, best known as a prison for "grinding rogues honest and idle men industrious"; maxim: "the more strictly we are watched, the better we behave". Authorised by the Penitentiary Act 1794; Bentham conceded defeat in 1802; in 1812 the government closed the matter, paying him £23,000 compensation [source: SEP-B; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panopticon]
- Foucault, *Discipline and Punish* (1975) made "panopticism" a "new physics of power" and the panopticon—"that cruel, ingenious cage"—a symbol of the disciplinary "society of surveillance"; SEP-B (citing Semple 1993) says Foucault "hardly does justice to the intricacies of the project". Simone Browne (*Dark Matters*) argues the slave ship haunts the panopticon's design [source: SEP-B; WP Panopticon]
- Sexuality: Bentham rejected the criminalisation of consensual sexual acts because offences should be classified only by harm, and wrote "the first systematic defence of sexual liberty in the English language" (CW: *Of Sexual Irregularities*, 2014) [source: SEP-B]. The essay "Paederasty (Offences Against One's Self)" (c. 1785) argued for liberalising laws against homosexual sex, stayed unpublished in his lifetime "for fear of offending public morality", and was published in 1978 in the *Journal of Homosexuality*, whose abstract called it the "first known argument for homosexual law reform in England"; he called such acts "irregularities of the venereal appetite", not unnatural [source: WP Jeremy_Bentham]
- Other positions: freedom of expression, separation of church and state, equal rights for women, divorce, abolition of slavery and of capital and physical punishment [source: WP Jeremy_Bentham]. IPML also holds that "race or lineage" makes "some difference in point of radical frame of mind and body" [source: WP Jeremy_Bentham]
- Justice objection: Postema: "No moral concept suffers more at Bentham's hand than the concept of justice"; P. J. Kelly argues Bentham had a theory of justice blocking such consequences [source: WP Jeremy_Bentham]
- "Each to count for one": Mill reports "Bentham's dictum 'everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one'" (U V 36) [source: SEP-MMP]. IEP-B quotes a variant. No verified Bentham text with this wording found (see NOT VERIFIED).

## James Mill and J. S. Mill's education and crisis
- James Mill (born James Milne, 6 April 1773 – 23 June 1836), Scottish, son of a shoemaker; licensed as a Church of Scotland minister; *The History of British India* (WP: 1817; SEP-JSM and IEP-JSM: 1818) won him a post at the East India Company [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Mill; SEP-JSM; IEP-JSM]
- John Stuart Mill was born 20 May 1806 in Pentonville, London, to Harriet Barrow and James Mill [source: SEP-JSM]
- Education under his father: Greek at three, Latin at eight; most of the classical canon by twelve, with algebra and Euclid; political economy, logic and calculus in early teens; a year in France at fifteen (IEP: 1820). He taught his younger siblings [source: SEP-JSM; IEP-JSM]
- He founded the Utilitarian Society in 1823 [source: SEP-B §10]
- Mental crisis, aged twenty (Autobiography, CW I: 139): asked himself whether realising all his reform goals would make him happy, "an irrepressible self-consciousness distinctly answered, 'No!'… I seemed to have nothing left to live for." The malaise ran through 1826–7; recovery came through Romantic poetry, especially Wordsworth, then Coleridge, Carlyle and Goethe; he came to stress "the culture of the feelings" and that his upbringing held only "one side of the truth" [source: SEP-JSM; IEP-JSM]
- Essays "Bentham" (1838) and "Coleridge" (1840), "the two great seminal minds of England in their age" [source: IEP-JSM]
- Mill never attended a university (as a Nonconformist he could not have entered Oxford or Cambridge, per WP) [source: SEP-JSM; WP John_Stuart_Mill]

## J. S. Mill: life and career
- East India Company: SEP-JSM and WP: joined at seventeen (1823) under his father, served "almost thirty-five years", retired 1858 when the Crown took over; rose to Chief Examiner (WP: "Examiner of Indian Correspondence", 1856). IEP-JSM instead says he began in 1826 and retired 1857. Use 1823–1858 [source: SEP-JSM; WP John_Stuart_Mill; IEP-JSM]
- When the Crown proposed to take over, Mill wrote the Company's defence, including *Memorandum on the Improvements in the Administration of India during the Last Thirty Years*; he declined a seat on the new Council of India [source: WP John_Stuart_Mill]
- Met Harriet Taylor in 1830 (dinner arranged by the Unitarian minister W. J. Fox); married 1851 after John Taylor's death (1849); she died at Avignon on 3 November 1858; Mill bought a house by the cemetery there [source: SEP-JSM; SEP-HTM]
- MP for Westminster (Liberal) 1865–68, elected without canvassing (he stayed mostly in Avignon); champions: women's suffrage, Irish reform, prosecution of Governor Eyre; defeated in 1868 [source: SEP-JSM; WP John_Stuart_Mill (term 25 July 1865 – 17 Nov 1868)]
- 7 June 1866 women's suffrage petition: Mill presented it, the second MP after Henry Hunt to call for votes for women [source: WP John_Stuart_Mill; History of Parliament article title via WebSearch]
- 20 May 1867: Mill moved to substitute "person" for "man" in clause 4 of the Reform Bill; defeated 196 to 73 (the most cited figure, e.g. *Spectator* 25 May 1867). History of Parliament gives 196 to 75 including tellers; a UK Parliament education page gives 194 to 73 [source: WebSearch results incl. https://historyofparliament.com/2025/03/28/john-stuart-mills-womens-suffrage-amendment/ and https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/25th-may-1867/6/mr-mills-case-for-women; IEP-JSM]. CONSISTENCY: the published Victorian Era chapter's "about 196 votes to 73" is fine.
- Jamaica Committee (1866): Mill led the campaign to prosecute Governor Eyre after Morant Bay (1865); unsuccessful [source: SEP-MMP; IEP-JSM]
- Rector of St Andrews 1865–68 (inaugural address 1 Feb 1867) [source: WP John_Stuart_Mill]
- Died at Avignon, 7 May 1873 (WP: of erysipelas), buried beside Harriet; last words to Helen Taylor: "You know that I have done my work" [source: SEP-JSM; IEP-JSM; WP]. *Autobiography* published 1873 [source: SEP-B]
- Bertrand Russell's godfather [source: WP John_Stuart_Mill]
- Sidgwick, days after Mill's death: "from about 1860-65 or thereabouts he ruled England in the region of thought as very few men ever did" [source: IEP-JSM, citing Collini 1991]

## J. S. Mill: A System of Logic (1843)
- Full title *A System of Logic, Ratiocinative and Inductive*; eight editions in his lifetime (1843–1872) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_System_of_Logic]
- Syllogism: "nothing ever was, or can be proved by syllogism which was not known, or assumed to be known, before" (System, VII: 183), illustrated with "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; Socrates is mortal" [source: SEP-JSM]. CONSISTENCY: the published Aristotle chapter says this schoolroom syllogism "is traced to John Stuart Mill"; SEP-JSM shows Mill using it, but no source read here says he originated it (see NOT VERIFIED).
- Methods of experimental inquiry (System, VII: 388–406): Agreement, Difference, the Joint Method of Agreement and Difference, Residues, Concomitant Variations. SEP-JSM counts four methods (treating the Joint Method as a combination); WP counts five [source: SEP-JSM; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mill%27s_methods]
- Mathematics is inductive: 2 + 1 = 3 is not a definition but a generalisation from experience (pebbles, horses); "there is in every step of arithmetical or algebraical calculation a real induction, a real inference of facts from facts" (System, VII: 254; SEP prints "algebraically"); "All numbers must be numbers of something: there are no such things as numbers in the abstract"; geometrical points and lines are idealisations of experienced space. Mill aimed to drive intuitionism from its "stronghold" (Autobiography, I: 233) [source: SEP-JSM]
- Harriet Taylor had "very little to do" with the *Logic* (Mill's own account) [source: SEP-HTM]

## J. S. Mill: Principles of Political Economy (1848) and Considerations (1861)
- *Principles of Political Economy* (1848), a leading textbook, revised to a 7th edition (1871) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principles_of_Political_Economy]. Mill credited Harriet Taylor with the chapter "On the Probable Futurity of the Labouring Classes" ("she was the cause of my writing it"); a dedication to her was barred by John Taylor in 1848 and pasted into friends' copies; Mill's bibliography calls the book a "joint production with my wife" [source: SEP-HTM]
- *Considerations on Representative Government* (1861) endorsed Thomas Hare's proportional representation (single transferable vote) and plural voting for the educated, and would exclude illiterate adults (CRG VIII) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Considerations_on_Representative_Government; SEP-MMP §3.12]
- Colonial rule is legitimate only as a trust "which in the existing state of civilization of the subject people, most facilitates their transition to a higher stage of development… unless some approach to it is, the rulers are guilty of a dereliction of the highest moral trust… if they do not even aim at it, they are selfish usurpers" (CW XIX 567–68) [source: SEP-MMP]

## J. S. Mill: On Liberty (1859)
- Published 1859; first conceived as a short essay in 1854; Mill says it was "more directly and literally our joint production than anything else which bears my name" [source: SEP-HTM; WP On_Liberty]
- The "one very simple principle" (OL I 9; CW XVIII: 223): see Quotations. Mill never calls it the "harm principle"; IEP-JSM: "often called the 'harm principle'" [source: SEP-MMP; SEP-JSM; IEP-JSM]
- Scope: "this doctrine is meant to apply only to human beings in the maturity of their faculties"; not to children nor "backward states of society in which the race itself may be considered as in its nonage"; "Liberty, as a principle, has no application to any state of things anterior to the time when mankind have become capable of being improved by free and equal discussion" (OL I 10). Until then "there is nothing for them but implicit obedience to an Akbar or a Charlemagne, if they are so fortunate as to find one" (CW XVIII: 224) [source: SEP-MMP; SEP-JSM]
- Grounded in utility, not abstract right: "I regard utility as the ultimate appeal on all ethical questions; but it must be utility in the largest sense, grounded on the permanent interests of man as a progressive being" (OL I 11) [source: SEP-MMP]
- Threat: the "tyranny of the majority" (phrase from Tocqueville), social as much as legal coercion [source: IEP-JSM; SEP-MMP]
- ARGUMENT for free expression (OL ch. II), per SEP-MMP's four reasons and SEP-JSM's "proof from the exhaustion of cases":
  1. A censored opinion might be true; to silence it assumes infallibility, which no one has (II 1–20).
  2. Even if false, it may contain part of the truth; on complex matters the truth is usually shared between conflicting doctrines and emerges only by "the reconciling and combining of opposites" (II 34–39).
  3. Even a wholly false opinion, if challenged, keeps true opinion from becoming "a dead dogma, not a living truth" (II 43; CW XVIII: 243).
  4. Unchallenged, a doctrine loses its meaning, held as "phrases retained by rote" (CW XVIII: 249).
  Conclusion: whatever its truth-status, suppression is epistemically damaging, so expression should not be controlled [source: SEP-MMP §3.3; SEP-JSM §4.5]
- Objection to the free-speech argument: the truth-tracking reasons condemn only incompetent censorship; they give no objection to perfectly reliable "philosopher-king" censors, and would seem to permit censoring opinions (e.g. flat-earthism) whose falsity is well established. Reply: Mill's deliberative/perfectionist rationale—free discussion is needed for people to exercise the capacities that make them "progressive beings" and to hold beliefs as their own, which even successful censorship destroys; and fallibilism should make us keep discussion open even on improbable views [source: SEP-MMP §3.3.1–3.4]
- Speech can still be restricted when it is imminently dangerous: criticism of corn dealers as "starvers of the poor" may circulate in the press but "may justly incur punishment when delivered orally to an excited mob assembled before the house of a corn dealer" (ch. III). SEP-FS: chapter II is a partial qualification of the harm principle for speech, not a mere application of it [source: SEP-FS]
- Mill's argument stresses the effect on the "calmer and more disinterested bystander"; it is doubtful he believed in a strong "marketplace of ideas" thesis (the phrase is from Holmes's *Abrams* dissent, 1919) [source: SEP-FS; SEP-MMP]
- Individuality (ch. III): "Human nature is not a machine to be built after a model… but a tree, which requires to grow and develope itself on all sides" (CW XVIII: 263) [source: SEP-JSM]
- Paternalism: Mill's two explicit reasons (state power is abused; the individual is the better judge of his own good) do not rule out successful paternalism; his perfectionism gives a stronger reason. He allows an exception: no one may sell himself into slavery, since "by selling himself for a slave, he abdicates his liberty" (OL V 11) [source: SEP-MMP §3.7]
- Offence: Mill says merely offensive conduct may not be regulated, yet allows prohibiting public "offences against decency" (OL V 7); SEP-MMP thinks he "may not have a consistent view about offense". Feinberg's *Offense to Others* (1985) proposes a balancing test [source: SEP-MMP §3.8]
- Legal moralism: James Fitzjames Stephen (1829–1894), *Liberty, Equality, Fraternity* (written on the voyage home from India 1872; published 1873, 2nd ed. 1874), attacked *On Liberty*, arguing for legal compulsion and restraint in the interests of morality and religion [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Fitzjames_Stephen]. Patrick Devlin (1905–1992), in his 1959 Maccabaean Lecture (collected in *The Enforcement of Morals*, 1965), answering the Wolfenden Report (1957) on homosexuality, argued that "society means a community of ideas" whose shared morality the law may protect against disintegration; H. L. A. Hart replied in *Law, Liberty and Morality* (1963). Devlin had not read Stephen; Hart noted the close similarity. Devlin signed a 1965 letter to *The Times* supporting the Wolfenden reforms [source: WP Patrick_Devlin; WP James_Fitzjames_Stephen; WP Hart–Devlin_debate; SEP-LL; SEP-MMP §3.9]. SEP-MMP: even if Mill rejects "strong moralism", liberals need not reject "weak moralism" to reach most liberal conclusions.
- Early critics also included Carlyle and Matthew Arnold; Hardy recalled undergraduates of the 1860s knowing the book "almost by heart" [source: WP On_Liberty]
- Translations: Nakamura Masanao (Japanese, 1872); Yan Fu (Chinese, 1903, "On the boundary between the self and the group") [source: published Periergia Intellectual History chapter; not re-verified here]

## J. S. Mill: Utilitarianism (1861/1863)
- First published in 1861 as three articles in *Fraser's Magazine* (vol. 64); collected as a book in 1863 (Parker, Son, and Bourn) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarianism_(book)]
- Greatest Happiness Principle: "actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness. By happiness is intended pleasure, and the absence of pain" (U II 2; CW X: 210) [source: IEP-ME; SEP-MMP]
- Higher pleasures ("of the intellect, of the feelings and imagination, and of the moral sentiments", CW X: 211) are better in quality, not just quantity; test of competent judges (U II 5): see Quotations [source: IEP-ME; SEP-MMP]
- Mill explains competent judges' preference by "a sense of dignity" [source: SEP-MMP §2.3]
- OBJECTION to higher pleasures: hard to square with hedonism, since a pleasure's being "higher" seems to be a value besides pleasure (Sidgwick, *Methods* 93n, 94, 121; F. H. Bradley, *Ethical Studies*; T. H. Green, *Prolegomena* §§162–63; Moore, *Principia* 71–72, 77–81). REPLIES: Mill may treat judges' preferences as evidence of value rather than constitutive of it, or be read as a perfectionist; Crimmins notes some recent commentators argue the Bentham–Mill gap is smaller than supposed, since intensity and purity are qualities in principle measurable [source: SEP-MMP §§2.2–2.4; SEP-B §3.2]
- The "proof" (U IV): reconstructed (SEP-MMP): (1) utilitarianism is true iff happiness is the only thing desirable for its own sake; (2) the only proof of desirability is desire; (3) each person desires his own happiness; (4) so happiness is desired by the aggregate of persons; (5) so happiness is desirable; (6) other things (virtue, money, music) are desired as parts of happiness; (7) so happiness is the only thing desirable for its own sake. Key text (CW X: 234): see Quotations [source: SEP-MMP §2.11; SEP-JSM §4.1]
- OBJECTIONS: equivocation on "desirable" (worthy of desire vs capable of being desired); the composition fallacy from individuals to the aggregate; even if valid, it concerns the good, not the right. G. E. Moore (*Principia Ethica*, 1903): "the fallacy in this step is so obvious, that it is quite wonderful how Mill failed to see it" (Macleod, citing Moore 1993: 118); "desired" is not to "desirable" as "heard" is to "audible" [source: SEP-MMP; SEP-JSM]. Moore named "the naturalistic fallacy" (identifying goodness with a natural property such as pleasure; 1903: 10) and found it in hedonists including Bentham [source: SEP-M]
- REPLIES: (a) Mill says desire is the only *evidence* of desirability, defeasible but real, as befits an empiricist who denies a priori moral intuition (Macleod); (b) Brink's impartiality reading: prudence aims at one's own happiness; morality is impartial, so aims at happiness as such; Mill is not deriving utilitarianism from egoism (see Hall 1949) [source: SEP-JSM; SEP-MMP]
- Act vs rule: J. O. Urmson, "The Interpretation of the Moral Philosophy of J. S. Mill", *Philosophical Quarterly* 3 (1953): 33–39, defended a rule-utilitarian reading (talk of "tendencies" and of secondary principles) [source: SEP-MMP §2.8; IEP-ME bibliography]. Brink: Bentham also spoke of the tendencies of individual acts, which counts against Urmson (Berger 1984); Brink also discusses "sanction utilitarianism" (wrong = what ought to be punished, U V) [source: SEP-MMP §§2.8–2.10]. Schefczyk: the act/rule distinction "had not yet been introduced in Mill's days"; Mill is an act utilitarian about objective rightness and a rule utilitarian about moral obligation [source: IEP-ME §5]
- Justice (U V): "Justice implies something which it is not only right to do, and wrong not to do, but which some individual person can claim from us as his moral right" (CW X: 247) [source: IEP-JSM]

## J. S. Mill and Harriet Taylor Mill: The Subjection of Women (1869)
- Written by 1861, published 1869 [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Subjection_of_Women]
- Thesis: "the legal subordination of one sex to the other—is wrong in itself, and now one of the chief hindrances to human improvement; and… it ought to be replaced by a principle of perfect equality, admitting no power or privilege on the one side, nor disability on the other" (SW, CW XXI: 261) [source: SEP-MMP]. CONSISTENCY: matches the Victorian Era chapter's quotation.
- ARGUMENT (SEP-MMP §§5.1–5.2; SEP-JSM §4.4):
  1. Defenders of subordination claim women are naturally unfit for equal rights.
  2. But women "have always hitherto been kept… in so unnatural a state, that their nature cannot but have been greatly distorted and disguised" (CW XXI: 304–05); on associationist psychology character is a product of upbringing.
  3. So no one can know what women's nature is under present conditions; actual incapacities produced by discrimination cannot justify that discrimination (circularity).
  4. Equality is therefore the presumption, rebuttable only by adequate evidence (CW XXI: 262), and the only test is the "experiment" of equality.
  5. Marriage law, which removed a wife's property and legal personhood, is a form of slavery: "Marriage is the only actual bondage known to our law. There remain no legal slaves, except the mistress of every house" (CW XXI: 323). Consent does not legitimate it, since one may not contract into slavery (cf. OL V 11).
  6. Equality would also benefit everyone (doubling the talent available) and above all women: "After the primary necessities of food and raiment, freedom is the first and strongest want of human nature" (CW XXI: 336; SEP prints "want to human nature").
- OBJECTION: wives are treated far better than slaves. REPLY (Brink): gilded cages are still cages, and often not gilded—husbands can be as violent as masters (285–86, 288–89) [source: SEP-MMP]
- OBJECTION (internal; Annas 1977; Okin 1979): Mill inconsistently assumes most women, given free choice, would choose the domestic division of labour, which he approves ("the common arrangement… seems to me in general the most suitable division of labor", CW XXI: 297). REPLY: Brink calls this "a significant blemish" but notes Mill's own method supplies the means to correct it [source: SEP-MMP §§5.3–5.4]
- Mill's *Autobiography*: the book "was enriched with some important ideas of my daughter's [Helen Taylor] and some passages of her writing. But all that is most striking and profound in what was written by me belongs to my wife" [source: WP The_Subjection_of_Women]

## Harriet Taylor Mill (1807–1858)
- Born Harriet Hardy in London, October 1807; married John Taylor 14 March 1826 (aged eighteen); three children (Herbert, Algernon, Helen); separate residence from 1833; widowed 1849; married J. S. Mill spring 1851; died 3 November 1858 at Avignon [source: SEP-HTM]
- "The Enfranchisement of Women" (*Westminster Review*, 1851), occasioned by American women's rights conventions, argues for "equality in all rights, political, civil, and social"; unlike *Subjection*, it defends married women earning outside the home. Mill's preface in *Dissertations and Discussions* calls it "hers in a particular sense, my share in it being little more than that of an editor and amanuensis"; Robson: "most of the evidence" favours her authorship; CW prints it in an appendix under her name; Lucretia Mott (1851): "she says, he wrote it—he says, she wrote it" [source: SEP-HTM]
- *The Complete Works of Harriet Taylor Mill*, ed. Jo Ellen Jacobs (Indiana UP, 1998), holds drafts mostly brief and fragmentary [source: SEP-HTM]
- *On Liberty* dedication: "Like all that I have written for many years, it belongs as much to her as to me" [source: SEP-HTM]
- A 2022 stylometric study (Schmidt-Petri, Schefczyk and Osburg, *Utilitas* 34: 120–38, "Who Authored On Liberty?") concluded several passages were written by her or jointly; SEP-HTM notes its conclusions are only as reliable as its ascriptions of the training texts [source: SEP-HTM]
- Hackett published *On Liberty: with Related Writings* (31 March 2026, some sellers give later dates), the first edition naming Harriet Taylor Mill as co-author, edited by Piers Norris Turner, Jo Ellen Jacobs, Helen McCabe, Lilly Osburg, Michael Schefczyk and Christoph Schmidt-Petri; reported by Daily Nous, 19 Mar 2026, "'On Liberty' Now Officially Has Two Authors", with disagreement in comments [source: WebSearch results incl. https://dailynous.com/2026/03/19/on-liberty-now-officially-has-two-authors/ (page not reachable; summary only); WP On_Liberty footnote agrees]
- See Debates for the co-authorship/influence debate.

## Mill, India and empire
- Mill "like his father" supported British colonialism, belonged to Wakefield's Colonization Society and was a founding member of the South Australian Association (1833) [source: WP John_Stuart_Mill]
- "A Few Words on Non-Intervention" (1859): "To characterize any conduct whatever towards a barbarous people as a violation of the law of nations, only shows that he who so speaks has never considered the subject", followed by "A violation of the great principles of morality it may easily be" [source: WP John_Stuart_Mill]
- Brink: the scope limitation need not be read as racist ("people" for "race"), since the criterion of normative competence is social and cultural; but questions remain about which societies Mill counted as backward (see Habibi 2017; Skorupski 2021). Mill opposed violent or excessive colonial measures (Eyre) [source: SEP-MMP §3.12]
- Critical readings: Uday Singh Mehta, *Liberalism and Empire* (1999), argues imperialism stemmed from liberal assumptions about reason and progress; Jennifer Pitts, *A Turn to Empire* (2005) [source: WebSearch on Mehta; SEP-S bibliography for Pitts]

## Henry Sidgwick (1838–1900)
- Born 31 May 1838, Skipton, Yorkshire; died of cancer 28 August 1900. Rugby and Trinity College, Cambridge (degree 1859: Senior Classic, 33rd Wrangler); Fellow of Trinity 1859; resigned the Fellowship in 1869 because he could no longer subscribe to the Thirty-Nine Articles (an act that helped end the requirement); Knightbridge Professor of Moral Philosophy from 1883 [source: SEP-S]
- Newnham College: Sidgwick played "a guiding role in the foundation" of one of the first women's colleges; he married Eleanor Mildred Balfour (sister of Arthur Balfour) in 1876; she became Principal of Newnham in 1892 [source: SEP-S]. Dating: Newnham's founding is given as 1871 (house of residence opened by Anne Clough at Sidgwick's suggestion) and its first building as 1875; WP Henry_Sidgwick says he "co-founded" it with Millicent Garrett Fawcett in 1875 [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newnham_College,_Cambridge; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Sidgwick]
- Co-founder (1882) and several times president of the Society for Psychical Research [source: SEP-S]
- *The Methods of Ethics*, first published 1874; seventh edition 1907 (posthumous). It examines three methods: egoism, intuitionism and utilitarianism [source: SEP-S; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Methods_of_Ethics]
- It "marked the culmination of the classical utilitarian tradition"; C. D. Broad and later Rawls admired it; utilitarians from Moore and Russell to Smart, Hare, Parfit, Singer and de Lazari-Radek acknowledge it [source: SEP-S]
- Axioms: utilitarianism is derived from two "rational intuitions": that the good of any one individual is of no more importance "from the point of view (if I may say so) of the Universe" than that of any other, and that as a rational being I am bound to aim at good generally (Methods 1907: 382) [source: SEP-S]
- Dualism of practical reason: rational egoism and utilitarianism end in a standoff, a "fundamental contradiction in our apparent intuitions of what is Reasonable in conduct", so that "the 'Cosmos' of Duty is thus really reduced to a Chaos" (1907: 473; SEP-S says the first edition concluded thus and later editions softened the tone, not the substance). Possible solutions: weaker epistemic standards or a theistic postulate of moral government of the universe [source: SEP-S]
- Esoteric morality: "it may be right to do and privately recommend, under certain circumstances, what it would not be right to advocate openly"; "the doctrine that esoteric morality is expedient should itself be kept esoteric" (1907: 489–90). Bernard Williams called this "Government House" utilitarianism, comporting with colonialism (Williams 1995); defended by de Lazari-Radek and Singer (2010, 2014), drawing on Parfit's point (1984) that a theory can be self-effacing without being false [source: SEP-S]
- Politically Liberal Unionist; SEP-S: "not able to rise above the marked imperialistic and orientalist, racist tendencies of the late Victorian era" (Schultz 2004; Bell) [source: SEP-S]
- Scholarly consensus mostly classes Sidgwick as an act utilitarian, of a very indirect kind [source: SEP-S]

## Debates
- Harriet Taylor Mill's authorship and influence. Mill himself: the writings are "the joint product of both"; *On Liberty* "more directly and literally our joint production than anything else which bears my name"; she was the source of his "ultimate aims", he of "theory". Contemporaries: the Carlyles and Bain were dismissive ("she imbibed all his views, and gave them back in her own form"); George Mill: "nothing like what John took her to be". Minimalists: Pappe (1960) ("Mill without Harriet would still have been Mill"), Mineka (1963), Reeves (2007), Kinzer (2007). Rossi (1970) attacked scholars who credited her only with ideas they disliked. Maximalists: Jacobs (2002) takes Mill at his word and credits her with ideas including the arts/sciences distinction in the *Logic*; Himmelfarb (1974) credits her influence but deplores it as making *On Liberty*'s liberalism absolute. Middle ground: Bain's "overflowed in suggestive talk"; McCabe (2017) on her emotional influence; Philips (2018) on her knowledge of patriarchy. Stylometry (2022) supports co-authorship of parts of *On Liberty*; Hackett's 2026 edition names her co-author. SEP-HTM (2022) concludes the evidence is "equivocal" [source: SEP-HTM; WebSearch]
- Bentham vs Mill on pleasure: traditionally quantitative vs qualitative hedonism; some recent commentators argue the distance is smaller [source: SEP-B §3.2; SEP-MMP]
- Act vs rule (vs sanction) readings of Mill: Urmson (1953) rule; Brink and Berger act (with indirect elements); Schefczyk mixed (act for rightness, rule for obligation); the distinction postdates Mill [source: SEP-MMP; IEP-ME]
- Mill's proof: fallacy (Moore) vs evidential reading (Macleod) vs impartiality reading (Brink, following Hall 1949) [source: SEP-JSM; SEP-MMP]
- Mill and empire: is the scope limitation racist (Brink says it need not be read so) or does liberalism itself generate imperialism (Mehta; Pitts)? [source: SEP-MMP; WebSearch]
- Foucault's reading of the panopticon vs historians of Bentham's project (Semple 1993) [source: SEP-B]
- Sidgwick: utilitarian or "dual-source" theorist (Crisp)? Esoteric morality: Williams's "Government House" charge vs de Lazari-Radek and Singer's defence [source: SEP-S]
- Bentham's legal positivism: command theory (standard) vs Rosen's "split" concept of sovereignty [source: SEP-B]
