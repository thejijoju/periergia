## 1. Why testimony matters

- SEP-Test opens: "So much of what we know about the world, e.g., history, science, politics, one another, etc., comes from the testimony of others." Testimony is "clearly an indispensable source of knowledge", but how we learn "from a speaker's say-so has proven to be a difficult task". [source: SEP-Test intro]
- IEP-Test: testimony in the philosophical sense "need not be formal testimony in a courtroom; it happens whenever one person tells something to someone else." [source: IEP-Test intro]
- Standard examples of things known only by testimony: that the earth is round, and "the mundane fact that we were born on such-in-such a day" (Webb 1993: 261). "No one person has the time, intellect, and experience necessary to independently learn" such facts (McLeod's summary of Hardwig 1991, Jones 1999, Webb 1993). [source: SEP-Trust §(trust and knowledge)]
- C. A. J. Coady, *Testimony: A Philosophical Study* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1992) is the classic book-length study; with Elizabeth Fricker (1987) and Edward Craig (1990) it made testimony "a central topic for social epistemology". The terms "reductionism" and "non-reductionism" are due to Coady 1973 ("Testimony and Observation", *American Philosophical Quarterly* 10: 149–155). [source: SEP-Soc §2; IEP-Test §1 and bibliography]
- Coady 1992: 79–82 documents places where Hume, describing the inductive base for trusting testimony, actually uses evidence drawn from other people; van Cleve 2006: 67: "the vast majority (or perhaps even the totality) of what passes for corroboration of testimony itself relies on other testimony." [source: IEP-Test §(insufficient inductive base)]
- Early modern individualism: Locke wrote that "other men's opinions floating in one's brain" do not constitute genuine knowledge; the Royal Society (1660) took the motto "Nullius in verba", roughly "take no one's word for it". O'Connor suggests these, plus philosophy's focus on scepticism, explain why social epistemology "only really come into its own in the last few decades". [source: SEP-Soc §1]
- Craig's *Knowledge and the State of Nature* (1990) — already used in *What Is Knowledge?* (concept of knowledge flags good informants); cite there, not repeated here. [source: SEP-Soc §2 (Craig 1990 listed); Periergia *What Is Knowledge?*]

## 2. The First Big Question: reductionism vs non-reductionism (ARGUMENT 1)

Leonard's framing: "Is testimony a basic source of justification, or can testimonial justification be reduced to a combination of other epistemic sources?" Motivating contrast: a friend who is a reliable sports reporter tells you your team won (clearly justified) vs a total stranger tells you the same (unclear). [source: SEP-Test §1]

### 2a. Reductionism
- **Positive Reasons** (Leonard's formulation): "A hearer is justified in believing what a speaker says if, and only if, they (a) have positive reasons for thinking that the speaker's testimony is reliable, where these reasons are not themselves ultimately based on testimony, and (b) do not have any undefeated defeaters that indicate that the speaker's testimony is false or unlikely to be true." [source: SEP-Test §1.1]
- Main motivation: avoiding **gullibility** — without positive reasons, "one could be justified in believing the say-so of a random blogger on an arbitrary website". Elizabeth Fricker (1994) calls denying reductionism a "recipe for gullibility"; Faulkner (2000) says it sanctions irresponsibility. [source: SEP-Test §1.1; SEP-Soc §3.1]
- Reductionism traces "at least as far back as David Hume's (1740, 1748)"; modern defenders include E. Fricker (1987, 1994, 1995, 2002, 2006), Adler, Lyons, Lipton, Shogenji, Malmgren, Kenyon. [source: SEP-Test §1.1]
- **Global reductionism**: you need positive reasons that testimony *in general* is reliable. Schema: "My friend said that he got a puppy. / Relying on testimony is generally a reliable way to form true beliefs… / Therefore, my friend got a puppy." Testimony thus reduces to perception (of the utterance), memory (past reports turned out right) and inference. [source: SEP-Test §1.1.1]
- **Three objections to global reductionism** (SEP-Test §1.1.1):
  1. *Circularity/regress* (Coady 1992): to check Alice I rely on Bill; to check Bill, on Carly; to check Carly, either back to Alice or Bill (vicious circle) or on to Donald (regress). So we are "rarely (if ever) justified in accepting what people tell us."
  2. *Insufficient base* (Coady 1992): checking testimony about history, medicine, biology etc. would need "loads and loads of facts" confirmed for ourselves; we lack the time and resources.
  3. *Testimony is not one category* (E. Fricker 1994): friends are reliable about their music tastes, much less so about politics; ask about kinds of testimony, not testimony "in general".
- **Local reductionism** (Elizabeth Fricker's view; "Against Gullibility", 1994, and "Telling and Trusting: Reductionism and Anti-Reductionism in the Epistemology of Testimony", *Mind* 104 (1995): 393–411, a critical notice of Coady 1992): you need non-testimonial reasons to think *this speaker* is reliable *on this occasion*. It escapes the circle because you can check a friend's pet reports yourself. [source: SEP-Test §1.1.2; IEP-Test bibliography]
- **Three objections to local reductionism** (SEP-Test §1.1.2): (1) *young children* (Audi 1997; empirical work by Harris, Koenig, Corriveau) lack the experience to check their parents, yet learn from them (also hits global reductionism); (2) *the stranger giving directions* in a new country: you are justified in believing "your hotel is three blocks down the road" though you know next to nothing about him (Webb 1994, Strawson 1994, Schmitt 1999, Lackey 2008); Kenyon (2013) replies with contextual information; (3) *social psychology*: humans are poor lie-detectors, so too little testimonial justification (Michaelian; Shieber; contra Sperber 2013, Harris et al. 2018). Reductionist replies: Bayesian analysis (Goldman 1999 ch. 4), inference to the best explanation (E. Fricker 1995; Lipton). "Whether or not these responses succeed remains an open question." [source: SEP-Test §1.1.2]
- E. Fricker's own refinement is partly **hybrid**: adults need positive reasons, but children "in the developmental phase" do not (E. Fricker 1995). [source: SEP-Test §1.3]

### 2b. Non-reductionism (anti-reductionism)
- **Presumptive Right**: "A hearer is justified (or warranted) in believing what a speaker says if they do not have an undefeated defeater that indicates that the speaker's testimony is false or unlikely to be true." (Goldberg & Henderson 2006 add: the hearer must be counterfactually sensitive to defeaters.) [source: SEP-Test §1.2]
- It "traces at least as far back as Thomas Reid" (*Inquiry* 1764, pp. 94–95 of the 1983 ed.); later defenders: Austin 1946, Welbourne, Evans 1982, Hardwig 1985, Coady 1992, Burge 1993, Plantinga 1993, Dummett 1994, McDowell 1994, Strawson 1994, Goldman 1999, and others. [source: SEP-Test §1.2]
- Reid's motivation (Leonard's paraphrase): whatever reason we have to treat perception as basic, we have an analogous reason to treat testimony as basic; humans are endowed, "perhaps by God or just by nature", with dispositions (a) to tell the truth, (b) to believe what they are told, and (c) to sense when a speaker is not to be trusted. [source: SEP-Test §1.2] O'Connor: Reid "argued that honesty (in speakers) and credulity (in audiences) are as much a part of our natural psychological endowment, and so are as worthy of being relied upon in belief-formation, as is the faculty of perception." [source: SEP-Soc §3.1]
- Reid's **argument from childhood** (IEP-Test's reconstruction): if credulity came from reasoning and experience it would grow as they grow; if it is "the gift of Nature" it will be strongest in childhood and restrained by experience; observation shows the latter; so credulity is natural, not learned. (Quotation in Quotations section.) [source: IEP-Test §1]
- Even Reid saw disanalogies: in the *Intellectual Powers* (1785, Essay II ch. 20) he says that relying on testimony is relying on a person's authority, which is not true of the senses. **Caution:** IEP-Test's wording of this passage ("When we believe something on the basis of someone's testimony, we rely on that person's authority…") is in modern English, so it is probably a modernised paraphrase, not Reid's text; do not quote it. [source: IEP-Test §1]
- **George Campbell** anticipated the point: in *A Dissertation on Miracles* (1763 per Broadie), written against Hume, he rejected "Hume's premiss that we believe testimony solely on the basis of experience": there is "a natural tendency to believe other people… not a learned response… but an innate disposition", gradually refined by experience; testimony is the default, "true unless or until proved false", like memory. Campbell (1719–1796), Principal of Marischal College, Aberdeen, was with Reid a member of the Aberdeen Philosophical Society (the "Wise Club"). [source: SEP-S18 §(George Campbell)] (Broadie writes "1763"; other sources give 1762 — hedge as "early 1760s".)
- **Main objection to non-reductionism — gullibility**: the anonymous blogger (E. Fricker 2002), and Lackey's **alien notebook**: you see aliens from another planet drop a notebook apparently in English testifying that hungry tigers ate their friends; you have no defeater, but no positive reason either; accepting it seems irrational (Lackey 2008: 168–169). [source: SEP-Test §1.2]
- **Hybrid views**: (i) E. Fricker 1995 (children exempt); (ii) Lackey 2008: "it takes two to tango" — the hearer needs some (weaker) positive reasons that make reliance "not irrational", *and* the speaker must actually be reliable. Whether any hybrid succeeds "is still very much an open debate". [source: SEP-Test §1.3]

### 2c. A priori arguments for non-reductionism
- **Coady's Davidsonian argument**: to interpret a language at all (e.g. Martians') we must assume their utterances generally track the world; so testimony, to be interpretable, must be generally reliable. **Objection (Graham 2000)**: honest, competent interpreters with bad perception or memory could understand each other perfectly yet be unreliable testifiers. [source: IEP-Test §2(d)(i)]
- **Burge's argument (1993, "Content Preservation", *Philosophical Review* 102(4): 457–488)**, as quoted by IEP-Test from pp. 472–473: "We are a priori entitled to accept something that is prima facie intelligible and presented as true… Intelligible affirmation is the face of reason; reason is a guide to truth. We are a priori prima facie entitled to take intelligible affirmation at face value." O'Connor's gloss: "intelligible speech itself is an indication of having been produced by a rational source, one which by nature aims at truth". **Objection** (IEP-Test): it assumes the speaker's rational faculties work properly in a truth-conducive environment, which fails in many possible worlds; yet the argument "would seem to work just as well in worlds where they are not". [source: IEP-Test §2(d)(ii); SEP-Soc §3.1; WP-Tyler_Burge for citation]
- Burge (born 1946, UCLA) also holds the Inheritance View (§4). [source: WP-Tyler_Burge; SEP-Test §3.1.2]

## 3. Hume's reductionism (cross-ref *Berkeley and Hume*; do NOT repeat the miracles argument)

- IEP-Test: "The earliest clear statements of these positions appear in David Hume and Thomas Reid." Hume's picture: "we properly form beliefs based on testimony only because we have seen other confirmed instances." (Quotation below.) [source: IEP-Test §1]
- Broadie: Hume argues "that the credence we place in testimony is based entirely on experience, the experience of the occasions when testimony has turned out to be true as against those experiences where it has not." [source: SEP-S18]
- Within §10 Hume lists considerations that lower a testimony's credibility: number, character and competence of witnesses; the "agreeable emotions" of surprise and wonder that make us credulous; that miracle reports "abound among ignorant and barbarous nations"; and that rival religions' miracles undercut one another (Russell's summary). These are useful as Hume's general criteria for weighing witnesses. [source: SEP-HR §(miracles)]
- Hume does allow a "universal propensity to credulity" as a fact of psychology (Russell's phrase, citing T 1.3.9–10); his point is normative: credence *ought* to be proportioned to experience of reliability. [source: SEP-HR]
- Section reference: "Of Miracles" is **Section X** of *An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding* (published 1748 as *Philosophical Essays concerning Human Understanding*, retitled 1758 — per the Periergia Berkeley/Hume notes). IEP-Test cites "Hume 1748, section X, at 74" (Hackett 1977). **Caution:** SEP-HR cites the same passages as "EU, 11.12/114" etc.; the section number there conflicts with the usual §10 numbering — use §10 (consistent with Periergia's *Berkeley and Hume*, which cites EHU 10.4). [source: IEP-Test; SEP-HR; research/early-modern--berkeley-and-hume.md]

## 4. The Second Big Question: transmission vs generation (ARGUMENT 2)

- Leonard: "Can testimony generate knowledge, or can it merely transmit it?" — can a hearer know p from a speaker who does not know p? Example: Gretchen does not know the bakery is closed but says so; it is. [source: SEP-Test §2]
- **Transmission View**, necessity half (TV-N): "B knows that p on the basis of A's testimony only if A knows that p too." (Defenders: Welbourne, Hardwig, Ross, Burge, Plantinga, Williamson, Audi, Owens, Adler, Faulkner, Schmitt.) Motivation: the **analogy with memory** — you cannot remember-know today what you did not know earlier. Sufficiency half (TV-S): if A knows p, B believes p on A's say-so and B has no undefeated defeaters, B knows p. [source: SEP-Test §2.1]
- **Lackey's Creationist Teacher** (from *Learning from Words*, 2008): Stella, a fourth-grade biology teacher, has read the textbooks but, because of her creationist commitments, does not believe (so does not know) that *Homo sapiens* evolved from *Homo erectus*; she teaches it anyway; her pupils come to know it. So testimonial knowledge can be **generated** by a speaker who lacks it, and TV-N is false. [source: SEP-Test §2.2; WP-Jennifer_Lackey]
- Lackey's **Persistent Believer**: Persia's optometrist Eyal (wrongly, on this occasion) tells her the eye drops make her vision unreliable for three hours; she ignores him, sees a badger in the car park, tells Fred. Persia's belief is defeated (unjustified), but Fred, unaware of the defeater, comes to know. Also aims to show *justification* can be generated. [source: SEP-Test §2.2]
- Lackey's **Compulsively Trusting** case against TV-S: Quinn would believe anything Kevin says; Kevin truthfully says he is moving to Brooklyn; Kevin knows, Quinn does not (his trust is insensitive to counterevidence). [source: SEP-Test §2.2]
- Lackey's positive view: focus on the linguistic items (statements) in testimonial exchanges rather than speakers' internal states (beliefs, knowledge) — the slogan "learning from words". Jennifer Lackey: Wayne and Elizabeth Jones Professor of Philosophy, Northwestern University. *Learning from Words: Testimony as a Source of Knowledge* (OUP, 2008; pb 2010); an earlier article "Learning from Words", *PPR* 73 (2006): 77–101. [source: WP-Jennifer_Lackey]
- Other teacher cases: Graham 2006, Carter & Nickel 2014; Goldberg 2005, Pelling 2013. Recent work distinguishes versions of TV-N/TV-S (Wright 2016). [source: SEP-Test §2.2]
- **Reply available to the transmission theorist** (to use as "reply"): defenders refine the principles, e.g. distinguishing transmission of knowledge from transmission of justification (Audi 1997: testimony can generate justification but only transmit knowledge), or arguing some versions of TV-N survive the cases. [source: SEP-Test §2 intro, §2.2 end]
- **Inheritance View** (Burge 1993, 1997; McDowell; Owens; Schmitt; Faulkner; Wright): the hearer's belief is justified by whatever evidence justifies the speaker's (Owens's example: you inherit my a priori proof of theorem T). Objections: Lackey's persistent believer; Leonard's two detectives (Dell's and Doris's evidence each support "exactly one thief" but conflict on who). [source: SEP-Test §3.1.2]
- **Assurance (interpersonal) view** (Moran 2006, Hinchman 2005, McMyler 2011): testifying is offering an assurance, akin to a promise; the hearer believes the *speaker*, not merely the evidence. [source: SEP-Soc §3.1; IEP-Test §2(c)(v)]
- **Group testimony** example: UN Population Commission (est. 1946, 47 members) report *Charting the Progress of Populations* (2002) testified to a claim about the Americas that no single member believed before publication (Tollefsen 2007). Wikipedia raises the same question (Tollefsen 2009; Wray 2009; Fallis 2008). Hardwig (1985) argues that if groups testify they are knowers; Faulkner (2018) denies it. [source: SEP-Test §6]
- AI: some deny that AI-produced sentences are testimony (Goldberg 2020, on instrument-based belief); others extend the epistemology of testimony to AI (Freiman and Miller 2020; Freiman 2023). [source: SEP-Soc §3.1]

## 5. Indian theories: śabda (cross-ref *The Classical Indian Schools…*, *Mahāyāna Philosophy…*, *Jains, Ājīvikas and Materialists…*)

- **Nyāya definition**: *Nyāya-sūtra* 1.1.7, "Testimony is the (true) statement of an expert (*āpta*)" (Phillips) / "Testimony is the assertion of a qualified speaker" (Dasti). Phillips: "expert" is "not entirely adequate"; *āpta* means a trustworthy authority. [source: SEP-IndEp §6; SEP-Gan §7; IEP-Ny §(d)] (IEP-Ny's text labels the definition "NS1.1.5" but then cites Vātsyāyana on 1.1.7; SEP and Periergia use **1.1.7** — keep 1.1.7.)
- **Vātsyāyana** (commentary on NS 1.1.7): an *āpta* "not only knows the truth but … wants to communicate it without deception" (Phillips' paraphrase) — "a moral dimension"; and "even foreigners—'barbarians,' *mleccha*—can be the experts", against priestly privilege. [source: SEP-IndEp §6]
- Dasti: Nyāya "does not require any kind of special expertise from such a speaker in normal situations. Nor does a hearer need positive evidence of trustworthiness. Mere absence of doubt in the asserter's ability to speak authoritatively about the issue at hand is enough." — i.e. structurally close to non-reductionism (a presumptive right with defeaters). Vātsyāyana (NB 2.1.69): "in accord with knowledge gained by testimony, people undertake their common affairs"; Uddyotakara: testimony has "the widest range of any source of knowledge". [source: IEP-Ny §(d)]
- Chains: the speaker must know p by perception, inference, or testimony ("chains of testimony are okay") — a **transmission** picture. Uddyotakara divides testimony by whether its content originated in perception or inference; Jayanta: veridicality depends on the speaker's knowledge and honesty. [source: SEP-IndEp §6; IEP-Ny §(d)]
- **Rumour** (*aitihya*), defined by Vātsyāyana (on NS 2.2.1) as "a testimony chain whose originator is unknown"; Nyāya treats even it as "presumptively veridical". [source: SEP-IndEp §7.4]
- **Against reduction to inference**: Nyāya resists the inferentialist account "from the earliest, in the sūtras of Gautama", saying it conflates inferential *certification* that p with testimonial knowledge that p at a first, unreflective level (Mohanty 1994). Jayanta Bhaṭṭa (*Nyāya-mañjarī* 322): "The conditions that determine inferential knowledge and those that determine verbal knowledge are not the same." Comprehension and acceptance are "normally fused". [source: SEP-IndEp §6] (Phillips calls Jayanta "eighth-century"; he is usually dated to the 9th century — not checked here; give no century or say "Jayanta Bhaṭṭa (Kashmir)" only if checked.)
- **Gaṅgeśa's argument against the Vaiśeṣika reduction** (ARGUMENT 3, step by step; Phillips' reconstruction): the Vaiśeṣika is "said to admit testimonial knowledge but to see its generation as coming from inference": (1) speaker S states p; (2) S is an *āpta*; (3) what such authorities state is true; so (4) p. **Gaṅgeśa's rebuttal**: how is premise (1) known? To know *what S stated* you must already understand the sentence, so "Testimony has to be admitted as a separate knowledge source to understand how the reductionist's inference could get started." Gaṅgeśa lived in the first half of the 14th century, NE India (Mithilā); *Tattva-cintā-maṇi* ("Jewel"). [source: SEP-Gan §§1, 7.1]
- Gaṅgeśa's reply to Buddhist sceptics who say words give only helpful/unhelpful advice: erroneous utterances "are not veritable testimony as we Naiyāyikas understand it"; true and false utterances differ in the processes producing them. [source: SEP-Gan §7.1]
- Gaṅgeśa's **parrot** case: a hearer who knows that sounds come from a parrot does not gain testimonial knowledge — "Knowing that the sounds are from a parrot is a block"; he folds this into *yogyatā* (semantic fit), defined negatively as "absence of knowledge of a blocker". (Periergia's *What Is Knowledge?* research notes already mention the parrot as a "Gettier case" — cross-ref.) [source: SEP-Gan §7; SEP-IndEp §6]
- Three conditions on a meaningful (transmitting) sentence: *ākāṅkṣā* (expectancy), *yogyatā* (semantic fit; stock counterexample "The gardener is watering the plants with fire"), *āsatti* (contiguity). [source: SEP-IndEp §6]
- **Vaiśeṣika and Buddhists** reject testimony as an independent *pramāṇa*; Vaiśeṣika "and Yogācāra join together" in treating it as inference with premises "S is trustworthy" and "S has said p". [source: SEP-IndEp §6] (Periergia *Classical Indian Schools* says the same; consistent.)
- **Mīmāṃsā**: Veda not composed by anyone (*apauruṣeya*); speakers err, the Veda does not. Nyāya and theistic Vedāntins counter that every sentence has an author; Udayana (11th c.) made God (*īśvara*) "the best or only candidate". The **Prābhākara** Mīmāṃsakas hold that testimonial knowledge "wears … its truth on its sleeve", speaker's intention is irrelevant: "A parrot can make us know something like a tape-recorder"; even a liar who is himself deceived can transmit the truth. Nyāya instead stresses speaker's intention (*tātparya*). [source: SEP-IndEp §6] Kumārila's intrinsic validity (*svataḥ prāmāṇya*) is already set out in *Classical Indian Schools* — cross-ref.
- **Buddhist reduction (Dignāga, Dharmakīrti)**: Dignāga (c. 480–540, per Periergia *Mahāyāna*) accepts two *pramāṇas*, perception and inference. On testimony: Dignāga eliminates "authority as a separate valid means of knowledge, stating that it is a kind of inference", acceptable only if it does not contradict one's perception and inference [WS-10 only; the verse number (PS 2.5) and the Sanskrit wording were NOT verified]. **Dharmakīrti** (verified): scripture should not be used on factual or rationally decidable matters; on "radically inaccessible things" (*atyantaparokṣa*, e.g. the details of karma) it must be relied on "because of the absence of any other means" (*Pramāṇavārttika* I.216); scripture can count as an inferential source but is "always fallible" — "it has no claim to certainty" (*nāto niścayaḥ*, Svavṛtti ad I.318). - **Cārvāka**: perception only. ## 6. Islamic: isnād, tawātur and āḥād (hedged — a theory of testimony in all but name)

- An **isnād** ("support") is the chain of transmitters of a report from the original authority to the present reciter; the report's text is the **matn**. [source: WP-Isnad]
- **Mutawātir** ("successive") report: conveyed by "narrators so numerous that it is not conceivable that they have agreed upon an untruth"; a significant though unspecified number at *each* level of the chain; **āḥād** ("solitary") reports are those whose chains fall short of that. Can be *mutawātir* in wording (*lafẓī*) or meaning (*maʿnawī*). Classic example of *lafẓī*: "Whoever intentionally attributes a lie to me, let him prepare his seat in the Fire", said to be transmitted by more than seventy Companions. [source: WP-Hadith_terminology; WP-Tawatur]
- **Tawātur** as epistemology: "certainty achieved about a piece of information when that information has been shown to have been corroborated to such an extent that it becomes inconceivable for it to have been created through collusion". First used as a technical term among rationalists and Muʿtazilites as early as the 9th century; adopted more widely by the 11th century, partly in response to Muʿtazilite scepticism; debate over the minimum number of independent channels ranged from two to seventy, and the "no fixed number" view came to dominate; a centuries-long debate over whether *any* hadith is *mutawātir*. The Qurʾān as a whole is regarded as transmitted by *tawātur*; most hadith are *āḥād*. Wikipedia also says the idea "originated in Greek empiricism" — unsourced in what was read; do not use. [source: WP-Tawatur]
- Philosophical point the writer may draw (interpretive, hedged): *tawātur* rests on the **independence** of many witnesses (they cannot have colluded), the same assumption Condorcet's theorem and the wisdom of crowds need (§10). This comparison is the chapter's own, not a cited scholar's.
- Modern critique: Joseph Schacht, *The Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence* (1950), argued isnāds were widely fabricated toward the end of the 2nd Islamic century; contemporary hadith studies hold isnāds were more often forged than traditionally presumed; *isnād-cum-matn* analysis was introduced independently by Harald Motzki and Gregor Schoeler in 1996. Ibn Sīrīn (d. 110/728) is reported as saying that after the *fitna* people demanded "name to us your informants". [source: WP-Isnad] (WP-Isnad prints the First Fitna as "656–616 AD", a typo for 656–661.)
- Book: Suheil Laher, *Tawātur in Islamic Thought: Transmission, Certitude and Orthodoxy* (Edinburgh University Press, 2024). [source: WP-Tawatur sources list]

## 7. Chinese: the Mohists (cross-ref *Mozi, the Daoists and the Legalists* and *What Is Philosophy?*)

- The early Mohists propose three *fa* (models/standards) for statements, in *Mozi* books 35–37 ("Condemning Fatalism"), three slightly different versions: a **root** (precedent of the ancient sage kings), a **source** ("statements must have an empirical basis; they must be checked against what people see and hear"), and a **use** (benefit to state, clan and people when applied in government). [source: SEP-Moh §"The Three Fa"]
- Loy's version: four distinct "Tests" across the three versions; one is "**Confirmation by the testimony of the masses' sense of sight and hearing**", listed in "Against Fatalism" A and C, with only two certain applications in the core chapters: the "Elucidating Ghosts" chapter (proof that ghosts exist) and "Against Fatalism" B. The standards are called *yi* ("standards", "gnomons"): "To expound doctrine without first establishing standards (*yi*) is like telling time using a sundial that has been placed on a spinning potter's wheel." [source: IEP-Moz §5] Wikipedia calls the three criteria *sān biǎo*. [source: WP-Mohism] (So "biao", "fa" and "yi" are all used; name the variation.)
- Testimony in action: for ghosts, "countless well-known stories report cases in which ghosts have been seen and heard"; against fate, "No one has ever actually seen or heard fate." Critics: vagueness; "does belief in ghosts really satisfy the second and third standards?"; Mozi argues that even if there are no ghosts we should act as if there are — the benefit standard seems to take priority. [source: SEP-Moh] Good illustration of how collective testimony can be credulous (ghost stories) — the chapter's own point.
- **Later Mohist Canon A80**: three sources of knowledge — **hearsay or testimony** (*wén* 聞), "receiving it as passed along"; explanation/inference (*shuō* 說, e.g. "squares do not rotate"); and "in person" (*qīn* 親), observation. Elsewhere "passing along" = "someone reports it" (A81), and "reporting" (*gào* 告) = "making someone know" (B9). Fraser: the Mohists "take observation and explanation to be on a par with a third source of knowledge, 'hearsay,' or the reports of other people. They thus explicitly recognize the social aspects of knowledge." Canons compiled late 4th to mid-3rd century BCE (per Periergia *Logic across Traditions*). [source: SEP-MC §5.2]

## 8. Expertise: the novice/expert problem

- **John Hardwig, "Epistemic Dependence"**, *Journal of Philosophy* 82(7) (July 1985): 335–349; also "The Role of Trust in Knowledge", *JPhil* 88 (1991): 693–708. Hardwig "claims that such novice reliance on an expert must necessarily be blind, and thus exhibit an unjustified trust" (Frances's summary); he also argues science depends on trust among researchers, and that if groups testify, groups are knowers. [source: SEP-Dis §4; SEP-Test §6, bibliography; SEP-Trust] (Hardwig's own sentences were NOT read — do not quote him.)
- **Alvin Goldman, "Experts: Which Ones Should You Trust?"**, *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 63(1) (2001): 85–110 — "the locus classicus" on how non-experts identify experts. The "novice/expert problem": a novice "seems ill-equipped to even determine who the experts in that domain are". Goldman's **five sources of evidence** (Goldman 2001: 93): (1) arguments presented by the contending experts for their own views and against rivals'; (2) agreement from additional putative experts on one side or the other; (3) appraisals by "meta-experts" of the experts' expertise (including formal credentials); (4) evidence of the experts' interests and biases on the question; (5) evidence of the experts' past "track-records". [source: SEP-Dis §4; SEP-Soc §5.1]
- Worked example suggestion (chapter's own): apply the five sources to two "experts" on vaccine safety or climate; note that (2) is only useful if the agreeing experts are not merely copying one another (independence, §10).
- Authority of experts: "pre-emptive" view (an authority's testimony replaces your other reasons) vs non-pre-emptive (it is a strong reason added to the others; Dormandy 2018, Lackey 2018a); Lackey's worry about pre-emption: you could be justified in believing your otherwise reliable pastor that women are inherently inferior. [source: SEP-Test §5]
- Periergia *Informal Fallacies* already teaches appeal to authority, Walton's critical questions and the Painter chromosome case (24 vs 23 pairs, corrected 1956) and points forward to this chapter "asks when trust is rational" — do not repeat; build on it. [source: Periergia fallacies chapter]

## 9. Peer disagreement (ARGUMENT 4)

- **Epistemic peer**: "someone who is roughly as likely as oneself to get it right on the matter at hand" (prior to the disagreement). **Conciliationism**: some revision is required; its most demanding form is the **Equal Weight View** (Christensen 2007; Elga 2007; Feldman 2006, 2007; Matheson 2015). [source: SEP-Soc §3.2]
- Equal Weight View = three claims (Frances/SEP-Dis): **Defeat** (learning that a peer disagrees gives you a reason to think you are mistaken); **Equal Weight** (that reason is as strong as your reason from your own opinion); **Independence** (reasons to discount the peer must be independent of the disagreement itself). [source: SEP-Dis §5.1]
- **Christensen's Restaurant Check Case** (Christensen 2007: 193; "Epistemology of Disagreement: The Good News", *Philosophical Review* 116: 187–218), quoted: "Suppose that five of us go out to dinner. It's time to pay the check, so the question we're interested in is how much we each owe. We can all see the bill total clearly, we all agree to give a 20 percent tip, and we further agree to split the whole cost evenly… I do the math in my head and become highly confident that our shares are $43 each. Meanwhile, my friend does the math in her head and becomes highly confident that our shares are $45 each." With equal track records and no special reason to think either is sharp or dull, Christensen says he should become "significantly less confident" in $43 and give the two roughly **equal credence**. [source: SEP-Dis §5.1]
  - Step by step: (1) Christensen and friend are peers at mental arithmetic; (2) they reach different answers from the same evidence; (3) nothing independent of the disagreement favours either; (4) so the disagreement is evidence that one of them erred, equally likely either (Defeat + Equal Weight); (5) so each should suspend judgement / split credence (Independence blocks "but I got $43, so she's wrong").
  - "Splitting the difference" applies to attitudes, not contents: it does **not** mean believing the shares are $44. Credences 0.7 vs 0.3 → 0.5 each. [source: SEP-Dis §5.1]
  - Supporting analogy: two equally reliable thermometers reading 75 and 72. Feldman's "Dean on the Quad" case (2007: 207–208): one sees the dean on the quad, the other sees no one. [source: SEP-Dis §5.1–5.2]
- **Steadfast View** (sticking to your guns can be rational): **Kelly 2005** ("The Epistemic Significance of Disagreement", *Oxford Studies in Epistemology* vol. 1) distinguishes (1) P is true, (2) evidence E is good evidence for P, (3) a competent peer believes P on E; (3) is evidence for (2) but not for (1); counting the peer's belief as extra evidence would **double-count** E. O'Connor's version: the disagreement "is not evidence bearing on whether interest rates will rise", so it is irrelevant. Other steadfast routes: private/incommunicable evidence (van Inwagen 1996), seemings (Plantinga 2000). [source: SEP-Dis §5.2; SEP-Soc §3.2]
  - Equal-weight replies: private evidence cuts both ways (Feldman 2006); Müller-Lyer: a line can keep seeming longer after you learn it isn't; a forensic expert's verdict is new evidence over and above the forensic evidence. [source: SEP-Dis §5.2]
- Middle positions: Kelly's **Total Evidence View** (2010: "[If] … your opinion is a reasonable response to the evidence while mine is not, then you are not required to give equal weight to my opinion…"); Lackey's **Justificationist** view (2010). [source: SEP-Soc §3.2; SEP-Dis §5]
- **Self-defeat objection** to the Equal Weight View: disagreement about disagreement is widespread, so a conciliationist who meets enough well-placed opponents must, by her own rule, give up the view. Plantinga (2000a: 522): "self-referentially inconsistent"; Elga (2010: 179): "incoherent" and "self-undermining". **Replies**: (a) at worst the view cannot be reasonably believed in some circumstances, which does not make it false (compare "No true sentences have more than 5 words"); (b) **Christensen's Minimal Humility** (2009: 763): if I thought casually about P for 10 minutes and then find that 1000 smarter, better-informed people have independently and unanimously judged P false, I am not justified in believing P — highly plausible, yet it too can call for its own rejection; (c) conciliatory principles are **self-exempting** (Elga 2010: fundamental principles must be dogmatic about their own correctness; Bogardus 2009; Pittard 2015), criticised as arbitrary (Pittard 2015, Blessenohl 2015). [source: SEP-Dis §5.5.1]
- **Numbers**: on controversial matters you know millions agree and millions disagree; if the numbers matter, disagreement threatens scepticism about most views in science, religion, politics and philosophy (Frances). "Deep" disagreements (Fogelin 1985). [source: SEP-Dis §6; SEP-Soc §3.2] (Cross-ref *Scepticism* — Agrippa's first mode is disagreement.)

## 10. Collective knowledge: wisdom of crowds and Condorcet (worked example)

- **Galton's ox**: at a 1906 livestock fair (Plymouth — West of England Fat Stock and Poultry Exhibition per common accounts; WP-Wisdom says "Plymouth"), nearly 800 people guessed the dressed weight of an ox. Galton, "Vox Populi", *Nature* 75 (no. 1949), 7 March 1907, pp. 450–451, reported the **median** ("the middlemost estimate expresses the *vox populi*, every other estimate being condemned as too low or too high by a majority of the voters") as **1,207 lb**, against the judges' measured weight of **1,198 lb** (within 0.8%; WP-Wisdom says "within 1%"). In a reply ("The Ballot Box", *Nature*, 28 March 1907) he gave the **mean** as **1,197 lb**. **Wallis (2014, *Statistical Science* 29(3): 420–424)** found transcription slips: the median was actually **1,208 lb** and the dressed weight **1,197 lb**, so the **mean had zero error**. [source: WP-Francis_Galton; WP-Wisdom_of_the_crowd] → Give "about 800" entries, median 1,207 (as published), true weight 1,198 (as published), and note Wallis's correction. Surowiecki's *The Wisdom of Crowds* (2004) opens with this example. [source: WP-Galton]
- Aristotle, *Politics* (credited by WP as the first statement): the many, "though not individually good men", may collectively be better, "just as public dinners to which many contribute are better than those supplied at one man's cost". [source: WP-Wisdom_of_the_crowd] (Translation unnamed — paraphrase only, or check *Politics* III.11 in a named translation.)
- **Condorcet's jury theorem** (Marquis de Condorcet, 1785, *Essai sur l'application de l'analyse à la probabilité des décisions rendues à la pluralité des voix*). Premises (Dietrich's modern statement): **Unconditional Independence** (individual correctness events are independent) and **Unconditional Competence** (each voter's probability of being right exceeds ½ and is the same for all). Conclusion: the probability that a majority is correct increases with (odd) group size and converges to 1. "Condorcet's text does not follow modern mathematics"; the formal statement is later (e.g. Grofman 1975). Intuition: "independent tosses of the same coin biased towards the truth". If p < ½, adding voters makes things worse: the best "jury" is one voter. Worked figures: n = 3, p = 0.8 → majority correct with probability 0.896. [source: SEP-Jury §§1, 2.1; WP-Condorcet's_jury_theorem]
  - Step by step: (1) each juror is right with p > ½; (2) jurors err independently; (3) by the law of large numbers the proportion of correct votes approaches p; (4) since p > ½, a majority is right with probability → 1.
  - **Main objection — common causes**: "Causal independence … by no means makes votes probabilistically independent, since common causes create correlations": shared evidence (e.g. witness reports), shared concepts/methods, shared context; the correlations are usually positive, which "reinforces tendencies and errors". [source: SEP-Jury §2.2] Natural link to echo chambers and to *tawātur*'s no-collusion condition.
  - **Reply**: later jury theorems weaken the premises (e.g. conditional independence given the evidence; competence-sensitive theorems); some derive rival conclusions (an optimal finite group size; fallibility of large bodies). [source: SEP-Jury §§1, 2]
- Wisdom-of-crowds effects depend on independence and diversity; "The wisdom of the crowd effect is easily undermined. Social influence can cause the average of the crowd's answers to be inaccurate"; Scott E. Page's diversity prediction theorem: "The squared error of the collective prediction equals the average squared error minus the predictive diversity". [source: WP-Wisdom_of_the_crowd]
- Epistemic democracy: Elizabeth Anderson (2006) sets out three epistemic models of democracy — Condorcet's theorem, "Diversity Trumps Ability", Dewey's experimentalism — and prefers Dewey's; List & Goodin (2001) and Landemore (2011) defend Condorcetian arguments. [source: SEP-Soc §5.1]

## 11. Group belief (brief)

- **Summativism**: a group believes p iff all or most members do (Quinton 1976: 17). **Margaret Gilbert**'s non-summativist joint-commitment account: "A group G believes that p if and only if the members of G are jointly committed to believe that p as a body" (Gilbert 1987, 1989 *On Social Facts*, 2004). Objections: it describes group *acceptance*, not belief (Wray 2001); joint commitment can have reasons unrelated to belief (Lackey 2021). Lackey's (2021) "Group Agent" account; Bird's "distributed model". [source: SEP-Soc §3.3]
- **Doctrinal paradox / discursive dilemma** (Kornhauser and Sager 1986): three judges; liability iff obligation and action. Judge 1: T, T → liable; Judge 2: T, F → not liable; Judge 3: F, T → not liable. Majority on premises: obligation T (2–1), action T (2–1); majority on conclusion: not liable (2–1). Each judge is consistent, the majority view is not. **List and Pettit (2002)** proved impossibility theorems for judgement aggregation; *Group Agency* (OUP 2011) offers escape routes. [source: SEP-Soc §4.1]

## 12. Epistemic injustice (ARGUMENT 5)

- **Miranda Fricker**, *Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing* (Oxford University Press, 2007). Epistemic injustice is "a wrong done to someone specifically in their capacity as a knower" (2007: 1). Fricker (born 12 March 1966) is now Julius Silver Professor of Philosophy at NYU; WP says she coined the term in a 1998 *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* paper ("Rational Authority and Social Power: Towards a Truly Social Epistemology"). **Do not confuse with Elizabeth Fricker** (the testimony reductionist of §2). [source: IEP-EI §1; WP-Miranda_Fricker; WP-Epistemic_injustice]
- **Testimonial injustice**: "prejudice or bias causes a hearer to give a deflated level of credibility to what someone says" (Sinclair's gloss); central case: identity prejudice producing a **credibility deficit**. Credibility judgements work like perception, shaped by the "social imagination" and stereotypes ("widely held associations between a given social group and one or more attributes", 2007: 30). [source: IEP-EI §§1–3]
- Fricker's examples: **Tom Robinson** in *To Kill a Mockingbird* (Harper Lee, 1960), a Black man on trial for raping a white woman whose testimony was "prejudicially rejected by the all-white jury"; **Marge Sherwood** in *The Talented Mr Ripley* (Fricker uses Anthony Minghella's screenplay): Herbert Greenleaf dismisses her suspicion of Ripley — based on Ripley having Dickie's rings, which Dickie said he would never remove — as "female intuition" (2007: 9, 87). [source: SEP-Soc §5.3; IEP-EI §§2, 4] (The date of Lee's novel is from general knowledge, not checked here — omit or verify.)
- Harm: primary harm — being wronged as a knower (an intrinsic injustice); secondary harms — practical (e.g. wrongful conviction) and epistemic. Remedy: the virtue of **testimonial justice**, a "reflexive critical social awareness" correcting for identity prejudice (2007: 91). [source: IEP-EI §§3–4; SEP-FSE]
- **Hermeneutical injustice**: "a gap in our collective interpretive resources unfairly disadvantages someone in terms of understanding their own specific social experiences"; it is "purely structural" — no perpetrator (2007: 159); tied to "hermeneutical marginalization" (2007: 153). Examples: **sexual harassment before the concept existed** (2007: 149–150); post-partum depression before recognition. Asymmetry: the harasser is not harmed by the gap, the harassed is. Welfare-state analogy: free healthcare but no dental cover (2007: 161–162). [source: IEP-EI §5; SEP-Soc §5.3]
- **Carmita Wood**: administrative assistant to the Cornell nuclear physicist Boyce McDaniel; after persistent advances (including forcible kissing at a Christmas party) and physical stress symptoms, and a denied transfer, she resigned; Cornell refused unemployment benefits, saying she had quit for "personal reasons". Cornell's Human Affairs Program staff Lin Farley, Susan Meyer and Karen Sauvigné took up her case; in a 1974 Cornell consciousness-raising class Farley had already begun to name the problem "sexual harassment"; Working Women United was formed and held the first "Speak Out" on sexual harassment, Ithaca, 4 May 1975, attended by 275 women; Farley testified to the New York City Human Rights Commission in April 1975. [source: WP-Working_Women_United; WP-Lin_Farley] Fricker's source for Wood is **Susan Brownmiller, *In Our Time: Memoir of a Revolution* (Dial Press, 1999)** [source: WS-6 (Fricker uses Brownmiller); WP-Susan_Brownmiller (book and date)]. Brownmiller (1935–2025) is best known for *Against Our Will* (1975). [source: WP-Susan_Brownmiller] (Resignation date conflicts: WP-WWU says both "In 1975" and "In June 1974" — say "in the mid-1970s" or "1974–75".)
- **Kristie Dotson, "Tracking Epistemic Violence, Tracking Practices of Silencing"**, *Hypatia* 26(2) (2011): 236–257: two silencing practices, both "epistemic violence": **testimonial quieting** — the audience fails to recognise the speaker as a knower and so gives no uptake; **testimonial smothering** — "a coerced truncation of one's testimony": a speaker, seeing her audience is unwilling or unable to give uptake, limits her testimony "to insure that the testimony contains only content for which one's audience demonstrates testimonial competence" (2011: 244). Dotson (2012) adds **contributory injustice** (dominant groups refuse to use marginalised groups' own hermeneutical resources), building on Pohlhaus's "willful hermeneutical ignorance". [source: SEP-FSE; WP-Epistemic_injustice]
- **José Medina**: criticises Fricker for treating hermeneutical resources as shared by society as a whole, ignoring plural interpretive communities (Mason 2011; Medina 2012); stresses credibility **excess** as well as deficit (Medina 2011); links testimonial and hermeneutical injustice and argues for an "epistemology of resistance" — *The Epistemology of Resistance: Gender and Race Oppression, Epistemic Injustice, and Resistant Imaginations* (OUP, 2013); also *The Epistemology of Protest* (OUP, 2023). [source: SEP-FSE; IEP-EI §6; WP-Epistemic_injustice]
- Other critiques: Anderson (2012) wants structural, institutional remedies, not only individual virtue; Davis (2016) on credibility excess from positive stereotypes; Berenstain's critique that Fricker overlooked resources Black women had developed to talk about harassment [WS-6 only]. Charles Mills's "white ignorance" (2007). [source: IEP-EI §6; SEP-Soc §5.3]

## 13. Misinformation, echo chambers, conspiracy theories, trust in science

- **Misinformation vs disinformation**: misinformation is false or inaccurate content not intended to mislead; disinformation is intended to mislead (Fallis 2016; Floridi 2013); "malinformation" is intended to mislead but may be true (Wardle and Derakhshan 2017); misleading content need not be false (O'Connor and Weatherall 2019). [source: SEP-Soc §5.2]
- **C. Thi Nguyen, "Echo Chambers and Epistemic Bubbles"**, *Episteme* 17(2) (June 2020): 141–161 (doi 10.1017/epi.2018.32). "An epistemic bubble is a social epistemic structure in which other relevant voices have been left out, perhaps accidentally"; "An echo chamber is a social epistemic structure from which other relevant voices have been actively excluded and discredited." Bubbles are popped by exposure to the missing information; in an echo chamber, exposure to contrary evidence can *reinforce* belief, because outsiders are already discredited — it works by manipulating **trust**. Members may follow apparently acceptable epistemic practices and still be misled (e.g. if raised inside one). [source: SEP-Soc §5.2; WS-7; WP-Echo_chamber_(media)] (Exact wording from WS-7, drafts and the WP article; Nguyen's final published text not read.) Nguyen (born 1977/78) is professor at the University of Utah. [source: WP-C._Thi_Nguyen]
- Filter bubbles: algorithms feeding users confirming content (Pariser 2011); platforms' engagement incentives cut against accuracy. [source: SEP-Soc §5.2]
- **Conspiracy theories**: Popper put them on the agenda ("the conspiracy theory of society", *Open Society*); **Brian Keeley, "Of Conspiracy Theories"**, *Journal of Philosophy* 96(3) (1999): 109–126, defined a conspiracy theory as an explanation of an event by "the causal agency of a small group of people acting in secret" (1999: 116), tried to characterise **unwarranted** conspiracy theories, stressed their appeal to "errant data" (1999: 117), argued that falsifiability is not a good test (evidence against is expected to be hidden), that the more institutions a theory implicates the less believable it is (1999: 122), and that such theories cast doubt on the very institutions that guarantee reliable data (1999: 121ff.). Pigden (1995) critiqued Popper. **Generalists** (conspiracy theories as such are epistemically suspect) vs **particularists** (judge each on its merits) — Buenting and Taylor 2010. David Coady (2006) defines them as opposed to the official account. [source: IEP-CT §§1–3] (Note: **David Coady** on conspiracy theories ≠ **C. A. J. Coady** on testimony.)
- **Quassim Cassam** (born 1961, Mombasa; Warwick): *Conspiracy Theories* (Polity, 2019) and *Vices of the Mind: From the Intellectual to the Political* (OUP, 2019); "vice epistemology"; conspiracy belief traced to intellectual vices such as gullibility, carelessness and closed-mindedness; "epistemic insouciance", "a careless attitude towards expertise". [source: WP-Quassim_Cassam; WP-Conspiracy_theory; SEP-Soc §5.2]
- **Naomi Oreskes and Erik M. Conway, *Merchants of Doubt: How a Handful of Scientists Obscured the Truth on Issues from Tobacco Smoke to Global Warming*** (Bloomsbury, 3 June 2010): a few politically conservative, anti-communist physicists — Frederick Seitz, S. Fred Singer, William Nierenberg (and Robert Jastrow) — with industry ties helped "keep the controversy alive" on tobacco, acid rain, ozone and climate long after scientific consensus. Philip Kitcher (in *Science*) called the authors "two outstanding historians". [source: WP-Merchants_of_Doubt]
- **Oreskes, *Why Trust Science?*** (Princeton University Press, 2019; ed. Stephen Macedo; based on her Tanner Lectures at Princeton, with responses by Edenhofer and Kowarsch, Krosnick, Marc Lange and Susan Lindee): there is no single scientific method; science's trustworthiness lies in its "sustained engagement with the world" and its "social character" — claims are trustworthy because of the social process by which they are critically vetted; consensus, diversity and openness are the indicators. [source: WP-Naomi_Oreskes (book details); WS-8 (content, from publisher/reviews)]
- **Wikipedia reliability**: *Nature* (December 2005) compared 42 science articles: 162 errors/omissions/misleading statements in Wikipedia vs 123 in *Britannica* (about 4:3); serious errors 4 vs 4. *Britannica* called the study "fatally flawed" (excerpts used, reviewers' claims not checked, etc.); *Nature* rebutted. A 2008 *Reference Services Review* study of nine history entries: Wikipedia 80% accurate vs 95–96% for the other reference works. [source: WP-Reliability_of_Wikipedia] Philosophers' "wikipistemology": Wikipedia entries as multi-authored or group testimony (Tollefsen 2009; Fallis 2008, 2011). [source: SEP-Soc §3.1; SEP-Test §6]
- Network models: O'Connor and Weatherall, *The Misinformation Age: How False Beliefs Spread* (Yale University Press, 2019). [source: SEP-Soc bibliography]

## 14. Social epistemology as a field

- Definition: social epistemology "is concerned with how people can best pursue the truth with the help of, or sometimes in the face of, other people or relevant social practices and institutions", including truth-pursuit by groups. [source: SEP-Soc §1]
- Background: the sociological "Strong Program" (Bloor, Collins; Bloor's "symmetry thesis") and Science and Technology Studies; Kuhn; the "science wars". Contemporary (analytic) social epistemology sees itself "as departing sharply from the debunking themes". [source: SEP-Soc §2]
- **Alvin Goldman, *Knowledge in a Social World*** (Oxford University Press, 1999) — "the seminal defense", its influence "hard to overstate"; **veritism**: evaluate social practices by how reliably they produce true beliefs (1999: 5). Goldman founded *Episteme* in 2004. "Systems-oriented" social epistemology (Goldman 2010). [source: SEP-Soc §§2, 5.1]
- **Steve Fuller** founded the journal *Social Epistemology: A Journal of Knowledge, Culture, and Policy* in 1987 and wrote *Social Epistemology* (Indiana University Press, 1988); his approach is interdisciplinary and STS-oriented, contrasted with Goldman's (Collin 2013). The 1987 *Synthese* issue edited by Fred Schmitt included both. [source: SEP-Soc §2]
- Kitcher, "The Division of Cognitive Labor" (1990); Longino; Solomon; Gilbert (1989) on plural subjects. [source: SEP-Soc §2, §4.2]

## Debates

- **Reductionism vs non-reductionism vs hybrid** — Hume, E. Fricker, Adler, Lipton, Kenyon vs Reid, Coady, Burge, Plantinga and others vs Lackey, E. Fricker 1995 (children), Faulkner; Leonard: whether reductionist replies succeed "remains an open question", and whether hybrids succeed "is still very much an open debate". Present as live. [source: SEP-Test §1]
- **Is Hume really a global reductionist?** IEP-Test: "Few contemporary philosophers will endorse Hume's reductionist or inferentialist approach … in anything close to full form", and Coady shows Hume himself relies on others' testimony in building his inductive base; SEP-Test lists Traiger, Faulkner, Root, Fogelin, van Cleve, Gelfert, Shieber "for more on Hume's view in particular" — interpretations differ (e.g. Root 2001, "Hume on the Virtues of Testimony"). Present as an interpretive debate; do not assert one reading. [source: SEP-Test §1.1; IEP-Test §1]
- **Transmission vs generation** — Burge, Audi, Williamson, etc. vs Lackey, Graham, Goldberg, Pelling. [source: SEP-Test §2]
- **Peer disagreement** — Equal Weight (Christensen, Elga, Feldman, Matheson) vs Steadfast (Kelly 2005, van Inwagen, Plantinga) vs Total Evidence (Kelly 2010) / Justificationist (Lackey 2010); self-defeat objection and replies. [source: SEP-Dis §5]
- **Can a non-expert rationally identify experts?** Hardwig (reliance must be blind) vs Goldman (five sources of evidence). [source: SEP-Dis §4]
- **Fricker's critics** — Medina and Mason (plural hermeneutical resources), Dotson (contributory injustice; quieting/smothering), Anderson (structural remedies), Davis/Medina (credibility excess). [source: SEP-FSE; IEP-EI §6]
- **Generalism vs particularism about conspiracy theories** — Popper/Keeley/Cassam lean generalist about unwarranted ones; Pigden, D. Coady, Dentith particularist. [source: IEP-CT]
- **Nyāya vs Mīmāṃsā on speaker's intention** (Prābhākara: a parrot or tape-recorder can make us know; Nyāya: knowing it is a parrot blocks knowledge) — a classical Indian version of the "learning from words vs learning from speakers" dispute. [source: SEP-IndEp §6; SEP-Gan §7] (Parallel to Lackey is the chapter's own observation.)
- **How reliable are isnāds?** Traditional hadith science vs Schacht (1950) and modern scepticism; ICMA (Motzki, Schoeler 1996). [source: WP-Isnad]
- **Do the Mohist "tests" concern truth or right guidance?** Loy: better read as criteria for normative soundness, not factual truth; Fraser: the Mohists run descriptive and normative together under shì/fēi. [source: IEP-Moz §5; SEP-Moh]
