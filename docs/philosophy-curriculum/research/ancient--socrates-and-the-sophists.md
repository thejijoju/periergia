## 1. The setting: the "turn" to human affairs, democracy and the demand for rhetoric
- In the fifth century BCE the word *sophistēs* (from *sophia*, wisdom), originally "sage" or "expert", came also to mean a new type of professional educator who toured the Greek world teaching a wide range of subjects, with emphasis on public speaking and the successful conduct of life. [source: SEP-Soph]
- SEP-Soph lists the causes: growing wealth of Greek cities (especially Athens) created demand for higher education beyond literacy, arithmetic, music and physical training; "the increase in participatory democracy, especially in Athens, led to a demand for success in political and forensic oratory"; and a rationalistic climate of thought on morality, religion and politics. [source: SEP-Soph]
- The sophists "belonged to no organization, shared no common body of beliefs and founded no schools"; there is "no canonical list" of sophists, and their writings are almost entirely lost. [source: SEP-Soph]
- Athenian men took part in the Assembly and in the city's many courts; "those who could afford it prepared themselves for success at public life by studying with rhetoricians and sophists from abroad". [source: SEP-Soc §1]
- Jurors were paid; by Socrates' time juries were largely made up of "elderly, disabled, and impoverished volunteers who needed the meager three-obol pay". [source: SEP-Soc §3]
- Most of what we know of the sophists comes from Plato, whose "strenuous efforts" to separate philosophers from sophists (and to show the Athenians were wrong to class Socrates as one) colour the picture; "the sophists have been underrated by philosophers going all the way back to Plato". The modern sense of "sophist" as a dishonest arguer derives from Plato's hostile portrait, which "considerably distorts" the historical reality. [source: SEP-Soph]
- Socrates himself (469–399 BCE) is "a central figure of the sophistic period", with "important parallels with the sophists, whether or not one agrees with Plato's efforts to categorize him as an authentic philosopher". [source: SEP-Soph]
- Aristotle reports that Socrates concerned himself with ethics, not nature, and was the first to ask the "what is it?" (*ti esti*) question and to use *epagōgē* (argument from analogous cases); he did not separate universals from particulars, unlike Plato (Metaphysics I.6, 987a29–b14; XIII.4, 1078b12–34). [source: IEP-Soc §1.b.v; SEP-Plato §11 cites Metaphysics 987b1 and Sophistical Refutations 183b7]
- Caution on the "turn from nature to man" cliché: IEP notes that calling the earlier thinkers "Presocratics" as if they ignored ethics "is misleading, for we have evidence that a number of Presocratics explored ethical issues", and some "Presocratics" were Socrates' contemporaries. [source: IEP-Soc §2.a]
- Plato's Socrates, in the Phaedo (96a–100a), describes an early interest in natural inquiry (eagerly acquiring Anaxagoras' books, Phaedo 98b) that he later abandoned. [source: SEP-Soc §2.1, §3]

## 2. Fees and celebrity
- At Apology 19e–20c Socrates names four men who "educate people" for fees: Gorgias of Leontini, Prodicus of Ceos, Hippias of Elis and Euenus of Paros. [source: SEP-Soph §4]
- Hippias boasts (Greater Hippias 282e) of earning more than 150 minas (15,000 drachmas, "something in the region of thirty years' wages for a skilled craftsman") on one visit to Sicily. [source: SEP-Soph §4]
- Socrates says (Meno 91d) Protagoras earned more than ten sculptors, including Phidias. [source: SEP-Soph §4]
- Socrates says (Cratylus 384b) he attended Prodicus' one-drachma lecture on names but could not afford the full fifty-drachma course; a drachma was about a day's wage for a skilled craftsman. [source: SEP-Soph §4]
- Socrates, by contrast, refused all his life to take money and insisted he was not a teacher (Apology 33a–b). [source: SEP-Soc §1]
- Gorgias, Hippias, Prodicus and possibly Thrasymachus served as diplomatic representatives of their cities. [source: SEP-Soph §4]
- Conservative Athenians such as Anytus (Meno 89e–94e) saw the sophists as subversive, "to a considerable extent unfairly". [source: SEP-Soph §4]

## 3. Protagoras of Abdera
- Born at Abdera in Thrace, c. 490 BCE (SEP-Soph "ca. 490–420"; SEP-Prot "490–420 BCE ca", birth in "the 490s"); Meno 91e says he practised as a sophist for over forty years and died at about seventy, so c. 420 BCE (other ancient sources say ninety years). [source: SEP-Prot §1.1; SEP-Soph §1]
- In Plato's Protagoras (316c–317b) he says he was the first to call himself openly a "sophist" and to claim to teach. [source: SEP-Prot §1.1; SEP-Soph §1]
- He claims to teach *euboulia*, "good deliberation": "good deliberation about household matters ... and about those of the city, so as to be most capable of acting and speaking in the city's interest" (Protagoras 318e–319a = DK 80A5). [source: SEP-Prot §2]
- In 444 BCE Pericles entrusted him with drafting the constitution (law-code) of the new pan-Hellenic colony of Thurii in southern Italy (Diogenes Laertius 9.50; SEP-Soph dates it 444/3). [source: SEP-Prot §1.1; SEP-Soph §4]
- Stories that he was tried for impiety, that his books were burned and that he drowned fleeing Athens are "probably fictitious"; they conflict with Plato's picture (Meno 91e) of a man who died universally esteemed. [source: SEP-Prot §§1.1, 4; SEP-Soph §3]
- Measure fragment, DK 80B1, the opening sentence of his work *Truth* (*Alētheia*), preserved in Plato, Theaetetus 151e–152a, and Sextus Empiricus, Against the Mathematicians 7.60 (see Quotations). [source: SEP-Soph §1; SEP-Prot §2.1]
- Fewer than ten authentic fragments survive in Diels-Kranz. [source: SEP-Prot §1.2]
- Theaetetus treatment: Plato equates the measure doctrine with "knowledge is perception" and paraphrases it (Theaetetus 152a) as "just as each thing appears to me, so too it is for me, and just as it appears to you, so too again for you" — e.g. the wind cold to one person and warm to another. [source: SEP-Prot §2.1]
- In the same dialogue Plato also has Protagoras (167c) hold that "whatever things seem to each city to be fine and just are so for that city, so long as it maintains them" (social relativism about value), and the "Apology of Protagoras" (Theaetetus 166a–168c) says the wise man changes worse appearances into better ones, as a doctor does — implying some judgments are better than others. SEP-Soph concludes the Theaetetus represents him as holding (a) universal subjectivism, (b) social relativism in morality and (c) objective realism about advantage, which are mutually inconsistent. [source: SEP-Soph §1; SEP-Prot §2.2]
- The Theaetetus is set in 399 BCE, after Protagoras' death, so his "Apology" there is Plato's reconstruction, not a fragment. [source: SEP-Prot §2.2]
- Self-refutation (*peritropē*) objection: first formulated by Democritus (DK 80A15), developed by Plato (Theaetetus 170e–171c; Euthydemus) and Aristotle (Metaphysics IV): if every judgment is true, then the judgment that the measure doctrine is false is true, so the doctrine is false. [source: SEP-Prot §2.2; SEP-Soph §1]
- Agnosticism fragment, DK 80B4, the opening of *On the Gods* (see Quotations). SEP-Prot calls him "the first thinker to take an agnostic stance", not an atheist: hostile ancient sources conflate "I do not know that P" with "I know that not-P". [source: SEP-Prot §4]
- Other reported claims: that on every matter there are two opposed *logoi* (DK 80B6a, Diogenes Laertius 9.51) and that he could "make the weaker *logos* stronger" (Aristotle, Rhetoric 1402a23–5 = DK 80B6b). SEP-Soph argues the latter was a sales pitch for forensic rhetoric about arguments from probability, not necessarily dishonest. [source: SEP-Soph §1]
- Linguistic pioneer: distinguished grammatical genders; criticised Homer's "Sing, goddess" as a command rather than a prayer (Aristotle, Poetics 1456b15–16). [source: SEP-Soph §1; SEP-Prot §3.1]
- The "Great Speech" in Plato's Protagoras (320c–328d), with the myth of Prometheus and Epimetheus: Zeus sends Hermes to give justice (*dikē*) and shame (*aidōs*) to all humans, not to a few experts — widely read as a theoretical defence of Athenian participatory democracy (Kerferd; Farrar 1988). [source: SEP-Prot §§5.1, 5.4; SEP-Soph §1]

## 4. Gorgias of Leontini
- Dates: SEP-Soph "ca. 485–380 BC"; IEP-Gorg "483-375 B.C.E."; WP-Gorgias born c. 483, died c. 375 aged c. 108, at Larissa in Thessaly. Ancient longevity claim; treat as approximate. [source: SEP-Soph; IEP-Gorg; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gorgias]
- In 427 BCE, aged about sixty, he led an embassy from Leontini to Athens seeking help against Syracuse, and dazzled Athenian audiences with his speeches. [source: WP-Gorgias; IEP-Gorg; SEP-Soc §3 timeline "427 Gorgias in Athens"]
- Meno (95c) says Gorgias never claimed to teach virtue and laughed at those who did; in Plato's Gorgias he presents persuasion as a value-neutral technique (456a–457c) but is induced to agree the orator must know right and wrong (460a–c). [source: SEP-Soph §4]
- *On Not-Being* (or *On Nature*, DK 82B3), surviving only in two paraphrases (Sextus Empiricus and the anonymous *On Melissus, Xenophanes and Gorgias*), argues three theses: nothing exists; if anything existed it could not be known; if it could be known it could not be communicated. Whether it is parody of the Eleatics or serious philosophy is disputed. [source: SEP-Soph §4; IEP-Gorg]
- *Encomium of Helen* (DK 82B11): Helen went to Troy through (per WP) the gods/fate, force, love or persuasion by speech, and in each case is blameless; speech (*logos*) is called a "powerful lord" (B11.8) and its effect on the soul compared to that of drugs on the body (B11.14). IEP-Gorg lists only three causes (force, love, logos); WP-Gorgias lists four (gods, force, love, speech) — use four, the standard reading, or say "several". [source: IEP-Gorg; WP-Gorgias]
- Also extant: *Defence of Palamedes* (DK 82B11a). [source: SEP-Soph §4; IEP-Gorg]
- Plato's Gorgias calls rhetoric "a phantom of a branch of statesmanship" (463d), a form of flattery, not an art (465a). [source: IEP-Gorg]

## 5. Prodicus, Hippias, Thrasymachus, Antiphon, Callicles
- Prodicus of Ceos (c. 465 – c. 395 BCE per WP), envoy of Ceos to Athens; known for fine distinctions between near-synonyms (Protagoras 337a–c); still alive in 399 (Apology 19e). [source: SEP-Soph §4; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prodicus]
- Prodicus' fable of the Choice of Heracles between Virtue and Vice survives only in Xenophon's paraphrase (Memorabilia 2.1.21–34). [source: SEP-Soph §4]
- Prodicus held that the names of gods were first given to useful things (sun, rivers, crops) or to their discoverers (DK 84B5), and was counted an atheist in antiquity (dissent: Sedley 2013). [source: SEP-Soph §3]
- Hippias of Elis (born c. 460 per WP; "active in the last third of the 5th century" per SEP), a polymath: lectured on astronomy, mathematics, genealogy and history; claimed a mnemonic technique; came to Olympia wearing nothing he had not made himself, down to his signet ring (Lesser Hippias 368b); compiled a list of Olympic victors. [source: SEP-Soph §4; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippias]
- Hippias in Plato's Protagoras (337c–d): intellectuals from different cities are "by nature" kin, though divided by convention — an appeal to *phusis* against *nomos* without immoralist conclusions. [source: SEP-Soph §2]
- Thrasymachus of Chalcedon: a teacher of rhetoric and speechwriter in Athens by 427 BCE (mocked in Aristophanes' Banqueters of that year); most active in the last three decades of the fifth century. [source: IEP-Thras §1]
- In Republic I Thrasymachus makes three claims about justice that are in tension: justice is the advantage of the stronger (338c); justice is obedience to laws (339b); justice is the good of another (343c). Scholars divide over which is central (natural-right reading: Wilamowitz, Zeller, Strauss; legalist/relativist: Grote, Hourani; ethical egoist: Kerferd, Nicholson; nihilist: A. E. Taylor, Burnet; or simply confused: Cross and Woozley). [source: IEP-Thras §2]
- Fragment DK 85B8: the gods do not care about human affairs, since they do not enforce justice. Whether Plato's Thrasymachus is historical is doubtful. [source: IEP-Thras §2]
- Antiphon: a papyrus fragment (DK 87B44, On Truth) contrasts conventional morality with nature to the disadvantage of the former: nature prompts us to our advantage, and breaches of convention harm us only if we are found out. [source: SEP-Soph §2]
- Whether "Antiphon the Sophist" is the same person as Antiphon the orator of Rhamnus (an oligarch of the 411 coup) is an open controversy: Gagarin and Laks–Most think one person, Pendrick two. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophistic_works_of_Antiphon]
- Callicles (Plato's Gorgias, 483e) holds that conventional morality is a contrivance of the weak and that by "the law of nature" the strong should take more; he may be a Platonic invention (known only from Plato). [source: SEP-Soph intro and §2]

## 6. The *nomos* / *phusis* debate
- The debate was "about the status of moral and other social norms": are they grounded in nature or mere human convention? Both sides treated nature as authoritative. [source: SEP-Soph §2]
- Anti-conventionalists: Callicles (Gorgias), Thrasymachus (Republic I), Glaucon's restatement with the Ring of Gyges (Republic II), Antiphon (DK 87B44). [source: SEP-Soph §2]
- Defenders of *nomos*: Protagoras' Great Speech (law and morality are natural developments needed for survival), Democritus, the Anonymus Iamblichi (DK 89), and appeals to unwritten natural law (Sophocles' Antigone; Hippias in Xenophon, Memorabilia 4.4.14–25). [source: SEP-Soph §2]
- "There is, then, no uniform sophistic position in the nomos-phusis debate." [source: SEP-Soph §2]
- Relativism caution: "Protagoras is the only sophist to whom ancient sources ascribe relativistic views, and even in his case the evidence is ambiguous" (citing Bett 1989). [source: SEP-Soph §1]

## 7. Socrates' life
- Born 469 BCE (SEP-Soc, IEP-Soc; WP gives "470 or 469"); executed 399 BCE, aged about 70. [source: SEP-Soc; IEP-Soc; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates]
- Deme Alopece (Alopeke), south-southeast of the city wall; tribe Antiochis. [source: SEP-Soc §3; WP-Socrates]
- Father Sophroniscus, a stoneworker (IEP: stonemason); mother Phaenarete, who became a midwife (Theaetetus 149a); she remarried and had a second son, Patrocles (Euthydemus 297e). [source: SEP-Soc §3; IEP-Soc §1.a.i]
- He wrote nothing. [source: SEP-Soc; IEP-Soc]
- Military service as a hoplite: Potidaea (from 432), where he saved the life and armour of the wounded Alcibiades (Symposium 220d–e); Delium (424), where his courage in the retreat is praised by Laches (Laches 181b) and Alcibiades (Symposium 221a); Amphipolis (422). [source: SEP-Soc §3; IEP-Soc §1.a.i]
- IEP-Soc says he fought at Delium "as one of 7,000 hoplites aside 20,000 troops". [source: IEP-Soc §1.a.i]
- Married Xanthippe (SEP: around 416–415, "at about this time"; WP: after 423, since Clouds presents him as unmarried); sons Lamprocles (b. c. 415/414), Sophroniscus and Menexenus. Some ancient sources report a second wife, Myrto; the Peripatetics spread a bigamy charge, refuted by the Stoic Panaetius. [source: SEP-Soc §3; IEP-Soc §§1.a.i, 4.a.v; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xanthippe]
- Plato mentions Xanthippe only once (Phaedo, with the youngest child, on the last day); the "shrewish Xanthippe" derives from Xenophon and later Diogenes Laertius, not Plato. [source: WP-Xanthippe; SEP-Soc §3]
- In 406 BCE, serving by lot on the presiding committee (prytanes) of the Council, he alone opposed the illegal collective trial of the generals after Arginusae (Apology 32b; Xenophon, Hellenica 1.7.15). [source: SEP-Soc §3]
- Under the Thirty (404–403) he refused an order to fetch Leon of Salamis for execution (Apology 32c–e); Critias and Charicles forbade him to talk with men under thirty (Xenophon, Memorabilia 1.2.35). [source: SEP-Soc §3; IEP-Soc §1.a.ii]
- Physical oddity: all extant sources agree he was ugly, satyr-like, snub-nosed, bulging-eyed, barefoot (Theaetetus 143e; Symposium 215a–c). SEP-Soc says the pot-belly is a later "iconic tradition" contradicted by references to going hungry; IEP-Soc says "many sources depict him with a potbelly" — sources disagree; avoid the pot-belly. [source: SEP-Soc §1; IEP-Soc §1.a.i]
- Plato (born 424/3 per SEP-Soc; 427 per IEP-Soc) was about twenty-five at the trial; Xenophon (c. 425 – c. 386) left Athens in 401 and was not at the trial. [source: SEP-Soc §2.1; IEP-Soc §1.b.iii]

## 8. The Delphic oracle and Socratic ignorance
- Socrates' friend Chaerephon asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than Socrates; the Pythia replied that no one was (Apology 20e–23b; 21a). [source: SEP-Soc §1; IEP-Soc §2.b.i]
- Puzzled, Socrates questioned politicians, then poets, then craftsmen; the politicians lacked wisdom, the poets composed by inspiration not wisdom, and the craftsmen knew their crafts but thought they knew much more. He concluded that human wisdom "is worth little or nothing" and that he was wiser only in not thinking he knew what he did not (Apology 21b–23b; 23a). [source: IEP-Soc §2.b.i; SEP-Short §11]
- Xenophon's version differs: the oracle said "no man was more free than I, more just, and more moderate" (Xenophon, Apology 14); in Xenophon it is not clear Chaerephon asked. [source: IEP-Soc §1.b.iii; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_know_that_I_know_nothing]
- IEP: "Socrates does not claim here that he knows nothing"; he says he is aware of his ignorance and that whatever he knows is worthless. "Simple ignorance" (aware) vs "double ignorance" (thinking one knows). [source: IEP-Soc §2.b.i]
- Socrates does claim moral knowledge at Apology 29b (that it is bad and shameful to do wrong and disobey one's better). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_know_that_I_know_nothing]
- His mission, as he understood the oracle: to examine his fellow citizens and shame them into caring for their souls above wealth and reputation (Apology 29d–30b); he calls himself a gadfly on a sluggish horse (Apology 30e). [source: IEP-Soc §2.b.ii; SEP-Short §11]
- In the Theaetetus (149a–151d) he likens himself to a midwife: barren himself, he delivers others' ideas and tests them. [source: IEP-Soc §3.b]
- Socratic irony: Thrasymachus (Republic 337a), Callicles (Gorgias 489e) and Alcibiades (Symposium 216e, 218d) accuse him of *eirōneia*; Aristotle treats irony as self-deprecation (NE 4.7, 1127b23–26). What his irony amounts to is disputed. [source: IEP-Soc §2.d; SEP-Soc §1]

## 9. The elenchus (method of cross-examination)
- Rules: the interlocutor must answer according to his own beliefs; he, not the audience, judges the outcome; Socrates starts from the interlocutor's claim (usually to knowledge of a virtue), elicits agreement to further premises, and shows them inconsistent with the original claim; usually ends in the interlocutor's discomfiture and *aporia* (impasse). [source: SEP-Short §2]
- Socratic definition must meet (1) a generality requirement (covers every case), (2) an exclusion requirement (covers nothing else), (3) an explanatory requirement (states the essence, *ousia*, that makes things what they are, not a mere attribute, *pathos*). [source: SEP-Short §3]
- "The problem of the Socratic elenchus" (Vlastos): how can Socrates conclude a thesis is false when he has shown only that it is inconsistent with other premises he has not established? Constructivists (Vlastos) say the elenchus can establish truths because Socrates' own beliefs have survived repeated testing; non-constructivists (e.g. Hugh Benson) say it can only show inconsistency. [source: IEP-Soc §3.a.ii; SEP-Short §2]
- "Priority of definition" (you must know what piety is before you can know whether an act is pious) was attacked as the "Socratic fallacy" by Peter Geach; no text unequivocally commits Socrates to it. [source: SEP-Short §4]
- Worked example — LACHES (on courage), dramatic date 424, set out step by step [source: SEP-Short §15]:
  1. Premise (190a): an expert in instilling X must know what X is. Military training aims to instil courage, so an expert adviser must be able to say what courage is.
  2. Laches' first answer (190e): courage is staying at one's post and not running away. Refuted by the generality requirement (191a–d): there is courage in retreat (as Socrates showed at Delium), and in seafaring, illness, poverty and politics (191d).
  3. Laches' second answer (192c): courage is a kind of endurance of the soul. Premise agreed: courage is always fine (*kalon*). But foolish endurance is not fine, so courage cannot be any endurance; yet wise endurance seems less courageous (the expert diver is less brave than the ignorant one). Laches is caught (192c–193e) and admits defeat.
  4. Nicias' answer (195a): courage is knowledge of what is to be feared and dared. Socrates takes this as knowledge of future goods and evils; but knowledge of good and evil is not limited to the future, and would be the whole of virtue (199e) — whereas Nicias had agreed courage is only a part of virtue (198a, cf. 190c–d). Contradiction; aporia.
  5. Interpretive debate: the Protagoras (360d) defends almost Nicias' definition, so either the Laches refutes Socrates' own view, or (as with Euthyphro) the definition is true of courage but cannot differentiate it from the rest of virtue — pointing to the unity of virtue. "This reading is not obvious." [source: SEP-Short §§7, 15]
- Alternative example — EUTHYPHRO (on piety), on the steps of the king archon's stoa, 399 [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euthyphro ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euthyphro_dilemma ; SEP-Short §13; IEP-Soc §3.a.i]:
  1. Piety is what I am doing now, prosecuting a wrongdoer (5d–6e) — rejected as an example, not a definition.
  2. Piety is what is dear to the gods (6e–7a) — but the gods quarrel, so one act would be both pious and impious (7e–8a).
  3. Piety is what all the gods love (9e) — Socrates asks (10a) whether the pious is loved because it is pious or pious because it is loved; Euthyphro accepts the first, which shows "loved by all the gods" names an attribute, not the essence (11a–b).
  4. Piety is the part of justice concerned with care of the gods (12e); 5. knowledge of sacrifice and prayer (13d–14a), which collapses back into "what is dear to the gods"; Euthyphro leaves.

## 10. Socratic paradoxes and doctrines
- "Virtue is knowledge" (Meno 87e–89a; cf. Euthydemus 278d–282a): goods like health, beauty, wealth benefit only when guided by wisdom; so virtue, which always benefits, must be knowledge. [source: IEP-Soc §2.c.i]
- Unity of virtue (Protagoras 329b–333b etc.): justice, wisdom, courage, piety, temperance are somehow one; Vlastos read this as the biconditional "whoever has one has all"; Penner and Woodruff argue for a stronger identity thesis. [source: IEP-Soc §2.c.i; SEP-Short §7]
- No one does wrong (errs) willingly/knowingly (Protagoras 345c–e, 352c, 358b–d); denial of *akrasia* (weakness of will) — Socrates argues in the Protagoras (351b–358d) that the popular explanation "overcome by pleasure" is incoherent if good = pleasure and the agent can measure pleasures correctly. Plato holds the "no one errs voluntarily" thesis "consistently from the Apology to the Laws". [source: IEP-Soc §2.c.ii; SEP-Short §§9, 20]
- Corollary (Apology 25e–26a): if Socrates corrupted the young, he did so unwillingly, and the right response is instruction, not prosecution. [source: SEP-Soc §3; SEP-Short §9]
- Scholars dispute whether Socrates is committed to the Protagoras' hedonism (Socrates opposes hedonism in the Gorgias) or argues dialectically against Protagoras. [source: SEP-Short §§9, 20; SEP-Soc §2.2]
- All desire is for the good (Gorgias 467c–468b; Meno 77b–78b). [source: IEP-Soc §2.c.iii]
- Better to suffer injustice than to do it (Gorgias 475a–d); better to be punished than to escape punishment (476d–478e). [source: IEP-Soc §2.c.iv]
- "Bedrock principle": it is never right to do wrong, even in return for wrong (Crito 49a–b, 49d) — a rejection of the traditional "help friends, harm enemies". [source: SEP-Short §§1, 12]
- Socrates appears to be "the first to make *eudaimonia* (happiness, the good life) a goal in ethics"; whether virtue is identical to, part of, or instrumental to happiness is disputed (Vlastos vs Irwin). [source: SEP-Short §6; IEP-Soc §2.c.v]
- Socrates doubts virtue can be taught: if it could, Pericles would have taught his sons (Protagoras 319a–320c). [source: SEP-Short §20]

## 11. The daimonion
- From childhood Socrates heard a divine sign or voice (*daimonion*) that held him back from certain actions, some trivial, often unrelated to right and wrong (Apology 31c–d, 40a–b; Euthyphro 3b; Phaedrus 242b; Theaetetus 150c–151b; Republic 496c). In Plato it only forbids; Xenophon says it also gave positive commands (Memorabilia 1.1.4, 4.3.12). [source: IEP-Soc §1.a.ii.2; SEP-Soc §1]
- SEP-Soc warns it is "not to be confused with ... a superego or a conscience". [source: SEP-Soc §1]
- Euthyphro (3b) links it to the charge of "new divinities". [source: IEP-Soc §1.a.ii.2; SEP-Soc §3]

## 12. Political context: Alcibiades, Critias, the Thirty, the amnesty
- 415 BCE: mutilation of the herms and profanation of the Mysteries; associates of Socrates accused included Alcibiades, Charmides, Critias, Eryximachus and Phaedrus. Alcibiades, recalled from Sicily, defected to Sparta (414). [source: SEP-Soc §3; IEP-Soc §1.a.ii.1]
- Critias, a relative of Plato's family and member of Socrates' circle, led the Thirty (404–403); Charmides, also Plato's relative (IEP calls him Critias' nephew), was among the Ten in the Piraeus. Both were killed in 403 in the fighting with the democrats returning from Phyle (Munychia). [source: SEP-Soc §3; SEP-Short §14; IEP-Soc §1.a.ii.1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critias]
- The Thirty executed many wealthy Athenians and metics; ancient sources (Xenophon; Aristotle, Ath. Pol.) report 1,500 executed without trial. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty_Tyrants]
- SEP-Soc notes no contemporary source denies the legitimacy of the Thirty's election, so whether refusing the Leon order was "civil disobedience" is debated. [source: SEP-Soc §3]
- After the restoration (403) an amnesty barred prosecutions for acts under the Thirty; IEP argues the charges were therefore framed in religious terms. Anytus was a returned democratic exile. [source: IEP-Soc §1.a.ii.1]
- Socrates had friends and enemies among both oligarchs and democrats (Chaerephon was a democratic exile at Phyle). [source: SEP-Soc §§1, 3; IEP-Soc §1.a.ii.1]
- Polycrates' pamphlet *Accusation of Socrates* (c. 392 BCE) put a prosecution speech in Anytus' mouth attacking Socrates' political influence; Xenophon's Memorabilia replies to it. [source: WP-Trial, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trial_of_Socrates]

## 13. Trial and death, 399 BCE
- Accusers: Meletus (a young poet, who wrote the indictment), with Anytus and Lycon. [source: SEP-Soc §3; IEP-Soc §1.a.ii.1]
- Charge (as recorded by Diogenes Laertius 2.40, IEP translation): "Socrates does criminal wrong by not recognizing the gods that the city recognizes, and furthermore by introducing new divinities; and he also does criminal wrong by corrupting the youth." Other versions: Xenophon, Memorabilia 1.1.1; Plato, Apology 24b; Euthyphro 2c–3b. Penalty demanded: death. [source: IEP-Soc §1.a.ii.2; WP-Trial]
- The verb *nomizein* in the charge covers both belief and worship: "he does not *nomizei* the gods whom the city *nomizei*". [source: SEP-Soph §3]
- Procedure: summons, preliminary hearing before the king archon (the setting of the Euthyphro), trial in the month Thargelion (May–June 399), lasting most of a day, timed by water clock. Plato gives no speeches by the prosecution. [source: SEP-Soc §3]
- Socrates names Aristophanes' Clouds as the source of the "old accusations" (Apology 18a–b, 19c). [source: SEP-Soc §2.1]
- Jury size: neither Plato nor Xenophon states it; 501 is "probably the legal norm" (Diogenes Laertius 2.41 / Ath. Pol. 68 support 500 or 501). [source: WP-Trial]
- Votes for conviction: Plato says that if thirty votes had gone the other way he would have been acquitted (Apology 36a). With 501 jurors that implies 280 to 221 (WP-Trial); with 500, 280 to 220 (WP-Apology). These are inferences, not reported numbers in Plato. [source: WP-Trial; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apology_(Plato)]
- Meletus would have been fined had he not received one fifth of the votes (Apology 36a–b) — a rule against frivolous suits. [source: SEP-Soc §3; WP-Apology]
- Counter-penalty: Socrates first proposed free meals in the Prytaneum, an honour for benefactors and Olympic victors (Apology 36b–37a region; exact Stephanus line not checked — 36d is the usual citation); then a fine of one mina (100 drachmas), then thirty minae (3,000 drachmas) guaranteed by Plato, Crito, Critobulus and Apollodorus (Apology 38b). SEP-Soc: thirty minae was about six times his net worth (Xenophon, Oeconomicus 2.3.4–5). [source: WP-Apology; WP-Socrates; WP-Trial; SEP-Soc §3]
- The death penalty passed "by a greater majority than the majority by which he had been convicted" (WP-Trial, citing Diogenes Laertius 2.42). Hicks' Loeb translation of Diogenes (per search snippet of the Perseids text) says sentence of death was passed "with an accession of eighty fresh votes", and gives the conviction as "281 votes more than those given for acquittal" (a garbled figure) and the fine he proposed as 25 drachmae (Eubulides says 100). WP-Apology instead says Diogenes reports 280 for death and 220 for a fine. Treat exact figures as uncertain; safe wording: "a narrow conviction, and a larger majority for death". [source: WP-Trial; WP-Apology; WebSearch result for Perseids DL 2.5.38–47, https://cts.perseids.org/read/greekLit/tlg0004/tlg001/perseus-eng2/2.5.38-2.5.47]
- "The unexamined life..." is spoken in the penalty phase (38a), after the guilty verdict and BEFORE the death sentence (the second vote falls at 38b). [source: SEP-Soc §3 (votes at 35d and 38b); WP-unexamined]
- Execution was delayed because no executions were allowed while the sacred ship was on its annual voyage to Delos (Phaedo 58a–b); Xenophon says the voyage took 31 days in 399 (Memorabilia 4.8.2), so Socrates lived about 30 days after the trial. [source: SEP-Soc §3]
- Crito: his childhood friend Crito, having bribed the jailers, urges escape; Socrates refuses, arguing from the bedrock principle and voicing the personified Laws (agreement implied by choosing to live in Athens; the Laws' benefits like a parent's). The Laws' "persuade or obey" (51b–c) seems to conflict with Apology 29d, where Socrates says he would disobey an order to stop philosophising — a tension first pointed out by Grote; reconciliation disputed. [source: SEP-Short §12; SEP-Soc §2.2]
- Phaedo: Plato notes he was not himself present; the account is narrated by Phaedo of Elis at Phlius. Socrates argues for the immortality of the soul, bathes, drinks the poison. [source: SEP-Soc §§2.1, 3]
- Poison: Plato calls it simply *to pharmakon*, "the drug"; identification with poison hemlock (*Conium maculatum*) has been debated; Enid Bloch (2001/2002) argued Plato's description fits *Conium* poisoning; Christopher Gill argued Plato idealised the scene. [source: WP-Trial; SEP-Soc §3 cites Bloch 2001]
- In Apology Socrates is agnostic about death (dreamless sleep or conversation in the underworld), whereas the Phaedo argues for immortality. [source: SEP-Soc §3; SEP-Short §11; SEP-Plato §11]

## 14. The sources and the "Socratic problem"
- The difficulty of separating the historical Socrates from the Socrateses of the texts "is generally referred to as the Socratic problem". [source: SEP-Soc intro, §2]
- Aristophanes (c. 450 – c. 386): Clouds produced at the City Dionysia in 423 BCE, placed third (last of three); our text is an unproduced revision. Socrates runs a "thinkery" (*phrontistērion*), studies the heavens, teaches the "Weaker Argument". Kenneth Dover held that the character amalgamates traits of several intellectuals. Further jibes in Birds (414; the verb "to socratize") and Frogs (405). [source: SEP-Soc §2.1; IEP-Soc §1.b.ii; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Clouds]
- Note: SEP-Short §10 dates Clouds "421 BCE", an error or reference to the revision; SEP-Soc, IEP and WP give 423 for the production.
- Xenophon: practical Socrates who advises on money-making and estate management; emphasises self-mastery (*enkrateia*), endurance (*karteria*), self-sufficiency (*autarkeia*); attributes to Socrates an argument from design for God (Memorabilia 1.4.2–7). Lived in Erchia, left Athens in 401. SEP-Soc: his Socratic works are "pastiches", several of which "could not have occurred as presented". Leo Strauss championed Xenophon as the more reliable source. [source: SEP-Soc §2.1 and note 4; IEP-Soc §1.b.iii]
- Schleiermacher's essay "The Worth of Socrates as a Philosopher" (written 1815, published 1818) turned scholarship from Xenophon to Plato: if Socrates were only as Xenophon shows him, his influence would be inexplicable. [source: IEP-Soc §1.b.i; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socratic_problem]
- Plato: more realism in names, places and relationships than once suspected (Nails), but he "may have shaped the character Socrates ... to serve his own purposes"; Plato never speaks in his own dialogues. [source: SEP-Soc §2.1–2.2]
- Aristotle (born 384, fifteen years after Socrates' death) relied mostly on Plato and the Academy. [source: IEP-Soc §1.b.v]
- Other "Socratic discourses" (*logoi sokratikoi*, Aristotle's term, Poetics 1447b11) by Antisthenes, Aeschines of Sphettus (Alcibiades, Aspasia), Phaedo, Euclides and others survive only in fragments or titles. [source: SEP-Soc §2.1 and note 2; IEP-Soc §1.b]
- Nehamas: "with the exception of the Epicureans, every philosophical school in antiquity ... saw in him either its actual founder or the type of person to whom its adherents were to aspire". [source: SEP-Soc §2]

## 15. Aspasia and Diotima (with reliability caveats)
- Socrates claims to have learned rhetoric from Aspasia of Miletus, Pericles' partner (Plato, Menexenus), and erotics from Diotima of Mantinea (Symposium). [source: SEP-Soc §1]
- Aspasia: born probably no earlier than c. 470 BCE in Miletus; relationship with Pericles began between 452 and 441; son Pericles the Younger. Old Comedy portrayed her as a courtesan and brothel-keeper; modern scholars (Bicknell; Madeleine Henry, 1995) treat this as comic slander. Her impiety trial (Plutarch) may derive from a comedy. She appears in Socratic dialogues by Antisthenes, Plato, Aeschines and Xenophon, usually as a skilled rhetorician. The Menexenus' attribution of a funeral speech to her is widely read as ironic. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspasia — the "ironic" reading is NOT verified, see below]
- Diotima: known only from the Symposium; "Most modern scholars believe that Diotima is a fictional character invented by Plato" (Ficino first suggested this; Kranz and Wilamowitz believed her historical; Mary Ellen Waithe, 1987, argued for her historicity; Nussbaum reads her name, "honour the god", as a foil to Alcibiades' consort Timandra). Armand D'Angour (2019) argues Diotima is modelled on Aspasia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diotima_of_Mantinea ; WP-Aspasia]
- She is presented as a foreign (*xenē*) wise woman who delayed a plague by advising sacrifices (Symposium 201d). [source: WP-Diotima]

## 16. The Socratic schools (brief)
- Cynics: claim a Socratic lineage; Antisthenes (a companion present at Socrates' death) is "controversially" named by Diogenes Laertius as the first Cynic; Diogenes of Sinope is the archetype; name from *kunikos*, "dog-like"; ethics as a way of life, *askēsis*, a "shortcut to virtue" (DL 6.104). [source: IEP-Cyn; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynicism_(philosophy); SEP-Soc note 2]
- Cyrenaics: founded by Aristippus of Cyrene (c. 435–356 BCE), a follower of Socrates; Cyrene was a Greek colony in North Africa; Aristippus taught his daughter Arete, who taught her son Aristippus the Younger, who may have formulated much of the doctrine. Doctrines: certain knowledge only of present experiences ("I am being whitened"), and present bodily pleasure as the highest good. School died out mid-3rd century BCE. [source: IEP-Cyr]
- Megarians: founded by Euclides of Megara, a pupil of Socrates (present at his death); combined the Socratic good with Eleatic unity ("the good is one"); successors developed logic (the "Dialectical school": Diodorus Cronus, Philo), influencing Stoic logic. Probably not a formal institution. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megarian_school; IEP-Soc §1.b]
- The Stoics also considered themselves authentically Socratic; the Epicureans criticised him; Academic sceptics (Arcesilaus) claimed his method. [source: IEP-Soc §4.a]

## 17. Argument reconstructions available for the chapter
- (A) The oracle argument (Apology 21b–23b) [source: IEP-Soc §2.b.i; SEP-Short §11]:
  P1 The god cannot lie. P2 Socrates knows he is not wise about the most important things. P3 Every reputedly wise person he examined (politicians, poets, craftsmen) thought he knew what he did not. C: Socrates is wiser only in this — he does not think he knows what he does not; true wisdom belongs to the god, and human wisdom is worth little.
  Objection: if he knows nothing, how can he be confident in his moral convictions (e.g. Apology 29b)? Reply (IEP; Fine 2008; C. C. W. Taylor 1998): he never claims to know nothing; he disclaims expert, definitional knowledge while holding well-tested convictions.
- (B) The Laches elenchus (§9 above), with the Vlastos "problem of the elenchus" as objection and the constructivist reply.
- (C) The self-refutation of Protagoras' measure doctrine (§3): P1 Every judgment is true (for its holder). P2 Many judge the doctrine false. C: The doctrine is false (for them, hence for Protagoras too). Reply: re-insert the qualifier "for me/for you" — the doctrine is false only for its opponents; cost: solipsism and the uselessness of Protagoras' own teaching; Protagoras' further reply in the Theaetetus "Apology": replace "truer" with "better" (more useful) appearances. [source: SEP-Prot §2.2]
- (D) Protagoras' denial of akrasia argument (Protagoras 351b–358d) [source: SEP-Short §§9, 20].

## Debates
- The Socratic problem: literary contextualists (Gadamer, Strauss tradition; whole dialogues as artworks) vs analytic developmentalists (Vlastos: Plato's views developed; the "elenctic" dialogues — Apology, Charmides, Crito, Euthyphro, Gorgias, Hippias Minor, Ion, Laches, Protagoras, Republic I — give Socrates' philosophy; ten Socratic theses opposed to ten Platonic ones; the "mouthpiece theory"). John Cooper (1997, Hackett *Complete Works*) called the early/middle classification "an unsuitable basis for bringing anyone to the reading of these works"; SEP-Soc says this "sounded the end of the developmentalist era" for many analysts. Charles Kahn (1996) argued the "Socratic" dialogues are Plato's literary constructions, not records. [source: SEP-Soc §§2.2–2.3; SEP-Plato §11; IEP-Soc §1.b.iv]
- Xenophon vs Plato as source: Schleiermacher (1818) against Xenophon; Strauss for him; recent scholarship rehabilitating Xenophon's Socrates as a philosopher of a way of life. [source: SEP-Soc note 4; IEP-Soc §1.b.iii]
- Was the trial political? IEP: the amnesty forced religious framing of what was largely political suspicion (Alcibiades, Critias). SEP-Soc stresses religious fear: superstitious jurors feared divine anger if they failed to execute a man guilty of irreverence. I. F. Stone (1988) argued Socrates was an anti-democrat who in effect courted death. [source: IEP-Soc §1.a.ii; SEP-Soc §3; WP-Trial]
- Constructivist vs non-constructivist elenchus (Vlastos vs Benson). [source: IEP-Soc §3.a.ii; SEP-Short §2]
- Is Socratic ignorance sincere or ironic? Mastery of definitions would amount to the expert knowledge he disclaims, so either he lacks it or the disclaimer is ironic. [source: SEP-Short §3; IEP-Soc §2.d]
- Intellectualism: full intellectualists (Penner and Rowe; Reshotko), moderates (Brickhouse and Smith), non-intellectualists (Gerson; Weiss; Woodruff). [source: SEP-Short §8]
- Crito vs Apology on obedience to law (Grote's problem). [source: SEP-Short §12]
- Protagoras: subjectivist, social relativist, pluralist (Guthrie; SEP-Prot) or perspectivist (van Berkel 2013)? Bett (1989) questions whether "relativism" is the right category at all. [source: SEP-Prot §2.2; SEP-Soph §1]
- Gorgias' *On Not-Being*: parody or serious philosophy? [source: SEP-Soph §4]
- Thrasymachus' real position (see §5). [source: IEP-Thras §2]
- Diotima: historical or invented? Aspasia's role (D'Angour). [source: WP-Diotima; WP-Aspasia]
- Hemlock: did Plato idealise the death (Gill) or describe *Conium* poisoning accurately (Bloch)? [source: WP-Trial]

C. W. Taylor (1998, "a clear misreading of Plato") argue it should not be attributed to him. Use only as a later paraphrase. [evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_know_that_I_know_nothing ; IEP-Soc §2.b.i]
- The exact vote figures for the death sentence (e.g. "360 to 140", "80 more votes") — Diogenes Laertius' numbers could not be read directly; secondary sources conflict (see §13).
- Grube's exact wording of Apology 38a ("the unexamined life is not worth living for men") — not found in any source read; use the SEP or literal rendering above.
- Protagoras' fee of 100 minae (Diogenes Laertius) — not found in sources read.
- Aeschines, Against Timarchus 173 ("you put Socrates the sophist to death because he was shown to have educated Critias") — well known but not checked in this session.
- That the Menexenus' attribution of the funeral speech to Aspasia is ironic — a common reading, not checked.
- "Socrates helped the Athenians win the battle of Potidaea" (IEP-Soc) — SEP-Soc describes a long siege and subsequent losses; avoid "win".
- The pot-belly — sources disagree (SEP-Soc rejects it; IEP-Soc asserts it).
- Wikipedia's statement that the dictum was spoken "after he chose death, rather than exile", and IEP's "After the jury has convicted Socrates and sentenced him to death" for 38a — both misplace it: 38a precedes the death vote (SEP-Soc: second vote at 38b).
- Gorgias' age at death (108/109) and teacher (Empedocles) — ancient tradition only, reported by IEP/WP; use with "reportedly".
- Antiphon as a proto-"libertarian" or egalitarian (WP) — contested interpretation; WP itself notes it may be obsolete.
