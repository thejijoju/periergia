## 1. What democracy is

- SEP-Dem's working definition: "a method of collective decision making characterized by a kind of equality among the participants at an essential stage of the decision-making process." The definition is deliberately non-normative; the equality "may be more or less deep" — from formal one-person-one-vote for representatives to substantive equality in deliberation; may be direct (referenda) or representative. [source: SEP-Dem §1]
- Word: Greek *dēmokratia* = *dêmos* ("people") + *krátos* ("force", "power"), literally "people power"; contrast *archē* in "monarchy"/"oligarchy". Earliest conceptual articulation generally put at Aeschylus' *Suppliants* (c. 470 BCE, l. 604), *dēmou kratousa cheir* ("the people's hand of power"); the word *dēmokratia* is attested in Herodotus (6.43.3); the earlier term for the idea was *isonomia*. [source: WP-Athenian_democracy]
- Justification vs authority: SEP-Dem separates the question whether democracy is better than alternatives (justification) from whether citizens have a duty to obey democratic decisions (authority); they "can come apart" (Simmons 2001). Justifications are either **instrumental** (by outcomes) or **intrinsic** (values inherent in the method). [source: SEP-Dem §2]

### Athens: direct democracy and sortition
- Development: Solon (594 BC), Cleisthenes (508–07 BC), Ephialtes (462/1 BC). Cleisthenes reorganised citizens into ten tribes based on where they lived rather than wealth. Pericles led Athens 461–429 BC. Democracy was interrupted by oligarchic coups in 411 and 404 BC (the Thirty Tyrants, pro-Spartan, after Athens' surrender in 404); restored after a year. [source: WP-Athenian_democracy]
- Overall span: WP-Ostracism gives "circa 506–322 BC" for the period of Athenian democracy. WP-Athenian_democracy says democratic forms persisted until Philip II's conquest in 338 BC and records Macedonian-backed governors (Demetrius of Phalerum) and a restoration in 307 BC — so the end date is given differently (338 vs 322 BC); say "until the Macedonian conquest in the late 4th century BCE" or give the range. [sources: WP-Ostracism; WP-Athenian_democracy]
- Who could take part: adult male citizens who had completed military training as ephebes; excluded were women, slaves, freed slaves, children and metics. Adult male citizens "probably constituted no more than 30 percent of the total adult population." After Pericles' law (451/450 BC) citizenship required two Athenian parents. Estimates: some 30,000 adult male citizens (perhaps 60,000 in the mid-5th century, falling during the Peloponnesian War); Hyperides (c. 338) claimed 150,000 slaves in Attica, probably an impression. [source: WP-Athenian_democracy]
- **Ekklesia (Assembly):** open to all citizens by right, not elected; four main functions: decrees (e.g. war, grants of citizenship), electing some officials, legislating, trying political crimes. Met on the Pnyx; 10 fixed meetings a year in the 5th century, 40 a year (four per state month) in the 4th. Voting usually by show of hands (*kheirotonia*); a quorum of 6,000 for a small category of votes (principally citizenship grants), with white/black stones. Pay for Assembly attendance introduced after the democracy's restoration in 403 BC; jury pay (raised from two to three obols by Cleon) came more than fifty years earlier. [source: WP-Athenian_democracy]
- **Boule (Council of 500):** after Cleisthenes, 500 members selected by lot every year; set the agenda, finance, fleet, ambassadors; a chairman chosen by lot each day. [source: WP-Athenian_democracy]
- **Courts:** juries of a minimum of 200, on occasion up to 6,000, drawn from an annual pool of 6,000 citizens over thirty. Citizens voting in Assembly and courts were not subject to review, unlike officeholders. [source: WP-Athenian_democracy]
- **Officials and sortition:** about 1,100 citizens (including the 500 councillors) held office each year, mostly chosen by lot; a much smaller, more prestigious group of about 100 was elected — notably the ten annually elected generals (*strategoi*). No office appointed by lot could be held twice by the same person. Allotment was seen as preventing purchase of office and giving equal chances. Allotment machines (*kleroteria*). Mogens Herman Hansen is cited on the courts. [sources: WP-Athenian_democracy; WP-Sortition] SEP-Dem: "Athenian democracy involved direct democracy for the making of laws and sortition for the choice of officials." [source: SEP-Dem §4.2.6]
- "Most Athenians believed sortition, not elections, to be democratic." Montesquieu (*Spirit of the Laws*, 1748) argued sortition is natural to democracy as elections are to aristocracy. [source: WP-Sortition]
- **Ostracism:** any citizen could be expelled from Athens for ten years (returning without stigma, property kept is not stated — do not add); named from *ostraka*, potsherds used as ballots; attributed to Cleisthenes by Aristotle's *Constitution of the Athenians* 22.3 (so in place from c. 506 BC), but first used in 487 BC; about a dozen known victims over the next 60 years, including Aristides "the Just" and Pericles' father Xanthippus (recalled before the Persian invasion of 479 BC); last known: Hyperbolus, c. 417 BC. Quorum of 6,000 (Plutarch: total votes cast; Philochorus: votes against the person — Plutarch's reading "generally preferred"). About 12,000 political ostraka have been excavated in the agora and Kerameikos. [source: WP-Ostracism]
- Josiah Ober (*Democracy and Knowledge*, 2008) argues Athens' enlarged decision body produced collective epistemic success. [source: SEP-JT §6.1]

### Representative democracy
- Aristotle's citizen: one who has the right to participate in deliberative or judicial office (1275b18–21); "The Athenian system differed from a modern representative democracy in that the citizens were more directly involved in governing." [source: SEP-AristPol §3]
- Hanna Pitkin, *The Concept of Representation* (1967): to represent is to "make present again". [source: SEP-Rep2]
- Madison (Federalist No. 10, 1787) distinguishes "pure democracy" (citizens vote directly on laws) from a republic (citizens elect a small body of representatives), arguing representation may "refine and enlarge" public views. (§9 below.) [source: WP-Federalist_No._10]
- Mill: direct democracy "impractical in anything but a small community" (CRG 412), so representative democracy; representatives are fiduciaries voting their own considered views of the common good (490), not delegates of sectional interests. [source: SEP-MMP §4.1]
- Bernard Manin, *Principes du gouvernement représentatif* (1995; English *The Principles of Representative Government*, 1997) is in SEP bibliographies (bibliographic entry only; his thesis that elections are aristocratic was not read in a source — do not state it). [source: SEP-Rep2 bibliography]

## 2. Plato's critique

- Republic VI, ship of state (488a–489d): WP describes the metaphor: the people are a strong but near-sighted (and somewhat deaf) shipowner/master; the quarrelling sailors are demagogues and politicians who claim navigation cannot be taught, flatter and drug the master, seize the ship; the true navigator, who studies "the year and seasons and sky and stars and winds", is dismissed as "a star-gazer". The metaphor predates Plato (Alcaeus, Aeschylus' *Seven Against Thebes*, Sophocles' *Antigone*, Aristophanes' *Wasps*). [source: WP-Ship_of_State] Range: WP and the Plato research notes give 488a–489d; the brief's 488a–489a covers the image itself; 489b–d draws the moral.
- Quotation of the passage: see Quotations; translator not named by WP (it links Wikisource's "The Republic of Plato"; probably Jowett but **not confirmed**).
- SEP-Dem on the argument: democracy undermines the expertise needed to govern; most people lack the talents to think well about politics; to win office politicians must appeal to these people, so the state is guided by "very poorly worked out ideas that experts in manipulation and mass appeal use to help themselves win office"; hence rule by philosopher-kings — "a version of what David Estlund calls 'epistocracy'" (Estlund 2003). [source: SEP-Dem §2.1.2]
- IEP: Plato criticises "the direct and unchecked democracy of his time" (557a–564a) for (1) excessive freedom — doing as one likes — leading to anarchy; (2) equality, the belief that everyone has an equal right and capacity to rule, which brings power-seekers and opens the gates to demagogues and tyranny. "Democracy depends on chance and must be mixed with competent leadership (501b)." If ruling is a craft (statecraft), it needs trained experts. [source: IEP-PlatoPol §5]
- Republic VIII: democracy "honors all pursuits equally, which opens the city to conflict and disorder"; without "publicly entrenched standards for evaluation … chaos and strife are unavoidable"; Adeimantus remarks on the criticism of Athenian democracy (563d). Democracy's tolerance extends to philosophers (561c–d, 557d), but the Republic is also sceptical of it (487a–499a). Statesman ranks democracy above oligarchy; the Laws gives unwise citizens a greater role. [source: SEP-Rep §5]

## 3. Aristotle: polity, mixed constitution, wisdom of the multitude

- Six constitutions (Politics III.7; adapted from Statesman 302c–d): correct (common advantage) — kingship, aristocracy, polity; deviant (rulers' advantage) — tyranny, oligarchy, democracy (1279a17–21). Democracy is rule of the poor, oligarchy of the wealthy. [source: SEP-AristPol §3] (Already in the Aristotle chapter — cross-reference.)
- Democrats hold that those equal in free birth should have equal political rights; Aristotle thinks both oligarchic and democratic justice mistaken because "the good life is the end of the city-state" (1280b39–1281a4). [source: SEP-AristPol]
- Second-best: "polity (in which citizens possess an inferior, more common grade of virtue) or mixed constitution (combining features of democracy, oligarchy, and, where possible, aristocracy, so that no group of citizens is in a position to abuse its rights)"; best for most cities is one controlled by "a numerous middle class", who find it "easiest to obey the rule of reason" (IV.11.1295b4–6). [source: SEP-AristPol]
- IEP quotes (translator not named; probably Lord): tyranny "arises from the most headstrong sort of democracy and from oligarchy, but much less often from the middling sorts" (1295b39); "Where the multitude of middling persons predominates … there a lasting polity is capable of existing" (1296b38); "democracies undergo revolution particularly on account of the wanton behavior of the popular leaders" (1304b20). [source: IEP-AristPol]
- **Wisdom of the multitude (Politics III.11):** "the many may turn out to be better than the virtuous few when they come together, even though the many may be inferior when considered individually. For if each individual has a portion of virtue and practical wisdom, they may pool these moral assets"; this "seems to anticipate … Condorcet's 'jury theorem'"; the chapter has been widely discussed in connection with deliberation and public reason. Aristotle allows the case "perhaps also involves some truth" (III.11.1281a39–42). [source: SEP-AristPol]
- Interpretive debate: Jeremy Waldron, "The Wisdom of the Multitude: Some Reflections on Book 3, Chapter 11 of Aristotle's Politics", *Political Theory* — SEP-Dem/SEP-JT date it 1995 (23(4): 563–584); SEP-AristPol cites 20 (1992), 613–41. **Dates conflict — use "1990s" or check.** The group-wisdom reading is "contested (Cammack 2013; Lane 2013)" — Daniela Cammack, "Aristotle on the Virtue of the Multitude", *Political Theory* 41(2) (2013); Melissa Lane, "Claims to Rule: The Case of the Multitude" (2013). [sources: SEP-JT §6.1 and bibliography; SEP-AristPol bibliography]
- SEP-Dem attributes the diversity argument to "Aristotle (Politics, Book II, Ch. 11; see Waldron 1995)" — **"Book II" is evidently a slip for Book III**; do not repeat it.

## 4. Rousseau: general will and the rejection of representation

- SEP-R: to hand over one's general right of ruling oneself to another person or body "is a form a slavery" [sic]; the hostility extends to elected representatives even with periodic re-election; this severely limits the size of legitimate states. Rousseau takes a different view in *Considerations on the Government of Poland*. He distinguishes sovereign (people making general laws) from government (executive), and many forms of government are compatible with popular sovereignty; whether he rejects all representative government is disputed (Fralin 1978 vs Cohen 2010). [source: SEP-R §3.4]
- SEP-Dem: two readings of the general will — **constitutive** (the results of a properly organised procedure *are* the general will) vs **epistemic** (the procedure tracks a procedure-independent common good); Rousseau is often read as appealing to something like Condorcet's theorem. SEP-Dem quotes Rousseau (Social Contract III.4, translator not named): "[i]f, when a sufficiently informed populace deliberates, the citizens were to have no communication among themselves, the general will would always result from the large number of small differences, and the deliberation would always be good." **SEP-Dem gives "Book III, ch. IV"; this passage is usually located in Book II, ch. 3 — flag, do not cite the chapter number without checking.** [source: SEP-Dem §§2.1.1.2, 3.2.1]
- The epistemic reading of Rousseau via the jury theorem is from Grofman and Feld (1988); Wyckoff (2011) is critical of that historical speculation. [source: SEP-JT §6.1]
- Rousseau, Social Contract IV.2 (as quoted by WP-Epistemic_democracy, cited "[1762] 1997: 124"; translator not named): "When a law is proposed in the People's assembly, what they are being asked is not exactly whether they approve the proposal or reject it, but whether it does or does not conform to the general will, which is theirs; everyone states his opinion about this by casting his ballot, and the tally of the votes yields the declaration of the general will." [source: WP-Epistemic_democracy]
- English-people passage (Social Contract III.15): see Quotations and NOT VERIFIED.

## 5. Mill, Considerations on Representative Government (1861)

- Published 1861 (Parker, Son and Bourn, London). Endorsed Thomas Hare's proportional representation / single transferable vote; legislatures are "to indicate wants, to be an organ for popular demands, and a place of adverse discussion for all opinions". [source: WP-Considerations_on_Representative_Government]
- Two criteria of good government: promoting the common good, conceived as the moral, intellectual and active traits of citizens, and making effective use of existing resources (CRG 390, 392). [source: SEP-MMP §4]
- **Two arguments for democracy:** (1) **epistemic/protective** — universal suffrage and participation are the best assurance the interests of the governed are appreciated, against bias and failure of imagination (404); (2) **educative/constitutive** — participation exercises and develops deliberative capacities: citizens learn to gather information, deliberate, form a conception of the common good, exchange reasons (404). [source: SEP-MMP §4.1] SEP-Dem files the second under "character-based arguments" (Mill 1861 [1991: 74]). [source: SEP-Dem §2.1.1.3]
- Responsiveness argument: democracy gives each a share of power, forcing decision-makers to consider a wider range of interests (Mill 1861 ch. 3). [source: SEP-Dem §2.1.1.1]
- Limits: excludes illiterate adults (CRG 470–71 — a failure society must correct) and those who pay no taxes / are on public assistance (472); supported women's suffrage (479–81); MP for Westminster 1865–68. [source: SEP-MMP §4.2]
- **Plural voting:** weighted by education, not property (CRG 474); quotation from "Thoughts on Parliamentary Reform" (1859) below; with an upper limit so the educated cannot form a class majority (CRG 476). Mill's defence: "Entire exclusion from a voice … is one thing; the concession to others of a more potential voice … is another" (CRG 474). Brink's criticism: Mill shows "something of a tin ear for egalitarian concerns"; plural votes send a message of second-class citizenship. Weighted voting may have been partly a concession to secure near-universal suffrage (476), and was meant as transitional. [source: SEP-MMP §4.2]
- SEP-Dem calls plural voting (1861 ch. 4 [sic; SEP-MMP locates it in CRG VIII]) "a form of epistocracy". [source: SEP-Dem §2.1.2] **Chapter discrepancy: use "Considerations, ch. VIII" per SEP-MMP.**
- Plural voting in practice: the UK had university constituencies and business/property plural votes into the 20th century (WP gives a Commons speaker with five votes from property and a university); Northern Ireland retained a Queen's University seat. [source: WP-Plural_voting] (Peripheral; use sparingly.)

## 6. Instrumental vs intrinsic justifications

- **Instrumental, for:** responsiveness (Mill); Sen: "no substantial famine has ever occurred in any independent country with a democratic form of government and a relatively free press" (Sen 1999: 152, *Development as Freedom*); correlation with protection of core liberal rights (Gaus, Christiano); African Americans' gains after regaining the vote in 1965 (Wright 2013); growth (Acemoglu et al. 2019); epistemic theories; character-based arguments; economic/contractarian (Buchanan and Tullock, *The Calculus of Consent*, 1962 [SEP-Dem says 1963]; Rae 1969, Rae–Taylor theorem: if each has equal prior probability of preferring either of two alternatives, majority rule maximises each person's expected utility). [source: SEP-Dem §2.1.1]
- **Instrumental, against:** Plato (expertise); Mill's plural voting; Hobbes (Leviathan ch. XIX: democracy fosters dissension; no one feels responsible); modern evidence that citizens are ill-informed and apathetic and engage in motivated reasoning (Lord, Ross & Lepper 1979; Bartels 2002; Kahan 2013; Achen & Bartels 2016); used to argue for modification or abandonment (Caplan 2007; Somin 2013; Brennan 2016). [source: SEP-Dem §2.1.2]
- **Pure instrumentalism:** utilitarianism; Arneson (power over others is justified only by outcomes for those subject to it); Wall 2007; Riker's social-choice argument that procedures cannot be intrinsically fair (Riker 1982: 116); Dworkin 2000 (later pulled back). [source: SEP-Dem §2.1.3]
- **Intrinsic:** (a) liberty / self-government (Carol Gould 1988) — objection: seems to require unanimity (Wolff 1970); (b) public justification (Habermas, Cohen); (c) equality — Singer 1973 (democracy as a fair compromise among rival claims to be "dictator"), Waldron 1999 ch. 5 — objection: infinite regress about how to decide on the procedure; (d) **public equality** (Christiano 2008); (e) relational equality (Kolodny 2014; Scheffler 2010: concern for relationships "unstructured by differences of rank, power, or status"). [source: SEP-Dem §2.2]
- Jeremy Waldron, *Law and Disagreement* (Oxford: Clarendon, 1999). [source: SEP-Dem bibliography]

### Christiano's public equality (from SEP-Dem, written by Christiano)
- Three ideas: people's interests ought to be equally advanced; people have "highly fallible and biased understandings" of their own and others' interests; people have "fundamental interests in being able to see that they are being treated as equals." Democracy is "a uniquely publicly egalitarian way to make collective decisions" amid disagreement. Limits: a democracy cannot legitimately abolish the democratic rights of some members, and must respect basic liberal rights (Christiano 2008; Valentini 2013). Authority: refusing to obey suitably egalitarian decisions treats fellow citizens as inferiors (Christiano 2008 ch. 6). Persistent minorities violate public equality and so undercut democratic authority over them (ch. 7). Book: Thomas Christiano, *The Constitution of Equality: Democratic Authority and Its Limits* (Oxford UP, 2008). [source: SEP-Dem §§2.2.3, 3.2.3, 3.3.2]

### Estlund's epistemic proceduralism
- David Estlund, *Democratic Authority: A Philosophical Framework* (Princeton UP, 2008). Estlund is Lombardo Family Professor of Philosophy at Brown (since 1991). [sources: SEP-Dem bibliography; WP-David_Estlund]
- Coin-flip argument: if procedural fairness were all that mattered, a fair lottery or coin flip would do as well as voting; so democratic authority "must have an epistemic element." But justification must also be acceptable to all reasonable ("qualified") points of view. Legitimacy "requires that the procedure can be held, in terms acceptable to all qualified points of view, to be epistemically the best (or close to it) among those that are better than random" (Estlund 2008: 98); the "qualified acceptability requirement" (2008: 40ff). [sources: SEP-Dem §3.1; SEP-Leg §4.3; SEP-Mer §5.3]
- Estlund distinguishes his view from a "correctness theory" of legitimacy (Estlund 2008: 99), under which incorrect decisions would be illegitimate. [sources: SEP-Leg §4.1; WP-Epistemic_democracy]
- Estlund coined "epistocracy" (Estlund 2003). His "authority tenet" (2008: 30): "When some citizens are morally unreasonable, ignorant, or incompetent about politics, this justifies not permitting them to exercise political authority over others" — which Brennan's "antiauthority tenet" (2016: 17) echoes. [sources: SEP-Dem §2.1.2; SEP-Leg §3.3; WP-Epistemic_democracy]
- **Demographic objection** (Estlund 2003): any criterion of expertise is likely to select demographically homogeneous people with biases that undermine the general welfare. Brennan replied in "Does the Demographic Objection to Epistocracy Succeed?", *Res Publica* 24(1) (2018) (content not read). [source: SEP-Dem §2.1.2; bibliography]
- Fabienne Peter's "pure epistemic proceduralism" (2008): authority from fairness in knowledge-production, not from tracking truth. [source: SEP-Dem §3.2.3; SEP-Leg]
- Cristina Lafont, *Democracy Without Shortcuts* (2019): epistemic arguments wrongly imply deference; democracy's legitimacy rests on each citizen's equal standing as addressee of justification. Estlund's qualified-acceptability requirement avoids the deference worry. [source: SEP-Leg §§4.1, 4.3]

## 7. Condorcet's jury theorem (1785)

- Source work: Condorcet, *Essai sur l'application de l'analyse à la probabilité des décisions rendues à la pluralité des voix* (1785). Marie Jean Antoine Nicolas de Caritat, Marquis de Condorcet. [sources: WP-Condorcet's_jury_theorem; SEP-JT §1.1]
- **Precise statement (SEP-JT §2.1):** choice between two alternatives, one correct; majority voting; assumptions: **Unconditional Independence** (individual correctness events are independent) and **Unconditional Competence** (each individual's probability of being correct exceeds ½ and is the same for all). Conclusion: the probability that the majority is correct increases with (odd) group size and converges to 1. Intuition: independent tosses of the same coin biased towards the truth. [source: SEP-JT §2.1]
- SEP-Dem lists three assumptions: competence, **sincerity** (voters vote for what they believe correct), independence. [source: SEP-Dem §2.1.1.2]
- Converse: if p < ½, adding voters makes things worse; "the optimal jury consists of a single voter". [source: WP-Condorcet's_jury_theorem]
- Example: 10,000 voters, each 51% likely to be right on two options → majority correct with probability 99.97%. [source: SEP-Dem §2.1.1.2]
- Small worked example: n = 3, p = 0.8 → 0.896. [source: WP-Condorcet's_jury_theorem]
- History: Condorcet's theorem largely forgotten after his death; Duncan Black (1958) rediscovered his work; Grofman (1975) "seems to have coined the term 'Condorcet Jury Theorem'"; Black dismissed an independent standard of correctness in elections; Rawls (1971) doubted votes could be sufficiently independent; Brian Barry (1965) perhaps first 20th-century theorist to affirm its importance. Galton (1907) on the 1906 ox-weighing: median guess within 0.8% of actual weight (SEP-JT) — WP-Wisdom_of_the_crowd: 800 people at a Plymouth fair, median 1,207 lb vs true 1,198 lb ("within 1%"). [sources: SEP-JT §6.1; WP-Wisdom_of_the_crowd]
- Objections (SEP-Dem): opinions are not independent (persuasion, coalition-building); information is segmented by class, race, religion etc.; voters bring shared ideological biases (Black 1963; Ladha 1992; Estlund 1997, 2008 ch. XII; Anderson 2006). Replies: variants with weaker assumptions (Grofman & Feld 1988; Austen-Smith & Banks 1996). Further objection: the theorem "proves too much" — it would give the minority overwhelming reason to change its mind, threatening the "loyal opposition" and demanding "obedience of thought" (Goodin 2003). [source: SEP-Dem §§2.1.1.2, 3.1]
- SEP-JT's verdict: two hypotheses — **increasing reliability** (larger bodies better) and **infallibility** (huge bodies infallible in the limit); "the infallibility hypothesis is untenable"; jury theorems reaching it "might be seen as arguments against their own premises"; the problem of common causes of votes (shared evidence) undermines independence; **competence heterogeneity** is "the main threat": if new members are less competent, majority reliability can fall with group size, even converging to ½. The epistemically optimal rule with unequal competence is weighted majority (Nitzan & Paroush 1982 etc.) — which meritocrats cite (SEP-Mer §5.3). [sources: SEP-JT §§1.1, 3.2, 5; SEP-Mer §5.3]
- Extension to more than two options: List & Goodin 2001. [source: SEP-Dem]

## 8. Arrow, Riker and Mackie

- Kenneth Arrow's impossibility theorem (Arrow called it the "general possibility" theorem): *Social Choice and Individual Values* (1951; 2nd edn 1963), preceded by "A Difficulty in the Concept of Social Welfare" (*Journal of Political Economy*, 1950). Achieved while a graduate student; Nobel Prize in economics 1972. [sources: SEP-Arrow intro; SEP-Dem §6 (Arrow 1951); WP-Arrow's_impossibility_theorem]
- Statement (SEP-Dem §6): with three or more alternatives, no social choice function satisfies all of: **unrestricted (unlimited) domain**; **non-dictatorship**; **transitivity and completeness** of the social ordering; **independence of irrelevant alternatives**; **(weak) Pareto**. SEP-SC: no method for aggregating the preferences of two or more individuals over three or more alternatives satisfies five plausible axioms. [sources: SEP-Dem §6; SEP-SC §1.2]
- **Condorcet's paradox of voting** (Condorcet 1785): rankings ABC / BCA / CAB → majorities prefer A to B, B to C, C to A — a cycle. [source: SEP-Arrow §1] Ways out: restrict the domain (e.g. single-peaked preferences), use cardinal/intensity information (violating independence), or a dictator. [source: SEP-Dem §6]
- **Riker**, *Liberalism Against Populism: A Confrontation Between the Theory of Democracy and the Theory of Social Choice* (San Francisco: W. H. Freeman, 1982). William H. Riker (1920–1993), Rochester school. He "interpreted it as a mathematical proof of the impossibility of populist democracy" (SEP-SC); "populist" interpretations of democracy as implementing a collective will are untenable, given Arrow and the McKelvey–Schofield chaos theorem; outcomes can be manipulated by agenda-setting and "heresthetic" (changing outcomes without changing preferences). The "liberal" alternative: elections let voters remove officials. Neither Riker nor Hardin opposes democracy; both see good instrumental reasons for it. [sources: SEP-SC §1.2; WP-William_H._Riker; SEP-Dem §6]
- **Mackie**, *Democracy Defended* (Cambridge UP, 2003): argues modern societies normally satisfy conditions that avoid cycles (SEP-Dem); WS: cycling is "an empirical improbability", "nearly every published political cycle example has been refuted", Mackie re-examines Riker's instances (chs. 14–15); Michael Munger's review: Mackie "demolishes" the bases of Riker's claim. Mackie also argues independence of irrelevant alternatives equivocates on "irrelevance" — e.g. in the 2000 US election Nader was on the ballot and so relevant to the Gore–Bush ranking, unlike Lincoln. [sources: SEP-Dem §6; SEP-Arrow §4.5; WS: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/democracy-defended/other-cycles-debunked/0B3F3CDD12F0630C920910985AFD4F78 and https://www.powells.com/book/democracy-defended-9780521534314]
- Others on the side of Riker: Ingham 2019 (cycles likely); Christiano's replies: fair procedures can have indeterminate outcomes (coin flips are fair); how prominent cycles are is disputed; if resources for strategy are equal, fairness holds; Sean Ingham's *Rule by Multiple Majorities* (2019) builds intensity of preference into fairness. Sen took Arrow to show ordinal information is insufficient. [sources: SEP-Dem §6; SEP-SC §1.2]
- Fishkin, List, Luskin and McLean (2013, *Journal of Politics*): deliberation increases single-peakedness (evidence from deliberative polls). [source: SEP-Arrow bibliography; WP-James_S._Fishkin bibliography]

## 9. Tyranny of the majority and minority rights

- **Madison, Federalist No. 10**: "The Utility of the Union as a Safeguard Against Domestic Faction and Insurrection"; first published in the New York *Daily Advertiser* on **22 November 1787** under "Publius" (WP-Federalist_No._10; WP-Tyranny_of_the_majority gives 23 November 1787 — **date conflict; use 22 November per the article's own page or say "November 1787"**). Faction: "a number of citizens, whether amounting to a minority or majority of the whole, who are united and actuated by some common impulse of passion, or of interest, adverse to the rights of other citizens, or to the permanent and aggregate interests of the community." "The latent causes of faction are thus sown in the nature of man"; "liberty is to faction what air is to fire"; "the most common and durable source of factions has been the various and unequal distribution of property". Remedy: control effects, not causes; impossible in a pure democracy, possible in an extended republic where representation may "refine and enlarge" public views, there are more "fit characters", and a greater variety of interests makes a factious majority harder to form. [source: WP-Federalist_No._10]
- Madison (Fed. 10, as quoted by WP-Tyranny): "When a majority is included in a faction, the form of popular government … enables it to sacrifice to its ruling passion or interest both the public good and the rights of other citizens." [source: WP-Tyranny_of_the_majority]
- **Tocqueville**, *Democracy in America* (*De la démocratie en Amérique*), vol. 1 1835, vol. 2 1840. The phrase "tyranny of the majority" is commonly attributed to him (chapter titles "What Moderates the Tyranny of the Majority in the United States…"), though John Adams used it in 1788; Mill popularised it in *On Liberty* (1859). Tocqueville also warned of "soft despotism". [sources: WP-Tyranny_of_the_majority; WP-Democracy_in_America]
- SEP-Dem distinguishes majority tyranny from **persistent minorities** (a minority that always loses, e.g. indigenous peoples), which can be oppressive "even if the majority does not try to act oppressively". [source: SEP-Dem §3.3.2]
- Limits on democratic authority: internal (rights needed for democratic process, Ely 1980; Locke; Dworkin 1996) vs external (independent liberal rights; unjust wars). [source: SEP-Dem §3.3]
- Mill: central government should protect local minorities from local majorities (CRG 544); proportional representation protects minority views (CRG 458). [source: SEP-MMP §4.1]

## 10. Epistocracy: Brennan and the voter-ignorance evidence

- Jason Brennan (b. 1979), Georgetown (McDonough School of Business); PhD Arizona 2007 under David Schmidtz. *Against Democracy* (Princeton UP, published 6 September 2016; paperback 26 September 2017; German *Gegen Demokratie*, 2017, a *Spiegel* bestseller). Earlier: *The Ethics of Voting* (2011). [sources: WP-Jason_Brennan; WP-Against_Democracy]
- **Hobbits, hooligans, vulcans** (ch. 1): hobbits — indifferent, ignorant, no fixed opinions (typical American non-voter); hooligans — "the rabid sports fans of politics", fixed opinions, biased consumption of information (most regular voters and politicians); vulcans — scientific, rational, can state opposing views to their holders' satisfaction; no one is a "true vulcan". [source: WP-Against_Democracy]
- Brennan's three targets: instrumental, educative and intrinsic justifications; argues participation tends to make people worse (ch. 3); rejects "semiotic" arguments (equal power symbolises respect, ch. 5); rejects arguments from mathematical theorems (ch. 7); proposes restricted suffrage (exams), plural voting, and (per WP-Epistemic_democracy) an epistocratic veto (ch. 8); responds to the demographic objection. [sources: WP-Against_Democracy; WP-Epistemic_democracy]
- **Competence argument:** citizens have a right "not to be subject to high stakes decisions made by incompetent and morally unreasonable people" (Brennan 2011: 702, "The Right to a Competent Electorate", *Philosophical Quarterly* 61); analogy with an ignorant, irrational or morally unreasonable jury; a deontological justification, unlike most consequentialist arguments for meritocracy. [source: SEP-Mer §3.2] SEP-Leg: Brennan's "antiauthority tenet" (2016: 17). [source: SEP-Leg §3.3]
- Evidence base: Converse (1964) pioneered research on mass belief systems; voters retrospective, follow cues, motivated reasoning (Lenz 2012; Flynn, Nyhan & Reifler 2017); Brennan 2016 provides a survey. [source: SEP-Mer §3] Downs (1957): rational ignorance; the chance of casting the decisive vote in a US presidential election is often put at about 1 in 100 million. [source: SEP-Dem §4.1] Achen & Bartels, *Democracy for Realists: Why Elections Do Not Produce Responsive Government* (2016); Caplan, *The Myth of the Rational Voter* (2007); Somin, *Democracy and Political Ignorance* (2013; advocates decentralisation/foot-voting). [sources: SEP bibliographies; WP-Epistemic_democracy]
- **Critics:** Kevin J. Elliott (*Contemporary Political Theory*): the book does not consider scholars who question the reliability of the survey data; Simone Chambers (*Perspectives on Politics*): "provocative", argues against democracy more than for a workable epistocracy; Nathan J. Robinson: epistocracy could restore abolished hierarchies such as Jim Crow; Valerie Soon: doubts that more knowledgeable people are likelier to be just. [source: WP-Against_Democracy] General critiques: an overly pessimistic reading of voter-competence literature, over-reliance on rational choice theory, neglect of democratic reforms, underestimation of the dangers of exclusion. [source: WP-Epistemic_democracy] Lafont and Estlund: epistemic authority ≠ political authority (Estlund 2008; Darwall 2010; Lafont 2019). [source: SEP-Leg §3.3] Elite theory precedent: Schumpeter (*Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy*, 1942) — elections a peaceful way of changing rulers; citizens can "throw the bums out". [source: SEP-Dem §4.2.1]
- A "defensible epistocracy" within representative democracy: Landa & Pevnick 2020 (pivotality and accountability effects). [source: SEP-Dem §4.2.1]

## 11. Landemore and the Hong–Page diversity theorem

- Hélène Landemore (b. 1976), French, Yale political scientist. *Democratic Reason: Politics, Collective Intelligence, and the Rule of the Many* (Princeton UP). **Date varies across sources: 2011 (SEP-SocEp), 2012 (SEP-Arrow, SEP-Leg, SEP-Mer), 2013 (SEP-Dem, SEP-JT), 2017 (WP, probably paperback).** Use "2012" and note the 2013 copyright date if needed. *Open Democracy* (2020). With Brennan, *Debating Democracy: Do We Need More or Less?* (Oxford UP; WP-Landemore 2021, WP-Epistemic_democracy 2022). [sources: as listed]
- Argument: drawing on Hong & Page's "diversity-trumps-ability" theorem, democracy (inclusive, cognitively diverse) can be expected to outperform rule by experts; Landemore prefers assemblies chosen by lot over assemblies of experts. [sources: SEP-Dem §2.1.1.2; WP-Hélène_Landemore]
- **Theorem:** Lu Hong and Scott E. Page, "Groups of Diverse Problem Solvers Can Outperform Groups of High-Ability Problem Solvers", *PNAS* 101(46) (2004): "a random collection of agents drawn from a large set of limited-ability agents typically outperforms a collection of the very best agents from that same set." Model: agents search a ring of numbered solutions with heuristics such as ⟨3, 7, 10⟩; top performers have similar heuristics. [sources: SEP-Dem §2.1.1.2; SEP-SocEp] Conditions (WP-Epistemic_democracy): hard problem; relatively smart solvers; diverse but able to recognise the best solution (the "oracle" assumption); large population and a not-too-small group. Scott E. Page is at the University of Michigan; *The Difference* (2007). [sources: WP-Epistemic_democracy; WP-Scott_E._Page]
- **Critics:** Abigail Thompson, "Does Diversity Trump Ability?", *Notices of the AMS* 61(9) (2014): 1024–1030 — the mathematics dresses up a trivial result; with realistic assumptions ability can beat diversity. Also Quirk 2014, Brennan 2014, Bajaj 2014; Singer 2019. The oracle assumption is the most criticised (moral pluralism, complexity). [sources: SEP-Dem §2.1.1.2; SEP-SocEp; WP-Epistemic_democracy]
- Other epistemic models: Dewey's experimentalism (democracy as "a consultation and a discussion which uncovers social needs and troubles", Dewey 1927), developed by Elizabeth Anderson, "The Epistemology of Democracy" (*Episteme*, 2006); the "miracle of aggregation" (Surowiecki 2004). [sources: SEP-Dem §2.1.1.2; SEP-SocEp; SEP-Mer §5.3]

## 12. Deliberative democracy

- Term coined by Joseph M. Bessette, *Deliberative Democracy: The Majority Principle in Republican Government* (1980). Academic attention in the 1990s. [source: WP-Deliberative_democracy]
- Habermas (briefly; cross-reference *Critical Theory*): Democratic Principle — "only those statutes may claim legitimacy that can meet with the assent of all citizens in a discursive process of legislation that in turn has been legally constituted" (Habermas 1992 [1996: 110]); "two-track" model (formal institutions + "wild" public sphere). Objection: consensus unrealistic in diverse societies (Estlund 2008 ch. 10). [source: SEP-Dem §2.2.2]
- **Joshua Cohen**, "Deliberation and Democratic Legitimacy", in Alan Hamlin and Philip Pettit (eds), *The Good Polity: Normative Analysis of the State* (Oxford: Blackwell, **1989**), pp. 17–34; reprinted in Bohman and Rehg (eds), *Deliberative Democracy* (MIT, 1997). Cohen was a student of Rawls. His five features: an ongoing independent association; institutions in which deliberation is decisive; commitment to pluralism; deliberative procedure as source of legitimacy, with traceable causal history of laws; mutual recognition of deliberative capacity. [source: WP-Deliberative_democracy] SEP-Dem (citing Cohen 1996): citizens justify laws by mutually acceptable reasons, aiming at reasoned consensus; the problem of remaining disagreement. [source: SEP-Dem §2.2.2]
- Gutmann and Thompson's definition: "a form of government in which free and equal citizens and their representatives justify decisions in a process in which they give one another reasons that are mutually acceptable and generally accessible, with the aim of reaching decisions that are binding on all at present but open to challenge in the future". [source: WP-Deliberative_democracy]
- **Fishkin's deliberative polls:** James S. Fishkin (b. 1948), Stanford Deliberative Democracy Lab; first described the concept in **1988**; a random representative sample is polled, given balanced briefing materials, deliberates in small groups and with experts, and is polled again. Applications: Texas electric utilities 1996–98 (more support for renewables/efficiency); Zeguo township, Wenling, China (mid-to-late 2000s, budgeting). Fishkin's "trilemma": political equality, mass participation, deliberation — any two sacrifice the third. Fishkin, Jowell and Luskin, "Considered Opinions: Deliberative Polling in Britain", *BJPS* 32 (2002). [sources: WP-Deliberative_opinion_poll; WP-James_S._Fishkin; WP-Deliberative_democracy] (Date/place of the first actual poll — often said to be Manchester, 1994 — **not verified**.)
- **Ireland:** Constitutional Convention 2012–14 (chair, 33 politicians, 66 randomly chosen citizens); **Citizens' Assembly 2016–18**: 99 randomly selected citizens (by the polling company Red C, representative by gender, age, location, social class) plus chair Mary Laffoy, former Supreme Court judge; inaugural meeting 15 October 2016 at Dublin Castle; first working meeting on the Eighth Amendment 25 November 2016; over 13,000 public submissions; final abortion votes from 22 April 2017; report laid before the Oireachtas 29 June 2017; other topics: ageing population, fixed-term parliaments, referendums, climate change. The abortion referendum (Thirty-sixth Amendment) on **25 May 2018** was approved by **66.4%**; the Convention preceded the 2015 same-sex-marriage referendum. Members received expenses only. [sources: WP-Citizens'_Assembly_(Ireland); WP-Thirty-sixth_Amendment; WP-Deliberative_democracy] Journalist Mary Minihan reported an Oireachtas view that the recommendations were "an overly-liberal interpretation" of middle Ireland. [source: WP-Citizens'_Assembly_(Ireland)]
- Other: France's Citizens' Convention for Climate (2019–20); the German-speaking Community of Belgium's standing Citizens' Council chosen by lot. [source: WP-Deliberative_democracy]
- **Sortition revived:** Alex Guerrero, "Against Elections: The Lottocratic Alternative" (*Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 2014) and *Lottocracy* (2024): lot avoids voter ignorance, unaccountable representatives and elite capture. Objections: Lafont (citizens' interest in taking part in self-government, not just an equal chance); no way to choose parties with an overall programme. Robert Dahl's "minipopuli". [sources: SEP-Dem §4.2.6; WP-Sortition]
- Brennan's objection: deliberation works only among vulcans; hooligans deceive and polarise. [source: WP-Against_Democracy]

## 15. Arguments step by step

### ARGUMENT 1: The epistemic (jury-theorem) argument for democracy
1. On many political questions there is a correct answer independent of the vote (procedure-independent standard). [SEP-Dem §2.1.1.2; SEP-JT §1.1]
2. Each voter is more likely than not to get a binary question right (competence), votes sincerely, and votes independently. [SEP-Dem]
3. Condorcet's theorem: then the probability that the majority is right rises with the number of voters and tends to 1. [SEP-JT §2.1]
4. A mass electorate is far larger than any expert body.
5. So majority rule by the whole people is more reliable than rule by the few — and its decisions deserve deference.
- **Objection 1 (premise 2):** votes are not independent (shared media, parties, persuasion), information is segmented, ideological bias is shared; with common causes or below-½ competence the result fails or reverses (if p < ½ "the optimal jury consists of a single voter"). Competence heterogeneity can make reliability fall with size. [SEP-Dem; SEP-JT §§2.2, 3.2; WP-CJT] The theorem itself recommends weighting competent voters more (Nitzan & Paroush) — the epistocrat's rejoinder. [SEP-Mer §5.3]
- **Objection 2 ("proves too much"):** if the theorem held, minorities should abandon their views — "obedience of thought". [SEP-Dem §3.1]
- **Reply:** weaker-premise theorems (conditional independence, competence-sensitive) support **increasing reliability** but not infallibility (SEP-JT); and Estlund's epistemic proceduralism needs only that democracy be better than random and the best epistemic procedure acceptable to all qualified points of view, not infallible; so it neither requires deference of thought nor licenses epistocracy, because no expertise criterion is beyond reasonable (e.g. demographic) objection. [SEP-Dem §3.1; SEP-Leg §4.3]

### ARGUMENT 2: Brennan's competence argument for epistocracy
1. People have a right not to be subjected to high-stakes decisions made by incompetent or morally unreasonable decision-makers (cf. a jury). [SEP-Mer §3.2; Brennan 2011: 702]
2. Electorates are high-stakes decision-makers, and much of the electorate is ignorant, misinformed and biased (hobbits and hooligans). [WP-Against_Democracy; SEP-Dem §2.1.2]
3. Universal suffrage therefore violates that right.
4. Some epistocratic arrangements (restricted suffrage by exam, plural voting, epistocratic veto) would plausibly produce better decisions. [WP-Against_Democracy]
5. So, if they would, we should prefer epistocracy (Brennan presents it as presumptively justified, to be tested empirically).
- **Objection (Estlund's demographic and "authority" objections):** any knowledge test will favour particular demographic groups whose biases distort outcomes; and that some know more does not make them "boss" — epistemic authority is not political authority; any expertise criterion is open to reasonable rejection. [SEP-Dem §2.1.2; SEP-Leg §3.3] Also: the survey evidence of ignorance is contested (Elliott), aggregation may cancel errors, and diversity may beat ability (Landemore). [WP-Against_Democracy; SEP-Mer §5.3]
- **Reply (Brennan):** he answers the demographic objection in "Does the Demographic Objection to Epistocracy Succeed?" (*Res Publica*, 2018) — content not read; state only that he replied. Verified framing for the writer: Brennan treats epistocracy as presumptively justified and to be evaluated "according to their expected institutional performance" (WP-Jason_Brennan), i.e. an empirical bet, and argues that democracy's alleged educative and intrinsic benefits do not outweigh the harms of incompetent rule (WP-Against_Democracy).

### ARGUMENT 3: Christiano's public-equality argument (intrinsic)
1. Each person's interests should be equally advanced. 2. People's judgments about interests are fallible and biased. 3. People have a fundamental interest in being able to *see* that they are treated as equals. 4. Amid persistent disagreement, the only publicly verifiable way to treat all as equals is to give each an equal say. 5. So democracy is required by justice, and disobeying it treats others as inferiors. Limits: no abolition of others' democratic or basic liberal rights; persistent minorities undermine authority. Objection: majority tyranny and persistent minorities; political equality may be incoherent under representation and division of labour; regress about choosing the procedure (raised against Singer/Waldron). [SEP-Dem §§2.2.3, 3.2.3, 3.3.2]

### ARGUMENT 4: Plato's craft argument (can also be presented)
Ruling is a craft requiring knowledge; crafts should be practised by experts, not by those chosen by popularity (ship's navigator); most citizens lack the knowledge; so democracy hands the helm to flatterers (demagogues). Objection (Aristotle III.11): the many may collectively judge better than the few; users judge some crafts better than makers (house-dweller vs builder — **this example not verified in a source read; do not use**); Estlund: expertise ≠ authority. [IEP-PlatoPol; SEP-Dem; SEP-AristPol]

## Debates
- **Is the wisdom-of-the-multitude passage a real argument for democracy?** Waldron (1990s) reads Politics III.11 as a deliberative "summation" argument anticipating epistemic democracy; Cammack (2013) and Lane (2013) contest the reading; SEP-AristPol: Aristotle allows it "perhaps also involves some truth" while remaining critical of democracy. [SEP-JT §6.1; SEP-AristPol]
- **Rousseau: epistemic or constitutive general will?** Grofman & Feld (1988) read Rousseau through the jury theorem; Wyckoff (2011) is critical; SEP-Dem sets out both readings; whether Rousseau rejects all representative government is disputed (Fralin 1978 vs Cohen 2010). [SEP-JT; SEP-Dem; SEP-R]
- **Instrumentalism vs proceduralism vs epistemic proceduralism**: Arneson, Wall (instrumental); Waldron, Christiano, Kolodny (intrinsic/egalitarian); Estlund (hybrid); Peter (pure epistemic proceduralism); Lafont (anti-deference). [SEP-Dem; SEP-Leg]
- **Riker vs Mackie** on whether social choice theory makes "the will of the people" meaningless. [SEP-Dem §6; SEP-Arrow; SEP-SC; WS]
- **Epistocracy**: Brennan, Caplan, Somin vs Estlund (demographic objection), Landemore (diversity), Lafont (equal authority), critics of voter-ignorance evidence (Elliott). [SEP-Dem; SEP-Leg; WP-Against_Democracy]
- **Diversity trumps ability?** Hong & Page, Landemore vs Thompson (2014), Quirk, Brennan, Bajaj. [SEP-Dem; SEP-SocEp]
- **Sortition vs elections**: Guerrero, Landemore vs Lafont; Fishkin's trilemma. [SEP-Dem §4.2.6; WP-Deliberative_democracy]
- **Confucian meritocracy vs Confucian democracy**: Bell, Chan, Bai, Jiang vs Angle, Kim, Tan, Hall & Ames, Ziliotti; Nathan and others on whether Bell's model describes China at all (WS). [SEP-Mer §3.1]
- **Consensus vs majority rule in Africa**: Wiredu vs Eze (1997), Jacques; Matolino on both sides over time (WS); Oruka/Hountondji on unanimity as a tool of one-party rule. [IEP-W; SEP-SAGE; WS]
