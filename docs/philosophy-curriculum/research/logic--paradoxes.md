## 1. What a paradox is
- Mark Sainsbury's definition: "This is what I understand by a paradox: an apparently unacceptable conclusion derived by apparently acceptable reasoning from apparently acceptable premises." R. M. Sainsbury, *Paradoxes*, Cambridge University Press, 3rd edition 2009, p. 1 (first edition 1987; 2nd edition 1995, cited by SEP-Sor and SEP-Super). [source: WP-Sorites_paradox, citing the book with that quotation; WP-Paradox (orig-date 1987); SEP-Sor bibliography "Sainsbury, R. M., 1995, Paradoxes"]
- Huggett's working definition in SEP-Zeno: "This is what a 'paradox' is: a demonstration that a contradiction or absurd consequence follows from apparently reasonable assumptions." [source: SEP-Zeno §1.1]
- Raffman (SEP-Sor) on the heap: "The argument is a paradox because apparently impeccable reasoning from apparently impeccable premises yields a falsehood." [source: SEP-Sor intro]
- Quine's classification. W. V. Quine's essay first appeared as "Paradox", *Scientific American* 206 (4), April 1962, reprinted as "The Ways of Paradox" in *The Ways of Paradox and Other Essays* (Random House 1966; revised and enlarged edn Harvard University Press 1976), pp. 1–21. Three classes: (a) **veridical** paradoxes, whose surprising conclusion is true (examples: the barber, read as proving that no such barber exists; Frederic in *The Pirates of Penzance*, who is 4n years old on his nth birthday); (b) **falsidical** paradoxes, whose conclusion is false because the proof contains a fallacy (example: the comic proof that 2 = 1 by dividing by x − 1 = 0; Quine counts some of Zeno's paradoxes, including the Achilles, as falsidical); (c) **antinomies**, which reach a self-contradiction by accepted ways of reasoning; "it is they that bring on the crises in thought". Quine also wrote: "One man's antinomy can be another man's veridical paradox, and one man's veridical paradox can be another man's platitude." [source: WP-Paradox §"Quine's classification"; WS-1, WS-6 (search summaries of the Dartmouth-hosted text of the essay, which could not be fetched). Treat the wording of the quotations as WS-level; the classification itself is confirmed by WP and by SEP-Russell (next item).]
- Quine called Russell's paradox an "antinomy" that "packs a surprise that can be accommodated by nothing less than a repudiation of our conceptual heritage" (Quine 1966, p. 11). [source: SEP-Russell §"Russell's paradox in contemporary logic"] (This quotation is verified via SEP.)
- Quine treated Grelling's (heterological) paradox as his main example of an antinomy. [source: WS-1; WP-Paradox (Grelling–Nelson as example of antinomy)]
- Criticism of the taxonomy: William Lycan ("The other ways of paradox", 2010) argues Quine's classes depend on the current state of one's knowledge (whether the fallacy has yet been found). [source: WS-6, philpapers record LYCTOW]
- "Dialetheia" is sometimes treated as a fourth kind or a special case of antinomy. [source: WP-Paradox]

## 2. Zeno: the sources and the four arguments against motion
- Zeno of Elea, born about 490 BCE (estimate from Plato's *Parmenides*: nearly 40 when Socrates was about 20, Socrates born 469). His book is lost; "almost none of these paradoxes are quoted in Zeno's original words ... but in paraphrase", principally via Aristotle and his commentators, especially Simplicius, "who, though writing a thousand years after Zeno, apparently possessed at least some of his book". [source: SEP-Zeno intro] (Matches the published Presocratics chapter.)
- Huggett: about 40 "paradoxes of plurality" (two definitely survive, a third probably); Aristotle gives four arguments against motion "all of which he gives and attempts to refute", and two more paradoxes (Place; the Grain of Millet). [source: SEP-Zeno intro] Dowden counts "ten known paradoxes". [source: IEP-Zeno intro]
- Location: the four motion paradoxes are in Aristotle, *Physics* Book VI, chapter 9 (Bekker 239b5–240b7 for the chapter): Dichotomy 239b11, Achilles 239b14–16, Arrow 239b30 (Aristotle's rejection of instants at 239b8–9), Stadium 239b33–240a18. [source: SEP-Zeno §§3.1–3.4 (line numbers); WP-Zeno's_paradoxes ("Physics VI:9, 239b10", "VI:9, 239b15", "Part 9 ... 239b5"); IEP-Zeno (stadium "Book VI, chapter 9, 239b33-240a18")] Caveat: IEP-Zeno is internally inconsistent here (it gives the Achilles as "Book VI, Chapter 8, 239b14-16" and the Arrow as "Book VI, chapter 5, 239b5-32"); the Bekker numbers all fall in VI.9, so use VI.9.
- Translation used for the Aristotle quotations below: the wording matches the Oxford translation by R. P. Hardie and R. K. Gaye (as on the MIT Internet Classics Archive, cited by WP). SEP-Zeno's bibliography credits the *Physics* in Barnes (ed.), *Complete Works of Aristotle* (Princeton 1984) to "W. D. Ross (trans)"; that attribution looks doubtful (the Barnes *Physics* is the revised Hardie–Gaye), so name Hardie and Gaye. [source: WP-Zeno's_paradoxes ref "translated by R. P. Hardie and R. K. Gaye"; SEP-Zeno bibliography] 
- Dichotomy (Aristotle): "The first asserts the non-existence of motion on the ground that that which is in locomotion must arrive at the half-way stage before it arrives at the goal." (*Physics* 239b11) [source: SEP-Zeno §3.1; WP-Zeno's_paradoxes]
- Huggett's runner is "mythical Atalanta" running for the bus; the series she must run has a final member (1/2) but no first member: "..., then 1/16 of the way, then 1/8 of the way, then 1/4 of the way, and finally 1/2 of the way"; the paradox can also be run forwards (1/2, then 1/4 of the way, then 1/8, ...). [source: SEP-Zeno §3.1]
- Achilles (Aristotle's version): "In a race, the quickest runner can never overtake the slowest, since the pursuer must first reach the point whence the pursued started, so that the slower must always hold a lead." (*Physics* VI.9, 239b15, Hardie & Gaye) [source: WP-Zeno's_paradoxes]
- Simplicius' fuller version (*On Aristotle's Physics* 6, 1014.10), as translated in SEP-Zeno (translator not named): "it is necessary that what is to overtake [something], before overtaking [it], first reach the limit from which what is fleeing set forth. In [the time in] which what is pursuing arrives at this, what is fleeing will advance a certain interval ...". [source: SEP-Zeno §3.2]
- Huggett's worked numbers: Achilles 1 m/s, tortoise 0.1 m/s, 0.9 m head start; catch-ups 0.9 m, 0.09 m, 0.009 m...; he should catch it after 1 s at 1 m. [source: SEP-Zeno §3.2]
- Who supplied the tortoise? IEP-Zeno: "There is no evidence that Zeno used a tortoise rather than a slow human. The tortoise was a later addition by Simplicius. Aristotle spoke simply of 'the runner'". BUT SEP-ZenoE §1 (Palmer) describes a mid-5th-century BCE red-figure cup from Falerii (Rome, Villa Giulia inv. 3591) showing a hero racing a tortoise that "has every appearance of being the first known 'response' to the Achilles paradox" (recorded in the Presocratics research notes from SEP-ZenoE). The sources disagree; say only that Aristotle's text speaks of "the slowest" runner and that the tortoise is in Simplicius. [source: IEP-Zeno §3a; SEP-ZenoE §1 via `ancient--presocratics.md`; WP Aristotle quotation]
- **The Arrow** (Aristotle, *Physics* 239b30): "The third is ... that the flying arrow is at rest, which result follows from the assumption that time is composed of moments .... he says that if everything when it occupies an equal space is at rest, and if that which is in locomotion is always in a now, the flying arrow is therefore motionless." Diogenes Laertius ix.72: "What is in motion moves neither in the place it is nor in one in which it is not". [source: SEP-Zeno §3.3]
- **The Stadium** (Aristotle 239b33): "concerning equal bodies which move alongside equal bodies in the stadium from opposite directions—the ones from the end of the stadium, the others from the middle—at equal speeds, in which he thinks it follows that half the time is equal to its double". [source: SEP-Zeno §3.4]
- One modern reading: the Achilles and Dichotomy are reductios of the view that space and time are continuous; the Stadium and Arrow of the view that they are discrete. [source: SEP-Pre §6 via `ancient--presocratics.md`; IEP-Zeno says "some commentators have speculated" this]

### Argument A (reconstruct step by step): the Arrow
- P1. Time is composed of instants ("nows") and nothing else.
- P2. At any instant the arrow occupies a space equal to itself (it travels no distance during an instant).
- P3. Whatever occupies a space equal to itself is at rest.
- C1. So at every instant the arrow is at rest.
- C2. So, since the whole flight is made only of instants, the arrow never moves.
[source: SEP-Zeno §3.3; IEP-Zeno §3a (Arrow)]
- Why P2 might seem forced: if instants are indivisible and the arrow moved during one, it would be in different places at the instant's start and end, so the instant would have parts. [source: SEP-Zeno §3.3]
- Objection 1 (Aristotle): reject P1: "time is not composed of indivisible moments any more than any other magnitude is composed of indivisibles" (*Physics* 239b8–9). [source: IEP-Zeno §4 (quotation, translator unnamed); WP-Zeno's_paradoxes gives "Time is not composed of indivisible nows any more than any other magnitude is composed of indivisibles"] (Pemberton 2022: Aristotle rejects the claim that time is composed of instants at all. [source: SEP-Zeno §3.3])
- Objection 2 (the modern "at-at" answer): reject the inference from C1 to C2 / reject P3. Velocity at an instant is not distance over the duration of the instant (0/0 m/s "is not any number at all"); it is the limit of average speeds over shrinking intervals (the derivative dx/dt). Motion is being at different places at different times. So the arrow moves *at* an instant though not *during* it. [source: SEP-Zeno §3.3; IEP-Zeno §3a]
- Reply / residual worry: this "carries the counter-intuitive implication that motion is not something that happens at any instant, but rather only over finite periods of time". Bergson thought the at-at view expressed an absurdity: "movement is composed of immobilities" (Bergson 1911, *Creative Evolution*, trans. A. Mitchell, p. 308). [source: SEP-Zeno §3.3] Dowden: the paradox still "seems especially strong to someone who would prefer instead to say that motion is an intrinsic property of an instant, being some propensity or disposition to be elsewhere." [source: IEP-Zeno §3a]
- Stadium diagnosis: the "general verdict is that Zeno was hopelessly confused about relative velocities" (B and C each moving at S relative to A move at 2S relative to each other); but Sattler (2015) argues against this and other common readings; Davey (2007) offers a cleverer reading in terms of numbers of point-instants (refuted because any two line segments have the same number of points, in Cantor's sense of 1:1 correspondence); a final reading takes it as an argument against atomic (quantised) space and time. [source: SEP-Zeno §3.4]

## 3. Responses to Zeno: Aristotle, the calculus, and why some think it is not over
- Aristotle on potential vs actual infinity (Physics VIII): "For motion..., although what is continuous contains an infinite number of halves, they are not actual but potential halves." (263a25–27) "...Therefore to the question whether it is possible to pass through an infinite number of units either of time or of distance we must reply that in a sense it is and in a sense it is not. If the units are actual, it is not possible: if they are potential, it is possible." (263b2–5) [source: IEP-Zeno §5a; translator not named in IEP]
- Huggett on Aristotle: he saw (*Physics* 263a15) that dividing the time "could not be the end of the matter"; his full answer: an actual infinity of discontinuous half-runs (with a stop at each) is impossible, but the potentially infinite halves of a continuous run are fine; problems: the potential/actual distinction "was never fully achieved", and it "has played no role in mathematics since Cantor tamed the transfinite numbers". [source: SEP-Zeno §3.1] Sattler (2020) argues that the distance–duration correspondence was a crucial advance by Aristotle. [source: SEP-Zeno §3.1]
- Dowden: Aristotle's treatment "satisfied nearly all scholars for 1,500 years", buttressed by the Church's doctrine that only God is actually infinite; Berkeley, Kant, Gauss and Poincaré defended potential infinity; "Some historians say Aristotle had no solution but only a verbal quibble." Aristotle's point in a phrase: Zeno "was correct to suppose that at any time a runner's path can be divided anywhere, but incorrect to suppose the path can be divided everywhere at the same time." [source: IEP-Zeno §§1, 5a] (Ties to the Kant chapter's mention of potential infinity.)
- Cantor's reply to potential infinity (1887): "each potential infinite...presupposes an actual infinite." [source: IEP-Zeno §5a; translator not named]
- The calculus: 19th-century rigour. Cauchy gave the rigorous theory of infinite sums (in Cauchy's system 1/2 + 1/4 + ... = 1 but 1 − 1 + 1 − ... is undefined); Huggett's warning: naive manipulation of 1 − 1 + 1 − ... "leads to the conclusion that 1 = 0", so "until one can give a theory of infinite sums ... one cannot say that Zeno's infinite sum is obviously finite". [source: SEP-Zeno §2.1] "In the 19th century, infinitesimals were eliminated from the standard development of calculus due to the work of Cauchy and Weierstrass on defining a derivative in terms of limits using the epsilon-delta method." Dedekind's 1872 definition of the reals; Cantor's set theory in the 1870s; Abraham Robinson's nonstandard analysis (1960) later revived infinitesimals rigorously. [source: IEP-Zeno §§2, 5d]
- The "received view" / "Standard Solution": following Russell (1929, *Our Knowledge of the External World*, 182–198), notably Grünbaum (1967, *Modern Science and Zeno's Paradoxes*) and Salmon (ed., *Zeno's Paradoxes*, 1970; 2nd edn 2001). Dowden's summary: the Standard Solution "implies Achilles's path contains an actual infinity of places and parts, but Zeno was mistaken to assume this is too many." [source: SEP-Zeno §1.2; IEP-Zeno intro and bibliography]
- The Standard Solution in Dowden's form: Zeno assumed one of (1) too far to run, (2) not enough time, (3) too many places, (4) no final step, (5) too many tasks; "they are all false assumptions, according to the Standard Solution" ((5) is the supertask question). [source: IEP-Zeno §3a]
- Grünbaum's point: an extended line is made of uncountably many unextended points, so it cannot be a Cauchy sum of zero lengths; length must be attributed to sets of points via measure theory. [source: SEP-Zeno §2.3 (lines on Grünbaum 1967)]
- Why it may not be over (1): the mathematics must also describe physical space and time. "Nothing guarantees a priori that space has the structure of the continuum"; almost all physical theories assume it, but "quantum theories of gravity likely imply that they do not". Salmon's illustration: alcohol and water mixed give less than the sum of their volumes. [source: SEP-Zeno §5]
- Why it may not be over (2): supertasks. "The term 'super-task' itself was coined by J.F. Thomson (1954)" ("Tasks and super-tasks", *Analysis* 15(1): 1–13). Max Black (1950–51, "Achilles and the Tortoise", *Analysis* 11) dubbed them "infinity machines" and argued that an infinite series of tasks cannot be completed, so a completable run cannot be an infinite series of sub-runs whatever mathematics says. [source: SEP-Super intro and bibliography; SEP-Zeno §5]
- Russell held Achilles does complete infinitely many tasks (a task in 1/2 minute, another in 1/4, and so on). Weyl, Black and Thomson objected. [source: IEP-Zeno §5c]
- Hilbert's hotel described in a 1924 Hilbert lecture as reported by Gamow (1947) (optional colour). [source: SEP-Super §1]

### Argument B (reconstruct step by step): Thomson's lamp
- Setup: switch a lamp on for 1/2 minute, off for 1/4, on for 1/8, off for 1/16, and so on; the switchings finish within one minute.
- P1. If this supertask can be completed, then at the end of the minute the lamp is either on or off.
- P2. It cannot be on, since every "on" period is followed by an "off" period; it cannot be off, since every "off" is followed by an "on".
- C. So the supertask is impossible; and (Thomson's moral) Russell was wrong to describe Achilles as completing infinitely many tasks.
[source: IEP-Zeno §5c; SEP-Super §1.2]
- Objection (Paul Benacerraf 1962, "Tasks, Super-Tasks, and the Modern Eleatics", *Journal of Philosophy* 59: 765–784): the description fixes the lamp's state at every moment *before* the minute ends but says nothing about the state *at* the end; the limit is not in the sequence, so the lamp "could be either on or off". Thomson has shown only that his description is incomplete. [source: IEP-Zeno §5c; SEP-Super §1.2]
- Further replies: whether it is possible may turn on what a "task" is (does it need a minimum time?); Grünbaum: "We need to heed the commitments of ordinary language ... only to the extent of guarding against being victimized or stultified by them." Physical implementations (a bouncing ball) and relativistic "Malament–Hogarth" spacetimes are discussed; Earman and Norton (1996) survey. Dowden: "there is no agreement in the philosophical community on most of the questions". [source: IEP-Zeno §5c; SEP-Super §§1.2, 2]
- Minority dissenters from the standard account of the continuum for physical processes: Bergson, Black, Brentano, Brouwer, Feferman, William James, Peirce, Thomson, Whitehead, Weyl. [source: IEP-Zeno §5b]
- Russell: "Zeno's arguments, in some form, have afforded grounds for almost all theories of space and time and infinity which have been constructed from his time to our own." [source: IEP-Zeno §6 (quoted, source work not named there)]

## 4. Parallels in China and India
- Zhuangzi ch. 33 ("Under Heaven", *Tianxia*) lists Hui Shi's ten theses and then 21 more paradoxes "which 'the disputers used to respond to Hui Shi without end for their whole lives.' All lack explanations". [source: SEP-SN §5.1]
- IMPORTANT CORRECTION: the one-foot-stick paradox is NOT one of Hui Shi's ten theses; it is among the 21 paradoxes of the anonymous "disputers" (*bian zhe*) who answered him. Fraser's translation: "A one-foot stick, every day take away half of it, in a myriad ages it will not be exhausted." Fraser calls it "self-explanatory, a version of Zeno's racetrack paradox". [source: SEP-SNp]
- Legge's translation (1891, *Sacred Books of the East*): "If from a stick a foot long you every day take the half of it, in a myriad ages it will not be exhausted." [source: WP-Hui_Shi (block quotation of Zhuangzi 33, Legge)]
- Mohist Canon B60 "appears to present a solution": if we try to move a distance taking half the length at a time we never reach the end, so "in moving a measured length, we do not first cover one fraction and then another but can instead move the whole distance in one stage." [source: SEP-SNp]
- Another of the 21: "The barbed arrow at its swiftest, there is a time when it neither moves nor stops" (Fraser). Fraser: "this is distinct from Zeno's arrow paradox, which is that the flying arrow is at rest in every instant of time and so does not move. Here the paradox is that the arrow is neither in motion nor at rest." (Legge: "Swift as the arrowhead is, there is a time when it is neither flying nor at rest.") [source: SEP-SNp; WP-Hui_Shi] — Contradicts IEP-Zeno, which says "the Arrow Paradox was discovered by the Chinese dialectician Kung-sun Lung (Gongsun Long, ca. 325–250 B.C.E.)" independently of Zeno. Prefer Fraser: it is anonymous in Zhuangzi 33 and a different paradox.
- Hui Shi's dates: give a range (fl. 313 BCE per SEP-SN; 370–310 per WP; 380–305 per IEP); our main source, Zhuangzi 33, was probably written long after his death. Two theses relevant here (Fraser): "The dimensionless cannot be accumulated, its size is a thousand miles"; "Today go to Yue but arrive yesterday". [source: `ancient--hundred-schools.md` from SEP-SN §5]
- Gongsun Long's "white horse is not horse": already reconstructed in the Hundred Schools chapter; cross-refer only. [source: published hundred-schools chapter]
- Nāgārjuna, MMK chapter 2: "In the second chapter of the MMK we find a long discussion of the impossibility of motion. Parallels with Zeno's paradoxes have been drawn and it has been argued that Nāgārjuna's aim was to refute certain views of the structure of space and time (Siderits and O'Brien 1976 ['Zeno and Nāgārjuna on Motion', *Philosophy East and West* 26(3): 281–299])." Westerhoff: there is "some evidence" that his main aims were different: (1) to reject a substantialist reading of key Buddhist notions; (2) to defend a view of individuals and properties. [source: SEP-Nag §3.2]
- Westerhoff's two arguments from MMK 2: (a) the "property-absence argument" (MMK 2.3–6 [SEP misprints "2:3–3"], 9–10): there is no mover that does not move, so mover and motion are mutually dependent and the mover cannot exist by *svabhāva*; (b) the "property-duplication argument" (MMK 2.5, 11): "the mover moves" would require two motions, one making the mover a mover and one by which it moves, but there is only one. And: the "mover" (*gati*) means both motion along a road and motion from one life to the next (*saṃsāra*), so the point bears on rebirth and liberation. [source: SEP-Nag §3.2]
- Sengzhao (5th c. CE, Chinese Buddhist): "things do not shift" (already in the published Chinese Buddhism chapter; optional cross-ref). [source: published chapter text]

## 5. The sorites (the heap) and vagueness
- Eubulides of Miletus (fl. 4th c. BCE), Megarian, pupil of Euclid of Megara; "a contemporary of Aristotle, against whom he wrote with great bitterness" (Diogenes Laertius 2.108–109). Diogenes credits him with seven puzzles: the Liar, the Masked Man, the Electra, the Overlooked Man, the Heap (*sōritēs*), the Bald Man (*phalakros*), the Horns. [source: WP-Eubulides] "The Megarian philosopher Eubulides (4th century BC) is usually credited with the first formulation of the puzzle. (The name 'sorites' derives from the Greek word soros, meaning 'heap'.)" His motives are unknown. [source: SEP-Sor §1] SEP-AncLog: "Eubulides of Miletus (mid-4th c.) is on record as the inventor of both the Liar and the Sorites". [source: SEP-AncLog §1] Caveat: SEP-Insol notes Diogenes says only that Eubulides *discussed* the Liar, not that he discovered it. [source: SEP-Insol §1]
- Later history: used "as a dialectical weapon, most notably by the Sceptics against the Stoics' claims to knowledge"; "attracted little subsequent interest until the late 19th century"; Plekhanov (1908) and other Marxists cited it against "customary" logic. [source: SEP-Sor §1]
- Peirce's 1902 definition (Baldwin's *Dictionary of Philosophy and Psychology*): "A proposition is vague when there are possible states of things concerning which it is intrinsically uncertain whether, had they been contemplated by the speaker, he would have regarded them as excluded or allowed by the proposition. By intrinsically uncertain we mean not uncertain in consequence of any ignorance of the interpreter, but because the speaker's habits of language were indeterminate." [source: SEP-Vag §1] Russell (1923) and Ramsey spread interest. [source: SEP-Vag §1]
- "There is wide agreement that a term is vague to the extent that it has borderline cases." Vagueness differs from ambiguity (disambiguating "tall" does not remove borderline cases). [source: SEP-Vag intro, §2]
- Three conditions for a sorites (Raffman): a sorites series exists for the predicate; neighbours differ only incrementally (Crispin Wright's term: the predicate's "tolerance"); the predicate is true of the first item and false of the last. [source: SEP-Sor §2]
- Everyday cousins (Dorothy Edgington 1997: 296): "There's the 'mañana paradox' ... the dieter's paradox: I don't care at all about the difference to my weight one chocolate will make." [source: SEP-Sor intro]

### Argument C (reconstruct step by step): the sorites
Conditional (modus ponens) form, SEP-Sor wording:
- P1. 1 grain of wheat does not make a heap.
- P2. If 1 grain doesn't make a heap, then 2 grains don't.
- P3. If 2 grains don't make a heap, then 3 grains don't.
- ... P1,000,000. If 999,999 grains don't make a heap, then 1 million grains don't.
- C. Therefore, 1 million grains don't make a heap.
[source: SEP-Sor intro]
- It runs in reverse too (1 million grains make a heap, so ... 1 grain makes a heap), so "soritical reasoning appears to show both that no number of grains make a heap and that any number of grains make a heap." [source: SEP-Sor intro]
- Other forms: mathematical-induction sorites (Φα₁; for all n, if Φαₙ then Φαₙ₊₁; so for all n, Φαₙ); the line-drawing sorites (by the least-number principle there must be an n with Φαₙ and not Φαₙ₊₁: "competent users of 'bald' both must, and must not, draw a line"); the "forced march" (Horgan 1994; Soames 1999). [source: SEP-Sor §2]
- Responses can deny a premise (type 2), deny validity (type 3), or accept the conclusion (type 4); or say vague language is beyond logic (type 1, Frege and Russell's ideal-language view). [source: SEP-Sor §3]
- **Epistemicism** (Sorensen 1988 *Blindspots*, 2001 *Vagueness and Contradiction*; Williamson 1994 *Vagueness*, Routledge): "vagueness is just a form of ignorance: vague terms have sharp boundaries whose locations are hidden from us"; one conditional premise is simply false; bivalence and classical logic are kept. Williamson: meaning supervenes on use; boundaries are fixed by the whole pattern of use, which we cannot survey ("the data are infinite"); our knowledge is inexact and governed by "margin for error" principles, so in borderline cases a correct classification would be right only by luck. Objection: even proponents grant it is "intuitively implausible"; it may have "the feel of a 'just so' story". Graff Fara (2008): the boundary is sharp but shifts with speakers' interests, so we never meet it. [source: SEP-Sor §3.2]
- **Supervaluationism** (Kit Fine, "Vagueness, Truth and Logic", *Synthese* 30 (1975): 265–300; Keefe 2000; adapting van Fraassen 1966): a sentence is "super-true" if true on every admissible precisification; borderline sentences are neither; classical laws all hold (excluded middle is kept: any number of grains either does or does not make a heap), but the semantics is not truth-functional. Verdict: the conditional sorites is valid but unsound; the induction premise is false; "it is true that for some n" the line is crossed, yet "there is no particular point of which it is true that it is the cut-off point". Objections: true disjunctions with no true disjunct and existentials with no true instance; Williamson (1994) on failures of conditional proof, reductio etc. once a "definitely" operator is added, and on higher-order vagueness. [source: SEP-Sor §3.3.1]
- **Degree theories / fuzzy logic**: fuzzy sets introduced by Lotfi Zadeh, "Fuzzy Sets", *Information and Control* 8(3) (1965): 338–353; truth values in the real interval [0,1]. Goguen (1969) and Zadeh ("Fuzzy Logic and Approximate Reasoning", *Synthese* 1975) proposed infinite-valued logic for vagueness; "Just as baldness comes in degrees so too ... does the truth of sentences predicating baldness"; Nicholas J. J. Smith (2008) defends a fuzzy semantics ("fuzzy plurivaluationism"); Edgington (1996) a non-truth-functional degree theory. Objections: what is a degree of truth? why these numbers? higher-order vagueness; not all predicates give a total ordering (two reddish patches differing in hue and brightness). [source: SEP-Fuzzy §1; SEP-Sor §3.3.3]
- **Contextualism**: Hans Kamp (1981): each instance of the induction premise is true in its own context, but adding the universal premise makes the context incoherent, so it is false; Diana Raffman (1994, "Vagueness Without Paradox", *Philosophical Review* 103) adds a psychological "backward spread" (a Gestalt-like shift when a speaker in a forced march switches category); Graff Fara's interest-relative view is related. [source: SEP-Sor §3.3.4]
- **Nihilism / "embracing the paradox"**: Peter Unger ("There Are No Ordinary Things", *Synthese* 41 (1979): 117–154) and Wheeler (1979): the negative sorites arguments are sound; "Terms like 'heap', 'bald', 'hirsute', 'rich' and 'poor' apply to nothing". In Sorensen's words, Unger concluded "that there are no children". Objection: Williamson 1994 ch. 6; and accepting all sorites as sound would make everyone bald and no one bald. Dummett (1975) held that vague observational predicates are incoherent. [source: SEP-Sor §3.4; SEP-Vag §5]
- **Higher-order vagueness**: "there is normally unclarity as to where the unclarity begins. Twilight governs times that are borderline between day and night. But we are equally uncertain as to when twilight begins ... 'borderline case' has borderline cases." Consequence: a three-valued "buffer zone" only moves the sharp line ("Any finite number of intermediates just delays the inevitable"). Some (Wright 2021; Raffman 2005) deny it is real. [source: SEP-Vag §1]
- Liar–sorites unification has been proposed (McGee 1991; Tappenden 1993; Field 2003). [source: SEP-Sor §4]
- Most theorists think the sorites is solvable (the argument is defective). [source: SEP-Sor intro]

### Chrysippus and the sorites (Hellenistic chapter defers this here)
- The Stoics recognised the importance of both the Liar and the Sorites (Cicero *Acad.* 2.95–8, Plutarch *Comm. Not.* 1059D–E, Chrysippus *Log. Zet.* col. IX). [source: SEP-AncLog §5.5]
- Bobzien's account: "Chrysippus' stand on the Sorites seems to have been that vague borderline sentences uttered in the context of a Sorites series have no assertibles corresponding to them, and that it is obscure to us where the borderline cases start, so that it is rational for us to stop answering while still on safe ground ... The latter remark suggests Chrysippus was aware of the problem of higher order vagueness. Again, bivalence of assertibles is preserved (cf. Bobzien 2002)." [source: SEP-AncLog §5.5]
- The "fall silent / stop answering" strategy is reported by Cicero, *Academica* II (Lucullus), around §§92–94; a translation describes Chrysippus, questioned step by step, choosing to pause; the image of a charioteer reining in his horses before danger appears in the passage. [source: WS-3 (search summary of secondary translations); section number not verified — see NOT VERIFIED]
- See Debates for epistemicist vs non-epistemicist readings of Chrysippus.

## 6. The Liar
- Eubulides' version (Diogenes Laertius 2.108 list): "A man says that he is lying. Is what he says true or false?" [source: IEP-Liar §1] (Contradiction: IEP-Liar calls Eubulides "a contemporary of Socrates"; WP and SEP put him mid-4th c. BCE, a contemporary of Aristotle. Use "contemporary of Aristotle".)
- Philitas (Philetas) of Cos (c. 330–c. 270 BCE) is said by Athenaeus (*Deipnosophists* IX.401e) to have wasted away worrying over the Liar; his supposed epitaph: "Philetus of Cos am I / 'Twas the Liar who made me die, / And the bad nights caused thereby." [source: SEP-Insol §1; IEP-Liar §1 (a story; "if we are to believe" Athenaeus)]
- Chrysippus wrote many Liar books (Diogenes Laertius VII.196–98), including six books on the Liar itself, *Reply to Those Who Think There Are Propositions That Are Both True and False*, and *On the Solution to the Liar* (three books); almost nothing survives. Theophrastus wrote three rolls on it (IEP). [source: SEP-Insol §1; IEP-Liar §1]
- Chrysippus's possible solution (Bobzien): an ineliminable ambiguity in "I am speaking falsely" between "I falsely say I speak falsely" and "I am speaking falsely", exactly one of which is true, though which is arbitrary; bivalence preserved (cf. Cavini 1993). [source: SEP-AncLog §5.5]
- Cicero's formulation, *Academica* II.xxix.95–xxx.97: "If you lie and speak that truth [namely, that you lie], are you lying or speaking the truth? ... If you say you lie, and you speak the truth, you lie; but you say you lie, and you speak the truth; therefore, you lie." Cicero calls such puzzles *inexplicabilia*. Aulus Gellius (2nd c. CE, *Attic Nights* XVIII.ii.10): "When I lie and say I am lying, am I lying or speaking the truth?" Seneca (*Ep.* 45.10) names the *pseudomenon*. [source: SEP-Insol §1.1 (translations as given there, translator not named)]
- **Epimenides vs the Liar.** Titus 1:12 (King James Version): "One of themselves, even a prophet of their own, said, The Cretians are alway liars, evil beasts, slow bellies." (KJV spells "alway"; SEP-Insol prints "always".) NRSV: "Cretans are always liars, vicious brutes, lazy gluttons." Clement of Alexandria (*Stromata* 1.14) identifies the prophet as Epimenides of Crete (c. 600 BCE / 6th c. BCE). [source: WP-Epimenides_paradox; SEP-Insol §1.2]
- The Epimenides is NOT a genuine paradox: if "all Cretans are always liars" is false, it follows only that some Cretan sometimes tells the truth, which need not be Epimenides; so the sentence can simply be false without contradiction. SEP-Liar's note: "a paradox nearly occurs in the New Testament". [source: WP-Epimenides_paradox; SEP-Liar note 1]
- No medieval author is known to have discussed the logical problem in Titus 1:12; "It is not known who was the first to link this text with the Liar Paradox." Callimachus (*Hymn to Zeus*) and Epimenides probably meant it without irony. Linking it with logical paradox is modern (WP says seen as a paradox "only since the 19th century"; Thomas Fowler 1869 states it as one). [source: SEP-Insol §1.2; WP-Epimenides_paradox]
- Aristotle, *Sophistical Refutations* 25 (180a27–b7): "The argument is similar too concerning the same man's lying and speaking the truth at the same time" (180b2–3); medieval logicians read this as the Liar and treated insolubles as a fallacy *secundum quid et simpliciter*. [source: SEP-Insol §1.3]
- Medieval *insolubilia*: discussed from the late 12th century; three periods (to the 1320s; an intense second quarter of the 14th c.; after c. 1350); "medieval interest seems not to have been prompted by any sense of theoretical 'crisis'". Thomas Bradwardine (c. 1300–1349) wrote his *Insolubles* at Oxford between 1321 and 1324; Ralph Strode called him "the first one who discovered something worthwhile about insolubles". [source: SEP-Insol intro, §3.1]
- **Buridan**: final view in the ninth treatise of the *Summulae de Dialectica*, the *Sophismata* (mid-1350s version). Early view: every proposition signifies (or implies) its own truth, so an insoluble, which signifies its own falsity, signifies that it is both true and false and so is false. Later view: every proposition "virtually implies" (*implicat virtualiter*) another asserting its truth; "Socrates utters a falsehood", said by Socrates, is false because that implied proposition cannot be true. Read calls the solution "deeply problematic"; Klima defends it. [source: SEP-Insol §3.8]
- Buridan's Sophism 9 is "structurally equivalent to the postcard paradox" (front: "the sentence on the back is true"; back: "the sentence on the front is false"). [source: SEP-SelfRef §1.1]
- Buridan's "proof of God" pair, as IEP presents it: "God exists." / "None of the sentences in this pair is true." The only consistent assignment makes "God exists" true. [source: IEP-Liar §1] (IEP paraphrase; present as IEP's illustration, not a quotation.)
- Arabic and Persian traditions: the Liar was discussed in the Islamic world from the mid-9th to mid-13th centuries (Alwishah and Sanson, "The Early Arabic Liar", *Vivarium* 47 (2009)); in 15th-century Shiraz Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Dashtakī and Jalāl al-Dīn al-Dawānī debated it (El-Rouayheb 2020); Dashtakī's solution synthesised those of Ṭūsī and Samarqandī (Zarepour 2024). SEP-Insol: these traditions "are only starting to be studied". [source: SEP-Insol §1.1 and bibliography]

### Argument D (reconstruct step by step): the Liar and the strengthened Liar
- Simple-falsity Liar ("FLiar: FLiar is false"): if true, then (by what it says) false; if false, then what it says is so, so true. So true iff false. With bivalence, it is both, a contradiction; and in classical logic a contradiction implies everything ("triviality"). [source: SEP-Liar §1.1]
- Obvious response: deny bivalence (a gap). [source: SEP-Liar §1.1]
- Strengthened / untruth Liar ("ULiar: ULiar is not true"): every sentence is either true or not true, gaps included, so the paradox returns even without bivalence; giving up excluded middle alone is not enough, since a Reductio-based version also reaches contradiction. [source: SEP-Liar §1.2] Terminology: van Fraassen (1968) coined "strengthened Liar" for this; the term is more often used for "revenge" paradoxes after C. Parsons (1974). [source: SEP-Liar note 2]
- Liar cycles without self-reference in a single sentence: Max: "Agnes' claim is true." Agnes: "Max's claim is not true." [source: SEP-Liar §1.3]
- The T-schema: Tr(⌜A⌝) ↔ A, "going back to Tarski (1935)". [source: SEP-Liar §2.2]

### Tarski
- Tarski published his analysis "in Polish" in 1933 ("The concept of truth in the languages of the deductive sciences", Warsaw); German version "Der Wahrheitsbegriff in den formalisierten Sprachen", *Studia Philosophica* 1 (1935): 261–405; English trans. J. H. Woodger, "The concept of truth in formalized languages", in *Logic, Semantics, Metamathematics* (1956; 2nd edn ed. Corcoran 1983), 152–278. [source: SEP-Tarski §1, bibliography; SEP-Liar bibliography]
- Convention T: a definition is "materially adequate" (Polish *trafny*, better "accurate") if it implies every instance of "φ(s) if and only if ψ" where s names a sentence S and ψ is its translation into the metalanguage (the familiar example: "'snow is white' is true if and only if snow is white" — the quotation-name 'snow is white' appears in SEP-Liar §2.1). Tarski's name for the approach: "the semantic conception of truth". [source: SEP-Tarski §1.3; SEP-Liar §2.1]
- Object language vs metalanguage: "Convention T rapidly leads to the liar paradox if the language L has enough resources to talk about its own semantics"; "a truth definition for a language L has to be given in a metalanguage which is essentially stronger than L"; consequence: truth for ZF set theory cannot be defined in set theory itself. [source: SEP-Tarski §1.3] "Working in classical logic, Tarski (1935) famously concluded from the Liar paradox that a language cannot define its own truth predicate." [source: SEP-Liar §4.1.3] (This is the undefinability theorem; Formal Logic covers Gödel.)
- Tarski "seems to have thought the Liar shows the ordinary notion of truth to be incoherent, and in need of replacement". [source: SEP-Liar §3]
- The hierarchy and its objections: Tr₁ for L₀ in L₁, Tr₂ for L₁ in L₂, and so on; problems: (a) "cases of self-reference unproblematically occur in natural languages, so declaring Liar sentences ill-formed seems unmotivated"; (b) "true at some level" would bring paradox back; (c) Kripke's (1975) objection: if Jc says everything Michael says is true and Michael says everything Jc says is true, each must speak at a higher level than the other, which is impossible. (Kripke's own example used Nixon and Dean; SEP restates it with Jc and Michael — the Nixon/Dean names are NOT verified here.) [source: SEP-Liar §4.3.1]
- Tarski 1944: "The semantic conception of truth", *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 4(3): 341–375 (SEP-Liar) or 341–376 (SEP-Tarski) — minor page discrepancy. [source: both bibliographies]

### Kripke
- Saul Kripke, "Outline of a Theory of Truth", *Journal of Philosophy* 72(19) (1975): 690–716 (related: Martin and Woodruff 1975). [source: SEP-Liar bibliography and §4.1.1]
- Idea: start with a classical language without a truth predicate; add Tr applying to all sentences, including those containing Tr; the predicate is partial (an extension and an anti-extension that need not exhaust everything), giving a Strong Kleene (K3) three-valued scheme; build up by stages from empty extension, adding true sentences and false ones, until the process "ceases to be productive (it reaches a 'fixed point')". In the fixed point, A and Tr(⌜A⌝) always have the same value; the Liar is in neither the extension nor the anti-extension. [source: SEP-Liar §4.1.1]
- Limitations: no "suitable conditional", so not all T-biconditionals hold; and "a central problem for the paracomplete approach is that it cannot accurately state its own solution to the Liar" (it cannot truly say "the Liar is not true"); Kripke himself noted some semantic concepts cannot be expressed; pressed by C. Parsons (1974). Field (2008) adds a new conditional and a hierarchy of "determinately" operators. [source: SEP-Liar §§4.1.1, 4.1.3]
- **Revenge**: whatever status a theory assigns the Liar ("neither", "not determinately true"), a new Liar built from that status ("This sentence is false or neither", "This sentence is not determinately true") seems to restore the paradox; discussed under "revenge paradoxes" (Murzi and Scambler 2026 overview). Formal point: in K3 and LP, "the Liar is neither true nor false" is equivalent to "the Liar is both true and false". [source: SEP-Liar §4.1.3]
- Other families (mention only): revision theory (Gupta 1982; Herzberger; Gupta and Belnap 1993); contextualism; substructural logics (noncontractive, nontransitive). [source: SEP-Liar §§4.2, 4.4, 4.5 and bibliography]

### Dialetheism and paraconsistency
- "A dialetheia is a sentence, A, such that both it and its negation, ¬A, are true"; dialetheism is the view that there are some. Word coined by Graham Priest and Richard Routley (later Sylvan) in 1981, inspired by Wittgenstein's "Janus head" remark on Russell's contradiction (*Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics* III.59); they "forgot to agree how to spell the 'ism'". [source: SEP-Dial intro, §1]
- Priest's "The Logic of Paradox" (1979) "developed eventually into the book In Contradiction (1987/2006)" (Dordrecht: Martinus Nijhoff, 1987; 2nd expanded edn Oxford 2006). His logic LP keeps excluded middle but rejects explosion (EFQ); it has truth-value "gluts" where K3 has "gaps". [source: SEP-Dial §2 and bibliography; SEP-Liar §4.1.2]
- Precursor: Florencio Asenjo (born in Buenos Aires), "A Calculus of Antinomies" (1966), based on his 1954 PhD dissertation: "Let us assume that atomic propositions have either one or two truth values." [source: SEP-Dial §2] (Global note: an Argentine logician; paraconsistency's name came from Peruvian Francisco Miró Quesada in 1976, per Formal Logic notes.)
- Dialetheic readings of Nāgārjuna (Priest, Garfield, Deguchi 2008; Priest 2002 ch. 16), e.g. MMK 18.8 in Garfield's translation: "Everything is real and not real, both real and not real, neither real nor not real. This is the Lord Buddha's teaching." Whether this is the right way to read Buddhist texts is debated (Tanaka 2013 collection). [source: SEP-Dial §2]
- Dialetheism is a minority view (cross-ref Formal Logic notes: "a 'small minority'"). [source: `logic--formal-logic.md` from SEP-CL]

## 7. Russell's paradox and its relatives
- Set-theoretic statement: naive (unrestricted) comprehension says that for any condition φ(x) there is a set {x : φ(x)}. Let R = {x : x ∉ x}. If R ∈ R, it fails the condition, so R ∉ R; if R ∉ R, it meets the condition, so R ∈ R. Contradiction. "The reasoning is entirely general": it is an instance of the theorem of pure logic ¬∃y∀x(Fxy ≡ ¬Fxx) (T269 in Kalish, Montague and Mar). [source: SEP-Russell §1]
- The barber "who shaves all and only villagers who don't shave themselves ... later associated with Russell's paradox". [source: SEP-Russell §1] Russell rejected it as an invalid form: "I once had a form suggested to me which was not valid, namely the question whether the barber shaves himself or not ... In this form the contradiction is not very difficult to solve." (*The Philosophy of Logical Atomism*, lectures 1918; WP cites "1919", *Collected Papers* vol. 8, p. 228.) The barber simply shows there is no such barber (Quine: veridical). [source: WP-Barber_paradox; WS-1]
- Zermelo's solution: replace comprehension by the Separation schema (*Aussonderungsaxiom*, Zermelo 1908): ∀A∃B∀x(x ∈ B ≡ (x ∈ A ∧ φ)) — you may only separate members out of an existing set. Applied to the universe V with φ = x ∉ x, the contradiction "shows is that V is not a set"; for any set B, R_B = {x ∈ B : x ∉ x} exists but cannot be in B, so "something ... is 'missing' from each set". [source: SEP-Russell §"untyped methods"]
- Type theory: Russell, "Mathematical Logic as Based on the Theory of Types", *American Journal of Mathematics* 30 (1908): 222–262; simple and ramified versions; Church (1940) gave an elegant simple type theory. Quine's New Foundations (1937) is another untyped route. [source: IEP-Liar bibliography; SEP-Russell] (History is in the Frege/Russell chapter.)
- Some set theorists (Lavine 1994) argue the paradox does not touch Cantor's "combinatorial" notion of a set; McLarty (1997): a crisis for Frege and Russell but "merely a source of problems and questions for set theorists". [source: SEP-Russell]
- **Grelling–Nelson (heterological)**, Kurt Grelling and Leonard Nelson, 1908: a predicate is heterological if it does not have the property it expresses ("German" is heterological, since it is not a German word; "deutsch" is not). Is "heterological" heterological? Either answer yields its opposite. Sometimes misattributed to Weyl. [source: SEP-SelfRef §1.1; WP-Grelling–Nelson_paradox]
- **Berry**: "the least number that cannot be referred to by a description containing less than 100 symbols" — a description of 93 symbols (SEP-SelfRef's version). First discussed in print by Russell (1906, "Les paradoxes de la logique"), who attributed it to G. G. Berry (1867–1928), a junior librarian at the Bodleian. [source: SEP-SelfRef §1.1; WP-Berry_paradox]
- **Curry's paradox** (Haskell Curry 1942; Löb 1955; Geach 1955; Prior 1955; Moh Shaw-Kwei 1954): uses only "if ... then" and truth, no negation. Step by step (SEP-Curry §1.1): let k = "If k is true then time is infinite". (1) Suppose k is true. (2) Then, by what k says, if k is true then time is infinite. (3) By modus ponens, time is infinite. (4) Discharging the supposition (conditional proof): if k is true then time is infinite. (5) But (4) is k, so k is true. (6) By modus ponens from (4) and (5): time is infinite. Any claim can replace "time is infinite" ("all numbers are prime"). Significance: since no negation is involved, many paraconsistent and paracomplete logics that defuse the Liar are still trivialised ("Curry paraconsistent", Slaney 1989); responses restrict contraction (first proposed by Moh). [source: SEP-Curry §§1.1, 4.2, 5.1]
- **Yablo's paradox** (Stephen Yablo, "Paradox Without Self-Reference", *Analysis* 53 (1993): 251–252; anticipated by Yablo 1985 and Visser 1984): an infinite list S₀, S₁, S₂, ..., each saying "all the later sentences are untrue". If any Sᵢ is true, all later ones are untrue, so Sᵢ₊₁ is untrue, so some later one is true — contradicting Sᵢ. So all are untrue; but then S₀'s claim is correct, so S₀ is true. Contradiction without any sentence referring to itself. Graham Priest (1997) argued it involves a hidden fixed point and so is circular after all; the debate continues. Kripke (1975) had raised the possibility of non-circular, infinitely descending paradoxes. [source: SEP-Liar §1.5; SEP-SelfRef; IEP-Yablo §§1–3; WP-Stephen_Yablo]
- **Gödel** (cross-ref Formal Logic): the first incompleteness theorem's sentence is often said to "say of itself that it is not provable"; SEP-Göd warns "Such imprecise statements ... should be taken at least with a grain of salt". The point to make here: Gödel turned a Liar-like construction about *provability* rather than truth into a theorem rather than a paradox. [source: SEP-Göd §2 (caution quotation); SEP-SelfRef §1 (diagonalisation from Cantor 1891)]
- Richard's paradox uses Cantor's diagonal method on phrases defining real numbers. [source: SEP-SelfRef §1.1]

## 8. Decision and knowledge paradoxes (optional, brief)
- Surprise test (Sorensen's version): a teacher announces a surprise test next week (classes Monday, Wednesday, Friday); the student eliminates Friday, then Wednesday, then Monday, and concludes a surprise test is impossible; yet surprise tests evidently are possible. First published reaction: D. J. O'Connor (1948), who judged the announcement self-defeating, like "I am not speaking now", which could "not conceivably be true in any circumstances". [source: SEP-Epist §1, §1.1]
- Newcomb's problem: devised by William Newcomb (Lawrence Livermore Laboratory); first analysed by Robert Nozick, "Newcomb's Problem and Two Principles of Choice" (1969, *Essays in Honor of Carl G. Hempel*): a transparent box with $1,000 and an opaque box with $1,000,000 or nothing, depending on a reliable predictor's forecast of whether you take one box or both. [source: WP-Newcomb's_problem]

## 9. What the solutions teach (synthesis points supported by sources)
- Zeno: the paradoxes forced theories of the continuum, infinite sums and instantaneous velocity (Aristotle's potential infinity; Cauchy, Weierstrass, Dedekind, Cantor), but whether physical space and time are mathematical continua remains an empirical question. [source: SEP-Zeno §5; IEP-Zeno]
- Sorites: forced a choice between classical logic with unknowable sharp lines (epistemicism) and non-classical semantics (supervaluations, degrees, contexts); "In effect, the sorites paradox is resolved only at a cost to other intuitions." [source: SEP-Sor §3.3.1]
- Liar: "something near or like logic alone leading us to incoherence ... perhaps the most virulent strain of paradox"; it has motivated non-classical logics (paracomplete: Kripke 1975, Field 2008; paraconsistent: Asenjo 1966, Priest 1984, 2006), though Ripley argues classical logic can be kept. [source: SEP-Liar intro, §3]
- Russell: axiomatic set theory "paved the way for stunning results in the metamathematics of set theory", including Gödel's and Cohen's independence results. [source: SEP-Russell]
- Liar paradoxes "cannot make do merely with ruling out ... problematic circular constructions" (Yablo). [source: IEP-Yablo §1]

## Debates
- **Zeno's purpose and target**: defend Parmenides' monism (Plato's *Parmenides*) vs "mocked the mockers" (Barnes) — in the Presocratics chapter; Huggett: some see a technical Pythagorean target, "most today see Zeno as opposing common-sense notions of plurality and motion". [source: SEP-Zeno §1.1]
- **Did Aristotle solve Zeno?** "Some historians say Aristotle had no solution but only a verbal quibble" (IEP takes no side); Sattler (2020) sees a crucial advance; Huggett finds it hard to see as fully satisfactory. [source: IEP-Zeno §1; SEP-Zeno §3.1]
- **Does the calculus dissolve Zeno?** Received view (Russell, Grünbaum, Salmon) yes, with the empirical caveat; supertask critics (Black, Thomson, Weyl) and continuum sceptics (Bergson, Brouwer, Peirce, Whitehead, James) no; Benacerraf vs Thomson on the lamp; "no agreement". [source: SEP-Zeno §§1.2, 5; IEP-Zeno §5]
- **Nāgārjuna and Zeno**: Siderits and O'Brien (1976) read MMK 2 as an attack on views of space and time like Zeno's; Westerhoff thinks the aims were Buddhist (anti-substantialism; individuals and properties). [source: SEP-Nag §3.2]
- **Was Chrysippus an epistemicist?** SEP-Sor lists "Chrysippus 3rd century BC" first among epistemicists; Bobzien (2002, "Chrysippus and the Epistemic Theory of Vagueness", *Proc. Aristotelian Society* 102: 217–238) argues he was not, but held that borderline sentences have no assertibles (no truth value). [source: SEP-Sor §3.2; SEP-AncLog §5.5; WS-3]
- **Which theory of vagueness?** epistemicism vs supervaluationism vs degree theory vs contextualism vs nihilism; whether higher-order vagueness is real (Sorensen, Williamson vs Wright 2021, Raffman 2005). [source: SEP-Sor §3; SEP-Vag §1]
- **Liar**: classical restriction (Tarski) vs paracomplete (Kripke, Field) vs paraconsistent/dialetheic (Priest) vs revision theory vs contextualism; whether any theory escapes revenge. [source: SEP-Liar §4]
- **Is Yablo's paradox circular?** Yablo: no; Priest (1997): a hidden fixed point makes it circular; Beall, Cook, Sorensen and others continue. [source: SEP-Liar §1.5; IEP-Yablo §3]
- **Buddhist texts and dialetheism**: Priest, Garfield, Deguchi read Nāgārjuna's contradictions literally; others dispute (Tanaka 2013). [source: SEP-Dial §2]
- **Quine's taxonomy**: Lycan (2010) says it depends on the state of our knowledge. [source: WS-6]

## Consistency with published Periergia texts
- *The Presocratics*: consistent (Zeno born c. 490 BCE; Aristotle preserves and names the motion paradoxes; Dichotomy; standard solution; supertasks; Diogenes walking; Huggett "would not impress Zeno"). No contradictions found. Its Dichotomy reconstruction should NOT be repeated in full here.
- *Hellenistic Philosophy*: consistent ("Chrysippus also worked on the Liar and the Sorites").
- *Hundred Schools*: consistent. But note: if this chapter quotes the stick paradox, it must NOT attribute it to Hui Shi himself (it is from the 21 disputers' paradoxes that answered him). The Hundred Schools chapter quotes only Hui Shi's ten theses, so there is no contradiction with it.
- *Frege, Russell*: consistent (barber "later attached"; Zermelo independently, 1897–1902; type theory 1903/1908). This chapter adds Russell's own rejection of the barber.
- *Scotus, Ockham*: no overlap on insolubles; consistent (Buridan in Paris arts faculty; SEP-Insol: from the 1320s until at least 1358; the published chapter gives born c. 1300, died about 1360).
- *Mahāyāna*: consistent (MMK 27 chapters, 450 verses; Garfield translation 1995).
- *Informal Fallacies*: refers to "the heap and the beard of the ancient Sorites puzzle"; Diogenes' list of Eubulides' puzzles has the *bald man*, not a beard (the "beard" phrasing came via SEP "Fallacies" §1.15). Not a factual error but this chapter should use "the heap and the bald man".
- *Kant*: expects this chapter to discuss infinity; covered in §3 (potential vs actual infinity; Cantor).
- *Formal Logic* notes: consistent (Kleene/Kripke; Zadeh 1975 cited there for fuzzy logic applied to vagueness; Zadeh's founding paper on fuzzy sets is 1965 — both dates correct for different papers).

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use
- That the one-foot-stick paradox is "Hui Shi's" (as the brief phrased it). Zhuangzi 33 attributes it to the anonymous disputers who answered Hui Shi (SEP-SNp; Legge via WP-Hui_Shi). Say "a paradox recorded in the *Zhuangzi*, from the disputers around Hui Shi".
- That Gongsun Long "discovered the Arrow paradox independently" (IEP-Zeno). Contradicted by SEP-SNp: the Chinese arrow paradox is anonymous in Zhuangzi 33 and is a different paradox (neither moving nor at rest).
