## 1. Framing: what feminist philosophy is
- SEP-FP: "Broadly understood, feminism is both an intellectual commitment and a political movement that seeks an end to gender-based oppression." [source: SEP-FP §1]
- The word: in the mid-1800s "feminism" still meant "the qualities of females"; after the First International Women's Conference in Paris in 1892, "feminism", following French *féministe*, was used regularly in English for advocacy of equal rights based on the equality of the sexes. [source: SEP-FP §1]
- The "waves" model (first wave mid-19th c. to the 19th Amendment, 1920; second wave late 1960s–1970s; third wave from the 1990s) is described by SEP-FP as having "some virtues" but overlooking "a great deal of heterogeneity"; it is a US-centred schema. [source: SEP-FP §1]
- SEP-G opens with bell hooks's definition: feminism is "the movement to end women's oppression" (hooks 2000, 26). [source: SEP-G intro]
- Alison Jaggar's *Feminist Politics and Human Nature* (1983) sorted feminist political philosophy into liberal, socialist, Marxist and radical feminism; SEP-P says this schema became much less relevant after the Cold War. [source: SEP-P §1–2.2]
- SEP-P: traditional political thought sharply separated public (state) from private (household), shielding domination in the home from scrutiny; Okin (1979) is cited for the claim that political thought presumed political actors were male. [source: SEP-P §1]

## 2. Forerunners (brief — see overlap)

## 4. Sex and gender: the distinction and its critics
- **Biological determinism as the target.** Geddes and Thompson (1889) held that women are "anabolic" (passive, conservative) and men "katabolic" (energetic, political), and that "what was decided among the prehistoric Protozoa cannot be annulled by Act of Parliament" (quoted from Moi 1999, 18). [source: SEP-G §1.1]
- In the 1970s sex differences were used to argue women should not become airline pilots (Rogers 1999, 11); a 1992 *Time* article attributed "women's intuition" to thicker corpora callosa; Anne Fausto-Sterling questioned this (variability; no difference in infants; skills improve with practice) (Fausto-Sterling 2000b, ch. 5). [source: SEP-G §1.1]
- **The word "gender."** Until the 1960s "gender" mostly meant grammatical gender. The psychologist **Robert Stoller (1968)** used "sex" for biological traits and "gender" for degrees of femininity/masculinity, to explain transsexuality ("sex and gender simply don't match"). Feminists then adopted the distinction. [source: SEP-G §1.2]
- **Gayle Rubin, "The Traffic in Women: Notes on the 'Political Economy' of Sex"** (1975), in R. Reiter (ed.), *Toward an Anthropology of Women* (Monthly Review Press), pp. 157–210: the "sex/gender system" is "a set of arrangements by which the biological raw material of human sex and procreation is shaped by human, social intervention" (165); gender is the "socially imposed division of the sexes" (179); women are oppressed "by having to be women" (204); the aim is a "genderless (though not sexless) society, in which one's sexual anatomy is irrelevant to who one is, what one does, and with whom one makes love" (204). [source: SEP-G §1.2 and bibliography]
- Linda Nicholson calls the early view "the coat-rack view": sexed bodies "provide the site upon which gender [is] constructed" (1994, 81). [source: SEP-G §1.2]
- Kate Millett (*Sexual Politics*, 1971 ed. cited): gender differences have "essentially cultural, rather than biological bases"; gender is "the sum total of the parents', the peers', and the culture's notions of what is appropriate to each gender…" (31). [source: SEP-G §2.1]
- Nancy Chodorow (1978): gendered personalities arise because women are primary carers of infants; remedy: equal parenting by both parents. [source: SEP-G §2.2]
- **MacKinnon on gender as sexuality**: genders are "created through the eroticization of dominance and submission. The man/woman difference and the dominance/submission dynamic define each other. This is the social meaning of sex" (MacKinnon 1989, 113 — *Toward a Feminist Theory of the State*). For her "'dominance' (power relations) is prior to 'difference'" (1989, ch. 12). [source: SEP-G §2.3]
- **Spelman's particularity argument** (*Inessential Woman*, 1988): gender realists assume a "golden nugget of womanness" (159); white middle-class feminists supposed "the womanness underneath the Black woman's skin is a white woman's…" (13), conflating "the condition of one group of women with the condition of all" (3). Example (Harris 1993; Stone 2007): MacKinnon's objectification criterion ignores that under slavery Black women were "hypersexualised" and their rape treated as impossible. [source: SEP-G §3.1.1]
- Iris Marion Young's worry about Spelman: it leaves a "gerrymandered collection of individuals"; without a category of women "it is not possible to conceptualize oppression as a systematic, structured, institutional process" (1997, 17). [source: SEP-G §3.1.1 end]

### Butler
- Judith Butler, born 24 February 1956, Cleveland; *Gender Trouble: Feminism and the Subversion of Identity* (1990; 2nd ed. 1999), *Bodies That Matter: On the Discursive Limits of Sex* (1993); at UC Berkeley since 1993. Uses she/her and they/them; said in 2020 they prefer they/them. [source: WP-JB] (SEP-G uses "they".)
- **Normativity argument**: (i) unitary notions of "woman" ignore "the multiplicity of cultural, social, and political intersections in which the concrete array of 'women' are constructed" (Butler 1999, 19–20); (ii) such definitions are normative: "[i]dentity categories [like that of women] are never merely descriptive, but always normative, and as such, exclusionary" (Butler 1991, 160). So the category of women "ought not to be the foundation of feminist politics" (1999, 9); 'woman' should be "a term in process, a becoming, a constructing that cannot rightfully be said to originate or end … open to intervention and resignification" (1999, 43). [source: SEP-G §3.1.2]
- **Performativity**: gender is not "a stable identity or locus of agency from which various acts follow; rather, gender is … instituted … through a stylized repetition of [habitual] acts" (Butler 1999, 179). Gender is "a doing rather than a being"; genders are "compelling illusion[s]" (Butler 1990, 271 — the 1990 essay "Performative Acts and Gender Constitution", in S.-E. Case (ed.), *Performing Feminisms*, pp. 270–282). [source: SEP-G §3.1.2 and bibliography]
- **Sex as constructed**: "perhaps this construct called 'sex' is as culturally constructed as gender; indeed, perhaps it was always already gender, with the consequence that the distinction between sex and gender turns out to be no distinction at all" (Butler 1999, 10–11). SEP-G stresses this is NOT a denial that bodies exist: bodies are "constructed as if they provide such material foundations" (Butler 1993). The doctor's "It's a girl" is treated as a performative, normative act. [source: SEP-G §3.3]
- Objection (Alison Stone 2007): social construction of both sex and gender does not make sex identical to gender; better to say claims about sex *imply* gender norms (70). [source: SEP-G §3.3]
- Martha Nussbaum's critique, "The Professor of Parody", *The New Republic*, 22 Feb 1999: Butler misreads J. L. Austin on performatives, makes erroneous legal claims, forecloses resistance by repudiating pre-cultural agency, and offers no "normative theory of social justice and human dignity". Susan Bordo: Butler reduces gender to language. Butler replied in the 1999 preface. In 1998 *Philosophy and Literature* gave Butler first prize in its "Bad Writing Competition". [source: WP-JB]

### Sex classification
- Ásta (Sveinsdóttir), *Categories We Live By: The Construction of Sex, Gender, Race, and Other Social Categories* (OUP 2018): **conferralism** — social properties are conferred by others and consist in "constraints and enablements". Schema (Ásta 2018, 8): conferred property / who / what / when / base property. For sex (72): conferred property *being female, male*; who: legal authorities drawing on doctors; what: "the recording of a sex in official documents…"; when: at birth or after surgery/hormonal treatment; base property: "the aim is to track as many sex-stereotypical characteristics as possible…". So sex is a conferred (institutional) status that *aims to track* physical features; gender is also conferred but differently, so the sex/gender distinction survives. [source: SEP-G §3.2]
- Stone (2007): sex is a cluster/spectrum concept; intersex and trans people are "at the centre of the sex spectrum". [source: SEP-G §3.2]
- Mikkola (2011) argues feminists should give up the sex/gender distinction on practical political grounds. [source: SEP-G §3.4]
- **Byrne (2020)**, "Are Women Adult Human Females?", *Philosophical Studies* 177: 3783–3803: "women are adult human females–nothing more, and nothing less" (3801), with six supporting considerations (dictionary definition; English should have a word for that category; we can know someone is a woman knowing only that she is an adult human female; parallel thesis for "girl"; role-reversal cases; "woman"/"female" as stylistic variants even in hyperintensional contexts). Tomas Bogardus (2020) similarly argues feminist philosophers merely assumed the distinction. [source: SEP-G §3.4]
- **Dembroff (2021)** replies: Byrne assumes one invariant meaning; cherry-picks the "dominant" meaning ignoring law and media; his own biological/social distinction fails by its own lights. Byrne (2021) "Gender Muddle: Reply to Dembroff", *Journal of Controversial Ideas*. [source: SEP-G §3.4]
- Holly Lawford-Smith (2021) treats "female/male" and "woman/man" alike as sex terms, with femininity/masculinity as gender norms. SEP-G declines to cover gender-critical discussion further because much of it "has taken place in social media, public fora, and other sources outside academic philosophy". [source: SEP-G §3.4]

## 5. "Women" as a group: Haslanger's ameliorative analysis (featured argument candidate A)
- **Paper**: Sally Haslanger, "Gender and Race: (What) Are They? (What) Do We Want Them To Be?", *Noûs* 34 (2000): 31–55; reprinted in *Resisting Reality: Social Construction and Social Critique* (OUP 2012), pp. 221–247. (SEP-T dates the reprint "2012 [2002]" — the original is 2000.) [sources: SEP-G bibliography; SEP-T bibliography; WP Sally_Haslanger]
- **Method**: an *ameliorative* analysis asks which concepts "best help feminists achieve their legitimate purposes" (2000b, 33) — not what we ordinarily mean. Feminists need gender terms "to identify, explain and talk about persistent social inequalities between males and females" (2000b, 36); societies "privilege individuals with male bodies" (2000b, 38). [source: SEP-G §4.2.1]
- **Definitions** (SEP-G quotes 2003a, 6–7; WP gives the same for the 2000 paper):
  - "S is a woman iff [by definition] S is systematically subordinated along some dimension (economic, political, legal, social, etc.), and S is 'marked' as a target for this treatment by observed or imagined bodily features presumed to be evidence of a female's biological role in reproduction."
  - "S is a man iff [by definition] S is systematically privileged along some dimension (economic, political, legal, social, etc.), and S is 'marked' as a target for this treatment by observed or imagined bodily features presumed to be evidence of a male's biological role in reproduction." [source: SEP-G §4.2.1; WP Sally_Haslanger]
- SEP-T's version (citing *Resisting Reality* 234–235): one *functions as a woman* in a context iff one is subordinated there on the basis of presumed female sex; one *is* a woman iff one "regularly, and for the most part" functions as a woman. [source: SEP-T §5.1]
- **Step-by-step (for the writer)**:
  1. Feminism's goal is to end sex-based injustice. 2. To do so we need concepts that pick out the groups that injustice creates. 3. The salient fact is that people perceived as having female reproductive bodies are systematically subordinated, those perceived as male privileged. 4. So the concept *woman* feminists should use is: subordinated on grounds of presumed female reproductive features. 5. Consequence: gender is a social hierarchy, and justice would abolish it ("If sexist oppression were to cease, women and men would no longer exist (although there would still be males and females)" — SEP-G's paraphrase: the view is **eliminativist**). [source: SEP-G §4.2.1]
- **Objection 1 (counterintuitive extension)**: females not subordinated on sex-marked grounds would not count as women — SEP-G's example: "At least arguably, the Queen of England … would not count as a woman"; unprivileged males would not count as men. **Reply 1**: the definitions are revisionary, not meant to capture ordinary usage. **Reply 2** (Haslanger 2005, 2006): perhaps not so revisionary after all — oppressive ideology can "mislead us about the content of our own thoughts" (2005, 12), so we may already apply "woman" on the basis of sex-marked subordination. **Counter (Saul 2006)**: extremely hard to show that this is what we in fact do, because ordinary application is "various and idiosyncratic" (129). Mikkola (2009): revisionary analyses are "politically unhelpful and philosophically unnecessary". [source: SEP-G §4.2.1]
- **Objection 2 (Stone 2007)**: defining woman by subordination means "any woman who challenges her subordinate status must by definition be challenging her status as a woman"; but "because norms of femininity can be and constantly are being revised, women can be women without thereby being subordinate" (160, 162). [source: SEP-G §4.2.1]
- **Objection 3 — inclusion (Katharine Jenkins, "Amelioration and Inclusion: Gender Identity and the Concept of Woman", *Ethics* 126 (2016): 394–421)**: an adequate feminist analysis must not wrongly exclude some who should count as women (the "inclusion problem"); Haslanger's analysis excludes trans women who are presumed biologically male (and counts trans men presumed female as women). Jenkins's revision: the ameliorative method yields two equally important target concepts — **gender as class** ("corresponds to Haslanger's proposed concept and captures the sense of gender as an imposed social class") and **gender as identity** ("captures the sense of gender as a lived identity") (397); female gender identity is "an internal 'map'" for navigating the realities characteristic of women as a class, experiencing feminine norms as relevant to oneself (410). [sources: SEP-G §4.2.1; SEP-T §5.1–5.2; WS-4]
- **Reply/counter to Jenkins**: Bettcher (2017a) worries that some trans women may lack such an identity before living as women, so may still be excluded; also Andler 2017, Barnes 2022; Jenkins (2018) responds. [source: SEP-T §5.2; SEP-G §4.2.1]
- **Other objection (Bach 2012)**: Haslanger's kind is not objective enough; women are a natural kind with a *historical essence* — "participation in a lineage of women" via gender socialisation (Bach 2012, 271). Mikkola (2016, 2020) replies that this has internal tensions and anti-trans implications (trans women not socialised female would count as men). [source: SEP-G §4.2.1]
- **Bettcher (2013)**: there is more than one correct understanding of "woman" — dominant and resistant (trans) — and trans women's self-identifications "should be taken at face value right from the start"; "in analyzing the meaning of terms such as 'woman,' it is inappropriate to dismiss alternative ways in which those terms are actually used in trans subcultures" (2013, 235). [source: SEP-G §4.2.1]
- **Saul (2012)**: semantic contextualism — in many contexts trans women count as women, but in contexts like restrooms the matter is settled by extra-semantic moral and political considerations; Saul does not finally endorse it. Díaz-León (2016) argues the relevant standards are semantic after all. [source: SEP-T §5.2]

### Other accounts of women as a group
- **Charlotte Witt, *The Metaphysics of Gender*** (OUP; SEP cites 2011a; WP lists 2010 — date varies by source/edition): **uniessentialism**, an Aristotelian *individual* essentialism: gender is "the principle of normative unity" (73) that unifies a *social individual* (distinct from person and human being). Gender is a social position that "cluster[s] around the engendering function … women conceive and bear … men beget" (40); "to be a woman is to be recognized to have a particular function in engendering" (39). Gender is the "mega social role" (life-long; inflects other roles). Witt holds "patriarchy is a social universal" (98), so gender rather than race is the unifying role. On trans people: transitioning means "the old social individual has ceased to exist and a new one has come into being", consistent with the same *person* persisting. [source: SEP-G §4.2.2; WP Charlotte_Witt]
- **Linda Alcoff, *Visible Identities* (2006)**: gender as **positionality**: "a position one occupies and from which one can act politically" (148); women and men are "differentiated by virtue of their different relationship of possibility to biological reproduction" (172); "there is no gender essence all women share" (147–8). SEP-T notes the view seems to imply no trans women count as women. [sources: SEP-G §4.2.3; SEP-T §5.1]
- **Mari Mikkola** (2009, 2016 *The Wrong of Injustice: Dehumanization and its Role in Feminist Philosophy*, OUP): feminists need no answer to "What is a woman?"; ordinary-language intuitions fix the extension; on trans inclusion she proposes it is settled politically, by negotiation. SEP-T (Bettcher) objects to the asymmetry this creates. [sources: SEP-G §4; SEP-T §5.1]
- **Robin Dembroff, "Beyond Binary: Genderqueer as Critical Gender Kind"**, *Philosophers' Imprint* 20 (2020): 1–23: genderqueer is "a category whose members collectively destabilize the binary axis, or the idea that the only possible genders are the exclusive and exhaustive kinds men and women" (2); principled vs existential destabilising (13). Dembroff says failure to understand non-binary identities produces hermeneutical injustice (link: *Testimony* chapter). [source: SEP-G §5]

### Trans inclusion and gender-critical feminism — evenhanded summary (all views attributed)
- Earliest major text: Janice Raymond, *The Transsexual Empire* (1979), which rejected trans women's self-identification; Sandy Stone's reply (founding essay of trans studies — SEP-T calls it so; title not recorded here, check before naming). [source: SEP-T §2–3]
- SEP-T: trans philosophy reached "critical mass" only after the "so-called 'transgender tipping point' of 2014" (*Time* cover with Laverne Cox, May 2014); "trans philosophy" circulated around the Trans Experience in Philosophy conference, Eugene, Oregon, 2016; the 2017 *Hypatia* controversy over Rebecca Tuvel's article on "transracialism" (retraction letter; counter-backlash over academic freedom). [source: SEP-T §7]
- SEP-T's characterisation: gender-critical feminism "expresses concern over the focus on the concept of gender which it takes to obscure sex-based oppression"; "typified by its rejection of trans self-identifying claims and right to access segregated spaces that align with their identities"; "can be regarded as continuous with trans-exclusionary forms of radical feminism, although it need not be". Gender-critical feminists played "a crucial role" in defeating the proposed reform of the UK Gender Recognition Act 2004 (2018 consultation; self-identification instead of a dysphoria diagnosis). [source: SEP-T §7 — Bettcher's description; present as hers]
- **Kathleen Stock, *Material Girls: Why Reality Matters for Feminism*** (London: Fleet, 2021): "woman" in ordinary use means "adult, female human"; anti-ameliorationist — no revision of the term, since sex is real and sex differences matter (medicine; pregnancy); argues that restrooms, changing rooms etc. should be segregated by "biological sex" because of males' greater average size and strength and patterns of male sexual aggression. [source: SEP-T §7] Stock was professor at Sussex until her resignation announced 28 Oct 2021 amid controversy; appointed OBE Dec 2020, criticised by an open letter of 600+ philosophers. [source: WP Kathleen_Stock]
- Replies reported by SEP-T: Finlayson, Jenkins and Worsdale (2018) say male violence is not explained by biology alone, so the inference to trans women is questionable; SEP-T cites Hasenbush et al. (2019) for evidence that trans-inclusive facility policies were not associated with increased risk to non-trans women; Heartsilver (2021) and Arvan (2023) argue trans women can count as "adult human females" in a social sense. [source: SEP-T §7]
- **Holly Lawford-Smith, *Gender-Critical Feminism*** (OUP 2022) and *Sex Matters: Essays in Gender-Critical Philosophy* (OUP 2023): women are a "sex caste"; sex is not socially constructed; gender is the norms assigned to the sexes to subordinate females; oppression of trans women is "a collateral effect"; she rejects intersectional feminism as generating too many issues for feminism to handle. SEP-T reports Killmister's criticisms. Lawford-Smith is associate professor at the University of Melbourne. [sources: SEP-T §7; WP Holly_Lawford-Smith]
- Butler's own recent book *Who's Afraid of Gender?* (2024) analyses the "anti-gender movement" as a "phantasm" aligned with authoritarian movements. [source: WP-JB]

## 6. Standpoint epistemology (featured argument candidate B)
- **Three core claims** (IEP-ST summary): "(1) Knowledge is socially situated. (2) Marginalized groups are socially situated in ways that make it more possible for them to be aware of things and ask questions than it is for the non-marginalized. (3) Research, particularly that focused on power relations, should begin with the lives of the marginalized." [source: IEP-ST]
- Genealogy: Hegel's master/slave dialectic → Marx/Engels and **Lukács**'s standpoint of the proletariat; feminist theorists named by IEP-ST: Dorothy Smith, Patricia Hill Collins, Nancy Hartsock, Alison Jaggar, Hilary Rose, Sandra Harding, Donna Haraway. [source: IEP-ST]
- SEP-E: a complete standpoint theory must specify (i) the social location, (ii) scope, (iii) which aspect of the location generates advantage, (iv) its ground, (v) the type of superiority, (vi) relative to which perspectives, (vii) modes of access. Uncontroversial example: auto mechanics know better than consumers what is wrong with cars. [source: SEP-E §2]
- **Nancy Hartsock** (1943, Ogden, Utah – 19 March 2015, Seattle): "The Feminist Standpoint: Developing the Ground for a Specifically Feminist Historical Materialism", in Harding and Hintikka (eds.), *Discovering Reality* (1983), pp. 283–310; also *Money, Sex, and Power: Toward a Feminist Historical Materialism* (Longman, 1983). [source: WP Nancy_Hartsock] Grounds of advantage per SEP-E: women's **centrality** to reproduction and caring for bodies (Hartsock, Rose); also, in early versions, a "cognitive style" drawn from object-relations theory. [source: SEP-E §2] Hartsock cites Marilyn French's *The Women's Room* (1978): "Washing the toilet used by three males … is, Mira thought, coming face to face with necessity." [source: IEP-ST]
- **Sandra Harding** (29 March 1935 – 5 March 2025): *The Science Question in Feminism* (1986), *Whose Science? Whose Knowledge?* (1991), "Rethinking Standpoint Epistemology: What is 'Strong Objectivity'?" (1993, in Alcoff & Potter eds., *Feminist Epistemologies*), ed. *The Feminist Standpoint Theory Reader* (Routledge 2004). [sources: WP Sandra_Harding; IEP-ST bibliography] Quotations: "Starting off research from women's lives will generate less partial and distorted accounts not only of women's lives but also of men's lives and of the whole social order" (1993, 56); "Standpoint theories map how a social and political disadvantage can be turned into an epistemic, scientific and political advantage" (2004, 7–8); a standpoint "is not something that anyone can have simply by claiming it. It is an achievement. A standpoint differs in this respect from a perspective, which anyone can have simply by 'opening one's eyes'" (1991, 127). [source: IEP-ST]
- **Standpoint ≠ perspective**: "a standpoint is an achieved collective identity or consciousness", earned through collective political struggle; Hartsock says "feminist standpoint", Dorothy Smith "women's standpoint". [source: IEP-ST §4] MacKinnon: "The privileged standpoint is not that of women, but of feminists" (SEP-E's summary of MacKinnon 1989). [source: SEP-E §2]
- **Patricia Hill Collins** (born 1 May 1948, Philadelphia): "Learning from the Outsider Within" (*Social Problems*, 1986); *Black Feminist Thought: Knowledge, Consciousness and the Politics of Empowerment* (1990; 10th-anniversary rev. ed. 2000; 30th 2023); C. Wright Mills Award 1990; Jessie Bernard Award 1993; ASA president; Berggruen Prize 2023. [sources: WP Patricia_Hill_Collins; WP Black_Feminist_Thought] Collins grounds Black feminist epistemology in Black women's experience of racism and sexism; "bifurcated consciousness" (Harding 1991, Collins 1990); "controlling images" — the Mammy, the Sapphire, the Jezebel. "Outsider within": Black women academics are insiders as academics, outsiders as Black women. [sources: SEP-E §2; IEP-ST §5–6] She cautions against assuming that "being Black and/or female generates certain experiences that automatically determine the variants of a Black and/or feminist consciousness" (1991, 21) [source: IEP-R]. Collins uses "matrix of domination" for interlocking social organisation, reserving "intersectionality" for its manifestation in lived experience (Collins 2000, 18). [source: SEP-M §1.2]
- Wheelchair example (Terri Elliot 1994, 424): person A sees "The Entrance"; person X sees "a great stack of stairs and the glaring lack of a ramp". [source: IEP-ST]
- Colonized/colonizer example: the colonized must learn the colonizer's language (Māori learned English), not vice versa — "double vision". [source: IEP-ST §5]
- **Objections**: (a) **automatic privilege** — reply: privilege is achieved, not given by location (IEP-ST); (b) **relativism** — reply: Harding's "strong objectivity", which demands critical reflection on the knower's own social location and claims to be *more* objective than "value-neutral" science; Harding calls her approach "neo-modern" (2001, 518) [source: IEP-ST §7b]; (c) **bias paradox** (Longino 1993, Hekman 1997): if all knowledge is situated, how can some situations be better? (IEP-ST §7c); Longino: no noncircular basis for deciding which standpoints are privileged (SEP-E §2); (d) **intersectional critique**: Crenshaw (1999 in SEP-E's citation) — no one group inequality is central; women's oppression takes different forms; postmodernists question a unified women's standpoint, seeing behind it the view of relatively privileged white women (Lugones & Spelman 1983) [source: SEP-E §2]; (e) Carby: standpoint talk segregates race and gender and posits white women as the norm [source: IEP-R].
- **Wylie's reformulation**: Alison Wylie (born 1954, Swindon; UBC), "Why Standpoint Matters" (2003, in Figueroa & Harding eds., *Science and Other Cultures*, pp. 26–48; reprinted in Harding 2004). SEP-E: Wylie argues consensus has emerged on two points — (1) **rejection of essentialism** (no fixed nature of the groups, no requirement that members think alike) and (2) **rejection of automatic epistemic privilege**; instead "insider-outsiders" sometimes have a **contingent** epistemic advantage on particular problems. Standpoint theorists (Collins 1996, Harding 1996, Hartsock 1996) "shifted from claims of general epistemic privilege to claims of practical advantage". [sources: SEP-E §5; WP Alison_Wylie] Wylie and Nelson's archaeology case: gender-sensitive inquiry into netting, basketry and skeletal remains widened the evidential base (2007, 64–70). [source: IEP-ST]
- Wylie: standpoint theory "may rank as one of the most contentious theories to have been proposed and debated in the twenty-five to thirty year history of second-wave feminist thinking about knowledge and science." [source: IEP-ST §7]
- **Step-by-step (for the writer)** — reconstruction from IEP-ST/SEP-E:
  1. What a knower can know is shaped by her social location (situated knowledge).
  2. Those in dominant positions have an interest in, and the means for, seeing current arrangements as natural; their partial view becomes "common sense".
  3. Members of subordinated groups must understand both their own world and that of the dominant to survive ("double vision"/"outsider within"); they also meet facts the dominant can ignore.
  4. When this experience is reflected on collectively and critically, it yields a *standpoint* (an achievement, not a birthright).
  5. Therefore inquiry that starts from marginalised lives yields "less partial and distorted accounts" — of everyone, not just the marginalised (Harding).
  - Objection: relativism / bias paradox — if (1) holds of everyone, (5) cannot claim superiority without a neutral standard. Reply: Harding's strong objectivity (situated knowledge plus systematic reflexivity is *more* objective); Wylie's weaker version: advantage is contingent, local and testable by ordinary epistemic virtues (empirical adequacy, coherence, explanatory power).

## 7. Feminist ethics and politics beyond care
- Care ethics: one paragraph only, link *Virtue Ethics* (Gilligan 1982; Noddings 1984; Held 2006; Kittay 1999). SEP-ET lists "Ethic of care and relational ethics" as one of four themes of feminist ethics, alongside gender binarism/essentialism/transfeminism, intersectionality, and criticisms/expansions of traditional moral theories. [source: SEP-ET contents]
- **Susan Moller Okin** (19 July 1946, Auckland – 3 March 2004): *Women in Western Political Thought* (Princeton 1979; parts on Plato, Aristotle, Rousseau, Mill, and "functionalism, feminism and the family"; reissued 2013 with intro by Debra Satz); *Justice, Gender, and the Family* (Basic Books 1989; critiques Rawls, Nozick, MacIntyre and Walzer); "Is Multiculturalism Bad for Women?" (1997 essay; book with respondents, Princeton 1999). Stanford professor from 1990. [sources: WP Susan_Moller_Okin; WS-3; SEP-L bibliography]
- **Okin's argument (featured argument candidate C; SEP-L §1.2)**:
  1. Rawls's principles apply to the basic structure, and the family is part of it; Rawls himself added sex to what is hidden behind the veil only later ("Fairness to Goodness", 1975, 537), not in *A Theory of Justice* (1971).
  2. Parties not knowing their sex would not accept a gender system that assigns roles by sex, since it circumvents "free choice of occupation" (Okin 1989, 103) — violating both the liberty and fair-equality-of-opportunity principles.
  3. So in a just society "gender could no longer form a legitimate part of the social structure, whether inside or outside the family" (1989, 103); there must be "congruence" between the principles governing public institutions and family life (1989, 21).
  4. Second, stability argument: Rawls needs citizens to develop a sense of justice, and children learn it in families; families lacking "equality and reciprocity", sites of "dependence and domination", will not teach it (1989, 99–100).
  5. Policy: the state should "encourage and facilitate the equal sharing by men and women of paid and unpaid work, or productive and reproductive labor" (1989, 171): subsidised daycare, parental leave, flextime (176, 186), autonomy-promoting education (177), and a legal entitlement for a homemaking spouse to half the earner's paycheck (181). She does not want the state to enforce 50-50 housework (171).
  - Objection (Lloyd 1995, 1998; Alstott 2004; McClain 2006): accept the policies but reject a state-promoted *ideal* of family life; Alstott: "The egalitarian family is, even in principle, a troubling ideal." Objection from multiculturalism (Shachar 2009; Khader 2011, 2018): it elevates one comprehensive conception of the good. [source: SEP-L §1.2, §1.3]
- Rawls's own view in *Political Liberalism* is "political", while Okin says hers is "in between" comprehensive and political liberalism (Okin 1999, 129). [source: SEP-L]
- **Martha Nussbaum** (born 6 May 1947, New York City; University of Chicago): *Sex and Social Justice* (1999), *Women and Human Development: The Capabilities Approach* (Cambridge UP; SEP-L cites 2000a, SEP-GL 2001 — 2000 is the usual date; use "2000"). Her liberal feminism centres "the idea of the citizen as a free and dignified human being, a maker of choices" (1999a, 46); "if one cares about people's powers to choose a conception of the good, then one must care about the rest of the form of life that supports those powers" (1999a, 45). Social arrangements are criticised if they leave people unable to function in central ways "regardless of their preferences" (adaptive preferences) (1999a, 50); she presents it as a basis for a "global feminism" (1999a, 40). [sources: SEP-L §1.1.3; WP Martha_Nussbaum] (Capabilities approach in general is in *Justice and Equality* — link.)
- Critics of "international feminists" Okin and Nussbaum (Ackerly and Attanasi 2009): too little attention to local mediation of injustice; privilege Western perspectives. [source: SEP-GL]
- **MacKinnon and the pornography debate**:
  - Catharine MacKinnon and Andrea Dworkin drafted an anti-pornography civil rights ordinance in 1983 for Minneapolis; Indianapolis passed a version in 1984. It sought **civil remedies** (suits for damages and injunctions), not criminal penalties. [source: SEP-PC §4.1; WP Antipornography_Civil_Rights_Ordinance]
  - Its definition: "We define pornography as the graphic sexually explicit subordination of women through pictures and words that also includes …" followed by eight clauses (i)–(viii) (MacKinnon 1987, 176). Pornography is defined functionally — what it *does* (subordinates) — and distinguished from "erotica" premised on equality. [source: SEP-PC §4.1]
  - Struck down: *American Booksellers Ass'n v. Hudnut*, 771 F.2d 323 (7th Cir., decided 27 Aug 1985, opinion by Judge Frank Easterbrook), aff'd mem., 475 U.S. 1001 (1986). [source: WP-H] **SEP-PC says it was "overturned on appeal by the U.S. Supreme Court" — inaccurate: the Seventh Circuit struck it down and the Supreme Court summarily affirmed.**
  - Cambridge, Mass. ballot initiative 1985 voted down 58%–42%; Bellingham, Washington, passed it 1988 (then challenged by ACLU). Canada's Supreme Court in *R. v. Butler* (1992) incorporated some of the equality approach into obscenity law. [source: WP Antipornography_Civil_Rights_Ordinance]
  - MacKinnon's silencing claims: pornography "strips and devastates women of credibility … We are stripped of authority and reduced and devalidated and silenced" (MacKinnon 1992, 483–484); Rae Langton (1993) and Hornsby & Langton (1998) develop "illocutionary disablement" — a woman's "no" may fail to secure uptake as refusal; critics include Jacobson (2001), Bird (2002), Antony (2011). [source: SEP-PC §4.1, §5.2]
  - Feminist opponents of the ordinance: the Feminist Anti-Censorship Taskforce (F.A.C.T.) amicus brief in *Hudnut* (Hunter and Law 1985) — worries about alliance with the evangelical right, discrimination against minority sexualities (e.g. lesbianism), women's freedom to produce/perform, reinforcing "sex is bad for women", and diverting resources; anti-censorship authors include Strossen (1995), Cornell (2000); sex-positive feminists (term from Ellen Willis's 1981 essay "Lust Horizons: Is the Women's Movement Pro-Sex?"); Gayle Rubin (1993). The broader late-1970s–1980s quarrel is called the "feminist sex wars". [sources: SEP-PC §4.2; WP Feminist_sex_wars]
  - Liberal objection (Ronald Dworkin 1993, 38): "no reputable study has concluded that pornography is a significant cause of sexual crime". [source: SEP-PC §5.1]
- **Radical feminism** per SEP-P: structures must be overhauled, not reformed; MacKinnon: "sexuality is the linchpin of gender inequality" (1989, 113); earlier radical feminists: separatism (Mary Daly), technologised procreation (Shulamith Firestone, *The Dialectic of Sex*, 1970). [source: SEP-P §2.2] (SEP-P's author calls biology-based radical feminism "generally recognized to be retrograde" — an evaluative judgment, don't adopt.)

## 8. Intersectionality
- **Kimberlé Williams Crenshaw** (born 5 May 1959, Canton, Ohio; UCLA School of Law from 1986, Columbia Law School from 1995; a founder of critical race theory; on Anita Hill's legal team 1991). [source: WP-KC]
- **1989**: "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex: A Black Feminist Critique of Antidiscrimination Doctrine, Feminist Theory and Antiracist Politics", *University of Chicago Legal Forum* 1989: 139–167 — coined "intersectionality". [sources: WP-KC; SEP bibliographies]
- The legal problem: Black women had to prove discrimination on grounds of race *or* sex, showing it extended to Black men or to white women; discrimination aimed at Black women as such slipped through. [source: SEP-M §1.2] Key case: *DeGraffenreid v. General Motors* (1976): five Black women challenged GM's seniority-based layoffs; the court treated race and sex separately — GM hired Black men (factory) and white women (offices), so neither claim succeeded. Crenshaw also discusses *Moore v. Hughes Helicopter* and *Payne v. Travenol*. [source: WP-I]
- The metaphor: "If an accident happens at an intersection, it can be caused by cars traveling from any number of directions and, sometimes, from all of them" (Crenshaw 1989, 149). Black women may suffer discrimination (a) on grounds of sex, (b) of race, (c) "the combined effects", or (d) a distinctive form "not the sum of race and sex discrimination, but [discrimination] as Black women" (149). "the intersectional experience is greater than the sum of racism and sexism" (1989, 140). [source: SEP-M §1.2]
- **1991**: "Mapping the Margins: Intersectionality, Identity Politics, and Violence against Women of Color", *Stanford Law Review* 43(6): 1241–1299 — extends the analysis to movement organising and advocacy (domestic violence, rape). [sources: SEP-M §1.2; SEP bibliographies; WP-KC]
- Crenshaw later describes intersectionality as "a heuristic term to focus attention on the vexed dynamics of difference and the solidarities of sameness in the context of antidiscrimination and social movement politics" (Cho, Crenshaw and McCall 2013, 787); Ann Garry: a "framework checker" (2011, 830). [source: SEP-M §1.2]
- Four claims intersectionality may support (SEP-M, Jenkins): oppression is **non-additive**; **non-separable**; (stronger) race and gender kinds are **mutually constitutive** (Lugones 2007, Garry 2011…); (strongest) intersectional kinds have **greater explanatory power** — a claim Crenshaw (2010) rejects in its strongest form. [source: SEP-M §1.2]
- **Combahee River Collective**: Black feminist lesbian socialist organisation, Boston, 1974–1980; split from the National Black Feminist Organization; named (at Barbara Smith's suggestion) after Harriet Tubman's raid on the Combahee River, 2 June 1863. The Statement (1977) was drafted by Barbara Smith, Beverly Smith and Demita Frazier; four parts (Genesis of Contemporary Black Feminism; What We Believe; Problems in Organizing Black Feminists; Black Feminist Issues and Projects). Credited with the first use of "identity politics" (Gerald Izenberg) and with "interlocking" oppressions. [source: WP-CRC] (SEP-ET cites "CRC 1977"; SEP bibliographies cite reprints of 1981/1982.)
- Quotations from the Statement: "the major systems of oppression are interlocking. The synthesis of these oppressions creates the conditions of our lives."; "We also often find it difficult to separate race from class from sex oppression because in our lives they are most often experienced simultaneously"; "if Black women were free, it would mean that everyone else would have to be free since our freedom would necessitate the destruction of all the systems of oppression." [source: WP-CRC]
- Other antecedents named by SEP: Frances Beal ("double jeopardy"), Deborah King's "multiple jeopardy" (1988), Audre Lorde (1984), Angela Davis (1981), bell hooks's *Ain't I a Woman?* (1981), Hull, Scott and Smith (eds.) *All the Women Are White, All the Blacks Are Men, But Some of Us Are Brave* (1982), Moraga and Anzaldúa, *This Bridge Called My Back* (1981); Kathryn Gines on 19th-century Black women; Mariana Ortega on Latina feminism. Anna Julia Cooper, Maria W. Stewart, Ida B. Wells; Pauli Murray's "Jane Crow" (1947). [sources: SEP-M §1.2; SEP-ET; SEP-P §1; WP-I]
- **Critiques (attribute)**: Sirma Bilge — the concept has been "commodified and colonized for neoliberal regimes" (2013, 407) [SEP-M]; Jennifer Nash (flattening; institutional superficiality); Tommy J. Curry (intersectional feminism imports stereotypes of Black men and erases Black male victims); Lisa Downing (too much focus on group identity); conservatives' "hierarchy of victimhood" (Ben Shapiro); Collins: needs to be a critical social theory or it is diluted; Brittney Cooper: it "does not deserve our religious devotion" but remains "one of the most useful and expansive paradigms we have"; methodological worries about testability (Bright, Malinsky and Thompson propose causal-graph models). Lawford-Smith rejects intersectional feminism (above). [sources: WP-I "Responses"; SEP-M; SEP-T]

### Sojourner Truth and the Gage text (handle carefully)
- Sojourner Truth, born Isabella (Bomefree/Baumfree), c. 1797 (she estimated 1797–1800), Ulster County, New York; died 26 November 1883; spoke only Dutch until about nine; free by 1827 (New York's emancipation completed 4 July 1827); took the name Sojourner Truth in 1843. [sources: WP Sojourner_Truth; WP-AIW]
- She spoke on **29 May 1851** at the Woman's Rights Convention in Akron, Ohio. Brief reports: *New York Tribune* 6 June 1851, *The Liberator* five days later; first full transcript by **Marius Robinson** (convention secretary, who went over it with her) in the *Anti-Slavery Bugle*, **21 June 1851** — **the question "Ain't I a woman?" does not appear in it.** [source: WP-AIW]
- **Frances Dana Barker Gage** published a very different version in **May 1863**, twelve years later, in a Southern dialect Truth (a New Yorker) did not speak, with additions: bearing the lash, no one helping her into carriages, and having borne "thirteen chilern" mostly sold into slavery (Truth is believed to have had five children, one sold). Gage's 1863 account also contradicts her own 1851 report about a hostile audience; other eyewitnesses describe a friendly session. Gage's version was reprinted 1875, 1881 (*History of Woman Suffrage*), 1889 and became standard; Miriam Schneir's 1972 anthology reprinted it without dialect. [source: WP-AIW]
- Historian **Nell Irvin Painter**'s biography *Sojourner Truth: A Life, a Symbol* (1996) and later the Sojourner Truth Project concluded Gage's version was very inaccurate and Robinson's the most accurate extant text. Painter: "At a time when most Americans thought of slaves as male and women as white, Truth embodied a fact that still bears repeating: Among the blacks are women; among the women, there are blacks." [sources: WP-AIW; WP Sojourner_Truth]
- Robinson's text (verified wording from WP-AIW): "I am a woman's rights. I have as much muscle as any man, and can do as much work as any man. I have plowed and reaped and husked and chopped and mowed, and can any man do more than that? … As for intellect, all I can say is, if a woman have a pint, and a man a quart – why can't she have her little pint full?" … "But man is in a tight place, the poor slave is on him, woman is coming on him, he is surely between a hawk and a buzzard." [source: WP-AIW]
- The motto "Am I not a woman and a sister?" was already used by abolitionists (the female version from the 1820s; image in *Genius of Universal Emancipation*, 1830); Maria W. Stewart used it in 1833. [source: WP-AIW]
- SEP-M still lists "Sojourner Truth's famous 'Ain't I a Woman?' speech" as an antecedent without noting the textual problem. **Writer: present the speech as an antecedent, but say the famous refrain comes from Gage's 1863 reconstruction.**

## 9. Critiques of the canon
- SEP-FH (Witt and Shapiro) identifies two problems: "the problem of historical exclusion" and the canon's negative characterisation of women and the feminine; three responses: criticism of the canon as misogynist, revision (recovery of women), and appropriation. [source: SEP-FH intro]
- Three levels of criticism: (1) explicit misogyny; (2) gendered concepts; (3) synoptic interpretations. [source: SEP-FH §1]
- **Explicit misogyny**: Cynthia Freeland's catalogue of Aristotle (1994, 145–46): a man's courage lies in commanding, a woman's in obeying; "matter yearns for form, as the female for the male and the ugly for the beautiful"; women have fewer teeth; a female is "as it were, a deformity"; "a woman is perhaps an inferior being". Similar critiques of Plato (Tuana; stressing *Timaeus* and *Laws* over the *Republic*) and Kant (Schott). [source: SEP-FH §1.1] Hegel §166 epigraph (already in early-modern chapter).
- **Gendered concepts**: Aristotle's hylomorphism links form with male, matter with female (Witt 1998); Lynda Lange, "Woman Is Not a Rational Animal" (1983); Spelman (1983); **Okin 1979, ch. 4**: Aristotle's functionalism was devised to legitimate the Athenian status quo, including slavery and women's inequality. Counter-reading: Sophia Connell (2016) — Aristotle's reproductive theory not intrinsically sexist. [source: SEP-FH §1.2]
- **Synoptic**: **Genevieve Lloyd, *The Man of Reason: "Male" and "Female" in Western Philosophy*** (Methuen 1984; first as a paper in *Metaphilosophy* 10, 1979): reason has been gendered male "from Aristotle to Hume, from Plato to Sartre"; for Lloyd the maleness is "symbolic and metaphorical rather than cultural or psychological". Susan Bordo, *The Flight to Objectivity* (Descartes as source of masculinised objectivity). Luce Irigaray reads for what is repressed, using parody. [sources: SEP-FH §1.3; WP Genevieve_Lloyd (b. 16 Oct 1941, Cootamundra NSW)]
- **Witt's argument (good short step-by-step)** (SEP-FH §1.3, Witt 1993): Even if reason has been gendered male, rejection does not follow. If the maleness is *extrinsic*, its history gives no reason to reject the concept. If *intrinsic*, rejection still follows only if reason ought to be otherwise — and arguing that reason would be better gender-neutral or inclusive is *reconceiving* reason, not rejecting it. [source: SEP-FH §1.3]
- **Revision**: Mary Ellen Waithe (1989, xxxviii): "These women are not women on the fringes of philosophy, but philosophers on the fringes of history." [source: SEP-FH §2] Historiographical questions: how political agendas shaped canonical histories in Germany and France (Ebbersmeyer 2020; Antoine-Mahut 2020); openness to genres (spiritual writing, polemics, poetry, pamphlets); how women's exclusion intersects with the exclusion of Africana philosophers and non-European traditions. SEP-FH notes recent work on womanhood in Chinese philosophy (Robin Wang 2010), seventeenth-century Korean Confucian women writers (Ivanhoe and H. Y. Wang 2021, 2023), and Waithe and Boos Dykeman (2023) on non-European women philosophers. [source: SEP-FH intro, §2.4] (Project Vox, O'Neill, Shapiro: link to early-modern chapter.)
- **Appropriation**: Nussbaum's *The Fragility of Goodness* (1986) on Aristotelian ethics; Marcia Homiak (1993) on Aristotle's rational ideal; Annette Baier on Hume (1987, 1993); Charlene Seigfried, *Pragmatism and Feminism* (1996). "It is likely that every philosopher, from Plato to Nietzsche, who has been subject to feminist criticism, also appears in some feminist's good books." [source: SEP-FH §3]

## 10. Feminisms beyond the Euro-American mainstream (evenhanded)
- General critique: Chandra Talpade Mohanty, "Under Western Eyes: Feminist Scholarship and Colonial Discourses" (*boundary 2* 12(3)/13(1), 1984; rev. *Feminist Review* 30, 1988): Western feminist scholarship constructs "the average Third World woman" as leading "an essentially truncated life based on her feminine gender (read: sexually constrained) and her being 'Third World' (read: ignorant, poor, uneducated, tradition-bound, domestic, family-oriented, victimized, etc.)" (Mohanty 2003, 22). Uma Narayan (1997) on sati: Western critics (e.g. Mary Daly) treat it as an isolated local practice, whereas it gained its symbolic power under British rule. [sources: SEP-GL; SEP-CG bibliography] Serene Khader, *Decolonizing Universalism: A Transnational Feminist Ethic* (OUP 2018/2019 — SEP entries differ). [source: SEP-ET, SEP-L, SEP-GL bibliographies]
- **China — He-Yin Zhen** (何殷震, c. 1884 – c. 1920; born He Ban, Yizheng, Jiangsu; married Liu Shipei 1903; signed with her mother's surname too). In Tokyo from 1907, contributed to the anarchist journal *Tianyi bao* (*Natural Justice*, 1907–08); founded the Women's Rights Recovery Association (*Nüzi fuquan hui*). Essays incl. "On the Question of Women's Liberation" (1907) and "What Women Should Know About Communism". She treated gender and class as inseparable, analysed women's oppression through labour (including unwaged reproductive labour), rejected suffrage and parliamentary politics (citing Norway: elected women would be wealthy), and called for communal child-rearing; unlike Liang Qichao, she made women's liberation an end, not a means to national revival. Many of her articles were misattributed to Liu. Some scholars (Deng) caution that her anarcho-feminism belonged to a brief 1907–08 period and that she and Liu later collaborated with the Qing. Late life uncertain (rumoured to have become a nun). Key English edition: Lydia H. Liu, Rebecca E. Karl and Dorothy Ko (eds.), *The Birth of Chinese Feminism: Essential Texts in Transnational Theory* (Columbia UP, 2013). [source: WP-HYZ]
- **Confucian gender (SEP-CG, Rosenlee)**: no Chinese conceptual equivalent of "gender"; man/woman are kinship- and ritual-dependent, organised by *nei* 內 (inside) / *wai* 外 (outside); Ban Zhao (c. 45–117 CE), author of *Nüjie* (*Lessons for Women*), as a woman who crossed the *nei–wai* boundary; footbinding is "unconnected to Confucian philosophy textually" and its exoticisation is called "orientalism"; Rosenlee herself proposes a hybrid "Confucian feminism"; from the mid-1990s comparisons of care ethics and *ren* 仁. [source: SEP-CG] (Note: Rosenlee is advocating; attribute.)
- **India — Tarabai Shinde** (1850–1910), Buldhana (Berar), associate of Jotirao and Savitribai Phule and member of the Satyashodhak Samaj; ***Stri Purush Tulana*** ("A Comparison Between Women and Men"), Marathi, **1882**, written in response to an 1881 *Pune Vaibhav* article on Vijayalakshmi, a young Brahmin widow in Surat sentenced to hang (commuted to transportation for life) for killing her illegitimate son. It attacked caste and patriarchy and challenged Hindu scriptures as a source of women's oppression; "often considered the first modern Indian feminist text"; Tharu and Lalita: "probably the first full fledged and extant feminist argument after the poetry of the Bhakti period". English: trans. Maya Pandit in Tharu & Lalita (eds.), *Women Writing in India*, vol. I; Rosalind O'Hanlon, *A Comparison Between Women and Men: Tarabai Shinde and the Critique of Gender Relations in Colonial India* (OUP Delhi, 2000). [source: WP Tarabai_Shinde]
- **Pandita Ramabai** (23 April 1858 – 5 April 1922) (already in *Age of Empire*): *Stri Dharma Niti* (1882); told the Hunter Commission (1882): "In ninety-nine cases out of a hundred the educated men of this country are opposed to female education and the proper position of women"; converted to Christianity in England (1883); *The High-Caste Hindu Woman* (1887, in the US); Sharada Sadan for child widows in Pune (1889), later the Mukti Mission at Kedgaon; attacked by Tilak's *Kesari*. [source: WP Pandita_Ramabai]
- **Islamic feminism**: **Fatema Mernissi** (27 Sep 1940, Fez – 30 Nov 2015): *Beyond the Veil* (1975); *Le harem politique* (1987), English *The Veil and the Male Elite: A Feminist Interpretation of Women's Rights in Islam* (trans. Mary Jo Lakeland, 1991) — questions the reliability of misogynist *hadith* via the history of transmission. **Amina Wadud** (born 1952 as Mary Teasley, Bethesda; converted 1972): *Qur'an and Woman: Rereading the Sacred Text from a Woman's Perspective* (Kuala Lumpur 1992; OUP 1999), *Inside the Gender Jihad* (2006); led a mixed-gender Friday prayer in New York in 2005, causing controversy. **Leila Ahmed** (born 29 May 1940, Cairo; Harvard Divinity School from 1999): *Women and Gender in Islam* (1992) — oppressive practices stem from patriarchal interpretations rather than Islam itself; coined/analysed "colonial feminism", a Western discourse holding "that progress for women could be achieved only through abandoning the native culture". [sources: WP Fatema_Mernissi; WP Amina_Wadud; WP Leila_Ahmed] A contrasting critique of liberal feminist assumptions: Saba Mahmood, *Politics of Piety: The Islamic Revival and the Feminist Subject* (Princeton 2005) — SEP-P groups her with those stressing multiplicity against a universal feminist subject (Okin, Nussbaum, Ackerly). [source: SEP-P §1 and bibliography] (No SEP entry treats Islamic feminism in depth; the SEP feminist-religion entry was checked.)
- **Africa — Oyèrónkẹ́ Oyěwùmí** (born 10 Nov 1957, Ogbomoso; Ibadan BA, Berkeley PhD; Stony Brook): *The Invention of Women: Making an African Sense of Western Gender Discourses* (University of Minnesota Press, 1997); ASA Sex and Gender Section Distinguished Book Award 1998; African Studies Association Distinguished Africanist Award 2021. Thesis: in Oyo-Yoruba society social organisation rested on **seniority** (relative age), not gender; Yoruba lacks gender-specific terms for son/daughter, brother/sister and many roles; Western "body-reasoning" (biological determinism) and the binary were imposed through colonialism. Her own gloss: "my work is the ultimate evidence about the fact that gender is indeed socially constructed." [source: WP-O]
- **Critics** (attribute): **Bibi Bakare-Yusuf** (2003, *African Identities* 1: 119–140): relies on linguistic determinism — absence of gendered pronouns does not prove absence of gender; mistranslation cuts both ways; **Nwando Achebe**: contrasts with her own findings and Ifi Amadiume's; **Sefinatu Aliyu Dogo** (2014): one ethnic group cannot ground claims about precolonial Africa, and Yoruba patriarchal features are under-addressed; Carole Boyce Davies also critical; Olalade Balogun broadly sympathetic but notes non-rigid categories did not mean equality. [source: WP-O] Nkiru Nzegwu's Igbo "dual-sex system" (link to African chapter).
- **Latin America — María Lugones** (26 Jan 1944 – 14 July 2020; *Pilgrimages/Peregrinajes*, 2003): "Heterosexualism and the Colonial/Modern Gender System", *Hypatia* 22(1) (2007): 186–219; "Coloniality of Gender" (2008). Building on Aníbal Quijano's "coloniality of power", she argues Quijano took for granted female inferiority, sexual dimorphism and heterosexualism; these are features of the Eurocentric system; the "colonial/modern gender system" has a "light side" (dimorphism, heterosexualism, female inferiority for colonisers) and a "dark side" for the colonised; later (2010) the colonised were treated as *ungendered* — "sex was made to stand alone" — and so as not fully human; she rejects "gender universality" and is critical of queer and trans theory for presuming it (2020). SEP-LA: she "was the first scholar to articulate the concept of the coloniality of gender". [sources: SEP-T §8; SEP-LA; WP Maria_Lugones]
- **Critics of Lugones** (attribute): Brooklyn Leo (2020) — presumes "cisness", erases Two-Spirit people, wrongly denies resistance within the system (citing C. Riley Snorton 2017); Bettcher (2025, *Beyond Personhood*) situates "reality enforcement" within the colonial/modern gender system. [source: SEP-T §8] (Lugones relied partly on Oyěwùmí's Yoruba thesis — I did not verify this in a source read this session; see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Lugones's earlier "world"-travelling (1987, "Playfulness, 'World'-Travelling, and Loving Perception") and Lugones & Spelman, "Have We Got a Theory for You!…" (SEP-E dates it 1983; SEP-P says 1986 — the paper is usually dated 1983, *Women's Studies International Forum*; use 1983 with caution). [sources: SEP-E; SEP-P]

## Debates
- **Did Beauvoir introduce the sex/gender distinction?** Butler (1986) reads "one becomes a woman" as distinguishing sex and gender; Moi and Bauer dispute this; SEP-B leaves it "a matter of feminist debate"; SEP-G treats it as one possible (socialisation) reading. [SEP-B §7; WP-SS; SEP-G §2.1]
- **Translation of "femme"**: Parshley's "a woman" vs Borde & Malovany-Chevallier's "woman" (construct/institution); reviewers split (Moi critical of both). [WS-1, WS-2; WP-SS]
- **Is sex itself socially constructed?** Butler (sex "always already gender") vs Stone (claims about sex imply gender norms, but sex ≠ gender) vs Ásta (sex is a conferred status that tracks real features) vs Byrne/Stock (sex is a biological category, and "woman" means adult human female). [SEP-G; SEP-T]
- **What is a woman?** Gender realism vs nominalism; Haslanger (subordination; eliminativist) vs Witt (uniessentialism) vs Alcoff (positionality) vs Mikkola (no definition needed) vs Bettcher (multiple meanings; self-identification) vs Jenkins (class + identity) vs Byrne/Stock (adult human female); non-binary kinds (Dembroff). [SEP-G; SEP-T]
- **Trans inclusion and gender-critical feminism**: Stock and Lawford-Smith (sex-based categories and spaces) vs Bettcher, Dembroff, Jenkins, Finlayson et al. (inclusion; empirical evidence on facilities). The SEP coverage is written from the inclusive side; present both sides in their own terms. [SEP-T §7]
- **Standpoint theory**: automatic privilege vs achieved standpoint; relativism and the bias paradox vs Harding's strong objectivity; Wylie's contingent-advantage reformulation; postmodernist and intersectional criticism of a single women's standpoint. [IEP-ST; SEP-E]
- **Liberal vs radical feminism on pornography**: MacKinnon/Dworkin (pornography subordinates and silences; civil remedy) vs liberal defenders (R. Dworkin: no reliable causal evidence; free speech) vs anti-censorship and sex-positive feminists (F.A.C.T.). [SEP-PC; WP sex wars]
- **Okin's egalitarian family**: should the state promote an egalitarian family ideal (Okin) or only remove injustices and protect choices (Lloyd, Alstott)? Is it culturally parochial (Shachar, Khader)? [SEP-L]
- **Universalism vs cultural critique**: Okin/Nussbaum ("international feminists") vs Mohanty, Narayan, Mahmood, Khader. [SEP-P; SEP-GL]
- **Intersectionality**: a heuristic (Crenshaw, Garry) or a substantive metaphysical thesis (mutual constitution; Bernstein's explanatory-priority claim, rejected in strongest form by Crenshaw); political critiques (Bilge, Nash, Curry, Lawford-Smith). [SEP-M; WP-I]
- **Gendered reason**: Lloyd/Bordo/Irigaray vs Atherton and Witt (gendered history does not entail rejecting reason). [SEP-FH]
- **Was gender colonial?** Oyěwùmí and Lugones vs Bakare-Yusuf, Achebe, Dogo, Leo. [WP-O; SEP-T §8]
- **He-Yin Zhen's anarcho-feminism**: a sustained position or a brief Tokyo episode (Deng)? [WP-HYZ]
