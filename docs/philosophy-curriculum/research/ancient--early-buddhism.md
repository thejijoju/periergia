## 1. Dates: the long and short chronologies
- No complete agreement exists among scholars or Buddhist traditions on the Buddha's dates. [source: IEP-Bud §1a]
- Tradition: he lived to 80. SEP: "Up until recently his dates were thought to be approximately 560–480 BCE, but many scholars now hold that he must have died around 405 BCE." SEP's lead gives "fl. circa 450 BCE". [source: SEP-Bud §1]
- The most common dates among Buddhists are those of the Theravāda school, 623–543 BCE (IEP). WP gives the Sri Lankan long-chronology dates as 624–544 BCE, "the accepted dates in Sri Lanka and South-East Asia". Small difference between the two sources; say "c. 624/623–544/543 BCE in Theravāda tradition". [source: IEP-Bud §1a; WP-The_Buddha]
- **Long chronology** (Sri Lankan chronicles): the Buddha died 218 years before Aśoka's coronation. With Aśoka crowned in 326 BCE (the chronicles' date) this gives 624–544 BCE; most scholars who accept the long chronology but date the coronation c. 268 BCE (Greek evidence) get 566–486 BCE. [source: WP-The_Buddha, citing Reynolds & Hallisey 2005 p. 1061]
- **Short chronology** (Indian sources and their Chinese and Tibetan translations): death 100 years before Aśoka's coronation, giving 448–368 BCE. [source: WP-The_Buddha, citing Reynolds & Hallisey 2005]
- From the mid-19th to the late 20th century Western scholars used c. 560–480 BCE (IEP); early-20th-century historians commonly used 563–483 BCE (WP). [source: IEP-Bud §1a; WP-The_Buddha]
- The 1988 Göttingen symposium on the date of the Buddha (proceedings published 1991–92, ed. Heinz Bechert) ended the old consensus. Most current scholars place his life about a century later, in the fifth century BCE. At the symposium the majority of those presenting put his death within 20 years either side of 400 BCE. [source: IEP-Bud §1a; WP-The_Buddha]
- Proposed death dates (IEP's list): Gombrich c. 404; K. R. Norman 410–390; Hikata c. 400; Sarao c. 397; Bechert c. 400–350; Nakamura 383; Hirakawa 368; Bareau 420–380 BCE. WP gives Gombrich as 405, Dundas 411–400 ("as is now almost universally accepted by informed Indological scholarship"), Norman a lifespan c. 480–400, Witzel 460–380. NOTE: IEP says Gombrich c. 404, WP and SEP's figure say 405. Use "c. 405 BCE (Gombrich)". [source: IEP-Bud §1a; WP-The_Buddha]
- The new dates "have not been accepted by all historians" (WP, citing Schumann 2003, Wayman 1997). [source: WP-The_Buddha]
- **Safe wording for the chapter:** traditional Theravāda dates c. 624–544 BCE; older Western scholarly dates c. 563/560–483/480 BCE; most scholars now place his death c. 410–370 BCE, often c. 400 or 405 BCE; he lived 80 years by tradition.
- Archaeology: a timber structure under the Māyādevī temple at Lumbinī was carbon-dated to possibly the sixth century BCE (Coningham, 2013). Archaeologists caution it may be pre-Buddhist tree worship; Gombrich dismissed the speculation as "a fantasy". Use, if at all, only as a disputed find. [source: WP-The_Buddha]

## 2. Life and legend
- Names: Gotama (Pāli)/Gautama (Skt.) was the family name; he is also called Śākyamuni, "Sage of the Śākyas". "Buddha" means "awakened". It is a title, conferred on one who discovers the path to nirvana and teaches it. Buddhist tradition holds there have been other buddhas; SEP takes an agnostic stance. [source: SEP-Bud §1; WP-The_Buddha; IEP-Bud §1c]
- He belonged to the Śākya clan, in the area of the present India–Nepal border. Tradition: born at Lumbinī (now in Nepal) while his mother was travelling to her parents' home; raised in Kapilavastu; died at Kuśinagara (Kushinagar). [source: SEP-Bud §1; IEP-Bud §1c; WP-The_Buddha]
- **Father as "king" is likely a later embellishment.** Many scholars think Śuddhodana was a Śākya aristocrat (*khattiya*) and the Śākya polity not a hereditary monarchy. Walshe: his father was "an elected chief of the clan rather than the king he was later made out to be". [source: WP-The_Buddha (citing Gethin 1998, Gombrich 1988, Walshe 1995)]
- Mother Māyā; according to the stories she died a week after the birth. Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī, her sister, raised him (see §13). [source: IEP-Bud §1c; WP-Mahāprajāpatī_Gautamī]
- **Sources on the life.** The early disciples were more interested in preserving the teachings than the life. The first four Nikāyas contain only fragments of biography, the most important being the Mahāpadāna, Ariyapariyesanā, Mahāsaccaka and Mahāparinibbāna suttas. The first complete biographies came centuries later: in Pāli the *Nidānakathā* (introduction to the Jātaka); in Sanskrit Aśvaghoṣa's *Buddhacarita* (2nd century CE) and the *Mahāvastu* and *Lalitavistara* (both 1st century CE, per IEP). [source: IEP-Bud §1c]
- **Legend vs early texts (a good example):** later traditions say he left his wife Yaśodharā immediately after the birth of their son Rāhula; the Pāli Nikāyas do not mention this story and refer to Rāhula only as a young monk. [source: IEP-Bud §1c]
- The Mahāpadāna sutta presents all buddhas' lives as following one pattern (descent from Tuṣita heaven, miraculous conception and birth, seven steps, mother dying a week after the birth). IEP calls the narratives "perhaps better understood as hagiographies rather than as biographies". [source: IEP-Bud §1c–d]
- Story (as summarised by SEP and IEP): in early adulthood he left his comfortable life (and wife and son) to seek an end to suffering; trained with the meditation teachers Ālāra Kālāma and Uddaka Rāmaputta, attaining the "base of nothingness" and "base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception", and found these did not lead to nirvana; then practised breath-control and fasting until his health failed; rejected extreme asceticism, resumed eating, and took the "middle path" between sensual indulgence and self-mortification; was awakened under a *bodhi* tree. He first inclined not to teach, and taught after the god Brahmā Sahampati asked him. He then taught for 45 years. IEP gives six years of seeking and 29 years in Kapilavastu before leaving. [source: SEP-Bud §1; IEP-Bud §1c]
- First discourse: by tradition the *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta* ("Setting in Motion the Wheel of the Dhamma", SN 56.11), given in the deer park at Isipatana (Sarnath) to five ascetics, seven weeks after the awakening. Its topics: the two extremes, the middle way, the four noble truths, the eightfold path; Koṇḍañña is the first to understand. Cousins: many scholars think "this discourse was identified as the first sermon of the Buddha only at a later date". [source: WP-Dhammacakkappavattana_Sutta; WP-Four_Noble_Truths]
- The Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta (SN 22.59; PTS S III 66) is traditionally his **second** discourse, to the same group of five. It has parallels in the Chinese *Saṃyukta Āgama* (SĀ 34) and in Gāndhārī. [source: WP-Anattalakkhaṇa_Sutta]
- Death: the Mahāparinibbāna sutta tells how, after a meal with Cunda, he fell ill and died at Kuśinagara; the body was cremated and the relics distributed among north Indian groups and placed in stūpas. Von Hinüber argues this sutta retains archaic material from close to the Buddha's lifetime. [source: WP-The_Buddha]
- **Earliest inscriptional evidence:** no written records about him survive from his lifetime or the one or two centuries after. The oldest are Aśoka's edicts (mid-3rd century BCE); the Lumbinī pillar inscription (c. 250 BCE) marks the spot where "the Buddha, sage of the Śākyas (Śākyamuni), was born". [source: WP-The_Buddha, citing Gethin 1998 p. 19]
- Brahmā and the gods: in the Nikāyas the gods are real but unenlightened, mortal beings; the universe is not created by an all-powerful god (M I 326–7). IEP: it is "inaccurate to define early Buddhism as atheistic or as non-theistic", because the Buddha denies the gods' cosmological and soteriological role, not their existence. [source: IEP-Bud §3a]

## 3. The śramaṇa context
- The Buddha can be classified as a *śramaṇa*, a wandering ascetic. Their existence shows dissatisfaction with the Vedic rituals and sacrifices of the Gangetic basin. Many, including the Buddha, rejected the authority of the Vedas (hence "heterodox"). [source: SEP-Bud §1]
- Ideas shared by the Upaniṣads and the heterodox śramaṇas whose doctrines are known (SEP): rebirth of all sentient beings; rebirth governed by karma; continual rebirth is unsatisfactory; an ideal state of liberation from rebirth; reaching it requires overcoming ignorance about one's true identity. The Buddha's distinctive claim is what that ignorance consists in: "the conceit that there is an 'I' and a 'mine'". [source: SEP-Bud §1]
- "Śramaṇa" probably means "those who make an effort" (IEP); WP: from the root *śram*, "to exert effort, labor or to perform austerity". [source: IEP-Bud §2a; WP-Śramaṇa]
- The Sāmaññaphala Sutta (Dīgha Nikāya) names six rival teachers: Pūraṇa Kassapa, Makkhali Gosāla (Ājīvika fatalism), Ajita Kesakambalī (materialism, no afterlife or karma), Pakudha Kaccāyana, Sañjaya Belaṭṭhiputta (scepticism) and Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta (Mahāvīra, Jain). Padmanabh Jaini warns this may be "a biased picture". (Next chapter, "Jains, Ājīvikas and Materialists", covers them.) [source: WP-Śramaṇa]
- IEP frames the Buddha's epistemology as a middle way between Brahmin dogmatism and śramaṇa scepticism. The sceptics' "eel-wriggling" (*amarāvikkhepa*): "I don't say it is like this. And I don't say it is like that…" (M I 521, as quoted in IEP; translator not named). [source: IEP-Bud §2a]
- The Buddha's two chief disciples, Sāriputta and Mahāmoggallāna, had been students of the sceptic Sañjaya. [source: WP-The_Buddha]
- Public debate was common and conferred prestige; defeat implied conversion. The Buddha discourages attachment to doctrines, including his own (simile of the raft, M I 135). [source: IEP-Bud §2b]
- Karma redefined: against the Brahmanical view of karma as ritual action and the Jain view that even involuntary acts produce karma, the Buddha defines karma as volition, so only voluntary actions produce it. [source: IEP-Bud §3a]

## 4. The sources: Nikāyas, Āgamas, oral transmission, councils, writing
- The teachings are preserved in the Pāli Nikāyas and the Āgamas (mostly in Chinese translation). [source: SEP-Bud lead]
- The Buddha wrote nothing. The teachings were transmitted orally "for at least three centuries" (IEP). SEP: probably rehearsed shortly after his death and preserved orally until written down. [source: IEP-Bud §1b; SEP-Bud §1]
- SEP's three problems with the sources: (1) written down several centuries after his death; (2) in languages (Pāli; Chinese translations of Sanskrit and other Indic originals) other than the one he probably spoke; (3) they disagree, suggesting insertions and deletions during sectarian disputes. SEP thinks (1) and (2) are manageable, (3) "troubling". [source: SEP-Bud §1]
- The Pāli canon is the *Tipiṭaka*, "three baskets": Sutta (discourses), Vinaya (monastic discipline) and Abhidhamma. The name refers to the receptacles for palm-leaf manuscripts. [source: WP-Pali_Canon]
- The Sutta Piṭaka has five Nikāyas: Dīgha (long), Majjhima (middle-length), Saṃyutta (connected/grouped), Aṅguttara (numerical) and Khuddaka (minor; includes Dhammapada, Udāna, Itivuttaka, Sutta Nipāta, Therīgāthā). [source: IEP-Bud §1b; WP-Therīgāthā]
- The Chinese Āgamas parallel the first four Nikāyas: Dīrgha, Madhyama, Saṃyukta and Ekottarika Āgamas. They come from schools such as the Sarvāstivāda, Mūlasarvāstivāda, Dharmaguptaka and Mahāsāṃghika. Some schools had a fifth, Kṣudraka Āgama. [source: WP-Āgama_(Buddhism)]
- The Pāli Sutta Piṭaka is "strikingly similar" to the Chinese Āgamas, which suggests much of it belongs to "a common textual tradition probably prior to the existence of Buddhist schools". Comparing the two helps to infer the Buddha's position. [source: IEP-Bud §1b]
- Pāli is a Middle Indo-Aryan language close to the Prakrits of 3rd-century BCE north India, including Magadha, where the Buddha spent most of his teaching years. The Pāli canon is "the only complete canon of early Indian Buddhism", of the only surviving early school, the Theravāda. [source: IEP-Bud §1b]
- **First council (tradition):** shortly after the Buddha's death (WP: three months after), 500 disciples met at Rājagṛha, presided over by Mahākāśyapa. Ānanda recited the Sūtra Piṭaka (hence suttas begin "Thus have I heard"); Upāli recited the Vinaya. The Abhidharma was not recited; Theravāda tradition says the Buddha taught it in the heaven where his mother lived. [source: IEP-Bud §1b; WP-Buddhist_councils; WP-Pali_Canon]
- **Historicity:** IEP calls the account "questionable"; "neither the Sūtra Piṭaka nor the Vinaya Piṭaka… could have been recited at once by one person". Many scholars since the late 19th century think the First Council improbable; Bareau and Oldenberg thought its account was modelled on the Second. [source: IEP-Bud §1b; WP-Buddhist_councils]
- **Second council:** at Vaiśālī, about 100 years after the Buddha's death, over monastic discipline (the "ten points", e.g. accepting money). The first schism, between Sthaviras ("Elders") and Mahāsāṃghikas ("Great Assembly"), is variously traced to this dispute or to Mahādeva's "five points" about the fallibility of arhats. SEP-Abh: "A generally accepted tradition" puts the split around the beginning of the 3rd century BCE. [source: WP-Buddhist_councils; SEP-Abh §1]
- **Third council (Theravāda sources only):** under Aśoka at Pāṭaliputra, led by Moggaliputta Tissa; the Kathāvatthu was added to the canon. Snellgrove calls the Theravāda third-council and Sarvāstivāda fourth-council accounts "equally tendentious". [source: WP-Buddhist_councils]
- **Writing down:** according to the Sri Lankan chronicles (Dīpavaṃsa, Mahāvaṃsa) the Pāli canon was written down in the reign of King Vaṭṭagāmaṇi (Valagamba), 1st century BCE, at the Alu Vihāra (Aluvihare) in Sri Lanka, because the oral tradition was at risk. WP gives 29 BCE; K. R. Norman notes a discrepancy between sources dating Valagamba's death to 77 BCE and his reign to 29–17 BCE. **Say "the 1st century BCE".** IEP is cautious: "It might be the case that a large collection of Buddhist texts was written down for the first time in Sri Lanka during the first century B.C.E." [source: WP-Pali_Canon; WP-Buddhist_councils; IEP-Bud §1b]
- Oral recitation continued alongside written texts for centuries (the *bhāṇaka* reciters). [source: WP-Pali_Canon]
- Layers: the Sūtra Piṭaka is older than the Vinaya Piṭaka; the Abhidharma Piṭaka is scholastic and originated "at least two centuries after the other two" (IEP). WP: Abhidhamma composition likely began c. 300 BCE. Even within the Sūtra Piṭaka older and later texts can be detected. [source: IEP-Bud §1b; WP-Pali_Canon]
- Lists (four truths, five aggregates, eightfold path, twelve links) are mnemonic devices, partly explained by centuries of oral composition and preservation. The Saṃyutta/Saṃyukta collection groups teachings by these topics. [source: SEP-Abh §1.1]
- Scholarly positions on authenticity: Gombrich and Warder stress "the fundamental homogeneity and substantial authenticity" of much Nikāya material (Gombrich: "I have the greatest difficulty in accepting that the main edifice is not the work of a single genius"); others (e.g. Ronald Davidson) doubt that much goes back to the Buddha. SEP names Gombrich 2009 and Shulman 2014 as more sanguine. [source: WP-The_Buddha; WP-Pali_Canon; SEP-Bud §1]
- Buddhaghosa (fl. 4th–5th century CE) compiled the commentaries that give the traditional Theravāda interpretation. [source: WP-Pali_Canon]

## 5. The four noble truths
- SEP's formulation: (1) There is suffering. (2) There is the origination of suffering. (3) There is the cessation of suffering. (4) There is a path to the cessation of suffering. Truth (1) is not the claim that every state is experienced as unsatisfactory; realising that all is impermanent undermines a precondition for real enjoyment. [source: SEP-Bud §2]
- Logical structure (SEP): (2) says suffering has causes and conditions; (3) follows: if suffering depends on causes, removing the causes prevents it; (4) specifies techniques that remove them. "Much then hangs on the correct identification of the causes of suffering." [source: SEP-Bud §2]
- *Dukkha* ordinarily means physical and mental pain, but in the first truth covers frustration and existential angst. Later tradition lists three kinds: ordinary suffering, suffering due to change, suffering due to conditions (being part of saṃsāra). WP: more aptly "unsatisfactoriness" or "unease". [source: IEP-Bud §3c; WP-The_Buddha]
- The first truth (S V 421) lists birth, ageing, illness, death, union with the displeasing, separation from the pleasing, not getting what one wants, and "the five aggregates of grasping". [source: IEP-Bud §3c]
- *Taṇhā* literally "thirst"; early Western translators (Burnouf, Fausbøll, Müller, Oldenberg, Warren) rendered it "desire", misleading readers into thinking Buddhism seeks the end of all desire. "Craving" is now the commonest translation. Keown: "it is an oversimplification of the Buddhist position to assume that it seeks an end to all desire" (1992: 222). So there is no "paradox of desire": one can will the cessation of craving. Three kinds: craving for sensual pleasures, for existence, for non-existence. [source: IEP-Bud §3b]
- The underlying root is not craving but ignorance (*avijjā*), a misconception rather than a mere lack of information. [source: IEP-Bud §3b]
- *Ariyasacca*: *sacca* means both truth and reality; "noble truths" can mislead (suggesting a creed). Alternatives: "ennobling truths", "truths of the noble ones". K. R. Norman: the term *ariya* was added to the formula later; the earliest mnemonic set was simply "dukkham samudayo nirodho marga". [source: IEP-Bud §3b; WP-Four_Noble_Truths]
- The third truth's goal is nirvana, defined as "the highest happiness" (Dhp 203; M I 505). IEP: so the Buddha's attitude is better called "realist" than pessimist: a problem and a solution. [source: IEP-Bud §3b]
- **Medical analogy:** Hendrik Kern proposed in 1882 that the four truths follow a medical scheme (diagnosis, cause, cure, prescription) with the Buddha as physician; it became popular. Carol Anderson: "there is not sufficient evidence to conclude that the Buddha deliberately drew upon a clearly defined medical model"; Anālayo thinks it "possible" the texts preserve a popular diagnostic scheme. Present as a modern analogy, not as the Buddha's own claim. [source: WP-Four_Noble_Truths]
- **Debate on their place:** since the 19th century some scholars (Bronkhorst, Schmithausen, Vetter, Anderson, Ui Hakuju) argue the four truths were a later formulation or became central later. Anderson: they "were probably not part of the earliest strata… but… emerged as a central teaching in a slightly later period that still preceded the final redactions". Anālayo finds "no compelling basis" for doubting they were the topic of the first sermon, given the parallels, though "noble" may be later. [source: WP-Four_Noble_Truths]

## 6. Dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda / paṭiccasamuppāda)
- The general principle: "when this exists, that comes to be; with the arising of this, that arises. When this does not exist, that does not come to be; with the cessation of this, that ceases" (M II 32; S II 28; IEP's rendering). [source: IEP-Bud §3f]
- Its importance in the texts: the Buddha realised it on the night of his awakening (M I 167); "whoever sees dependent arising sees the Dharma" (M I 191, paraphrased by IEP); it holds whether or not buddhas appear (S II 25). [source: IEP-Bud §3f]
- The twelve links: (1) ignorance, (2) formations, (3) consciousness, (4) mentality-materiality (name-and-form), (5) six senses, (6) contact, (7) feeling/sensation, (8) craving, (9) grasping, (10) becoming, (11) birth, (12) old age and death. [source: IEP-Bud §3f]
- SEP: "Modern scholarship has established that this list is a later compilation", because the texts give two slightly different twelve-link versions and shorter versions. But "it seems safe to say" the Buddha taught roughly: given a functioning assemblage of psychophysical elements, ignorance of the three characteristics leads to appropriation ("I", "mine"), then attachment (desire and aversion), strengthening ignorance and ensuring rebirth. [source: SEP-Bud §2]
- Each link is the most necessary condition of the next, not its sole cause; there is always a multiplicity of conditions. The traditional (commentarial) reading spreads the twelve links over three lives (1–2 past, 3–10 present, 11–12 future). [source: IEP-Bud §3f]
- *Upādāna* (grasping) also means the fuel that feeds a fire; nirvana is the extinction of the fires of craving, aversion and delusion. [source: IEP-Bud §3f]
- The monk Sāti claimed "it is the same consciousness that wanders through the cycle of rebirth"; the Buddha rebuts him: consciousness arises dependent on conditions (M I 258ff). [source: IEP-Bud §3f]
- IEP: the idea that "everything is interdependent" or related to everything else "is a later development of Buddhist thought, not a characteristic of early Indian Buddhism". [source: IEP-Bud §3f]
- **Middle path between eternalism and annihilationism:** the Buddha identifies dependent origination as the key to avoiding both extremes: eternalism (persons are eternal) and annihilationism (persons go utterly out of existence, usually at death). Both share the presupposition that there is an "I" that might continue or end. All of life's continuities are explained by a causal series of psychophysical elements, so "the question of the ultimate fate of this 'I'… simply does not arise". Dependent arising is called right view and the middle way at S II 17. [source: SEP-Bud §2; IEP-Bud §4b]

## 7. Impermanence and the three marks
- The three marks (*tilakkhaṇa*): impermanence (*anicca*), suffering/unsatisfactoriness (*dukkha*) and non-self (*anattā*). Formula: *sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā* (all conditioned things are impermanent), *sabbe saṅkhārā dukkhā*, *sabbe dhammā anattā* (all dharmas, conditioned or unconditioned, are non-self). [source: WP-Three_marks_of_existence]
- SEP calls them "the three characteristics of sentient existence—suffering, impermanence and non-self". [source: SEP-Bud §2]
- Canonical linking: "What is impermanent is suffering. What is suffering is non-self" (S IV 1ff, as given in IEP). [source: IEP-Bud §3d]
- **Impermanent ≠ momentary in the early texts.** SEP: by "permanent" the Buddha meant continued existence over at least several lives; he rules out the body as self because it lasts only one lifetime. "This also demonstrates that the Buddha did not mean by 'impermanent' what some later Buddhist philosophers meant, viz., existing for just a moment; the Buddhist doctrine of momentariness represents a later development." [source: SEP-Bud §3]

## 8. The five aggregates (skandhas)
- The five aggregates of grasping (*pañcupādānakkhandhā*): material form (*rūpa*), feeling/sensation (*vedanā*), perception (*saññā*), mental formations (*saṅkhāra*), consciousness (*viññāṇa*). The first is material, the other four mental. [source: IEP-Bud §3c]
- Rūpa = the four great elements (earth, water, fire, air: solid, liquid, heat/digestion, breath) and the body's shape (M I 185ff). Vedanā = pleasant, unpleasant, neutral feelings after contact between the six senses (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind) and their objects. Saññā = identifying objects. Saṅkhāra = emotional and intellectual dispositions, "literally volitions", products of past conditioning (past karma). Viññāṇa = awareness of the six kinds of object (S III 59ff). [source: IEP-Bud §3c]
- SEP: listing five kinds (not one) shows a kind of dualism, but not substance dualism like Descartes' or Nyāya's: "mind" is "a kind of aggregate term for bundles of transient mental events". The mental elements arise dependent on sense–object contact and last no longer than that contact event. [source: SEP-Bud §3]
- The Buddha defines "personal identity" (*sakkāya*) as the five aggregates (M I 299). "Personality view" (*sakkāyadiṭṭhi*) relates the aggregates to a self in four ways: as identical with it, possessed by it, in it, or containing it (M I 300ff). [source: IEP-Bud §3d–e]

## 9. The arguments for non-self (step by step)
**Standard refrain.** Any aggregate, "whether past, future or present, internal or external, gross or subtle, inferior or superior, far or near", is to be seen "as it actually is with right wisdom thus: 'this is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self'" (M I 138–9; IEP's rendering). Seeing this, the disciple becomes disenchanted, dispassionate and liberated. [source: IEP-Bud §3d]

**Argument A — from impermanence (S III 66–8; SEP's reconstruction):**
1. If there were a self, it would be permanent.
2. None of the five kinds of psychophysical element is permanent.
3. ∴ There is no self.
- Implicit premise (needed for validity): the person consists of nothing but the five aggregates. SEP argues the Buddha accepted it, citing his hostility to unobservable entities: the Poṭṭhapāda Sutta (D I 178–203) compares positing an unseen seer to longing for "the most beautiful woman in the world" because she must surely exist; the Tevijja Sutta (D I 235–52) rejects Brahmins' path to union with a Brahman no one has seen. [source: SEP-Bud §3]
- Premise 1's motive: a self would account for diachronic identity across rebirths; "permanent" means lasting at least several lives. [source: SEP-Bud §3]
- The catechetical form (IEP's rendering of M I 138 and the Anattalakkhaṇa): "What do you think, monks, is material form permanent or impermanent?" – "Impermanent, venerable sir." – "Is what is impermanent suffering or happiness?" – "Suffering, venerable sir." – "Is what is impermanent, suffering, and subject to change, fit to be regarded as: 'this is mine, this I am, this is my self'?" – "No, venerable sir." [source: IEP-Bud §3d]

**Argument B — from control / lack of mastery (S III 66–8, the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta; SEP's reconstruction):**
1. If there were a self, one could never desire that it be changed.
2. Each of the five kinds of psychophysical element is such that one can desire that it be changed.
3. ∴ There is no self.
- The text (Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation, opening words): "Bhikkhus, form is nonself. For if, bhikkhus, form were self, this form would not lead to affliction…" The argument continues that it would then be possible to say of form, "Let my form be thus; let my form not be thus". [source: WS-Bodhi (buddhadust.net mirror of *Connected Discourses*, 2000; second-hand); IEP-Bud §3d gives the same argument at S III 66–7 in similar wording]
- **Objection to premise 1** (SEP): it seems to demand that the self have complete control over itself, which is implausibly strong; we do not require that vision see itself. **Reply** (SEP): read premise 1 through the Indian *irreflexivity principle* (an entity cannot operate on itself: the knife cannot cut itself, the fingertip cannot touch itself). Then a self that was the controller could never find itself seeking to change its own state; so premise 1 is true, premise 2 is plainly true, and given that the person is wholly the aggregates, no controlling self exists. [source: SEP-Bud §3]
- **Counter-move (Sāṃkhya):** the phenomena of control show there must be a controller beyond the aggregates; Sāṃkhya agrees the self is never observed but posits one that "transcends all possible experience". [source: SEP-Bud §3]

**Argument C — from dependent arising** (IEP's third argument; M III 280ff): "If anyone says: 'the visual organ is self,' that is unacceptable. The rising and falling of the visual organ are fully known… it would follow that: 'my self arises and falls.'" Conditioned, arising-and-passing things cannot be a self. [source: IEP-Bud §3d]

- IEP's summary of the assumption behind all three: something could be a self only if it were permanent, not leading to suffering, not dependently arisen, and subject to one's own will; no aggregate meets any condition. [source: IEP-Bud §3d]
- SEP's overall strategy: (i) argue that "I" is "a mere enumerative term like 'pair'" with nothing it genuinely denotes; (ii) explain our false sense of "I" by the useful fiction of the person. Part (ii) is fully articulated only in the later two-truths theory. [source: SEP-Bud §3]

**The chariot (Vajirā and Nāgasena):**
- The chariot simile is already in the canon, in the mouth of a **nun**, Vajirā (SN 5.10, Bhikkhunī Saṃyutta; S I 134–5). Māra asks who created "this being"; she replies (Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation): "Why now do you assume 'a being'? / Mara, have you grasped a view? / This is a heap of sheer constructions: / Here no being is found. // Just as, with an assemblage of parts, / The word 'chariot' is used, / So, when the aggregates are present, / There's the convention 'a being.'" [source: WP-Vajira_(Buddhist_nun), quoting Bodhi, *Discourses of the Ancient Nuns* (1997)]
- The *Milindapañha* ("Questions of Milinda"): a dialogue between the monk Nāgasena and the Indo-Greek king Menander I (Pāli Milinda; 2nd century BCE; SEP-Mind "ca. 150 B.C.E."). The text dates from sometime between 100 BCE and 200 CE, is composite, and survives complete only in Pāli; the Chinese version corresponds to its first three chapters. Von Hinüber: Menander is historical, but Nāgasena is otherwise unknown, the text has anachronisms, and the dialogue "lacks any sign of Greek influence". Canonical only in Burma. [source: WP-Milinda_Panha; SEP-Mind]
- The argument (summary): the king asks who Nāgasena is. Nāgasena: "Nāgasena" is only "a generally understood term, a designation in common use. For there is no permanent individuality (or self) involved in the matter" (SEP-Mind's quotation, *Questions of King Milinda* II.1.25). The king objects: then who does good and bad deeds? Nāgasena asks him, of the chariot he came in, whether the pole, axle, wheels, frame and so on are the chariot, or all of them together, or something besides them; the king denies each. He concedes that "chariot" is the name used when the parts are assembled. Nāgasena: just so with "Nāgasena", and he quotes Vajirā. Rhys Davids' rendering of the quoted verse: "Just as it is by the condition precedent of the co-existence of its various parts that the word 'chariot' is used, just so is it that when the Skandhas are there we talk of a 'being.'" [source: SEP-Mind; WP-Vajira_(Buddhist_nun) (quoting T. W. Rhys Davids, *The Questions of King Milinda*, Sacred Books of the East 35, 1890, Book II ch. 1); WS-Milinda]
- As an argument: (1) A whole is nothing over and above its parts suitably arranged (it is not any part, nor the parts unarranged, nor something apart from them). (2) What is nothing over and above its parts is real only conventionally: its name is a convenient designation (*paññatti/prajñapti*). (3) A person stands to the aggregates as a chariot to its parts. (4) ∴ The person is real only conventionally; there is no self over and above the aggregates. (Reconstruction; premise structure follows SEP-Bud's two-truths account and SEP-Mind's "nominalist" account.) [source: SEP-Bud §2; SEP-Mind]
- SEP-Bud: the chariot is "the stock example of a conventionally real entity", accepted because "it is more convenient, given our interests and cognitive limitations, to have a single name for the parts when assembled in the right way"; it is a "mere conceptual fiction", and "King Milinda rode in a chariot" is conventionally true. Not all conceptualisation is falsification: only concepts that allow reductive analysis inflate our ontology. [source: SEP-Bud §2]
- Abhidharma: things with substantial existence (*dravyasat*) vs nominal/conventional existence (*prajñaptisat*). Abhidharma philosophers do not deny the physical reality of the parts; they reject partite entities as ultimately real. [source: SEP-Mind]

**Interpretive debate on what non-self denies:** see Debates.

## 10. Rebirth without a self: the objection and the replies
- **The objection** (SEP's statement): the body ends at death and no mental element transmigrates, yet the Buddha says the unenlightened are reborn. "If there is no constituent whatever that moves from one life to the next, how could the being in the next life be the same person as the being in this life?" Sharper still: karmic reward and punishment "could be just only if the recipient… is the same person as the agent". Indian self-theorists found this decisive. [source: SEP-Bud §3]
- IEP's version: "If there is no self, who is the agent of actions? Who experiences the consequences of actions? Is the person who performs an action in this life the same person that experiences the consequences…?" The Buddha calls such questions improper for disciples, who explain things by causes and conditions (S II 61ff; S II 13ff). [source: IEP-Bud §5]
- **Option rejected: drop rebirth.** Some Buddhists (some East Asian, many Western Buddhists today) give up rebirth. SEP: the evidence that the Buddha accepted karma and rebirth "seems quite strong"; he taught it to advanced monastics (A III 33), so it cannot be explained away as a "noble lie" for householders. [source: SEP-Bud §4]
- **Reply 1 — neither the same nor different.** The Buddha says the earlier and later person are "neither the same nor different" (S II 62; S II 76; S II 113). SEP: since any two existing things must be either identical or distinct, this says strictly speaking they do not exist; ultimately there are only impersonal events in causal sequence (ignorance, desire, intention, action, feeling, suffering). Persons and desert are conventionally real: we minimise suffering by acting as though there are persons with moral properties. So "rebirth without transmigration is logically possible". [source: SEP-Bud §§4–5]
- In the Pāli the Buddha unambiguously denies both numerical identity and numerical distinctness (so this is not an English ambiguity). [source: SEP-Bud §5]
- **Reply 2 — continuity of a process.** IEP: the moral agent is "not a substance-self but rather the five aggregates, a dynamic and dependently-arisen process-self who, like a flame or the water of a river, changes all the time and yet has some degree of continuity". Gowans 2003: the Buddha "rejects substance-selves but accepts process-selves". [source: IEP-Bud §§3f, 5]
- **The Milindapañha's similes for rebirth** (second-hand): asked whether he who is born remains the same or becomes another, Nāgasena answers "Neither the same nor another" (Rhys Davids). He compares the person to a lamp burning through the night: the flame of the first watch is not the same as that of the second, yet it is one lamp; and to milk that becomes curds, butter and ghee, which are "produced out of it". [source: WS-Milinda (sacred-texts and dhammatalks.net copies; not read directly)]
- **Abhidharma version:** each momentary event conditions the next of its kind, giving "an uninterrupted, flowing continuum (*santāna*) of causally connected momentary events". [source: SEP-Abh §3]
- **Personalist alternative:** the Pudgalavādins (Personalists) held that the person is ultimately real, "neither identical with nor distinct from" the aggregates, and ineffable. Most Buddhists rejected this, partly because it leads to an ineffability paradox. [source: SEP-Bud §5; IEP-Pud]
- **Parfit comparison.** Parfit's reductionism: "persons do exist. But they exist only in the way in which nations exist. Persons are not, as we mistakenly believe fundamental" (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984, p. 445, as quoted in SEP-Pers). Siderits classified the Abhidharma view as "Reductionism" in "Buddhist Reductionism" (1997). Chadha (2021) challenges this, reading Vasubandhu as an *Eliminativist* about persons: persons "inherit the work of the selves, so they are just as dangerous as selves". Parfit held that what matters is "psychological connectedness and/or continuity" (1984, 311). [source: SEP-Pers §2]
- Parfit himself wrote that "Buddha would have agreed" with his view (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984, p. 273, §92 "Wittgenstein and Buddha"), and quotes Buddhist passages in Appendix J. [source: WS-Parfit (secondary literature quoting p. 273; not read in the book); WP-Reasons_and_Persons says only that Parfit's conclusion is similar to the Buddhist skandha view]
- Ethical use of the reductionist view (later, not early): Śāntideva's "Ownerless Suffering Argument" ("no sufferings belong to anyone") resembles Parfit's arguments for consequentialism (Parfit 1984 ch. 15), as Siderits (2003) notes. [source: SEP-Eth §3]

## 11. The unanswered questions (avyākata) and nirvana
- The ten undetermined questions in the Pāli Nikāyas: whether (1) the world is eternal; (2) not eternal; (3) infinite; (4) finite; (5) body and soul are one; (6) two different things; whether a *tathāgata* (liberated being) (7) exists after death; (8) does not; (9) both exists and does not; (10) neither exists nor does not. Sanskrit texts list fourteen, adding the "both" and "neither" options for the world questions. [source: IEP-Bud §4a]
- The Buddha's three responses: he does not hold such views; he has left them undetermined; and (for the tathāgata only) the questions "do not apply" (*na upeti*). [source: IEP-Bud §4a]
- **The poisoned arrow** (Cūḷamālukya Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya; M I 429): the monk Mālunkyaputta threatens to leave unless answered; the Buddha compares him to a man shot with a poisoned arrow who refuses treatment until told who shot it, his caste, name and height, and the kind of bow. Parallel in the Chinese *Madhyama Āgama* (MĀ 221). [source: SEP-Bud §5; WP-Parable_of_the_Poisoned_Arrow]
- **The fire simile** (M I 483–8): a fire that has gone out for lack of fuel has not gone north, south or anywhere; the question presupposes the fire still exists. IEP's rendering of M I 487–8: "The fire burned in dependence on its fuel of grass and sticks. When that is used up… it is reckoned as extinguished. Similarly, the enlightened being has abandoned the five aggregates… he is profound, immeasurable, unfathomable like the ocean." [source: SEP-Bud §5; IEP-Bud §4a]
- Nirvana (Pāli *nibbāna*) literally "blowing out" or "extinguishing": the extinction of the fires of craving, aversion and delusion (S IV 251). Two kinds: with remainder (the arhat's aggregates continue until death) and without remainder (It 38). A few texts speak of nirvana as a domain or reality, "unconditioned", "deathless" (e.g. Ud 8.1). [source: IEP-Bud §§3b, 4a]
- IEP: nirvana "is not primarily an absolute reality beyond or behind the universe but rather a special state of mind in which all the causes and conditions responsible for rebirth and suffering have been eliminated" (Vélez de Cea's reading). [source: IEP-Bud lead]
- The Buddha rejects the charge of annihilationism: "the ascetic Gotama… teaches the annihilation, the destruction, the extermination of an existing being" is a misrepresentation (M I 140); "formerly and now I only teach suffering and the cessation of suffering". [source: IEP-Bud §4b]
- Tetralemma (*catuṣkoṭi*): rejecting all four options raises the question whether the Buddha uses a deviant logic. SEP: no; the negations are "commitmentless" (illocutionary: "do not say that 7 is green, and do not say that it is not green either"), and option 3 can be read with parameters. [source: SEP-Bud §5]

## 12. Ethics: karma as intention, precepts, path
- "It is intention, monks, what I call action. Having intended, someone acts through body, speech, and mind" (A III 415; IEP's rendering; Pāli *cetanā*). SEP-Bud: by action the Buddha means "not the movement or change involved, but rather the volition or intention that brought about the change". [source: IEP-Bud §5; SEP-Bud §4]
- Gombrich (2009): this marks "the transition from an earlier ritualistic view of action to a view that brings action within the purview of ethics". Example (SEP): uttering profanities near an elder is not blameworthy if done by a parrot or infant. [source: SEP-Bud §4]
- Karma literally "action"; strictly the theory is a causal link between action (*karma*) and "fruit" (*phala*) for the agent. Three types of action: bodily, verbal, mental. The Buddha's version is strict: each action has its own specific consequence for the agent, not merely a habit-forming tendency. [source: SEP-Bud §4]
- The three defilements (*kleśas*): desire, aversion, ignorance (SEP); the three unwholesome roots (*lobha, dosa, moha*: greed, aversion, delusion, M I 47) (IEP). Acts of one in whom these are destroyed produce no karmic fruit (A III 33). [source: SEP-Bud §4; IEP-Bud §5]
- Not fatalism: past karma influences but does not dictate; "conditioning of the will… but no hard determinism". Not everything experienced is due to past karma. [source: IEP-Bud §5; WP-The_Buddha (Gombrich 2009 p. 20)]
- Channa's/Cunda's meal: the Buddha tells followers not to condemn the giver of the meal that preceded his death, since his intention was generous (SEP-Eth calls the giver "Channa"; WP-The_Buddha calls him "Cunda", which is the usual name — use **Cunda**). [source: SEP-Eth §3; WP-The_Buddha]
- *Śīla* (Pāli *sīla*), the term usually translated "ethics", means something like "moral discipline". It is unclear that Buddhist thinkers have a concept of moral obligation at all. *Kuśala* is often translated "good" but also "skillful". [source: SEP-Eth §1]
- **Five precepts** (lay): refrain from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying, and drunkenness (intoxicants). Monastics follow the *prātimokṣa*, which forbids all sexual activity. [source: SEP-Eth §1]
- **Ten unwholesome courses of action**: 3 bodily (killing, stealing, sexual misconduct), 4 verbal (lying, divisive, harsh, idle speech), 3 mental (covetousness, malice, wrong view). IEP calls the ten bright/ten dark actions the most comprehensive formulation of early Buddhist ethics. [source: SEP-Eth §1; IEP-Bud §5]
- **Eightfold path**: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration. Traditional threefold grouping: wisdom (view, intention), ethics (speech, action, livelihood), concentration (effort, mindfulness, concentration). *Sammā* ("right") means "perfect" or "complete" rather than the opposite of "wrong" (IEP). [source: IEP-Bud §§3b, 5; WP-Four_Noble_Truths]
- Advice to Rāhula at Ambalaṭṭhikā (MN 61; Ñāṇamoli and Bodhi trans. 1995, 524–25): if an intended bodily act "would lead to my own affliction, or to the affliction of others, or to the affliction of both", it is unwholesome and should not be done. Goodman: this suggests a consequentialist reading. [source: SEP-Eth §3]
- **Kālāma Sutta** (Kesamutti Sutta, AN 3.65; A I 188–9), the "charter of free inquiry" (Soma Thera). Soma's translation: "Do not go upon what has been acquired by repeated hearing (anussava), nor upon tradition (paramparā), nor upon rumor… nor upon what is in a scripture… nor upon surmise… nor upon an axiom… nor upon specious reasoning… nor upon a bias towards a notion that has been pondered over… nor upon another's seeming ability… nor upon the consideration 'The monk is our teacher'… Kalamas, when you yourselves know 'These things are good; these things are not blameable; these things are praised by the wise; undertaken and observed, these things lead to benefit and happiness,' enter on and abide in them." Bhikkhu Bodhi warns against making the Buddha "a pragmatic empiricist who dismisses all doctrine and faith" on the basis of "a single passage, quoted out of context". IEP agrees it is not an iconoclastic rejection of tradition and faith. [source: WP-Kesamutti_Sutta; IEP-Bud §2c]
- The "incontrovertible teaching" (*apaṇṇaka*, M I 403) resembles Pascal's wager: being moral is the better bet whether or not there is rebirth. [source: IEP-Bud §2b]
- Faith (*saddhā*) is valued but must be "based on critical examination… and eventually verified" (IEP). Jayatilleke (1963) and Kalupahana read the Buddha as an empiricist; IEP: "In this sense, the Buddha is not an empiricist." [source: IEP-Bud §2]

## 13. Women: the bhikkhunī order, Mahāpajāpatī, the Therīgāthā
- Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī (Skt. Mahāprajāpatī Gautamī): the Buddha's maternal aunt and foster-mother, who raised him after Māyā's death; by tradition the first woman to seek ordination and the first bhikkhunī. Her request is in the Aṅguttara Nikāya (AN 8.51, Gotamī Sutta) and the Vinaya (Cullavagga X.1), and in Sarvāstivāda and Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinayas. [source: WP-Mahāprajāpatī_Gautamī; WP-Bhikkhunī]
- The story: after Śuddhodana's death she asked three times; the Buddha refused; she cut her hair, put on robes and walked with many Śākya and Koliya women to Vesālī; Ānanda interceded. Asked whether a woman who goes forth could realise stream-entry up to arahantship, the Buddha answers "Yes, Ānanda, she would…" (AN 8.51, Ṭhānissaro's translation, as quoted in WP). Ordination was conditioned on accepting the **eight *garudhammas*** ("heavy rules") subordinating nuns to monks. [source: WP-Mahāprajāpatī_Gautamī; WP-Bhikkhunī]
- In AN 8.51 the Buddha predicts that admitting women will shorten the life of the teaching from a thousand to five hundred years; this prophecy occurs only once in the canon. [source: WP-Bhikkhunī]
- **Historicity debated.** Bhikkhu Bodhi: the background stories show the garudhammas originating at different points, which "casts doubt on the historicity of the present account". Hirakawa, cited by In Young Chung: the rules "were appended later". Anālayo regards the thousand-to-five-hundred-year detail as a later development. Some (Nakamura) have even regarded the nuns' order as a later invention. [source: WP-Bhikkhunī]
- Canonical position: women are as capable of enlightenment as men. Vajirā's chariot verses (§9) show a nun stating the non-self doctrine in the canon itself. [source: WP-Bhikkhunī; WP-Vajira_(Buddhist_nun)]
- **Therīgāthā** ("Verses of the Elder Nuns"), in the Khuddaka Nikāya: 73 poems in 16 chapters, 494 verses (the commentary attributes them to 101 nuns). Called "the earliest known collection of women's literature composed in India" (von Hinüber 1997, via WP). Composed over centuries, some dating to the late 3rd century BCE; orally transmitted until written down in Pāli c. 80 BCE. CAVEAT: authorship debated; Bernard Faure: no evidence the verses were composed by the women they are attributed to; the text was compiled, edited and annotated by the monk Dhammapāla, whose commentary WP dates to the 400s CE. [source: WP-Therīgāthā]
- Kisā Gotamī: the mustard-seed story (find seed from a house where no one has died). It is a **later** story (WP's sources are *Tibetan Tales*, 1906, and the Theravāda tradition), not attested in the four Nikāyas in any source read. Use as story, flagged as later; WP associates a Dhammapada verse with her story. [source: WP-Kisa_Gotami]
- The Theravāda bhikkhunī order lapsed in Sri Lanka in the 11th century CE and in Burma by about the 13th; it was revived in 1996 when 11 Sri Lankan women were fully ordained at Sarnath. [source: WP-Bhikkhunī]

## 14. The Abhidharma: dharmas and momentariness
- *Abhidharma* (Pāli *abhidhamma*): probably "concerning (*abhi*) the teaching(s)", but traditionally "higher" or "further" teaching. It is both a body of texts (the third basket, its commentaries, manuals) and a method: technical, carefully defined terms in place of the colloquial suttas. It provides "the theoretical counterpart to the Buddhist practice of meditation" and "a systematic account of sentient experience". [source: SEP-Abh lead, §1.1]
- The tradition contrasts the sūtras' "way of putting things" in conventional terms (*saṃvṛti/sammuti*) needing interpretation with Abhidharma exposition in ultimate terms (*paramārtha/paramattha*). [source: SEP-Abh §1.1]
- Only two complete canonical Abhidharma collections survive: the **Sarvāstivāda** (emerged from the Sthaviras c. 2nd or 1st century BCE; dominant in north and northwest India, spread to Central Asia; its seven texts, including the *Jñānaprasthāna*, survive in full only in Chinese) and the Sinhalese **Theravāda** (seven texts in Pāli: Dhammasaṅgaṇi, Vibhaṅga, Dhātukathā, Puggalapaññatti, Kathāvatthu, Yamaka, Paṭṭhāna). [source: SEP-Abh §1]
- The "eighteen schools" is a conventional number; sources list more. Gethin: some schools were probably "informal schools of thought in the manner of 'Cartesians,' 'British Empiricists,' or 'Kantians'". Names: Sarvāstivāda ("those who hold that all exists"), Sautrāntika ("those who rely on the sūtras"), Pudgalavāda ("those who affirm the person"). [source: SEP-Abh §1]
- Later manuals: Buddhaghosa's *Visuddhimagga* (5th century CE); Vasubandhu's *Abhidharmakośa* (5th century CE), whose auto-commentary criticises orthodox Sarvāstivāda; Saṅghabhadra's reply; the Sarvāstivāda *Mahāvibhāṣā* (1st–2nd century CE). These belong to the next era's chapter but can be mentioned. [source: SEP-Abh §1]
- **From Dharma to dharmas.** The suttas analyse experience as name-and-form, five aggregates, twelve sense fields or eighteen elements. The Abhidharma "reduces the time scale of these processes so they are now seen as operating from moment to moment" and calls the discrete events *dharmas* (plural, distinct from *dharma*, the teaching). Dharmas are physical and mental "evanescent events, occurrences, or dynamic properties rather than enduring substances"; "dharmas are all that there is". [source: SEP-Abh §1.2]
- **Taxonomies.** Theravāda: 82 dhamma types: consciousness (*citta*, 1), mental factors (*cetasika*, 52), materiality (*rūpa*, 28), all conditioned, plus one unconditioned, nirvana. Sarvāstivāda: 75 types in five groups: consciousness (1), mental factors (46), matter (11), factors dissociated from thought (14), unconditioned (3: space and two cessations). The 82 are types of occurrence, not 82 occurrences. Theravāda describes 89 basic types of consciousness moment. [source: SEP-Abh §2]
- Soteriological purpose: the analysis maps the mind for practice and shows "that no essence or independent self could be found in any phenomenon". Gethin: "Try to grasp the world of the Dhammasaṅgaṇi, or the Paṭṭhāna, and it runs through one's fingers." [source: SEP-Abh §2]
- **Momentariness** (*kṣaṇavāda*): both the Sarvāstivāda and post-canonical Theravāda atomise phenomena into discrete momentary events "that pass out of existence as soon as they have originated". Not a topic in the Buddha's discourses; developed from the principle of impermanence. [source: SEP-Abh §3]
- Sarvāstivāda: a moment is the smallest indivisible unit of time; texts suggest figures between 0.13 and 13 milliseconds in modern terms (Gethin 1998; von Rospatt 1995); all physical and mental phenomena are momentary. Theravāda commentaries divide each moment into arising, enduring and dissolving sub-moments; only mental phenomena are momentary, and a material phenomenon lasts 16 or 17 mind-moments. [source: SEP-Abh §3]
- **Sarvāstivāda "all exists":** past, present and future dharmas all exist as real entities; a dharma's present "activity" (*kāritra*) marks its presentness, while past and future dharmas keep "capability" (*sāmarthya*). This was meant to explain continuity and causal efficacy, and how past karma bears fruit. SEP-Bud adds that one school's "four-dimensionalism" was motivated by the belief that the Buddha could cognise past and future events. [source: SEP-Abh §§3, 5; SEP-Bud §5]
- Sautrāntika rejected this: only present dharmas exist; it explained causal links by "seeds" (*bīja*), a precursor of Yogācāra's store-consciousness. Theravāda's *Paṭṭhāna* lists 24 conditional relations. [source: SEP-Abh §5]
- Intrinsic nature (*svabhāva/sabhāva*) is absent from the sūtras and becomes standard only in post-canonical literature: "dhamma means but intrinsic nature" (Visuddhimagga VIII 246). This is what Nāgārjuna later attacks (next era). [source: SEP-Abh §4]
- Gombrich (1996) reads the shift from impermanence to "endurance" as scholastic literalism and reification; Ronkin: rather "a shift in time scales", from past/present/future lives to phases of each dharma. [source: SEP-Abh §3]
- Two truths: the conventional/ultimate distinction is a later articulation (SEP-Bud: "This reductionist view of sentient beings was later articulated in terms of the distinction between two kinds of truth"). [source: SEP-Bud §2]
- **Pudgalavāda**: five schools (Vātsīputrīya, the original, plus Dharmottarīya, Bhadrayānīya, Sāmmitīya, Ṣaṇṇagarika). The Vātsīputrīya arose about two centuries after the Buddha's death. Xuanzang (7th century CE) found the Sāmmitīya the largest of the early schools, about a quarter of all Indian monks. Their analogy: person is to aggregates as fire to fuel; their key text: the sutta of the burden and its bearer (the aggregates are the burden, the person the bearer). Their doctrine was criticised in the Kathāvatthu and by Vasubandhu. [source: IEP-Pud; SEP-Pers]

## 15. Aśoka (briefly)
- Aśoka reigned c. 268–232 BCE (WP lead also gives 269). His edicts record that in his eighth regnal year (c. 260 BCE) he conquered Kalinga; Major Rock Edict 13 records 100,000 killed, many times that number dying, 150,000 carried away, and his remorse; he turned to promoting *dhamma* (non-violence, compassion, tolerance). [source: WP-Ashoka]
- Conversion was gradual: a Minor Rock Edict says he had been a lay follower (*upāsaka*) for more than two and a half years. Sri Lankan tradition says he was already Buddhist before Kalinga; Rock Edict 13 is omitted from the inscriptions in Kalinga itself. John Strong: helpful to read his messages partly as propaganda. [source: WP-Ashoka]
- Sri Lankan tradition credits him with the Third Council and with missions, including his son Mahinda to Sri Lanka; North Indian tradition does not mention these. [source: WP-Ashoka; WP-Buddhist_councils]
- His Lumbinī pillar is the oldest inscriptional reference to the Buddha's birthplace (§2). [source: WP-The_Buddha]

## 16. Philosophy, religion or therapy?
- SEP: treating the Buddha as a philosopher "is controversial". Two reasons given against it: (a) Albahari (2006) correctly notes the Buddha "never categorically denies the existence of a self that transcends what is empirically given"; some say he points to something beyond philosophical reason, so the later tradition "simply got the Buddha wrong"; (b) he rejects theorising without practical application, so non-self is "not a bit of metaphysics, just some practical advice" against identifying with the transitory. SEP's reply: both assume we can reach the Buddha's own thought without the later tradition, which the state of the sources makes doubtful; SEP itself treats him as the tradition understood him. [source: SEP-Bud §1]
- SEP on the arrow and fire similes: the problem with the unanswered questions is not that reason oversteps its domain but that they "rest on a false presupposition—something that is disclosed through the employment of philosophical rationality". [source: SEP-Bud §5]
- Authority: SEP notes M I 68, where the Buddha calls it libel to say he reached his conclusions by reasoning alone; Gombrich (2009: 164) takes this as later devotional material. Collins (1982: 183) wondered whether non-self is more than "a sort of linguistic taboo". [source: SEP-Bud §5]
- Siderits, *Buddhism as Philosophy* (2nd ed. 2021) is the standard statement of the philosophical reading (SEP bibliography). IEP-Bud similarly reads the Buddha's views as philosophy (epistemology, metaphysics, ethics). [source: SEP-Bud bibliography; IEP-Bud lead]
- Gowans, *Philosophy of the Buddha* (2003), reads the self as a "process-self" (IEP). Separately, Gowans has written on the "medical analogies" shared by Buddhist and Hellenistic schools (Epicurean, Stoic, Pyrrhonian) in the Royal Institute of Philosophy volume on philosophy as therapy, arguing the analogies are apt but limited. [source: IEP-Bud §3f; WS-Gowans (Cambridge Core description; second-hand)] — useful cross-link to the Hellenistic chapter.
- Ethics: IEP holds early Buddhist ethics is pluralistic, "a sui generis normative theory inassimilable to Western ethical traditions"; Keown (1992) reads it as virtue ethics (nirvana as analogous to eudaimonia), mainly for Theravāda; Goodman (2009) defends "character consequentialism". [source: IEP-Bud §5; SEP-Eth §3]

## Debates
- **Dates.** Long chronology (Theravāda: 624/623–544/543 BCE; scholarly long chronology 566–486) vs short chronology (448–368 BCE) vs the post-1988 consensus of a death c. 410–370 BCE (Gombrich c. 405/404; Norman 410–390; Bechert 400–350; Bareau 420–380). Not all historians accept the redating. [source: IEP-Bud; WP-The_Buddha; SEP-Bud]
- **Can we know what the Buddha himself taught?** Gombrich, Warder, Wynne: the Nikāya core goes back to one mind. Davidson and others are sceptical. IEP: "most scholars [are] skeptical about the possibility of knowing what the Buddha really taught", yet the Pāli Nikāyas and Āgamas are the best sources. SEP sets the question aside and follows the later tradition. [source: IEP-Bud §1b; WP-The_Buddha; SEP-Bud §1]
- **What does non-self deny?** (a) The mainstream (and the whole classical Indian tradition, Buddhist and orthodox) read it as denying any self. (b) A minority (Bhattacharya 1973, Pérez-Remón 1980; Albahari 2006 in a related form) argues the Buddha only denies that the aggregates are the self, leaving open a transcendent self; he never says "the self does not exist", only that a self "is not apprehended" (M I 138). (c) A "strategy" reading treats non-self as practical advice, not metaphysics. (d) IEP's middle reading: it denies a permanent, independent self, while accepting conventional "process-selves". Within the reductionist camp, Siderits (Reductionism) vs Chadha (Eliminativism). [source: SEP-Bud §§1, 3; IEP-Bud §3e; SEP-Pers]
- **Were the four noble truths the earliest teaching?** Bronkhorst, Schmithausen, Vetter, Anderson: a later formulation or later central. Anālayo: early, attested in parallels; "noble" perhaps later. [source: WP-Four_Noble_Truths]
- **The meaning of the Buddha's silence (avyākata).** Pragmatic (they do not lead to liberation: the arrow); metaphysical (they presuppose a self: the fire; SEP and IEP both stress this); apophatic (the liberated being is beyond reckoning). Readings of nirvana after death: annihilation; mysterious eternal existence (Harvey 1995; some Theravāda; the Dalai Lama); or strict silence beyond death (Vélez de Cea). Thích Nhất Hạnh reads the parable as anti-metaphysical. [source: IEP-Bud §4; SEP-Bud §5; WP-Parable_of_the_Poisoned_Arrow]
- **Did the Buddha really teach karma and rebirth?** Some modern (Western and some East Asian) Buddhists drop rebirth. SEP: the evidence he taught it, even to monks (A III 33), is strong. Some scholars (Vetter, Schmithausen, Bronkhorst via WP) think karma was of minor importance in earliest soteriology, rebirth being tied to craving or ignorance. [source: SEP-Bud §4; WP-Karma_in_Buddhism]
- **Empiricist or not?** Jayatilleke (logical empiricism), Kalupahana (radical empiricism) vs Hoffman and IEP. [source: IEP-Bud §2d]
- **Philosophy, religion or therapy?** SEP's two reasons against "Buddha as philosopher" (transcendent-self reading; anti-theoretical pragmatism) and its reply; IEP and Siderits read him as a philosopher; the medical/therapy model (Kern's 1882 analogy; Gowans on medical analogies; Anderson's caution); and the authority problem (M I 68; Collins's "linguistic taboo"). [source: SEP-Bud §§1, 5; WP-Four_Noble_Truths; WS-Gowans]
- **What kind of ethics?** Virtue ethics (Keown 1992, mainly Theravāda); consequentialism (Goodman; MN 61; Śāntideva later); character consequentialism (Goodman 2009); pluralist sui generis (Vélez de Cea). Intention-only readings are disputed by IEP. [source: SEP-Eth §3; IEP-Bud §5]
- **Nuns and the garudhammas.** Canonical account (AN 8.51) vs evidence that the rules were appended later (Bodhi, Hirakawa, Anālayo); modern revival debates. [source: WP-Bhikkhunī]
- **Was Abhidharma faithful?** Abhidharmikas saw themselves as stating the sūtras' ultimate meaning; Gombrich sees reification; Ronkin a change of time scale; Pudgalavādins dissented on persons. [source: SEP-Abh; IEP-Pud]
- **Aśoka's conversion.** Dramatic conversion after Kalinga vs gradual (epigraphy) vs before Kalinga (Sri Lankan tradition, Eggermont); propaganda (Strong). [source: WP-Ashoka]
