## Life: family, education, Cambridge (1889–1914)
- Ludwig Josef Johann Wittgenstein, born 26 April 1889 in Vienna, "to a wealthy industrial family, well-situated in intellectual and cultural Viennese circles" [source: SEP-W §1; IEP-W §1; WP-LW]. Born in the "Villa Wittgenstein" in Neuwaldegg on the outskirts of Vienna [source: WP-LW].
- Father Karl Wittgenstein (1847–1913), steel magnate, "one of the most successful businessmen in the Austro-Hungarian Empire, leading the iron and steel industry there" [source: IEP-W §1]; WP-LW: by the late 1880s one of the richest men in Europe; the family second in wealth in the Empire only to the Rothschilds [source: WP-LW].
- Ludwig was the youngest of the children (IEP: "the youngest of eight children"; WP-LW: nine children, one daughter dying as a baby). Of his four (surviving) brothers three committed suicide (Hans 1902, presumed; Rudi 1904; Kurt 27 Oct 1918) [source: IEP-W §1; WP-LW]. Brother Paul became a concert pianist despite losing an arm in WWI [source: WP-LW].
- Brahms was a friend of the family; music remained important to Wittgenstein all his life [source: IEP-W §1; WP-LW].
- Jewish background, stated carefully: his father's parents were born Jewish but converted to Protestantism; his mother Leopoldine (née Kalmus) was Catholic but her father was of Jewish descent; Ludwig was baptized Catholic and given a Catholic burial, "although between baptism and burial he was neither a practicing nor a believing Catholic" [source: IEP-W §1]. WP-LW: three of four grandparents were of Jewish birth; under the 1935 Nuremberg laws, after the 1938 Anschluss, he counted as a Jew; around 1936–37 he confessed to friends that he had let them underestimate his "Jewishness" (IEP-W: "allowing others to underestimate the extent of his 'Jewishness'"). In 1929–31 notebooks he often called himself Jewish, self-deprecatingly [source: IEP-W §1; WP-LW §Jewishness, citing McGuinness 2001 and Stern 2001].
- Sexuality, stated carefully: IEP-W: "His sexuality was ambiguous but he was probably gay; how actively so is still a matter of controversy." WP-LW: generally believed to have loved at least three men — David Pinsent (1912), Francis Skinner (1930s), Ben Richards (late 1940s) — and in the 1920s considered marrying Marguerite Respinger [source: IEP-W §1; WP-LW].
- Secondary school: the Realschule in Linz, 1903–1906; Adolf Hitler attended the same school, but in a different class, and "there is no evidence the two ever had anything to do with each other" (Monk, Sluga) [source: WP-LW].
- Studied mechanical engineering at the Technische Hochschule in Berlin-Charlottenburg (from October 1906; diploma 5 May 1908) [source: WP-LW; IEP-W §1].
- 1908: went to Manchester (Victoria University of Manchester) for aeronautical research, experimenting with kites at Glossop; patented a propeller design in 1911 [source: WP-LW; IEP-W §1]. SEP-W phrases it as "In 1908 he began his studies in aeronautical engineering at Manchester University where his interest in the philosophy of pure mathematics led him to Frege." 
- He read Russell's *Principles of Mathematics* (1903) and Frege; visited Frege at Jena in summer 1911; Frege advised him to study with Russell [source: WP-LW; SEP-W §1; IEP-W §1].
- Arrived unannounced at Russell's rooms in Trinity College, Cambridge, on 18 October 1911 [source: WP-LW]. At Cambridge 1911–1913 [source: SEP-W §1].
- Russell (quoted by Monk 1990: 38f): "An unknown German appeared … obstinate and perverse, but I think not stupid"; and within a year: "I shall certainly encourage him. Perhaps he will do great things … I love him and feel he will solve the problems I am too old to solve" (Monk 1990: 41) [source: SEP-W §1]. (WP-LW gives the second as a letter to Ottoline Morrell three months after arrival: "I love him & feel he will solve the problems I am too old to solve ... He is *the* young man one hopes for.")
- Russell's hippopotamus anecdote: Wittgenstein "maintained … that all existential propositions are meaningless"; Russell invited him to consider "There is no hippopotamus in this room at present", looked under all the desks, "but he remained unconvinced" [source: WP-LW, quoting Russell].
- Father died 20 January 1913; Ludwig inherited a fortune and gave part to poets and artists chosen by Ludwig von Ficker (incl. Trakl, Rilke, Loos, Kokoschka) [source: WP-LW; IEP-W §1].
- 1913–14: retreated to Skjolden, Norway; built a hut there; wrote "Notes on Logic" (1913); dictated notes to Moore in Norway (April 1914) [source: WP-LW; SEP-W §2.1]. He learned Danish to read Kierkegaard [source: WP-LW].

## Life: war and the *Tractatus* (1914–1922)
- Volunteered for the Austro-Hungarian army in 1914 despite eligibility for exemption; served on the Russian front (from 1916 in an observation post, during the Brusilov Offensive) and from 1918 on the Italian front; decorated several times for bravery [source: WP-LW; IEP-W §1 "won several medals for bravery"; SEP-W §1].
- Carried Tolstoy's *The Gospel in Brief*, bought in Tarnów, and was known to fellow soldiers as "the man with the gospels" [source: WP-LW].
- The *Tractatus* came out of "Notes on Logic" (1913), "Notes Dictated to G. E. Moore" (1914), the *Notebooks* 1914–16 and correspondence with Russell, Moore and Keynes; "it was during the war that he wrote the notes and drafts" [source: SEP-W §1, §2.1]. Completed in August 1918 during military leave, at the family's Neuwaldegg house; submitted under the title *Der Satz* to the publisher Jahoda und Siegel, who turned it down [source: WP-LW; WP-TLP]. So: "written at the front" is accurate for the notebooks/drafts; the final text was completed on leave in Vienna.
- Captured by the Italians in November 1918 (WP-LW: 3 November, in Trentino); nine months in an Italian prisoner-of-war camp (Monte Cassino is not named in my sources — do not add) [source: SEP-W §1; WP-LW].
- Back in Vienna 25 August 1919; gave away his fortune to his siblings [source: WP-LW; SEP-W §1; IEP-W §1].
- Publication: German, 1921, as "Logisch-Philosophische Abhandlung" in Wilhelm Ostwald's journal *Annalen der Naturphilosophie* (vol. 14); Wittgenstein, sent no proofs, called it a pirate edition [source: SEP-W bibliography; WP-LW; WP-TLP]. Wittgenstein always called the book "Logisch-Philosophische Abhandlung" [source: SEP-W §2.1].
- English: London, Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1922, bilingual, in a series edited by C. K. Ogden, with Russell's introduction [source: SEP-W §2.1; WP-LW]. The Latin title was suggested by G. E. Moore as an allusion to Spinoza's *Tractatus Theologico-Politicus* [source: WP-LW].
- The "Ogden translation" was mainly the work of Frank Ramsey, who "aged 19, … completed a translation" while still an undergraduate; "C. K. Ogden got all the credit"; Wittgenstein scrutinised it and "gave it his seal of approval" [source: SEP-FR §2; WP-LW]. SEP-W bibliography: "C. K. Ogden (ed.) and F. P. Ramsey (trans.) with Wittgenstein". Re-translated by D. F. Pears and B. F. McGuinness in 1961 [source: SEP-W §2.1, §6]. Philosophers often prefer Pears–McGuinness, but Ogden–Ramsey is the version Wittgenstein approved [source: WP-LW; SEP-FR].
- Recent new translations: Michael Beaney (Oxford UP, 2023), Alexander Booth (Penguin, 2023), Damion Searls (Liveright, 2024); a Cambridge UP translation by Stern, Saporiti and Schulte forthcoming [source: SEP-W §6].
- Russell's introduction: Russell called the book something that "certainly deserves … to be considered an important event in the philosophical world"; Wittgenstein "thought little of Russell's introduction, claiming that it was riddled with misunderstandings" [source: SEP-W §2.1]. Wittgenstein to Russell: "The main point is the theory of what can be expressed (*gesagt*) by propositions … and what can not be expressed by propositions, but only shown (*gezeigt*); which, I believe, is the cardinal problem of philosophy" [source: WP-LW].
- The book is short (WP-LW: "75 pages long"); to Ogden: "If you were to squeeze me like a lemon you would get nothing more out of me" [source: WP-LW].
- The *Tractatus* was "the only philosophy book that Wittgenstein published during his lifetime" [source: IEP-W intro]. The only other book published in his lifetime was a 42-page spelling dictionary for elementary schools, *Wörterbuch für Volksschulen* (1926) [source: WP-LW].
- Letter to the publisher Ludwig von Ficker (before publication): "My work consists of two parts, the one presented here plus all that I have not written. And it is precisely this second part that is the important point. For the ethical gets its limit drawn from the inside, as it were, by my book" [source: SEP-W §2.4, quoting the letter as printed with ProtoTractatus p. 16]. IEP-W §2: Wittgenstein "told Ludwig von Ficker that the point of the Tractatus was ethical."

## Life: schoolteacher, architect, Vienna Circle (1920–1929)
- Trained at the teacher-training college in Vienna (Kundmanngasse), 1919–20; taught in rural Lower Austrian village schools: Trattenbach (1920–22), Hassbach (briefly, 1922), Puchberg am Schneeberg (1922–24), Otterthal (1924–26) [source: WP-LW]. IEP-W: "his approach was strict and unpopular, but apparently effective."
- He used corporal punishment, including on girls, which villagers thought went too far (Monk) [source: WP-LW].
- Haidbauer incident, April 1926 (Otterthal): he struck an 11-year-old pupil, Josef Haidbauer, two or three times on the head; the boy collapsed. A parent (Piribauer) tried to have him arrested; Wittgenstein resigned on 28 April 1926; proceedings began in May and the judge ordered a psychiatric report; nothing is known of the outcome [source: WP-LW]. In 1936 he returned to the village to apologise personally to children he had hit [source: WP-LW].
- Ramsey visited him at Puchberg in September 1923 (a fortnight working through the *Tractatus*) and again in 1924; corrections were made, incorporated in the 1933 second edition [source: SEP-FR §1–2; WP-LW].
- 1926: worked as a gardener at a monastery (Hütteldorf) and asked about becoming a monk [source: WP-LW].
- Haus Wittgenstein, Kundmanngasse, Vienna: commissioned by his sister Margaret (Gretl) Stonborough-Wittgenstein from the architect Paul Engelmann; Ludwig took over much of the design (1926–1928), concentrating on windows, doors, door handles and radiators; finished by December 1928; IEP-W: he "spent 1926-28 meticulously designing and building an austere house in Vienna for his sister Gretl." Wittgenstein's own verdict: it had "good manners" but lacked "primordial life" [source: WP-LW; WP Haus_Wittgenstein; IEP-W §1].
- Vienna Circle: Moritz Schlick first met him in 1927; 1927–28 he met small groups (Schlick, Waismann, sometimes Carnap and Feigl); from 1929 only Schlick and Waismann (conversations Dec 1929–Mar 1932, recorded by Waismann, published as *Ludwig Wittgenstein and the Vienna Circle*, 1979) [source: WP-LW]. "Wittgenstein was never a member of the Circle" (Hanfling); McGuinness doubts he ever attended a meeting of the Circle proper [source: WP-LW]. He sometimes read Tagore's poetry to them with his chair turned to the wall [source: WP-TLP].
- Carnap on him: his attitude was "much more similar to those of a creative artist than to those of a scientist; one might almost say, similar to those of a religious prophet or a seer" [source: WP-LW, quoting Carnap's autobiography].

## Life: Cambridge again, professor, war work, death (1929–1951)
- Returned to Cambridge in January 1929, "primarily to discuss philosophy with Ramsey" (reportedly) [source: SEP-FR §2; WP-LW]. Keynes: "Well, God has arrived. I met him on the 5.15 train." [source: WP-LW].
- PhD 1929 with the *Tractatus* as thesis; Ramsey nominal supervisor; examined by Russell and Moore; Wittgenstein clapped them on the shoulder: "Don't worry, I know you'll never understand it"; Moore's report (as recalled by Braithwaite): "I myself consider that this is a work of genius; but, even if I am completely mistaken and it is nothing of the sort, it is well above the standard required for the Ph.D. degree." [source: WP-LW; SEP-FR §1]. Then research fellow of Trinity; lectured from 1930 [source: WP-LW; IEP-W §1].
- 17 November 1929: "A Lecture on Ethics" to the Heretics Society, Cambridge [source: WP-LW]. In it: "the tendency of all men who ever tried to write or talk Ethics or Religion was to run against the boundaries of language" [source: IEP-W §3].
- Ramsey died 19 January 1930, aged 26 [source: SEP-FR §1].
- Dictated the *Blue Book* (1933–34) and *Brown Book* (1934–35); published together by Rush Rhees in 1958 [source: WP Blue_and_Brown_Books].
- 1936–37: in Norway working on the *Investigations* [source: WP-LW].
- Professor of philosophy at Cambridge in 1939, succeeding G. E. Moore; naturalised British on 12 April 1939 [source: IEP-W §1; SEP-MO; WP-LW].
- Alan Turing attended his 1939 lectures on the foundations of mathematics [source: WP-LW; SEP-W bibliography lists *Wittgenstein's Lectures on the Foundations of Mathematics, Cambridge, 1939*, ed. Cora Diamond, 1976].
- WWII: from autumn 1941 a dispensary porter at Guy's Hospital, London; from April 1943 to February 1944 a laboratory assistant at the Royal Victoria Infirmary, Newcastle [source: WP-LW]. IEP-W: "worked as a hospital porter in London and as a research technician in Newcastle."
- 25 October 1946: the Moral Sciences Club meeting with Karl Popper ("Wittgenstein's poker"); accounts vary [source: WP-LW].
- Resigned the chair in 1947 to write; worked in Ireland (Wicklow, Connemara) 1947–48; visited Norman Malcolm in Ithaca, NY, in 1949 [source: WP-LW; IEP-W §1]. G. H. von Wright succeeded him in the chair in 1948 [source: WP Georg_Henrik_von_Wright].
- Diagnosed with prostate cancer (late 1949); died in Cambridge at the home of his doctor, Edward Bevan, on 29 April 1951 [source: WP-LW infobox and §Death; WP-OC "his death on 29 April 1951"; IEP-W "died of prostate cancer in Cambridge in April 1951"]. Buried (Catholic rite) at the Ascension Parish Burial Ground, Cambridge [source: WP-LW].
- Last words: WP-LW: before losing consciousness on 28 April he told Mrs Joan Bevan: "Tell them I've had a wonderful life"; Norman Malcolm (*Memoir*, 1958) called it "a mysterious and strangely moving utterance." IEP-W reports the words flatly. SEP-W hedges: "Legend has it that, at his death in 1951, his last words were 'Tell them I've had a wonderful life' (Monk: 579)." Write as: reported by Malcolm and Monk as said to Joan Bevan the night before he died.
- Will of 29 January 1951: Rush Rhees named executor, Elizabeth Anscombe and G. H. von Wright literary administrators; the three are usually called his literary executors [source: WP-LW; WP Georg_Henrik_von_Wright; WP Rush_Rhees].
- Nachlass: 83 manuscripts, 46 typescripts and 11 dictations, about 20,000 pages; the Bergen Electronic Edition (Oxford UP, 2000), prepared at the Wittgenstein Archives, University of Bergen [source: WP-LW; SEP-W §6].

## Posthumous publications (selected, with editors and translators)
- *Philosophical Investigations*, 1953, Blackwell; edited by G. E. M. Anscombe and Rush Rhees; translated by Anscombe; Part I = 693 numbered remarks, "ready for printing in 1946" but withdrawn by Wittgenstein (SEP-W §1 says he prepared the final manuscript in 1945 and withdrew it "at the last minute"); Part II added by the editors [source: SEP-W §1, §3.2; WP-PI].
- Editions: 2nd 1958; 3rd 1973 (Anscombe); 50th-anniversary 2001; 4th edition 2009, Wiley-Blackwell, revised by P. M. S. Hacker and Joachim Schulte, crediting Anscombe, Hacker and Schulte as translators [source: WP-PI; SEP-W bibliography].
- In the 4th edition the old Part II is printed as "Philosophy of Psychology – A Fragment" (PPF), "now recognized as an essentially separate entity", described as "work in progress" [source: SEP-W §3.2, §5].
- Motto (from Nestroy): "The trouble about progress is that it always looks much greater than it really is" [source: SEP-W §3.8; WP-PI, which notes this is the 4th-edition translation].
- Preface: the new thoughts "could be seen in the right light only by contrast with and against the background of my old way of thinking" (WP-PI wording "be seen in the right light only by contrast with and against the background of my older way of thinking"); he once proposed publishing both books together [source: SEP-W §3.2, §3.7; WP-PI].
- Preface acknowledges Ramsey: "I was helped to realize these mistakes – to a degree which I myself am hardly able to estimate – by the criticism which my ideas encountered from Frank Ramsey, with whom I discussed them in innumerable conversations during the last two years of his life" [source: SEP-FR §2, quoting PI preface]. He also credited Piero Sraffa's criticism: "It is to this stimulus that I owe the most fruitful ideas of this book" [source: WP-PI].
- Other works: *Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics* (1956; eds. von Wright, Rhees, Anscombe; trans. Anscombe; notes 1937–44; received many negative reviews from logicians, e.g. Dummett, Bernays, Kreisel) [source: SEP-W bibliography; WP-RFM]; *Notebooks 1914–1916* (1961); *Blue and Brown Books* (1958); *Philosophical Remarks* (1964); *Zettel* (1967); *On Certainty* (1969; eds. Anscombe and von Wright; trans. Denis Paul and Anscombe; 676 numbered remarks) [source: SEP-W bibliography; WP-OC]; *Philosophical Grammar* (1974); *Remarks on Colour* (1977); *Culture and Value* (1980; ed. von Wright; trans. Peter Winch) [source: SEP-W bibliography].
- A pre-publication manuscript of the *Tractatus*, the *Prototractatus*, was found in Vienna in 1965 by von Wright (published 1971) [source: WP-TLP].

## The *Tractatus*: structure and the seven main propositions
- Seven basic propositions numbered 1–7; others numbered by decimals so that 1.1 comments on 1, 1.11 on 1.1, etc. [source: SEP-W §2.1; IEP-W §2]. Proposition 7 has no sub-propositions [source: WP-TLP]. WP-LW: the numbering may echo Tolstoy's *Gospel in Brief*.
- The seven, in both standard translations [source: SEP-W §2.1 table]:
  1. Ogden: "The world is everything that is the case." / Pears–McGuinness (P&M): "The world is all that is the case."
  2. Ogden: "What is the case, the fact, is the existence of atomic facts." / P&M: "What is the case—a fact—is the existence of states of affairs."
  3. Ogden: "The logical picture of the facts is the thought." / P&M: "A logical picture of facts is a thought."
  4. Ogden: "The thought is the significant proposition." / P&M: "A thought is a proposition with sense."
  5. Ogden: "Propositions are truth-functions of elementary propositions." / P&M: "A proposition is a truth-function of elementary propositions." (Both: "(An elementary proposition is a truth function of itself.)")
  6. The general form of a (truth-)function is [p̄, ξ̄, N(ξ̄)]. "This is the general form of (a) proposition."
  7. Ogden: "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent." / P&M: "What we cannot speak about we must pass over in silence." German: "Wovon man nicht sprechen kann, darüber muß man schweigen." [source: SEP-W; WP-TLP]
- From 6.4 to 6.54 the book turns to God, ethics, death, the will and "the mystical" [source: WP-TLP].

## The *Tractatus*: facts, objects and the picture theory (argument step by step)
Reconstruction assembled from SEP-W §2.1–2.2, SEP-TA and IEP-W §2. Section numbers are the book's; quoted wording as given in SEP-W (translation not always specified there; most quotations follow Pears–McGuinness).
- Step 1 — The world is the totality of facts, not of things (TLP 1, 1.1): "Wittgenstein sees the world as consisting of facts (1), rather than the traditional, atomistic conception of a world made up of objects" [source: SEP-W §2.1].
- Step 2 — Facts are existing states of affairs; states of affairs are combinations of objects; "Objects are simple" (2.02). An object's possible combinations with other objects are its logical form. States of affairs could have been otherwise; "the totality of states of affairs—actual and possible—makes up the whole of reality" [source: SEP-W §2.1].
- Step 3 — Objects "form the substance of the world" (2.021); they exist necessarily, the "fixed" element that every possible world shares: "The object is the fixed, the existing; the configuration is the changing" (2.0271). Argument for substance (2.0211–2.0212): if the world had no substance, whether a proposition had sense would depend on whether another proposition was true, and then it would be impossible to draw up a picture of the world [source: SEP-TA §3–3.1; SEP-W §2.4].
- Step 4 — We picture facts: "the picture is a model of reality" (2.12). Elements of the picture stand for objects; the way the elements are combined represents the way the objects are combined. Picture and pictured share a "pictorial form": "That is how a picture is attached to reality; it reaches right out to it" (2.1511) [source: SEP-W §2.1].
- Step 5 — A thought is a logical picture of facts (3); a proposition is a picture of reality (4.01, quoted in IEP-W as "A proposition is a picture of reality"). "Only the proposition has sense; only in the context of a proposition has a name meaning" (3.3) — two conditions of sense: the proposition must conform to logical form, and its ultimate elements (names) must have reference (*Bedeutung*) [source: SEP-W §2.1; IEP-W §2].
- Step 6 — Analysis must end in elementary propositions made of names in immediate combination: "It is obvious that in the analysis of propositions we must come to elementary propositions, which consist of names in immediate combination" (4.221); the *Tractatus* "contains no explicit argument" for this linguistic atomism [source: SEP-TA §2]. Everyday names like 'London' would not count as Tractarian names; whether objects are particulars only or include properties and relations is disputed (Anscombe and Copi vs Stenius and the Hintikkas) [source: SEP-TA §1].
- Step 7 — Bipolarity: every proposition with sense can be true and can be false; "The general form of a proposition is: This is how things stand" (4.5). All other propositions are truth-functions of elementary ones (5); Wittgenstein gave an early presentation of truth-tables [source: SEP-W §2.1; IEP-W §2].
- Step 8 — Tautologies and contradictions are "senseless" (*sinnlos*), not nonsense: they say nothing ("all the propositions of logic say the same thing, to wit nothing", 5.43 — IEP-W), are "not pictures of the reality … for the one allows every possible state of affairs, the other none" (4.462), and are recognised as true "in the symbol alone … and this fact contains in itself the whole philosophy of logic" (6.113). "My fundamental thought is that the logical constants do not represent" (4.0312, SEP-W wording; IEP-W: "My fundamental idea is that the 'logical constants' are not representatives") [source: SEP-W §2.2; IEP-W §2].
- Step 9 — Logical form cannot be said, only shown: "What can be shown, cannot be said" (4.1212); propositions "show the logical form of reality. They display it" (4.121) [source: IEP-W §2; SEP-W §2.2].
- Conclusion — What can be said is only what pictures possible facts: "The totality of true propositions is the total natural science" (4.11). Ethics, aesthetics, traditional metaphysics, the "metaphysical subject" and the truth in solipsism fall outside; Wittgenstein calls such propositions nonsensical (*unsinnig*), distinct from senseless tautologies [source: SEP-W §2.2].
- The self-undermining step: by the book's own standards the *Tractatus*'s own sentences ("There are objects", talk of "logical form") are nonsense; IEP-W: "the Tractatus pulls the rug out from under its own feet"; WP-TLP: "for the Tractatus to be true, it will necessarily have to be nonsense by self-application" [source: IEP-W §2; WP-TLP; SEP-W §2.3].
- Colour-exclusion problem (main internal objection): TLP 6.3751 says it is "logically impossible" for a point in the visual field to be both red and blue; this required elementary propositions to be logically independent, "a pillar of the Tractatus". Ramsey pressed the objection in his 1923 *Mind* review; Wittgenstein's 1929 recognition ("Some Remarks on Logical Form") that he could not sustain the claim "was to provoke the downfall of the Tractatus" [source: SEP-FR §2; SEP-W §5 (colour exclusion "credited with being the cause of Wittgenstein's abandonment of the rigid logical system in TLP")].

## The *Tractatus*: philosophy, limits of language, the mystical, the ladder
- Preface: "The book will … draw a limit to thinking, or rather—not to thinking, but to the expression of thoughts …. The limit can … only be drawn in language and what lies on the other side of the limit will be simply nonsense" [source: SEP-W §2.2]. WP-LW gives the fuller sentence: "...for, in order to draw a limit to thinking we should have to be able to think both sides of this limit (we should therefore have to be able to think what cannot be thought)."
- Preface: the book's value lies in "that thoughts are expressed in it" and "that it shows how little is achieved when these problems are solved" [source: IEP-W §2]. Debt: "indebted to Frege's great works and to the writings of my friend Mr Bertrand Russell" [source: IEP-W §10].
- "Most of the propositions and questions to be found in philosophical works are not false but nonsensical" (4.003, SEP-W wording); IEP-W (P&M): "Most of the propositions and questions of philosophers arise from our failure to understand the logic of our language" [source: SEP-W §2.3; IEP-W §2].
- "Philosophy aims at the logical clarification of thoughts" (4.112); "philosophy is not one of the natural sciences" (4.111) [source: IEP-W §4]. Philosophy "is not a theory, or a doctrine, but rather an activity" [source: SEP-W §2.3, paraphrasing 4.112].
- 5.6 "The limits of my language mean the limits of my world." [source: IEP-W §2, whose cited translation is Pears–McGuinness]. 5.62 "The world is my world" — IEP-W links this to Schopenhauer's "The world is my idea" [source: IEP-W §10].
- 6.4 "All propositions are of equal value" [source: SEP-W §2.3].
- 6.522 "There are, indeed, things that cannot be put into words. They make themselves manifest. They are what is mystical" (SEP-W gives: "things that cannot be put into words. They make themselves manifest. They are what is mystical") [source: SEP-W §2.2].
- 6.53 "The correct method in philosophy would really be the following: to say nothing except what can be said, i.e. propositions of natural science … and then whenever someone else wanted to say something metaphysical, to demonstrate to him that he had failed to give a meaning to certain signs in his propositions" [source: SEP-W §3.7].
- 6.54 (Pears–McGuinness): "My propositions serve as elucidations in the following way: anyone who understands me eventually recognizes them as nonsensical, when he has used them—as steps—to climb beyond them. (He must, so to speak, throw away the ladder after he has climbed up it.) He must transcend these propositions, and then he will see the world aright." German: "…Er muss sozusagen die Leiter wegwerfen, nachdem er auf ihr hinaufgestiegen ist." [source: WP-LAD, citing Klement's side-by-side edition, P&M translation; IEP-W §2 quotes the first sentence identically]. The German word rendered "nonsensical" is *unsinnig* [source: WP-LAD].
- Source of the ladder image: sources differ — WP-TLP says Wittgenstein "uses an analogy from Arthur Schopenhauer"; WP-LAD cites Alastair Hannay (Cambridge ed. of Kierkegaard's *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*, 2009) that Wittgenstein may have borrowed it from Kierkegaard's Climacus, and notes that Sextus Empiricus and Fritz Mauthner used it earlier. Present as possible echoes, not settled.

## Debate: how to read the *Tractatus* (traditional/ineffability vs resolute/"New Wittgenstein")
- The problem: 6.54 says the book's own propositions are nonsense; yet the book seems to point at something shown but unsayable ("the mystical") [source: SEP-W §2.4; WP-TLP].
- Traditional reading: "The traditional readings of the Tractatus accepted, with varying degrees of discomfort, the existence of that which is unsayable"; WP-TLP calls this the "Ineffable-Truths View": philosophical truths cannot be said but can be shown [source: SEP-W §2.4; WP-TLP]. P. M. S. Hacker is its leading defender and a named critic of the New Wittgenstein [source: WP-NW]; his reply to the resolute readers, "Was He Trying to Whistle It?", is in *The New Wittgenstein* (2000), pp. 353–388 [source: WS-3]. Hans-Johann Glock: the "plain nonsense" reading "is at odds with the external evidence, writings and conversations in which Wittgenstein states that the Tractatus is committed to the idea of ineffable insight" [source: WP-NW; WP-LW].
- Resolute reading: "More recent readings tend to take nonsense more seriously as exactly that—nonsense", taking 6.54 at its word and throwing away even the saying/showing distinction; the book "does not point at ineffable truths … but should lead us away from such temptations" [source: SEP-W §2.4]. Associated with Cora Diamond ("Throwing Away the Ladder", *Philosophy* 63, 1988) and James Conant; Conant and Diamond, "On Reading the Tractatus Resolutely", in Kölbel and Weiss (eds.), *Wittgenstein's Lasting Significance* (Routledge, 2004) [source: WP-TLP]. The label "resolute reading" or "The New Wittgenstein" dates from about 2000 [source: SEP-W §2.4], after the anthology *The New Wittgenstein*, eds. Alice Crary and Rupert Read (Routledge, 2000) [source: WP-NW]. Resolute readers rely on the "frame" of the book (Preface and 6.54) [source: WP-TLP].
- The resolute reading links nonsense, therapy, the ethical point of the book and continuity with the later work [source: SEP-W §2.4]. Others named with it: Alice Crary, Stanley Cavell, John McDowell, Warren Goldfarb, Thomas Ricketts, Hilary Putnam, Stephen Mulhall; critics: Hacker, Ian Proops, Genia Schönbaumsfeld [source: WP-NW]. "There is no unitary 'New Wittgenstein' interpretation" [source: WP-NW].
- WP-TLP also lists a third option, a "No-Truths-At-All View" (Morris and Dodd 2009) [source: WP-TLP].
- Ramsey's quip, often cited in this debate: "But what we can't say we can't say, and we can't whistle it either" — Ramsey, "General Propositions and Causality" (1929), printed in *The Foundations of Mathematics* (1931) [source: WS-6 (Wikiquote and secondary sources; text not read directly); SEP-FR (passage on facts) alludes to it: "if you can't say that facts exist, you can't whistle it either"]. Cora Diamond has an article "'We Can't Whistle It Either': Legend and Reality" (2010) [source: WS-6].

## The *Tractatus* and the Vienna Circle (and the "misreading")
- The Circle's "conception of logical empiricism was indebted to his Tractatus account of logic as tautologous, and his philosophy as concerned with logical syntax" [source: SEP-W §1]. The Circle extended "Wittgenstein's insight about logical truths to mathematical ones", treating both as tautological [source: SEP-VC §2.3].
- The Circle read the book "line by line" aloud over months (Schlick, Carnap) [source: WP-TLP].
- Verification: Wittgenstein discussed "The sense of a proposition is [the method of] its verification" with Schlick and Waismann on 22 December 1929 and 2 January 1930; Waismann's "Theses" presented this to the Circle as Wittgenstein's view; Wittgenstein "appears to have thought of his dictum also as a constitutive principle of meaning", whereas the Circle used it "mainly as a demarcation criterion against metaphysics" [source: SEP-VC §3.1 "Verificationism and the Critique of Metaphysics"].
- The misreading: Carnap "hailed the book as containing important insights but encouraged people to ignore the concluding sentences"; Wittgenstein: "I cannot imagine that Carnap should have so completely misunderstood the last sentences of the book and hence the fundamental conception of the entire book" [source: WP-TLP]. IEP-W §10: "Wittgenstein, though, said that it was what is not in the Tractatus that matters most."
- Caution on influence: SEP-VC: the literature "tends to suffer from an over-simplified conception of the latter, and an over-estimation of their commitment to the Tractarian program"; "by no means all members of the Vienna Circle sought to emulate Wittgenstein" [source: SEP-VC]. A. C. Grayling (via WP-LW): the Circle's basic views were formed before they met him; "it is no longer possible to think of the Tractatus as having inspired a philosophical movement". Hanfling (via WP-LW): his influence "at least as important as that of any of its members". This is a debate.
- He broke off with Carnap, believing Carnap had used his ideas without permission [source: WP-TLP].

## Ramsey's criticism and influence
- Ramsey's 1923 critical notice of the *Tractatus* in *Mind* "raised many serious objections", incl. colour exclusion [source: SEP-FR §2].
- At first "under the spell", Ramsey became disillusioned: 1924, "He is no good for my work" [source: SEP-FR §2].
- How much the later Wittgenstein owes Ramsey is disputed: from "literally none" of his key ideas (Kienzler 1997) to substantial influence (Glock, Marion, Methven, Misak, Sahlin); SEP-FR suggests "a confluence of ideas" [source: SEP-FR §2].
- SEP-P: "Wittgenstein's later thought also acquired a pragmatist flavour through conversations with Ramsey (Misak 2018; Boncompagni 2016; Goodman 2002), and his reading of James's *Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902)" [source: SEP-P §1.1].

## The middle period
- After 1929: "Some Remarks on Logical Form" (1929), "A Lecture on Ethics" (1929), *Philosophical Remarks* (1929–30), *Philosophical Grammar* (1932–33), *The Big Typescript* (1932–33), *Blue and Brown Books* (1933–35) [source: SEP-W §4].
- By 1931 he called his early work "dogmatic" ("On Dogmatism", in *Wittgenstein and the Vienna Circle*, p. 182) [source: SEP-W §3.1].
- Dating of the "middle Wittgenstein" varies: Hacker 1929–30; Hintikkas 1929; Kienzler 1931; Bouwsma 1933–35; Pichler 1930–35; Stern "late 1920s and early 1930s"; Schulte finds it already in TLP [source: SEP-W §4]. "Ironically, it is 'orthodox' interpreters like Baker and Hacker or Anthony Kenny that have brought the importance of the middle period to the fore; New Wittgensteinians disown it" [source: SEP-W §4].
- About half his work 1929–44 was on philosophy of mathematics; in 1943 he said his chief contribution lay there [source: SEP-W §4].
- Sraffa anecdote (Malcolm): Sraffa made a Neapolitan gesture and asked "What is the logical form of *that*?" — told by Malcolm; treat as anecdote [source: WP-PI].

## The *Investigations*: Augustine, use, language-games, family resemblance
- Style: numbered remarks, "sometimes jumping, in a sudden change, from one area to another" (Preface); dialogue with an interlocutor [source: SEP-W §3.7].
- §1 opens with Augustine's *Confessions* (I.8) on how he learned to speak; this gives "a particular picture of the essence of human language": "the words in language name objects" and "sentences are combinations of such names" [source: SEP-W §3.2, 4th-ed. wording]. Anscombe-tradition wording (WP-PI): "The individual words in a language name objects—sentences are combinations of such names. In this picture of language, we find the roots of the following idea: Every word has a meaning. This meaning is correlated with the word. It is the object for which the word stands."
- §2 builders: "The language is meant to serve for communication between a builder A and an assistant B. A is building with building-stones: there are blocks, pillars, slabs and beams. B has to pass the stones, in the order in which A needs them. For this purpose they use a language consisting of the words 'block', 'pillar', 'slab', 'beam'. A calls them out;—B brings the stone which he has learnt to bring at such-and-such a call.—Conceive this as a complete primitive language." [source: WP-LG, citing PI Blackwell 1958 (Anscombe 2nd ed.) §2]. SEP-W: the builders' game illustrates the limited part of the Augustinian picture that is correct while ignoring "the essential role of action in establishing meaning" [source: SEP-W §3.4].
- §11 tools: "the functions of words are as diverse as the functions of these objects" (tools in a toolbox) [source: SEP-W §3.3].
- §23: the word "language-game" is "used here to emphasize the fact that the speaking of language is part of an activity, or of a form of life"; list of language-games incl. reporting an event, forming and testing a hypothesis, making up a story, play-acting, guessing riddles, making a joke, translating, asking, thanking, praying (WP-PI list overlaps) [source: SEP-W §3.4, §3.6].
- §43 (two translations; both verified):
  - Anscombe (as quoted IEP-W §5 and WP-PI): "For a large class of cases—though not for all—in which we employ the word 'meaning' it can be defined thus: the meaning of a word is its use in the language."
  - 4th ed. Hacker–Schulte wording (as quoted SEP-W §3.3): "For a large class of cases of the employment of the word 'meaning'—though not for all—this word can be explained in this way: the meaning of a word is its use in the language."
  - IEP-W: "It is quite clear that here Wittgenstein is not offering the general theory that 'meaning is use,' as he is sometimes interpreted as doing." The main rival views he warns against: meaning as the object named, and meaning as a psychological feeling [source: IEP-W §5].
- Already in the *Blue Book* (1933): "if we had to name anything which is the life of the sign, we should have to say that it was its use" (BB 4) [source: SEP-W §3.3].
- §65–67 family resemblance: no definition of "game" or of language: we cannot find "what is common to all these activities and what makes them into language" (§65) [source: SEP-W §3.4]. §66: "consider for example the proceedings that we call 'games' … look and see whether there is anything common to all"; "Don't think, but look!"; result: "we see a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing: sometimes overall similarities." §67: "I can think of no better expression to characterize these similarities than 'family resemblances'; for the various resemblances between members of a family: build, features, colour of eyes, gait, temperament, etc. etc. overlap and criss-cross in the same way.—And I shall say: 'games' form a family." [source: WP-FR (citing PI, Blackwell 2001 = Anscombe 3rd ed.); SEP-W §3.3–3.4]. Thread image (§67): "the strength of the thread does not reside in the fact that some one fibre runs through its whole length, but in the overlapping of many fibres" [source: WP-FR].
- Earlier uses of "family resemblance": WP-FR says the term occurs in Schopenhauer (*World as Will and Representation* §§17, 27, 28) and that Wittgenstein may have taken it from Nietzsche (Sluga 2006) — WP-only, use cautiously.
- Craving for generality: *Blue Book* names the philosopher's "craving for generality", rooted in "preoccupation with the method of science", "the real source of metaphysics" (BB 18) [source: SEP-W §3.7].
- Form of life: used "sparingly—five times in the Investigations"; readings range from culturally relative to a shared human background (§206: "shared human behaviour is the system of reference by means of which we interpret an unknown language") [source: SEP-W §3.6].
- §241–242: "agreement not in opinions, but rather in form of life"; "not only agreement in definitions but also (odd as it may sound) in judgments" [source: SEP-W §3.6].
- "If a lion could talk, we could not understand him" (WP-PI wording; PPF/Part II, not section-numbered in Part I) [source: WP-PI; IEP-W §5 paraphrases].

## The *Investigations*: philosophy as therapy, grammar
- §90: "Our investigation is a grammatical one. Such an investigation sheds light on our problem by clearing misunderstandings away. Misunderstandings concerning the use of words, caused, among other things, by certain analogies between the forms of expression in different regions of language." [source: IEP-W §4, Anscombe translation].
- §97: if the words "language", "experience", "world" have a use, "it must be as humble a one as that of the words 'table', 'lamp', 'door'" [source: SEP-W §3.8].
- §109: "All explanation must disappear, and description alone must take its place" [source: SEP-W §3.7]. The famous sentence from the same remark, "Philosophy is a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language", is the wording most widely quoted (WS-1, WS-2 secondary sources); German *Kampf* / *Verhexung*. The exact translator of this wording could not be confirmed from a primary edition (it is believed to be Anscombe's; no source read this session states that). A variant "...by means of our language" also circulates. See Quotations.
- §116 (WP-LW paraphrase): bring words back "from their metaphysical to their everyday use"; WP-LW: problems arise "when language is forced from its proper home into a metaphysical environment"; "language gone on holiday" (WP-LW) [source: WP-LW].
- §107 "rough ground" vs "slippery ice": "We have got on to slippery ice where there is no friction … Back to the rough ground!" — WP-LW paraphrases ("frictionless ice", "rough ground"); exact wording not verified.
- §119: "The results of philosophy are the discovery of some piece of plain nonsense and of bumps that the understanding has got by running up against the limits of language" [source: SEP-PL §1.2].
- §123: "A philosophical problem has the form: 'I don't know my way about'" [source: SEP-W §3.7].
- §126: "Philosophy just puts everything before us, and neither explains nor deduces anything.—Since everything lies open to view there is nothing to explain" [source: SEP-W §3.7].
- §127: "The work of the philosopher consists in marshalling reminders for a particular purpose" [source: SEP-W §3.7].
- §133: "the philosophical problems should completely disappear"; "The real discovery is the one that enables me to break off philosophizing when I want to.—The one that gives philosophy peace, so that it is no longer tormented by questions which bring itself in question"; "there is not a single philosophical method, though there are indeed methods, different therapies, as it were" [source: SEP-W §3.7; SEP-PL §1.1]. Rhees reports Wittgenstein saying: "In my book I say that I am able to leave off with a problem in philosophy when I want to. But that's a lie; I can't" (Rhees 1984, p. 219 n. 7) [source: SEP-PL §1.1].
- §309: "What is your aim in philosophy?—To show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle." SEP-W quotes "to show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle" (PI 309); German "Der Fliege den Ausweg aus dem Fliegenglas zeigen" [source: SEP-W §3.7; WP-PI]. (The question "What is your aim in philosophy?" is from memory — use only the verified clause.)
- §371, §373: "Essence is expressed by grammar … Grammar tells what kind of object anything is. (Theology as grammar.)" [source: SEP-W §3.6].
- "A whole cloud of philosophy condensed into a drop of grammar" (PI 1953, p. 222, i.e. Part II/PPF) [source: SEP-W §3.6].
- §464: "What I want to teach is: to pass from unobvious nonsense to obvious nonsense" [source: SEP-PL §1.2].
- 1931 remark (*Big Typescript*/*Culture and Value* material): "Language sets everyone the same traps; it is an immense network of easily accessible wrong turnings … What I have to do then is erect signposts at all the junctions where there are wrong turnings" [source: IEP-W §4].
- Ordinary-language critics called this, derogatively, "therapeutic positivism" (Farrell 1946) [source: IEP-OLP].

## Rule-following (PI §§138/185–242) — argument step by step
- Scope: WP-PI says rules are discussed §§138–242; SEP-W says "mainly in PI 185–243"; SEP-PL calls §§185–242 the "earlier sections on rules" [source: SEP-W §3.5; WP-PI; SEP-PL §2]. Use "roughly §§185–242".
- §185 example: a pupil told to continue the series "+2" writes beyond 1000 "1000, 1004, 1008, 1012", and when corrected says "But I did go on in the same way" [source: SEP-W §3.5].
- Step 1 — A rule seems to determine its own future applications, as if grasping it were grasping an abstract object or having a mental item that contains all applications (the "Platonistic and mentalistic pictures") [source: SEP-W §3.5].
- Step 2 — But any finite set of examples or instructions is compatible with many continuations; any formulation of the rule ("add 2") can itself be interpreted in more than one way [source: SEP-W §3.5; WP-PLA §Following a rule].
- Step 3 — If what fixes the rule is an interpretation, the interpretation itself needs interpreting; §198: "any interpretation still hangs in the air along with what it interprets, and cannot give it any support" [source: IEP-K §4 (quoting PI §198, Anscombe)].
- Step 4 — §201 paradox. Anscombe wording (verified via SEP-W, WP-PLA and WP-PI): "This was our paradox: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule. The answer was: if everything can be made out to accord with the rule, then it can also be made out to conflict with it. And so there would be neither accord nor conflict here." (4th ed., as quoted in SEP-PL §4: "…because every course of action can be brought into accord with the rule.")
- Step 5 — Wittgenstein's own response in the same remark: "It can be seen that there is a misunderstanding here … What this shews is that there is a way of grasping a rule which is *not* an *interpretation*, but which is exhibited in what we call 'obeying the rule' and 'going against it' in actual cases" [source: IEP-K §4 quoting McDowell's use of PI §201; WP-PLA].
- Conclusion — following a rule is a practice: §199 "to understand a language means to be master of a technique"; "to obey a rule, to make a report, to give an order, to play a game of chess, are customs (uses, institutions)"; and §202 "'obeying a rule' is a practice" (the §202 sentence: WP-PLA paraphrase "following a rule is a practice"; exact wording not verified). "Thinking one is following a rule is not the same as following it" [source: IEP-K §3 quoting PI §199; WP-PLA].
- §206 / §241–242 agreement in form of life underlies rule-following [source: SEP-W §3.6; SEP-PL §2].

## Kripke's sceptical reading ("Kripkenstein") vs its critics — step by step
- Saul Kripke, *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (Oxford: Blackwell, 1982) [source: SEP-PL bibliography; IEP-K intro]. Fogelin (1976) had earlier read §201 as a sceptical paradox with a sceptical solution [source: SEP-W §3.5].
- Kripke disclaims exegesis: it is "Wittgenstein's argument as it struck Kripke, as it presented a problem for him" (Kripke 1982, 5); "probably many of my formulations and re-castings of the argument are done in a way Wittgenstein would not himself approve" [source: IEP-K intro]. Hence the nickname "Kripkenstein", used "facetiously" by critics [source: IEP-K intro; WP-PI]. Kripke calls the paradox "the most radical and original sceptical problem that philosophy has seen to date" [source: WP-LW].
- Step 1 (the challenge): I have never added numbers above 57. Asked "68 + 57", I answer 125. A "bizarre sceptic" (1982: 8) proposes that by "plus" I meant "quus": x quus y = x + y if x, y < 57, otherwise 5. Kripke grants the hypothesis is "wild", but if false "there must be some fact about my past usage that can be cited to refute it" (1982: 9) [source: SEP-RF §2; IEP-K §1].
- Step 2 (the rules of the game): the sceptic grants perfect memory and access to all facts about my mind and behaviour; any meaning-fact must (a) single out addition rather than quaddition and (b) "show how I am justified in giving the answer '125'" (1982: 11) — the extensionality and normativity conditions [source: SEP-RF §2–2.2; IEP-K §1].
- Step 3 (candidates fail): past behaviour (finite, fits quus); a general instruction (itself interpretable deviantly); dispositions (finite; can include systematic mistakes; and descriptive, not normative: "The relation of meaning and intention to future action is normative, not descriptive" (1982: 37)); a special introspectible quale; a mental image; a primitive irreducible state ("completely mysterious", 1982: 51); a Fregean sense [source: SEP-RF §2; IEP-K §2].
- Step 4 (sceptical conclusion): "there is no fact about me that distinguishes my meaning a definite function by 'plus' … and my meaning nothing at all" (1982: 21); taken with a truth-conditional view this yields "the entire idea of meaning vanishes into thin air" (1982: 22), which Kripke calls "insane and intolerable" (60) and "incredible and self-defeating" (71) [source: SEP-RF §2; SEP-PL §4].
- Step 5 (sceptical solution, modelled on Hume on causation): replace truth conditions for "Jones means addition by 'plus'" with assertibility conditions — we may say it when his answers agree with the community's "in enough cases" (1982: 92); the grocer and "five red apples" example [source: SEP-RF §3.1; IEP-K §3; SEP-PL §4].
- Step 6 (private language as corollary): "if one person is considered in isolation, the notion of a rule as guiding the person who adopts it can have no substantive content" (1982: 89); "The impossibility of private language emerges as a corollary of [Wittgenstein's] sceptical solution of his own paradox" (1982: 68) [source: IEP-K §3; SEP-PL §4].
- Objection 1 (exegetical, Baker and Hacker, *Scepticism, Rules and Language*, Oxford: Blackwell, 1984): Wittgenstein "throughout his life found philosophical scepticism nonsensical"; the §201 paradox reveals a misunderstanding: "no interpretation, i.e. no rule for the application of a rule, can satisfy us … For each interpretation generates the same problem" (1984: 13); Kripke's argument "is not a sceptical problem but an absurdity" (1984: 5); and practice requires regularity over time, not many agents: "nothing in this discussion involves any commitment to a multiplicity of agents" (1984: 20) [source: IEP-K §4]. (IEP-K misspells them "Backer and Hacker" in its in-text citations.)
- Objection 2 (McDowell, "Wittgenstein on Following a Rule", 1984): Kripke ignores the second half of §201; the paradox rests on the assumption that understanding requires interpretation; reject it and there is no paradox and no need for a sceptical solution; "the right response to the paradox, Wittgenstein in effect tells us, is not to accept it but to correct the misunderstanding on which it depends" (1984 [1998: 229]); rule-following is initiation into "a communal custom" (1984: 352) — so McDowell is communitarian but factualist [source: SEP-RF §5; IEP-K §4].
- Objection 3 (McGinn, *Wittgenstein on Meaning*, 1984; Blackburn 1984): individualist readings; a practice needs repeated occasions, not a community; "lack of a theory of a phenomenon is not in itself a good reason to doubt the existence of it" (McGinn 1984: 161) [source: IEP-K §4].
- Also: Boghossian (1989) argues irrealism about meaning is incoherent; the majority view is that Kripke's own Wittgenstein is a semantic non-factualist (McGinn, Wright, Boghossian) [source: SEP-RF §3]. Defenders of Kripke: Martin Kusch, *A Sceptical Guide to Meaning and Rules* (2006) [source: WP-LW; SEP-PL bibliography].
- SEP-PL's verdict (Candlish & Wrisley, a view, not consensus): Kripke's account "is thus vitiated by his unargued reliance on ideas which Wittgenstein argued against", though it "gave new life to the debate over the community view" [source: SEP-PL §4]. IEP-W: Kripke's theory is "doubtful as an interpretation of Wittgenstein", yet "one of the best ways into Wittgenstein's philosophy is to study Kripke and his Wittgensteinian critics" [source: IEP-W §6].

## The private language argument (PI §§243–315) — step by step, objections and replies
- Location: the idea is introduced at §243; the main attack is §§244–271 (esp. from §256); ramifications pursued to §315 [source: SEP-PL §1; WP-PLA].
- §243 definition (4th ed., SEP-PL): "The words of this language are to refer to what only the speaker can know — to his immediate private sensations. So another person cannot understand the language." Not a personal code, which could in principle be deciphered; a language "necessarily comprehensible only to its single originator" [source: SEP-PL intro]. SEP-PL notes Anscombe's translation of §243 loses a contrast present in the German [source: SEP-PL §3.1].
- Wittgenstein "never employs the phrase 'private language argument'" [source: SEP-PL §1; SEP-W §3.5].
- Target: a way of thinking found from Descartes (inward identification of experiences, *Principles* I.9), Locke and Mill (problem of other minds) to Fodor's language of thought; most explicitly Russell's "logically perfect language", whose names would be "very largely private to one speaker" ("The Philosophy of Logical Atomism", lecture 2) [source: SEP-PL §2; WP-PLA].
- Step 1 (§§244–255): sensations as ordinarily talked of are not private in the relevant senses; we learn sensation words as replacements for natural expressions of pain [source: SEP-PL §3.1].
- Step 2 (§256–257): one cannot reach the idea by subtracting all expression from our language; mere "mental association" does not make something a name (cf. ostensive definition, §§33–35); naming needs "a place for the new word" [source: SEP-PL §3.1].
- Step 3 (§258, the diary): I associate a sensation with the sign "S" and write it in a diary each time the sensation occurs; "a definition of the sign cannot be formulated"; I concentrate my attention on the sensation. Anscombe-tradition wording (WP-PLA): "A definition surely serves to establish the meaning of a sign.—Well, that is done precisely by the concentrating of my attention; for in this way I impress on myself the connexion between the sign and the sensation.—But 'I impress it on myself' can only mean: this process brings it about that I remember the connexion *right* in the future. But in the present case I have no criterion of correctness. One would like to say: whatever is going to seem right to me is right. And that only means that here we can't talk about 'right'." (last two sentences as quoted by IEP-K §3) [source: WP-PLA; IEP-K §3; SEP-PL §3.2].
- Step 4: if "S" means "whatever I now have", then "whatever is going to seem correct to me is correct", and the distinction between being right and seeming right — hence between meaning and truth — collapses [source: SEP-PL §3.4 "First Answer"].
- Step 5: if "S" means "the sensation I named 'S' before", appeal to memory presupposes there was a determinate original correlation to remember; §265: "As if someone were to buy several copies of today's morning paper to assure himself that what it said was true"; "justification consists in appealing to something independent" [source: SEP-PL §3.4; WP-PLA].
- Step 6 (§§270–271, manometer): if "S" is tied to a public check (rising blood pressure), it works — but then the meaning is public and the private object is idle [source: SEP-PL §3.4 "Closing Stages"].
- Conclusion: a language in principle intelligible only to its user is impossible, because its user could not establish meanings for its signs [source: SEP-PL §1]. §261: "the use of [a] word stands in need of a justification which everybody understands" [source: SEP-W §3.5].
- Beetle in the box (§293): "Suppose everyone had a box with something in it: we call it a 'beetle'. No one can look into anyone else's box, and everyone says he knows what a beetle is only by looking at *his* beetle." The thing in the box "has no place in the language-game at all; not even as a *something*: for the box might even be empty"; conclusion: "if we construe the grammar of the expression of sensation on the model of 'object and designation' the object drops out of consideration as irrelevant" [source: WP-PLA; WP-PI; IEP-W §6 quotes the last sentence; WS-4 for the first sentence (Anscombe 1953/1958), with 4th ed. reading "just by looking" for "only by looking"]. ("has no place in the language-game at all…" — from memory, not verified; use only the first two sentences and the final conclusion.)
- §304: a sensation is "not a *something*, but not a *nothing* either!" — IEP-W §6 paraphrases ("A pain is not 'a something' … but it is no more 'a nothing'"); exact wording not verified.
- Objection 1 (orthodox, memory scepticism): the argument rests on the fallibility of memory, but memory is fallible in public language too, so the argument would rule out public language [source: SEP-PL §3.3 (quoting Kenny 1973: 191–2); WP-PLA].
- Reply: Kenny showed the issue is remembering a *meaning*, not infallibly re-identifying sensations; Candlish & Wrisley go further: the problem is whether a private ostensive definition was ever made, not whether it is remembered; so no threat to public language [source: SEP-PL §3.3–3.5].
- Objection 2 (Robinson Crusoe): the "community view" would make a lifelong solitary language-user impossible, which seems conceivable [source: SEP-PL §3.5, §4.1]. Malcolm defended the community view; Baker and Hacker opposed it [source: SEP-PL §4.1; IEP-W §6]. Reply (SEP-PL, Canfield): the argument requires only that language be *potentially* shareable; a Crusoe living in a world independent of his impressions could make determinate definitions [source: SEP-PL §3.5, §4.1]. IEP-W: the Rhees–Ayer exchange on this (1954) — "Unfortunately for Wittgenstein, Ayer is generally considered to have won" [source: IEP-W §6].
- Objection 3 (behaviourism/verificationism): IEP-W: "This does not, as has been alleged, make Wittgenstein a behaviorist. He does not deny the existence of sensations" [source: IEP-W §6; SEP-PL §1].
- Interpretive debate: is the claim that a private language is false, or that the idea is nonsense? Resolute/Pyrrhonian readings (Mulhall 2007) vs substantial readings; Baker (1998) reads it as nonsense masquerading as a possibility [source: SEP-PL §1.1–1.2].

## Seeing-as and the duck-rabbit
- In Part II / PPF xi Wittgenstein distinguishes two uses of "see"; "noticing an aspect": "I observe a face, and then suddenly notice its likeness to another. I see that it has not changed; and yet I see it differently. I call this experience 'noticing an aspect'" (PPF 113) [source: SEP-W §5].
- The duck-rabbit figure first appeared unattributed in *Fliegende Blätter*, 23 October 1892; used by the psychologist Joseph Jastrow; made famous by Wittgenstein's use of it in the *Investigations* to distinguish "seeing that" from "seeing as" [source: WP Rabbit–duck_illusion; WP-PI].
- Kuhn: N. R. Hanson (*Patterns of Discovery*, 1958) extended Wittgenstein's discussion to theory-ladenness; Thomas Kuhn, like Hanson, used the duck-rabbit for paradigm change [source: WP Rabbit–duck_illusion]; Kuhn uses Wittgenstein's family resemblance in ch. V of *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962), p. 45 of 4th ed. [source: WP-FR]. So a Kuhn link is supportable but only via Wikipedia; phrase as "Kuhn borrowed both the duck-rabbit and the idea of family resemblance".

## *On Certainty*: hinge propositions vs Moore
- Written in four stints over the last eighteen months of his life, the last six weeks before death producing over half; begun mid-1949 at Norman Malcolm's instigation; 676 remarks; ed. Anscombe and von Wright, trans. Denis Paul and Anscombe, 1969 [source: WP-OC; IEP-W §8].
- Target: G. E. Moore's "A Defence of Common Sense" (1925) and "Proof of an External World" (1939) — "Here is one hand … and here is another" [source: WP-OC; IEP-W §8].
- Against Moore: "Moore's mistake lies in this—countering the assertion that one cannot know that, by saying 'I do know it'" (OC §521) [source: IEP-MO (section on common sense), quoting Wittgenstein 1969].
- Against the sceptic: "If you tried to doubt everything you would not get as far as doubting anything. The game of doubting itself presupposes certainty" (OC §115); "A doubt that doubted everything would not be a doubt" (OC §450) [source: SEP-CE; WP-OC].
- Hinges: "the questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn" (OC §341) [source: SEP-CE]. River-bed image: such certainties are like the bed of a river, which can shift [source: IEP-W §8].
- "The difficulty is to realize the groundlessness of our believing" (OC §166) [source: SEP-CE]. "Giving grounds … comes to an end … it is our *acting* which lies at the bottom of the language-game" (OC §204) [source: WP-OC].
- Debate: are hinges epistemically foundational, naturally certain, or logically exempt from doubt? Is the target Cartesian or radical scepticism? Is Wittgenstein even unequivocally anti-sceptical? [source: SEP-W §5]. Baron Reed (SEP-CE): hard to see the certainty as epistemic rather than psychological (cites Pritchard 2005, Coliva 2010, 2012); "hinge epistemology" today: Coliva (2015), Pritchard (2016) [source: SEP-CE; IEP-C].
- "Third Wittgenstein": the distinct post-*Investigations* phase (1946–51: PPF, *On Certainty*, *Remarks on Colour*, etc.), so named "initially by Danièle Moyal-Sharrock" (ed., *The Third Wittgenstein: The Post-Investigations Works*, Ashgate, 2004) [source: SEP-W §5 and bibliography].

## Continuity vs discontinuity (a debate)
- Orthodox two-stage view: early (*Tractatus*) vs later (*Investigations*), with a sharp break; "for about two decades since the publication of Philosophical Investigations in 1953 there seemed to be wide agreement" [source: SEP-W intro, §3.8].
- Wittgenstein himself: the *Investigations* contains "grave mistakes in what I set out in that first book" (Preface, quoted SEP-FR) and suggested publishing both together "to make the contrast obvious" [source: SEP-W §3.7].
- Continuity readings: the anti-theoretical, therapeutic conception of philosophy as critique of language is the "fundamental continuity" [source: SEP-W §3.7]; IEP-W §9: "the differences between his early and late work can be exaggerated"; "The main change may have been one of method and style." Resolute readers stress continuity [source: SEP-W §2.4; WP-NW].
- Discontinuities named by IEP-W: the *Tractatus*'s concern with the general form of the proposition vs the later attack on "the craving for generality"; central problems vs none central; style [source: IEP-W §9].
- More stages: "middle Wittgenstein" and "post-later" (third) Wittgenstein [source: SEP-W intro, §4, §5].

## Influence, reception, critics
- Two-fold influence on analytic philosophy: early on the Vienna Circle; later on Oxford "ordinary language" philosophy and Cambridge (Hacker's view) [source: WP-LW; IEP-W §10].
- Ordinary language philosophy: "generally associated with the (later) views of Ludwig Wittgenstein, and with the work done by the philosophers of Oxford University between approximately 1945-1970", but began in Cambridge with his 1929 return; his pupils Ambrose, Lazerowitz, Malcolm, Gasking, von Wright, Black, Bouwsma, Anscombe spread his thought; Wittgenstein "never accept[ed] any label for his work, let alone 'Ordinary Language' philosophy" [source: IEP-OLP §1–2].
- G. E. M. Anscombe (born Limerick 18 March 1919; died Cambridge 5 January 2001): met Wittgenstein at Cambridge in 1942; one of his closest friends; chief translator after his death; *Intention* (1957); "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958); held his former Cambridge chair from 1970 [source: SEP-AN]; *An Introduction to Wittgenstein's Tractatus* (1959) [source: SEP-W bibliography].
- Peter Winch (1926–1997), *The Idea of a Social Science and its Relation to Philosophy* (1958), an attack on positivism in the social sciences drawing on the later Wittgenstein (and Collingwood); one of the "Swansea Wittgensteinians" with Rush Rhees, R. F. Holland and D. Z. Phillips; never met Wittgenstein; translated *Culture and Value* (1980) [source: WP Peter_Winch].
- Stanley Cavell (1926–2018): *Must We Mean What We Say?* (1969), *The Claim of Reason: Wittgenstein, Skepticism, Morality, and Tragedy* (1979); showed that "description", "ordinary" and "usage" are "not as innocuous as they had seemed"; Cavell-inspired readers see the whole effort as "ethical, cultural, or even political" [source: SEP-W §3.8; WP Stanley_Cavell].
- Baker and Hacker's analytical commentary on the *Investigations* (vol. 1, 1980; 4 vols, vols 3–4 by Hacker alone) "advocated and developed the orthodox reading", centred on "grammar" and surveyable representation (*Übersicht*, PI 122) [source: SEP-W §3.8].
- Rush Rhees (1905–1989), co-editor of PI with Anscombe [source: WP Rush_Rhees]; G. H. von Wright (1916–2003), succeeded him at Cambridge 1948 [source: WP Georg_Henrik_von_Wright].
- 1999 poll (Douglas Lackey, *The Philosophical Forum* 30(4)) of American college teachers ranked the *Investigations* the most important book of 20th-century philosophy [source: WP-LW; WP-PI].
- Russell's later dismissal (*My Philosophical Development*, 1959, pp. 216–17): "I have not found in Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* anything that seemed to me interesting and I do not understand why a whole school finds important wisdom in its pages … The later Wittgenstein, on the contrary, seems to have grown tired of serious thinking and to have invented a doctrine which would make such an activity unnecessary … if it is true, philosophy is, at best, a slight help to lexicographers, and at worst, an idle tea-table amusement." [source: WP-LW, WP-PI].
- Wittgenstein on Russell's books (to Drury): those on mathematical logic should be bound in red and read by all students, those on ethics and politics in blue "and no one should be allowed to read them" [source: WP-LW].
- Ernest Gellner, *Words and Things: A Critical Account of Linguistic Philosophy and a Study in Ideology* (London: Gollancz, 1959), with an approving foreword by Russell, attacked "linguistic philosophy" — Wittgenstein, Austin, Ryle, Flew, Strawson; Ryle refused to have it reviewed in *Mind*, provoking a correspondence in *The Times* (10–23 November 1959) [source: WP Ernest_Gellner; WP-PI].
- Michael Dummett, Paul Bernays and Georg Kreisel reviewed *Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics* negatively; his remarks on Gödel are widely read as a misunderstanding (Floyd and Putnam 2000 dissent) [source: WP-RFM].
- Quentin Skinner's contextualism drew on Wittgenstein and Austin [already in Periergia's Intellectual History chapter; not re-verified here].

## Other Wittgenstein biography relevant to his ethics and religion
- IEP-W §3: four themes in his ethics/religion: value not found in the world; living rightly as acceptance; seeing the world as a miracle; "the solution is the disappearance of the problem". He said he could not "help seeing every problem from a religious point of view" (WP-LW) [source: IEP-W §3; WP-LW].
- He attended the funeral of Otto Weininger and admired *Sex and Character* (1903) while saying its arguments were wrong; "if you just add a '~' to the whole book it says an important truth" (letter to Moore, 23 Aug 1931) [source: WP-LW].
- Schopenhauer: read as a teenager at Gretl's suggestion; adopted his epistemological idealism, then Frege's realism; later: "One could call Schopenhauer a quite *crude* mind.... Where real depth starts, his finishes." [source: WP-LW]. IEP-W §10: "most influenced by the work of Schopenhauer, Russell and Frege".

## Debates
- How to read the *Tractatus*: traditional/ineffability (Hacker, Glock) vs resolute/New Wittgenstein (Diamond, Conant, Crary) vs a "no-truths-at-all" view [source: SEP-W §2.4; WP-TLP; WP-NW].
- Realism of the *Tractatus*: realist readings ("Objects form the substance of the world") vs linguistically oriented readings giving priority to the symbolism [source: SEP-W §2.4].
- Is the *Tractatus* an ethical book? Von Ficker letter vs the bulk of logical construction; "existentialist" readers (Engelmann, Drury, Janik and Toulmin) stress mysticism [source: SEP-W §2.4, §3.8].
- Continuity vs break between early and later; how to date the middle period; whether there is a "third Wittgenstein" [source: SEP-W §3.8, §4, §5; IEP-W §9].
- Kripke's sceptical reading vs Baker & Hacker, McGinn, Blackburn (individualist critics) and McDowell, Peacocke (communitarian critics); Kusch defends Kripke [source: IEP-K §4; SEP-RF; WP-LW].
- Private language: memory-scepticism (orthodox) vs meaning/definition readings (Kenny; Candlish & Wrisley); community view (Malcolm) vs potential sociality (Baker & Hacker, Canfield); false or nonsense? (Baker 1998, Mulhall 2007) [source: SEP-PL].
- Hinges: epistemic vs psychological vs grammatical certainty [source: SEP-W §5; SEP-CE].
- Was the Vienna Circle inspired by the *Tractatus*? Hanfling vs Grayling; SEP-VC warns against over-estimation [source: WP-LW; SEP-VC].
- Ramsey's share in the later philosophy: Kienzler (none) vs Misak, Marion, Methven, Sahlin (substantial) [source: SEP-FR §2].
- Forms of life: relativist vs universal readings [source: SEP-W §3.6].

## Consistency with published Periergia texts (checked)
- *History of Philosophy* overview (§IX): says the later Wittgenstein "repudiated his earlier self — meaning is use, language is a motley of games, philosophy a therapy for bewitchment by grammar". Tension: SEP-W and IEP-W present the early/late break as a live debate (IEP-W: differences "can be exaggerated"), and IEP-W says §43 is not a general theory "meaning is use" ("for a large class of cases"). The chapter should present continuity vs discontinuity as a debate and quote §43's qualifier; not a factual error in the overview, but simplified. Overview's date "*Tractatus*, 1921" and "*Philosophical Investigations*, 1953" are correct.
- *What Is Philosophy?*: "Wittgenstein treated it as therapy for confusions caused by ordinary language". Tension: in the later work ordinary language is in order; confusion comes from "misunderstandings concerning the use of words, caused, among other things, by certain analogies between the forms of expression in different regions of language" (PI §90, IEP-W) and from taking words out of their everyday use (WP-LW, PI §116). Suggest the Wittgenstein chapter phrase it as "confusions that arise when we are misled by the forms of our language", without contradicting the earlier chapter outright.
- *American Pragmatism*: "Frank Ramsey developed Peirce's ideas … through him, Misak argues, Wittgenstein's later thought acquired 'a pragmatist flavour' (see *Wittgenstein*)." Consistent with SEP-P, though SEP-P attributes the claim to Misak 2018, Boncompagni 2016 and Goodman 2002 and adds his reading of James's *Varieties*. Date wrinkle: SEP-FR dates Misak's *Cambridge Pragmatism* 2016, SEP-P 2018. The chapter should honour the cross-reference and note that the extent of Ramsey's influence is disputed (SEP-FR).
- *Schopenhauer and Kierkegaard*: says Schopenhauer "shaped … the young Wittgenstein" and that Wittgenstein called Kierkegaard "by far the most profound thinker" of the century (from SEP Kierkegaard; not re-read here). Consistent with IEP-W §10 and WP-LW (teenage Schopenhauer reading; learned Danish to read Kierkegaard). Add nuance only: Wittgenstein later called Schopenhauer "a quite crude mind" (WP-LW). The ladder image is variously traced to Schopenhauer, Kierkegaard or Sextus (WP-TLP, WP-LAD).
- *Augustine*: "*Philosophical Investigations* opens with Augustine on learning language" — consistent (SEP-W §3.2).
- *Classical Indian Schools*: Arnold compares Kumārila with Wittgenstein's criticism of Augustine (PI §32) — consistent; possible cross-reference.
- *Mahāyāna Philosophy*: "therapeutic" Wittgensteinian readings of Nāgārjuna — consistent; possible cross-reference (only genuinely cross-cultural link available).
- *Descartes*: the published chapter does not mention Wittgenstein. SEP-PL treats Descartes's inward identification of experience as a main target (Kenny); the 1947 Jowett Society exchange on the *cogito* with Prichard (WP-LW) is a possible anecdote. No contradiction.
- History subject, *Intellectual History*: Skinner drew on Wittgenstein and Austin — consistent.
