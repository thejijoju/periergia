## 1. The recovery story: canon, exclusion and the historians who recovered these women

- SEP-FH identifies two problems for feminist historians: "the problem of historical exclusion" (a tradition "that believes that there are no women philosophers and, if there are any, they are unimportant") and canonical philosophers' derogatory views of women. [source: SEP-FH intro]
- *The Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (ed. Edwards, 1967), with articles on over 900 philosophers, had no entry for Wollstonecraft, Arendt or Beauvoir, and by its index did not mention Wollstonecraft or Beauvoir at all; *The Routledge Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (ed. Craig, 1998) had entries for all three. [source: SEP-FH §2]
- Mary Ellen Waithe's *A History of Women Philosophers* documented "at least 16 women philosophers in the classical world, 17 women philosophers from 500–1600, and over 30 from 1600–1900". [source: SEP-FH §2]
- Waithe: "If we except the Pythagorean women, we find little differences in the ways men and women did philosophy" (Waithe 1987: xxi); Mary Warnock: "In the end, I have not found any clear 'voice' shared by women philosophers" (Warnock 1996: xlvii). SEP-FH: the recovered women "are not all proto-feminists nor do they speak in a uniform, and different, voice". [source: SEP-FH §2]
- **Eileen O'Neill, "Disappearing Ink: Early Modern Women Philosophers and Their Fate in History"**, in Janet A. Kourany (ed.), *Philosophy in a Feminist Voice: Critiques and Reconstructions* (Princeton UP), pp. 17–62. Dated **1998** by SEP-FH, SEP-Ast, SEP-Cav and SEP-Eli bibliographies; SEP-Rat dates it **1997**. Note the volume title is *Feminist* Voice, not "Feminine". [sources: SEP-FH bibliography; SEP-Rat bibliography; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_philosophy]
- What O'Neill did: SEP-FH: early modern women are well recorded, so "the doxographic task of retrieving these women thinkers" was "relatively straightforward, even if labor intensive. O'Neill (1998) catalogs a long list these women, and her doxographical work has provided a starting point". SEP-Rat: "O'Neill (1997) was instrumental in this movement by emphasizing the recovery of women's contributions". WS-9/10: the essay "presented the names, major works, and principal theses of literally dozens of forgotten women", and also gave reasons, internal and external to philosophy, for their erasure (e.g. women's choice of topics, genres and methods, such as theology-centred subjects, that later historians dismissed), while arguing these do not explain the "thoroughgoing erasure of women by the nineteenth century". [sources: SEP-FH §2.1; SEP-Rat §1; WS-9, WS-10 (weaker)]
- O'Neill's essays were collected posthumously as *Disappearing Ink: Essays in Early Modern Philosophy* (Oxford UP; ed. with Gary Ostertag per the OUP listing; 2025 per bookseller listings). [source: WS-9, WS-10 (weaker; OUP page not opened)]
- **Lisa Shapiro** (2004, "Some Thoughts on the Place of Women in Early Modern Philosophy") argues "it is not enough to simply add a woman philosopher or two to the reading list"; we need "internal reasons" for inclusion: "The history of philosophy is a story and we need to find a plot line that includes new, female characters." [source: SEP-FH §2]
- SEP-FH names the "Best Supporting Actress" approach (show how women contributed to male philosophers' central problems): easy and anchoring, but it "reinforces the secondary status of women thinkers" (example: Beauvoir read as applying Sartre). The alternative (Shapiro): find issues women developed "in a sequential, interactive fashion", e.g. women's rationality, nature and education in the 17th century, continued by Rousseau, Catharine Macaulay and Wollstonecraft. [source: SEP-FH §2]
- SEP-FH: contemporary recovery scholars "have tended to adopt a strategy that does not assume that standard philosophical concepts or the canon itself are gender biased", aiming (1) to recover out-of-print texts and (2) to interpret them to show their philosophical content and the women's involvement in the period's debates. A third route is "rethinking the questions through which that history is structured" (e.g. education, reasoning and the passions). [source: SEP-FH §2, §2.1]
- Concerted effort "from about the mid-1990s". Examples SEP-FH gives of fitting women into canonical themes: Elisabeth on causation (1643); Cavendish's vitalist causation in *Observations* (1666), in which motion is not transferred but one body moves itself "through a self-patterning in harmony with another body"; du Châtelet on the Principle of Sufficient Reason (1740). [source: SEP-FH §2.1]
- Edited volumes SEP-FH names: Emily Thomas (ed.) 2018 (metaphysics); **Jacqueline Broad and Karen Detlefsen (eds), *Women and Liberty, 1600–1800: Philosophical Essays* (OUP 2017)** (Cavendish, Masham, Suchon, Cockburn, du Châtelet, Macaulay, Sophie de Grouchy, Wollstonecraft); Eileen O'Neill and Marcy Lascano (eds), *Feminist History of Philosophy: The Recovery and Evaluation of Women's Philosophical Thought* (Springer 2019). [source: SEP-FH §2.1 and bibliography]
- Digital resources: SEP-FH names the New Narratives bibliography and **Project Vox** ("contentful resources and curricular support"), now being extended to other periods and regions. [source: SEP-FH §2.1]
- Project Vox: a Duke University project (Duke University Libraries with Trinity College of Arts and Sciences) begun **spring 2014**, website launched March 2015; first entries Cavendish, Conway, du Châtelet and Masham; Andrew Janiak (Duke philosophy) on the original team; since 2020–21 widened beyond early modern Europe (e.g. Sor Juana, Mary Shepherd, Anton Wilhelm Amo). Its stated aim (as quoted by WP): to "recover the lost voices of women who have been ignored in standard narratives of the history of modern philosophy". [sources: WS-8 (weaker; Duke pages); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_philosophy]
- **Margaret Atherton**: edited *Women Philosophers of the Early Modern Period* (Hackett 1994); in "Cartesian Reason and Gendered Reason" (1993, in Antony and Witt eds, *A Mind of One's Own*), she "has argued that [Descartes's] concept of reason was interpreted in egalitarian rather than masculinist terms by several women philosophers", and used in arguments for equal education for women — a reply to Genevieve Lloyd's *Man of Reason* thesis that reason has been "gendered male". [source: SEP-FH §1.3, §3 and bibliography]
- **Jacqueline Broad**: *Women Philosophers of the Seventeenth Century* (Cambridge UP 2002); *The Philosophy of Mary Astell: An Early Modern Theory of Virtue* (OUP 2015), "first book-length examination of Astell's wider philosophy". [sources: SEP bibliographies; IEP-Ast references]
- **Karen Detlefsen**: SEP author on du Châtelet; co-editor with Broad (2017) and with Lisa Shapiro of *The Routledge Handbook of Women and Early Modern European Philosophy* (Routledge 2023). [sources: SEP-Ch; SEP-Rat bibliography]
- **Christia Mercer** (Columbia): "Descartes' Debt to Teresa of Ávila, Or Why We Should Work on Women in the History of Philosophy" (*Philosophical Studies*, 2016 online) argues the evil-deceiver argument of the *Meditations* is indebted to Teresa of Ávila; she says she was "motivated by her friend, Eileen O'Neill"; she is preparing a new edition and translation of Conway's *Principles* (with Andrew Arlig and Jasper Reid); co-edits *Oxford New Histories of Philosophy*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christia_Mercer]
- **Canonical philosophers on women** (for the "standard story" framing): Hegel, *Philosophy of Right* (1820) §166, trans. T. M. Knox (1973 printing): "Women are capable of education, but they are not made for activities which demand a universal faculty such as the more advanced sciences, philosophy and certain forms of artistic production. … Women regulate their actions not by the demands of universality, but by arbitrary inclinations and opinions." [source: SEP-FH §1 epigraph and bibliography]
- Kant cited du Châtelet in his first publication, *Thoughts on the True Estimation of Living Forces* (1747); in *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime* (1764) he wrote that a woman "who conducts disputations about mechanics, like the Marquise du Châtelet might as well also wear a beard". (WP does not name the translation.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Émilie_du_Châtelet]
- Dag Herbjørnsrud (Aeon, Dec 2018, "First women of philosophy") argues women and philosophers of colour were excluded from the canon by Kant, Hegel and their followers. Susan Price (*The Atlantic*, 13 May 2015) notes the 1,000-plus-page *Norton Introduction to Philosophy* names no woman philosopher before the mid-20th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_philosophy]
- SEP-Rat: the "rationalist versus empiricist" narrative, still dominant in teaching, is under pressure from efforts to bring in under-represented figures; one alternative is to prefer labels authors used themselves ("Cartesian"). [source: SEP-Rat §1]
- SEP-Con: Conway's anonymity "ensured that she suffered the same neglect that has been the lot of most pre-modern female philosophers"; "the dominance of the teaching canon" means her Platonism is still neglected; most recent studies "focus largely on its relationship to canonical philosophers". [source: SEP-Con §6]

## 2. Elisabeth of Bohemia (1618–1680) — her own views (interaction objection: see *Descartes*)

- Born 26 December 1618, third of thirteen children and eldest daughter of Frederick V, Elector Palatine (briefly King of Bohemia) and Elizabeth Stuart (daughter of James I); died 8 February 1680 at Herford, where she was abbess (WP gives 11 February 1680; the *Descartes* notes already record the discrepancy). Siblings called her "La Greque". [sources: SEP-Eli §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisabeth_of_the_Palatinate]
- Correspondence with Descartes began at her initiative in 1643 and ran until his death in early 1650; her last letter discussed is dated 4 December 1649. Her letters "constitute what we currently know of her extant philosophical writings". She refused Chanut's request to publish her side; the surviving copies (early 18th-century, found at Rosendael near Arnhem) were published by Foucher de Careil. [source: SEP-Eli intro, §4, §4.1, §4.5]
- Early interest in the passions: Edward Reynolds dedicated his *Treatise of the Passions and Faculties of the Soule of Man* (1640) to her. [source: SEP-Eli §2]
- Natural philosophy and mathematics: in November 1643 Descartes set her Apollonius's three-circles problem; her (lost) solution used a single variable and had a "symmetry and transparency" his lacked (Descartes's comments). She criticised his accounts of magnetism and the heaviness of mercury after the *Principles* (1644) was dedicated to her, and promoted Cartesian physics in Germany. Shapiro (2019) reads her as a Cartesian in natural philosophy; Kambouchner (2021) argues she is not Cartesian about practical and civil life. [source: SEP-Eli §4.3]
- Virtue and happiness (1645–46 letters, beginning with Seneca's *De vita beata*): (1) Aristotelian objection: Descartes's virtue (resolution to do what we judge best) makes virtue "impervious to fortune or moral luck"; she holds even our ability to reason is subject to luck; (2) Stoic-style objection: a virtuous agent who errs will feel regret, so virtue need not bring contentment; contentment would require "an 'infinite science'" (AT 4:289) to know all the effects of our actions; (3) we lack an objective measure of value, given biases of temperament and self-interest — a view SEP-Eli likens to "Hobbes and other contractarians". On 13 September 1645 she asked him to "define the passions, in order to know them better" (AT 4:289); she commented on the draft on 25 April 1646; published 1649 as *The Passions of the Soul*. [source: SEP-Eli §4.4]
- When Descartes (15 Sept 1645, AT 4:292) offered metaphysical truths (God, the soul's immortality, the vastness of the universe) as practical guides, she replied that they open more problems (free will; why knowing immortality might make us seek death; particular providence). [source: SEP-Eli §4.4]
- Politics: on 25 April 1646 (AT 4:406) she asked for his maxims "concerning civil life" and his view of Machiavelli's *Prince*; he gave the latter in September 1646; her own reading (10 October 1646): Machiavelli's focus on the hardest-to-govern state gives guidance for achieving stability but little for governing a stable state. "Paganani" (2021; so spelled in SEP — probably Gianni Paganini, unverified) reads her as starting from Machiavelli's realism and favouring a "pragmatic and operational approach", against Descartes's appeal to a divine guarantee. Shapiro (2021), on her letter of 4 December 1649 (AT 5:452) on the apparent incompatibility of governing and philosophising: rulers have their own passions, shaped by the time pressure of decisions. Pal (2021): her overarching concern was "how best to rule". [source: SEP-Eli §4.5, §3]
- She raised her own sex as a bodily "condition" affecting reason ("weakness of my sex"); Descartes disregarded it (Shapiro 1999); Pellegrin (2021) argues "the very idea of a weak sex is antithetical to Cartesianism". [source: SEP-Eli §4.2]
- Later life: entered the Lutheran convent at Herford 1660, abbess 1667; sheltered Labadists (at Anna Maria van Schurman's request) and Quakers (Penn and Barclay visited); involved in Westphalia negotiations and Restoration efforts; Francis Mercury van Helmont reportedly at her deathbed; Hutton (2021) asks whether she and Anne Conway knew each other through these networks. Sister Sophie became Electress of Hanover and Leibniz's patron. [source: SEP-Eli intro, §1, §3]

## 3. Margaret Cavendish (1623–1673)

- Born Margaret Lucas, 1623, Colchester, Essex; youngest of eight children; no formal education; talked philosophy with her brother John, later a founding member of the Royal Society. Maid of honour to Queen Henrietta Maria 1643; followed her into exile in France 1644; married William Cavendish (then Marquess, later Duke of Newcastle) 1645; in exile in Paris, Rotterdam and Antwerp until 1660. [sources: SEP-Cav §1; IEP-Cav §1]
- Died December 1673 (WP infobox: 16 December 1673), aged 50; buried in Westminster Abbey (WP: 7 January 1674). [sources: SEP-Cav §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Cavendish,_Duchess_of_Newcastle-upon-Tyne]
- The "Cavendish Circle": SEP-Cav says William hosted it in the 1640s, attended by Hobbes, Descartes, Mersenne, Gassendi and Digby, and Margaret attended "many of these meetings, though we do not know the extent to which she participated". IEP-Cav says the regular salon was held by William's brother **Sir Charles Cavendish**, attended by Hobbes and Digby and "occasionally" Descartes, Mersenne and Gassendi, and that Margaret "denies having spoken to them about any, but the most superficial of matters". (The two entries differ on host and frequency.) [sources: SEP-Cav §1; IEP-Cav §1]
- Philosophical works: *Poems and Fancies* and *Philosophical Fancies* (1653), *The World's Olio* (1655), *Philosophical and Physical Opinions* (1655; 2nd ed. 1663; reworked as *Grounds of Natural Philosophy*, 1668), *Philosophical Letters* (1664), *Observations upon Experimental Philosophy* (1666; 2nd ed. 1668), *The Description of a New World, Called the Blazing World* (1666, printed with *Observations*; again 1668). IEP-Cav dates *Blazing World* to 1668 (the second edition). She wrote eight philosophical monographs (SEP); IEP: possibly "the most prolific woman philosopher" of early modern Europe. [sources: SEP-Cav §1 and bibliography; IEP-Cav §1; WP]
- Dismissive replies: she mailed her books to scholars and universities; Walter Charleton thanked her for spending "great summs of Money in Printing" and said she was "secure from being Copied"; apart from Joseph Glanvill, correspondents barely engaged (Cunning 2022). Hence she argued with them in letters to a fictional correspondent (*Philosophical Letters*). Her line: "For a Philosopher or Philosopheress is not produced on a sudden." [source: SEP-Cav §1]
- Central tenet: "Nature is material, or corporeal, and so are all her Creatures, and whatsoever is not material is no part of Nature"; no vacuum; matter infinitely divisible; all bodies at every level "intelligent and perceptive". Mind is not "raggs and shreds" but "the purest, simplest and subtillest matter in Nature" (Cavendish 1664, 180). [source: SEP-Cav §1]
- IEP-Cav summary: "Against Descartes … she rejected dualism and incorporeal substance of any kind. Against Hobbes … she argued for a vitalist materialism, according to which all things in nature were composed of self-moving, animate matter", and for panpsychism. Matter comes in degrees: "dull" (inanimate), "sensitive" and "rational" spirits (IEP likens the latter to Stoic *pneuma*). Anti-atomism in *Observations* ch. 31: "whatsoever has body, or is material, has quantity; and what has quantity, is divisible"; atoms would make a body "a swarm of bees" rather than a unity. [source: IEP-Cav §2a]
- **Variability argument** (IEP-Cav §2b): nature shows both variety and order; if matter were uniform and passive, merely transferring motion by collision, nature would be either homogeneous or chaotic; so parts of matter must move themselves in regular, distinctive ways, which requires that they perceive and know their motions. Quote: "there can be no order, method or harmony, especially such as appears in the actions of nature, without there be reason to cause that order and harmony" (*Observations*). [source: IEP-Cav §2b]
- **Argument for material minds** (SEP-Cav §3): thinking is located (in the head and body); "[p]lace [is] an attribute that belongs onely to a Body" (1664, 8); "Motion cannot be without matter … it is impossible … that there should be an Immaterial Motion in Nature"; minds move with us; so minds are material. SEP notes Locke later uses a similar Oxford-to-London argument. [source: SEP-Cav §3]
- **No transfer of motion** (SEP-Cav §5): when a hand moves a bowl, no animate matter passes from hand to bowl (or the hand "would in a short time become weak and useless, by losing so much substance"); one body is the occasion for the other to move itself; "cessation from motion" is "a mistake of change for rest" (1664, 436). [source: SEP-Cav §5]
- Hobbes: "For Hobbes, thoughts and sensations are motions in the brain … For Cavendish thinking and intelligence are basic features of bodies"; in social and political philosophy they "to a significant degree align" (self-love as the root of action; stability of civil society). IEP-Cav: she was a "staunch royalist and aristocrat", critical of social mobility. She later allowed a supernatural immaterial soul beside the natural material mind (*Observations*), and in 1668 said only God is immaterial (IEP: the texts are not decisive). (Detail is in *Hobbes*; don't repeat.) [sources: SEP-Cav §7; IEP-Cav §2a, §3]
- Art versus nature: "Natural reason is above artific[e]: wherefore those Arts are the best and surest Informers, that alter Nature least, and they the greatest deluders that alter Nature most" (Cavendish 1668a, 12–13). [source: SEP-Cav §4]
- **Hooke and microscopy** (weaker): *Observations* (1666) is often read as a response to Robert Hooke's *Micrographia* (1665); her epistemic objection was that what lenses show cannot be checked against unaided sight, so magnified images may distort; scholars (e.g. Ian Lawson, *BJHS*) argue the objection also had social/political motives, and that *Blazing World* satirises the claim that microscopes extend the senses. The phrase "deluding glasses" was **not** found. [source: WS-1 (weaker)]
- **Royal Society visit, May 1667**: she was invited to a meeting, "an extremely rare invitation", "though to be sure she was regarded as a spectacle by many in attendance" (SEP, citing Whitaker 2002); WP: "In May 1667, she became the first woman to attend a meeting at the Royal Society of London". WS: the date was **30 May 1667**; demonstrations linked to Boyle and Hooke; Pepys recorded the crowds; Emma Wilkins (*Notes and Records*, 2014) argues her relations with the Society were more nuanced than the traditional story. [sources: SEP-Cav §1; IEP-Cav §1; WP; WS-7 (weaker, for the exact date and demonstrators)]
- Contemporary verdicts: Samuel Pepys called her "a mad, conceited, ridiculous woman"; John Evelyn "a mighty pretender to learning, poetry, and philosophy"; Bathsua Makin praised her as over-topping "many grave Gown-Men". Virginia Woolf revived interest (*The Common Reader*, 1925). [source: WP]
- *Blazing World*: a woman becomes Empress of another world; she consults "worm-men and bear-men and bird-men" on matter, mind and chemistry; asking for the soul of Galileo, Descartes, Hobbes or More as a scribe, she is told "they would scorn to be scribes to a woman" (Cavendish appears as a character). One of the earliest works of science fiction (WP). [sources: SEP-Cav §9; WP]

## 4. Anne Conway (1631–1679)

- Born Anne Finch, London, 14 December 1631 (posthumous daughter of Sir Heneage Finch); died 23 February 1679 at Ragley Hall, Warwickshire. Raised in the house now Kensington Palace. Married Edward, 3rd Viscount Conway (later Earl of Conway), 1651; only child Heneage died in infancy (WP: of smallpox, about two years old). [sources: SEP-Con §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Conway_(philosopher)]
- Taught philosophy by letter by Henry More (Christ's College, Cambridge), since as a woman she could not attend the university; the course was Cartesian. More dedicated *An Antidote against Atheism* to her (1652/3, WP). [sources: SEP-Con §1; WP]
- Illness: "from her teens she suffered from periodic bouts of illness" (SEP); WP: severe **migraines** from age twelve; treatments included opening her jugular veins; consultations with Thomas Willis, Mayerne, Boyle and Harvey; in 1666 the Irish healer Valentine Greatrakes tried to cure her. Search for relief brought her to **Francis Mercury van Helmont**, who lived in her household in her last decade, introduced her to the Kabbalah and to Quakerism; she became a Quaker shortly before she died. [sources: SEP-Con §1; WP]
- The *Principles*: written near the end of her life (WP: about 1677); published **anonymously in Amsterdam in 1690 in Latin** as *Principia philosophiae antiquissimae et recentissimae*; the English original is lost; English retranslation London 1692 as *The Principles of the Most Ancient and Modern Philosophy*. [sources: SEP-Con §2; WP]
- System: three "species" — God; Christ or "Middle Nature"; "Creature". All created substance is spirit, living, capable of motion and perception; "inert corporeal substance would contradict the nature of God, who is life itself". Body and spirit are modes of one created substance, differing only in density (light/dark metaphor). Creatures are unities of infinitely many spirit particles: "monadic (though not in a Leibnizian sense)". [source: SEP-Con §2–3]
- Targets: chapters refuting the dualism of **More and Descartes** (though she admired Descartes's physics); **Hobbes and Spinoza** charged with "material pantheism, which confounds God and created substance". [source: SEP-Con §2]
- Change and identity: creatures can transform up or down the scale over successive lives (a horse may become a human), but Peter cannot become Paul (divine justice); a "captain spirit" keeps identity. Pain is purgative; she denies eternal hell (universal salvation). [source: SEP-Con §4–5]
- Leibniz: SEP: her philosophy "was received favorably by Leibniz (who in fact owned a copy of her treatise—probably a gift to him by their mutual friend, Van Helmont)"; her system "anticipates" his, and he "recognized affinities". WP: "Conway's work was an influence on Gottfried Leibniz"; Hugh Trevor-Roper called her "England's greatest female philosopher". (The wording of Leibniz's acknowledgement: see NOT VERIFIED.) [sources: SEP-Con §2, §6; WP]
- Modern recovery: Loptson's edition (SEP: "Loptson, Conway1998"), Coudert and Corse's translation (Cambridge UP 1996), Hutton's revised *Conway Letters* (1992, from Marjorie Hope Nicolson's 1930 edition). [source: SEP-Con §6; WP]

## 5. Mary Astell (1666–1731)

- Born Newcastle upon Tyne, 12 November 1666; died Chelsea, London, May 1731 (IEP: 9 May; WP: 11 May), a few months after a mastectomy for breast cancer; buried at Chelsea. Father died 1678; no dowry; never married. Uncle Ralph Astell (Cambridge-educated clergyman) probably taught her. Moved to London probably late 1680s; patronised by Archbishop Sancroft. [sources: IEP-Ast §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Astell]
- Works: *A Serious Proposal to the Ladies*, Part I (1694, signed "By a Lover of her Sex") and Part II (1697); *Letters Concerning the Love of God* with John Norris (1695; correspondence begun 1693); *Some Reflections upon Marriage* (1700; eds 1703, **1706 with a new preface "in answer to some objections"**, 1730); Tory pamphlets of 1704; *The Christian Religion, As Profess'd by a Daughter of the Church of England* (1705); *Bart'lemy Fair* (1709) against Shaftesbury. Withdrew 1709; ran a charity school for girls in Chelsea. [sources: SEP-Ast intro; IEP-Ast §1; WP]
- *Serious Proposal*: an all-female academic retreat; never founded, "possibly due to the suspicion that it sounded like a Catholic nunnery". [source: IEP-Ast §6a]
- Cartesian and Malebranchean: knowledge is "that clear Perception which is follow'd by a firm assent to Conclusions rightly drawn from Premises of which we have clear and distinct Ideas" (SPL II 149); six rules of method modelled on Descartes's *Discourse* (1637) and Arnauld and Nicole's *Logic* (1662). IEP: "an unorthodox Cartesian"; her moral theology resembles the Augustinianism of Norris and Malebranche. In the *Letters* she criticised Norris's occasionalism (it makes the creation of bodies vain and offends God's majesty) and proposed a "sensible congruity" between bodies and the soul's powers. [sources: IEP-Ast intro, §2; SEP-Ast §1.3.1]
- Women's reasoning: "The Meanest Person shou'd Think as Justly, tho' not as Capaciously, as the greatest Philosopher" (SP 168); "the difference between a plow-man and a Doctor does not seem to me to consist in this, That the Business of the one is to search after Knowledge, and that the other has nothing to do with it" (SP 153). Sowaal names the target the "Women's Defective Nature Prejudice". [source: SEP-Ast §2.2, §3]
- Limits of her audience (SEP-Ast §3): the "Ladies" are gentry and noble, English, "likely identify as White"; Astell theorises their domination by men but "not these women's privilege", including servants they "paid, indentured, and/or enslaved" and patrons' colonial ventures (Perry 1986). [source: SEP-Ast §3]
- Marriage: *Reflections* responds to the separation of Hortense Mancini, Duchess of Mazarin, from her abusive husband; most men marry from lust or greed; a wife is expected to give "blind submission"; remedy: education; marriage ought to rest on friendship. [sources: IEP-Ast §6b; WP]
- Politics: High Church Tory; passive obedience; never active resistance even to tyranny; against Locke's right of resistance she argues true self-preservation is preservation of the immortal soul (*Christian Religion* §274). She saw William and Mary as illegitimate (WP). Her feminism rests on philosophical principles, not progressive politics, which partly explains why she did not demand political equality. [sources: IEP-Ast §7; WP]
- She also attacked Locke's suggestion of "thinking matter" (*Christian Religion* §228; Squadrito 1987, Taylor 2001). In her lifetime her writings were known to Locke, Leibniz and Berkeley. [source: IEP-Ast §3, §8 and references]
- Reception: two revivals, 1890s–early 1900s and mid-1980s onward, the latter helped by Ruth Perry's biography *The Celebrated Mary Astell* (1986); Perry thinks Astell saw herself "more as a metaphysician and philosopher than a political reformer". [source: IEP-Ast §8]

## 6. Damaris Masham (1658/9–1708), Catharine Trotter Cockburn (1679–1749)

- Masham: daughter of the Cambridge Platonist Ralph Cudworth; SEP gives born 1658; WP gives 18 January 1659 (consistent if Old Style 1658/9); died 20 April 1708. Taught herself Latin later in life by the method of Locke's *Some Thoughts Concerning Education*. Met Locke before 1682; early letters as "Philoclea" and "Philander". Married Sir Francis Masham (widower with nine children) 1685; Locke lived at her house at Oates, Essex, from 1691 (SEP: guest from his return, later permanent resident). She wrote a life of Locke that is the source of the first printed biographies. [sources: SEP-Mas §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damaris_Cudworth_Masham]
- *A Discourse Concerning the Love of God* (1696, anonymous): against John Norris (and indirectly Malebranche and Astell); denies Norris's split between love of God and love of creatures; we come to love God by reasoning from the world; occasionalism "undermines the basis of morality"; Lockean epistemology (idea of God from ideas "received from Sense and Reflection"). French translation by Pierre Coste, Amsterdam 1705. [source: SEP-Mas §2]
- *Occasional Thoughts in Reference to a Vertuous or Christian Life* (1705, anonymous): partly an answer to Astell's *Christian Religion* (itself a reply to the *Discourse*); "an Irrational Religion can never Rationally be conceived to come from God" (p. 36); argues for women's education, since a mother cannot give her children a moral education if she is denied one. IEP-Ast lists Joanne Myers (2013) on "the so-called debate between Astell and fellow feminist Masham". [sources: SEP-Mas §3; IEP-Ast references]
- Leibniz correspondence: SEP-Mas "Between 1704 and 1705–6 … at his instigation"; she disputed pre-established harmony, substance and free will (the *Leibniz* notes record editions dating it from 1703). [source: SEP-Mas §4]
- Cockburn: born London 16 August 1679; playwright (*Agnes de Castro* staged 1695; *Fatal Friendship* 1698); **A Defence of Mr. Locke's Essay of Human Understanding (1702)** answered the anonymous *Remarks upon an Essay Concerning Humane Understanding* (three pamphlets, 1697–99), long attributed to Thomas Burnet (attribution now challenged, Walmsley et al. 2016); Locke gave her "a substantial gift of money and books". Later *Remarks upon some Writers* (written 1739, published 1743) defending Samuel Clarke; *Remarks upon … Dr. Rutherforth's Essay* (1747). Died May 1749 (WP: 11 May); collected works ed. Thomas Birch, 1751. Her ethics: a rationalist natural-law theory combining Lockean reflection with Clarkean moral "fitness". [sources: SEP-Coc §1–2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catharine_Trotter_Cockburn]

## 7. Gabrielle Suchon, Marie de Gournay, Poulain de la Barre

- Suchon: born Semur-en-Auxois, Burgundy, 24 December 1632; died Dijon, 5 March 1703. Placed in a Dominican convent; petitioned Rome to be released from her vows (petition read 10 September 1672); lived as a lay woman teaching and writing in Dijon. *Traité de la morale et de la politique* (1693, as "G. S. Aristophile"), in three parts — **liberty, knowledge (science), authority** — the three "prerogatives" denied to women; *Du célibat volontaire* (1700), defending a freely chosen single life. Both reviewed in the *Journal des savants*. She answered Poulain de la Barre's *De l'égalité des deux sexes* (1673). WP: "considered the first philosopher to produce a significant body of work dedicated solely to the topic of women". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabrielle_Suchon]
- Gournay (1565–1645; born 6 October 1565, died 13 July 1645): *Equality of Men and Women* (1622), *The Ladies' Grievance* (1626) — consistent with the published *Renaissance Philosophy* chapter. [source: IEP-Gour]
- Poulain de la Barre (1647–1723): *De l'égalité des deux sexes* (1673), *De l'éducation des dames* (1674), *De l'excellence des hommes* (1675); from the Cartesian real distinction of mind and body he concluded intellect is not sexed, and that social disparities are "culturally constructed" (Shapiro 2021). [source: SEP-Rat §4]

## 8. Émilie du Châtelet (1706–1749) (brief mention in *Enlightenment and Rousseau*)

- Born Paris 17 December 1706; married the Marquis du Châtelet 1725; met Voltaire 1733 (his lover and "life-long intellectual companion"); they lived at Cirey, with a laboratory; daughter by Saint-Lambert born 4 September 1749; she died 10 September 1749. [source: SEP-Ch intro]
- Contributed substantially, especially on technical material, to Voltaire's *Éléments de la philosophie de Newton* (1738); entered the Academy's 1737/38 prize on fire secretly (essay printed 1739). [sources: SEP-Ch intro; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Émilie_du_Châtelet]
- ***Institutions de physique* (1740)**, ostensibly a physics textbook for her son, "in reality a highly original work in natural philosophy" (2nd ed. 1742 as *Institutions physiques*); supplies a Leibnizian–Wolffian metaphysics for Newtonian physics; withdrawn from the press in 1738 for redrafting; structure mirrors Descartes's *Principles*. Samuel Koenig (her tutor from 1739) accused her of plagiarism, but the censor Pitot had noted Leibnizian ideas before Koenig arrived. W. H. Barber (1967) judged her "essentially derivative"; Ira Wade (1947) argued for her independence, the now-dominant view. [source: SEP-Ch §1]
- First principles: the principle of contradiction and the **principle of sufficient reason**: "we cannot force our mind to accept something without a sufficient reason, that is to say, without a reason that makes us understand why the thing is what it is, rather than something completely different" (IP §8); "if there can be effects without sufficient reason, all might have been produced by accident, that is to say, by nothing" (IP §8). [source: SEP-Ch §2]
- Hypotheses: "those who wish to ban hypotheses from philosophy, intend harm to the interests of science" (IP §71); SEP places her "at the start of the hypothetico-deductive tradition", against Newton's "hypotheses non fingo" as read by his admirers. [source: SEP-Ch §5]
- ***Vis viva***: she defended *force vive* (siding with Leibniz), leading to a dispute with Jean-Jacques Dortous de Mairan, secretary of the Academy of Sciences; her *Réponse … à la lettre que M. de Mairan* (1741) rebutted him point by point, and (WP) he withdrew. She publicised 's Gravesande's experiment of balls dropped into clay, whose dents scaled with *mv²*. [sources: SEP-Ch intro; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Émilie_du_Châtelet; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vis_viva]
- **Newton translation**: two volumes, translation of and commentary on the *Principia*, begun early 1740s, completed just before her death, "unpublished until 1759. It is still the leading French translation of Newton's book." [source: SEP-Ch intro]
- Other: partial translation of Mandeville's *Fable of the Bees* (preface argues for women's education), *Examens de la Bible*, *Discours sur le bonheur*. [sources: SEP-Ch intro; WP]

## 9. Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–1797)

- Born Spitalfields, London, 27 April 1759; second of seven children; family's decline; schoolmistress at Newington Green (met Richard Price), governess to the Kingsborough family; *Thoughts on the Education of Daughters* (1787); reviewer for Joseph Johnson's *Analytical Review* (from 1788). Married William Godwin March 1797; daughter Mary (later Shelley) born 30 August 1797; died 10 September 1797 (WP) of complications of childbirth. [sources: SEP-W §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Wollstonecraft]
- ***A Vindication of the Rights of Men* (1790)**: answer to Burke's *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (November 1790), which had attacked Price's sermon *A Discourse on the Love of our Country* (4 Nov 1789). Published anonymously **29 November 1790**; second edition with her name **18 December 1790**; "almost certainly the first" of 50–70 replies. She assumed "a Lockean conception of God-given rights discoverable by reason"; only institutions that withstand rational scrutiny and conform to natural rights merit obedience. [sources: SEP-W §1, §3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Vindication_of_the_Rights_of_Men]
- ***A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792)**: begun September 1791; WP: published January 1792; prompted by Talleyrand's 1791 report proposing domestic education for girls; dedicated to Talleyrand (SEP: dedication in the second imprint). It proposes curriculum change, co-education, civil and political rights for women "and have elected representatives of their own", training to support themselves, women physicians, marriage based on friendship. Reviewed favourably on publication (WP); reputation collapsed after Godwin's *Memoirs* (1798). [sources: SEP-W §1, §3, §5; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Vindication_of_the_Rights_of_Woman]
- SEP-W: for her, the moral reformation of men "would be incomplete for as long as they themselves retained a despotic power over women"; quotes "virtue can only flourish amongst equals" and "among unequals there can be no society" (Wollstonecraft 1995, 61, 39 — the Cambridge volume containing both Vindications). [source: SEP-W §2]
- Philosopher, not only polemicist: more than 200 reviews; views on aesthetics (Gilpin), theory of mind (self-control, not will), passions and reason in harmony (Reuter 2019); Lockean pedagogy. Bergès (2013) reads *Rights of Woman* primarily as a treatise on education. Later, after Imlay and Scandinavia (*Letters Written During a Short Residence…*, 1796), a more nuanced view of reason and passion. [source: SEP-W §2, §4]
- WP on the shape of her equality claim: men and women "are equal in the eyes of God … subject to the same moral law"; she concedes men may be designed "to attain a greater degree of virtue" collectively, while denying that the *nature* of virtue differs. Barbara Taylor warns calling her "feminist" is anachronistic and has led to neglect of her religious beliefs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Vindication_of_the_Rights_of_Woman]

## 10. Beyond Europe: Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz (1648/1651–1695)

- Hieronymite nun of New Spain (Mexico); born San Miguel Nepantla; WP infobox 12 November 1648, but there are two baptism records (1648 "Juana", 1651 "Inés") and the date is "still a matter of academic research and debate" (Encyclopedia.com uses 1651); died 17 April 1695. Self-taught; entered a convent 1667; asked to attend university disguised as a man. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juana_Inés_de_la_Cruz]
- In November 1690 the Bishop of Puebla, Manuel Fernández de Santa Cruz, writing as "Sor Filotea", published without permission her critique of a sermon by the Jesuit António Vieira (*Carta Atenagórica*) with his own letter urging her to turn to religious studies; her reply, ***Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz* (1691)**, defends women's right to study and to teach other women. By 1693 she seemingly stopped writing; a 1694 penance document is signed "Yo, la Peor de Todas". [source: WP]
- SEP-Rat treats her as applying rationalist commitments to gender: *Primero sueño* depicts the soul's failed ascent to knowledge, ending "yo despierta" ("I awake", feminine form); in the *Respuesta* she "advocates for the equal education of men and women, using her own disposition toward learning as evidence". Octavio Paz (*Sor Juana, or, The Traps of Faith*, Eng. 1988) is credited with re-establishing her importance. [sources: SEP-Rat §4; WP]

## 11. Arguments set out step by step

### A. Conway against dualism (from SEP-Con §3, SEP-Cav §2)
1. Every created thing bears some likeness to God (principle of similitude).
2. God is life itself, infinitely good; "how can a dead thing come from him who is infinite life and love?" (*Principles*, Coudert & Corse trans., p. 45, as quoted SEP-Cav).
3. Matter conceived as "nothing but inert extension" ("dead") has no likeness to God.
4. So no such dead matter exists; everything created is alive and perceptive.
5. Further, things can change into one another only if they are alike; body and spirit do change into one another (bodies become more spirit-like as they improve, and vice versa).
6. So body and spirit are not two substances but two modes of one created substance, differing by degree ("relative density").
- Main objection: this blurs creature and Creator — pantheism, the charge she herself makes against Hobbes and Spinoza. Reply: creatures differ from God by mutability and multiplicity, and Christ/"Middle Nature" is "both a bridge and a buffer" between God and creatures, so the continuum never collapses into God. [sources: SEP-Con §2–3; SEP-Cav §2]
- Second objection (Cartesian): we clearly conceive thought without extension, so they are distinct. (Not discussed by SEP; leave out unless sourced.)

### B. Astell for women's education (from IEP-Ast §6a, SEP-Ast §1.2, §3)
1. Whoever can think is a thinking thing; a woman need only turn within to see that she thinks (an inward-consciousness argument, not an appeal to famous women).
2. Reasoning in everyday affairs (household management, romance, even a petticoat's design) is evidence of the capacity to reason.
3. "Just" thinking is available to everyone; only "capacious" learning depends on time and training (SP 168).
4. God does nothing in vain; if he gave women rational minds, they are meant to use them for the best ends (knowledge and love of God).
5. So women's observed defects are "acquired rather than natural" (custom, flattery, an education in vanity) and correctable by method.
6. So women ought to be educated (hence the academy of the *Proposal*).
- Objection (contemporary view, SEP-Ast §1.2): women are more closely united to their bodies, so less fit for reason. Reply: on the Cartesian account the mind is really distinct from the body, so differences in performance are explained "not metaphysically, but epistemologically" — by social practices that misdirect attention. [sources: IEP-Ast §6a; SEP-Ast §1.2, §2.2, §3]
- Complication (debate, below): Astell's argument concerns souls and salvation and gentry women, not political equality.

### C. Wollstonecraft: reason and virtue are the same for both sexes
1. Virtue "has only one eternal standard" (from God); so the virtues of women cannot "differ in respect to their nature" from men's (WP quoting *Rights of Woman*).
2. Virtue is acquired by the exercise of reason and self-command; "the most perfect education" is "an exercise of the understanding … to enable the individual to attach such habits of virtue as will render it independent" (WP quoting).
3. If woman "be not prepared by education to become the companion of man, she will stop the progress of knowledge and virtue; for truth must be common to all" (preface, WP quoting).
4. Women's apparent frivolity and weakness are the product of an education aimed at pleasing (sensibility, beauty), not of nature.
5. Virtue "can only flourish amongst equals" (SEP-W quoting); despotic power over women corrupts men too.
6. So women must receive the same rational education and, as citizens, civil and political rights.
- Objection 1 (Rousseau, *Emile* V; see *Enlightenment and Rousseau*): woman is made to please man; her education should be relative to men. Reply: this assumes the sexes have different virtues; but if virtue has one standard, an education for pleasing makes women slaves or "capricious tyrants", not virtuous (WP §Rhetoric).
- Objection 2 (Burke, *Reflections*): abstract rights ignore custom, prejudice and the national inheritance. Reply (*Rights of Men*): laws are products of historical contingency (citing Hume's *History*); only institutions that survive the scrutiny of reason and conform to natural rights merit obedience (SEP-W §3).
- Concession worth noting: she grants men may "collectively" attain "a greater degree of virtue" through bodily strength — equality in kind, not necessarily in degree (WP). [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Vindication_of_the_Rights_of_Woman; SEP-W §2–3]

## Debates
- **Inclusion versus re-thinking the canon.** (a) Recovery and add-in (O'Neill's catalogue; editions; the "Best Supporting Actress" strategy of fitting women into existing debates on causation, PSR etc.); (b) Shapiro: adding "a woman philosopher or two" is not enough; we need internal reasons and new plot lines, such as the 17th-century sequence on women's reason and education; (c) re-framing the questions (education, passions) and dropping the rationalist/empiricist frame (SEP-Rat). SEP-FH notes most early modern recovery work does *not* assume the canon's concepts are gender-biased; Lloyd's *Man of Reason* does, and Atherton replies that women used Cartesian reason in egalitarian ways. [sources: SEP-FH §1.3, §2, §2.1; SEP-Rat §1]
- **Why the women disappeared.** O'Neill gives internal and external reasons (genres, theological topics) but holds these do not explain the near-total erasure by the 19th century (WS-9/10); Herbjørnsrud blames Kant, Hegel and their followers; SEP-Rat points to "narratives originating in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries". [sources: SEP-Rat; WP Women_in_philosophy; WS (weaker)]
- **Elisabeth's own position:** open to revising dualism without an alternative (Shapiro) vs "naturalistic dualism" (Janssen-Lauret 2018); Cartesian in natural philosophy (Shapiro 2019) vs not in civil life (Kambouchner 2021). [source: SEP-Eli §4.2–4.3]
- **Conway and Leibniz:** influence on the monad (Carolyn Merchant, "The Vitalism of Anne Conway: Its Impact on Leibniz's Concept of the Monad", *JHP* 1979) vs Stuart Brown (1990, "Leibniz and More's Cabbalistic Circle"), who "challenges the view that Conway influenced Leibniz"; SEP-Con: her units are "monadic (though not in a Leibnizian sense)". [sources: SEP-Con; SEP-Con bibliography; WP Anne_Conway further reading]
- **Cavendish and the Royal Society:** hostile spectacle (traditional, from Pepys and Birch) vs a more nuanced relationship (Wilkins 2014; new manuscript evidence that Nehemiah Grew read her, WP). Her critique of microscopy: epistemic vs socio-political motives (Lawson). [sources: SEP-Cav §1; WP; WS-1, WS-7 (weaker)]
- **Astell: feminist and High Tory.** Is *Reflections* a feminist work urging women to stay single (Lister 2004), or an ironic Tory challenge to Whigs to accept passive obedience in the state as they demand it in marriage (Springborg; IEP "some scholars")? Broad reads the "born slaves" line as alluding to Locke's definition of slavery; Springborg holds Astell does not seriously regard marriage as slavery in Locke's sense. SEP-Ast adds the class/race limits of her audience. [sources: IEP-Ast §6b, §7 and references; WS-2; SEP-Ast §3]
- **Du Châtelet's originality:** "essentially derivative" (Barber 1967; Koenig's plagiarism charge) vs independent (Wade 1947; now dominant). [source: SEP-Ch §1]
- **Wollstonecraft as "feminist":** anachronism worry (Barbara Taylor, WP); "republican manifesto" (Claudia Johnson); treatise on education (Bergès). SEP-FH: Wollstonecraft is a candidate for, not yet fully part of, the canon. [sources: WP; SEP-W §2; SEP-FH §2]

## Consistency with published Periergia texts
- *History of Philosophy* overview: calls Wollstonecraft (1792) "the first systematic philosophical feminism". **Contradicted/at least contestable**: IEP-Ast says Astell (1694–1705) "might be justly regarded as one of the earliest feminist philosophers of the modern age"; WP calls Suchon (1693) "the first philosopher to produce a significant body of work dedicated solely to the topic of women"; Gournay's *Equality* (1622) and Poulain (1673) precede both. Recommend the overview be softened (e.g. "a landmark of philosophical feminism").
- *Hobbes*: says Cavendish attended "Cavendish Circle" meetings with Hobbes, Descartes, Mersenne and Gassendi — matches SEP-Cav, but IEP-Cav says the salon was Charles Cavendish's, Descartes, Mersenne and Gassendi came only "occasionally", and Margaret denied discussing more than superficial matters with them. Minor; this chapter should note the uncertainty.
- *Descartes*: correspondence "lasted until Descartes' death" (consistent: last letter cited Dec 1649, he died Feb 1650); Elisabeth dates consistent (WP/SEP death-date discrepancy already noted there); "Poulain de la Barre used dualism to argue that 'the mind has no sex'" consistent with SEP-Rat.
- *Locke*: Astell (1666–1731), High Church Tory, 1706 preface, Broad's reading — consistent with IEP-Ast. Masham *Occasional Thoughts* (1705) for women's education — consistent. Locke notes give Masham born 18 Jan 1659; SEP-Mas says 1658 (Old Style year; give "1659" or "1658/9"). Cockburn *Defence* 1702 — consistent.
- *Leibniz*: Masham–Leibniz letters "about 1703/4 to 1705" — consistent with SEP-Mas ("1704 and 1705–6") given the editions' disagreement.
- *Enlightenment and Rousseau*: du Châtelet (1706–1749), *Institutions* (1740), Newton translation "still the leading French version" — consistent with SEP-Ch. Wollstonecraft material consistent.
- *Renaissance Philosophy*: Gournay (1565–1645), *Equality* 1622, *Grievance* 1626 — consistent with IEP-Gour.
- History *Scientific Revolution*: Cavendish "in 1667 became the first woman to attend a meeting of the Royal Society" — consistent with WP; SEP/IEP phrase it as a rare invitation.
- History *Enlightenment*: Wollstonecraft argued women's inferiority came from lack of education; Gouges 1791, guillotined 1793 — consistent.

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use

Say "Leibniz admired her book and saw affinities with his own views".
- "Virtue has no sex" as a Wollstonecraft quotation: appears to be a modern paraphrase (WS-6); the nearest wording found is about prejudices that "give a sex to virtue" (not verified against a text). Use "virtue has only one eternal standard" instead.
- Cavendish's "deluding glasses" or any verbatim *Observations* quotation about Hooke's microscope: not found. The 1668a "deluders" quotation is about arts that "alter Nature", and is not confirmed to concern microscopes.
