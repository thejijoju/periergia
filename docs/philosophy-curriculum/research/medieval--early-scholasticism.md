## 1. Background: Boethius, Porphyry's questions, Eriugena (brief; cross-ref *Augustine and Early Christian Philosophy*)
- Porphyry (ca. 234–305) raised, in his *Isagoge* (introduction to Aristotle's *Categories*), three questions he declined to answer. In Spade's translation: "I shall beg off saying anything about (a) whether genera and species are real or are situated in bare thoughts alone, (b) whether as real they are bodies or incorporeals, and (c) whether they are separated or in sensibles and have their reality in connection with them. Such business is profound, and requires another, greater investigation." [source: SEP-UM §3, quoting Spade 1994, p. 1] IEP-Ab gives the same three questions in the same wording. [source: https://iep.utm.edu/abelard/]
- Boethius (ca. 480–524) wrote two commentaries on the *Isagoge*, the first on Marius Victorinus' translation, the second on his own. [source: SEP-UM]
- Boethius' criteria for a universal: it must be common to several particulars (1) in its entirety, not only in part, (2) simultaneously, not in temporal succession, and (3) it should constitute the substance of its particulars. He argued nothing in reality can meet them (one thing cannot share the one act of being of many distinct things; a collection of "animalities" would need its own genus, regress), so the universal exists only in the mind; and he answered the objection that a mental universal must be false ("what is understood otherwise than the thing is is false") by distinguishing understanding a thing *to be* otherwise from understanding it *in a different manner* (abstraction is not falsification). [source: SEP-UM §§3–4]
- Logic in the Latin West, from the late tenth century, was studied on Boethius' translations of and commentaries on Porphyry's *Isagoge* and Aristotle's *Categories* and *On Interpretation* plus his own textbooks (the "old logic"). [source: SEP-Med §2.2; SEP-Ab §1.2 lists the *logica vetus*]
- Carolingian stimulus: Latin philosophy in the ninth to eleventh centuries took place in monasteries and later cathedral schools, "first stimulated at the end of the eighth century by Alcuin, Theodulf of Orleans and other scholars at the court of Charlemagne"; based on the seven liberal arts (trivium: grammar, logic, rhetoric; quadrivium: arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, music), usually focused on the first two. [source: SEP-Med §2.2]
- Charlemagne's *Admonitio generalis* (789) and the letter *De litteris colendis* served as manifestos of the revival; Alcuin, a Northumbrian monk, headed the Palace School 782–796 (except 790–793). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolingian_Renaissance]
- John Scottus Eriugena (c. 800 – c. 877), Irish ("scottus"), worked at the court of Charles the Bald in northern France c. 850–c. 870; knew Greek; translated pseudo-Dionysius and Maximus the Confessor; masterpiece the *Periphyseon*, arguing God is not among the things that exist. [source: SEP-Eri; SEP-Med §2.2] His *On Divine Predestination* was condemned at the councils of Valence (855) and Langres (859), in part for over-use of dialectic ("Irish porridge", *pultes scottorum*); the *Periphyseon* was condemned in the thirteenth century for promoting the identity of God and creation. [source: SEP-Eri]
- SEP-Med calls Eriugena and Anselm "the two outstanding Latin thinkers" of 800–1100, both exceptions to the general pattern. [source: SEP-Med §2.2]

## 2. Cathedral schools and Paris before the university
- Cathedral schools arose in Chartres, Orléans, Paris, Laon, Reims, Rouen and German cities; they trained clergy and administrators. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_school]
- Chartres: Bishop Fulbert (c. 1020) made it a leading school; associated with Bernard of Chartres, Thierry of Chartres, William of Conches, John of Salisbury. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_of_Chartres]
- Anselm of Laon (d. 15 July 1117) taught at the cathedral school of Laon from around 1080. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anselm_of_Laon] Not to be confused with Anselm of Canterbury. [source: SEP-Ab §1.1]
- Paris became the centre of Latin philosophy in the twelfth century "in part because the cathedral authorities, rather than just running one school, allowed many, competing ones." [source: SEP-Med §2.5]
- Bec in Normandy: the Benedictine abbey's school, under its prior Lanfranc, was a centre of learning "especially in dialectic". [source: SEP-Ans §1]

## 3. Anselm: life and works
- Dates: SEP-Ans and IEP-Ans give 1033–1109; Wikipedia gives 1033/4, born "sometime between April 1033 and April 1034", in or around Aosta (then in Burgundy). Died 21 April 1109. [source: SEP-Ans §1; IEP-Ans; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anselm_of_Canterbury] WRITER: "1033/4–1109" covers both.
- Left home at 23; came to Normandy 1059; entered Bec as a novice 1060; prior 1063 (when Lanfranc became abbot of Caen); abbot 1078 on Herluin's death. [source: SEP-Ans §1]
- Works at Bec: *Monologion* (1075–76), *Proslogion* (1077–78), dialogues *De grammatico* (probably 1059–60, much disputed), *De veritate*, *De libertate arbitrii*, *De casu diaboli* (1080–86). [source: SEP-Ans §1] IEP-Ans dates the *Monologion* to 1077 and the *Proslogion* to 1078; WP-Proslogion "between 1077 and 1078". WRITER: say "c. 1077–78" for the *Proslogion*.
- Archbishop of Canterbury: enthroned 1093 (SEP); the see had been left vacant by William Rufus to take its revenues; exiled after going to Rome without permission in 1097; recalled by Henry I in 1100; exiled again 1103–1107 (SEP) — Wikipedia gives the exiles as 1097–1100 and 1105–1107. Canonized 1494; Doctor of the Church 1720. [source: SEP-Ans §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anselm_of_Canterbury]
- *Cur Deus Homo* (SEP: 1095–98; WP: 1094–1098), finished in exile (IEP), a dialogue with his student Boso. It argues "on purely rational grounds" that the debt of human sin and the affront to God's infinite dignity could be discharged only by one fully divine and fully human offering his own life; God cannot simply forgive out of mercy without contravening justice, and humans cannot make the required satisfaction. This is the "satisfaction" theory of the atonement. [source: SEP-Ans §4.3; IEP-Ans §7; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cur_Deus_Homo]
- Method: Anselm wrote dialogues and monographs "that scarcely mentioned an author", having absorbed Boethian logic and Augustine; he hit "by his own account in a flash of inspiration" on "an unprecedented argument for God's existence". [source: SEP-Med §2.2]
- "Faith seeking understanding" (*fides quaerens intellectum*) is Anselm's motto (SEP) and was the *Proslogion*'s original title (IEP; WP-Proslogion). [source: SEP-Ans §2.1; IEP-Ans §3]
- Williams warns of two misreadings: (1) that Anselm wants to replace faith with understanding (for Anselm faith is "more a volitional state", love of God; mere belief is "dead", M 78); (2) that the proofs are only for believers: they are meant to convince even unbelievers. Evidence: *Monologion* 1 says someone who does not know these truths "could at least convince himself of most of these things by reason alone, if he is even moderately intelligent" (Williams's translation). [source: SEP-Ans §2.1]
- *Monologion* arguments (ch. 1–4): things good in different degrees are good through one thing that is good through itself and so supremely good; everything exists through something, and ultimately through one thing that exists through itself; a hierarchy of natures (a horse better than wood, a human better than a horse) cannot rise without limit and has one highest member. [source: SEP-Ans §2.2; IEP-Ans §6]
- *Proslogion* preface: after the *Monologion*'s chain of arguments he sought "a single argument that needed nothing but itself alone for proof" (Williams's translation; Sadler: "a single argument [unum argumentum] that needed nothing other than itself alone for proving itself"). [source: SEP-Ans §2.3; IEP-Ans §3] Holopainen (1996) argues the "single argument" is the formula itself, "that than which a greater cannot be thought". [source: SEP-Ans §2.3]
- Anselm also defined freedom of choice as "the power to preserve rectitude of will for its own sake" (DLA 3, Williams's translation). [source: SEP-Ans §4.1] Marenbon: this notion of the will's freedom "cast a long shadow from Scotus to Kant and beyond". [source: SEP-Med §2.2]
- Anselm attacked dialecticians "who think universal substances are nothing but the breath of a voice" (Latin *flatum vocis*), in *De Incarnatione Verbi*, a passage aimed at Roscelin. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roscellinus, footnote quoting the Latin from Schmitt's edition] WRITER: no published English translation of this line was read; give the Latin *flatus vocis* with a gloss such as "a mere breath of the voice".

## 4. The Proslogion argument (ARGUMENT 1), Gaunilo and Anselm's reply
- Name: Anselm never used the term "ontological argument", nor did critics or defenders until Kant's time. [source: IEP-Ans §4]
- Setting: Psalm 14:1 / 53:1, "The fool has said in his heart, 'There is no God'". [source: SEP-Ans §2.1]
- Proslogion 2, key passage, in William Mann's translation (as quoted by SEP-OA §6): "Thus even the fool is convinced that something than which nothing greater can be conceived is in the understanding, since when he hears this, he understands it; and whatever is understood is in the understanding. And certainly that than which a greater cannot be conceived cannot be in the understanding alone. For if it is even in the understanding alone, it can be conceived to exist in reality also, which is greater. Thus if that than which a greater cannot be conceived is in the understanding alone, then that than which a greater cannot be conceived is itself that than which a greater can be conceived. But surely this cannot be. Thus without doubt something than which a greater cannot be conceived exists, both in the understanding and in reality." [source: SEP-OA §6] (Sadler's translation in IEP-Ans §4 uses "thought" for *cogitari*; Latin formula *quo maius cogitari non potest*.)
- Step by step (SEP-OA §6's "first effort" reconstruction): (i) the fool understands the words "that than which no greater can be conceived"; (ii) whatever is understood exists in the understanding; (iii) so it exists in the understanding; (iv) if it exists in the understanding, it can be conceived to exist in reality; (v) it is greater if it exists in reality than if only in the understanding; (vi) it is impossible to conceive something greater than that than which no greater can be conceived; (vii) so it exists in reality. Oppy and Rasmussen note it is "not exactly clear" how (vii) follows from (iii)–(vi), and that modal or Meinongian recastings "seem not to do justice" to Anselm's text. [source: SEP-OA §6] IEP-OA gives a parallel six-step version with "existence is a great-making property" as premise 3. [source: IEP-OA §2]
- Gaunilo, a monk of Marmoutier (near Tours), wrote *On Behalf of the Fool* (*Pro insipiente*) soon after the *Proslogion*. [source: IEP-Ans §5; SEP-OA timeline; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaunilo_of_Marmoutiers] Nothing else of his survives under his name. [source: WP-Gaunilo]
- Gaunilo's four objections (IEP-Ans): (1) a further argument is needed to show the being cannot fail to exist; (2) we cannot really understand "that which is greater than everything" from any genus or species we know; (3) the formula is grasped only as words, so the being is not really "in the understanding"; (4) the Lost Island parody. Throughout he restates Anselm's formula as "that which is greater than everything else that can be thought". [source: IEP-Ans §5]
- The Lost Island, Deane's translation (via IEP-OA): "Now if some one should tell me that there is … an island [than which none greater can be conceived], I should easily understand his words... 'You can no longer doubt that this island which is more excellent than all lands exists somewhere, since you have no doubt that it is in your understanding. And since it is more excellent not to be in the understanding alone, but to exist both in the understanding and in reality, for this reason it must exist. For if it does not exist, any land which really exists will be more excellent than it...'" [source: IEP-OA §2b]
- Anselm's reply (Sadler's translation): (a) Gaunilo misquotes him: "that which is greater than all" and "that than which nothing greater can be thought" "do not have the same value for proving" real existence; (b) we can form an idea of the being by ascending "from lesser goods to greater ones"; (c) not fully understanding something is like not being able to gaze on the sun yet still seeing sunlight; (d) on the island: "if someone should find for me something existing either in reality or solely in thought, besides 'that than which a greater cannot be thought,' to which the schematic framework [*conexionem*] of my argument could rightly be adapted, I will find and give him this lost island, nevermore to be lost." [source: IEP-Ans §5]
- Williams's reading (SEP-Ans §2.3): Anselm offered no specific rejoinder to the island because he rejected Gaunilo's reading; he insisted the argument did not rely on a general principle that existing in reality is greater than existing in the understanding. Correctly understood (Reply to Gaunilo): (1) That than which a greater cannot be thought can be thought; (2) if it can be thought, it exists in reality; (3) therefore it exists in reality. (1) is defended by ascending from lesser to greater goods (Reply 8); (2) because a being capable of non-existence is less great than one that exists necessarily. This reading is developed in Visser and Williams 2008, ch. 5. [source: SEP-Ans §2.3]
- Other assessments of the island: Klima (2000) and Ward (2018) offer Anselmian replies; Wolterstorff (1993) argues Anselm offered no rejoinder because he knew it was unanswerable. [source: SEP-Ans §2.3] Himma's reply on Anselm's behalf: "island than which none greater can be imagined" is incoherent because island-making qualities (e.g. abundance of fruit) have no intrinsic maximum. [source: IEP-OA §2b]
- Proslogion 3, Deane's translation (via IEP-OA §3): "And [God] assuredly exists so truly, that it cannot be conceived not to exist. For, it is possible to conceive of a being which cannot be conceived not to exist; and this is greater than one which can be conceived not to exist. Hence, if that, than which nothing greater can be conceived, can be conceived not to exist, it is not that, than which nothing greater can be conceived. But this is an irreconcilable contradiction." WRITER: use Deane here; Sadler's IEP-Ans version of ch. 3 contains an apparent slip ("which is greater than what cannot be thought not to be").
- Proslogion 4: the fool "thinks" God does not exist only in the sense of thinking the word (*vox*), not the thing. [source: IEP-Ans §4] Proslogion 15: God is "something greater than can be thought". [source: IEP-Ans §3]
- Proslogion 1 contains "I believe in order to understand" (*credo ut intelligam*); Deane's 1903 translation: "For I do not seek to understand that I may believe, but I believe in order to understand. For this also I believe,—that unless I believed, I should not understand." Benedicta Ward's: "I do not seek to understand so that I may believe, but I believe so that I may understand; and what is more, I believe that unless I do believe I shall not understand." The last clause echoes Isaiah 7:9 in the Old Latin/Septuagint form. [source: WS-1 (search results quoting reprints of both translations)] Medium confidence; wording seen only in search snippets.

## 5. Later fortunes of the argument (cross-refs to *Thomas Aquinas*, *Descartes*, *Kant*, *God: Arguments For and Against*)
- Aquinas (1224/5–1274) rejected the deduction: "not everyone who hears this word 'God' understands it to signify something than which nothing greater can be thought, seeing that some have believed God to be a body"; and even granting the concept, "it does not therefore follow that he understands what the word signifies exists actually, but only that it exists mentally" (*Summa*, Ia q.2 a.1, as quoted by IEP-OA). [source: IEP-OA §2c] SEP-OA: Aquinas "criticises an argument which somehow descends from Anselm". [source: SEP-OA timeline]
- Descartes, Fifth Meditation (*Meditations* 1641; foreshadowed in *Discourse* part 4, 1637): a proof from the idea of a supremely perfect being; objections by Caterus and Gassendi. [source: SEP-OA intro and timeline]
- Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason* (SEP timeline lists 1787): three objections including "existence is not a predicate". Meiklejohn's translation: "Being is evidently not a real predicate, that is, a conception of something which is added to the conception of some other thing. It is merely the positing of a thing, or of certain determinations in it." [source: SEP-OA; IEP-OA §2d] Russell: "it is much easier to be persuaded that ontological arguments are no good than it is to say exactly what is wrong with them" (paraphrased by SEP-OA citing Russell 1946: 586). [source: SEP-OA intro]
- Modern defenders/re-formulators: Gödel, Hartshorne, Malcolm, Plantinga. [source: SEP-OA intro] Malcolm, "Anselm's Ontological Arguments", *Philosophical Review* 69 (1960); Plantinga, *The Nature of Necessity* (1974) and *God, Freedom, and Evil* (1974). [source: IEP-OA bibliography; SEP-OA]
- Malcolm objected that "the doctrine that existence is a perfection is remarkably queer" (quoted in IEP-OA). Plantinga: "At first sight, Anselm's argument is remarkably unconvincing if not downright irritating; it looks too much like a parlor puzzle or word magic." [source: IEP-OA §§2d, 3]
- Plantinga's modal version: a maximally great being (maximally excellent — omnipotent, omniscient, morally perfect — in every possible world) is possible; therefore it exists in every world, including ours; critics say it relies on the S5 axiom or begs the question in the possibility premise. [source: IEP-OA §3]

## 6. The problem of universals in the twelfth century (ARGUMENT 2)
- Roscelin of Compiègne (c. 1050 – c. 1121): knowledge of him comes mainly from Anselm and Abelard; teaching by 1087; condemned for tritheism at a council at Soissons in 1092/1093 and recanted; later taught Abelard (at Loches); only a letter to Abelard on the Trinity survives. Otto of Freising: he "was the first in our times to institute the theory of words" (*sententia vocum*); universals are mere words (*voces*), "a breath of the voice" (*flatus vocis*). Often called the founder of nominalism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roscellinus] IEP-Ab dates Abelard's study with Roscelin 1092–1099. [source: IEP-Ab §1]
- William of Champeaux (ca. 1070–1122; birth date may be a decade earlier): Master at Notre-Dame from 1094; archdeacon probably 1104; moved to the abbey of St Victor 1108; bishop of Châlons-sur-Marne 1113; took the Cistercian habit 18 January 1122 and died at Clairvaux eight days later. Taught Hugh of St Victor and was patron of Bernard of Clairvaux. [source: SEP-WC §1]
- Guilfoy's warning: most of what we know of William comes from Abelard, "a man who hated him"; William did not retire in shame but became a papal negotiator; "William prodded Abelard to give up naïve vocalism". [source: SEP-WC §1]
- William's first theory, material essence realism: ten most general things, one per Aristotelian category ("this common thing, substance, which is dispersed through all substances"); genera formed into species by differentiae, species into individuals by accidental forms: "a species is nothing other than a formed genus, an individual nothing other than a formed species". [source: SEP-WC §2, quoting Marenbon's translations]
- Abelard's argument against it (step by step, from SEP-Ab §2, SEP-UM §6, IEP-Ab §2):
  1. Suppose the universal *animal* is one real thing, wholly present in Socrates and in Brunellus the ass at once, constituting the substance of each.
  2. In Socrates it is informed by rationality; in the ass by irrationality.
  3. Since it is one and the same thing in both, one thing is at once rational and irrational: contraries in the same subject, which is impossible.
  4. Further, since each is wholly animal and the animal is one thing, by transitivity Socrates is Brunellus, and so both rational and irrational.
  5. So there is no real universal thing of this kind.
  - Objection: the contraries are present only potentially in the genus. Reply: each species is actually informed by its contrary and the genus is actually and wholly present in each, so the genus is actually informed by both. [source: SEP-Ab §2]
  - Objection: individuals differ by their accidents. Reply: this "makes accidents prior to substance" (SEP-Ab); and the accidents are themselves un-individuated universal essences in their categories, so they cannot individuate (IEP-Ab, quoting Abelard in Spade's translation: "Socrates and Plato are no more diverse from one another because of the nature of quality than they are because of the nature of substance"). [source: SEP-Ab §2; IEP-Ab §2]
- William's second theory, indifference realism: Socrates and Plato are "the same" only in the weak sense that they do not differ in being human: "it is not the same humanity in each one, but a similar [humanity], since they are two men" (*Sententiae* 236). [source: SEP-WC §2; IEP-Ab §2]
- Abelard against indifference: it makes the individual the universal (then Socrates is predicated of Plato when we say "Plato is human"); and "not differing" explains nothing, since Socrates and Plato also do not differ in being a stone. [source: IEP-Ab §2] Against collection theories: collections are posterior to their members and arbitrary; "two men, one squirrel and paper cup could be a universal" (IEP's illustration). [source: IEP-Ab §2; SEP-Ab §2; SEP-UM §6]
- Abelard's account in brief: universality is "not an ontological feature of the world but a semantic feature of language"; a universal is a word (*nomen*, significant word *sermo*) predicable of many. [source: SEP-Ab §2; IEP-Ab §2] His three questions (Spade's translation): "What is the common cause in accordance with which a common name is imposed? What is the understanding's common conception of the likeness of things? Is a word called 'common' on account of the common cause things agree in, or on account of the common conception, or on account of both together?" [source: SEP-UM §6]
- The common cause is the *status*, e.g. "being a man" (*esse hominem*): not a thing, yet not nothing; compared to the cause in "the ship was wrecked because the pilot was absent". [source: SEP-UM §6] Abelard (*Logica 'ingredientibus'*, King/Arlig's translation): "we mean precisely that they are human and don't differ in this regard ... although we're not appealing to any thing". [source: SEP-Ab §4]
- SEP-Ab calls Abelard "the first great nominalist" and his metaphysics "the first great example of nominalism in the Western tradition", while preferring the label "irrealism"; his followers were the *nominales*. [source: SEP-Ab intro, §2; SEP-Med §2.5]
- John of Salisbury's *Metalogicon* (II 17–20) catalogues twelfth-century theories of universals and does not even mention William's. [source: SEP-WC §2]
- Gilbert of Poitiers (c. 1085/90–1154) built a rival metaphysical system; his followers were the *Porretani*. [source: SEP-Med §2.5]

## 7. Abelard: life, method, ethics, condemnations
- Dates: 1079 – 21 April 1142 (SEP); WP gives 12 February 1079. Born at Le Pallet near Nantes, Brittany, into the lesser nobility; renounced inheritance and knighthood. [source: SEP-Ab §1.1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Abelard]
- Studied with Roscelin and William of Champeaux; taught at Melun and Corbeil; returned to Paris between 1108 and 1113 and debated William on universals; around 1113 studied theology with Anselm of Laon. [source: SEP-Ab §1.1] IEP: disputes with William 1108–1110. [source: IEP-Ab §1]
- Scholar-in-residence at Notre Dame (WP: master of the cathedral school c. 1115); affair with Heloise from 1115 or 1116; son Astrolabe; secret marriage; castration on Fulbert's orders; Abelard became a monk at Saint-Denis, Heloise a nun at Argenteuil. [source: SEP-Ab §1.1; WP-Peter_Abelard]
- Council of Soissons condemned his first *Theology* (*Theologia 'summi boni'*) and made him burn it and make a public avowal of faith. Date: 1121 (WP, Mews's dating of related works); IEP-Ab says 1122. [source: SEP-Ab §1.1–1.2; WP-Peter_Abelard; IEP-Ab §1]
- Founded the oratory of the Paraclete; around 1126 became abbot of Saint-Gildas-de-Rhuys in Brittany; handed the Paraclete to Heloise and her nuns (expelled from Argenteuil in April 1129 by Abbot Suger). [source: SEP-Ab §1.1; WP-Peter_Abelard]
- Council of Sens and Bernard of Clairvaux (1090–1153): Abelard asked for a public debate with Bernard; Bernard secretly had the bishops condemn propositions the evening before; Abelard refused to answer and appealed to Rome; nineteen propositions condemned (SEP); Innocent II's bull of 16 July 1141 excommunicated him and imposed perpetual silence (WP); Peter the Venerable of Cluny reconciled him with Bernard and had the sentence lifted. Died at the priory of St Marcel near Chalon-sur-Saône; buried at the Paraclete; now (with Heloise) at Père Lachaise. [source: SEP-Ab §1.1; WP-Peter_Abelard; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_of_Clairvaux] Date of Sens: see Debates.
- His last *Theology* (*Theologia 'scholarium'*) was the work condemned at Sens. [source: SEP-Ab §1.2]
- *Historia calamitatum* ("The Story of My Troubles"), autobiographical letter, written 1132 or soon after (WP). [source: SEP-Ab §1.1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historia_Calamitatum]
- *Sic et non* ("For and Against" / "Yes and No"): 158 questions, each with patristic citations implying yes and others implying no, left unharmonized; the preface lays down rules: look for ambiguity, check context, draw distinctions. [source: SEP-Ab §1.2] First question: whether human faith must be completed by reason. Earliest recension dated 1121 by Mews; revised to 1127–32. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sic_et_Non] His method of reconciling contradictory authorities "was widely adopted ... and would form the basis for later medieval theology". [source: SEP-Med §2.5]
- Abelard "was the first to use 'theology' in its modern sense"; "said by supporter and detractor alike never to have lost an argument". [source: SEP-Ab intro]
- Ethics (*Ethica* or *Scito teipsum*, "Know Yourself", before 1140, breaks off at start of book 2): the agent's intention alone determines moral worth; deeds are morally indifferent. [source: SEP-Ab §§1.2, 6; WP-Peter_Abelard] Sin lies in consent: components of behaviour are vice, will/desire, pleasure, voluntariness, consent and intention, and the deed; only consent/intention bears praise or blame. [source: IEP-Ab §5]
- Ethics arguments (ARGUMENT 3, optional): two men intend to found shelters for the poor; one is robbed first; to rank the one who acts higher is to hold "the richer men were the better they could become ... this is the height of insanity!" (King/Arlig's translation); twins separated at birth who unknowingly marry are blameless; those who crucified Christ in non-negligent ignorance did not sin in that act, and would have sinned had they acted against conscience. Objections: how can one sin voluntarily? (we can "want what we by no means want to want"); intentions are hidden (only God judges; human punishment is exemplary and deterrent, so may even punish the innocent mother who smothered her baby). The formal criterion is the Golden Rule, knowable by reason. [source: SEP-Ab §6]
- *Collationes* (Dialogue of a Philosopher with a Jew and a Christian) contains the "Slave's Wager", apparently the earliest-known version of Pascal's Wager. [source: SEP-Ab §§1.2, 6]
- Students included "three popes and several heads of state". [source: SEP-Ab §1.1]

## 8. Heloise
- Dates: c. 1100? – 1164. WP lede: c. 1101 – 16 May 1164; WP notes a tradition she died at 63 in 1163 or 1164; Abelard calls her *adolescentula*, hence the usual birth c. 1100–01; Constant Mews has suggested she was in her early twenties (born c. 1090), resting on a debatable reading of a letter of Peter the Venerable. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heloise] WRITER: "born around 1100 (some argue c. 1090), died 1164".
- Ward of her uncle Fulbert, canon of Notre Dame; educated at the convent of Argenteuil; Abelard calls her *nominatissima*, most renowned for letters. [source: WP-Heloise]
- Became prioress at Argenteuil, then abbess of the Paraclete, which founded daughter houses. [source: WP-Heloise; SEP-Ab §1.1]
- Corpus: after the *Historia calamitatum* (Ep. 1), seven letters (Ep. 2–8): four "personal letters" (2–5), three "letters of direction" (6–8, including Abelard's Rule for the Paraclete); also the *Problemata Heloissae*, 42 questions on scripture with Abelard's answers. [source: WP-Heloise; WP-Letters_of_Abelard_and_Heloise; SEP-Ab §1.2] Earliest manuscripts late thirteenth century; Jean de Meun translated them into French (c. 1280s). [source: WP-Letters_of_Abelard_and_Heloise]
- As philosopher: Marenbon (SEP-Med §2.9): "Héloïse ... was a philosopher, though not at all a mystic. Writing to Abelard (early 1130s), she radicalized the idea of selfless love, applying it to relations with another human rather than God; and, arguably, she moved Abelard's ethical thought in a new direction, towards an ideal of moderation" (citing Marenbon 1997, 298–303, 310–315). [source: SEP-Med §2.9] Mews argues Heloise shaped some of Abelard's ethical and theological ideas (NDPR review of Mews 2005, via WS-7).
- Her arguments against marriage are reported by Abelard in the *Historia calamitatum* and in her own first letter; she preferred to be his mistress rather than wife. Gist verified (WP-Heloise; WS-3/WS-4 summarising several translations): "if the name of wife seems holier, the name of mistress (Latin *amica*) has always been sweeter to me, or, if you will not be offended, concubine or whore (*scortum*)", and she would rather be called his whore than Augustus' empress. Translations vary ("whore", "mistress", "harlot"; "dearer and more honourable"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heloise; WS-3; WS-4] WRITER: quote only with "in one translation" or paraphrase; the exact Radice (Penguin, 1974) wording was NOT verified.
- She also writes that she "preferred love to wedlock, freedom to a bond" (WP-Heloise, citing the Fordham Medieval Sourcebook text, which uses C. K. Scott Moncrieff's 1925 translation per WS-3); other translations have "freedom to chains". [source: WP-Heloise; WS-3] Medium confidence on the translator attribution.
- Intention: WP quotes her letter as "For it is not the deed itself but the intention of the doer that makes the sin" (translator not named) and reads her as anticipating *Scito te ipsum*. [source: WP-Heloise] Low confidence on exact wording; paraphrase only.
- Context for Heloise's position: "Women were excluded from the universities and, indeed, from most forms of higher education in the Latin Middle Ages." [source: SEP-Med §2.9]

## 9. Hildegard of Bingen (brief, if used)
- Hildegard of Bingen (1098–1179), aristocratic Benedictine abbess; wrote on medicine and natural science and visionary theology in "vivid, poetic Latin, drawing on the Platonic tradition that went back to pseudo-Dionysius"; listed by Marenbon among medieval women recognized only recently as philosophers. [source: SEP-Med §2.9] Major works *Scivias* (1142–1151), *Liber vitae meritorum* (1158–1163), *Liber divinorum operum* (c. 1163/4 – 1172/4); papal approval from Eugenius III at the synod of Trier (1147–48); named Doctor of the Church 7 October 2012. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hildegard_of_Bingen] WRITER: she is outside the schools and the universals debate; at most one or two sentences.

## 10. Peter Lombard's Sentences
- Peter Lombard (c. 1096 – 21/22 August 1160), from near Novara; taught at the cathedral school of Notre-Dame; bishop of Paris 1159. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Lombard]
- *Sentences* written circa 1155 (SEP-Med), four books, setting out "systematically all the problematic questions in theology" with solutions, quoting heavily from Augustine; with the Bible, one of the two set texts of the university theology faculties; much of the best work 1250–1550 was done in commentaries on it. [source: SEP-Med §1.1] From the 1220s to the sixteenth century the most commented Christian work after the Bible. [source: WP-Peter_Lombard]

## 11. Translation movement: Aristotle and Averroes reach Latin
- Before the twelfth century the Latin West had only Boethius' translations of Aristotle's logic (Aristotle chapter: "practically unknown from about 600 to 1100, apart from Boethius' Latin logic"). In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries the rest of Aristotle "was put into Latin, sometimes first via Arabic". [source: SEP-Med §1.3.2]
- James of Venice (d. c. 1147), worked in Constantinople from Greek; translated the *Posterior Analytics* (1125–1150), completing the logical corpus (the "new logic"), and also the *Physics*, *On the Soul* and *Metaphysics* (oldest known Latin version). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_of_Venice; WP-Latin_translations_of_the_12th_century]
- Toledo (taken by Alfonso VI of Castile in 1085): Archbishop Raymond (1126–1151) began translation work. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toledo_School_of_Translators] Translation of philosophical texts "assumed full pace in Toledo in the second half of the twelfth century", with two very prolific translators, Gerard of Cremona and Dominicus Gundisalvi (Gundissalinus); Gundisalvi worked with the Jewish scholar Avendauth on Avicenna's *De anima*. Motives: translators' interest, demand from the French schools, Arabic manuscripts in newly conquered cities, the archbishop's patronage. [source: SEP-AII §1]
- Gerard of Cremona (c. 1114–1187) went to Toledo no later than 1144 to read Ptolemy's *Almagest*; credited with 87 translations, including Aristotle's *Posterior Analytics*, *Physics*, *On the Heavens*, *On Generation and Corruption*, *Meteorology*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerard_of_Cremona; WP-Latin_translations_of_the_12th_century]
- Michael Scot (1175 – c. 1232): worked in Toledo (translated al-Biṭrūjī in 1217) and then at Frederick II's court in Sicily; translated Averroes' commentaries (*De anima*, *De caelo*, with Aristotle's texts). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Scot] SEP-AII names Michael Scot and William of Luna as the most productive Averroes translators, associated with the Hohenstaufen or papal court in Sicily/southern Italy, and says Latin Averroes translations reached the universities "only about thirty years after Averroes' death in 1198", i.e. around 1228–30. [source: SEP-AII §1]
- William of Moerbeke (1215–1286) later made very accurate literal translations of all Aristotle from the Greek. [source: SEP-Med §1.3.2]
- Effect: these translations "transformed the university syllabuses in the thirteenth century". [source: SEP-Med §1.3.3]

## 12. The new universities, faculties and the disputation
- Bologna: traditionally 1088 (Carducci's committee, 19th century); Frederick Barbarossa's *Authentica habita* 1158; Paul Grendler: unlikely "university" before the 1150s and perhaps not before the 1180s; organized as student guilds (*universitas scholarium*) by the late twelfth century; famous for law. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bologna]
- Paris: "By about 1200, the Paris schools had become a university, and at much the same time a university developed at Oxford." [source: SEP-Med §2.7] Emerging c. 1150 from the cathedral school; royal charter of Philip II in 1200; papal recognition 1215; Gregory IX's bull *Parens scientiarum* of 13 April 1231 after the strike of 1229. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parens_scientiarum]
- Oxford: teaching from c. 1096; grew from 1167 when Henry II barred English students from Paris; chancellor from at least 1214; masters recognized as a *universitas* 1231; Cambridge founded by Oxford émigrés in 1209. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford]
- Faculties: an Arts Faculty (by far the most students) and the higher faculties of Theology, Law and Medicine; in the thirteenth century Arts Faculties "became in effect Aristotelian Faculties"; theology faculties came to be dominated by Franciscan and Dominican friars. [source: SEP-Med §2.7]
- Method: *lectio* (reading with commentary), then *quaestio*; *disputationes* were arranged to resolve controversial questions, including open *quodlibet* disputations where the master later "determined" the question; the quaestio method began where two authorities seemed to contradict each other. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism] The written form puts a question, gives opposing arguments, a counter-position, the master's answer and replies (WP-Scholasticism; Periergia's overview describes the same structure). The "Question Commentary" was a distinctive university form. [source: SEP-Med §1.1]

## 13. Bans on Aristotle at Paris and their lifting; 1277 as cross-ref
- 1210: the provincial synod of Sens (including the bishop of Paris) ruled: "Neither the books of Aristotle on natural philosophy or their commentaries are to be read at Paris in public or secret, and this we forbid under penalty of excommunication"; it had only local force and applied to the arts faculty; theologians were free to read them. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condemnations_of_1210–1277]
- 1215: the statutes of the papal legate Robert of Courçon allowed Aristotle's logic but said his books "on Metaphysics or Natural Philosophy, or the abridgements of these works, are not to be read". [source: WS-5 (Medieval Sourcebook text of the statutes and Pasnau, "The Latin Aristotle", as summarised)] WRITER: WP-Parens_scientiarum says the statutes "mandat[ed] the teaching of Aristotle" — true only of the logic; do not repeat.
- 1231: Gregory IX reaffirmed the ban "until these books shall have been examined and purged from all suspicion of errors" and appointed a commission (William of Auxerre, Simon of Authie, Stephen of Provins); whether it ever acted is unknown. [source: WS-5]
- Toulouse (founded 1229) advertised that students could hear "the books of Aristotle which were forbidden at Paris"; the works were probably read privately at Paris and publicly discussed by about 1240; Oxford had no ban. [source: WP-Condemnations_of_1210–1277]
- 1255: the statutes of the Paris arts faculty declared all known works of Aristotle mandatory reading (and the *Liber de causis*, then thought Aristotle's), which helped make Averroes' commentaries "the principal secondary literature of Latin university culture". [source: SEP-AII §§1, 3]
- 1270: Bishop Étienne (Stephen) Tempier condemned 13 propositions (e.g. one intellect for all humans; the world is eternal). [source: WP-Condemnations_of_1210–1277]
- 1277: on 7 March Tempier prohibited 219 theses discussed in the arts faculty; motives, targets and effects are disputed. [source: SEP-1277] Cross-ref *Thomas Aquinas* and *Scotus, Ockham and the Late Medieval Debates*; *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes and the Quarrel over Reason* covers Latin Averroism and the "double truth".

## Debates
- Where is Anselm's proof, and is Proslogion 3 a separate (modal) argument? Charlesworth (1965): main proof in ch. 2, the rest corollaries; Malcolm (1960): main proof in ch. 3, ch. 2 an inferior first attempt; Campbell (1976), Tapp & Siegwart (2022): one proof across chs. 2–3; La Croix (1972): the whole work; Hartshorne defended modal arguments "allegedly derived from Proslogion" 3; Holopainen (1996): the "single argument" is the formula itself. [source: SEP-OA §6, timeline; SEP-Ans §2.3; IEP-Ans §4 ("what some commentators take to be a second ontological argument")]
- Did Anselm rely on "existence in reality is greater"? Consensus/Gaunilo reading (SEP-OA, IEP-OA) vs Williams (SEP-Ans): Anselm denied relying on that general principle; the argument is "if it can be thought, it exists". Some deny Anselm meant a proof at all (SEP-OA §6).
- Is the island objection answerable? Wolterstorff (no; Anselm knew it), Klima and Ward (yes), Himma (the perfect island is incoherent). [source: SEP-Ans §2.3; IEP-OA §2b]
- Origin of the proof: SEP-Aug (Tornau): "Anselm's proof of the existence of God develops the argument of *De libero arbitrio*, bk. 2"; Marenbon (SEP-Med) calls it "unprecedented", by Anselm's account a flash of inspiration; SEP-Ans cites *On Free Choice* 2.10 only for the value-intuitions behind the divine attributes. [source: SEP-Aug; SEP-Med §2.2; SEP-Ans §3.1]
- Is "faith seeking understanding" only for believers? Williams rejects both the "replace faith" and the "only for believers" readings. [source: SEP-Ans §2.1] Debate over whether Anselm's work is philosophy or theology is "long debated". [source: IEP-Ans §2]
- William of Champeaux's reputation: Abelard's self-serving account (defeated, shamed) vs Guilfoy: William became a papal statesman, and pushed Abelard beyond "naïve vocalism". [source: SEP-WC §1]
- What is Abelard's *status*? Klima: a real non-thing, "an object of the divine mind" from eternity; Guilfoy (IEP): not an item in his ontology, a basic feature of individuals, and Abelard concedes God's understanding of a not-yet-created kind is "empty". [source: SEP-UM §6; IEP-Ab §2]
- Date of the Council of Sens: SEP-Ab gives 3 June 1140; IEP-Ab gives both 1140 and 1141 in different paragraphs; WP gives 2 June 1141 and calls the traditional 1140 "mistaken", following Constant Mews, *Speculum* 77 (2002). WRITER: "1141 (traditionally dated 1140)". [source: SEP-Ab §1.1; IEP-Ab §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Sens]
- Date of Soissons: 1121 (WP) vs 1122 (IEP-Ab). WRITER: "1121". [source: WP-Peter_Abelard; IEP-Ab §1]
- Authenticity of the Abelard–Heloise letters: John Benton (from 1972) was the main sceptic, partly retracting in 1980; Gilson, Dronke, Mews, Waithe defend authenticity; by the early 2000s most scholars accepted them (Barbara Newman 1992, "Authority, authenticity, and the repression of Heloise", called "a sensible discussion"). [source: WP-Letters_of_Abelard_and_Heloise; WP-Heloise]
- *Epistolae duorum amantium* (113 anonymous letters, "V" and "M", copied by Johannes de Vepria, prior of Clairvaux, late fifteenth century): Ewald Könsgen proposed Abelard and Heloise as authors (1974 edition); Newman (1992) rejected, later accepted; Mews (1999, *The Lost Love Letters of Heloise and Abelard*) and C. Stephen Jaeger accepted; Dronke and von Moos rejected; Ziolkowski (2005) disputed the evidence; Schnell (2022) reads them as parody; Ostrowski (2020) notes misattribution could silence a different real woman. WP claims "the majority of scholars" now reject the attribution (unsourced claim). [source: WP-Letters_of_Abelard_and_Heloise; WP-Heloise]
- Consent in the relationship: Abelard's later letter says he "frequently forced your consent ... by threats and blows"; Mary Ellen Waithe (1989) read Heloise as a victim; others (Levitan, Wulstan) read Abelard as exaggerating his guilt; Heloise's letters present her as having chosen him. [source: WP-Heloise]
- Heloise as philosopher and her influence on Abelard's ethics: Marenbon ("arguably"), Mews. [source: SEP-Med §2.9; WS-7]
- Was the "Toledo School" a school? WP: translation efforts "were not methodically organized" before 1085 and the formal school is associated with Alfonso X in the thirteenth century; SEP-AII speaks of a "translation movement" with a likely coherent program modelled on al-Fārābī's *Enumeration of the Sciences*. [source: WP-Toledo_School_of_Translators; SEP-AII §1]
- Effects of 1210/1215 bans: unclear whether they stopped study at Paris. [source: WP-Condemnations_of_1210–1277] Effects of 1277 disputed (Duhem's thesis no longer endorsed — as the published *Aristotle* chapter says). [source: SEP-1277]

## Consistency with published Periergia texts
- *History of Philosophy* overview: states Anselm's argument as "existing in reality is greater than existing in the understanding alone" — this is the consensus/Gaunilo reading; Williams (SEP-Ans) argues Anselm disowned that premise. Not a contradiction if the chapter presents it as the standard reading and names the debate. The overview also says "it was Averroes's Aristotle, translated into Latin, that ignited the European thirteenth century": an oversimplification — Aristotle came also directly from Greek (James of Venice, later Moerbeke), and Latin Averroes arrived only c. 1228–30 (SEP-AII). Chapter should say "Aristotle, read with Avicenna and Averroes".
- *Augustine* chapter: "Anselm developed his proof of God from *On Free Choice of the Will*". Supported by SEP-Aug, but Marenbon calls the proof unprecedented; present as "drew on" / debate rather than flat derivation.
- *Aristotle* chapter: 1210 synod at Sens; 1277 Tempier 219 theses; Aristotle practically unknown 600–1100 except Boethius' logic — all consistent.
- *Falsafa*: Toledo, Gerard of Cremona, Gundissalinus with Avendauth — consistent with SEP-AII.
- *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes*: Latin Averroism, double truth, 1277 — consistent; no overlap problems.
- *Neoplatonism*: Porphyry's questions via Boethius; Eriugena translated pseudo-Dionysius — consistent.
- History *Lords, Peasants and Towns*: Bologna "traditionally dated to 1088 though securely attested only in the twelfth century"; Paris "emerging around 1150 and recognised by the king in 1200" — consistent.
- History *Sex, Love and Marriage*: Heloise "around 1115–16"; letters accepted by most scholars; "would rather be called Abelard's whore than the empress of Augustus" — consistent in gist (translation-dependent); Peter Lombard on consent in marriage — not checked here.
- History *Early Medieval Europe*: Alcuin and the Carolingian Renaissance — consistent.
- No contradictions found requiring correction of published texts.
