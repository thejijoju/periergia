## 1. The Warring States setting and the "Hundred Schools" label
- **Dates of the Warring States period differ by source; give a range and say why.** Sima Qian's start date of 475 BCE is the one most often cited; others use 453 BCE (the partition of Jin) or 481 BCE (end of the Lu chronicle). The end is 221 BCE (Qin unification). [source: WP-Warring_States_period] SEP-Leg uses 453–221 BCE; SEP-Mo, SEP-MC and SEP-SN use 479–221 BCE; IEP-Mozi and IEP-Zh use 403–221 BCE. [source: SEP-Leg §1.2; SEP-Mo intro; IEP-Mozi §1; IEP-Zh §1] The published History chapter uses 475–221 BCE (consistent with the most-cited convention).
- The name "Warring States" comes from the *Record of the Warring States* (*Zhanguo ce*), compiled in the early Han. [source: WP-Warring_States_period]
- Seven major states at the start of the period: Qin (west), Qi (east), Chu (south), Yan (north), and the "Three Jins" Wei, Han and Zhao. [source: WP-Warring_States_period]
- Context (Pines): the end of the Spring-and-Autumn period saw "progressive disintegration of political structures in the Zhou world"; by the 4th c. BCE individual states had re-centralised but interstate warfare intensified. Most thinkers came to believe that the unity of "All-under-Heaven" (*tianxia*) was the only means to peace. [source: SEP-Leg §1.2]
- Changes of the period (Pines): loose aristocratic polities replaced by centralised, bureaucratised territorial states (Lewis 1999); iron tools raised agricultural yields, with population growth, urbanisation and commercialisation; the crossbow and mass infantry armies of peasant conscripts replaced aristocratic chariot armies; the hereditary aristocracy was eclipsed by the *shi* ("men of service"), who owed their position to ability rather than pedigree. "For intellectuals ... this was a golden age." [source: SEP-Leg §1.2]
- Itinerant scholars wandered from court to court offering advice on government, war and diplomacy. [source: WP-Hundred_Schools_of_Thought]
- **Jixia Academy**, in Linzi, capital of Qi. The *Shiji* passages are generally read as crediting it to King Xuan of Qi c. 318 BCE, but Xu Gan credited it to King Xuan's grandfather, Duke Huan of Tian Qi, and Sima Qian's passages are consistent with King Xuan having *restored* rather than founded it. So "founded" needs a hedge. [source: WP-Jixia_Academy]
- **The label "hundred schools" (*baijia*).** The phrase "writings of the hundred schools" (百家語) appears in Li Si's memorial of 213 BCE as reported in the *Shiji* (ch. 6), which ordered such books handed in for burning. [source: WP-Burning_of_books_and_burying_of_scholars (quotes the Chinese); SEP-Leg §6, "Discourses of the Hundred Schools"] "Hundred" means "many", not a count.
- **Sima Tan's six schools (FLAG: a later Han classification).** Sima Tan (c. 165–110 BCE), father of Sima Qian, wrote the essay "On the Essentials of the Six Schools" (*Lun liujia yaozhi*), preserved in the last chapter (ch. 130) of the *Shiji*. The six: Ru (Confucians), Mo (Mohists), Dao (Daoists), Fa ("Legalists"), Ming (School of Names), Yin-Yang. [source: WP-Sima_Tan; SEP-Leg §1]
- Sima Tan coined *fajia*, and the essay is "primarily an argument for the Daojia", the school Sima Tan favoured. Neither Sima Tan nor Sima Qian names any individual thinker under these categories; the imperial librarians Liu Xiang (79–8 BCE in SEP-Leg; 77–6 BCE in WP) and Liu Xin later used them to catalogue texts in the imperial library. [source: WP-Sima_Tan; SEP-Leg §1]
- Hansen states it bluntly: the Simas' intellectual history "fabricated" the *dao*, *fa*, *ming* and Yin-Yang "schools"; counting Confucians and Mohists, this "reduced the 'hundred schools' of the period to six". [source: SEP-Zh §3] Fraser: the "School of Names" is "a taxonomical fiction". [source: SEP-SN intro] Pines: *fajia* "was designated primarily as a bibliographic category"; "no early thinker identified himself as a member of the 'School of fa'". [source: SEP-Leg §1]
- Han Feizi ch. 50 ("Illustrious teachings") already speaks of "the prominent teachings of this generation" as those of the Ru and the Mo, says the Ru split into eight and the Mo into three after their founders' deaths. [source: WP-Burning_of_books (quoting Han Feizi 50); SEP-MoTriads]
- Sima Tan studied Daoism "under Master Huang" (a Huang-Lao master). [source: WP-Sima_Tan; WP-Huang–Lao]

## 2. Mozi and the Mohist movement
- **Mozi = Mo Di**, "Master Mo". **Dates as ranges:** SEP-Mo: "fl. ca. 430 BCE", "most likely ... flourished during the middle to late decades of the 5th century BCE, roughly contemporaneous with Socrates". IEP-Mozi: "active from the late 5th to the early 4th centuries B.C.E.", before Mencius. SEP-Leg: "ca. 460–390 BCE". WP-Mozi: c. 470–c. 391 BCE. The published History chapter says "about 470–391 BCE": acceptable, but the chapter should present a range and say the dates are uncertain. [sources as named]
- "Little is known, not even what state he was from." The *Shiji* says only that he was an official of the state of Song and lived at the same time as or after Confucius (d. 479 BCE). Some sources make him a native of Lu. [source: SEP-Mo §1; IEP-Mozi §1]
- "Mo" means "ink"; scholars have speculated it was an epithet for a labourer's or convict's dark or tattooed skin, but it may just be a rare surname. [source: SEP-Mo §1]
- One passage shows King Hui of Chu (488–432 BCE) refusing him an audience because of his low status. Anecdotes depict him as master craftsman and military engineer; the Han-dynasty *Huainanzi* claims he was an apostate Confucian, but the *Mozi* gives no reason to believe this; Fraser thinks he was probably an artisan, perhaps a carpenter. [source: SEP-Mo §1] (WP-Mozi states as fact that he was a carpenter and schooled in Confucianism: treat both as tradition/conjecture.)
- Fraser: "A strong argument can be made that it is Mozi, not Confucius, who deserves the title of China's first philosopher." Mozi and his followers "initiated philosophical argumentation and debate in China" and were "the first in the tradition to engage, like Socrates in ancient Greece, in an explicit, reflective search for objective moral standards and to give step-by-step, tightly reasoned arguments for their views, though their reasoning is sometimes simplistic or rests on doubtful assumptions". [source: SEP-Mo intro, §1]
- They advanced "the world's earliest form of consequentialism, a remarkably sophisticated version based on a plurality of intrinsic goods taken as constitutive of collective human welfare". [source: SEP-Mo intro]
- **Organisation.** Mohist groups were each led by a *juzi* (grand master); members trained, were placed in government or military posts, contributed funds, and could be expelled. The *Hanfeizi* (Book 50, c. 233 BCE or later) and *Zhuangzi* (Book 33) mention six groups in total; the *Lüshi chunqiu* (c. 239 BCE) names at least three more *juzi*. [source: SEP-Mo §1]
- **Defensive warfare.** "Far from being pacifists", they saw strong defence as crucial; some became specialists in defending cities under attack. The last quarter of the *Mozi* preserves texts on defence tactics and siege fortification (Yates 1980). [source: SEP-Mo §1] IEP: the community "functioned as an international rescue organization". [source: IEP-Mozi §1]
- A Han text in the *Zhuangzi* (Book 33, "Under Heaven") describes later Mohists wearing rough clothes and grass sandals, "never resting day or night, taking self-sacrifice as the highest", modelling themselves on the sage-king Yu, "until there's no flesh left on their calves or hair on their shins" (Fraser's translation). Fraser notes this is from "an unsympathetic source" and that the *Mozi* texts themselves do not advocate self-sacrificing altruism. [source: SEP-Mo §1]
- **Influence and decline.** At its peak in the 4th–3rd centuries BCE "no school was more influential"; Mencius lamented that "the statements of Mo Di and Yang Zhu fill the world". Critics paired Ru and Mo ("Ru-Mo") as the two moralising schools. [source: SEP-Mo §9]
- After the Qin unification (221–207 BCE) the movement declined, "eventually vanishing altogether by the middle of the Western Han". After Confucianism won imperial favour in 136 BCE, the *Mozi* fell into neglect; it survived only because it was copied into the Daoist canon. Interest revived in the late Qing. [source: SEP-Mo §9]
- Reasons for decline (Fraser): Graham's view that after unification the Mohists lost their political role as craftsmen and defence specialists; but "the major factor is probably that ... Mohism gradually collapsed into irrelevance": its appealing ethics were absorbed by Confucianism, its anti-war stance was made redundant by unification, the later canons were nearly unintelligible, and what was left was "harsh, unappealing" frugality and anti-music views. [source: SEP-Mo §9]
- Confucian debt: the Han Confucian Gongsun Hong described *ren* in Mohist terms, "extending benefit and eliminating harm, inclusively caring without partiality" (*Han Shu* 58). [source: SEP-Mo §9]
- The Qing scholar Sun Yirang published a reconstruction of the Mohist Canon in 1897, which encouraged Chinese intellectuals (Yan Fu, Liang Qichao, Hu Shi) to see classical Chinese thought as philosophical in the European sense. [source: SEP-Zh §4]

## 3. The *Mozi*: texts and the "three-voice" (triad) structure
- The *Mozi* is "an anthology of diverse writings probably composed at different times by different writers or editors. No part of the anthology purports to be from the hand of Mo Di himself." [source: SEP-MoTexts]
- A Han-or-earlier table of contents lists 71 books (*pian*), of which 18 are lost, in five groups: (1) seven miscellaneous short texts, probably the latest (mid-late 3rd c. BCE); (2) the core: ten "triads" plus anti-Confucian books (books 8–37 are the triads); (3) six "Later Mohist" books (books 40–45: Canons, Explanations, "Greater Selection", "Lesser Selection"); (4) five "dialogues" (books 46–50, perhaps mid-late 4th c. BCE, conversations "at least partly fictional"); (5) 21 books on siege defence, ten lost. [source: SEP-MoTexts; SEP-MC §2]
- **The triads.** Each of the ten doctrines was represented by three identically titled essays labelled "upper", "middle" and "lower". 23 of the 30 triad essays survive (seven lost); six triads are complete. The three versions run parallel for paragraphs at a time but differ in language, structure and content. [source: SEP-MoTexts; SEP-MoTriads]
- **Debate over why there are three versions:**
  - "Three factions" theory, first proposed by the late Qing scholar Yu Yue (1821–1907): the upper/middle/lower series are the texts of the three sects into which, according to *Hanfeizi* 50, the Mohists split after Mozi's death. [source: SEP-MoTriads]
  - A. C. Graham's modified three-factions theory (1985): three factions with distinct tendencies he called "Purist", "Compromising" and "Reactionary" (1989, p. 51). IEP: Graham proposed that the triads "correspond to oral traditions of Mohist doctrine transmitted by the three Mohist sects". [source: SEP-MoTriads; IEP-Mozi §2]
  - "Successive revision" theory (Brooks 1996; Fraser 2010): the versions are chronological strata, later, longer essays revising earlier ones. Fraser rejects the faction theories because no evidence links the three sects to the triads, and the *Zhuangzi* and *Lüshi chunqiu* suggest six or more Mohist groups, not three. Fraser also notes the strata "do not coincide exactly" with the upper/middle/lower labels. [source: SEP-MoTriads; SEP-MoTexts]
- Dating clues: Book 18 must postdate 431 BCE (mentions a war that year); Book 19 probably predates 334 BCE (lists Yue as a warlike state; Yue was absorbed by Chu that year). [source: SEP-MoTexts]
- All but three triad essays begin "Our Master Mozi states..." Because pre-Han writers commonly put their own ideas in a venerated teacher's mouth, this formula does not show that Mozi held the views. [source: SEP-MoTexts]
- Style: IEP quotes Burton Watson that the style is "marked by a singular monotony of sentence pattern, and a lack of wit or grace", but the arguments "are almost always presented in an orderly and lucid, if not logically convincing fashion". [source: IEP-Mozi §2]

## 4. The ten doctrines
- Five pairs, matching the ten triads (Fraser's labels): Promoting the Worthy / Identifying Upward; Inclusive Care / Condemning Aggression; Moderation in Use / Moderation in Burial; Heaven's Intent / Understanding (Elucidating) Ghosts; Condemning Music / Condemning Fatalism. [source: SEP-Mo §2]
- Book 49 ("Questions of Lu") tells Mohists which to expound depending on the state's problem: disorder → promoting the worthy and identifying upward; poverty → moderation in use and burial; musical excess → condemning music and fate; dissolution → honouring Heaven and serving ghosts; aggression → inclusive care and condemning aggression. [source: SEP-Mo §2]
- "Benefit" (*li*) = an objective list of three goods: material wealth, a large population, and social order (*zhi*). [source: SEP-Mo §2, §7] IEP and WP cite Ivanhoe & Van Norden's label "state consequentialism" (maximising "the wealth, order, and population of the state"). [source: WP-Mozi]
- Key line (Fraser's translation): "Does it benefit people? Then do it. Does it not benefit people? Then stop." (Book 32, "Condemning Music"). [source: SEP-Mo §7]
- Fraser: Mohist ethics resembles rule utilitarianism but is better called "practice consequentialism" or "*dao* consequentialism": the unit of evaluation is a way/practice, not individual acts. Unlike Bentham and Mill it promotes not individual happiness but mostly collective goods. [source: SEP-Mo §7]
- **Custom is not morality (argument).** Defenders of lavish burials say the gentlemen of the central states have always done it. Mozi replies with the country of Gai Shu east of Yue, where they ate the first-born son "calling this 'advantageous to the younger brothers'" and abandoned the grandmother when the grandfather died: "These practices superiors took as government policy and subordinates took as custom ... Yet how could these really be the Way of benevolence and righteousness?!" (Book 25, "Moderation in Burial", Fraser's translation). Fraser compares Herodotus. [source: SEP-Mo §3] IEP makes the same point. [source: IEP-Mozi §4]
- **Fa (models, standards).** "The hundred artisans make squares with the set square, circles with the compass, straight lines with the string, vertical lines with the plumb line, and flat surfaces with the level ... Now for the greatest to order the world ... without models to measure by, this is to be less discriminating than the hundred artisans." (Book 4, "Models and Standards", Fraser's translation). [source: SEP-Mo §3]
- The same text rejects parents, teachers and rulers as models ("Those in the world who are parents are many, but those who are benevolent are few"), concluding "Nothing is like modeling on Heaven. Heaven's conduct is broad and impartial ..." [source: SEP-Mo §3]
- *Fa* later became central to Xunzi and his student Han Fei and gave the *fa jia* its name. [source: SEP-Mo §3]
- **Moderation in use:** IEP/WP quote (Van Norden's translation, *Mozi* ch. 20): "What is the purpose of houses? It is to protect us from the wind and cold of winter, the heat and rain of summer, and to keep out robbers and thieves. Once these ends have been secured, that is all." [source: WP-Mozi, citing Van Norden 2011 p. 52] (second-hand)

## 5. The argument for inclusive care (*jian ai*), step by step
- Translation: *jian ai* is "sometimes translated as 'universal love' but probably better rendered as 'inclusive care'" (Fraser); IEP (Loy) uses "impartial concern"; *ai* "typically seems to refer to a dispassionate concern". [source: SEP-Mo §7; IEP-Mozi intro]
- Full slogan: "inclusively caring for each other, and in interaction benefiting each other". The attitude is all-inclusive; the beneficial behaviour is directed at those we actually interact with. [source: SEP-Mo §7]
- **Reconstruction (from Book 16, "Inclusive Care", and Fraser's analysis):**
  1. The great harms of the world (states attacking states, clans disordering clans, people injuring people) are to be explained by their causes. [SEP-Mo §7; IEP-Mozi §3]
  2. They arise from "exclusion" (*bie*): being "for" oneself, one's family or one's state and not for others. [SEP-Mo §7]
  3. If people were "for others' states as for their state", "who alone would deploy his state to attack others' states? One would be for others as for oneself." (same for cities and clans). [SEP-Mo §7, Book 16, Fraser's translation]
  4. States not attacking each other and people not injuring each other is "benefit to the world". [ibid.]
  5. These benefits are produced "from caring about others and benefiting others", which is "inclusion", not "exclusion". [ibid.]
  6. Whatever produces great benefit to the world is right (the benefit standard). [SEP-Mo §7]
  7. Conclusion: "Inclusion is right (*shi*)." [ibid.]
- A second, independent justification: inclusive care is Heaven's intention. [source: SEP-Mo §7; IEP-Mozi §7]
- **Main objection 1 (false dilemma).** *Bie* means "despising and hurting others", excluding them from moral consideration altogether; the Mohists never seriously consider graded concern. So the argument shows that total exclusion is wrong and inclusive care permissible, but if meant to show inclusive care obligatory it "pos[es] a false dilemma": another guideline might promote benefit equally well without requiring equal concern. [source: SEP-Mo §7] IEP: the same false-dilemma problem affects the "caretaker" thought experiment. [source: IEP-Mozi §7]
- **The caretaker thought experiment** ("Impartial Concern" C): if you were going on a long journey and had to entrust your family to someone, you would choose an impartialist over a partialist. Objection (Chad Hansen, Bryan Van Norden): this shows only that partialists want *others* to be impartial, not that they have reason to be impartial themselves. Reply (Loy): perhaps the Mohists only need to show that even partialists have reason to *propagate* the doctrine. [source: IEP-Mozi §7] Fraser: the Mohists treat choosing a *dao* as choosing a universal code binding oneself too, so the rational egoist who wants everyone else but himself to follow it is, to them, incoherent. [source: SEP-Mo §7]
- **Objections raised in the texts and Mohist replies:** (a) too difficult → rulers have got people to do harder things (dieting, rough clothes, dying at command), and with leadership and incentives people can practise it "within a generation"; (b) impossible → the sage-kings practised it; (c) cannot be "used" → the caretaker scenarios; (d) undermines filial piety → a filial son wants others to benefit his parents, and the way to get this is to benefit others' parents (reciprocity). [source: SEP-Mo §7; IEP-Mozi §8]
- **Demandingness and its softening.** In theory, equal concern ("regard others' states as though regarding one's state", Book 15) looks untenable; but the practical examples ask little more than "commonsense moral decency". The later Mohist *Da Qu* (Book 44) says we care for all equally but benefit some more than others according to relationship, roughly the view attributed to the Mohist Yi Zhi in *Mencius* 3A5: "In care, no degrees; in practice, begin from what is close" (Fraser's translation). Fraser: "Contrary to a common misperception, Mohist ethics does not advocate that we treat everyone alike, but only that we have similar moral concern for everyone." [source: SEP-Mo §7]
- **The Confucian objection (Mencius).** Mencius "equated inclusive care with renouncing one's father (for this reason, he dismissed Mozi as a 'beast')"; later Confucians followed him, arguing that it runs counter to human nature. [source: SEP-Mo §7] Chinese text of *Mencius* 3B9: 楊氏為我，是無君也；墨氏兼愛，是無父也。無父無君，是禽獸也。 ("Yang is 'for oneself': this is to have no ruler. Mo's inclusive care: this is to have no father. To have no father and no ruler is to be a beast.") [source: WS-1, Warp Weft and Way page quoting the Chinese with Van Norden's translation; my gloss of the Chinese, which the chapter should replace with a named translation] Legge's version, as quoted on WP-Yang_Zhu (via Durant 1963): "Mo's principle is, 'To love all equally'—which does not acknowledge the peculiar affection due to a father. To acknowledge neither king nor father is to be in the state of the beast." (second-hand)
- Fraser: inclusive care "was generally not the doctrine that attracted criticism from the Mohists' contemporaries"; ancient critics mostly attacked their austerity, opposition to music and plain burials. Mencius was the exception. [source: SEP-Mo §7]
- **Interpretive debate on the foundation of Mohist ethics:** Fung Yu-lan, Graham and Schwartz read it as utilitarianism (benefit primary); Dennis Ahern, David Soles and Augustine Tseu read "Heaven's Will" as a divine command theory (criticised by Kristopher Duda). Loy thinks both modern labels fit badly. [source: IEP-Mozi §6] Fraser: the consequentialist standard "was justified by appeal to the intention of Heaven". [source: SEP-Mo §1]

## 6. The three standards (*fa*) and anti-fatalism
- Three *fa* for statements (*yan*), given in the three "Condemning Fatalism" essays (Books 35–37): a statement must have a **root** (the deeds of the ancient sage-kings), a **source** (what people see and hear) and a **use** (when applied in punishment and government, it benefits the state, clan and people). [source: SEP-Mo §4] The three versions differ, so four distinct tests can be identified in all; "conformity to the Will of Heaven and the spirits" appears only in version B. [source: IEP-Mozi §5]
- Version C (IEP's translation): "To expound doctrine without first establishing standards (*yi*) is like telling time using a sundial that has been placed on a spinning potter's wheel." [source: IEP-Mozi §5]
- **Anti-fatalism argument (all three tests):** (1) history: the sage-kings brought order and the tyrants turmoil under the same conditions, so outcomes depend on policy, not fate; (2) no one has ever seen or heard fate; (3) believing in fate is harmful: people would stop trying to be virtuous or industrious. Fraser's objection: the Mohists "oddly ... overlook the obvious fatalist rejoinders" (that the outcomes were predestined, that fate is not a physical object, that some are fated to be diligent). [source: SEP-Mo §4]
- Fatalism undermines the three goods (wealth, population, order). [source: SEP-Mo §2]
- Ghosts: Book 31 ("Elucidating Ghosts") has Mozi argue that even if there really are no ghosts, we should still act as if there are, because of the social benefit. Fraser takes this to show the third test has priority, applying a utilitarian criterion even to descriptive questions. [source: SEP-Mo §4]
- **Debate over truth:** Chad Hansen (1985, 1992) argues that early Chinese thinkers addressed the pragmatic question of appropriate word use rather than semantic truth; criticised by Graham (1989) and Harbsmeier (1998). Fraser thinks the strong thesis "unconvincing" but that the Mohists do merge descriptive and normative questions under "the proper *dao*". [source: SEP-Mo §4]
- Knowledge for the Mohists is a reliable ability to draw distinctions in practice. Blind people can say "what's bright is white" but cannot pick white from black: "it's not by their naming, it's by their selecting" (Book 47). [source: SEP-Mo §4]

## 7. Against aggressive war
- **Argument by analogy** ("Against Military Aggression" A; "Heaven's Will" C): the military aggressor acts like the thief, robber or murderer; everyone agrees these are wrong; the greater the harm, the greater the wrong ("The more one injures another, the greater his unbenevolence, and the more severe the crime", Book 17, Fraser's translation); so aggressive war is a great wrong. [source: IEP-Mozi §11; SEP-Mo §7]
- Inconsistency charge: rulers who forbid killing one person but praise killing many are like someone who calls a little black "black" but much black "white", or a little bitter "bitter" but much bitter "sweet" (Book 17 and "Heaven's Will" C). [source: SEP-Mo §4; SEP-MC §7.2]
- Pragmatic arguments (versions B and C): war costs even the aggressor; the few states that profited are like a medicine that cured four or five out of myriads. [source: IEP-Mozi §11]
- Not pacifism: version C distinguishes justified "punishment" of tyrants by the sage-kings from aggression; the Mohists defended besieged states. [source: IEP-Mozi §11; SEP-Mo §1]
- Anecdote (dialogues): Mozi persuades the ruler of Chu not to attack Song, and on the way home is refused shelter from the rain by a Song gatekeeper. [source: SEP-MoTexts]

## 8. The later Mohist Canons: logic, optics, geometry
- The Canons are Books 40–45 of the *Mozi*: two books of terse "canons" (*jing*), two of "explanations" (*jing shuo*), the "Greater Selection" (*Da Qu*) and the "Lesser Selection" (*Xiao Qu*), together called the *Mo Bian* ("Mohist Dialectics"). Written and compiled most likely between the late 4th and mid 3rd century BCE. [source: SEP-MC intro, §2]
- The Canons begin with 87 explications of terms, then (after a corrupt section) 86 theses. They are "the most difficult and corrupt in all the classical Chinese literature"; Graham's 1978 reconstruction is the best, though questioned (Geaney 1999). [source: SEP-MC §2; SEP-MoTexts]
- Topics: semantic theory, epistemology, consequentialist ethics, analogical argument, causality, space and time, mereology, and "geometry, mechanics, optics, and economics". The later Mohists "decisively refute the once widespread view that ancient Chinese thinkers were ... uninterested in language, epistemology, and logic". [source: SEP-MC intro]
- No axiomatic system; nothing clearly corresponding to axioms or theorems. [source: SEP-MC §2]
- **Disputation (*bian*).** Canons A73–74: disputation is contending over which of two complementary terms (e.g. "ox" / "non-ox") fits a thing; the text states versions of the law of excluded middle (A73) and the law of non-contradiction (A74). [source: SEP-MC §7]
- Four argumentative moves in the "Lesser Selection" include analogy (*pi*), "pulling" and "pushing" (*tui*); these were probably standard 3rd-century practice (Mencius uses them). [source: SEP-MC §7.2]
- "Killing robbers is not killing people": critics charged that since robbers are people, killing robbers is killing people, inconsistent with inclusive care; the Mohists replied that "robbers are people, but killing robbers is not killing people" (a "this but not so" case). [source: SEP-MC] (Use only as an example; Fraser's full analysis was not extracted.)
- **Optics:** one of the earliest known written accounts of a pinhole image is in the *Mozi*: the image in a "collecting-point" or "treasure house" is inverted by an intersecting point; light from the feet is partly hidden below and forms the top of the image. [source: WP-Camera_obscura, citing Needham, *Science and Civilisation in China* IV.1] The text is from the Canons (WP says "4th century BC"). Aristotle (or a follower) discusses pinhole images in *Problems* XV. [source: WP-Camera_obscura]
- Geometry example: the Canons note that a circle is "nowhere straight" (A98). [source: SEP-MC]
- The later Mohists are associated with the School of Names through shared disputes over "the hard and white" and "the same and different" (*Zhuangzi* 33). [source: SEP-MC intro]

## 9. Laozi and the *Daodejing*
- "Laozi" is best taken to mean "Old (*lao*) Master (*zi*)"; some read "Master Lao" as a surname. The *Zhuangzi* consistently says "Lao Dan", not "Li Er". [source: SEP-Lao §1] It is an honorific, not a personal name. [source: WP-Laozi]
- **The legend (Sima Qian, *Shiji* 63, c. 100 BCE):** native of Chu; family name Li, given name Er, also called Dan; keeper of archives at the Zhou court; consulted by Confucius on ritual; seeing Zhou decline he left; at the north-west border the official Yin Xi asked him to write down his teachings; he wrote "some five thousand" characters in two parts on "the meaning of Dao and virtue", then left, "no one knew where". [source: SEP-Lao §1]
- "Few scholars outside China today would subscribe fully to the *Shiji* report." Fung Yu-lan: Sima Qian "confused" the legendary Lao Dan with a later Li Er. Graham (1986): the story conflates legends; Laozi and Zhuangzi were classed together as one "Daoist school" only in the Han. [source: SEP-Lao §1]
- **Debate (attribute by name):** Alan Chan (SEP) writes that he finds "little reason not to accept the traditional claim that Laozi was a senior contemporary of Confucius", and argues the figure appears favourably in both Confucian and Daoist sources, so was unlikely to be invented polemically; but he warns against calling Laozi "founder" or "father" of Daoism. [source: SEP-Lao §1] By contrast Littlejohn (IEP): the single-author tradition "is almost universally rejected by scholars now"; the text is a collection of materials from "ancient masters" collected in versions from about 300 BCE until Wang Bi's standard edition (226–249 CE). [source: IEP-CP] WP: "Modern scholarship generally regards his biographical details as later inventions." [source: WP-Laozi] The chapter should say that Laozi is legendary *as described*, and that the existence of some historical Lao Dan is debated.
- The *Zhuangzi* (late 4th c. BCE) is the first text to use Laozi as a personal name and to identify Laozi with Lao Dan; 17 *Zhuangzi* passages feature Laozi, three in the Inner Chapters. [source: IEP-Lao]
- In religious Daoism Laozi is revered as a supreme deity; the Tang imperial Li family traced its ancestry to him. [source: SEP-Lao intro, §1]
- **Date and composition.** Bodies of sayings were probably written down from the second half of the 5th century BCE, in overlapping collections consolidated during the 4th century; the *Laozi* "probably had reached a relatively stable form" by the middle of the 3rd century BCE. D. C. Lau called it an "anthology" (1963). Liu Xiaogan (rhyme patterns closer to the *Shijing*) defends the traditional early date; William Baxter puts "the bulk ... [in] the mid or early fourth century". [source: SEP-Lao §2]
- **Mawangdui silk manuscripts (1973):** two manuscripts, "A" and "B", near Changsha, Hunan, in a tomb sealed 168 BCE; "A" probably copied before 195 BCE. Both put the *De* part (ch. 38 onward) *before* the *Dao* part, so Henricks titled his translation *Te-Tao Ching* (1989). [source: SEP-Lao §3]
- **Guodian bamboo slips (1993):** late 1993 (October 1993 per WP), tomb M1 at Guodian, Jingmen, Hubei, near the old Chu capital; tomb dated around 300 BCE; robbed before excavation. Some 800 slips, 730 inscribed, over 13,000 characters, of which about 2,000 match the *Laozi*, in three groups (A: 39 slips; B: 18; C: 14). Published as *Guodian Chumu zhujian* (1998). "They remain the oldest extant *Laozi* texts." [source: SEP-Lao §3; WP-Bamboo_Texts_of_Guodian] WP: the tomb's occupant is identified as a tutor to the crown prince who became King Qingxiang of Chu, and the texts are dated c. 300 BCE because Qin sacked the Chu capital Ying in 278 BCE. [source: WP-Bamboo_Texts_of_Guodian]
- Guodian chapter 19 reads "cut off artificiality, discard deceit" where the received text reads "Cut off benevolence (*ren*), discard rightness (*yi*)". Some infer the text became more anti-Confucian in transmission; Chan warns this is hasty, because Guodian C has the ch. 18 claim that *ren* and *yi* arose after the great Dao declined. [source: SEP-Lao §3]
- **Guodian dating debate:** unclear whether the slips are one text in three parts, selections from a longer original, or three texts from different sources; A and C give different versions of part of ch. 64, suggesting different sources; Ding (2000) dates group A to c. 400 BCE. [source: SEP-Lao §3] Guodian does **not** include chapter 1. [source: WP-Tao_Te_Ching] Before Guodian, Graham (1989) argued for a late date (Inner Chapters of the *Zhuangzi* show no knowledge of the text); after Guodian "few modern scholars follow a full late theory". [source: WP-Tao_Te_Ching]
- A Western Han bamboo *Laozi* (the "Beida Laozi") was given to Peking University in January 2009; dated to the second half of Emperor Wu's reign; authenticity challenged but accepted by those who worked on it. [source: SEP-Lao §3]
- Received text: 81 chapters in two parts (*Daojing* chs. 1–37; *Dejing* chs. 38–81); "five-thousand character" text; most versions exceed 5,000 by 5–10%. The 81-chapter division was not universal until perhaps the Tang. The title *Daodejing* was not widely used until the close of the Han; the text was accorded "classic" status in the Han (tradition credits Emperor Jing, r. 156–141 BCE). [source: SEP-Lao §3] Two main commentaries: Wang Bi (226–249 CE) and Heshang Gong. [source: SEP-Lao §3]
- Over 2,000 translations according to Misha Tadd (2022). [source: SEP-Lao §1] (WP gives other estimates: over 250 Western translations; 1,930 into 94 languages. Give "over a thousand" or name the counter.)
- In 733 CE Emperor Xuanzong ordered all officials to keep a copy and put it on the examination syllabus. [source: SEP-Lao §1]

## 10. Dao, de, wuwei, ziran, pu
- *Dao*: the graph suggests a path; "most commentators agree in translating *dao* as 'way'"; as a verb it also means "to speak", so *dao ke dao* is literally "Dao that can be dao-ed", parallel to *ming ke ming*, "the name that can be named". [source: SEP-Lao §6]
- The Dao is nameless and formless (chs. 1, 14), "dark" (*xuan*), *wu* (not-having), and generative: "Dao gives birth to One; One gives birth to Two; Two gives birth to Three; Three gives birth to the ten thousand things" (ch. 42, Chan's translation). "I do not know its name; I style it Dao" (ch. 25). [source: SEP-Lao §6]
- *De*: virtue, potency, efficacy, power; commentaries from the *Hanfeizi* onward pun on *de* "to obtain": what a thing has obtained from the Dao. Arthur Waley: a "latent power". [source: SEP-Lao §6]
- *Ziran*: literally "self-so"; "naturalness" or "spontaneity"; Dao is not modelled on anything (ch. 25). [source: SEP-Lao §7]
- *Wuwei*: "nonaction"; translators also use "non-assertive action", "non-coercive action" or "effortless action". "*Wuwei* does not mean total inaction"; in the *Laozi* it contrasts with action driven by self-serving desire. "The highest virtue is not virtuous; therefore it has virtue" (ch. 38). [source: SEP-Lao §7]
- Political side: the *Laozi* condemns war (ch. 30), cruel punishment (ch. 74) and heavy taxation (ch. 75); if the ruler rid himself of desire, the world would be at peace of its own accord (chs. 37, 57); ch. 65 tells the ruler to keep the people "ignorant", which may be literal political advice. Ch. 80 depicts the ideal polity as "a small country with few inhabitants". "Governing a large country is like cooking a small fish" (ch. 60). [source: SEP-Lao §§1, 5, 7]
- **Uncarved block (*pu*):** "the utterly simple, unaffected, and seemingly valueless *pu*, a plain uncarved block of wood", symbol of Daoist integrity (chs. 28, 32). [source: SEP-Lao §7] D. C. Lau: "the uncarved block is in a state as yet untouched by the artificial interference of human ingenuity and so is a symbol for the original state of man before desire is produced in him by artificial means". Pas and Leung (1998) criticise the reified "uncarved block" rendering, preferring "wholeness". [source: WP-Pu_(Taoism)] Littlejohn's image: the Daoist wants to be "unhewn and natural wood", the Confucian a carved sculpture. [source: IEP-Dao]
- **Six readings of the *Laozi* (Chan's survey):** mythological (Girardot: chaos myth; mother goddess); mystical (Mair: indebted to Indian mysticism; Schwartz: *sui generis*); spiritual/religious; philosophical-metaphysical (W. T. Chan; Creel: "purposive" not contemplative); a philosophy of life (Liu Xiaogan; Ames and Hall: "making this life significant"); and **political**: "an ethical and political masterpiece intended for the ruling class" (Lau 1963, LaFargue 1992, Moeller 2006). Graham: mystical and political, probably aimed at the ruler of a small state (1989). Hansen (1992): an "anti-language" philosophy. [source: SEP-Lao §5]
- Chan's own view: the Heshang Gong (cosmological, *qi*-based) and Wang Bi (ontological, "nonbeing") readings are both within the text's range. [source: SEP-Lao §§6–7]
- Han Feizi chs. 20–21 contain the earliest known commentaries on the *Laozi*, read politically. [source: WP-Tao_Te_Ching; SEP-Lao §3]

## 11. The *Zhuangzi*
- Zhuangzi = Zhuang Zhou, "Master Zhuang", late 4th century BCE; roughly contemporary with Mencius. Han biographers say he came from Meng (in Song, per IEP), held minor posts, and lived in the reign of King Wei of Chu (ended c. 327 BCE). Details cannot be confirmed outside the text. [source: SEP-Zh §1; IEP-Zh §1] Some recent scholars doubt his authorship of any of the text and even his existence (IEP; Klein 2010). [source: IEP-Zh §1, §2]
- **Text:** Guo Xiang (d. 312 CE) cut a 52-chapter collection to 33 chapters: Inner Chapters (1–7), Outer (8–22), Miscellaneous (23–33). Only the Inner Chapters are conventionally attributed to Zhuangzi, but "Zhuangzi's direct responsibility for any of the 'inner' chapters remains a hypothesis" (Hansen, citing Klein 2010). [source: SEP-Zh §2; IEP-Zh §2]
- Graham (after Kuan Feng; Liu Xiaogan and Harold Roth with variations) divides the rest among: the School of Zhuangzi; Yangists (followers of Yang Zhu); "Primitivists" (close to the *Laozi*, anti-civilisation); and Syncretists (Liu: Huang-Lao), dominant in the miscellaneous chapters. [source: SEP-Zh §2; IEP-Zh §2]
- Graham (1989): "[Zhuangzi] never knew he was a [Daoist]"; the Inner Chapters Zhuangzi neither met Laozi nor knew the *Daodejing*; the Laozi link comes from the Outer Chapters. [source: SEP-Zh §3]
- Style: the parable, often a dialogue; characters include Confucius, Lao Dan, fish, birds, a talking skull, butchers, cicada catchers, wheelwrights. [source: SEP-Zh §§2–3] Arthur Waley (1939): "one of the most entertaining as well as one of the profoundest books in the world". [source: WP-Zhuangzi_(book), quoted in Graham 1981]
- The second chapter, *Qi wu lun* (Watson: "Discussion on Making All Things Equal"; IEP: "Discussion on Smoothing Things Out"), is the philosophical core. [source: IEP-Zh §3]
- Skill exemplars: Butcher Ding carving an ox for Lord Wen Hui (ch. 3). [source: SEP-Zh §4.8]
- Xunzi's "Dispelling Obsessions" names Zhuangzi as one who saw the value of nature but not of the human. [source: IEP-Zh §4]
- **Butterfly dream** (end of ch. 2, Inner Chapters) — see Quotations. Graham's reading: "the distinction between waking and dreaming is another false dichotomy. If [one] distinguishes them, how can [one] tell if [one] is now dreaming or awake?" (Graham 1981, pp. 21–22). [source: WP-Zhuangzi_(book)] Hansen: an illustration of "mild skepticism" about whether our sense of liberation from error goes only one way. [source: SEP-Zh §4.8] Note the final line ("there must be some distinction! This is called the Transformation of Things") — the passage does NOT simply say there is no difference.
- **The happy-fish debate** (ch. 17, "Autumn Floods" — an OUTER chapter, not an inner one). Set out as an argument (from Watson's translation as quoted in WP, and Hansen's in SEP):
  1. Zhuangzi: the minnows darting about are happy.
  2. Huizi: you are not a fish; so you cannot know that the fish are happy. (Implicit premise: one can know X's state only if one is X.)
  3. Zhuangzi turns the premise against him: you are not I; so (by your premise) how do you know that I do not know?
  4. Huizi accepts the premise: I am not you, so I don't know about you; but you are not a fish, so "the case is complete" that you don't know.
  5. Zhuangzi: "Let's go back to the beginning": you asked *how* (literally *whence*, 安 *an*) I know, which presupposes you already knew that I know; "I know it right here above the Hao."
  - Readings: Graham notes Huizi's question uses the locative 安 ("whence?/from where?") rather than 何 ("how?"), so Zhuangzi's last move can be read as answering "from here", a point about perspective, not a cheap pun. [source: WP-Zhuangzi_(book), citing Graham 2001 p. 123; SEP-Zh §4.7] Hansen: Huizi "cannot consistently insist that speakers can only use *zhi* (know) when they occupy the perspective of the object"; Zhuangzi "uses perspective relativism to justify a way of claiming to know". [source: SEP-Zh §4.7–4.8] Nivison: it shows reason "subtly employed in order to make an anti-rationalist point"; others read it as wordplay. Victor Mair compares it to a Socratic dialogue. [source: WP-Zhuangzi_(book)] Monograph: Roger Ames and Takahiro Nakajima (eds.), *Zhuangzi and the Happy Fish* (2015). [source: IEP-Zh bibliography]
- Zhuangzi's wife's death: Huizi finds him "pounding on a tub and singing" (ch. 18, Watson). [source: WP-Zhuangzi_(book)]
- **Debate: how to read Zhuangzi's perspectivism.**
  - *Relativist*: Guo Xiang's reading, which "has become the received interpretation": each thing has its own nature and value; the great Peng bird's view is no better than the little birds'. Coutinho (IEP): radical relativism is self-undermining, since Zhuangzi would have to admit his view is no better than Confucianism or Mohism, "a consequence that Zhuangzi does not recognize", so radical relativism "is clearly a misreading". Graham, following Guo Xiang, gives a relativistic reading based on the conventionality of language. [source: IEP-Zh §3–4] Hansen calls Zhuangzi's view an "indexical" or "perspectival" relativism, "mildly skeptical of the relativism itself". [source: SEP-Zh intro, §4.8]
  - *Sceptical*: Paul Kjellberg and Lisa Raphals compare Zhuangzi with Hellenistic (Pyrrhonian) scepticism: fallibilism, suspension of ultimate judgments, equanimity. Hansen reads "linguistic skepticism". [source: IEP-Zh §3–4]
  - *Therapeutic*: Hansen notes "non-substantive therapeutic skepticism (Raphals, Kjellberg, and Schwitzgebel, in Kjellberg and Ivanhoe (eds) 1996)" — scepticism as a recommendation that loosens our confidence rather than a doctrine. [source: SEP-Zh §4.7] Book: P. J. Ivanhoe and Paul Kjellberg (eds.), *Essays on Skepticism, Relativism, and Ethics in the Zhuangzi* (SUNY, 1996). [source: IEP-Zh bibliography]
  - *Ironic*: Ziporyn (2012) treats references to perfect, transcendent guidance as "ironic"; Moeller and D'Ambrosio see poking fun at such pretence as the point. [source: SEP-Zh §2]
  - *Mystical/cosmological*: Coutinho's third option: settling into "the axis of the way" (*daoshu*), non-conceptual "unknowing" (*wuzhi*), meditative practice. [source: IEP-Zh §3] Traditional orthodoxy read Zhuangzi as a mystical follower of Laozi; Hansen says recent discoveries "have largely laid that ancient orthodoxy to rest" (Hansen's claim). [source: SEP-Zh intro]
- Coutinho: Zhuangzi is responding to the later Mohist insistence on bivalent distinctions (a judgment is either *ke* or *bu ke*, never both). [source: IEP-Zh §3]

## 12. The School of Names (*mingjia*): Hui Shi and Gongsun Long
- The label was given by 2nd-century BCE Han archivists; "a taxonomical fiction": Deng Xi, Yin Wen, Hui Shi, Gongsun Long and others "never formed a distinct circle or movement". Before the Han they were called *bian zhe*, "disputers". Han archivists listed seven figures. They disappeared with the Qin. [source: SEP-SN intro, §1]
- They have been called Chinese "sophists"; Fraser notes "superficial similarities" (rhetoric, lawsuits, paradoxes, accused of arguing to win) but different social roles. [source: SEP-SN §1]
- **Hui Shi (Huizi):** fl. 313 BCE (SEP-SN); 370–310 BCE (WP); 380–305 BCE (IEP-Zh) — give a range. A minister of Wei (Liang). Zhuangzi's friend and foil. [source: SEP-SN §5; WP-Hui_Shi; IEP-Zh §1]
- Our main source for his doctrines is the "Under Heaven" chapter (*Zhuangzi* 33), probably written long after his death, which may not record his own views; their arguments are lost. "His books filled five carts." [source: SEP-SN §5.1]
- **The ten theses (Fraser's translation, SEP-SN §5.1):** (1) "The ultimately great has no outside, call it the Great One. The ultimately small has no inside, call it the Small One." (2) "The dimensionless cannot be accumulated, its size is a thousand miles." (3) "Heaven is as low as earth, mountains are level with marshes." (4) "Just as the sun is at noon, it is declining. Just as things are alive, they are dying." (5) same and different on a small and large scale; "The myriad things all being the same or all being different, this is called 'same and different on a large scale.'" (6) "The south has no limit yet has a limit." (7) "Today go to Yue but arrive yesterday." (8) "Linked rings can be disconnected." (9) "I know the center of the world. It is north of Yan and south of Yue." (10) "Universally care for the myriad things. Heaven and earth are one unit."
- Fraser's reading: the theses "revolve around the theme that distinctions are not inherently fixed, but relative to a perspective"; thesis 5 is the key; 1, 5 and 10 are doctrines rather than paradoxes; thesis 10 is the only one with ethical import (it echoes Mohist inclusive care). Any interpretation is "partly speculative". [source: SEP-SN §5.1] Hansen calls Hui Shi "a rival linguistic relativist". [source: SEP-Zh intro]
- Hui Shi's defence of analogy to the King of Liang (*Shuo Yuan* 11): explaining a *dan* as "like a bow, but with a bamboo string"; "Explanations are inherently a matter of using what a person knows to communicate what he doesn't know". [source: SEP-SN §5]
- **Gongsun Long:** c. 320–250 BCE; retainer to the Lord of Pingyuan (d. 252 BCE) in Zhao; advised King Hui of Zhao against war and cited Mohist inclusive care to him, so possibly once a Mohist. Of 14 scrolls listed in the *Han History*, the extant *Gongsun Longzi* has five short dialogues and a late introduction; Graham argued three dialogues are later forgeries, so probably only "White Horse", "Indicating Things" and a bit of another are genuine pre-Han. [source: SEP-SN §6]
- **"White horse is not horse" (*bai ma fei ma*). Reconstruction of the five arguments (Fraser's translations):**
  - Arg. 1: "'Horse' is that by which we name the shape. 'White' is that by which we name the color. Naming the color is not naming the shape. So white horse is not horse." Reply: reject premise 3; naming the object with the colour is naming the object with the shape (the arguer "separates hard and white").
  - Arg. 2: If someone seeks a horse, a brown or black horse will do; if someone seeks a white horse, it will not; if white horse were horse, what is sought would be the same; so white horse is not horse. Fraser: this trades on the ambiguity between predication ("white horses are among horses", true) and identity ("white horse = horse", false), using something like Leibniz's law.
  - Arg. 3: horse combined with white is not the same as horse; so white horse is not horse.
  - (Args 4–5 continue in the same vein.) [source: SEP-SN §6.1]
  - Context (the lost bow): Gongsun Long claimed Confucius himself distinguished "Chu person" from "person" (the King of Chu story); Kong Chuan replied that dropping "Chu" broadens the referent, while "white" fixes the colour. [source: SEP-SN §6.1]
- **Readings of the White Horse (a named debate):** denial of identity of universals (Fung Yu-lan 1958; Cheng 1983) — now widely rejected, since "no ancient Chinese philosopher held a realist doctrine of universals"; kind and identity relations (Cikoski 1975, Harbsmeier 1998); part-whole relations (Hansen 1983, Graham 1989); extensions of compound phrases (Hansen 1992); use/mention (Thompson 1995). Harbsmeier and Fraser: Gongsun Long was probably a court entertainer or "intellectual prankster", his arguments "a medley of gross fallacies and logical subtleties, roughly a Chinese analogue to ... Lewis Carroll". Graham: "No one has yet proposed a reading of the dialogue as a consecutive demonstration which does not turn it into an improbable medley of gross fallacies and logical subtleties" (1990). [source: SEP-SN §6, §6.1]
- The "John Doe has three ears" anecdote (*Lüshi chunqiu* 18.5): Kong Chuan asks whether the Lord of Pingyuan will follow "what is easy and right, or what is difficult and wrong". [source: SEP-SN §6]
- Xunzi cites a version of the white-horse sophism as an incorrect use of names (*Xunzi* 22.3). [source: SEP-SN §6]

## 13. Legalism / the *fa* tradition
- Pines uses "the *fa* tradition" because "Legalism" is "a popular—albeit quite inaccurate—designation"; *fa* means "standards", "models", "methods" as often as "law" (Goldin 2011); and "school" implies a self-aware movement that did not exist. Waley (1939) proposed "Realists"; Kai Vogelsang (2016) defends "political realism". [source: SEP-Leg §1]
- Goal: "a rich state and a powerful army" (*fu guo qiang bing*). Graham (1989: 269): the Legalists were the first Chinese political philosophers "to start not from how society ought to be but how it is". Schwartz (1985) compares them to modern social science. [source: SEP-Leg §2]
- Core four (texts associated with): Shang Yang, Shen Buhai, Shen Dao, Han Fei. Han Fei (ch. 43, "Defining the standards") presents himself as synthesiser and improver of Shang Yang and Shen Buhai; ch. 40 defends and improves Shen Dao. [source: SEP-Leg §1]
- Of ten *fajia* texts in the Han catalogue, six were lost over a millennium ago; two survive intact (*Book of Lord Shang*, *Han Feizi*); two in fragments (*Shenzi* of Shen Buhai; *Shenzi* of Shen Dao). The *Guanzi* was reclassified as *fajia* from the Sui (581–618). [source: SEP-Leg §1.1]
- **Shang Yang** (Gongsun Yang, Wei Yang, Lord Shang; c. 390–338 BCE): from the small state of Wey; entered Qin service in 359 BCE under Duke Xiao; chief minister for about twenty years; two rounds of reform, 356 BCE and 350 BCE. [source: WP-Shang_Yang]
  - Reforms: mutual responsibility and compulsory denunciation (failing to report a crime punished like the crime); ranks of merit replacing hereditary aristocracy; promotion of agriculture and war, suppression of commerce and of "talkative intellectuals"; second round standardised land allocation and taxation; double tax on households with more than one adult son. [source: WP-Shang_Yang; SEP-Leg §3]
  - Pines: the system of 20 (initially fewer) ranks of merit, awarded especially for decapitating enemies or purchasable for grain, "one of the most daring acts of social engineering in human history"; ranks not fully inheritable, so considerable social mobility. Qin became "arguably the most formidable military machine in China's long history". [source: SEP-Leg §3.2]
  - Book of Lord Shang (Pines's translations, via SEP): "When the people are weak, the state is strong" (20.1); "to eradicate punishments with punishments" (18.1); "There is no single way to order the generation; to benefit the state, one need not imitate antiquity" (1.4); Poems, Documents, rites, music, goodness, self-cultivation, benevolence, uprightness, argumentativeness, cleverness — "when the state has these ten", it cannot fight (3.5). Pines: this "alienating rhetoric" is concentrated in a few chapters; the text attacks "moralizing discourse" rather than morality as such. [source: SEP-Leg §§2.1, 3.1, 6]
  - The *Book of Lord Shang* is heterogeneous: some chapters perhaps by Shang Yang, others decades or more than a century after his death. English translations: Duyvendak (1928), Pines (2017). [source: SEP-Leg §1.1]
  - Death (338 BCE): after Duke Xiao died, King Huiwen turned on him; executed by being torn apart by chariots (*cheliè*), with his family. Sima Qian's version: he fled, raised a rebellion, was killed in battle, and his corpse was torn apart. Pines: the events of his death "are too legendary to reliably comment on". [source: WP-Shang_Yang]
  - Even critics such as Sima Qian acknowledged that he turned the Qin people into law-abiding subjects (*Shiji* 68). [source: SEP-Leg §3.1]
- **Shen Buhai** (c. 400–337 BCE): chancellor of the state of Hán under Marquis Zhao for about fifteen years, dying in office 337 BCE; associated with administrative technique (*shu*), though the term is Han Fei's and likely posthumous. Fewer than three dozen fragments of his text survive (Creel 1974). [source: WP-Shen_Buhai; SEP-Leg §1.1]
- **Shen Dao** (dates uncertain: WP gives c. 360–c. 285 or c. 395–c. 315 BCE; SEP "fourth century BCE"); associated with the Jixia Academy; possibly a conflation of several people; over 120 authentic fragments survive (translated by Eirik Lang Harris 2016). Associated with positional power (*shi*). Shen Dao (Harris's translation): "Even if the law is bad, it is better than absence of laws"; "the Son of Heaven is established for the sake of All under Heaven, it is not that All under Heaven is established for the sake of the Son of Heaven". [source: WP-Shen_Dao; SEP-Leg §§1.1, 5.1]
- **Han Fei** (c. 280–233 BCE): a scion of the ruling house of the state of Hán (not the Hàn dynasty); the *Shiji* says he studied with Li Si under Xunzi, stuttered and so wrote rather than spoke. [source: WP-Han_Fei; SEP-Leg §1.1] The *Han Feizi* has all 55 chapters attested in the Han catalogue; whether he wrote all of them is debated (Queen 2013 doubts; Goldin and Pines find most chapters coherent). Full English translation by W. K. Liao (1939–1959); selections by Burton Watson (2003); new one by Harbsmeier forthcoming. [source: SEP-Leg §1.1]
- **Han Fei's three key terms:** *fa* (standards/laws, which must be public: "compiled and written down on charts and documents ... promulgated to the hundred clans", 38.8.2); *shu* (techniques of rule, which must be hidden: "techniques of rule ... are hidden in the breast ... laws (*fa*) are best when they are clear, whereas techniques should not be seen", 38.8.2); *shi* (positional power, from Shen Dao). Plus *xingming* ("performance and title"): matching a minister's proposal to his task and results, rewarded or punished accordingly (ch. 7, "Two levers"; Goldin compares a call for bids). [source: SEP-Leg §§2.3, 4.2, 5.1; WP-Han_Fei]
- "The two handles" = reward and punishment, which the ruler must never delegate. [source: WP-Han_Fei; SEP-Leg §5.1]
- Han Fei on ministers: "If a minister does not murder his ruler, this is because the cliques and cabals are not formed yet" (8.8). "The sage orders the officials, not the people" (35.4.1). [source: SEP-Leg §4.2]
- **Han Fei's critique of Confucian (and Mohist) moralism:**
  - Human motivation: people are selfish and this cannot be changed by education; there are "no more than ten honest and trustworthy men of service, but there are hundreds of offices"; so government must not depend on virtuous men (49.11, "Five Vermin"). [source: SEP-Leg §2.2]
  - Appeals to antiquity are unverifiable: "those who openly adduce the former monarchs as evidence and claim they can determine indubitably Yao and Shun, are either fools or impostors" (50.1, "Illustrious Teachings"); since Confucius and Mozi died, the Ru split into eight and the Mo into three, each claiming to be the true one, and Yao and Shun "cannot return to life" to adjudicate. [source: SEP-Leg §2.1; WP-Burning_of_books]
  - Changing times: in remote antiquity "the people were few whereas goods were plenty; hence people did not compete" — moral suasion worked then, not now. [source: SEP-Leg §2.1]
  - Lists the Confucians among the "five vermin" (ch. 49). [source: WP-Han_Feizi]
  - The "spear–shield" (*maodun*, contradiction) argument: one cannot praise both Yao and Shun. [source: WP-Han_Feizi]
  - Designed for average rulers: ch. 40 says the system should work for average (*zhong*) rulers, neither paragons nor monsters. [source: SEP-Leg §5.2] Graham: Han Fei's ruler "has no functions which could not be performed by an elementary computer ... Might one even say that in Han Fei's system it is ministers who do the ruling?" (1989: 291). [source: SEP-Leg §5.2]
  - Ideal state: "there are no texts written on bamboo strips, but the law is the teaching; there are no 'discourses' of the former kings, but officials are the teachers" (49.13). [source: SEP-Leg §6]
- **Debate on Han Fei:** often condemned as a defender of "monarchic despotism" (Hsiao 1979); 20th-c. scholars called the *fa* thinkers "totalitarians" (Creel 1953 and others), a label Pines thinks "equivocal". Pines stresses the "entrapped sovereign" paradox. Comparisons with Machiavelli are common. [source: SEP-Leg §§5–6; WP-Han_Fei]
- **Han Fei's death (233 BCE):** the King of Qin (later the First Emperor) admired his writings; Han Fei came to Qin; Li Si and Yao Gu had him imprisoned, suspecting loyalty to Hán; Li Si sent him poison and he killed himself; the king regretted it. This is Sima Qian's account; Masayuki Sato (2013) doubts its details, since it dramatically makes Li Si his destined opponent; Goldin (2013) thinks the outline "probably not far from the truth" (executed 233 BCE "after being entrapped by Li Si"). [source: WP-Han_Fei; SEP-Leg §1.1, "allegedly killed in the custody of the King of Qin"]
- **Qin unification:** the king of Qin, Ying Zheng, conquered the six other states 230–221 BCE and took the title First Emperor (Shi Huangdi) in 221 BCE. [source: WP-Qin_Shi_Huang; consistent with published History chapter] Qin dynasty 221–207/206 BCE (SEP-Leg: 221–207). Li Si (c. 280–208 BCE) is called the architect of the Qin empire. [source: SEP-Leg §1.1; WP-Li_Si]
- **"Burning of books" (213 BCE) and "burying of scholars" (212 BCE) — the historicity debate:**
  - The *Shiji* (ch. 6) reports Li Si's 213 BCE proposal: burn all histories other than Qin's; anyone outside the court erudites (*boshi*) holding the *Poems*, *Documents* or "writings of the hundred schools" must hand them in; books on medicine, divination, agriculture and forestry exempt; "Those who have interest in laws shall instead study from officials." The next year more than 460 scholars were reportedly "buried alive" at Xianyang. [source: WP-Burning_of_books; SEP-Leg §6]
  - Pines: the burning was "often misinterpreted as the victory of 'Legalist' over 'Confucian' ideology, but this is wrong. Confucianism as such was not targeted; actually, it prospered among the court erudites (Kern 2000)." The target was private learning, not doctrine. It "backfired", producing lasting literati hatred of Qin and the *fa* tradition. [source: SEP-Leg §6]
  - Michael Nylan (2001): the story does not bear scrutiny; the court kept specialists on the *Poems* and *Documents*; "Confucian" as a label for the classics came only in the Han; losses may reflect the razing of the Qin palace in 206/207 BCE. Martin Kern (2010): Qin and early Han writings cite the classics freely, which would be impossible if they had been destroyed; no text before the *Shiji* mentions the executions, no victim is named, and the phrase "burning the books and burying the Confucians" is first attested in the early 4th c. CE. Ulrich Neininger: *keng* in Sima Qian means "to kill", not "to bury alive". Li Kaiyuan (2010): the burning is real, the "burying of Confucians" a "half-faked history". [source: WP-Burning_of_books]
  - Consensus per WP: modern scholars agree the First Emperor destroyed many works and had scholars killed, but probably not buried alive, and the victims were not "Confucians" as a school. [source: WP-Burning_of_books, citing Goldin 2005, Nylan 2001, Kern 2010]
- **The Huang–Lao synthesis:** "Huang–Lao" = Huangdi (the Yellow Emperor) + Laozi; the term first appears in the *Shiji*; no pre-Qin text uses it. It was influential at the courts of Emperor Wen (r. 180–157 BCE) and Emperor Jing (r. 157–141 BCE) and with Empress Dowager Dou, before Emperor Wu (r. 141–87 BCE) made Confucianism the state philosophy. Early Han chancellors Cao Shen and Chen Ping reportedly applied its *wuwei* policy. The 1973 Mawangdui finds included four silk manuscripts (*Huang-Lao boshu*), "controversially identified" as the lost *Huangdi sijing* ("Four Classics of the Yellow Emperor"); the *Boshu* grounds *fa* and *xingming* in the Dao. Peerenboom warns against making "Huang-Lao" "a dustbin". [source: WP-Huang–Lao] Sima Tan's "Daojia" probably had Huang–Lao content in mind. [source: WP-Huang–Lao; WP-Sima_Tan]
- **Afterlife of the *fa* tradition:** the Salt and Iron Debates of 81 BCE (Sang Hongyang defended Shang Yang, Shen Buhai and Han Fei) were the last time a leading statesman openly identified with them. Su Shi (1036–1101): "from the Han onward, scholars have been ashamed to speak about Shang Yang and Sang Hongyang". The Maoist campaign "Reappraise the *fa* thinkers, criticize Confucians" ran 1973–1975. A Chinese scholarly society for *fajia* studies formed in 2015. [source: SEP-Leg §7] The Han phrase "Confucian on the outside, Legalist on the inside" is in the published Han China chapter; not separately verified here.
- **Is "Legalism" a coherent school? (debate)** Against: Goldin (2011, "Persistent Misconceptions about Chinese 'Legalism'"): *fa* ≠ law and *jia* ≠ school; Pines: a bibliographic category, "heuristically useful" but misleading as an analytical tool; debates about who "belongs" (1970s China; Creel 1974) "of little scholarly value". For a qualified unity: Pines nonetheless thinks the four core texts "present a sufficiently coherent outlook to merit their grouping together as belonging to a single intellectual tradition". Alternatives: "Realists" (Waley 1939), "political realism" (Vogelsang 2016), "the *fa* tradition" (Pines). [source: SEP-Leg §1, §1.1] (Note: SEP-Leg's bibliography dates Goldin's article "2005" but its text and the journal volume, *JCP* 38, point to 2011; cite as 2011.)

## Debates
- Whether "schools" existed at all: Sima Tan's six *jia* are a Han classification (Hansen: "fabricated"; Fraser: School of Names a "taxonomical fiction"; Pines: *fajia* a bibliographic category). [SEP-Zh §3; SEP-SN; SEP-Leg §1]
- Mohist ethics: consequentialist/utilitarian (Fung, Graham, Schwartz, Fraser) vs. divine command (Ahern, Soles, Tseu; criticised by Duda). [IEP-Mozi §6]
- How demanding inclusive care is (equal concern vs. commonsense decency; IEP's three interpretations). [SEP-Mo §7; IEP-Mozi §7]
- The triads: three factions (Yu Yue; Graham) vs. successive revision (Brooks; Fraser). [SEP-MoTriads]
- Did the Mohists have a concept of truth? Hansen vs. Graham, Harbsmeier, Fraser. [SEP-Mo §4]
- Historical Laozi: Chan (senior contemporary of Confucius plausible) vs. the near-consensus that the biography is legendary (Littlejohn; WP); Graham's conflation thesis; Fung's confusion thesis. [SEP-Lao §1; IEP-CP]
- Date of the *Daodejing*: traditional early (Liu Xiaogan), 4th c. bulk (Baxter), late (Graham before Guodian); Guodian shows collections existed c. 300 BCE. [SEP-Lao §§2–3; WP-Tao_Te_Ching]
- Nature of the *Laozi*: mythological, mystical, philosophical, life-philosophy, political (six approaches). [SEP-Lao §5]
- Authorship of the Inner Chapters (Klein 2010 sceptical) and Zhuangzi's existence. [SEP-Zh §2; IEP-Zh]
- Zhuangzi: relativist (Guo Xiang, Graham, Hansen's indexical version) vs. sceptical (Kjellberg, Raphals) vs. therapeutic (Kjellberg & Ivanhoe 1996 volume) vs. ironic (Ziporyn; Moeller) vs. mystical/cosmological (Coutinho). [IEP-Zh; SEP-Zh]
- The White Horse: universals, part-whole, kinds/identity, scope, use/mention, or playful sophistry. [SEP-SN §6.1]
- Legalism as a coherent school; "Legalism" vs. "*fa* tradition" vs. "Realists". [SEP-Leg §1]
- Han Fei: theorist of despotism vs. Pines's "entrapped sovereign"; single author vs. multiple. [SEP-Leg §§1.1, 5]
- Historicity of the 213/212 BCE burning and burying (Nylan, Kern, Neininger, Li Kaiyuan). [WP-Burning_of_books]
- Huang–Lao: a real movement or a "dustbin" category (Peerenboom); identity of the Mawangdui silk texts. [WP-Huang–Lao]
