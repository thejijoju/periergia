## 1. Mill's harm principle (On Liberty, 1859, ch. 1)
- *On Liberty* published 1859; the "one very simple principle" is at OL I 9 (CW XVIII: 223). Full wording in Quotations. [source: SEP-MMP §3.1]
- Mill never calls it "the harm principle"; the label is later. [source: SEP-MMP; nineteenth--utilitarianism.md NOT VERIFIED list]
- Brink's three categories (SEP-MMP): A's restriction of B's liberty is *paternalistic* if done for B's own benefit; *moralistic* if done to ensure B acts morally; an application of the *harm principle* if done to prevent harm to someone other than B. Mill claims harm prevention is the sole legitimate basis. [source: SEP-MMP §3.1]
- "Categorical approach": decide by category (offence, moralism, paternalism, harm prevention); Mill does not object to moralistic or paternalistic laws that can also be justified by harm prevention (e.g. laws against murder and assault). [source: SEP-MMP §3.1]
- Harm must "violate or risk violation of those important interests of others in which they have a right" (Brink's paraphrase, citing OL I 12; III 1; IV 3, 10, 12; V 5). [source: SEP-MMP §3.1]
- Harm prevention is necessary but not sufficient: it gives a pro tanto case; whether to regulate depends on a further cost–benefit (utility) judgement: "As soon as any part of a person's conduct affects prejudicially the interests of others, society has jurisdiction over it, and the question whether the general welfare will or will not be promoted by interfering with it, becomes open to discussion" (OL IV 3). [source: SEP-MMP §3.1]
- Self-regarding vs other-regarding: Mill sometimes equates the harm principle with permitting regulation of other-regarding conduct (I 11; IV 2), but concedes very little conduct is purely self-regarding (IV 8), and some other-regarding conduct causes mere offence (IV 3; IV 12). [source: SEP-MMP §3.1]
- Brink's verdict: harm prevention is neither necessary (Mill allows the slavery-contract exception) nor sufficient, so the "one very simple principle" is "over-simple". [source: SEP-MMP §3.6]
- "Setback to interests" reading of harm: John Rees (1960) seminal essay; Feinberg defines harm as "those states of set-back interest that are the consequence of wrongful acts or omissions by others" (Feinberg 1984: 215). Objection: defines one unclear concept by another (Miller 2010). [source: SEP-LL §5.2]
- Mill's line "the only freedom which deserves the name, is that of pursuing our own good in our own way, so long as we do not attempt to deprive others of theirs" (1859 [1991, 17]). [source: SEP-REP §1.1]

## 2. Harm vs offence; Feinberg's offence principle
- Mill says merely offensive conduct may not be regulated (I 12; III 1; IV 3, 10, 12; V 5) yet, discussing drunkenness, allows prohibiting public "offenses against decency" (V 7). Brink: "Mill may not have a consistent view about offense"; the exception "threatens to swallow the rule". [source: SEP-MMP §3.8]
- Joel Feinberg (19 Oct 1926 – 29 Mar 2004), *The Moral Limits of the Criminal Law*, 4 vols, Oxford UP: vol. 1 *Harm to Others* (1984), vol. 2 *Offense to Others* (1985), vol. 3 *Harm to Self* (1986), vol. 4 *Harmless Wrongdoing* (1988). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joel_Feinberg; SEP-MMP §3.8]
- Feinberg sees himself as Millian but permits some offence regulation, modelled on nuisance law, via a balancing test: weigh the seriousness of the offence (magnitude, avoidability) against the importance of the offender's interests (and availability of alternative avenues of expression). [source: SEP-MMP §3.8]
- "A ride on the bus" (in *Offense to Others*): imagined harmless but deeply offensive acts on a bus you cannot easily leave (affronts to senses, disgust, shocks to religious/patriotic sensibilities, public sex); question: "whether there are any human experiences that are harmless in themselves yet so unpleasant that we can rightly demand legal protection from them even at the cost of other persons' liberties." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joel_Feinberg]
- Feinberg nonetheless makes a sweeping exception for "[e]xpressions of opinion, especially about matters of public policy, but also about matters of empirical fact, and about historical, scientific, theological, philosophical, political, and moral questions" (1985: 44). [source: SEP-FS §3.2]
- Feinberg concedes at the end of vol. 4 that liberals may have to allow rare cases where harmless immoralities are outlawed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joel_Feinberg]
- Feinberg's definition of legal moralism: "it can be morally legitimate to prohibit conduct on the ground that it is inherently immoral, even though it causes neither harm nor offence to the actor or to others" (Feinberg 1984: 27). [source: SEP-LL §1]

## 3. Legal moralism: Stephen, the Wolfenden Report, Devlin vs Hart (1957–65)
- James Fitzjames Stephen, *Liberty, Equality, Fraternity* (1873) attacked *On Liberty* (see utilitarianism notes; cross-link). [source: nineteenth--utilitarianism.md]
- Wolfenden Report (Report of the Departmental Committee on Homosexual Offences and Prostitution) published 4 September 1957; recommended "homosexual behaviour between consenting adults in private should no longer be a criminal offence" (all but James Adair in favour); "It is not, in our view, the function of the law to intervene in the private life of citizens, or to seek to enforce any particular pattern of behaviour." Led eventually to the Sexual Offences Act 1967 (England and Wales only). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfenden_report]
- Patrick Devlin argued initially in his 1959 Maccabaean Lecture in Jurisprudence at the British Academy (WP spells "Maccabean"); expanded in *The Enforcement of Morals* (Oxford UP, 1965). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Devlin,_Baron_Devlin; SEP-LL bibliography]
- H. L. A. Hart replied in *Law, Liberty and Morality* (1963), originally three lectures at Stanford University in 1962; Hart applied the label "legal moralist" to Devlin and "the label stuck". [source: SEP-LL §1; WS-14 (Cane 2006)]
- Devlin: "society means a community of ideas; without shared ideas on politics, morals and ethics no society can exist" (1965: 10); "the suppression of vice is as much the law's business as the suppression of subversive activities"; treason analogy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Devlin,_Baron_Devlin]
- Devlin: "It is not possible to set theoretical limits to the power of the State to legislate against immorality…" (1965: 12–13). [source: SEP-LL §2.1; WP Devlin]
- Devlin's limiting principle: "toleration of the maximum individual freedom that is consistent with the integrity of society" (1965: 16); the limits of tolerance are reached when ordinary feeling reaches "intolerance, indignation and disgust". [source: SEP-LL §2.2; WP Devlin]
- Devlin treats immorality as a *prima facie* ("pro tanto") ground for legislation, defeasible by factors such as privacy and liberty: society has a "prima facie right to legislate" (1965: 11). [source: SEP-LL §1]
- Devlin's morality is relativist: what counts is the society's "deeply felt and not manufactured" morality (1965: 17); monogamy "is built into the house in which we live and could not be removed without bringing it down" (1965: 10). [source: SEP-LL §3]
- SURPRISE: Devlin's own answer was that private consensual homosexual sex was *within* the bounds of toleration and should NOT be criminalised (he signed a letter to *The Times*, 11 May 1965, "Law on Homosexuals", supporting the Wolfenden reforms); SEP-LL calls the contrary view "a widespread erroneous view … in the literature". Animal cruelty was his example of conduct beyond toleration. [source: SEP-LL §2.2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Devlin,_Baron_Devlin]
- Hart's main reply: Devlin moves "from the acceptable proposition that some shared morality is essential to the existence of any society to the unacceptable proposition that a society is identical with its morality as that is at any given moment of its history, so that a change in its morality is tantamount to a destruction of society" (Hart 1963 [1982: 51]); taken strictly this "would prevent us saying that the morality of a given society has changed" (51–52). Devlin gives no empirical evidence of societies disintegrating through non-enforcement. [source: SEP-LL §3]
- Hart's distinction: "positive morality" (the morality actually accepted by a group) vs "critical morality" (general principles used to criticise actual institutions, including positive morality); whether to enforce morality is itself a question of critical morality. [source: SEP-LL §3 (cites Hart 1963 [1982: 17–24]); WS-14]
- Feinberg (1987, "Some Unswept Debris from the Hart–Devlin Debate", *Synthese* 72): Devlin's replies "seem feeble and perfunctory" to a modern reader. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Devlin,_Baron_Devlin; WP Hart–Devlin_debate bibliography]
- Living legal moralists: Michael Moore and Antony Duff (self-declared); responses to Devlin include Ronald Dworkin (1965), Feinberg (1987), Nussbaum (2004, 2010). [source: SEP-LL §1]
- Brink's argument on moralism (SEP-MMP §3.9): (1) society has reason to prohibit immoral behaviour, whether or not harmful; (2) Junior's taking his parents' car without permission (nothing untoward happens) is harmless wrongdoing; (3) so society may prohibit it. Liberals reject (3) — but Brink argues one can reject *strong* moralism while keeping *weak* moralism and still reach most liberal conclusions. Both sides in the 1960s "for the most part" conceded the sexual conduct at issue was immoral but harmless. [source: SEP-MMP §3.9]

## 4. Paternalism
- Gerald Dworkin (born 1937; UC Davis emeritus — NOT Ronald Dworkin), "Paternalism", *The Monist* 56 (1972): 64–84. [source: SEP-PAT bibliography; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_Dworkin]
- Dworkin's SEP definition: X acts paternalistically towards Y by doing (omitting) Z iff (1) Z interferes with Y's liberty or autonomy; (2) X does so without Y's consent; (3) X does so only because X believes Z will improve Y's welfare or promote Y's interests, values or good. [source: SEP-PAT §2]
- Everyday examples (SEP-PAT §1): compulsory pension contributions, motorcycle helmets, no swimming without lifeguards, bans on ineffective drugs, consent not a defence to some assaults, seatbelts. [source: SEP-PAT §1]
- Soft vs hard paternalism, with Mill's damaged-bridge example: soft paternalism may only check voluntariness/knowledge (stop him to find out if he knows; if he knows and wants to proceed, he must be allowed); hard paternalism may prevent even a knowing, voluntary choice. Feinberg's term "soft paternalism". Also weak (interfere with means) vs strong (interfere with mistaken ends); broad vs narrow. [source: SEP-PAT §§2.1–2.3, 3]
- Mill's two explicit reasons against paternalism: state power is abused (V 20–3); the individual is the more reliable judge of his own good (IV 4, 12). Brink: these do not rule out *successful* paternalism; Mill's perfectionism (exercising one's capacities is part of one's good) gives a stronger reason. [source: SEP-MMP §3.7]
- Mill's exception: no one may sell himself into slavery: "by selling himself for a slave, he abdicates his liberty; he foregoes any future use of it beyond that single act. He, therefore, defeats in his own case, the very purpose which is the justification of allowing him to dispose of himself" (V 11). [source: SEP-MMP §3.7]
- Contractualist justification: we would all agree to some interventions (short-term anti-suicide interventions; seat-belts, knowing our tendency to discount future benefits). [source: SEP-PAT §3]
- Dworkin 1972 frames justified paternalism as a "social insurance policy" that fully rational persons would accept; distinguishes "pure" from "impure" paternalism (pure: the class whose freedom is restricted is the class meant to benefit). [source: WS-6 — snippet-level only; "future-oriented consent" wording NOT confirmed]
- Libertarian paternalism: Cass Sunstein & Richard Thaler, "Libertarian Paternalism Is Not an Oxymoron", *University of Chicago Law Review* 70(4) (2003): 1159–1202; Thaler & Sunstein, *Nudge: Improving Decisions about Health, Wealth, and Happiness* (Yale UP, 8 April 2008); revised "Final Edition" 2021. Thaler is a Nobel laureate in economics. [source: SEP-PAT §4 and bibliography; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nudge_(book)]
- Definition of a nudge: "any aspect of the choice architecture that alters people's behavior in a predictable way without forbidding any options or significantly changing their economic incentive" (2008: 6). "Libertarian" because no option is removed or made costlier; "paternalistic" because it aims at the chooser's good as judged by the chooser. [source: SEP-PAT §4]
- Examples: cafeteria (healthy food at eye level), opt-out pension enrolment, "Save More Tomorrow", smaller plates, painted traffic lanes. [source: SEP-PAT §4]
- Objections: nudges can be non-transparent; they harness non-rational tendencies (framing: "90% survive" vs "10% die"); some are manipulative. Sunstein proposes a transparency condition. [source: SEP-PAT §§4.2–4.5]
- Terminology caution: WP's *Nudge* article says libertarian paternalism is "also called soft paternalism"; this is NOT Feinberg's sense of "soft paternalism" (voluntariness-checking). Writer should keep the two apart. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nudge_(book); SEP-PAT]

## 5. Free speech: Mill's chapter 2, Holmes, Scanlon
- ARGUMENT (Mill, OL ch. II, "Of the Liberty of Thought and Discussion"), per SEP-MMP §3.3 and SEP-FS §2.1 (fuller version in utilitarianism notes):
  1. A silenced opinion may be true; to suppress it assumes infallibility. "We can never be sure that the opinion we are endeavouring to stifle is a false opinion; and if we were sure, stifling it would be an evil still." [source: WS-7 (econlib/standardebooks text of ch. II)]
  2. Even a false opinion may contain part of the truth; truth on complex matters emerges from collision of partial views.
  3. Even a wholly false opinion, if challenged, keeps the true one from becoming "a dead dogma, not a living truth".
  4. Unchallenged, a doctrine loses its meaning and its effect on character.
  Conclusion: suppression of opinion is epistemically harmful whatever the opinion's truth-value, so opinion should not be suppressed. [source: SEP-MMP §3.3; SEP-FS §2.1]
- OBJECTION (SEP-MMP): the truth-tracking argument condemns only incompetent censorship; it has nothing against a perfectly reliable censor, and seems to permit censoring well-established falsehoods. Mill does not need the claim that the censor assumes infallibility (OL II 3): a censor can admit fallibility yet act on the best evidence. REPLY: proper recognition of fallibility should keep discussion open; and Mill's deliberative/perfectionist rationale — free discussion is needed for people to exercise their rational capacities and hold beliefs as their own — is violated even by successful censorship. [source: SEP-MMP §3.3]
- Mill's focus on audiences: "It is not on the impassioned partisan, it is on the calmer and more disinterested bystander, that this collision of opinions works its salutary effect" (1859: ch. 2, p. 94). [source: SEP-FS §2.1]
- SEP-FS: chapter II is a partial *qualification* of the harm principle for speech, not a mere application of it; much harmful speech must still be allowed; only imminently dangerous speech (no time for discussion) may be restricted — corn-dealer example (ch. 3, p. 100). [source: SEP-FS §2.1]
- "Marketplace of ideas": few now hold the strong teleological thesis that open debate inevitably produces truth; it is doubtful Mill held it (Gordon 1997); his point was that open discussion is the best mechanism available relative to an arbiter who suppresses what he thinks false. [source: SEP-FS §2.1]
- Holmes's *Abrams v. United States*, 250 U.S. 616, decided 10 November 1919 (argued 21–22 Oct): Court upheld (7–2) convictions under the Sedition Act of 1918 of anarchists who distributed leaflets against US intervention in Russia; Holmes dissented, joined by Brandeis. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrams_v._United_States]
- Holmes did not use the phrase "marketplace of ideas"; his dissent speaks of "free trade in ideas" and "the competition of the market" (quotation below). Holmes had written for a unanimous Court in *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47 (1919), upholding Espionage Act convictions. [source: WP Abrams_v._United_States; WP Marketplace_of_ideas; WP Schenck_v._United_States]
- Earlier roots of the idea: Milton's *Areopagitica* (1644) and Mill (1859). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketplace_of_ideas]
- *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444 (1969): mere advocacy of violence is protected; only incitement to "imminent lawless action" may be punished; the Court endorses counter-speech as the remedy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandenburg_v._Ohio; SEP-FS §3.5]
- T. M. Scanlon (born 28 June 1940; Harvard), "A Theory of Freedom of Expression", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 1(2) (Winter 1972): 204–226. Listener-autonomy theory: the state must act toward citizens as autonomous and so entitled to make up their own minds; free speech functions as a side-constraint forbidding laws that restrict speech on certain grounds (in Hohfeld's terms, an immunity rather than a claim-right; Scanlon 2013: 402). Scanlon later complicated the theory (1978). [source: SEP-FS §§1, 2.1, 3.3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._M._Scanlon]
- Scanlon on balancing: "The only balancing is balancing of interests. Rights are not balanced, but are defined, or redefined, in the light of the balance of interests and of empirical facts about how these interests can best be protected" (2008: 78). [source: SEP-FS §3.2]
- Other free-speech justifications named in SEP-FS: speaker autonomy (Baker 1989), democracy (Meiklejohn 1948), Shiffrin's thinker-based theory; "minimalists" who protect speech mainly against government malice/incompetence (Schauer 1982). Content neutrality vs viewpoint neutrality (G. Stone) are central to US doctrine, less so elsewhere. [source: SEP-FS §§1–2]

## 6. Hate speech: Waldron and his critics
- Jeremy Waldron (born 13 Oct 1953, Invercargill, New Zealand; NYU; formerly Chichele Professor at Oxford), *The Harm in Hate Speech* (Harvard UP, 2012), from his 2009 Oliver Wendell Holmes Lectures. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Waldron; SEP-FS bibliography]
- Waldron's view: hate speech is best understood as group defamation (cf. *Beauharnais v. Illinois*, 1952) that undermines its targets' standing as respected equals — their "basic social standing, the basis of [their] recognition as social equals and as bearers of human rights and constitutional entitlements" (2014 pb: 59); public hate speech is "an environmental threat to social peace, a sort of slow-acting poison, accumulating here and there, word by word" (2014: 4); it attacks the public *assurance* of dignity. [source: SEP-FS §3.2; SEP-HS §§2, 3]
- Germany's penal code §130 (Volksverhetzung) as an example of a dignity-based law: prohibits "attacks on human dignity by insulting, maliciously maligning, or defaming part of the population" (as quoted by Waldron 2014: 8). [source: SEP-HS §2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hate_speech]
- Bhikhu Parekh (2012) rejects the "imminent danger" limit as short-sighted ("Imminent danger occurs against, and is imminent because of, the prevailing social climate", 2012: 45–46) and answers six standard objections to bans. [source: SEP-HS §5]
- Critics' objections (SEP-HS §5.2): chilling effect/slippery slope; too much state power over content; paternalism; bans drive extremists underground and feed a sense of victimisation (C. Edwin Baker 2012: 77); resources better spent on material conditions of racism (Baker). [source: SEP-HS §5.2]
- Ronald Dworkin's "legitimacy" argument (Foreword to Hare & Weinstein eds, *Extreme Speech and Democracy*, OUP 2009): banning hate speech "upstream" spoils the democratic legitimacy of "downstream" anti-discrimination laws, since those who oppose them were denied a voice; Waldron replies in his book; Dworkin published a "Reply to Jeremy Waldron" (Cambridge volume). James Weinstein calls it "the most powerful argument against hate speech" regulation. [source: WS-9 — weaker; check page refs before quoting]
- Brian Leiter's review (NDPR) objects that the line between dignitary harm and offence is neither stable nor clear, and asks why assurance of dignity, rather than e.g. healthcare, deserves this protection. [source: WS-8 — weaker]
- Nadine Strossen, *Hate: Why We Should Resist It With Free Speech, Not Censorship* (OUP, 2018) — counter-speech position. Eric Heinze, *Hate Speech and Democratic Citizenship* (OUP, 2016). [source: SEP-FS bibliography]
- Internal-limits arguments for restriction: hate speech can silence targets (Pallikkathayil 2020; Langton; Maitra & McGowan); Brison 1998 on autonomy. Critical race theory: Matsuda, Lawrence, Delgado & Crenshaw, *Words that Wound* (1993) on "assaultive speech". [source: SEP-FS §3.3; SEP-HS §3]
- Goebbels (as quoted in Fox & Nolte 1995: 1): "This will always remain one of the best jokes of democracy, that it gave its deadly enemies the means by which it was destroyed." (Attribution "is said to have remarked" — secondhand.) [source: SEP-FS §3.3]

## 7. Free expression internationally (dated)
- England: Bill of Rights 1689 established freedom of speech *in Parliament*. Sweden's Freedom of the Press Act 1766 (Anders Chydenius) among the first press-freedom laws. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_speech]
- France, Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, 26 August 1789, Art. XI: "The free communication of thoughts and of opinions is one of the most precious rights of man: any citizen thus may speak, write, print freely, but shall be responsible for such abuses of this freedom as shall be defined by law." (WP translation.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Man_and_of_the_Citizen]
- USA: First Amendment adopted 1791. [source: WP Freedom_of_speech]
- UDHR (10 Dec 1948) Art. 19: "Everyone has the right to freedom of opinion and expression; this right includes freedom to hold opinions without interference and to seek, receive and impart information and ideas through any media and regardless of frontiers." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_speech]
- European Convention on Human Rights signed Rome 4 November 1950, in force 3 September 1953; Art. 10 protects expression; *Handyside v United Kingdom* (1976): Art. 10 covers ideas that "offend, shock or disturb" (yet the obscenity conviction was upheld). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Convention_on_Human_Rights; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_10_of_the_European_Convention_on_Human_Rights]
- ICCPR adopted by UN GA 16 December 1966, in force 23 March 1976 (175 parties per WP infobox). Art. 19 protects expression but it "carries with it special duties and responsibilities"; Art. 20 requires prohibition of "propaganda for war" and "advocacy of national, racial or religious hatred that constitutes incitement to discrimination, hostility or violence". The US ratified in 1992 with reservations including that no article restricts free speech (Art. 20 seen as possibly unconstitutional). [source: SEP-FS §3.2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Covenant_on_Civil_and_Political_Rights]
- Regional: American Convention Art. 13; African Charter Art. 9; ECHR Art. 10. [source: WP Freedom_of_speech; SEP-HR §3.1]
- Most liberal democracies restrict hate speech (Canada, Germany, France, India, etc.); the US is the outlier. [source: SEP-FS §3.2; WP Hate_speech]
- Cairo Declaration Art. 22(a): "Everyone shall have the right to express his opinion freely in such manner as would not be contrary to the principles of the Shariah." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo_Declaration_on_Human_Rights_in_Islam]

## 8. Berlin, "Two Concepts of Liberty" (1958)
- Delivered as Berlin's inaugural lecture as Chichele Professor of Social and Political Theory, Oxford, 31 October 1958 (elected 1957); published as a 57-page pamphlet by the Clarendon Press (1958); reprinted in *Four Essays on Liberty* (1969, pp. 118–72) and *Liberty* (2002, ed. Henry Hardy, pp. 166–217). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Concepts_of_Liberty; SEP-BER §1; SEP-LPN bibliography]
- Berlin: born Riga 1909, emigrated to Britain 1921, first Jew elected to a Prize Fellowship at All Souls (1932), knighted 1957, founding President of Wolfson College, died 1997. [source: SEP-BER §1]
- Two questions (1969: 121–22): negative liberty answers "What is the area within which the subject—a person or group of persons—is or should be left to do or be what he is able to do or be, without interference by other persons?"; positive liberty answers "What, or who, is the source of control or interference that can determine someone to do, or be, this rather than that?" [source: SEP-LPN §1; WP Two_Concepts_of_Liberty]
- Negative liberty in Berlin's words: "I am normally said to be free to the degree to which no man or body of men interferes with my activity. Political liberty in this sense is simply the area within which a man can act unobstructed by others…" (1969: 122); inability (cannot jump ten feet; blindness) is not coercion; "Coercion implies the deliberate interference of other human beings within the area in which I could otherwise act." [source: SEP-LIB (block quotation from Berlin 1969); WS-1]
- Positive liberty: "The 'positive' sense of the word 'liberty' derives from the wish on the part of the individual to be his own master." [source: WS-2 only — weaker; wording consistent across two snippets]
- Berlin gave positive liberty two definitions: "freedom to" (ability to pursue willed goals) and autonomy/self-rule; "These are not the same." Treatment "less than fully even-handed". Berlin said there were more than two hundred senses of liberty, echoing Lord Acton's "two hundred definitions" (Acton 1895: 32). [source: SEP-BER §5.1]
- Berlin associated negative liberty with Hobbes, Bentham, Mill (also Constant, Locke, Tocqueville); positive with Rousseau, Kant's German successors (Fichte), Hegel; Carter lists classical-liberal negative theorists as Constant, Humboldt, Spencer, Mill and positive as Rousseau, Hegel, Marx, T. H. Green. [source: SEP-REP §1.1; SEP-LPN §1; SEP-BER §5.1]
- The "paradox"/slippery slope of positive liberty (Berlin as reconstructed by Carter): divided self → "higher", rational, "true" self vs lower empirical self → some are more rational and know others' true interests → coercing them "liberates" them → the self identified with a collectivity ("a tribe, a race, a church, a state, the great society of the living and the dead and the yet unborn") → "Once I take this view, I am in a position to ignore the actual wishes of men or societies, to bully, oppress, torture in the name, and on behalf, of their 'real' selves…" (1969: 132–33). [source: SEP-LPN §2]
- "It is one thing to say that I may be coerced for my own good which I am too blind to see… [But] it is another to say that if it is my good, then I am not being coerced, for I have willed it…" (1969: 134). [source: SEP-REP §1.1]
- "Retreat into the inner citadel": the ascetic/Stoic/Buddhist strategy of freedom by extinguishing desires; Berlin's wounded-leg image: "there are two methods of freeing myself from pain. One is to heal the wound. But if the cure is too difficult or uncertain, there is another method. I can get rid of the wound by cutting off my leg" (1969: 135–36). Contented-slave problem: if freedom = no obstacle to *desires*, one could become free by desiring less. [source: SEP-LPN §2]
- Berlin called the perversion a "strange … reversal" or "monstrous impersonation" (1958 [L 198, 180]); championed as "truer and more humane" negative liberty and an empirical self. [source: SEP-BER §5.1]
- Interpretive point: Berlin was NOT simply an enemy of positive liberty: both were for him genuine values; his targets were monism and metaphysical/collective conceptions of the self; his main argument is "individualism, empiricism and pluralism against collectivism, holism, metaphysical rationalism … and monism". Later regretted not saying more about the evils negative liberty had justified (laissez-faire exploitation). [source: SEP-BER §5.1]
- Pluralism–liberty link: value conflicts require choices; there may be no single right choice; so a main rationale for coercing choice fails. Crowder once argued liberalism follows from pluralism alone, later (2019, 2025) only under modern conditions; Gray argued pluralism may undermine liberalism. [source: SEP-BER §5.2]
- "Freedom for the pike is death for the minnows": Berlin puts it in quotation marks ("'Freedom for the pike is death for the minnows'; the liberty of some must depend on the restraint of others. Freedom for an Oxford don, others have been known to add, is a very different thing from freedom for an Egyptian peasant."). The line was already in R. H. Tawney, *Equality* (4th ed. 1952, p. 180). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Concepts_of_Liberty; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_liberty]
- Distinction "goes back at least to Kant". [source: SEP-LPN intro]
- Hobbes's negative definition: "a free man is he that in those things which by his strength and wit he is able to do is not hindered to do what he hath the will to do" (*Leviathan* II.21). The "pure negative" conception (Steiner 1994; Carter & Kramer) derives from Hobbes, *Leviathan* chs 14 and 21. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_liberty; SEP-LPN §6]
- Critics: Charles Taylor (1979), "What's Wrong with Negative Liberty": opportunity concept vs exercise concept of freedom; G. A. Cohen: lack of money is a lack of negative freedom, because money tracks whether others (backed by the state) will stop you (e.g. taking a jacket without paying); Berlin had treated poverty like natural inability. Christman's procedural (content-neutral) positive liberty: what matters is how desires were formed, not their content. [source: SEP-LPN §§3, 8; WP Two_Concepts_of_Liberty]
- Locke as a "false positive" for the dichotomy: liberty is "to be free from restraint and violence from others", yet liberty is not licence, and "that ill deserves the name of confinement which hedges us in only from bogs and precipices" (*Second Treatise* §§6, 57). [source: SEP-LPN §8]

## 9. MacCallum's triadic analysis (1967)
- Gerald C. MacCallum Jr., "Negative and Positive Freedom", *Philosophical Review* 76 (1967): 312–34. [source: SEP-LPN bibliography]
- Freedom is always a triadic relation: an agent (x) is free from certain preventing conditions (y) to do or become something (z). Every freedom claim has both a "from" and a "to", so "freedom from" vs "freedom to" is a false dichotomy; disputes are about the *extension* of the three variables (what counts as the agent, as a constraint, as a purpose). There is one concept and many conceptions (concept/conception distinction from Rawls 1971, p. 5). [source: SEP-LPN §5]
- The triadic definition was first put forward by Felix Oppenheim (1950s–60s) for a negative concept; MacCallum generalised it and left each variable open — a meta-theoretical position. [source: SEP-LPN §5]
- Objection: MacCallum's formula captures only opportunity concepts, not Taylor's exercise concept. [source: SEP-LPN §8]

## 10. Republican freedom as non-domination
- Constant, "The Liberty of the Ancients Compared with that of the Moderns", speech at the Athénée Royal (Royal Athenaeum), Paris, 1819. Ancient liberty: "active and constant participation in the collective power", compatible (except at Athens) with "the complete subjection of the individual to the authority of the whole"; modern liberty: "The aim of the moderns is the enjoyment of security in private pleasures; and they call liberty the guarantees accorded by institutions to these pleasures." Revolutionary error: applying ancient liberty to a modern state; examples Terpander (punished by the Spartan ephors for adding a string to his lyre) and ostracism; Constant concludes the two must be combined, political liberty being the guarantee of individual liberty. Berlin cited it as an inspiration. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Liberty_of_Ancients_Compared_with_that_of_Moderns (translation not named); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Constant]
- Two senses of "republicanism": a historical tradition (Machiavelli; Milton, Harrington, Sidney; Montesquieu, Blackstone; commonwealthmen; American founders) drawing on Cicero and Roman historians — "neo-roman"; and the contemporary programme (Skinner's history; Pettit's theory) whose central value is freedom as non-domination. [source: SEP-REP intro]
- Civic-humanist reading (Arendt 1958; Pocock 1975; Rahe 1992: participation as intrinsically valuable, Aristotelian) vs instrumental/"neo-Roman" reading (Skinner 1984; Pettit 1989/1997; roots in Roman law's free/slave distinction). [source: SEP-REP §3]
- Philip Pettit (born 1945, Irish; Princeton and ANU), *Republicanism: A Theory of Freedom and Government* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997); his ideas informed José Luis Rodríguez Zapatero's Spanish government (Pettit & Martí, 2010). [source: SEP-REP bibliography; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Pettit]
- Quentin Skinner (born 26 Nov 1940, Oldham; Regius Professor of History, Cambridge, 1996), *Liberty before Liberalism* (Cambridge UP, 1998), based on his inaugural lecture as Regius Professor, November 1997; chapters "The neo-Roman theory of free states", "Free states and individual liberty", "Freedom and the historian". Developed fully in *Liberty as Independence* (2025). [source: SEP-REP bibliography; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quentin_Skinner; WS-12]
- Pettit's definition: a person enjoys freedom to the extent that no other has "the capacity to interfere in their affairs on an arbitrary basis" (1999: 165). [source: SEP-REP §1.2]
- The kindly-master case (ARGUMENT; see §13). Douglass: "it was slavery—not its mere incidents—that I hated" (1855: 161). Priestley (1769) on the lightly governed American colonies: "by the same power, by which the people of England can compel them to pay one penny, they may compel them to pay the last penny they have." [source: SEP-REP §§1.2–1.3]
- Non-domination differs from non-interference both ways: one can be dominated without interference (kindly master) and interfered with without domination (interference constrained to track one's avowed interests, e.g. non-arbitrary law). It differs from positive liberty: it is not participation, and it builds in liberal-democratic institutions. [source: SEP-LPN §4]
- Pettit: non-domination is secured "from the moment that the institutions are in place" (1997: 107). Harrington: "empire of laws and not of men" (1656). Republicans want not a better master (the "mirror for princes" aim) but less of a master. [source: SEP-REP §§1.3, 2.1]
- Liberal tradition (Bentham, Paley, Constant) displaced liberty-as-independence with liberty-as-non-interference, on the republican historical story. [source: SEP-REP §4]
- Applications: employer/worker, husband/wife, able-bodied/disabled domination. [source: SEP-LPN §4]

## 11. Rights: Hohfeld; will vs interest
- Wesley Newcomb Hohfeld (9 Aug 1879 – 21 Oct 1918), American legal scholar (Stanford 1905–14, then Yale); articles in *Yale Law Journal* 1913 and 1917 ("Fundamental Legal Conceptions as Applied in Judicial Reasoning", YLJ 26(8): 710–770, 1917); posthumous book *Fundamental Legal Conceptions as Applied in Judicial Reasoning and Other Legal Essays* (1919). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wesley_Newcomb_Hohfeld; SEP-FS bibliography; SEP-R cites "Hohfeld 1919: 36"]
- Four incidents: privilege (liberty), claim, power, immunity. Privilege: A has a privilege to φ iff A has no duty not to φ (shell on the beach). Claim: A has a claim that B φ iff B has a duty to A to φ (wages). Power: ability to alter one's own or others' incidents (promising, waiving). Immunity: B lacks the power to alter A's incidents. Rights are "molecular" combinations. [source: SEP-R §2.1]
- Will theory: a right makes its holder "a small scale sovereign" (Hart 1982: 183) — control (a power) over another's duty. Will theorists: Kant, Savigny, Hart, Kelsen, Wellman, Steiner. Interest theory: rights further the holder's interests; Raz: "X has a right if X can have rights, and, other things being equal, an aspect of X's well-being (his interest) is a sufficient reason for holding some other person(s) to be under a duty" (1986: 166). Interest theorists: Bentham, Ihering, Austin, Lyons, MacCormick, Raz, Kramer. [source: SEP-R §2.2.2]
- Objections to will theory: no unwaivable rights (right not to be enslaved, MacCormick 1977: 197); infants, animals, comatose adults could have no rights (MacCormick 1982). Objections to interest theory: third-party beneficiaries have interests without rights (Lyons); office-holders (a judge) have rights not explained by their interests; Raz's journalist case (Kamm 2002). [source: SEP-R §2.2.2]
- History of "a right": objective *ius* (what is just; Ulpian — the *ius* of a parricide was to be sewn into a sack with snakes and thrown into the Tiber, Tierney 1997: 16) vs subjective "a right"; scholars push the subjective sense back from Locke, Hobbes, Grotius to Gerson (15th c.), Ockham (14th), perhaps Gratian (12th) (Tierney 1997; Brett 1997); some find it in Roman jurists or Aristotle. [source: SEP-R §3]

## 12. Natural rights and human rights
- Grotius (10 Apr 1583 – 28 Aug 1645), *De iure belli ac pacis* (Paris, 1625). Prolegomena §11: natural law "would have a degree of validity even if we should concede [etiamsi daremus] that which cannot be conceded without the utmost wickedness, that there is no God, or that the affairs of men are of no concern to him" (SEP translation; standard English translations are Barbeyrac/Tuck 1738/2005 and Kelsey 1925). A right (*ius*) is "a moral quality of a person, making it possible to have or to do something correctly" (DIB I.1.4): usually credited with shifting the paradigm to subjective rights (Haakonssen, Schneewind; contra Irwin). [source: SEP-GRO; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Grotius]
- Natural rights theory peaked with Grotius, Hobbes, Pufendorf and especially Locke ("life, liberty, and estate", *Second Treatise* §87); echoed in the US Declaration of Independence (1776). [source: SEP-R §6.1] (Cross-link Locke chapter.)
- Bentham: "Natural rights is simple nonsense: natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense,—nonsense upon stilts" (*Anarchical Fallacies*); also the doctrine "is from beginning to end so much flat assertion: it lays down as a fundamental and inviolable principle whatever is in dispute" (Bentham 1796 [1987: 66]). Dating: SEP-R says written 1796, first published 1816 in French, in English in Bowring's *Works* vol. 2 (1843); the utilitarianism notes give "written 1791–95". Reprinted in Waldron (ed.), *'Nonsense upon Stilts': Bentham, Burke, and Marx on the Rights of Man* (Methuen, 1987). [source: SEP-R bibliography and §6.1; nineteenth--utilitarianism.md quotations]
- Marx, "Zur Judenfrage" ("On the Jewish Question"), written 1843, published February 1844 in the *Deutsch–Französische Jahrbücher* (one issue only), replying to Bruno Bauer; distinguishes political from human emancipation. Marx attacked the "rights of man" of the American and French declarations as rights of the egoistic member of civil society: the rights-bearing individual is an "isolated monad… withdrawn behind his private interests and whims and separated from the community" (Marx 1844 [1987: 146], in Waldron 1987a). Translations vary ("so-called" vs "supposed" rights of man). Whether the essay is antisemitic is debated (Avineri; McLellan's "extended pun"). [source: SEP-R §7; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Jewish_Question; WS-13]
- Rights critiques from African and East Asian traditions: Ihara (2004) on games/ritual; Confucianism-and-rights debate (Ihara, Rosemont: no room vs Joseph Chan 2011: rights as a "fallback apparatus"); Menkiti (1984: 171): "as far as Africans are concerned, the reality of the communal world takes precedence over the relative individual life histories" (building on Mbiti 1969). [source: SEP-R §7]
- UDHR: adopted by UN General Assembly Resolution 217 A (III), 10 December 1948, Palais de Chaillot, Paris: of 58 members, 48 in favour, none against, 8 abstentions (USSR, Byelorussian SSR, Ukrainian SSR, Czechoslovakia, Poland, Yugoslavia, Saudi Arabia, South Africa), Honduras and Yemen did not vote. Commission on Human Rights chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt approved the draft 18 June 1948 (12–0–4). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights]
- Abstentions: Saudi Arabia chiefly over Art. 18 (right "to change his religion or belief") and Art. 16 (equal marriage rights); South Africa to protect apartheid (its delegate Te Water had fought to remove "dignity"); Soviet bloc claimed it did not condemn fascism enough (Roosevelt thought Art. 13 on leaving one's country was the real reason). Pakistan voted for it and argued for religious freedom. [source: WP UDHR]
- Drafters: John Peters Humphrey (Canada, 1905–1995), Director of the UN Human Rights Division, prepared the first draft/"blueprint"; René Cassin (France) the second draft and final structure (the "portico" image); P. C. Chang (Zhang Pengchun, 1892–1957, Republic of China), Vice-Chairman; Charles Malik (1906–1987, Lebanon), Rapporteur. [source: WP UDHR; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Peters_Humphrey; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._C._Chang; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Malik]
- Chang: Columbia PhD under John Dewey; playwright and diplomat; urged removing references to religion and used Confucianism to settle stalemates; quoted Mencius; Roosevelt's memoir: Chang "suggested that the Secretariat might well spend a few months studying the fundamentals of Confucianism!" Members agreed the philosophical debate centred on Chang vs Malik. [source: WP UDHR; WP P._C._Chang]
- Malik: Greek Orthodox Lebanese philosopher, studied at Harvard under Whitehead and briefly under Heidegger at Freiburg (1932); expounded Aquinas/Thomism; proposed what became Art. 28 (a "social and international order" in which the rights can be realised); later President of the UN General Assembly (1958–59). [source: WP Charles_Malik; WP UDHR]
- Article 1's "reason and conscience": Chang is widely credited with pressing the Confucian *ren* ("two-man-mindedness"), rendered as "conscience"; sources differ on how much of his framing survived (Columbia project notes "conscience" also echoes the Preamble's "conscience of mankind"). [source: WS-10 — weaker; treat as an interpretation]
- Hansa Mehta (3 Jul 1897 – 4 Apr 1995), Indian delegate to the UN Human Rights Commission 1947–48, one of only two women delegates on the Commission alongside Roosevelt, secured the change in Art. 1 from "all men are born free and equal" to "all human beings are born free and equal"; Roosevelt had said "men" was "generally accepted to include all human beings"; Mehta later vice-chair of the Commission (1950). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hansa_Mehta]
- Other contributors: Hernán Santa Cruz (Chile) for socioeconomic rights; Bedia Afnan (Iraq) for gender-equality wording in ICCPR/ICESCR Art. 3; Shaista Suhrawardy Ikramullah (Pakistan). [source: WP UDHR]
- 1947 American Anthropological Association Executive Board warned of "a statement of rights conceived only in terms of the values prevalent in Western Europe and America"; "standards and values are relative to the culture from which they derive". [source: SEP-HR §5]
- Maritain (UNESCO inquiry 1946–47): "Yes… we agree about the rights but on condition no one asks us why" (Maritain 1949: 9). [source: SEP-HR §5]
- International Bill of Rights completed 1966, in force 1976. [source: WP UDHR]
- Asian values debate: in the 1990s Singapore's Lee Kuan Yew and others argued UN human rights were insensitive to "Asian values" (family/community over individualism, harmony over personal freedom, respect for leaders, hard work/thrift); they wanted to de-emphasise civil-political rights and stop Western criticism. Lee's Foreign Affairs interview with Fareed Zakaria, "Culture Is Destiny" (Mar/Apr 1994); Kim Dae-jung's reply "Is Culture Destiny? The Myth of Asia's Anti-Democratic Values", *Foreign Affairs* 73(6) (Nov/Dec 1994), citing Mencius and Buddhism. [source: SEP-HR §5; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_values; WS-11]
- Bangkok Declaration: Final Declaration of the Regional Meeting for Asia of the World Conference on Human Rights, adopted by ministers and representatives of Asian states meeting at Bangkok 29 March – 2 April 1993; reaffirms the UN Charter and UDHR but says human rights must be considered in the context of "national and regional particularities and various historical, cultural and religious backgrounds", stresses sovereignty, non-interference, and development. A parallel Bangkok NGO Declaration (over 100 Asian NGOs, from ~23 March) rejected cultural exemptions. [source: WS-3; WP UDHR §Bangkok_Declaration] (Note: WP's UDHR article describes it as adopted by foreign ministers of five ASEAN states — this conflicts with the official title and WS-3; prefer "Asian states at a UN regional meeting".)
- Vienna World Conference on Human Rights; Vienna Declaration adopted by consensus 25 June 1993; Art. 5: "All human rights are universal, indivisible and interdependent and interrelated… While the significance of national and regional particularities and various historical, cultural and religious backgrounds must be borne in mind, it is the duty of States, regardless of their political, economic and cultural systems, to promote and protect all human rights and fundamental freedoms." At Vienna, Singapore, Malaysia, China and Iran sought accommodations. [source: SEP-HR §5; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_Declaration_and_Programme_of_Action]
- Amartya Sen, *Human Rights and Asian Values* — Sixteenth Morgenthau Memorial Lecture on Ethics & Foreign Policy, Carnegie Council, New York, 1 May 1997 (pub. 1997); revised in *The New Republic*, July 1997. Argues: "Asia" is too diverse to have one set of values; Confucius allowed dissent against mistaken authority; Ashoka (3rd c. BCE) urged tolerance without Aristotle's restriction to free men; Akbar's court discussed religious difference while the Inquisition raged in Europe; Western tradition itself long endorsed slavery etc.; authoritarianism is not shown to aid development. Sections include "Order and Confucianism", "Freedom and Tolerance", "Akbar and the Moghuls". [source: SEP-HR bibliography; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_values; WS-4/WS-5 — paraphrase only, no verified quotations]
- African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights ("Banjul Charter"): drafting called for by the OAU in 1979; approved unanimously at the OAU's 18th Assembly, June 1981, Nairobi; in force 21 October 1986 (now African Human Rights Day); African Commission set up 2 November 1987 (now in Banjul); Court protocol 1998, in force 25 January 2004; 53 states parties (2019). Distinctive: "peoples'" rights (self-determination, development, a "generally satisfactory environment", Arts 19–24) and individual *duties* (Art. 29: to family, national community, solidarity, independence, to work and pay taxes, to preserve "positive African cultural values", to promote African unity). Critics note gaps (no explicit privacy right). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Charter_on_Human_and_Peoples'_Rights]
- Cairo Declaration on Human Rights in Islam (CDHRI): adopted by OIC foreign ministers in Cairo, 5 August 1990; 45 OIC signatories (2012); presented to the UN Commission on Human Rights 1992, where the International Commission of Jurists condemned it; Arts 24–25 make all rights subject to the Shari'a ("All the rights and freedoms stipulated in this Declaration are subject to the Islamic Sharia"); criticised over apostasy and freedom of expression/religion; widely seen as a response to the UDHR using universalist language; OIC resolved to support it 30 June 2000; revised version adopted 28 November 2020 (Niamey) that does not specifically invoke Shari'a. Its Art. 1 begins "All human beings form one family whose members are united by their subordination to Allah and descent from Adam" and forbids discrimination on many grounds. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo_Declaration_on_Human_Rights_in_Islam; WP UDHR]
- Evenhandedness: most Muslim-majority UN members in 1948 voted for the UDHR (Afghanistan, Egypt, Iraq, Iran, Syria, Turkey, Pakistan); Saudi Arabia was the only Muslim-majority abstainer. Iran's 1982 envoy called the UDHR "a secular understanding of the Judeo-Christian tradition". Earlier Universal Islamic Declaration of Human Rights (Islamic Council; WP gives Paris/London, 1981 not confirmed by WP text) — Ann Elizabeth Mayer finds the Arabic and English versions differ "very problematic[ally]". Muslim reformist scholarship also argues for human rights from within Islam: Abdullahi Ahmed An-Na'im (born 1946, Sudan; Emory), *Toward an Islamic Reformation: Civil Liberties, Human Rights and International Law* (Syracuse UP, 1990), influenced by Mahmoud Mohamed Taha. [source: WP UDHR; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Islamic_Declaration_of_Human_Rights; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdullahi_Ahmed_An-Na'im]
- Arab Charter on Human Rights exists as a further regional instrument (League of Arab States) — dates not checked. [source: WP UDHR mentions]

## 13. Arguments reconstructed step by step

**A. Mill's argument from fallibility (OL ch. II)** — see §5 for premises, objection (perfect censor / no need to assume infallibility) and reply (fallibilism; deliberative-perfectionist value). [SEP-MMP §3.3; SEP-FS §2.1]

**B. The republican argument from the kindly master** (SEP-REP §§1.2–1.3, 2.1; SEP-LPN §4):
1. If freedom were simply non-interference, a slave whose master happens never to interfere would be largely free.
2. Worse, the slave of a kindly master would be freer than the slave of a cruel one, and a slave who learns to ingratiate himself would become freer over time; a lightly governed colony would lose freedom by winning independence and passing more laws.
3. These verdicts are absurd: no slave is free while slavery exists ("it was slavery—not its mere incidents—that I hated").
4. So freedom requires not being subject to another's capacity to interfere arbitrarily: non-domination.
- OBJECTION (liberal negative-liberty theorists: Paley 1785; Goodin 2003; Carter 1999, 2008; Kramer 2003, 2008; Moen 2024): non-domination is just *secure*/probable non-interference. Overall negative freedom counts unprevented act-combinations and the probability of future constraint; the dominated are less negatively free because interference is likelier (fear, exposure, "bowing and scraping"). So republicans gave good empirical arguments for institutions, not a new concept.
- REPLY (Pettit; Skinner): what matters is not improbability but the *impossibility* (with impunity) of arbitrary interference — a kind husband in a patriarchal legal order still dominates his wife; we want not a better master but no master.
- COUNTER: impossibility is over-demanding (anyone could stab me; coalitions could always form: Dowding 2011, Simpson 2017); republicans restrict to interference "with impunity" (Pettit 2008; Skinner 2008), to "capable teams" (Lovett & Pettit 2018), or to non-"ignorable" types (Ingham & Lovett 2019); critics (Jafarov & Huseynli 2022) say ignorability collapses into low probability. "The jury is still out." [source: SEP-LPN §4]

**C. Devlin's disintegration argument and Hart's reply** (SEP-LL §§1–3; WP Devlin):
1. A society is constituted by a shared morality ("society means a community of ideas").
2. Undermining that morality threatens the society with disintegration (as treason threatens government).
3. A society may use law to protect itself against threats to its existence.
4. So society has a prima facie right to legislate against immorality, limited by toleration of maximum freedom consistent with its integrity, judged by "intolerance, indignation and disgust".
- OBJECTION (Hart): premise 1 is true only of a minimal Hobbes–Hume morality (no violence, honesty, promises, property), which the harm principle already covers; Devlin's version identifies a society with its morality at a moment, so any moral change becomes "destruction of society"; no empirical evidence; and positive morality cannot settle what critical morality permits.
- REPLY (Devlin/moralists): moralism need only claim a defeasible pro tanto reason (Moore, Duff); Brink: weak moralism compatible with liberal results. Few have found Devlin's relativism convincing since Hart. [source: SEP-LL; SEP-MMP §3.9]

**D. Berlin's paradox of positive liberty and MacCallum's objection** — see §§8–9: divided-self slippery slope (premises from SEP-LPN §2); objection: there is one triadic concept, and the authoritarian danger comes from how the agent and constraint variables are filled in, not from "positive liberty" as such; Berlin's own defenders reply that his targets were monism and the collective "true self" (SEP-BER §5.1).

## Debates
- **Was Berlin against positive liberty?** Popular reading: yes. Cherniss & Hardy (SEP-BER): no — both are genuine values; his target was monism and collective selves; Berlin's own account "less than fully even-handed"; historical accuracy of his genealogy (Rousseau, Fichte, Hegel) contested (Harris 2002; Crowder 2026). [source: SEP-BER §5.1]
- **One concept or two?** MacCallum (1967): one triadic concept; Taylor (1979): the opportunity/exercise distinction survives; Carter & Huseynli: the distinction is best seen as historically developed "stances" rather than a clean conceptual divide. [source: SEP-LPN §§5, 8]
- **Is republican freedom a third concept?** Pettit/Skinner/Lovett yes; Carter, Kramer, Goodin, Moen: reducible to (probabilistic) negative liberty. "The jury is still out." Civic-humanist (Arendt, Pocock) vs neo-Roman (Skinner, Pettit) readings of the tradition. [source: SEP-LPN §4; SEP-REP §§2.1, 3]
- **Is the harm principle Mill's only principle, and is it consistent?** Brink: harm neither necessary nor sufficient; offence exception inconsistent; free speech a partial qualification (Howard; Jacobson 2000; Turner 2014). [source: SEP-MMP; SEP-FS]
- **Legal moralism**: Devlin vs Hart; Feinberg's verdict; revival by Moore and Duff; strong vs weak moralism (Brink). Also the "Devlin wanted to keep homosexuality criminal" misreading (SEP-LL). [source: SEP-LL; SEP-MMP §3.9]
- **Paternalism and nudges**: Mill's anti-paternalism vs soft/hard paternalism; libertarian paternalism vs transparency/manipulation objections. [source: SEP-PAT]
- **Hate speech**: Waldron, Parekh, critical race theorists (restriction for dignity/assurance) vs Dworkin (legitimacy), Baker, Strossen, Heinze (counter-speech, democratic citizenship); the US vs most other democracies; ICCPR Art. 20. [source: SEP-FS; SEP-HS; WS-9]
- **Will vs interest theory of rights** — "waged for hundreds of years". [source: SEP-R §2.2.2]
- **Universality of human rights**: AAA 1947 relativism; Maritain's practical agreement; Asian values (Lee Kuan Yew; Bangkok 1993) vs Sen and Kim Dae-jung; African communitarianism (Menkiti; Gyekye's moderate vs radical communitarianism); Confucianism and rights (Ihara/Rosemont vs Chan); Islamic declarations (Cairo 1990/2020) vs critics (ICJ) and reformist Muslim scholars (An-Na'im). Present evenhandedly: SEP-HR notes Western delegates saw Asian-values claims as excuses for repression, while proponents stressed community, development and non-interference. [source: SEP-HR §5; SEP-R §7; WP sources]
- **Origins of subjective rights**: Locke/Hobbes vs Grotius vs Ockham vs Gratian vs Roman jurists vs Aristotle (Tierney, Brett, Donahue, Miller). [source: SEP-R §3]
- **Marx's essay**: critique of liberal rights vs charge of antisemitism (Avineri vs McLellan readings). [source: WP On_the_Jewish_Question]

## Surprises
- Devlin, the archetypal legal moralist, did not favour keeping private homosexual acts criminal: he judged them within the bounds of toleration and signed the 1965 *Times* letter backing the Wolfenden reforms (SEP-LL calls the opposite view a widespread error).
- Holmes never wrote "marketplace of ideas"; he wrote "free trade in ideas" and "the competition of the market". And SEP-FS doubts Mill held the strong marketplace thesis at all.
- "Freedom for the pike is death for the minnows" is not Berlin's coinage: he puts it in quotation marks, and it appears in Tawney's *Equality* (1952 ed.).
- Mill never used the phrase "harm principle".
- Gerald Dworkin (paternalism, 1972) and Ronald Dworkin (rights as trumps; hate-speech legitimacy argument) are different philosophers — easy to conflate.
- Most Muslim-majority UN members voted for the UDHR in 1948; Saudi Arabia was the only Muslim-majority abstainer, alongside the Soviet bloc and South Africa.
- "Libertarian paternalism" is sometimes called "soft paternalism" (WP), which clashes with Feinberg's older technical sense.
