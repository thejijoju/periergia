## 1. Framing: between medieval and modern (cross-ref *Scotus, Ockham and the Late Medieval Debates*)
- Marenbon: 15th-century thinkers outside the universities include Cusa, Lorenzo Valla (1407–57), Ficino (1433–99) and Pico (1463–94). Ficino and Pico are usually called "Renaissance", but neither lived to 1500, so they "do not fit into a chronology that places Renaissance Philosophy after Medieval Philosophy". The published Scotus/Ockham chapter already makes this point, so this chapter should pick it up and not repeat it. [source: SEP-Med §2.8, §5.2; dates from SEP-Fic, SEP-Pico]
- Copenhaver: "humanism" as a name for the new philology of ancient languages came about "not until the nineteenth century". Pico's 900 Conclusions were "thoroughly medieval" in content and presentation. [source: SEP-Pico §2.1]
- Lines: older accounts described Aristotelianism as "unified, static, and sterile", based in the universities and "beaten into retreat by the rise of Platonism". That picture is "now largely outdated". Charles Lohr: "the number of Latin commentaries on Aristotle composed within this brief period [1500–1650] exceeds that of the entire millennium from Boethius to Pomponazzi." Charles Schmitt urged speaking of "Renaissance Aristotelianisms" in the plural. [source: SEP-ArR §1]
- Lines's opening paraphrase of Whitehead: "Renaissance philosophy consisted, broadly speaking, of a series of footnotes to Aristotle." The Aldine Greek Aristotle of 1495–1498 filled five volumes. [source: SEP-ArR preamble]
- Copenhaver: Pico's "persistently esoteric and aggressively recondite manner" helps explain why the Renaissance "has had so little attention in the canonical history of the discipline as presented today in Anglophonia". [source: SEP-Pico preamble]

## 2. Petrarch and the humanist critique of scholasticism
- Petrarch (Francesco Petrarca), 20 July 1304 – 19 July 1374. He was one of the earliest Renaissance humanists, "often referred to as the father of humanism". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrarch]
- In 1345, in the Chapter Library of Verona Cathedral, he found Cicero's letters to Atticus (*Epistulae ad Atticum*), a collection not previously known to exist. The find "is often credited with initiating the 14th-century Italian Renaissance". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrarch]
- He knew no Greek: "Homer was dumb to him, while he was deaf to Homer". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrarch]
- Ascent of Mont Ventoux, 26 April 1336, described in a letter to Dionigi di Borgo San Sepolcro written some time afterwards. At the summit he opened Augustine's *Confessions*. Burckhardt noted that Buridan had climbed the same mountain a few years earlier. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrarch]
- He is credited with creating the idea of a historical "Dark Ages", which "most modern scholars now find inaccurate and misleading". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrarch]
- In *On His Own Ignorance and That of Many Others* (*De sui ipsius et multorum ignorantia*) he attacked scholars who followed Aristotle "blindly". He objected to Aristotle's useless or false topics, such as the crocodile moving its upper jaw or the number of hairs on a lion's head. He said Aristotle's ethics "lacks the words that sting and set afire and urge toward love of virtue and hatred of vice". Lines explains the clash as one of subject and method: humanists "prized eloquence and saw it as key to the production of virtue". Petrarch and others favoured Plato, though few could read him in Greek. [source: SEP-ArR §2, citing the translation in Cassirer, Kristeller and Randall (eds.), *The Renaissance Philosophy of Man* (CKR), pp. 47–133, quote at p. 103]
- IEP: Petrarch "praised Plato as the prince of philosophers … as an antidote to Scholastic Aristotelianism". Before Ficino, only the *Phaedo*, *Meno* and parts of the *Parmenides* and *Timaeus* were available in Latin. [source: IEP-Fic §2]
- Humanists did not simply reject Aristotle. Leonardo Bruni (1370–1444), chancellor of Florence, wrote a *Life of Aristotle* and retranslated his moral philosophy into Ciceronian Latin. By the 1490s Poliziano (1454–1494) was teaching all of Aristotle at Florence. [source: SEP-ArR §2]
- Civic humanism: Hans Baron's thesis presented civic humanism as a response to a crisis and as the work of the Florentine chancellors Coluccio Salutati and especially Leonardo Bruni. SEP-Civ treats civic humanism as distinct from republicanism. [source: SEP-Civ §1]

## 3. Lorenzo Valla
- Born in Rome, "most likely" 1406 (WP: c. 1407). Died 1457 (WP: 1 August 1457). Nauta calls him "one of the most important humanists of his time", with "a sense of self-importance verging on the pathological". [sources: SEP-Val preamble, §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorenzo_Valla]
- Career: taught rhetoric at Pavia from 1431 and fled in 1433 after attacking the jurist Bartolus. From 1435 he served Alfonso of Aragon, who was fighting to take Naples and was at odds with the pope. He finished the first version of his critique of scholastic philosophy in 1439 and the *Elegantiae linguae Latinae* (a Europe-wide bestseller) two years later. At Alfonso's court he showed the Donation of Constantine to be a forgery. He was tried for heterodoxy at Naples (he had denied the Apostles' Creed was apostolic) and was rescued by the king. He made peace with the pope in 1447 and became papal secretary in 1455. [source: SEP-Val §1]
- Donation of Constantine: *De falso credita et ementita Constantini donatione declamatio*, written 1439–1440. The Donation claimed that Constantine gave the Western Empire to the Church in thanks for being cured of leprosy by Pope Sylvester I. Valla's text was not formally published until 1517. Protestants such as Ulrich von Hutten and Luther took it up, and it was placed on the Index in the mid-16th century. An English translation was made for Thomas Cromwell in 1534. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorenzo_Valla; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donation_of_Constantine]
- Caveat on priority: Nicholas of Cusa had already declared the Donation a forgery, and Reginald Pecock reached the same conclusion independently. Valla "is credited with first exposing the forgery with solid philological arguments". The evidence includes Latin that could not have been written in the 4th century, anachronistic terms such as "fief", and internal inconsistencies of date. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donation_of_Constantine]
- Valla was the first to compare Jerome's Latin Bible with the Greek New Testament, "laying the foundations of critical biblical scholarship". Erasmus found and published a copy of his New Testament annotations near Louvain. [source: SEP-Val preamble, §1]
- **Critique of scholastic logic** (*Repastinatio dialectice et philosophie*, "re-ploughing", which also means "weeding out", of dialectic and philosophy; three versions):
  - Ten categories reduced to three: substance, quality and action, modelled on noun, adjective and verb. The six transcendentals (being, thing, something, one, true, good) are reduced to *res* (thing). "A stone is a being" (*lapis est ens*) is an awkward way of saying "a stone is a thing". Coinages such as *entitas*, *hecceitas* and *quidditas* are rejected because they break the rules of Latin word formation. [source: SEP-Val §2]
  - Nauta warns against equating this with Ockham. Ockham kept the categories as groups of terms; Valla treats them as the real aspects of things. [source: SEP-Val §2]
  - Dialectic is only a part of rhetoric's "invention", and it treats the syllogism "bare". The orator must teach, please and move. The syllogism is artificial and "puerile": why conclude Socrates is an animal once you have said every man is an animal and Socrates is a man? Reducing figures 2 and 3 to figure 1 is "useless and absurd". He accepts "deviant" valid forms, e.g. "God is in every place; Tartarus is a place; therefore God is in Tartarus", and "Homer is the greatest of poets; this man is the greatest of poets; therefore this man is Homer". He calls the third figure "completely foolish". [source: SEP-Val §3]
- Later reception: Luther and Leibniz both cited his dialogue on free will. Vives and Eck complained of his lack of philosophical acumen. [source: SEP-Val §1]

## 4. Byzantine scholars and Greek in Italy
- Manuel Chrysoloras was invited to Florence by Coluccio Salutati (in 1396) to teach Greek and arrived in the winter of 1397. His pupil Leonardo Bruni recalled that no one had studied Greek in northern Italy for 700 years. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Chrysoloras] (Consistent with the History *Renaissance* chapter: "taught Greek in Florence from 1397".)
- Council of Ferrara–Florence: a Byzantine delegation led by Emperor John VIII Palaiologos attended from 1438 to discuss church union. The union was demanded as the price of Western military help against the Ottomans. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemistos_Plethon; SEP-Bes §1]
- Gemistos Plethon (c. 1355/1360 – 1452 or 1454; Monfasani prefers 1454) was a Byzantine philosopher of Mistra. In 1438–39, at the Council of Florence, he "reintroduced Plato's ideas to Western Europe". While still in Florence he wrote *On the Differences of Aristotle from Plato* (*De differentiis*), and George Scholarius replied with a *Defence of Aristotle*. His posthumous *Nomoi* (*Book of Laws*) proposed a pagan revival. Scholarius, by then Patriarch Gennadius II, advised that it be destroyed. In 1466 Sigismondo Malatesta took his remains to Rimini. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemistos_Plethon]
- Plethon's Greek comparison of Plato and Aristotle, "written in Greek and therefore little read", praised the piety of Platonism and attacked the "frail Aristotelian foundation of Scholasticism". [source: SEP-Bes §2.3]
- The Cosimo–Plethon–Academy story is a DEBATE. Ficino's preface to his Plotinus was traditionally read as saying that Cosimo de' Medici attended Plethon's lectures and was inspired to found an Academy. James Hankins argues Ficino was misunderstood: there is no independent evidence the two met, a language barrier stood between them, and Ficino's "Academy" was "more of an 'informal gymnasium'". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemistos_Plethon] (WP's *Platonic Academy* article states the attendance as fact, so WP is internally inconsistent.)
- Ficino called Plethon "the second Plato", and Bessarion wondered whether Plato's soul occupied his body. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemistos_Plethon]
- Bessarion: born in Trebizond between 1399 and 1408. He became a monk (taking the name Bessarion) in 1423 and studied with Plethon at Mistra. Named metropolitan of Nicaea in 1437, he came to the Council. In April 1439 he gave an oration for union, and in July 1439 he read the act of union in Florence. Cardinal in 1439. His Greek manuscripts were given to Venice around 1468, forming the nucleus of the Biblioteca Marciana. He died at Ravenna in 1472. [source: SEP-Bes §1]
- Bessarion was "before Marsilio Ficino … the not so grey eminence responsible for the return of Plato in the Western world". George of Trebizond's *Comparatio philosophorum Platonis et Aristotelis* (c. 1458) attacked Plato as ignorant, impious and vicious. Bessarion answered with *In calumniatorem Platonis* (*Against the Slanderer of Plato*), printed 1469, after about ten years' work with his circle. [source: SEP-Bes preamble, §2.3]

## 5. Ficino and Florentine Platonism
- Born 19 October 1433 at Figline Valdarno; died 1 October 1499. His father was a physician to Cosimo de' Medici. Ordained priest on 18 December 1473; later a canon of Florence Cathedral. [sources: SEP-Fic §1, §2.2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsilio_Ficino]
- In 1462 Cosimo commissioned the Plato translation. Cosimo interrupted it to have the *Corpus Hermeticum* translated first. In 1464 Ficino read Plato translations to the dying Cosimo (the story comes from Ficino's own later account). [sources: IEP-Fic §1; SEP-Fic §2]
- **"Platonic Academy": a DEBATE.** Facts that can be documented: a letter of September 1462 to Cosimo speaks of "the Academy you arranged for us on the estate in Careggi", and in 1463 Cosimo gave Ficino a small property at Careggi. Celenza: sources "do not permit us to understand the Platonic Academy as a formal school … with regular meetings". "Academy" could mean Plato's dialogues themselves, a private school for youths, or a gathering of literary men. Ficino's academy "was not a specific place but a mindset". In a 1491 letter to Martin Prenninger he divided his circle into patrons, *confabulatores* (among them Alberti, Poliziano and Pico) and *auditores*, and described his role as Socratic midwifery. [source: SEP-Fic §1]
  - Hankins, "The Myth of the Platonic Academy of Florence", *Renaissance Quarterly* 44.3 (1991): 429–475. [source: IEP-Mac §6] IEP-Fic: "some are skeptical that any such academy ever really existed". WP: it had no statutes or membership records, and "there is no contemporary evidence that it was ever known as a 'Platonic Academy'". Arthur Field, *The Origins of the Platonic Academy of Florence* (1988), is the other side. [sources: IEP-Fic §2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy_(Florence); SEP-Fic bibliography]
- *Platonic Theology* (*Theologia platonica*), subtitled *On the Immortality of Souls*: composed 1469–1474, printed 1482, in 18 books. It was meant as a Christian corrective to Proclus's *Platonic Theology* and aimed "to paint a portrait of Plato as close as possible to the Christian truth". One book refutes Averroism, mostly with Thomist arguments. [sources: SEP-Fic §2.1; IEP-Fic §1]
- Complete Plato in Latin: 36 works (35 dialogues plus the Letters), drafts done by 1468–69, printed October 1484. The year was "almost certainly chosen for astrological reasons" (a Jupiter–Saturn conjunction). It was the first translation of all of Plato into a Western language, and Ficino's versions stayed standard "for over three centuries". [sources: SEP-Fic §2.3, §4; IEP-Fic §1]
- Plotinus: "the first full Greek to Latin translation of Plotinus's works along with commentary", begun in the late 1480s. [source: SEP-Fic §2.4] The 1492 printing date was not confirmed in SEP or IEP this session (see NOT VERIFIED).
- Other works: *De vita libri tres* (*Three Books on Life*, 1489), on medicine, astrology and talismans. Book 3 drew questions about his orthodoxy at the papal court in 1490, settled by August 1490. *On the Christian Religion* (Italian 1474, Latin 1476). [source: SEP-Fic §2.2, §2.4]
- Ancient theology (*prisca theologia*): wisdom passed down a chain from Hermes Trismegistus through Orpheus, Aglaophemus, Pythagoras and Philolaus to "our divine Plato". After 1469 he put Zoroaster first. Isaac Casaubon in the early 17th century showed the Hermetic texts were late antique. [source: SEP-Fic §3.1, quoting Ficino's preface in the translation of Copenhaver and Schmitt 1992, p. 147]
- Ontology: a five-level hierarchy of God, Angelic Mind, Rational Soul, "Quality" and matter (a variation on Plotinus's four levels). [source: SEP-Fic §3.2]
- **Ficino's main argument for immortality (natural appetite)**, as reconstructed by IEP:
  1. The soul's primary goal, a basic natural desire acted on by most people, is to ascend to union with God.
  2. Nature and God do nothing in vain; a universal natural desire left unfulfilled would be "otiose, vain, and even perverse", violating the perfection of nature and God's wisdom.
  3. This union is not reached in this life.
  4. So the desire must be fulfilled in an actual afterlife: the soul is immortal.
  - Supporting arguments: the intellect works best when freed from body and senses (it "discerns truly and is at its brightest" when "the clouds of phantasmata" are dispersed, *PT* IX.2.2), and body, being passive matter and extension, cannot produce the mind's operations. [source: IEP-Fic §5]
  - The motto of book 1, chapter 1 is "Were the soul not immortal, no creature would be more miserable than man." Elsewhere: "man alone … is a pilgrim in these regions". God would be a "wicked tyrant" to implant an aspiration that could not be fulfilled. [source: SEP-Fic §3.3–3.4, quoting the ITRL translation by Allen and Warden]
  - (Pomponazzi's reply, §7 below, works as the objection: perfect contemplation is reached by "barely one" in centuries, so the desire cannot ground human immortality in general.)
- Ficino's soul–body dualism "was not the same as 'mind-body' dualism" in Descartes's sense: for him soul acts on matter through *spiritus*, "a vapor of blood". [source: SEP-Fic §3.3–3.4]
- Love: "the purpose of love is the enjoyment of beauty". Beauty is "bait" and a "hook" drawing the soul up toward God. Ficino "invented the phrase Platonic love" (*amor platonicus*). His *Symposium* commentary (*De amore*) identified Platonic love with Christian love, as preparation for the love of God. Castiglione, Bembo, du Bellay and Ronsard took up "Platonic" love. "Platonic love" entered English in the 1630s, and its modern sense of a "sexless relationship" dates from about the 18th century. [sources: SEP-Fic §3.4, §4; IEP-Fic §2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_love]
- Legacy: Agostino Steuco coined *philosophia perennis*, and Leibniz took the term up in 1714. Francesco Patrizi held a Platonism chair at Ferrara 1578–92 and then at Rome. Brucker in the 1740s: Ficino "feigns, re-feigns, and changes Plato". [source: SEP-Fic §4]
- Ficino held, with the late ancient Platonists, that one should start with Aristotle: "Peripatetic learning represents the way toward Platonic wisdom" (letter to Diacceto, July 1493, Celenza's translation). [source: SEP-Fic §1]

## 6. Pico della Mirandola
- Born 24 February 1463 to the counts of Mirandola and Concordia. Studied canon law at Bologna from about age 14, then went to Ferrara and Padua, where he studied with Elia del Medigo, "a Jew and an Averroist Aristotelian". He went to Paris in 1485. In 1486 at Arezzo he tried to abduct a woman married to Giuliano Mariotto de' Medici. He died 17 November 1494 ("by poison, some said"), the day Florence fell to the French. Late in life he was drawn to Savonarola. [source: SEP-Pico §1]
- **900 Conclusions**: printed at Rome at the end of 1486, without a title, for a public disputation planned for early 1487. They were drawn from "ancient and medieval, pagan and Christian, Muslim, and Jewish" authorities. Part 1 (402 theses) is listed under authorities including Aquinas, Scotus, Ibn Rushd, Ibn Sina, al-Farabi, Maimonides, Ibn Bajja, Plotinus, Proclus and the "Kabbalist Jews". Part 2 (498) is his own. 119 theses, including the final 72, are explicitly Kabbalist. Most propositions are scholastic theology. [source: SEP-Pico §1, §2.1, §2.3]
- Concord thesis: "There is no inquiry, natural or theological, where Aristotle and Plato don't agree in meaning and content (*sensu et re*) even though they seem to disagree in words (*verbis*)." [source: SEP-Pico §2.1, translation evidently Copenhaver's]
- Condemnation: Innocent VIII indicted 13 theses. A commission declared 6 suspect and condemned 7. Pico's *Apology* (1487) led the pope to denounce all 900. Pico fled to France, was briefly jailed at the pope's request, and was back in Tuscany as Lorenzo's guest by summer 1488. Alexander VI pardoned him in 1493. [source: SEP-Pico §1] (WP-Pico says simply "thirteen theses were condemned", a simplification.)
- Other works: *Heptaplus* (1489), a Kabbalist commentary on Genesis 1; *On Being and the One* (after 1490), against Ficino on the One above being; *Disputations against Predictive Astrology* (unfinished; published by his nephew; Kepler noticed it). [source: SEP-Pico §2.3, §2.5]
- Kabbalah: Pico was "the first Christian who described Kabbalah in detail or treated it as worthwhile". His main informant and translator was the convert Flavius Mithridates, who inserted Christian messages into his translations. Pico held that Kabbalah, being Mosaic, was holier than Ficino's pagan ancient theology. [source: SEP-Pico §2.3]
- **Title and meaning of the *Oration*: a DEBATE.**
  - Copenhaver: "After he died in 1494, others mistakenly called this address an *Oration on the Dignity of Man*: Pico never gave it a title." The 1496 *Commentationes* (edited by his nephew Gianfrancesco) printed it as *Oratio quaedam elegantissima* ("A Very Elegant Oration"). The words *de dignitate hominis* first appear inside the 1504 edition, and "On the Dignity of Man" first appears as a title in a Basel collection of 1557. The speech was never given and never published in his lifetime. It runs about 7,000 words. [source: SEP-Pico §1, §2.1]
  - Copenhaver: dignity "was not his subject". His project was "to convince people that they could save their souls by using magic and Kabbalah to change themselves into angels". The reading of it as a manifesto of freedom and dignity runs from Tennemann (1761–1819) through Burckhardt (1860), Cassirer, Kristeller and Garin (1937). "The *Oration on the Dignity of Man* that located the human condition in freedom and dignity was written—and still is being written—by us post-Kantians, not by Giovanni Pico." [source: SEP-Pico §2.4]
  - The traditional reading, as WP gives it: "the 'dignity' described is that a human can become anything they desire". The Pico Project (Bologna and Brown) calls it the "Manifesto of the Renaissance". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oration_on_the_Dignity_of_Man]
  - Real treatises on human dignity existed before Pico: Giannozzo Manetti, *De dignitate et excellentia hominis* (shortly after 1450), against Lotario dei Segni (later Innocent III), *On Human Misery*. [source: SEP-Pico §2.1]
  - Copenhaver's seven-step outline of the *Oration*: (1) humans are amazing because they can choose to change what they are; (2) to choose well they must change into angels; (3) to emulate angels they must live like Cherubs; (4) they can learn this from ancient sages (Paul and Dionysius, Jacob, Job, Moses, Orpheus, Plato, Plotinus, Socrates, Pythagoras, Abraham, the Chaldeans, Jeremiah, Zoroaster); (5) because philosophy leads to the Cherubic life, Pico proclaims himself a philosopher; (6) philosophy has taught him magic and Kabbalah; (7) he will ignore his critics and debate. The curriculum runs through moral philosophy, dialectic, natural philosophy, theology, magic, Kabbalah and mystical union. [source: SEP-Pico §2.4]
  - Self-fashioning passage, in paraphrase only: in the opening pages "the Creator tells Adam that he, alone of all creatures, can make himself what he wants to be." [source: SEP-Pico §2.4] (For verbatim wording see NOT VERIFIED.)
- English translations: Elizabeth Forbes's translation, introduced by Kristeller, has been Pico's "main vehicle in English" (in print about 76 years). Others: Borghesi, Papio and Riva (Cambridge 2012); Copenhaver with Allen (ITRL 93, 2022). [source: SEP-Pico bibliography]
- Pico's nephew Gianfrancesco Pico (1469–1533; WP's short description gives 1470) became "an early voice for reviving scepticism as an instrument of Christian faith". [sources: SEP-Pico §1; IEP-RS §5a]

## 7. Pomponazzi on the soul (cross-ref *Thomas Aquinas*)
- Pietro Pomponazzi, 16 September 1462 (Mantua) – 18 May 1525. Arts degree at Padua in 1487; taught at Padua 1488–96 and 1499–1509, briefly at Ferrara, and at Bologna from 1511 until his death. Doctorate in medicine around 1496. He never learned Greek. [sources: SEP-Pom preamble, §1–2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietro_Pomponazzi]
- Background: his teacher Nicoletto Vernia and colleague Achillini held Averroes's thesis that there is one material intellect for all humans. Bishop Barozzi condemned the thesis in 1489 under threat of excommunication. [source: SEP-Pom §1]
- In 1513 the Fifth Lateran Council's bull *Apostolici regiminis* forbade teaching that the soul is mortal or one for all, and told philosophers to defend Christian doctrine. *Tractatus de immortalitate animae* (1516) came three years later. [source: SEP-Pom §1; cf. SEP-ArR §4.2.2]
- Method: he argued *in naturalibus* and by the *via peripatetica*, without "supposing something from faith or revelation", to find what is "in conformity with the words of Aristotle" (ch. 8), "in the way of doubt and not of assertion". [source: SEP-Pom §2–3]
- **The argument of *De immortalitate* (1516), step by step:**
  0. He first rejects Averroes's unicity thesis as "most false, unintelligible, monstrous, and quite foreign to Aristotle" (ch. 4), and then argues against Aquinas's view that the individual intellect is immortal, separable and unqualifiedly immaterial.
  1. The human soul is one, with vegetative, sensitive and rational powers, and the whole of it falls under natural philosophy.
  2. Aristotle: "the soul does not know at all without some phantasm" (*De anima* 3.7, 431a16–17). Experience confirms this, including the effects of injuries to the relevant organs.
  3. Phantasms are produced by the sensitive soul, which works in a bodily organ.
  4. So the intellective soul depends on matter for its object, though not as its subject (understanding itself uses no organ).
  5. What cannot work without the body cannot work after the body is destroyed.
  6. So the human soul is immortal only "in a relative or qualified sense" (more immortal than an animal soul, less than the separated intelligences) and is, absolutely speaking, mortal. It sits "midway between the material and the immaterial".
  - Further Aristotelian arguments: (a) a surviving soul must be somewhere, which needs locomotion that indivisibles cannot have, or nowhere, which multiplies intelligences toward an infinite number; (b) in an eternal world, immortal individual souls would be infinite in number or would transmigrate, and Aristotle rejects both; (c) Aquinas's creation of each soul is unacceptable to natural philosophy, where the soul is generated "by the sun and man" (*Physics* 2.2, 194b13), and whatever is generated perishes (ch. 8).
  - **Objection 1 (happiness):** humans need an immortal soul to reach their end, the perfection of the intellect. **Reply:** that perfection belongs only to the very rare gifted, healthy and withdrawn ("even in long centuries, barely one is found"). Humanity's common end is practical virtue, available in this life (ch. 13).
  - **Objection 2 (morality):** if the soul is mortal, people will commit crimes, since there is no punishment after death. **Reply:** "the essential reward of virtue is virtue itself". Religious teaching on immortality was introduced to make those incapable of philosophy behave, so that society can function (ch. 14).
  - Conclusion: immortality is a "neutral problem" (*problema neutrum*): no argument from nature alone proves either mortality or immortality, so faith decides, and Christians must hold immortality (ch. 15). Scotus had said much the same (Pomponazzi noted this). [sources: SEP-Pom §3; SEP-ArR §4.2.2]
- Reaction: he was attacked and defended, and denounced as a heretic but never tried. He never abjured, protected by Cardinal Pietro Bembo. *Apologia* (1518) answered his former student Gasparo Contarini and the preacher Ambrogio Fiandino. He was unsuccessfully accused of heresy in 1514 and 1518. *De immortalitate* was never put on the Index. Its only official 16th-century reprint (1525) carried the Dominican Javelli's refutation. [sources: SEP-Pom §1, §3; SEP-ArR §4.2.2]
- Sincerity is a DEBATE: his provisos deferring to the Church are seen by some (Renan 1852, Pine 1986) as protective and disingenuous. Others (Perrone Compagni 1999; Bianchi) see a coherent medieval separation of natural philosophy from theology, going back to Albertus Magnus. [source: SEP-Pom §2]
- Later works: *De incantationibus* (1520, printed 1556), giving natural explanations of marvels; *De fato* (1520, printed 1567), where he "never understood how Christian theologians preserve divine providence and human free will". [source: SEP-Pom §1, §5]
- He was known as an "Alexandrist", following Alexander of Aphrodisias, as opposed to the Averroists. [source: SEP-ArR §4.2.2]
- Consistency: the published Aquinas chapter says "Pomponazzi objected in 1516 that even on Aristotle's account we cannot think without mental images, which need the brain". This is CONSISTENT with steps 2–5. SEP speaks of organs and the sensitive soul rather than "the brain", a harmless gloss.

## 8. Renaissance Aristotelianism continuing: Padua and Zabarella
- Giacomo (Jacopo) Zabarella, b. Padua 5 August 1533, d. Padua 15 September 1589. "The prime representative of Renaissance Italian Aristotelianism". Works: *Opera logica* (1578) and *De rebus naturalibus* (1590). [sources: SEP-Zab preamble; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacopo_Zabarella]
- *Regressus* method: (1) from a known effect, infer the existence of its cause (resolution, *demonstratio quia*); (2) an intermediate "mental examination" (*examen mentale*) makes the confused knowledge of the cause distinct, which is meant to avoid circularity; (3) from the cause, demonstrate the effect (composition, *demonstratio propter quid*), so the effect is now known scientifically. [source: SEP-Zab §5]
- DEBATE: John Herman Randall (1940, 1961), following Cassirer, held that "the School of Padua" prepared Galileo's method (William Wallace defended this). Against it: the shared terminology was "commonplaces of late medieval and Renaissance thought", and Galileo's more important source was the Jesuits of the Collegio Romano (Paolo della Valle). [source: SEP-Zab §8]
- Cesare Cremonini (1550–1631), another Paduan professor, continued the tradition. [source: SEP-ArR §5]

## 9. Machiavelli
- Born 3 May 1469 in Florence, died 21 June 1527. He had a humanist education (Latin with Paolo da Ronciglione); his father Bernardo was a doctor of law. Second Chancellor of the Florentine Republic from 1498, serving under the gonfaloniere Piero Soderini (from 1502). In 1512 the Medici returned with Spanish and papal troops. He was dismissed and, wrongly suspected of conspiracy, imprisoned and tortured in early 1513. [sources: SEP-Mac §1; IEP-Mac §1]
- *The Prince*: written at the end of 1513 (perhaps early 1514). It was first dedicated to Giuliano de' Medici and then to Lorenzo de' Medici the Younger, "who almost certainly did not read it". Published posthumously in 1532 (Blado, Rome, with Clement VII's permission). Its working Latin title was *De Principatibus*. [sources: SEP-Mac §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince]
- *Discourses on the First Ten Books of Livy*: written c. 1514/15–1518/19, published 1531. *Art of War* published 1521. *Florentine Histories* commissioned in 1520 by Cardinal Giulio de' Medici and presented to him as Clement VII in 1525. The comedy *Mandragola* was first published in 1524. [sources: SEP-Mac §1; IEP-Mac §3]
- The Prince and the Discourses were placed on the Index of Paul IV in 1559. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince]
- Against the "mirrors for princes", Machiavelli held that "the only real concern in politics is the acquisition and maintenance of power". [source: SEP-Mac §2]
- "Effectual truth" (*verità effettuale*) appears in *Prince* ch. 15, the only place in his writings where he uses the phrase: it is more fitting "to go directly to the effectual truth of the thing than to the imagination of it". [sources: IEP-Mac §2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince (translation unnamed)]
- *Virtù*: not moral virtue but the qualities needed "to maintain his state" and "achieve great things". Above all a "flexible disposition", able to vary conduct "from good to evil and back again 'as fortune and circumstances dictate'" (*Prince* CW 66). [source: SEP-Mac §3]
- *Fortuna*, *Prince* ch. 25: fortune is like a destructive river, and it "shows her power where virtù and wisdom do not prepare to resist her". "Fortuna is a woman and it is necessary, in order to keep her under, to beat and maul her". She is "the friend of young men" (CW 90–92). WP: fortune is judge of "half of our actions". Hanna Pitkin's *Fortune is a Woman* (1984) studies the gendering. [sources: SEP-Mac §3 and bibliography; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince]
- **The argument from human nature to fear (*Prince* ch. 17), reconstructed:**
  1. A ruler's aim is to maintain the state.
  2. Men "are ungrateful, disloyal, insincere and deceitful, timid of danger and avid of profit".
  3. "Love is a bond of obligation which these miserable creatures break whenever it suits them"; "fear holds them fast by a dread of punishment that never passes."
  4. So, where one cannot have both, it is safer to be feared than loved. The limit is that the prince must avoid hatred, chiefly by keeping his hands off subjects' property and women.
  - The same reasoning gives "Since there cannot be good laws without good arms, I will not consider laws but speak of arms". [sources: SEP-Mac §2 (Gilbert CW 47, 62); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince]
  - Objection (moralist): this makes politics simple immorality. Replies: Skinner reads Machiavelli as preferring conventional virtue *ceteris paribus*, with vice as a "last best" option (the prince should do good if he can but be prepared to do evil if he must, CW 58); Croce sees a separate sphere of political necessity. [source: SEP-Mac §4]
- **How to read him: a DEBATE** (SEP and IEP survey it differently):
  - "Teacher of evil": Leo Strauss, *Thoughts on Machiavelli* (1958), pp. 9–10. Strauss also wrote that "even if we were forced to grant that Machiavelli was essentially a patriot or a scientist, we would not be forced to deny that he was a teacher of evil" (p. 11). In the 16th century he was "denounced as an apostle of the Devil". [sources: SEP-Mac §4; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince]
  - Realist or pragmatist: Croce (1925). "Galileo of politics", the scientist reading: Olschki 1945, Cassirer 1946; "largely gone out of favor" (Viroli). [source: SEP-Mac §4]
  - Republican, with context stressed: Skinner (1978), Pocock (*The Machiavellian Moment*, 1975, which traces Machiavellian republicanism into the Atlantic world and the American founders), Baron and Viroli. The "neo-Roman" thinkers Pettit, Skinner and Viroli use him for "freedom as non-domination". McCormick (2011) argues for a democratic Machiavelli. [sources: SEP-Mac §4, §7; IEP-Mac §5]
  - Satire or irony: Alberico Gentili in the 16th century, Rousseau and Spinoza; more recently Garrett Mattingly (1958) and Erica Benner. Mary Dietz (1986) argues the book was a "trap" for the Medici. [sources: SEP-Mac §4; IEP-Mac §5]
  - Rousseau, *Social Contract* III.6 note: "Machiavelli was a proper man and a good citizen; but, being attached to the court of the Medici, he could not help veiling his love of liberty in the midst of his country's oppression. The choice of his detestable hero, Cesare Borgia, clearly enough shows his hidden aim…" [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince, quoting the constitution.org text (translator unnamed; probably G. D. H. Cole, not checked)]
  - Viroli rejects the "book of Republicans" defence, which "comes from Rousseau's desire to rescue its author's bad reputation". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince]
  - Unity of *Prince* and *Discourses* (Strauss, Mansfield, Zuckert) versus change of mind (Baron). Mansfield and Tarcov treat the two as "a pair of works, not much different, if at all, in time of composition". [sources: IEP-Mac §5; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince]
  - IEP's own five "camps": (1) satire (Gentili, Rousseau, Spinoza, Benner); (2) contextual republican (Skinner, Pocock, Baron, Wootton, Viroli); (3) unity from different starting points (Berlin, Wolin, Croce); (4) unity, Straussian (Strauss, Mansfield, Zuckert, Tarcov, Sullivan); (5) Hegelian or Marxist (Lefort, Vatter, Gramsci, Althusser). [source: IEP-Mac §5]
- History cross-ref: the History *Renaissance* chapter compares him with the Indian *Arthashastra*. That is fine as an analogy, but no philosophy source here makes the comparison.

## 10. The revival of scepticism
- Before 1562 the main sources on ancient scepticism were Augustine, Cicero (*Academica*), Diogenes Laertius, Galen and Plutarch. [source: IEP-RS §2]
- Gianfrancesco Pico's *Examination of the Vanity of the Doctrines of the Gentiles* (1520) is "often acknowledged as the first use of Pyrrhonism in Christian apologetics". He used a Sextus manuscript in Florence. [source: IEP-RS §5a]
- Erasmus–Luther (1524–25): Erasmus, *De libero arbitrio* (1524): "I take so little pleasure in assertions that I will gladly seek refuge in Scepticism" (CWE 76: 7). He deferred to the Church's consensus. Luther, *On the Bondage of the Will* (1525): "Permit us to be asserters … The Holy Spirit is no Sceptic!" Popkin uses this exchange as the problem of the criterion entering religious debate. [sources: SEP-Era §5 (CWE translation); IEP-RS §5b]
- **Sextus in Latin:** in 1562 the Calvinist printer Henri Estienne (Henricus Stephanus, c. 1528–1598) published at Geneva the first Latin translation of the *Outlines of Pyrrhonism*. In 1569 a complete Latin Sextus followed, with Gentian Hervet (1499–1584, a Catholic Counter-Reformer) translating *Adversus mathematicos*. The Greek text was first printed only in 1621 (by the Chouets). Both prefaces present Pyrrhonism as useful to Christians for humbling natural reason. [sources: IEP-RS §6; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sextus_Empiricus]
- **Popkin thesis: a DEBATE.** Richard Popkin (1923–2005), *The History of Scepticism from Erasmus to Descartes* (1960), expanded in 1979 (to Spinoza) and 2003 (*from Savonarola to Bayle*). Thesis: the revival of Pyrrhonism, combined with Reformation quarrels over the rule of faith (the "problem of the criterion"), caused a "crisis of doubt" that shaped early modern philosophy, with Montaigne launching the "Pyrrhonian crisis". Criticisms: (1) it neglects Academic scepticism (Charles Schmitt, 1972, on Cicero's *Academica*; Maia Neto); (2) Sextus was not wholly forgotten (Luciano Floridi, 2002, on the manuscripts), and medieval thinkers discussed sceptical challenges (Perler, Lagerlund). [sources: IEP-RS §3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Popkin]
- Others: Francisco Sanches (1551–1623), *That Nothing Is Known* (1581), against Aristotelian *scientia*; Omer Talon and Petrus Ramus and the Academic method *in utramque partem*. Pierre Charron (1541 – 16 November 1603; IEP-RS misprints "1541–1643"), *Les Trois Véritez* (1594) and *De la Sagesse* (1601), which spread Montaigne's thought. [sources: IEP-RS §5c, §7b–c; IEP-Mon §6; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Charron]
- Consistency: the *Hellenistic Philosophy* chapter says "Sextus was printed in Latin in 1562", and the overview says "Sextus in print by 1562, weaponized by Montaigne". Both are CONSISTENT. The writer should add that 1562 covered only the *Outlines*, that the full Sextus came in 1569, and that "weaponized" reflects Popkin's thesis, which is contested.

## 11. Montaigne
- Michel Eyquem de Montaigne, born 28 February 1533 at the château de Montaigne, about 30 miles east of Bordeaux; died 13 September 1592. His father had a German tutor and the household speak only Latin to him, so he knew no French until about age six. He attended the Collège de Guyenne (under George Buchanan). He was a magistrate at Périgueux and in the Bordeaux Parlement, where he met Étienne de La Boétie. [source: IEP-Mon §1]
- La Boétie (1 November 1530 – 18 August 1563) wrote the *Discourse on Voluntary Servitude*: tyrants have power because the people give it to them. Montaigne's "Of Friendship" (I.28): "If you press me to say why I loved him, I can say no more than because it was he, because it was I." The last words were added in the margins of his 1588 copy, first "because it was he" and then, in different ink, "because it was I". [sources: IEP-Mon §1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89tienne_de_La_Bo%C3%A9tie; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_de_Montaigne (translation unnamed)]
- He sold his office in 1570 and retired in 1571 (aged 37) to his tower library, where he had maxims painted on the beams, from Ecclesiastes, Sextus, Lucretius and others. In 1569 he translated Raymond Sebond's *Natural Theology*. [sources: IEP-Mon §1; SEP-Mon §1; IEP-RS §7a.ii]
- *Essais* editions: 1580 (Books I–II, Bordeaux, 94 chapters); 1582; 1588 (Paris, adding Book III; IEP calls it the fifth edition); 1595 (posthumous, edited by Marie de Gournay). Modern editions mark the layers A/B/C. The "Bordeaux Copy" (a 1588 copy with his manuscript additions) became the 20th-century standard (Strowski, Villey). The 2007 Pléiade edition returned to the 1595 text, so the debate continues. [sources: SEP-Mon §2; IEP-Mon §1]
- Political life: Mayor of Bordeaux 1581–85 (two terms). He was briefly held in the Bastille in July 1588 and released the same day at Catherine de' Medici's request. He remained Catholic; three siblings became Protestant. Context: the French Wars of Religion from 1562. [source: IEP-Mon §1]
- "Que sais-je?": around 1576 he had a medal struck with his age, "Epecho" ("I abstain", in Greek), and "Que sais-je?" ("What do I know?"). [source: SEP-Mon §4]
- **The sceptical argument of the "Apology for Raymond Sebond" (II.12), step by step** (IEP-RS §7a.ii; page numbers are Frame, F):
  - Setting: probably written at the request of Marguerite de Valois, to defend Sebond's claim that the articles of faith can be shown by natural reason. It is structured like a scholastic disputed question (Hartle).
  - Objection 1: faith comes from grace, not reason. Reply: true, but faith can still use rational support (F 321).
  - Objection 2: Sebond's arguments fail to prove what they claim. Reply: no human reasoning proves anything conclusively, in any field (F 327–418), argued as follows:
  1. Animals: comparing animal behaviour shows we have no criterion for preferring our impressions to theirs (Sextus's first mode).
  2. Human disagreement: centuries of philosophy have produced no theory that commands universal assent (second mode).
  3. Uselessness: knowledge has not made people happier or better.
  4. Three possible outcomes of inquiry are finding the truth, denying it can be found, or continuing to search. The Pyrrhonists rightly keep searching (paraphrasing the opening of the *Outlines*, F 371). Pyrrhonism "presents man naked and empty … annihilating his judgment to make room for faith" (F 375).
  5. Senses: with fewer senses we would have a different world-picture, and there may be qualities we cannot perceive; humans disagree with animals and with each other (third, first and second modes; F 443–54).
  6. The criterion, as a circle: "To judge the appearances that we receive of objects, we would need a judicatory instrument; to verify this instrument, we need a demonstration; to verify the demonstration, an instrument: there we are in a circle" (F 454). If reason is the judge instead, demonstration runs into an infinite regress.
  7. The Academic way out ("probable" appearances) needs a criterion for likelihood, so it regresses too. The Pyrrhonian way out (follow appearances and custom) rests on changeable custom.
  8. Conclusion: "nothing certain can be established about one thing by another, both the judging and the judged being in continual change and motion" (F 455). Knowledge comes only by divine grace.
  - In the middle he warns the addressee that this is a "final fencer's trick" and a "desperate stroke", to be used "reservedly" (F 419).
  - **Interpretation: a DEBATE.** Popkin (and Brahami) read him as a sincere sceptical fideist. Hartle (2003, 2013) and Maia Neto read the argument as dialectical, drawn from the premises of Sebond's secular critics; if knowledge is what those critics say it is, there is none. Others stress Academic, Ciceronian probabilism: SEP-Mon says that after the 1570s he "no longer read Sextus" and turned to Cicero. Villey's three stages (Stoic, then a "Pyrrhonian crisis", then Epicurean) are now largely rejected. Charge of atheism: the *Essais* were placed on the Index in 1676. [sources: IEP-RS §7a; SEP-Mon §4; IEP-Mon §3, §6]
- Montaigne's own Pyrrhonist practice matches Sextus's "fourfold observances": nature, feelings, laws and customs, and the arts. This underlies a conservative obedience to custom. [source: IEP-Mon §3; IEP-RS §7a.iii]
- **"Of Cannibals" (I.31) and relativism:** written c. 1580, it describes the Tupinambá of Brazil. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Of_Cannibals]
  - "Each man calls barbarism whatever is not his own practice; for indeed it seems we have no other test of truth and reason than the example and pattern of the opinions and customs of the country we live in." [source: IEP-Mon §4, Frame translation]
  - "I think there is more barbarity in eating a man alive than in eating him dead, and in tearing by tortures and the rack a body still full of feeling" (F 189). This is aimed at the cruelty of Christians toward one another in the French civil wars. [source: SEP-Mon §5]
  - "So we may well call these people barbarians, in respect to the rules of reason, but not in respect to ourselves, who surpass them in every kind of barbarity" (F 189). SEP: there are "degrees of barbarity", so he is not a thoroughgoing relativist. [source: SEP-Mon §5]
  - DEBATE: Lévi-Strauss hailed Montaigne as "the pioneer of cultural relativism", and Villey first used the terms "relativity" and "relativism" of him. Against this, he criticises customs and appeals to "reason" and "nature" (IEP-Mon calls this an apparent "moral objectivism"). [sources: SEP-Mon §5; IEP-Mon §4]
  - Custom: "there is nothing that custom will not or cannot do" (I.23); "Circe's drink". [source: SEP-Mon §5 (Frame)]
  - In "Of coaches" he condemns European conduct in the New World. Florio's 1603 English translation of "Of Cannibals" is often seen as an influence on *The Tempest*. [sources: IEP-Mon §5; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Of_Cannibals]
- Other quotations (Frame, per SEP-Mon notes): "Truly man is a marvelously vain, diverse, and undulating object" (I.1, F 5); "Others form man; I tell of him" (III.2, F 740); "We have no communication with being" (II.12, F 553); "The question is not who will hit the ring, but who will make the best runs at it" (III.8, F 860). [source: SEP-Mon §3 and notes 13, 23, 26, 27]
- Influence: Bacon borrowed the title *Essayes* (1597). Descartes drew on him without naming him (*Discourse*, 1637). Pascal used and opposed him, and the Port-Royal *Logic* (1662) rejected him. Emerson's "Montaigne; or, the Skeptic"; Nietzsche. [sources: IEP-Mon §6; SEP-Mon §6]

## 12. Bruno
- Filippo Bruno, born January or February 1548 at Nola near Naples. He took the name Giordano as a Dominican at San Domenico Maggiore in Naples and was ordained priest in 1572. He was investigated for heresy (probably on the Trinity) and fled the order in 1576. He then wandered through Italy, France, Switzerland, England (1583–85; *The Ash Wednesday Supper*, London 1584), Germany and Bohemia. About 40 works survive. [sources: SEP-Bru §1; IEP-Bru §1]
- Cosmology: taking Copernicus as his starting point, Bruno held that the universe is infinite, animate, eternal and "populated by numberless solar systems". He rejected the Aristotelian elemental spheres and finite cosmos. He drew on Cusa: the infinite universe as "an infinite likeness" or "mirror" of God. Key dialogues: *De la causa, principio et uno* and *De l'infinito universo e mondi* (both 1584). Later Latin poems (a trilogy published at Frankfurt, where he went in 1590, per IEP): *De minimo*, *De monade*, *De immenso*. [sources: SEP-Bru preamble, §3; IEP-Bru §1]
- Trial: in 1591 he returned to Venice, invited by Giovanni Mocenigo, who denounced him to the Venetian Inquisition on 22 May 1592. He was extradited to Rome in January 1593. On 14 January 1599 eight heretical propositions were drawn up by Bellarmine and Tragagliolo (the list is now lost). On 21 December 1599 he said he "neither needed nor wished to recant". On 20 January 1600 he was condemned with Clement VIII presiding. At dawn on 17 February 1600, in the Campo de' Fiori, "With a metal plate clamped over his tongue", he was burned alive. [source: SEP-Bru §1] Schoppe reports him saying "Perhaps you pronounce this sentence against me with greater fear than I receive it" (*Maiori forsan cum timore …*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giordano_Bruno]
- **Why he was burned: a DEBATE.** The eight propositions are lost. Luigi Firpo's reconstruction of the charges lists opinions against the Catholic faith; on the Trinity, Christ's divinity and the Incarnation; on Mary's virginity; on transubstantiation and the Mass; the plurality of worlds and their eternity; metempsychosis, including into animals; and magic and divination. Bruno held firm on the plurality of worlds. "Some historians believe his heresy trial was a response not to his cosmological views but rather to his views on religion and the afterlife" (Yates 1964, p. 450), "while others reject this view." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giordano_Bruno]
- Reputation: "martyr for science" for 19th- and early 20th-century commentators. The Risorgimento statue by Ettore Ferrari was erected in Campo de' Fiori in 1889 over Vatican opposition. Catholic apologists spoke of "Brunomania". Bayle called him the "knight errant of philosophy". Frances Yates, *Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition* (1964), made magic and Hermeticism central. Knox (SEP) says such readings "obscure Bruno's stated intention to articulate a 'new philosophy'". [sources: SEP-Bru §9; IEP-Bru §3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giordano_Bruno]

## 13. Campanella and the late-Renaissance "novatores"
- Tommaso Campanella (Stilo, 1568 – Paris, 21 May 1639), a Dominican and follower of Telesio's sense-based natural philosophy (*Philosophia sensibus demonstrata*). He was arrested by the Inquisition in 1594. In 1599 he led a conspiracy against Spanish rule in Calabria, avoided death by feigning madness, and was imprisoned for almost 27 years (released 1626). He went into exile in Paris in 1634. *The City of the Sun*, an ideal communal city, was written in Italian in 1602 and published in Latin at Frankfurt in 1623. *Apologia pro Galileo* was written 1616 and printed 1622. [sources: SEP-Cam §1, §4, §7; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tommaso_Campanella]
- Bernardino Telesio (1509–1588), an empiricist critic of metaphysics, influenced Campanella, Bruno, Gassendi, Bacon and Hobbes. Francesco Patrizi of Cherso (1529–1597) was an anti-Aristotelian. [sources: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/telesio/ preamble; https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/patrizi/ preamble]

## 14. Women (cross-ref Christine de Pizan in *Scotus, Ockham* and History *Renaissance*)
- Isotta Nogarola (1418–1466) of Verona, pupil of Martino Rizzoni (a student of Guarino), wrote the *Dialogue on the Equal or Unequal Sin of Adam and Eve* (*De pari aut impari Evae atque Adae peccato*, 1451). She was ridiculed; to Guarino: "Why... was I born a woman, to be scorned by men in words and deeds?" [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotta_Nogarola (translation unnamed)]
- Laura Cereta (Brescia, September 1469 – 1499) wrote Latin letters 1488–92 on women's education, war and marriage. She argued for women's right to higher education, and Diana Robin calls her an early feminist. Critics charged that a woman should not be educated and that her father wrote her letters. One letter is a "Defense of the Liberal Instruction of Women" to "Bibulus Sempronius". Her letters were first printed by Tomasini in 1640. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laura_Cereta]
- Cassandra Fedele (Venice, c. 1465 – 1558) gave a Latin oration at Padua in 1487 (*Oratio pro Bertucio Lamberto*, printed 1487/1488/1489). She corresponded with humanists (Poliziano praised her) and was invited by Isabella of Castile. She spoke before Doge Agostino Barbarigo and the Senate on higher education for women. After marrying in 1499 she wrote little. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassandra_Fedele]
- Marie Le Jars de Gournay (6 October 1565 – 1645) met Montaigne in 1588; he called her his "adopted daughter". She edited the posthumous *Essais* (1595) and many later editions. She lived precariously as a single woman writing in Paris and translated Cicero, Ovid, Tacitus, Sallust and Virgil (the *Aeneid*). [sources: IEP-Gou §1–2; IEP-Mon §1]
  - **Her argument in *Equality of Men and Women* (*Égalité des hommes et des femmes*, 1622):**
    1. If women were naturally inferior, the best authorities and the historical record would show it.
    2. Philosophers support equality. Plato, "to whom no one denies the title of 'divine,' assigns them [women] the same rights, faculties, and functions in the *Republic*". So do Aristotle, Cicero, Plutarch, Boccaccio, Tasso and Erasmus.
    3. Scripture: Genesis makes both sexes in the divine image, hence both rational and bearers of the same rights and duties. Deborah, Judith and Mary Magdalene ("Apostle to the Apostles") held roles like men's. Paul's restrictions were only a precaution.
    4. History: Sappho, Hypatia and Catherine of Siena show women's capacity.
    5. The gap in achievement is explained by lack of education: "If the ladies arrive less frequently to the heights of excellence than do the gentlemen, it is because of this lack of good education."
    6. So present subordination rests on prejudice, not nature.
    - Companion piece: *The Ladies' Grievance* (*Grief des dames*, 1626), which calls women's condition a tacit slavery. [source: IEP-Gou §3e (Conley's translations); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_de_Gournay]
- Joan Kelly's question "Did women have a Renaissance?" is already in the History chapter; do not repeat it at length.

## 15. Beyond Europe and the connected Renaissance (brief; cross-ref the planned "Beyond Europe" chapter)
- Pico's 900 theses drew on Ibn Rushd, Ibn Sina, al-Farabi, Ibn Bajja and Maimonides. His Jewish teachers included Elia del Medigo (c. 1458 – c. 1493, from Candia on Crete; seen as an Averroist by Christians, a Maimonidean by himself according to J. J. Ross), Flavius Mithridates and Yohanan Alemanno. [sources: SEP-Pico §2.1, §2.3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elia_del_Medigo]
- Judah Leon Abravanel (Leone Ebreo, c. 1460 Lisbon – c. 1530?) left Spain after the expulsion of 1492. His *Dialoghi d'amore* (*Dialogues of Love*) is "regarded as one of the most important philosophical works of its time". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judah_Leon_Abravanel]
- Renaissance Averroism at Padua: the Aristotle of the Italian universities was read through Averroes's commentaries; Pomponazzi lectured from them even though he rejected unicity. [source: SEP-Pom §1]
- Contemporaries elsewhere: Wang Yangming (26 October 1472 – 9 January 1529), the Ming Neo-Confucian of the School of Mind, lived at the same time as Machiavelli. Mullā Ṣadrā (c. 1571/2 – c. 1635/40) of Safavid Iran, whom Oliver Leaman calls perhaps "the single most important and influential philosopher in the Muslim world in the last four hundred years". Mention only as cross-references to the *Beyond Europe* and *Chinese Buddhism and Neo-Confucianism* chapters. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_Yangming; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulla_Sadra]

## 16. Erasmus and the Reformation (context only)
- Erasmus: born in Rotterdam on 28 October 1469 (Goudriaan 2019), died 1536. Works: *Enchiridion* (1503), *Praise of Folly* (1511), which mocks scholastic theologians, the *Adagia*, and the Greek New Testament (1516), "the first to reach the market". His thought is best called "Christian Humanism". He never endorsed schism. *De libero arbitrio* (1524) answered Luther, who replied in *De servo arbitrio* (1525). [source: SEP-Era §1, §5]

## Debates
- **Is "Renaissance philosophy" a period at all?** Marenbon: Ficino and Pico died before 1500, and university scholasticism continued for centuries. Lines and Schmitt: Aristotelianism(s) dominated and did not retreat. Copenhaver: "humanism" is a 19th-century label, and Pico was "thoroughly medieval". [sources: SEP-Med; SEP-ArR §1; SEP-Pico §2.1]
- **Platonic Academy**: a formal institution (older view; Field 1988) or a myth or "mindset" (Hankins 1991; Celenza)? Likewise, did Cosimo hear Plethon? (Hankins: no evidence.) [sources: SEP-Fic §1; IEP-Fic §2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemistos_Plethon]
- **Pico's *Oration***: a manifesto of human dignity and freedom (Burckhardt, Cassirer, Garin, Kristeller; WP, "Manifesto of the Renaissance") or a call to self-annihilating mystical ascent through magic and Kabbalah, untitled by Pico (Copenhaver)? [sources: SEP-Pico §2.1, §2.4; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oration_on_the_Dignity_of_Man]
- **Pomponazzi's sincerity**: were his provisos protective (Renan, Pine) or a coherent separation of philosophy and theology (Perrone Compagni, Bianchi)? [source: SEP-Pom §2]
- **Padua and modern science**: Randall and Wallace say *regressus* prepared Galileo's method; critics call the shared terms commonplaces and point to the Jesuit Collegio Romano instead. [source: SEP-Zab §8]
- **Machiavelli**: "teacher of evil" (Strauss); amoral realist (Croce); scientist (Olschki, Cassirer; now out of favour); republican (Skinner, Pocock, Viroli); satirist (Gentili, Rousseau, Spinoza, Mattingly, Benner); "trap" (Dietz). [sources: SEP-Mac §4; IEP-Mac §5]
- **Popkin thesis** on the sceptical crisis: influential, but criticised for neglecting Academic scepticism (Schmitt, Maia Neto) and overstating Sextus's medieval oblivion (Floridi, Lagerlund). [source: IEP-RS §3]
- **Montaigne's scepticism**: Pyrrhonian fideist (Popkin, Brahami); dialectical, not his own view (Hartle, Maia Neto); Academic and Ciceronian (Limbrick, Eva, Prat; SEP-Mon); not really a sceptic (Hartle, Sève); or even an atheist. Villey's "sceptical crisis" is now mostly rejected. [sources: IEP-RS §7a; IEP-Mon §3; SEP-Mon §4]
- **Montaigne's relativism**: a pioneer of cultural relativism (Lévi-Strauss; Villey's terms) or a critic of custom who appeals to reason and degrees of barbarity (SEP-Mon; IEP-Mon's "moral objectivism")? [sources: SEP-Mon §5; IEP-Mon §4]
- **Bruno**: condemned for cosmology (plurality of worlds) or for theology (Trinity, Christ, transubstantiation, metempsychosis)? Yates 1964 says religion; others disagree. Was he a martyr of science, a Hermetic magus (Yates), or the philosopher of a "new philosophy" (Knox)? [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giordano_Bruno; SEP-Bru §9]
