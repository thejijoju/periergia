## 1. Kinds of knowledge

- Standard threefold division (SEP-KH opening): (1) acquaintance knowledge ("we know our mothers, our friends, our pets"); (2) knowledge of facts, propositional knowledge, knowledge-that; (3) knowledge-how ("play the piano, make a pie, walk, speak…"). [source: SEP-KH]
- The analysis-of-knowledge project targets propositional knowledge, "S knows that P"; "knows-wh" locutions (knows whether/who) also count as propositional ("Sooyung can know whether the shop is open by knowing that the shop is not open"). Acquaintance (Susan knows Alyssa, knows Vancouver) is distinguished. [source: SEP-KA intro]
- The acquaintance/description terminology is "most clearly associated with Bertrand Russell" (Russell 1910–11, "Knowledge by Acquaintance and Knowledge by Description", Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society 11: 108–128; and *The Problems of Philosophy*, 1912, ch. 5). Russell 1912: 78: "We shall say that we have acquaintance with anything of which we are directly aware, without the intermediary of any process of inference or any knowledge of truths." [source: SEP-KAD §1]
- Ryle, *The Concept of Mind* (1949), brought the knowing-how/knowing-that distinction "to scrutiny"; he attacked the "intellectualist legend" (knowledge-how is knowledge-that). Anti-intellectualism "has been the received view… for a long time"; renewed debate in "the last twenty years". [source: SEP-KH intro; WP-The_Concept_of_Mind for 1949]
- Ryle's regress (quoted from Ryle 1949: 19): "if, for any operation to be intelligently executed, a prior theoretical operation had first to be performed and performed intelligently, it would be a logical impossibility for anyone ever to break into the circle." And (1949: 20): "'Intelligent' cannot be defined in terms of 'intellectual' or 'knowing how' in terms of 'knowing that'". Reconstruction is contested. [source: SEP-KH §1]
- ARGUMENT (Ryle's regress, as reconstructed in SEP-KH §1.1 "contemplation argument"): SI: knowing how to Φ consists in knowing some proposition p. AP: if one Φs, one employs one's knowledge-how to Φ. CP: to employ knowledge that p one must contemplate p. Then Φ-ing requires contemplating p; contemplating is an action, so requires knowledge-how to contemplate p, so contemplating another proposition q… infinite regress; so no one could act in finite time; so SI is false. OBJECTION/REPLY (Stanley & Williamson 2001, following Ginet 1975): AP holds only for intentional actions; contemplating a proposition can be non-intentional (braking at a red light without intentionally contemplating "there is a red light"), so the regress stops. Ginet also denies CP (opening a door automatically). [source: SEP-KH §1.1]
- Stanley & Williamson, "Knowing How", *Journal of Philosophy* 98(8) (2001): 411–444 — revived intellectualism via a linguistic argument: "knows how to Φ" has the form "knows + embedded question", like "knows where her piano is"; so knowing how to Φ = knowing a proposition answering "how could one Φ?" (knowing, of some way w, that w is a way to Φ). [source: SEP-KH §4 and bibliography]
- Objection to intellectualism ("Swimming"): an instructor points at a swimmer and says "That is a way in which you could swim"; I know a true proposition about how to swim yet might drown. Intellectualist reply: knowledge-how requires knowing the proposition under a "practical mode of presentation". [source: SEP-KH §2]
- Ability: Stanley & Williamson (2001) held a ski instructor may know how to do a stunt without being able to; anti-intellectualists (Noë, Glick, Setiya) distinguish general from circumstantial ability. [source: SEP-KH §8]
- Mohist parallel (see §10 below): later Mohists characterise knowing (zhī 知) as a practical ability (néng 能) to describe/recognise a thing, "an ability or know-how" rather than an inner representation. [source: SEP-MC §5.1]

## 2. Plato: the Theaetetus

- The *Theaetetus* "probably dates from about 369 BC"; SEP calls it "arguably Plato's greatest work on epistemology"; the only Platonic "set-piece discussion of the question 'What is knowledge?'". Frame: Eucleides and Terpsion at Megara in 369 BC hear a slave read Eucleides' memoir of a conversation of 399 BC, shortly before Socrates' trial. Respondents: Theaetetus (young mathematician) and his tutor Theodorus. [source: SEP-Th §§1–2]
- The dialogue is aporetic: it reviews three definitions (plus a preliminary non-definition) and rejects each; at the end (210a–b) knowledge is not perception (aisthêsis), not correct opinion (orthê doxa), not correct opinion with an account (logos). The object is epistēmē. [source: SEP-Th §1]
- Structure with Stephanus numbers (SEP section headings): question "What is knowledge?" at 145d; D0 definition by examples 146a–151d (Socrates rejects lists of examples, 146e–147c; midwife analogy); D1 "Knowledge is perception" 151e–187a (proposed 151d7–e3: "Knowledge is nothing other than perception"; equated with Protagoras' measure doctrine; final refutation 183c4–187a8 via the koina — existence, sameness, difference — which the mind grasps not through any sense); D2 "Knowledge is true judgement" 187b–201c (five puzzles about false belief incl. Wax Tablet 190e5–196c5 and Aviary 196d1–200d4; final refutation 200d5–201c7); D3 "Knowledge is true judgement with an account" (meta logou alêthê doxan) 201d–210a: Dream of Socrates 201d8–202d7, critique 202d8–206c2, three senses of logos 206c2–210a9. [source: SEP-Th table of contents and §§5–8]
- Jury argument against D2 (200d–201c): a skilled lawyer can bring jurymen into true belief without knowledge; so knowledge ≠ true belief. SEP notes it seems also to count against D3, and that Plato gives two explanations of why the jury lacks knowledge (limited time, 201b3/172e1; second-hand judgement, 201b9). [source: SEP-Th §7.6; SEP-KA §1.3 uses the lawyer example as the origin of the justification condition]
- Dream Theory (201d8–202d7): two kinds of existents, complexes and simples; an account = analysis of a complex into its simple elements; so knowledge is only of complexes and simples are unknowable (only "perceived", 202b6). [source: SEP-Th §8.1]
- ARGUMENT (Socrates' dilemma against the Dream Theory, 202d8–206c2): a complex (syllable SO) is either (a) no more than its elements (letters S, O) or (b) something over and above them. (a) Then knowing SO requires knowing S and O; so if elements are unknowable, so is the complex. (b) Then the syllable is "a single Form that comes to be out of the fitted-together elements" (204a1–2), without parts, hence as simple as an element, hence equally unknowable. Either way the Dream Theory's asymmetry (complexes knowable, elements not) fails. Further point (206a1–c2): learning letters shows knowledge of elements is "primary" (Burnyeat 1990, 192). [source: SEP-Th §8.2]
- Three senses of logos (206c2–210a9): (1) speech/statement — too weak; (2) enumeration of elements — someone who can spell "Theaetetus" letter by letter has true belief plus enumeration but not knowledge (208b; 207c8–d1; 208a9–10); (3) citing the sēmeion/diaphora (distinguishing mark) — but true belief about Theaetetus must already include his distinguishing mark; and if "account" means *knowledge* of the mark, the definition is circular/regressive. "This is where the argument ends, and Socrates leaves to meet his accusers." [source: SEP-Th §8.3]
- ARGUMENT (the circularity/regress against D3, sense 3): Knowledge of O = true belief about O + logos of O's mark. Either logos = true belief about the mark (then nothing is added, since true belief about O already involves the mark) or = knowledge of the mark (then "knowledge" appears in its own definition; and repeating the move gives knowledge of the mark of the mark… a vicious regress). [source: SEP-Th §8.3 and §9]
- DEBATE (interpretation): Unitarians (Aristotle, Proclus, ancient and medieval commentators, Cornford, Cherniss) vs Revisionists (Ryle, Robinson, Owen, McDowell, Bostock). SEP's authors favour a Unitarian reading: Plato elsewhere defends a version of D3 — "true belief with an account of the reason why" (Meno 98a2; also Phaedo, Symposium 202a5–9, Republic 534b3–7, Timaeus 51e5) — and none of the four versions refuted in 201–210 is that version. Whether D3 concerns objectual or propositional knowledge is disputed (Ryle 1990 propositional; White, Crombie, Bostock objectual); one reading treats the Dream Theory as a kind of logical atomism. [source: SEP-Th §§3, 8, 8.1]
- Dream Theory may respond to Antisthenes' doctrine of definition (SEP's suggestion, Brancacci, Mársico). [source: SEP-Th §8.1]
- The *Theaetetus* contains no clear reference to the theory of Forms (SEP: "very likely no clear and explicit reference"). [source: SEP-Th §1]
- Theaetetus 210b6: Theaetetus says he has given birth to more than he had in him; 210d Socrates proposes to meet again next morning. [source: SEP-Th §9]

## 3. Plato: the Meno (true belief vs knowledge; value)

- Meno 97ff: Socrates distinguishes knowledge from true belief; knowledge is true belief "tied down" by an account of the reason why (aitias logismos, 98a). SEP-PMeta: "perhaps the first attempt to offer a justified true belief account of knowledge". SEP-PMeta's own illustration is a modern road example (NYC to Chicago via I-80). [source: SEP-PMeta §10]
- The road to Larissa: Socrates notes a true belief that this is the way to Larissa gets you to Larissa as well as knowledge does; so why is knowledge more valuable? ("the Meno problem" / "primary value problem"). Plato's answer: knowledge is "tied down" to the truth, "like the mythical tethered statues of Daedalus", hence more stable and better suited to guide action (e.g. you are less likely to give up when the road initially seems to go the wrong way). [source: SEP-KV §1]
- Wikipedia places the value question at Meno 97b–d and the distinction at 98a; IEP-Get and WP-Gettier_problem cite Meno 97a–98b as an early version of JTB; WP-Daedalus describes Daedalus' "moving" statues that would wander off unless shackled (citing Meno 97a–98b, tr. Adam Beresford). [source: WP-Meno; WP-Daedalus; IEP-Get references]
- IEP-Meno: the Meno is "probably about 385" (per earlier Plato notes), set 402; at its close Socrates suggests virtue may be correct belief, which "can direct our behavior well, too, though not nearly as reliably as knowledge". English translations listed: G. M. A. Grube (Hackett, 2nd ed. 1980); Alex Long and David Sedley (CUP 2011); Adam Beresford (Penguin 2006). [source: IEP-Meno; dating in docs/philosophy-curriculum/research/ancient--plato.md]
- Gettier's own footnote 1: "Plato seems to be considering some such definition at Theaetetus 201, and perhaps accepting one at Meno 98." [source: WS-3 (search excerpts of transcriptions of Gettier 1963); wording consistent across excerpts but original not read]

## 4. Was JTB ever "the traditional analysis"?

- The tripartite analysis: S knows that P iff (1) P is true, (2) S believes P, (3) S is justified in believing P; abbreviated "JTB". [source: SEP-KA §1]
- SEP-KA: "it's a bit of a convenient fiction to say that this so-called 'traditional' analysis was ever widely accepted; it was primarily articulated in the 20th century by its attackers." Cites Kaplan 1985, Dutant 2015, Antognazza 2015, Le Morvan 2017. [source: SEP-KA §1]
- Plantinga (*Warrant: The Current Debate*, 1992, pp. 6–7): "According to the inherited lore of the epistemological tribe, the JTB [justified true belief] account enjoyed the status of epistemological orthodoxy until 1963, when it was shattered by Edmund Gettier… it isn't easy to find many really explicit statements of a JTB analysis of knowledge prior to Gettier. It is almost as if a distinguished critic created a tradition in the very act of destroying it." Plantinga does accept that C. I. Lewis and A. J. Ayer gave JTB-like accounts. [source: WP-Gettier_problem, quoting Plantinga 1992]
- Julien Dutant, "The Legend of the Justified True Belief Analysis", *Philosophical Perspectives* 29(1) (2015): 95–145. Thesis (per WS-4 summary of the PhilArchive draft): the "Legend" that JTB was the traditional analysis is false; the traditional view was "Classical Infallibilism" — knowledge requires grounds discernible to the subject that entail truth; Gettier's cases depend on justification that does not entail truth. SEP-KA: Dutant argues that factive/truth-entailing justification "better reflects the history of epistemology than contemporary fallibilist approaches". [source: SEP-KA §7 and bibliography; WS-4 for the summary]
- Gettier's named targets (his footnotes 2 and 3): Roderick M. Chisholm, *Perceiving: A Philosophical Study* (Ithaca: Cornell UP, 1957), p. 16; A. J. Ayer, *The Problem of Knowledge* (London: Macmillan, 1956), p. 34 (Ayer's version: being "sure" and having "the right to be sure"). SEP-KA: Gettier listed three slightly different tripartite analyses, in terms of "believes", "accepts" and "is sure that". IEP-Get lists Ayer 1956 ch. 1 as "a well-regarded pre-Gettier JTB analysis". [source: WS-3; SEP-KA §1.2; IEP-Get references]
- The label "justification" for the third condition is itself a "relatively recent (20th century) development". [source: SEP-KA §1.3.1]

## 5. Gettier 1963

- Edmund Lee Gettier III (31 Oct 1927, Baltimore – 23 Mar 2021); BA Johns Hopkins 1949; PhD Cornell 1961 (dissertation on Russell's theories of belief, supervised by Norman Malcolm); taught at Wayne State 1957–67 (colleagues included Alvin Plantinga), UMass Amherst from 1967 (emeritus 2001). [source: WP-Edmund_Gettier]
- Exact title and venue: "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?", *Analysis* 23(6) (1963): 121–123. [source: SEP-KA bibliography; SEP-XP bibliography; IEP-Get]
- Length: WP-Edmund_Gettier "a three-page article" (pp. 121–123); IEP-Get "a single two-and-a-half page article". Say "about three pages". [source: WP-Edmund_Gettier; IEP-Get intro]
- Gettier "made no suggestions" about what should replace JTB. [source: IEP-Get §3]
- Duncan Pritchard reports Gettier wrote the paper partly to secure tenure, never published anything else in epistemology, and declined to attend a 2013 Edinburgh conference on its 50th anniversary (Pritchard, *What Is This Thing Called Knowledge?*, 4th ed. 2018, p. 23). Anecdote, single source. [source: WP-Edmund_Gettier]
- Gettier's two assumptions: one can be justified in believing a false proposition; and justification is preserved by (known) entailment — if S is justified in P and deduces Q from P, S is justified in Q. [source: WP-Gettier_problem; Dutant summary in WS-4 notes Gettier "stresses that his counterexamples assume justification does not entail truth"]
- CASE I (ten coins), as quoted on WP from Gettier: Smith and Jones have applied for a job. Smith has strong evidence for (d) "Jones is the man who will get the job, and Jones has ten coins in his pocket" — the company president assured him Jones would be selected, and he counted the coins in Jones's pocket ten minutes ago. (d) entails (e) "The man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket"; Smith sees this and accepts (e). Unknown to Smith, he himself will get the job, and he himself has ten coins in his pocket. So (e) is true, believed and justified, but Smith does not know it, "for (e) is true in virtue of the number of coins in Smith's pocket, while Smith does not know how many coins are in his pocket". [source: WP-Gettier_problem; IEP-Get §3]
- CASE II (Ford/Barcelona), Gettier's wording as quoted by SEP-KA (Gettier 1963: 123): Smith has strong evidence for "(f) Jones owns a Ford" (Jones has owned a Ford in Smith's memory and has just offered Smith a ride while driving a Ford — WS-5). Smith's friend Brown's whereabouts are unknown to him; Smith picks three place-names "quite at random": (g) Either Jones owns a Ford, or Brown is in Boston; (h) … or Brown is in Barcelona; (i) … or Brown is in Brest-Litovsk. Each is entailed by (f), so Smith is "completely justified" in believing each. "First, Jones does not own a Ford, but is at present driving a rented car. And secondly, by the sheerest coincidence, and entirely unknown to Smith, the place mentioned in proposition (h) happens really to be the place where Brown is." Smith does not know (h). [source: SEP-KA §3; WS-5]
- The disjunctions are inclusive. [source: IEP-Get §4]
- "Gettier presented two cases in which a true belief is inferred from a justified false belief." [source: SEP-KA §3]
- STEP-BY-STEP (Case I): P1 If JTB is correct, any justified true belief is knowledge. P2 Smith's belief (e) is true. P3 Smith believes (e). P4 Smith is justified in believing (e) (strong evidence for (d) + valid inference). P5 Smith does not know (e). C JTB is false: truth, belief and justification are not jointly sufficient (they may still each be necessary). IEP: "JTB is false if there is even one actual or possible Gettier situation." [source: reconstruction from IEP-Get §§3, 6 and WP-Gettier_problem]
- "It is nearly universally accepted amongst contemporary epistemologists that there are counterexamples to the analysis of knowledge as justified true belief", while most agree each element is necessary. [source: SEP-KA §3]
- Structure of Gettier cases (IEP-Get §5): (1) fallibility — the justification leaves the falsity of the belief possible; (2) luck — the belief is true only by an abnormal or odd circumstance. Zagzebski's "double luck" recipe: bad luck that would normally make the justified belief false, cancelled by good luck that makes it true. [source: IEP-Get §5; SEP-VE §5]
- Other classic Gettier-style cases: Chisholm's sheep in the field (1966/1977/1989: a dog disguised as a sheep; a real sheep hidden behind a hill); Skyrms's pyromaniac (1967: a match that lights only because of a rare burst of "Q-radiation"); Goldman's fake barns (1976); SEP-KA's robot dog. [source: IEP-Get §4; SEP-KA §4]

## 6. Earlier Gettier-style cases (before 1963)

- Dharmottara (8th-century Indian Buddhist; WP gives 740–800 from a Heidelberg project page; SEP-KA "c. 770 CE"), commentator on Dharmakīrti; only his *Nyāyabinduṭīkā* survives in Sanskrit, others in Tibetan. [source: WP-Dharmottara; SEP-KA §3]
- Dharmottara's mirage case, as SEP-KA quotes it from Dreyfus 1997: 292 (Dreyfus's English rendering/paraphrase, not a direct translation of a passage): "Imagine that we are seeking water on a hot day. We suddenly see water, or so we think. In fact, we are not seeing water but a mirage, but when we reach the spot, we are lucky and find water right there under a rock. Can we say that we had genuine knowledge of water? The answer seems to be negative, for we were just lucky." [source: SEP-KA §3]
- Dharmottara's fire/insects case (Jennifer Nagel's retelling, *Knowledge: A Very Short Introduction*, 2014, p. 58): a fire has just been lit to roast meat; no smoke yet, but the smell attracts a cloud of insects; a distant observer mistakes the swarm for smoke and says there is a fire there. WP says both cases are in Dharmottara's commentary (c. 770) on Dharmakīrti's *Pramāṇaviniścaya* ("Ascertainment of Knowledge"). [source: WP-Gettier_problem citing Nagel 2014, Dreyfus 1997, Stoltz 2007]
- Jonathan Stoltz, "Gettier and Factivity in Indo-Tibetan Epistemology", *Philosophical Quarterly* 57(228) (2007): 394–415. [source: WP-Gettier_problem]
- Śrīharṣa (12th century CE, northern India; poet-philosopher; often read as Advaita Vedāntin, disputed) in the *Khaṇḍanakhaṇḍakhādya* ("Sweets of Refutation") attacks Udayana's definition "Knowledge is non-mnemonic awareness of the truth (tattvānubhūti)" because it overextends to "accidentally true (kākatālīya-samvāda) awareness" (KKh 207–208). His cases: Shells (a gambler's hunch that a bookie holds five shells); Mist (you see mist, take it for smoke, infer fire, and there is fire on the hill; KKh 211); Horns (believing falsely that only cows have horns, you infer "that animal is a cow", and it is; KKh 213). SEP-Sri: Mist and Horns "clearly conform to the structure of standard Gettier cases"; similar cases in Dharmottara, Kumārila and Śrīdhara (Matilal 1986 ch. 4; Ganeri 2007 ch. 5; Stoltz 2007). Śrīharṣa uses them to argue there may be no satisfactory definition of knowledge, anticipating problems for sensitivity, safety and relevant alternatives, and denies treating knowledge as primitive (as Williamson does) escapes the problem. [source: SEP-Sri §§1, 2.1; WP-Shriharsha]
- SEP-Sri argues these Sanskrit cases cast doubt on early x-phi claims that Gettier intuitions are cultural artefacts. [source: SEP-Sri §2.1]
- Peter of Mantua (14th-century Italian), *De scire et dubitare*: you see Plato running nearby, mistake him for Socrates and believe Socrates is running; Socrates is in fact running in Rome (Boh 1985: 95). [source: SEP-KA §3]
- Russell's stopped clock: "If you look at a clock which you believe to be going, but which in fact has stopped, and you happen to look at it at a moment when it is right, you will acquire a true belief as to the time of day, but you cannot be correctly said to have knowledge." Book: *Human Knowledge: Its Scope and Limits* (London: Allen & Unwin, 1948). SEP-KA's note: Russell's focus "is on the nature of belief, not the analysis of knowledge"; he does not highlight justification. Page: IEP-Saf cites "Russell (1948: 170)"; SEP-KA cites "Russell 1923: 113" (date is a misprint; see Debates/NOT VERIFIED). [source: SEP-KA §3 and note 10; SEP-Rus (1948); IEP-Saf]
- Gaṅgeśa (14th century; first half per WP; older estimates late 13th), founder of Navya-Nyāya, *Tattvacintāmaṇi* ("Jewel"): includes a "Gettier case" of a parrot uttering a sentence a hearer would take as true — the hearer does not gain testimonial knowledge; knowing the sounds come from a parrot is a block. Nilanjan Das (2021) treats Gaṅgeśa on epistemic luck ("epistemic localism"). WP: Gaṅgeśa "advanced a detailed causal theory of knowledge" (citing Nagel 2014). [source: SEP-Gan §§1, 3 and testimony section; SEP-IndEp §9.1; SEP-KA §8; WP-Gaṅgeśa; WP-Gettier_problem]
- Meinong: WP-Gettier_problem claims Meinong (and Russell) "acknowledged" the problem, citing Turri 2019; no text or case found. See NOT VERIFIED.

## 7. Responses (each with case/objection)

### 7.1 No false lemmas
- Fourth condition: S's belief that P is not inferred from any falsehood (Clark 1963; Armstrong 1973). Michael Clark, "Knowledge and Grounds: A Comment on Mr. Gettier's Paper", *Analysis* 24(2) (1963): 46–48. [source: SEP-KA §4 and note 11, bibliography]
- Objection 1 (Richard Feldman, "An Alleged Defect in Gettier Counterexamples", *Australasian Journal of Philosophy* 52 (1974): 68–9): modify Case I so Smith believes not the falsehood "Jones will get the job" but the truth "The company president told me that Jones will get the job"; the justification is as good, no false evidence is used, yet Smith still seems not to know. [source: IEP-Get §9 and references]
- Objection 2: non-inferential cases — robot dog; fake barns (§7.4). [source: SEP-KA §4]
- Refinement: Lehrer (1965, "Knowledge, Truth and Evidence", *Analysis* 25: 168–75): no significant, ineliminable ("core") falsehoods in one's evidence — charged with vagueness. [source: IEP-Get §9]

### 7.2 Defeasibility
- Lehrer and Paxson, "Knowledge: Undefeated Justified True Belief", *Journal of Philosophy* 66(8) (1969): 225–37: knowledge is undefeated justified true belief — no further truth which, added to the subject's evidence, would defeat the justification. In Case I the truth "Jones will not get the job" would defeat Smith's justification. [source: WP-Gettier_problem; IEP-Get §10]
- IEP's definition: a truth t defeats justification j for p if adding t to j yields j* too weak to make the true belief knowledge. Problem: vagueness (how much weakening?). [source: IEP-Get §10]
- Misleading defeaters, the Tom Grabit case (Lehrer & Paxson 1969): you see Tom Grabit take a book from the library; unknown to you, Mrs Grabit (his mother) reports that Tom was not in the library; Lehrer & Paxson hold that this misleading report should not defeat your knowledge, and refine "defeat" accordingly. Peter Klein is among other defeasibility theorists (with Sosa, Hilpinen, Swain). [source: WS-7 (Hauptli lecture supplement, FIU; Canadian J. Phil. record) — secondary; the rest of the story (an alleged twin) is NOT VERIFIED]

### 7.3 Causal theory
- Goldman, "A Causal Theory of Knowing", *Journal of Philosophy* 64(12) (1967): 357–372: replace justification with a requirement that the fact that P be appropriately causally connected with the belief. In Case I, the facts making (e) true (Smith gets the job; coins in Smith's pocket) play no part in causing the belief. [source: SEP-KA §6.2; IEP-Get §11]
- Objections: (1) knowledge of non-causal facts (2 + 2 = 4, general facts); (2) "deviant causal chains" — vague; (3) fake barns: the real barn does cause Henry's belief. [source: IEP-Get §11; SEP-KA §6.2]

### 7.4 Fake barns
- "Barn County"/"fake barns": "first given by Carl Ginet, and published by Alvin Goldman (1976)" — Goldman, "Discrimination and Perceptual Knowledge", *Journal of Philosophy* 73(20): 771–791 (case at 772–3). Henry, driving through a county of barn façades that look like barns from the road, happens to look at the one real barn; justified true belief, no inference from a falsehood, yet apparently not knowledge. Some epistemologists do not count it as a genuine Gettier case. [source: SEP-KA §4; SEP-VE §5; IEP-Get §4]
- Kripke (2011) on sensitivity and barns: if façades are all green and real barns red, Henry's belief "there is a red barn" is sensitive though "there is a barn" is not. [source: SEP-KA §5.1]

### 7.5 Reliabilism
- Precursors: Ramsey (1931: a belief is knowledge if true, certain and obtained by a reliable process); Unger (1968: "not at all accidental"); Armstrong (1973: thermometer analogy). [source: SEP-Rel §1.1]
- Goldman, "What Is Justified Belief?" (1979, in G. S. Pappas (ed.), *Justification and Knowledge*, Reidel, pp. 1–25): a belief is justified iff produced by a reliable process — one with a high truth-ratio (not necessarily 1.0, "presumably, quite a lot greater" than .5). Michael Williams called it "the Reliabilist Revolution". Extended to knowledge in Goldman 1986, *Epistemology and Cognition*. [source: SEP-Rel §1; SEP-KA §6.1]
- "Simple K-Reliabilism": S knows P iff P is true, S believes P, and the belief was produced by a reliable process. Fake barns refute it (perception is reliable). [source: SEP-KA §6.1–6.2]
- Objections: BonJour's (1980) clairvoyant Norman (reliable but no reasons; intuitively unjustified); the new evil demon problem (Cohen 1984 etc.: demon-world victims with our experiences seem justified though unreliable); the generality problem (anticipated by Goldman 1979; pressed by Feldman 1985 and Conee & Feldman 1998): a token process (Smith seeing a maple tree on a sunny afternoon) belongs to many types (vision; visual experience of a maple on a sunny afternoon; perception) with different reliabilities; which type counts? [source: SEP-Rel §§2.1–2.3]
- Dretske (1985) suggests reliability better allows knowledge in non-human animals. [source: SEP-KA §6.1]

### 7.6 Truth-tracking / sensitivity and safety
- Nozick, *Philosophical Explanations* (Harvard UP, 1981): S knows P iff (1) P true, (2) S believes P, (3) if P were true S would believe P, (4) if P were not true S would not believe P. Developed from Dretske's "conclusive reasons" (1971). [source: WP-Gettier_problem; SEP-KA §5.1]
- Sensitivity: if it were false that P, S would not believe P. Handles Dharmottara's mirage (with no water you'd still believe). Objections: not all Gettier cases (barns, depending on details; Kripke 2011); DeRose (1995) "abominable conjunction": "George knows that he has hands, but he doesn't know that he's not the handless victim of a Cartesian demon". "Most epistemologists today reject sensitivity requirements." [source: SEP-KA §5.1]
- Safety (Sosa 1999, "How to Defeat Opposition to Moore", *Philosophical Perspectives* 13: 141–153): if S were to believe P, it would not be false that P; better: in all nearby worlds where S believes P, P is not false. Sosa presents it as the counterfactual contrapositive of sensitivity, which (he says) is not equivalent. [source: SEP-KA §5.2]
- IEP-Saf: "Timothy Williamson, Duncan Pritchard, and Ernest Sosa are the foremost defenders of safety": S knows P only if S "could not easily have falsely believed P". Williamson (2000: 124): "One avoids false belief reliably in α if and only if one avoids false belief in every case similar enough to α." Pritchard (2005) defines luck modally. Williamson (2009: 305): "One may have to decide whether safety obtains by first deciding whether knowledge obtains, rather than vice versa." So for Williamson safety is not part of an analysis. [source: IEP-Saf; SEP-KA §5.2]
- Objections to safety: Comesaña (2005) Halloween-party case; vagueness of "nearby"; lottery cases. [source: SEP-KA §5.2; IEP-Saf]
- Relevant alternatives (Stine 1976, Goldman 1976, Dretske 1981; Lewis 1996): one must rule out relevant alternatives in which P is false. [source: SEP-KA §5.3]

### 7.7 Zagzebski's inescapability argument (good candidate for the chapter's main reconstructed argument)
- Linda Zagzebski, "The Inescapability of Gettier Problems", *The Philosophical Quarterly* 44(174) (1994): 65–73. [source: SEP-KA bibliography]
- ARGUMENT: Any analysis JTB + X, where X is logically independent of truth (so that a belief can meet J and X and still be false), faces Gettier cases. Recipe: (1) take a case where the subject's belief meets J and X but is false; (2) modify it so the belief is true merely by luck. The result is still intuitively not knowledge. So any non-redundant addition leaves the problem. Illustration against Plantinga's proper-function condition (1996): Mary, whose eyesight works properly in a suitable environment, sees her husband's look-alike brother in the living room and believes "My husband is in the living room" (false); now add that her husband is, by luck, also in the room, out of sight (true, still not knowledge). [source: SEP-KA §7; SEP-VE §5]
- Replies: (a) knowledge is unanalysable (Williamson; §7.9); (b) make justification entail truth (Sturgeon 1993; Merricks 1995; Dutant 2015; Sutton 2007, Littlejohn 2012) — not mainstream; (c) give "redundant" analyses whose extra condition entails truth: anti-luck condition (Unger 1968; Zagzebski 1994: 72 herself outlines it), virtue theories. Problem for anti-luck: which kind of luck? (raffle-won encyclopedia luck is compatible with knowledge). Pritchard (2016) prefers "risk". [source: SEP-KA §§7–8]

### 7.8 Virtue epistemology
- Contemporary virtue epistemology began with Ernest Sosa's work in the early 1980s (papers in Sosa 1991); other early contributors Lorraine Code (1987), James Montmarquet (1993), Jonathan Kvanvig (1992), Linda Zagzebski (1996). Virtue reliabilists (Goldman, Greco, Sosa: faculties like perception, memory) vs virtue responsibilists (Battaly, Code, Hookway, Montmarquet, Zagzebski: traits like open-mindedness, conscientiousness); the taxonomy is criticised (Fleisher 2017; Battaly 2015). [source: SEP-VE §§2–3]
- Common core: "to know is to believe the truth because of your intellectual virtue" (Sosa 1991: 277; Zagzebski 1996: 271–2; Riggs 2002; Lehrer 2000; Greco 2003: 111; Turri 2011). Riggs: knowledge is "an achievement for which the knower deserves credit"; Greco: knowers deserve credit because they believe the truth because of their virtue. [source: SEP-VE §5]
- Sosa's AAA (archer): a shot is accurate (hits), adroit (manifests skill), apt (accurate because adroit). Two gusts of wind: skilful shot diverted by one gust, put back on target by another — accurate and adroit but not apt. Applied to belief: knowledge = apt belief, true because competent (Sosa 2007, *A Virtue Epistemology: Apt Belief and Reflective Knowledge*, Vol. I, ch. 2; 22–3). Case II: Smith's accuracy is due to luck (Brown in Barcelona), not his skill. Sosa (2007: 29): a competence "is a disposition, one with a basis resident in the competent agent, one that would in appropriately normal conditions ensure (or make highly likely) the success of any relevant performance issued by it." [source: SEP-KA §10.1–10.2; SEP-VE §5]
- Objection (Pritchard 2009, echoing Lackey 2007): fake barns — Henry's true belief manifests his perceptual skill, so it's apt; yet he doesn't know. Responses: Henry lacks the competence in that environment; or his competence isn't manifested; Sosa himself bites the bullet — Henry has (animal) apt knowledge but lacks "reflective knowledge" (Sosa 2007: 31–32). Pritchard raises the same objection to Greco. [source: SEP-KA §10.2; SEP-VE §5]
- John Greco, *Achieving Knowledge: A Virtue-Theoretic Account of Epistemic Normativity* (CUP 2010). [source: SEP-VE bibliography]

### 7.9 Knowledge first
- Timothy Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits* (OUP 2000): the analysis project was a mistake; knowledge is among the most fundamental mental states; "knowledge first". He accepts: knowledge is "the most general factive mental state"; S knows P iff S's total evidence includes P (E = K: "one's total evidence is simply one's total knowledge", 2000: 9). He endorses a safety requirement (2000: 126) but not as part of an analysis. Traditional necessary conditions (truth, belief, justification) are compatible with knowledge-first. [source: SEP-KA §11; IEP-Saf; IEP-KF]
- "There is no very straightforward argument for this conclusion; its case consists largely in the attempted demonstration of the theoretical success of the knowledge first stance." [source: SEP-KA §11]
- Vaidya (2022) reads Gaṅgeśa's epistemology as a version of knowledge-first (knowledge not a form of belief; seeing that p is a direct way of knowing that p), though it is unclear Gaṅgeśa makes knowledge first in the order of explanation. [source: SEP-IndEp §9.1]

### 7.10 Other options
- Methodological alternatives: Edward Craig, *Knowledge and the State of Nature* (1990) — the concept's point is to flag reliable informants; Kornblith (2002) — knowledge as a natural kind. [source: SEP-KA §9]
- Infallibilism (IEP-Get §7) — rejected by most as leading to scepticism. Hetherington's "knowing luckily" proposal (1998, 2001) — Gettiered beliefs are knowledge, but poor-quality; a minority view. "Lightweight knowledge" (mere true belief) senses (Hawthorne 2002; Goldman & Olsson 2009). [source: IEP-Get §§7, 13; SEP-KA §2]
- "No analysis has been widely accepted"; IEP: "No analysis has received general assent". [source: SEP-KA intro; IEP-Get §14]
- Shope, *The Analysis of Knowing: A Decade of Research* (Princeton 1983) chronicles the post-Gettier iterations. [source: SEP-KA §7]

## 8. Internalism vs externalism

- Internalists: justification depends wholly on states internal to the subject — "access internalism" (directly or introspectively available) vs "state internalism" (intrinsic states). Conee & Feldman's evidentialism (believing P is justified iff it best fits S's evidence). Externalists: external factors (e.g. reliability of process) can matter. [source: SEP-KA §1.3.2]
- Two debates: about knowledge (does it require an internal condition? internalist BonJour 1980; externalists Audi 1995, Kornblith 2008, Greco 2010, Williamson 2000) and about justification (does it supervene on the internal?). [source: SEP-IE intro]
- Safety and sensitivity are externalist conditions in both senses. [source: SEP-KA §5.2]
- Propositional vs doxastic justification (Ingrid and the black cat example). [source: SEP-KA §1.3.3]
- Gettier's paper is said to have helped motivate the shift to externalist theories (McGrew & McGrew 2007). [source: WP-Gettier_problem]

## 9. The value problem

- Primary value problem (Meno): why is knowledge more valuable than mere true belief? Secondary: than any proper subset of its parts (e.g. justified true belief)? Tertiary (Pritchard 2009: 14): why is it *qualitatively* better than anything falling short? (Pritchard 2007 distinguishes primary/secondary.) [source: SEP-KV §1]
- ARGUMENT (swamping problem; Zagzebski 2003a and elsewhere; named "swamping" by Kvanvig 2003): (1) The value of a reliable process is wholly instrumental: it tends to produce true beliefs. (2) A good cup of coffee from a reliable machine is no better than an equally good cup from an unreliable one. (3) Likewise, once a belief is true, its having been reliably produced adds no further value — the value of truth "swamps" it. (4) So process reliabilism cannot explain why knowledge beats true belief. OBJECTION/REPLY: reliable true belief may have extra practical instrumental value (Plato's own stability answer appeals to this); Goldman & Olsson (2009) "conditional-probability" solution (a reliably formed true belief makes future true beliefs more likely; empirical backing Olsson et al. 2023); agent reliabilism (Greco 1999, 2000) may escape. Also the problem may not be specific to reliabilism (any purely instrumental, truth-based account). [source: SEP-KV §2]
- Some deny the problems are genuine: Kaplan (1985) — Gettier-proofing adds no value; Sartwell (1991) — knowledge just is true belief. [source: SEP-KV §1]
- Jonathan Kvanvig, *The Value of Knowledge and the Pursuit of Understanding* (CUP 2003). [source: SEP-KV bibliography]

## 10. Experimental philosophy and Gettier intuitions

- Weinberg, Nichols and Stich, "Normativity and Epistemic Intuitions", *Philosophical Topics* 29(1–2) (2001): 429–460 — often treated as the start of x-phi and of its "negative program". They reported that respondents of East Asian or Indian-subcontinent descent were more willing than European Americans to say a Gettier subject knows; a similar disparity with socio-economic status. [source: IEP-Get §12; SEP-XP §§3, 4; WP-Experimental_philosophy]
- Later studies "consistently failed to replicate" the cross-cultural Gettier result: Seyedsayamdost, "On Normativity and Epistemic Intuitions: Failure of Replication", *Episteme* 12(1) (2015 vol.; online 2014): 95–116; Kim and Yuan, "No cross-cultural differences in the Gettier car case intuition", *Episteme* 12(3) (2015): 355–361; Nagel 2012. [source: WP-Gettier_problem]
- Machery, Stich, Rose, Chatterjee, Karasawa, Struchiner, Sirker, Usui and Hashimoto, "Gettier Across Cultures", *Noûs* 51(3): 645–664 (online 2015; issue 2017; DOI 10.1111/nous.12110): 521 participants in the USA, India, Japan and Brazil; two Gettier cases plus clear-knowledge and false-belief controls; Gettier intuitions found in all four, suggesting a possible "core folk epistemology". Note Stich is a co-author of both the 2001 and the 2017 papers. [source: WP-Gettier_problem; WS-8]
- IEP-Get (older text) says "other epistemologists have not sought to replicate those surveys" — outdated. [source: IEP-Get §12]
- SEP-XP: Knobe (2019, 2021) questions how widespread intuitional variation is; Stich and Machery (2023) reply. The "restrictionist challenge" relies on heterogeneity and instability of judgments. [source: SEP-XP §4.3]
- Myers-Schutz [sic in SEP; usually spelled Myers-Schulz] & Schwitzgebel (2013) find ordinary speakers share Radford's intuition that the unconfident quiz-taker (Albert, "Elizabeth died in 1603") knows without believing. [source: SEP-KA §1.2]

## 11. Indian epistemology (brief; cross-ref *Classical Indian Schools* and *Mahāyāna Philosophy*)

- Theory of knowledge = pramāṇa-śāstra, spanning "almost twenty centuries"; dominated by theories about "pedigree" — knowledge-generating processes (pramāṇa, "knowledge sources"), principally perception, inference and testimony. [source: SEP-IndEp intro]
- Translation point: jñāna is often rendered "knowledge", but Sanskrit allows "false jñāna" — better "cognition". Pramā ("knowledge") matches "practically perfectly" the analytic usage; pramāṇa-words are factive: "no knowledge source ever generates a false belief. Only pseudo-sources do" (e.g. pratyakṣa-ābhāsa, "pseudo-perception"). "A knowledge source is then not merely a reliable doxastic practice." "Knowledge cannot arise by accident. A lucky guess… would not have the right pedigree." [source: SEP-IndEp §1.1]
- Knowledge is occurrent cognition "generated in the right fashion" (episodes), with memory/dispositions (saṃskāra) deriving their status from it. [source: SEP-IndEp §1]
- Nyāya/Gaṅgeśa: "a reliabilist theory of knowledge and justification, indeed a super-reliabilism, an infallibilism for externalists" (sources conceived factively). Pramā = knowledge, "entailing truth and right production"; apramā = non-knowledge. Awareness (anubhava, "presentation of fresh news") is presumptively knowledge. [source: SEP-Gan §§1, 3.1]
- Prāmāṇya debate (do not repeat Kumārila's regress argument, which *Classical Indian Schools* already reconstructs): Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta — svataḥ prāmāṇya, intrinsic validity/self-certification (Nāgārjuna's regress worry is one motive); Nyāya — parataḥ prāmāṇya, extrinsic certification: it "denies that Kp entails KKp"; certification via apperception (anuvyavasāya), success in action, recognising "merits" (guṇa) and "flaws" (doṣa). Gaṅgeśa argues against intrinsic validity from the phenomenology of doubt: one can doubt a veridical perception. Vātsyāyana (4th c.) rebuts the regress charge under Nyāya-sūtra 2.1.20 (Phillips's translation): everyday action shows we need not certify the certifiers. [source: SEP-IndEp §3; SEP-Gan §3]
- Buddhist: Dharmakīrti defines a source of knowledge (Pramāṇavārttika II.1) as a "reliable (avisaṃvādin) cognition (jñāna)" — right and reliable as a basis for action; Dharmottara unpacks reliability as leading one to obtain (prāpaka) the desired object. A second criterion (PV II.5): it "illuminates what has not been previously known" (novelty; excludes memory). DEBATE: was Dharmakīrti a pragmatist about truth (Powers, Cabezón, Katsura) or a weak correspondence theorist with a pragmatic theory of justification (Tillemans 1999)? Confirmation is by causal efficacy (arthakriyāsthiti), e.g. the seen vase holds water; some cognitions are intrinsically sources (svataḥ). "Non-deceptive" is a common alternative rendering of avisaṃvādin (Tillemans uses "reliable"). [source: SEP-Dk §2.1 and notes 29–31]
- DEBATE (does Indian epistemology concern JTB?): Karl Potter, "Does Indian Epistemology Concern Justified True Belief?", *Journal of Indian Philosophy* 12(4) (1984): 307–328 — no: pramā/jñāna are occurrent episodes, not dispositional belief, and truth is often "workability". J. L. Shaw (2000) — Nyāya-inspired reply to Gettier: Gettier's cases aren't JTB because justification and truth attach to different parts (Smith's justification attaches to "Jones owns a Ford", truth to "Brown is in Barcelona"). Das (2021) — Gaṅgeśa's "epistemic localism". Vaidya (2022) — Gaṅgeśa as knowledge-first. [source: SEP-IndEp §9.1]
- SEP-KA notes anti-luck subtleties "were also a focus in antiquity, especially in the Indian Nyāya tradition" (citing Das 2021b on Gaṅgeśa). [source: SEP-KA §8]

## 12. Chinese (brief)

- Later Mohist epistemology (the *Mohist Canons*, Dialectics): focus "is not on the justification or truth of beliefs… but on knowing how to distinguish which things… are shì 是 (this)… and fēi 非 (not-this)"; knowing (zhī 知) is "by means of 'the knowing', passing by something and being able to describe it" — a practical ability (néng 能). [source: SEP-MC §5, 5.1]
- Canon A80: three sources of knowledge — hearsay/testimony (wén 聞, "receiving it as passed along"), explanation/inference (shuō 說; example "squares do not rotate"), personal observation (qīn 親, "observing it there oneself"); four objects — names (míng 名), stuff/objects (shí 實), matching (hé 合), acting (wéi 為). [source: SEP-MC §5.2]
- Zhuangzi 2 (Gaptooth/Wang Ni passage, "Zhuangzi 2:11", Chad Hansen's translation, "Kingsley" = Wang Ni): "How would I know that what I call 'knowing' is not 'not-knowing'? And what I call 'not-knowing', 'knowing'." Then: people who sleep in the damp get ill; would eels? which creature "knows the correct place to live"? Hansen: the scepticism concerns norms of word use, "unlike Western skepticism of beliefs". [source: SEP-Zh §4.7]
- Zhuangzi 17 (the happy fish, Hao River bridge, "Zhuangzi 17:13", Hansen's translation): Hui Shi: "You are not a fish; from whence do you know the leisure of fish?" Zhuangzi: "You are not me, from whence do you know my not knowing fish at leisure?"… "I knew it here above the Hao". A. C. Graham noted the locative question word ān 安 "whence", not hé 何 "how". [source: SEP-Zh §4.7]

## 13. Islamic (brief; Wikipedia-only, treat cautiously)

- 'Ilm (علم) = knowledge; the root ʿ-l-m and derivatives occur around 750 times in the Qur'an; in Islamic contexts often religious knowledge; plural 'ulūm = sciences; opposite jahl (ignorance). Franz Rosenthal, *Knowledge Triumphant: The Concept of Knowledge in Medieval Islam* (1970; Brill reprint 2007). [source: WP-Ilm_(Arabic)]
- Classical kalām definition reported by Louay Safi: knowledge is "the knowledge of the thing (shay') as it exists in itself" (Muʿtazilite origin); al-Bāqillānī (Ashʿarī) replaced "thing" with "knowable" (maʿlūm). Al-Farrāʾ's scale: ʿilm (certain) – ẓann (probable) – shakk (doubt, two interpretations equally plausible) – jahl. [source: WP-Ilm_(Arabic), citing Safi 1997: 39]

## Debates
- Was JTB ever the traditional analysis? Legend view (Gettier-era textbooks; WP-Edmund_Gettier says most philosophers at the time accepted it, notably C. I. Lewis and Chisholm) vs Plantinga 1992, Kaplan 1985, Dutant 2015, Antognazza 2015, Le Morvan 2017, SEP-KA ("convenient fiction"). Dutant: the tradition was infallibilist. [source: SEP-KA; WP-Gettier_problem; WP-Edmund_Gettier; WS-4]
- Was Plato's D3 rejected or endorsed? SEP-Th (Unitarian): Plato defends true belief + account of the reason why elsewhere; *Theaetetus* refutes other versions. Revisionists: Plato abandoned it. Two Worlds readers (Republic V): knowledge can't be any kind of justified true belief, since knowledge and belief have different objects; Fine's veridical reading allows a JTB-like interpretation. [source: SEP-Th §§3, 8; SEP-PMeta §12]
- Can knowledge be analysed at all? JTB+X theorists vs Zagzebski's inescapability argument, Williamson's knowledge-first, Śrīharṣa's scepticism about definitions. [source: SEP-KA §§7, 11; SEP-Sri]
- Sensitivity vs safety vs virtue vs anti-luck: "Most epistemologists today reject sensitivity requirements"; safety "remains a popular one"; Sosa bites the bullet on fake barns, Pritchard objects. [source: SEP-KA §§5, 10; SEP-VE §5]
- Internalism vs externalism. [source: SEP-IE; SEP-KA §1.3.2]
- Are Gettier intuitions universal? WNS 2001 vs failed replications and Machery et al. 2017; Śrīharṣa/Dharmottara cases as historical evidence for universality (SEP-Sri). Knobe vs Stich & Machery on how widespread variation is. [source: §10 sources]
- Intellectualism (Stanley & Williamson) vs anti-intellectualism (Ryle; Noë) on know-how. [source: SEP-KH]
- Does Indian epistemology concern JTB? Potter 1984 (no) vs Shaw, Das, Vaidya (engagement); Dharmakīrti pragmatist? (Powers/Katsura vs Tillemans). [source: SEP-IndEp §9.1; SEP-Dk §2.1]
- Is Nyāya reliabilist? *Classical Indian Schools* quotes Dasti: "a sophisticated precursor to contemporary reliabilism"; Phillips (SEP-Gan) "super-reliabilism, an infallibilism for externalists", while SEP-IndEp says a knowledge source is "not merely a reliable doxastic practice" because it is factive. Compatible but worth stating precisely. [source: SEP-Gan; SEP-IndEp §1.1]
- Value problem real? Kaplan, Sartwell deny; Goldman & Olsson reply to swamping. [source: SEP-KV]

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use
- Exact English wording of Meno 97d–98a in any named translation (e.g. Grube's "they are not willing to remain long… until one ties them down by (giving) an account of the reason why"): Perseus, Gutenberg, Wikisource and translation sites were blocked; two searches did not return the text. Use paraphrase: true beliefs, like Daedalus' statues, "run away" unless "tied down" by an account of the reason why (aitias logismos, 98a) [SEP-PMeta; SEP-KV].
- Verbatim translation of the Theaetetus jury passage (201a–c) or 210a–b: not read; paraphrase from SEP-Th.
- Meinong as a Gettier precursor: only a bare Wikipedia claim (citing Turri 2019); no case, text or date found. Do not use.
- Russell's stopped clock page number and the date "1923": SEP-KA cites "Russell 1923: 113" and lists *Human Knowledge* as 1923 — wrong; the book is 1948 (SEP-Rus, IEP-Saf). IEP gives p. 170; WebSearch of the 1948 table of contents puts p. 170 in ch. XI "Fact, Belief, Truth, and Knowledge" (consistent) but the page was not seen. Cite the book, not the page.
- Tom Grabit details (mother's testimony that Tom has a twin John): confirmed only in outline from secondary search results; the exact story in Lehrer & Paxson was not read. Mention only in outline, attributed.
- Gettier's "Case II" evidence wording (Jones "has at all times in the past within Smith's memory owned a car, and always a Ford") — only a search summary confirms "just offered Smith a ride while driving a Ford"; avoid quoting the evidence sentence.
- WP-Gettier_problem's statement that Nozick's third condition "has come to be known as epistemological safety": contradicts SEP-KA, which identifies safety with Sosa's 1999 condition (in nearby worlds where S believes P, P is true); Nozick's third condition is usually called "adherence" (not verified here). Do not equate them.
- WP-Gettier_problem's paraphrase of Case II mentioning a "hidden interlocutor": not in Gettier as quoted by SEP; ignore.
- SEP-IndEp calls Śrīharṣa "the eleventh-century Advaitin"; SEP-Sri, SEP-KA and WP say 12th century. Use 12th century.
- Gettier "for tenure" story: single source (Pritchard via WP); if used, attribute to Pritchard.
- Al-Farrāʾ and kalām definitions of ʿilm: Wikipedia only (citing Safi 1997); no SEP/IEP check. Use cautiously or omit.
- The claim that Dharmottara's two cases come from his commentary on the *Pramāṇaviniścaya* specifically: WP (via Nagel/Stoltz); SEP-KA gives only Dreyfus 1997. Acceptable with "according to Nagel".

No contradiction.
