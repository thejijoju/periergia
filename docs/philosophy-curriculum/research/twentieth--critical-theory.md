## The Institute for Social Research: founding, exile, return
- The Institute for Social Research (Institut für Sozialforschung) was founded in 1923 at Frankfurt am Main [source: SEP-CT §1.1; SEP-AD §1 "founded as a free-standing center for Marxist scholarship in 1923"]. WP-FS gives the date 3 February 1923. SEP-HO says it "formally opened in 1924 under the direction of ... Carl Grünberg"; WP Carl_Grünberg: Grünberg became first director in 1924. Safe wording: founded 1923, opened 1924. (SEP-MA's "formed in 1922" is an outlier — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Patron: Felix Weil (1898–1975), born in Buenos Aires, son of the wealthy grain merchant Hermann Weil; the Institute began as a Marxist study group funded with his inheritance/his father's endowment [source: SEP-HO §1; WP Felix_Weil; WP-IfS]. It was "the first Marxist research center at a German university" [source: WP-FS].
- Precursor: Weil's "First Marxist Work Week" (Erste Marxistische Arbeitswoche), attended by Lukács, Karl Korsch, Pollock, Wittfogel and others; SEP-LU dates it 1923 and says it "laid the foundation" for the Institute; WP Felix_Weil says 1923 at Ilmenau; WP-FS says 1922 [sources: SEP-LU §2; WP Felix_Weil; WP-FS]. Give "1922 or 1923" or omit the year.
- Carl Grünberg (1861–1940), Austrian Marxist economic historian, "father of Austromarxism", first director; fell ill soon after taking the post [source: WP Carl_Grünberg; SEP-HO §1].
- Max Horkheimer (b. 14 Feb 1895, Stuttgart – d. 7 Jul 1973) was appointed Professor of Social Philosophy at Frankfurt in July 1930 and director of the Institute in October 1930; inaugural lecture 24 January 1931, "The Present Situation of Social Philosophy and the Tasks of an Institute for Social Research", which set out an interdisciplinary programme combining philosophy with psychology, political economy and cultural analysis [source: SEP-HO §1; SEP-CT §1.1].
- He edited the Institute's journal, the *Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung* [source: WP-IfS].
- Exile: after Hitler became Chancellor in 1933 the Institute in Frankfurt was closed and its building seized; Horkheimer lost his chair and moved to Geneva, where a branch office had been set up; the endowment had been moved to Holland [source: SEP-HO §1; SEP-MA §1]. In July 1934 Horkheimer accepted Columbia University's offer to house the Institute in New York [source: SEP-HO §1; SEP-CT §1.1 "re-established it at Columbia University in 1934"]. (WP-FS says New York "in 1935" — use 1934.) The journal became *Studies in Philosophy and Social Science* [source: WP-IfS].
- Horkheimer moved to the Los Angeles area in 1941 (WP-IfS: 1940); Horkheimer, Adorno and Pollock moved the Institute's centre to Los Angeles in 1941 [source: SEP-HO §1; SEP-CT §1.1].
- Return: Horkheimer first revisited Germany in April 1948; restored to his Frankfurt professorship in July 1949; the Institute returned "the following year" and was formally reopened in 1951 [source: SEP-HO §1; WP-IfS "began returning ... in 1950 ... reopening and re-affiliation in 1951"]. Horkheimer directed it 1951–1958 (SEP-CT) and was twice university rector in the early 1950s; Adorno directed it 1958–1969 [source: SEP-CT §1.1; SEP-HO §1; SEP-AD §1].
- Membership: first generation includes Horkheimer, Adorno, Marcuse, Benjamin, Erich Fromm, Friedrich Pollock, Leo Löwenthal, Franz Neumann, Otto Kirchheimer, with Siegfried Kracauer in the broader circle [source: SEP-CT §1.1]. "The work of the largely Jewish members of the first generation was deeply marked by the rise of National Socialism, the experience of exile" [source: SEP-CT §1.1].
- "The 'Frankfurt School' of critical theory is not really a school at all"; it is "a loosely held together tradition constituted by ongoing debates" [source: SEP-CT §1]. Habermas recalled: "Critical Theory, at Frankfurt School—there was no such thing … no coherent doctrine" [source: SEP-HA §1.1].
- Generations: second generation Habermas (leading figure), Alfred Schmidt, Albrecht Wellmer, Oskar Negt, Claus Offe; third generation includes Axel Honneth, Seyla Benhabib, Nancy Fraser, Christoph Menke; later Rahel Jaeggi, Rainer Forst, Amy Allen and others [source: SEP-CT §1.1–1.2].
- Honneth directed the Institute 2001–2018 and is credited with reviving it; took over the Frankfurt chair in social philosophy in 1996 [source: SEP-CT §1.1; WP Axel_Honneth].

## Other members and collaborators (including women)
- Friedrich Pollock (1894–1970), co-founder of the Institute, Horkheimer's closest friend; the School's political economist; theory of "state capitalism" (articles 1932–33; "State Capitalism: Its Possibilities and Limitations", 1941): state intervention meant the primacy of the economy had given way to the primacy of politics [source: WP Friedrich_Pollock; SEP-CT §1.5; SEP-AD §4.1]. He was supposed to contribute a chapter to *DoE* but never did [source: SEP-AD §4.1].
- Leo Löwenthal (1900–1993), joined 1926; sociology of literature; with Norbert Guterman wrote *Prophets of Deceit: A Study of the Techniques of the American Agitator* (1949), on American demagogues' rhetoric [source: WP Leo_Löwenthal; SEP-CT §1.4].
- Erich Fromm (1900–1980), psychoanalyst brought in by Horkheimer; moved via Geneva to Columbia 1934; in *Studies in Authority and the Family* (1936) wrote that the "drives underlying the authoritarian character" are "the pleasure of obedience, submission, and the surrender of one's personality" and "aggression against the defenseless and sympathy with the powerful" (Fromm 1936 [2020, 39, 41]); later *Escape from Freedom* (1941) [source: SEP-CT §1.4; SEP-MA §4; WP Erich_Fromm]. SEP-AD: Adorno criticised Fromm's "revisionist" psychoanalysis [source: SEP-AD §4.3].
- Franz Neumann (1900–1954), labour lawyer turned political scientist; *Behemoth: The Structure and Practice of National Socialism* (1942; expanded 1944); worked for the OSS 1943–45 [source: WP Franz_Neumann; WP Behemoth; SEP-CT §1.4]. Neumann and Kirchheimer were "the main legal and political analysts of the first generation, but were outside the inner circle" [source: SEP-CT §1.4].
- Otto Kirchheimer (1905–1965), jurist and political scientist; originated the concept of the "catch-all party" [source: WP Otto_Kirchheimer].
- Gretel Adorno (née Karplus, 1902–1993), doctorate in chemistry (Berlin, 1925); married Adorno 1937; close friend and correspondent of Benjamin; helped develop the *DoE* manuscript and is acknowledged in its preface as "a precious helper"; the 1969 reissue's preface acknowledged her contribution [source: WP Gretel_Adorno; WP-DoE]. SEP-BE lists the Benjamin–Gretel Adorno *Correspondence 1930–1940* (trans. Wieland Hoban, Polity 2008).
- Else Frenkel-Brunswik (1908–1958), psychologist, co-author of *The Authoritarian Personality* (1950) [source: WP Else_Frenkel-Brunswik; SEP-CT bibliography]. SEP-CT §4.1.1 notes a new Institute research project highlighting female researchers "such as Käthe Weil and Else Frenkel-Brunswik".
- "In most accounts, the first generation of the Frankfurt School is portrayed as not including any women and, with the exception of Marcuse in the 1970s, its main protagonists largely failed to theorize about gender-based oppression" [source: SEP-CT §4.1.1].
- Herta Herzog (1910–2010): NOT an Institute member. She was a media researcher with Paul Lazarsfeld's Radio Project (Lazarsfeld also employed Adorno on the Princeton Radio Project) [source: WP Herta_Herzog; SEP-AD §4.2]. Do not list her as a Frankfurt School figure.
- Angela Davis studied with Marcuse in the US and with Adorno in Frankfurt [source: SEP-CT §4.1.2].

## Influences: Marx, Lukács, Weber, Freud, Kant/Hegel, Nietzsche (cross-ref *Marx*, *German Idealism*, *Nietzsche*)
- The first generation "revised and updated Marxism by integrating it with the work of Sigmund Freud, Max Weber, and Friedrich Nietzsche" [source: SEP-CT §1.1].
- Why revision was needed: the workers Marx expected to lead change "not only lacked revolutionary consciousness, but would soon embrace fascist politics when faced with economic crisis and mass unemployment"; hence the turn to psychoanalysis to explain why people turn to authoritarian politics against their class interests [source: SEP-CT §1.2]. "This effort to combine Marx and Freud is one of the distinctive features of the Frankfurt School" [source: SEP-CT §1.2].
- Weber: rationalisation, the growing dominance of instrumental (means–end) rationality, "iron cage", "disenchantment of the world"; crucial for Horkheimer and Adorno's critique of instrumental reason and Habermas's theory of communicative action [source: SEP-CT §1.2].
- Georg (György) Lukács (1885–1971), *History and Class Consciousness* (1923), essay "Reification and the Consciousness of the Proletariat": combined Marx's "fetishism of commodities" with Weber's rationalisation; reification is "the necessary, immediate reality of every person living in capitalist society" (1923 [1971, 197], tr. Rodney Livingstone, MIT 1971); all social relations, even to oneself, take on a "thing-like" character [source: SEP-CT §1.2, §3.2; SEP-LU]. SEP-LU: Lukács is "widely viewed as one of the founders of 'Western Marxism'"; the book "had a decisive influence on the early Frankfurt School".
- Western Marxism: Perry Anderson's *Considerations on Western Marxism* (1976) defines it by its "structural divorce from political practice" [source: WP Western_Marxism] (matches the published *Marx* chapter).
- Lukács later accused the Frankfurt theorists of taking up residence in the "Grand Hotel Abyss" (Lukács 1963, 22) [source: SEP-CT §4.3].
- Kant: the ideal of *Mündigkeit* (maturity, autonomy) from "What is Enlightenment?" animates the ideal of emancipation; Hegel is "a continual reference point" (Marcuse 1941, Adorno 1963, Benhabib 1986, Honneth 1992, 2011) [source: SEP-CT §1.2].
- Nietzsche: *DoE* represents a shift to "doing critical theory in a more Nietzschean spirit with the genealogy of reason"; Habermas took a more critical view of Nietzsche's "supposed irrationalism" (1985) [source: SEP-CT §1.2].

## "Traditional and Critical Theory" (Horkheimer, 1937)
- Published in the *Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung* 6(2) (1937): 245–294; English: Matthew J. O'Connell (trans.), in *Critical Theory: Selected Essays* (New York, 1972), 188–243; plus a "Postscript" (1937), 244–252 [source: SEP-CT bibliography; SEP-HO bibliography].
- "One largely undisputed reference for defining Frankfurt School critical theory" [source: SEP-CT §1.3].
- Traditional theory takes the existing social order as given; social sciences modelled on natural science mirror facts or seek law-like generalisations, failing to analyse their own social context; the two forms correspond to Descartes' *Discourse on Method* and Marx's critique of political economy (Postscript, p. 244) [source: SEP-CT §1.3; SEP-HO §3].
- Traditional theory misses that "bringing hypotheses to bear on facts is an activity that goes on, ultimately, not in the savant's head but in industry" (p. 196) [source: SEP-HO §3, O'Connell tr.].
- Critical theory is "a human activity which has society itself for its object" (p. 206) [source: SEP-HO §3].
- The theorist forms "a dynamic unity with the oppressed class" (p. 215); emancipatory interest defined negatively as the "abolition of social injustice" (p. 242), positively as "reasonable conditions of life" (p. 199); "The theory never aims simply at an increase of knowledge as such" but at "emancipation from slavery" (Postscript, p. 246) [source: SEP-CT §1.3, O'Connell tr.].
- Tension: "Even the situation of the proletariat is…no guarantee of correct knowledge" (pp. 211–215); "it is the task of the critical theoretician to reduce the tension between his own insight and oppressed humanity in whose service he thinks" (p. 221); the commodity economy "finally hinders further development and drives humanity into a new barbarism" (p. 227) [source: SEP-HO §3]. Marcuse's companion essay "Philosophy and Critical Theory" (1937) asks what happens "if the development outlined by the theory does not occur?" [source: SEP-HO §3].
- Later continuation: the 1960s "positivism dispute" in German sociology (Adorno and Habermas vs Popper and Hans Albert, from 1961 to 1969) [source: SEP-CT §1.3; WP Positivism_dispute].
- SEP-CT's four methodological aspirations: (1) self-reflexive, (2) interdisciplinary, (3) materialist, (4) emancipatory [source: SEP-CT §2].

## Authority, authoritarianism and the Studies in Prejudice
- *Studies in Authority and the Family* (1936), product of five years of Institute research [source: SEP-CT §1.4].
- *The Authoritarian Personality* (1950), Adorno with Else Frenkel-Brunswik, Daniel Levinson and Nevitt Sanford (University of California, Berkeley), in the series *Studies in Prejudice* edited by Horkheimer, sponsored by the American Jewish Committee [source: SEP-CT §1.4; WP-AP].
- Measured susceptibility to fascism by psychological traits rather than explicit politics, on scales: Anti-Semitism (AS), Ethnocentrism (E), Political-Economic Conservatism (PEC) and fascism (F); high-F traits include conventionalism, submissiveness, aggression, aversion to subjectivity and imagination, superstition/stereotypy, toughness, concern with others' sexuality [source: SEP-AD §4.3; SEP-CT §1.4].
- Criticisms: unrepresentative sampling; "acquiescence response set" (all F-scale items worded in the affirmative), raised by Couch and Keniston (1960) and others [source: WP-AP]. Christopher Lasch's complaint that it taught liberals to dismiss opponents "on psychiatric grounds" [source: WP-FS quotation]. Renewed interest with today's authoritarian populism [source: SEP-CT §1.4; SEP-AD §4.3].

## Dialectic of Enlightenment (Horkheimer and Adorno)
- Written 1939–1944 in California exile; circulated 1944 in a limited hectographed edition titled *Philosophische Fragmente* (New York, Institute for Social Research); revised version, which "mitigated the Marxist, economic vocabulary", published as *Dialektik der Aufklärung* by Querido, Amsterdam, 1947; reissued 1969 (S. Fischer) with a new preface [source: SEP-AD §3; WP-DoE].
- English translations: John Cumming (New York: Herder and Herder, 1972; Verso reissues from 1979 reverse the authors' names) and Edmund Jephcott (Stanford UP, 2002, based on the text in Horkheimer's collected works, ed. Gunzelin Schmid Noerr) [source: WP-DoE; SEP-HO bibliography].
- Opening (Jephcott): "Enlightenment, understood in the widest sense as the advance of thought, has always aimed at liberating human beings from fear and installing them as masters. Yet the wholly enlightened earth is radiant with triumphant calamity" (1947 [2002, 1]) [source: SEP-CT §1.5; SEP-AD §3; SEP-HO §4.1]. Cumming's version (as quoted by IEP-AD, 1979: 3): "In the most general sense of progressive thought, the Enlightenment has always aimed at liberating men from fear and establishing their sovereignty. Yet the fully enlightened earth radiates disaster triumphant."
- Aim: to explain "why humanity, instead of entering a truly human state, is sinking into a new kind of barbarism" (2002, xiv) [source: SEP-AD §3].
- The two theses (Jephcott, Preface, p. xviii): "myth is already enlightenment, and enlightenment reverts to mythology" [source: SEP-AD §3; SEP-HO §4.1; WP-DoE]. IEP-AD gives the same wording, with a comma, citing the Cumming translation (1979: xvi). (The semicolon form "Myth is already enlightenment; and enlightenment reverts to mythology" could not be confirmed — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- "Enlightenment is totalitarian": Jephcott, cited by SEP-AD as (2002, 5) but by SEP-CT as (2002, 50) — page uncertain; Cumming, cited by IEP-AD as (1979: 24) [sources: SEP-AD §3; SEP-CT §1.5; IEP-AD §3].
- Myth is already enlightenment because myths already try to control nature: "myth sought to report, to name, to tell of origins, but therefore also to narrate, record, explain" (p. 5); "The substitution which takes place in sacrifice marks a step towards discursive logic" (p. 6) [source: SEP-HO §4.1; SEP-AD §3].
- Enlightenment reverts to myth: "Just as myths already entail enlightenment, with every step enlightenment entangles itself more deeply in mythology… The principle of immanence, the explanation of every event as repetition, ... is that of myth itself" (pp. 7–8) [source: SEP-AD §3]. "Thought is reified as an autonomous, automatic process, aping the machine it has itself produced, so that it can finally be replaced by the machine" (p. 19) [source: SEP-CT §1.5].
- The root goes back to "humanity's earliest attempts to dominate nature", combined with a historical claim about modern science, capitalism and bureaucracy [source: SEP-CT §1.5]. Domination of outer nature entails domination of inner nature and of other people (Excursus II: Kant and Sade) [source: SEP-HO §4.2; SEP-AD §3].
- Odysseus (Excursus I): the *Odyssey* is a "basic text [Grundtext] of European civilization" (p. 37); "the hero turns out to be the prototype [Urbild] of the bourgeois individual" (p. 36); "the history of civilization is the history of the introversion of sacrifice … the history of renunciation" (p. 43) [source: SEP-AD §3].
- The Sirens: Odysseus orders his rowers to stop their ears with wax and has himself bound to the mast; he hears the song's promise of happiness but only as semblance, while the rowers, the labourers, are denied even that [source: SEP-AD §3]. (Common gloss: the bound listener is the bourgeois who enjoys art as powerless contemplation; the rowers are the workers — derived from SEP-AD's paraphrase; do not quote.) Odysseus calling himself "Nobody" to the Cyclops (p. 53) = loss of self through self-preservation [source: SEP-HO §4.2].
- "At the moment when human beings cut themselves off from the consciousness of themselves as nature, all the purposes for which they keep themselves alive ... become void" (pp. 42–43) [source: SEP-AD §3].
- Aporia and hope: the book speaks of intending "to prepare a positive concept of enlightenment which liberates it from its entanglement in blind domination" (p. xviii); "if enlightenment does not assimilate reflection on this regressive moment, it seals its own fate" (p. xvi) [source: SEP-AD §3; SEP-CT §1.5].
- "Elements of Anti-Semitism": antisemitism as a "release valve" letting rage be "vented on those who are both conspicuous and unprotected" (2002, 140); "false projection" ("the reverse of genuine mimesis", p. 154); the image of the Jew suggests "happiness without power, reward without work, a homeland without frontiers, religion without myth" (pp. 164–5) [source: SEP-CT §1.5; SEP-AD §4.3].
- The shift from critique of political economy (Marx) to critique of instrumental reason (Weber) was motivated partly by Pollock's state-capitalism thesis [source: SEP-CT §1.5 citing Benhabib 1986].
- Gretel Adorno acknowledged in the foreword as "a precious helper" [source: WP Gretel_Adorno].

## Instrumental reason: Horkheimer's *Eclipse of Reason* (1947)
- Published 1947 in English, sole author Horkheimer; based on Columbia lectures of 1944; presents "in epitome some aspects" of the joint work with Adorno; German title *Zur Kritik der instrumentellen Vernunft* ("On the Critique of Instrumental Reason") [source: SEP-HO §4, §4.1].
- First chapter "Means and Ends": instrumental reason determines means to a goal "without reasoning about ends in themselves"; "subjective reason" (oriented to the subject's self-preservation) vs "objective reason" (the great metaphysical systems from Plato to German Idealism, concerned with ends) [source: SEP-HO §4.1].
- In the 1930s irrationality was blamed; "In the works from the 1940s, rationality itself is impugned" [source: SEP-HO §4.1].
- Chapter "The Revolt of Nature": "domination becomes 'internalized' for domination's sake" (p. 64) [source: SEP-HO §4.2].

## The culture industry (argument for step-by-step treatment)
- Chapter title: "The Culture Industry: Enlightenment as Mass Deception" [source: SEP-HO §4.1]. The authors introduced the term "culture industry" to stress that "mass culture" is not something "the masses" spontaneously generate but is manufactured by standardised, profit-oriented industrial methods (Adorno, "Culture Industry Reconsidered", 1967 [1991, 98]) [source: SEP-CT §1.5].
- Reconstruction (from SEP-AD §4.2, SEP-CT §1.5, IEP-AD §5):
  - P1. Cultural products under late capitalism are produced, distributed and consumed as commodities, by the same rationalised, standardised methods as other industrial goods: "Film, radio, and magazines form a system. Each branch is unanimous within itself and all are unanimous together" (2002, 94).
  - P2. Commodity production for a mass market favours standardised, formulaic, interchangeable products, behind a show of variety ("pseudo-individualisation") [IEP-AD §5].
  - P3. Such products require no interpretative effort; they are consumed passively and "regressively" (Adorno's "On the Fetish Character in Music and the Regression of Listening", 1938) [SEP-AD §4.2; IEP-AD §5].
  - P4. Leisure becomes a continuation of work: "amusement under late capitalism is the prolongation of work" (Cumming 1979: 137) [IEP-AD §5].
  - P5. Products work by a "cycle of manipulation and retroactive need" (2002, 95) and duplicate existing reality, pursuing "the idolization of the existing order" (p. xix) [SEP-AD §4.2].
  - C. So the culture industry integrates individuals into the existing order, numbs critical capacities, and "impedes the development of autonomous, independent individuals who judge and decide consciously for themselves" (1967 [1991, 104–6]), which democracy needs [SEP-AD §4.2, §7.2]. Even resistance is absorbed: "anyone who resists can survive only by being incorporated" (2002, 104) [SEP-HO §4.1]. "Cultural entities typical of the culture industry are no longer also commodities, they are commodities through and through" (1967 [1991, 129]) [SEP-CT §1.5].
  - Nuance: consumers may "see through the deception" but adapt anyway for self-preservation; Horkheimer and Adorno see fleeting resistance in "stubbornly purposeless" circus energy and the absurdity of writers like Mark Twain (2002, 113–114) [SEP-AD §4.2].
- Objection 1 (elitism/ totalising): critics charge Adorno with dismissiveness toward popular forms; his jazz writings are "notoriously dismissive"; Richard Taruskin called him "preposterously overrated"; Eric Hobsbawm criticised his jazz writing [source: WP-TA "Reception"; SEP-AD §7.2]. The essay "On Jazz" appeared in the *Zeitschrift* in 1936 [source: WP-TA]. Fumi Okiji, *Jazz as Critique: Adorno and Black Expression Revisited* (2018), argues Adorno's analyses "presuppose a subjectivity denied black people", yet jazz exhibits qualities of the avant-garde Adorno praised [source: SEP-AD §7.2].
- Objection 2 (Benjamin's more hopeful view): Benjamin saw in technologised mass culture, especially film, possibilities for collective experience and a "democratization of culture" [source: SEP-CT §1.4]. Wellmer: "in technologized mass culture, Benjamin sees elements of an antidote to the psychic destruction of society, whereas Adorno regards it above all as a medium of conformism and psychic manipulation" [source: SEP-CT §1.4]. Kracauer too was more optimistic [source: SEP-CT §1.4].
- Objection 3 (active audiences — Birmingham cultural studies): the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies, University of Birmingham, founded 1964 by Richard Hoggart (Stuart Hall directing it by 1968, formally 1971); cultural studies "criticizes the traditional view of the passive consumer", stressing how people read, rework or challenge cultural texts; hegemony theory treated subordinate groups not "as the passive dupes of the dominant class" [source: WP Cultural_studies; WP Centre_for_Contemporary_Cultural_Studies]. (The WP pages do not name Adorno as their target; present this as the general cultural-studies objection, not as a documented exchange.)
- Objection 4 (empirical): critics have sought to elaborate the "cognitive and psychological complexities" of reception in light of changed cultural conditions (Cook 1996; Gunster 2000) [source: SEP-AD §4.2].
- Replies: Adorno's claim concerns the production structure and social function of mass culture, not the content of particular works or the taste of audiences [source: WP-TA "Culture industry" section: "Adorno located the problem, not with the content, but with the objective realities of the production of mass culture"]; he also allowed incipient gestures of autonomy within the culture industry (Mariotti 2016) and saw "autonomous" art (e.g. Schoenberg's atonality) as resistance [source: SEP-AD §7.2; SEP-CT §1.4]. Defenders: Alex Ross (*New Yorker*, 2014) said the digital age had "magnified Adorno's relevance" [source: WP Culture_industry].

## Adorno (1903–1969)
- Born Theodor Ludwig Wiesengrund, 11 September 1903, Frankfurt; father Oscar Wiesengrund, wealthy wine merchant of assimilated Jewish background; mother Maria Calvelli-Adorno, Catholic singer "of Corsican descent" (SEP-AD; IEP-AD says "of Italian descent") [source: SEP-AD §1; IEP-AD §1].
- Studied philosophy with Hans Cornelius; composition with Alban Berg in Vienna; Habilitation on Kierkegaard's aesthetics 1931 under Paul Tillich; expelled by the Nazis after two years as Privatdozent [source: SEP-AD §1]. 1931 inaugural lecture "The Actuality of Philosophy", indebted to Benjamin [source: SEP-AD §2].
- Left Germany spring 1934: Merton College, Oxford (advanced student under Gilbert Ryle's direction), then New York (1938) and southern California [source: SEP-AD §1; WP-TA; SEP-CT §1.1]. Married Gretel Karplus 1937 [source: SEP-AD §1].
- Returned to Frankfurt 1949; Institute director 1958; *Negative Dialectics* 1966; died of a heart attack 6 August 1969 (in Visp, Switzerland); *Aesthetic Theory* posthumous 1970 [source: SEP-AD §1; WP-TA].
- *Minima Moralia: Reflexionen aus dem beschädigten Leben* (1951; written 1944–1949, dedicated to Horkheimer); 153 aphorisms; English: *Minima Moralia: Reflections from Damaged Life*, tr. E. F. N. Jephcott (NLB/Verso; IEP-AD cites Verso 1978; SEP-AD cites Verso 2005) [source: WP-MM; SEP-AD bibliography; IEP-AD references].
- German: "Es gibt kein richtiges Leben im falschen"; SEP-AD renders it "there is no right living in the false [life]" (1951a [2005, 39]); Jephcott's translation reads "Wrong life cannot be lived rightly"; it closes aphorism 18 of Part I, "Refuge for the Homeless" (sometimes "Asylum for the homeless") [source: SEP-AD §7.1; WS: Newcastle PhD thesis; Cambridge chapter title "Wrong life cannot be lived rightly" in J. M. Bernstein, *Adorno: Disenchantment and Ethics* (2001)]. Also from *MM*: "The whole is the false" (2005, 50) [source: SEP-AD §7.1].
- Auschwitz and poetry: "To write poetry after Auschwitz is barbaric" — Adorno's essay "Cultural Criticism and Society" ("Kulturkritik und Gesellschaft"), written 1949, published 1951 in a Festschrift (ed. Karl Gustav Specht), republished in *Prismen* (1955); English in *Prisms*, tr. Samuel and Shierry Weber (1967) [source: SEP-CT bibliography 1951a; WP-TA ("Adorno's 1949 dictum"); WS]. Context: the sentence follows "Cultural criticism finds itself faced with the final stage of the dialectic of culture and barbarism" and continues "And this corrodes even the knowledge of why it has become impossible to write poetry today" (Weber wording, WS — snippet-level only).
- Later qualification, *Negative Dialectics* (1966; tr. Ashton 1973, p. 362): "Perennial suffering has as much right to expression as a tortured man has to scream; hence it may have been wrong to say that after Auschwitz you could no longer write poems" [source: WP-TA (first clause); WS (p. 362, Ashton)]. In "Commitment" (1962) he said the dictum "expresses in negative form the impulse which inspires committed literature" [source: WP-TA].
- *ND*: Auschwitz "confirmed the philosopheme of pure identity as death" (1973, 362); "A new categorical imperative has been imposed by Hitler upon unfree mankind: to arrange their thoughts and actions so that Auschwitz will not repeat itself, so that nothing similar will happen" (1973, 365) [source: SEP-AD §5, §7.1].
- Identity thinking vs the non-identical; negative dialectics keeps Hegel's mediation but abandons the unifying synthesis [source: SEP-CT §1.4; SEP-AD §5].
- Normativity problem: Habermas's charge (1987, 119) of a "performative contradiction" in totalised critique; Freyenhagen (2013) reads Adorno as a "negativist" for whom knowledge of the bad suffices for critique [source: SEP-AD §3, §7.1].
- Public role: radio talks, "Education after Auschwitz" (1966/67), education for *Mündigkeit* — "an actual working democracy can only be conceived as a society of politically mature people" [source: SEP-AD §7.2; WP-TA].
- Student protests: Adorno, "in contrast to Marcuse, demurred" [source: SEP-AD §7.2]. In January 1969, when his student Hans-Jürgen Krahl led students to occupy a room at the Institute, Adorno called the police to remove them; Marcuse criticised the decision [source: WP-FS]. In April 1969 (summer semester) a student wrote "If Adorno is left in peace, capitalism will never cease" on the blackboard and three women students bared their breasts and scattered petals over him; he cancelled the lecture course [source: WP-TA]. *Spiegel* interview (5 May 1969): "Professor Adorno, two weeks ago, the world still seemed in order…" — "Not to me." [source: SEP-AD §7.2]. Adorno–Marcuse correspondence on the student movement (1969), published in *New Left Review* 233 (1999) [source: SEP-AD bibliography].

## Walter Benjamin (1892–1940)
- Born 15 July 1892, Berlin, assimilated Jewish family; friendship with Gershom Scholem from 1915; doctorate Bern 1919 (*The Concept of Art Criticism in German Romanticism*); Habilitation on the *Trauerspiel* withdrawn from Frankfurt in 1925; book published 1928 [source: SEP-BE §1].
- Turned to historical materialism after reading Lukács's *History and Class Consciousness* on Capri (1924) and meeting Asja Lacis; friend of Brecht from 1929 [source: SEP-BE §1].
- Exile from 1933 (Paris, Ibiza, San Remo, Brecht's house in Denmark); the Institute's stipend was his main income in the 1930s; Adorno was instrumental in securing it; editorial pressure on his essays [source: SEP-BE §1]. Benjamin was never a formal Institute member (SEP-CT lists him among first-generation "key figures"; WP-TA notes Adorno himself was not an Institute member in the early 1930s).
- "The Work of Art in the Age of Its Technological Reproducibility" (German "Das Kunstwerk im Zeitalter seiner technischen Reproduzierbarkeit"): first drafted 1935 in Paris; rewritten Dec 1935–Feb 1936 (the "second version", now preferred as Ur-text); first published 1936 in French, tr. Pierre Klossowski, "L'œuvre d'art à l'époque de sa reproduction mécanisée", *Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung* 5(1): 40–68; revised to 1939; third version first appeared in German in 1955 [source: WP-WoA; SEP-CT bibliography].
- Title translations: Harry Zohn's "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (in *Illuminations*, ed. Hannah Arendt, 1968 per SEP-CT; WP-WoA says 1969); Harvard 2008 volume "The Work of Art in the Age of Its Technological Reproducibility" (second version); SEP-BE uses "Technical Reproducibility"; Michael W. Jennings's translation in *Grey Room* 39 (2010) [source: WP-WoA; SEP-CT bibliography; SEP-BE §7]. Literal German: "technical reproducibility".
- Aura: "the 'authenticity' associated with the unique presence of the original in space and time" declines with photography and film [source: SEP-CT §1.4]. Basic definition (from "Little History of Photography", 1931, repeated in the artwork essay): "A strange weave of space and time: the unique appearance or semblance of distance, no matter how close it may be" (SW 2, 518) [source: SEP-BE §7]. Zohn's rendering in the artwork essay (natural objects): "the unique phenomenon of a distance, however close it may be" [WS, secondary quotations only].
- Politics: the essay contrasts the "aestheticization of politics" under fascism with communism's politicisation of art; it aims at concepts "completely useless for the purposes of fascism" (SW 3, 102) [source: SEP-CT §1.4; SEP-BE §7].
- Adorno's objection, letter of 18 March 1936: Benjamin had "rather casually transferred the concept of the magical aura to the 'autonomous work'" and "flatly assigned a counter-revolutionary function to the latter" (*Complete Correspondence*, tr. Nicholas Walker) [source: SEP-BE §7]. Brecht found the aura idea "all mysticism" [source: SEP-BE §7].
- "On the Concept of History" ("Über den Begriff der Geschichte"), popularly "Theses on the Philosophy of History": written early 1940; twenty numbered theses; first printed in the mimeographed *Walter Benjamin zum Gedächtnis* (spring 1942); English tr. Harry Zohn in *Illuminations* (ed. Arendt, 1968); also Zohn's translation in Harvard *Selected Writings* vol. 4 (2006), pp. 389–400 [source: WP-Th; SEP-CT bibliography; SEP-BE §9].
- Thesis IX, the angel of history (Klee's *Angelus Novus*, a 1920 monoprint Benjamin bought in 1921, now in the Israel Museum) — standard (Zohn) wording [source: WP-WB; WP Angelus_Novus; WP-Th]: "A Klee painting named *Angelus Novus* shows an angel looking as though he is about to move away from something he is fixedly contemplating. His eyes are staring, his mouth is open, his wings are spread. This is how one pictures the angel of history. His face is turned toward the past. Where we perceive a chain of events, he sees one single catastrophe which keeps piling wreckage upon wreckage and hurls it in front of his feet. The angel would like to stay, awaken the dead, and make whole what has been smashed. But a storm is blowing from Paradise; it has got caught in his wings with such violence that the angel can no longer close them. The storm irresistibly propels him into the future to which his back is turned, while the pile of debris before him grows skyward. This storm is what we call progress." (WP does not name the translator on the page; the wording matches the Zohn text as widely cited — WS.)
- Thesis VII (Zohn): "There is no document of civilization which is not at the same time a document of barbarism." German: "Es ist niemals ein Dokument der Kultur, ohne zugleich ein solches der Barbarei zu sein." [source: WS (secondary quotation sites; Zohn, *Illuminations*, p. 256/258 depending on edition)]. The Portbou grave epitaph quotes it in German and Catalan ("document of culture") [source: WP-WB].
- Critique of progress: aim of "a historical materialism which has annihilated within itself the idea of progress" (AP [N2,2], 460); "Classless society is not the final goal of historical progress but its frequently miscarried, ultimately achieved interruption" (SW 4, 402) [source: SEP-BE §9]. Debate whether his history is "theological" (Scholem) or "materialist" (Brecht), Adorno seeking compatibility [source: SEP-BE §9].
- *The Arcades Project* (*Passagen-Werk*), on 19th-century Paris, notes from 1927 to 1940, unfinished; published 1982; English tr. Howard Eiland & Kevin McLaughlin (Belknap, 1999); notes left with Georges Bataille at the Bibliothèque Nationale when Benjamin fled Paris in 1940 [source: SEP-BE §1, §6; WP-WB].
- Death: interned in a French camp in 1939; fled Paris in summer 1940; crossed the Pyrenees with a guided party lacking a French exit visa; arrived Portbou 25 September 1940; told they would be returned to France, he took an overdose of morphine that night at the Hotel de Francia; the Portbou register records 26 September (SEP-BE says 27 September) [source: WP-WB; SEP-BE §1]. The rest of his party was let through and reached Lisbon on 30 September [source: WP-WB].
- Wider influence waited until Adorno (with Gretel Adorno) edited his *Schriften* (1955) and Arendt edited *Illuminations* (1968) [source: SEP-CT §1.7; SEP-CT bibliography].

## Marcuse (1898–1979)
- Born 19 July 1898, Berlin, assimilated Jewish family; military service 1916–18; doctorate on the German artist-novel (Freiburg, 1922); studied with Heidegger at Freiburg 1928–32 ("Heideggerian Marxism"), broke with him in 1932 [source: SEP-MA §1, §3]. Hired by the Institute in 1932–33 (letter of support from Husserl), worked first in Geneva, then New York from July 1934 [source: SEP-MA §1].
- 1942 to Washington (Office of War Information, then OSS); later Brandeis (1954–1965) and UC San Diego (1965–1970); US citizen 1940; stayed in the US [source: SEP-MA §1; WP-HM]. Died 29 July 1979 at Starnberg, West Germany, after a stroke, on a visit at Habermas's invitation [source: WP-HM].
- *Eros and Civilization: A Philosophical Inquiry into Freud* (1955), from 1950–51 lectures at the Washington School of Psychiatry; answers Freud's *Civilization and Its Discontents*; distinguishes "basic repression" (needed "for the perpetuation of the human race in civilization") from "surplus repression" ("the restrictions necessitated by social domination"), under the "performance principle", "the prevailing form of the reality principle" (1955: 35) [source: SEP-MA §4].
- *One-Dimensional Man: Studies in the Ideology of Advanced Industrial Society* (1964, written at Brandeis); first sentence: "A comfortable, smooth, reasonable, democratic unfreedom prevails in advanced industrial civilization, a token of technical progress" (1964: 1) [source: SEP-MA §5; WP One-Dimensional_Man]. Key ideas: false needs, "repressive desublimation", the "Great Refusal" carried by "the substratum of the outcasts and outsiders, the exploited and persecuted of other races and other colors, the unemployed and the unemployable" (1964, 256) [source: SEP-CT §1.5].
- "Repressive Tolerance" (1965), in *A Critique of Pure Tolerance* with Robert Paul Wolff and Barrington Moore Jr.; calls for "discriminating tolerance" (Postscript 1968: 123) [source: SEP-MA §5; WP A_Critique_of_Pure_Tolerance]. Quotation: "Liberating tolerance, then, would mean intolerance against movements from the Right and toleration of movements from the Left" (WS — snippet-level; marcuse.org unreachable). Interpretive debate: read as advocating censorship of the Right; Ingram (2024) argues it favours encouraging the Left rather than suppressing the Right (WS).
- New Left: called the "Guru" or "Father of the New Left" — a title he rejected [source: SEP-MA intro; WP-HM]. Supported the 1960s student movements and feminism (1974), unlike Horkheimer and Adorno [source: SEP-CT §1.5; SEP-AD §7.2]. Criticisms: totalising diagnosis, objectivist account of needs, paternalistic implications (Jaeggi) [source: SEP-CT §1.5].

## Habermas (1929–2026)
- Born 18 June 1929, Düsseldorf; grew up in Gummersbach; born with a cleft palate (two operations), which he later linked to his interest in communication; member of the Jungvolk/Hitler Youth as a teenager; manned anti-aircraft defences from 1944 [source: WP-JH; SEP-HA §1.1].
- **Died 14 March 2026 in Starnberg, aged 96** [source: WP-JH, citing AP and *New York Times* obituaries of 14 March 2026; SEP-HA header "b. 1929, d. 2026"]. The chapter must not say "b. 1929" alone or describe him as living.
- 1945 and the Nuremberg revelations a turning point: "I am myself a product of 'reeducation'" (1979 interview) [source: SEP-HA §1.1].
- 1953 FAZ article against Heidegger's unretracted phrase "the inner truth and greatness" of National Socialism [source: SEP-HA §1.1; WP-JH].
- Doctorate Bonn 1954 on Schelling; Adorno's assistant at the Institute 1956–1959; Horkheimer thought him a dangerous Marxist and pushed him out; Habilitation (*Structural Transformation*) at Marburg under Wolfgang Abendroth; extraordinary professor at Heidelberg (1962, with Gadamer's and Löwith's support); took over Horkheimer's Frankfurt chair in philosophy and sociology in 1964 [source: SEP-HA §1.1; WP-JH; SEP-CT §1.1].
- 1968: criticised the students' tactics, using the phrase "left-wing fascism", later admitted too harsh [source: SEP-HA §1.1].
- 1971 to the Max Planck Institute in Starnberg (co-director with Carl Friedrich von Weizsäcker); returned to Frankfurt in 1981 (SEP-HA; SEP-CT) or 1983 (WP-JH) — give "early 1980s"; retired 1994 [source: SEP-HA §1.1; WP-JH; SEP-CT §1.1].
- *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (1962; English tr. Thomas Burger with Frederick Lawrence, 1989): the bourgeois public sphere arose in the 17th–18th centuries first as a literary public sphere — "coffee houses, salons, and literary societies" (Richardson's *Pamela* cited) — then political; "a realm of private individuals assembled into a public body"; status differences bracketed; both "ideal" and "ideology"; later "refeudalization" under welfare-state mass democracy, PR and mass media turning a reasoning public into consumers [source: SEP-HA §1.2; SEP-CT §1.6]. A 2022 sequel: *A New Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere and Deliberative Politics* [source: WP Structural_Transformation].
- Critiques: participants were de facto educated male property-owners; Negt & Kluge (1972) on excluded workers; Joan Landes, *Women and the Public Sphere in the Age of the French Revolution* (1988), on women's exclusion; Nancy Fraser, "Rethinking the Public Sphere: A Contribution to the Critique of Actually Existing Democracy", *Social Text* 25/26 (1990): 56–80 — "subaltern counterpublics", "parallel discursive arenas" where subordinated groups form counterdiscourses; Michael Warner (2002) [source: SEP-HA §1.2; SEP-CT §1.6 and bibliography; WS for the phrase]. Habermas conceded "there may be a multitude of intersecting public spheres" (1992) [source: SEP-HA §1.2].
- *Knowledge and Human Interests* (1968; tr. Jeremy Shapiro, 1971): three knowledge-constitutive interests — technical (natural sciences, "labor"), practical (historical-hermeneutic sciences, "interaction"), emancipatory (critique, self-reflection); psychoanalysis as "the only tangible example of a science incorporating methodological self-reflection" (1971, 124); "That we disavow reflection is positivism" [source: SEP-CT §1.6; SEP-HA §1.3]. Largely abandoned in the early 1970s [source: SEP-CT §1.6].
- *The Theory of Communicative Action* (2 vols., 1981; tr. Thomas McCarthy, 1984/1987) [source: SEP-CT bibliography].
  - Communicative action is oriented to reaching understanding/consensus through validity claims (truth, rightness, truthfulness); instrumental action (non-social) and strategic action (social) are oriented to success; strategic actors influence others "through egocentric calculations of success" [source: SEP-HA §2.2].
  - Irreducibility thesis: communicative action is "the basic form", strategic uses of language "parasitic"; most commentators think he fails to establish this by argument (Steinhoff, Blau), others defend the distinction (Heath) [source: SEP-HA §2.2.2].
  - System (economy and state administration, steered by the "delinguistified" media of money and power) vs lifeworld (background of shared meanings; family, everyday life, civil society); the market "the most important example of a norm-free regulation of cooperative contexts" [source: SEP-HA §2.3; WP-JH].
  - Colonisation of the lifeworld: "systemic mechanisms suppress forms of social integration even in those areas where a consensus-dependent coordination of action cannot be replaced" (1987: 196); leads to loss of meaning, loss of freedom, anomie [source: SEP-HA §2.4.1]. Updates Lukács's reification [source: SEP-CT §3.2].
  - Objections: reifies the "system" as norm-free (Honneth & Joas); idealises the lifeworld and ignores women's domination (Fraser 1985, "What's Critical about Critical Theory? The Case of Habermas and Gender"); Eurocentric progress narrative (Allen 2016); underestimates power inside communication (Allen 2008) [source: SEP-CT §1.6].
- Habermas's critique of *DoE*: the authors needed to leave "at least one rational criterion intact for their explanation of the corruption of all rational criteria" (1985 [1987, 127–9]); a "performative contradiction" (1987, 119) [source: SEP-CT §1.5; SEP-AD §3]. *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985; tr. Frederick Lawrence, 1987).
- Drew on Peirce and Mead (and Dewey); postmetaphysical thinking includes "pragmatists such as C.S. Peirce and G.H. Mead"; Mead's "ideal role taking" guides (U) [source: SEP-HA §3.4, §5.2; WP-JH] (consistent with the published *American Pragmatism* chapter).
- Engaged developmental psychology (Piaget, Kohlberg) in place of psychoanalysis [source: SEP-CT §1.7].
- *Between Facts and Norms* (1992; tr. William Rehg, 1996): discourse theory of law and democracy; two-track model (informal "wild" public sphere and formal institutions); "co-originality" of private and public autonomy, of rights and popular sovereignty [source: SEP-HA §4; SEP-CT §1.6].
- Historikerstreit: essay "A Kind of Settlement of Damages" in *Die Zeit*, 11 July 1986, against "apologetic" tendencies in Ernst Nolte, Michael Stürmer, Klaus Hildebrand and Andreas Hillgruber; attacked by Joachim Fest and others, supported by Broszat, Jäckel, Mommsen, Wehler [source: WP-JH]. He developed "constitutional patriotism" (term coined by Dolf Sternberger, 1979) from this dispute [source: SEP-HA §6.1].
- Gadamer debate: over whether hermeneutics' respect for tradition and prejudice blinds it to ideology and power [source: WP-JH].
- Foucault: competing critiques of power — genealogy vs communicative rationality [source: WP-JH].
- Derrida: disputes from the 1980s (Habermas's 1985 chapter on Derrida), reconciliation in the late 1990s; in 2003 a joint manifesto against the Iraq war: Habermas's text "February 15, or What Binds Europeans Together", with Derrida subscribing [source: WP-JH].
- Rawls: exchange in *Journal of Philosophy* 92(3) (1995): Habermas, "Reconciliation Through the Public Use of Reason: Remarks on John Rawls's Political Liberalism", pp. 109–131; Rawls charged that Habermas's theory rests on a "comprehensive doctrine" (Rawls 1995: 139) [source: SEP-HA §4.5 and bibliography; WP-JH].
- 2004 dialogue with Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger; "post-secular" society [source: WP-JH; SEP-HA §7].

## Discourse ethics (argument for step-by-step treatment)
- Developed in essays collected in *Moral Consciousness and Communicative Action* (1983; tr. Christian Lenhardt and Shierry Weber Nicholsen, 1990) and *Justification and Application* (1991; tr. 1993) [source: SEP-HA §3]. Habermas later said it should have been named "the discourse theory of morality" [source: SEP-HA §3].
- Rules of discourse (from Robert Alexy, quoted 1990: 89): "(1) Every subject with the competence to speak and act is allowed to take part in a discourse. (2) a. Everyone is allowed to question any assertion whatever. b. Everyone is allowed to introduce any assertion whatever into the discourse. c. Everyone is allowed to express his attitudes, desires, and needs. (3) No speaker may be prevented, by internal or external coercion, from exercising his rights as laid down in (1) and (2)." [source: SEP-HA §3.1]
- The "unforced force of the better argument" [source: SEP-HA §3.1; SEP-AD §3].
- (D), phase one, as printed by SEP-HA (1990: 66): "Only those norms can claim to be valid that meet (or could meet) with the approval of all in their capacity as participants in a practical discourse." SEP-HA adds that the scope of consensus is "all those affected". (The commonly cited form "...approval of all affected in their capacity as participants..." was not checked against the 1990 translation; quote SEP-HA's text or the BFN version below.) [source: SEP-HA §3.3]
- (D), phase two (*BFN*, 1996: 107): "Only those action norms are valid to which all possibly affected persons could agree as participants in rational discourse." [source: SEP-HA §3.3]
- (U) (1990: 65): "All affected can accept the consequences and the side effects its general observance can be anticipated to have for the satisfaction of everyone's interests (and these consequences are preferred to those of known alternative possibilities)." [source: SEP-HA §3.3]. Later strengthened: "a norm is valid if and only if the foreseeable consequences and side effects of its general observance for the interests and value-orientations of each individual could be freely accepted jointly by all concerned" (1998a: 42) [source: SEP-HA §3.3].
- Reconstruction:
  - P1. Anyone who argues seriously about a norm already presupposes the rules of discourse (equal participation, freedom to question and propose, no coercion); denying them involves a "performative self-contradiction" (Apel's idea; for Habermas the test identifies rather than justifies the rules) [SEP-HA §3.2].
  - P2. To justify a norm is to show it could be agreed by all affected (the "weak idea of normative justification") [SEP-HA §3.3].
  - C. Hence (U): a norm is valid only if all affected could accept the consequences of its general observance for everyone's interests; moral validity is settled dialogically in real discourse, not by each person alone [SEP-HA §3.3–3.4].
  - Versus Kant: Kant's categorical-imperative test is "monological"; (U) is "dialogical". McCarthy: "The emphasis shifts from what each can will without contradiction to be a general law, to what all can will in agreement to be a universal norm" (quoted by Habermas 1990: 67) [SEP-HA §3.4].
  - Objection A (justification fails): almost no one thinks the derivation of (U) goes through (Gunnarsson, Lumer, Kettner); Habermas retreats to treating (U) as an abductive hypothesis [SEP-HA §3.3].
  - Objection B (Benhabib, feminist): early on she called (U) redundant and proposed a weaker justification from "universal respect" and "egalitarian reciprocity" (1986; 1992); following Carol Gilligan, she argued that restricting morality to justice leads to "epistemological blindness towards the concrete other" (*Situating the Self*, 1992: 164) [SEP-HA §3.3, §3.8].
  - Reply to B: ideal role-taking requires switching perspective with concrete others, so Benhabib's view may explicate rather than refute Habermas (Finlayson 2013a) [SEP-HA §3.8].
  - Objection C (communitarian): Charles Taylor and Martin Seel defend the priority of the good over the right; Benhabib, McCarthy, Putnam, Kettner deny the sharp ethics/morality line; Habermas admits "the remnant of the good at the core of the right" [SEP-HA §3.5].
  - Objection D (dialogical vs monological is "a distinction without a difference"): McMahon (2000); advocatory discourse with absent parties [SEP-HA §3.6].
  - Objection E (Rawls/Cohen/Larmore): rests on a comprehensive philosophical doctrine; reply (Lister, Laden): philosophical controversy is not the kind that undermines political legitimacy [SEP-HA §4.5].
  - Objection F (Eurocentrism): Allen and Dussel: wedded to a progressive theory of social evolution [SEP-HA §3.8].

## Honneth (b. 1949) and recognition (cross-ref *German Idealism*)
- Axel Honneth, born 18 July 1949, Essen; studied at Bonn, Bochum, Free University Berlin; Habermas's assistant; Frankfurt chair 1996; director of the Institute 2001–2018; Columbia professor since 2011 [source: WP Axel_Honneth; SEP-CT §1.1].
- *Critique of Power* (1985; tr. Kenneth Baynes 1991) on the Frankfurt School and Foucault [source: SEP-CT bibliography].
- *Kampf um Anerkennung* (1992); *The Struggle for Recognition: The Moral Grammar of Social Conflicts*, tr. Joel Anderson (Polity, 1995) [source: SEP-CT bibliography].
- Following Hegel (1807; 1821) and G. H. Mead (1934), three spheres/patterns of recognition — love, rights, solidarity — yielding self-confidence, self-respect and self-esteem [source: IEP-RE §3b]. "For it is only due to the cumulative acquisition of basic self-confidence, of self-respect, and of self-esteem… that a person can come to see himself or herself, unconditionally, as both an autonomous and an individuated being" (1995: 169) [source: IEP-RE §3b].
- Love modelled partly on Winnicott's object-relations account of infant–mother relations (1992, ch. 5) [source: SEP-CT §1.7].
- Experiences of disrespect (misrecognition) are the "raw material" of social struggles [source: IEP-RE §3b]. Honneth's is "the most prominent alternative paradigm, within Habermasian critical theory broadly construed", to communicative action; "undistorted recognition" in place of "undistorted communication"; critique of "social pathologies" [source: SEP-CT §1.7].
- Later: *Reification* (2005) — forgetting of recognition; *Freedom's Right* (2011; tr. 2014) [source: SEP-CT §3.2 and bibliography].
- Fraser–Honneth, *Redistribution or Recognition? A Political-Philosophical Exchange* (Verso, 2003): Fraser — "perspectival dualism": maldistribution and misrecognition are irreducible; recognition as status, judged by "parity of participation" ("justice requires that social arrangements permit all (adult) members of society to interact with one another as peers"); Honneth — monism: "questions of distributive justice are better understood in terms of normative categories that come from a sufficiently differentiated theory of recognition" (2003: 126); Fraser objects that grounding critique in psychology yields "an excessively personalized sense of injury" (p. 204); Honneth replies that Fraser can see only struggles already visible in the public sphere [source: IEP-RE §3c, §4].
- Feminist critique of Honneth's conception of love, the family and care (Young 2007, Rössler 2007) [source: SEP-CT §4.1.1].

## Later and broader critical theory; Eurocentrism
- Seyla Benhabib (b. 9 Sept 1950, Istanbul): *Critique, Norm, and Utopia* (1986); *Situating the Self* (1992) [source: WP Seyla_Benhabib; SEP-CT bibliography].
- Nancy Fraser (b. 20 May 1947), The New School [source: WP Nancy_Fraser]; "What's Critical about Critical Theory?" (1985) called on critical theory to frame its programme "with an eye to the aims and activities of those oppositional social movements with which it has a partisan, though not uncritical, identification" [source: SEP-CT §1.7].
- Rahel Jaeggi (b. 19 July 1966), Swiss, Humboldt University Berlin since 2009 (announced move to The New School from fall 2026); *Alienation* (2005; tr. 2014), *Critique of Forms of Life* (2014; tr. Ciaran Cronin, 2018); Honneth's assistant 1996–2001 [source: WP Rahel_Jaeggi; SEP-CT bibliography].
- Amy Allen, *The End of Progress: Decolonizing the Normative Foundations of Critical Theory* (Columbia UP, 2016): Habermas, Honneth and Forst ground normativity in a Eurocentric idea of historical progress; she proposes a contextualist critique inspired by Foucault and Adorno [source: SEP-CT §4.1.3 and bibliography; SEP-HA §3.8].
- Edward Said (1993) noted the School's failure to address European colonialism; Enrique Dussel built an ethics of liberation in critical dialogue with Apel and Habermas (1998); Gurminder Bhambra (2021) [source: SEP-CT §4.1.3].
- Race: little sustained engagement until recently beyond antisemitism studies; Angela Davis and Stuart Hall as near-neighbours [source: SEP-CT §4.1.2].

## Debates
- Was *DoE* a dead end rescued by Habermas, or one of several continuing models (Adorno's line through Wellmer, Menke, Seel; Allen; Kompridis)? [source: SEP-CT §1.7].
- Normative deficit: Habermas's performative-contradiction charge against *DoE* and Adorno vs defences as "world-disclosive critique" (Kompridis 2006; Honneth "rhetorical condensation"), Allen's "dialectical conception of progress", Freyenhagen's negativism [source: SEP-CT §1.5; SEP-AD §3, §7.1].
- Benjamin's history: theological (Scholem) vs materialist (Brecht) vs Adorno's synthesis [source: SEP-BE §9].
- Aura: nostalgic loss vs affirmative technological modernism; Adorno's 1936 objection [source: SEP-BE §7].
- Repressive Tolerance: censorship of the Right vs selective encouragement of the Left (WS).
- Habermas's system/lifeworld: whether system is norm-free (Honneth & Joas, McCarthy, Fraser) [source: SEP-CT §1.6; SEP-HA §2.3.2].
- Discourse ethics vs feminist (Benhabib), communitarian (Taylor) and liberal (Rawls) critics [source: SEP-HA §3–4].
- Recognition vs redistribution (Fraser–Honneth) [source: IEP-RE §4].
- Psychoanalysis: pro-social (Honneth, Winnicott) vs negativistic, death-drive readings (Allen, Whitebook, McAfee) [source: SEP-CT §1.7].
- Eurocentrism of progress narratives (Allen, Dussel, Bhambra) [source: SEP-CT §4.1.3].

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use

- The page number of "Enlightenment is totalitarian" in Jephcott: SEP-AD gives p. 5 (probably 4–5), SEP-CT gives p. 50 — do not give a page.
