## 1. What an argument is

- IEP-Arg (McKeon): an argument in the technical sense is "a collection of truth-bearers (that is, the things that bear truth and falsity, or are true and false) some of which are offered as reasons for one of them, the conclusion." The reasons are "premises". This differs from "argument" as a dispute or fight, and from the formal logician's sense "according to which an argument is merely a list of statements, one of which is designated as the conclusion and the rest of which are designated as premises regardless of whether the premises are offered as reasons." [source: IEP-Arg]
- Arguments are distinct from implications and inferences: "frogs are mammals" implies "frogs are not reptiles", but asserting an implication is not offering a reason; an inference is "a form of reasoning", a "movement of mind", distinct from the argument (a collection of propositions). [source: IEP-Arg]
- Structural characterization: "A collection of propositions, P1, …, Pn, C, is an argument if and only if there is a reasoner R who puts forward the Pi as reasons in support of C." The structure "is imposed on these propositions by the intentions of a reasoner." [source: IEP-Arg §1]
- Indicator words (IEP-Arg): "Typical premise indicators include: 'because', 'since', 'for', and 'as'; typical conclusion indicators include 'therefore', 'thus', 'hence', and 'so'. Note well: these expressions do not always function in these ways, and so their mere use does not necessitate the presence of an argument." [source: IEP-Arg §1] (Periergia's *What Is Philosophy?* already gives "because"/"since" and "therefore"/"so".)
- Standard form: premises listed first, a solid line, conclusion prefaced by "∴" ("therefore"). Linked premises support the conclusion only jointly (example: "Tom is happy only if he is playing guitar. Tom is not playing guitar. ∴ Tom is not happy."); convergent premises each support it independently ("Tom said that he didn't go to Samantha's party." / "No one at Samantha's party saw Tom there." ∴ "Tom did not attend"). An extended argument has a premise that is itself argued for (keys example: "I just searched the kitchen and I did not find the keys" ∴ "The keys are not in the kitchen"; with "The keys are either in the kitchen or the bedroom" ∴ "The keys are in the bedroom"). Imperatives ("Let's look there!") are not truth-apt and so not components. [source: IEP-Arg §1]
- Argument vs explanation: IEP-Arg's example: "A: Why did this metal expand? B: It was heated and all metals expand when heated." — explanatory reasons for the explanandum. One criticism of structural definitions is that they turn explanations into arguments; some (Thomas 1986) bite the bullet and hold all explanations are arguments. [source: IEP-Arg §1]
- Pragmatic approach: an argument is offered "in order to rationally persuade an audience of the truth of the conclusion" (Johnson 2000; Walton 1996; Hitchcock 2007). Explaining aims to increase comprehension of something already accepted; arguing aims to make a standpoint acceptable that is not yet accepted (van Eemeren & Grootendorst 1992; Snoeck Henkemans 2001). "It is generally accepted that the same line of reasoning can function as an explanation in one dialogical context and as an argument in another." [source: IEP-Arg §2]
- Doctor/paresis example: explaining a patient's paresis by past syphilis is an explanation, not an argument, because syphilis does not make paresis "more likely than not". [source: IEP-Arg §1]
- Exploratory arguments: arguments can be used for inquiry, not only persuasion (Meiland 1989; Johnson & Blair). [source: IEP-Arg §1]
- SEP-Argn (Dutilh Novaes): "argument" and "argumentation" have an action–product ambiguity; the entry uses "argumentation" for the activity (which can be "persuasive, heated, or multi-agent") and "argument" for the product (which can be "valid, sound, inductive, or defeasible"). An argument "can be defined as a complex symbolic structure where some parts, known as the premises, offer support to another part, the conclusion." [source: SEP-Argn §1]
- SEP-Argn classifies argument types as deduction, induction, abduction and analogy (plus fallacies). Analogy: "if two things are (relevantly) similar, what is true of one of them is likely to be true of the other as well (Bartha 2010)". [source: SEP-Argn §2]

## 2. Deductive vs inductive (ampliative)

- SEP-LC: "Some arguments are such that the (joint) truth of the premises is necessarily sufficient for the truth of the conclusions." That "necessary sufficiency" distinguishes deductive from inductive validity; in inductively valid arguments the truth of the premises is "very likely (but not necessarily) sufficient". Example: "All swans observed so far have been white. Smoothy is a swan. Therefore, Smoothy is white." — not deductively valid: "Smoothy may well be a black swan." [source: SEP-LC §1]
- IEP-Arg: "A deductive argument is an argument that an arguer puts forward as valid"; "An inductive argument is an argument that an arguer puts forward as inductively strong" (premises would make it "unlikely, although possible, that the conclusion is false"). Example: "97% of the Republicans in town Z voted for McX, Jones is a Republican in town Z; therefore, Jones voted for McX." "It is inappropriate to criticize an inductively strong argument for being invalid." "Charity dictates that an invalid argument which is inductively strong be evaluated as an inductive argument unless there is clear evidence to the contrary." [source: IEP-Arg §3]
- Conductive arguments (Govier 2010) proposed as a third category: premises convergent, counting separately ("It most likely won't rain tomorrow. The sky is red tonight. Also, the weather channel reported a 30% chance of rain"). [source: IEP-Arg §3]
- IEP-DI (Shanahan): textbook terms — deductive arguments are valid/invalid, sound/unsound; inductive ones strong/weak and "cogent" (strong with true premises). Its examples: "Socrates is a man. All men are mortal. Therefore, Socrates is mortal." (deductive); "Most Greeks eat olives. Socrates is a Greek. Therefore, Socrates eats olives." (inductive). [source: IEP-DI §1]
- IEP-DI history: Aristotle distinguished syllogistic reasoning (*sullogismos*) from "reasoning from particulars to universals" (*epagôgê*); Francis Bacon's *New Organon* (1620) promoted induction; Descartes's *Discourse on the Method* (1637) favoured deduction; Einstein's essay "Induction and Deduction in Physics" (1919). [source: IEP-DI §1]
- DEBATE: IEP-DI argues there is "no consensus about how to draw the supposedly straightforward deductive-inductive argument distinction": proposals range from psychological (arguer's intentions/beliefs; e.g. Olson 1975), behavioural (what the arguer claims), "purport", evidential completeness, necessity vs probability, to formal criteria; each has problems. Example: "Dom Pérignon is a champagne; so, it is made in France" is deductive or inductive depending on the arguer's belief, on the psychological view. Shanahan concludes the distinction "should be dispensed with entirely" in favour of an "evaluative approach" (Skyrms 1975; Bergmann, Moor and Nelson 1998): ask whether the argument is valid; if so whether sound; if not, whether strong; if so whether cogent. [source: IEP-DI §§1–2, 10]
- Govier (1987) called the two-kinds view "the positivist theory of argument" and criticised "the hallowed old distinction between inductive and deductive arguments"; she wrote that "Few arguers are so considerate as to give us a clear indication" of which kind they intend. [source: IEP-DI §§1, 3] (Periergia's *What Is Philosophy?* already quotes "the hallowed old distinction".)
- Monotonicity: SEP-Argn: deductive arguments are monotonic — "if premises A and B deductively imply conclusion C, then the addition of any arbitrary premise D will not invalidate the argument"; this differentiates them from inductive and abductive arguments. [source: SEP-Argn §2.1]
- "Ampliative": the term is used in Periergia's *What Is Philosophy?* (induction and abduction are "ampliative": conclusions go beyond premises). I did not find the word in SEP-LC or IEP-DI wording during this session; the idea (conclusion "goes beyond its premise") is in SEP-LF on inference (6): "the conclusion of (6) goes beyond its premise". [source: SEP-LF intro]

## 3. Validity: definitions

- IEP-VS: "A deductive argument is said to be valid if and only if it takes a form that makes it impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion nevertheless to be false. Otherwise, a deductive argument is said to be invalid." [source: IEP-VS]
- IEP-VS: "A deductive argument is sound if and only if it is both valid, and all of its premises are actually true. Otherwise, a deductive argument is unsound." [source: IEP-VS]
- IEP-VS: "an argument is valid if the truth of the premises logically guarantees the truth of the conclusion." Premises need not be actually true for validity. [source: IEP-VS]
- IEP-VS examples: valid: "Elizabeth owns either a Honda or a Saturn. Elizabeth does not own a Honda. Therefore, Elizabeth owns a Saturn." Valid but unsound: "All toasters are items made of gold. All items made of gold are time-travel devices. Therefore, all toasters are time-travel devices." Sound: "In some states, no felons are eligible voters... In those states, some professional athletes are felons. Therefore, in some states, some professional athletes are not eligible voters." [source: IEP-VS]
- IEP-VS: sound arguments always have true conclusions (not by definition but because of form + true premises). "both invalid, as well as valid but unsound, arguments can nevertheless have true conclusions. One cannot reject the conclusion of an argument simply by discovering a given argument for that conclusion to be flawed." "all invalid arguments are unsound." [source: IEP-VS]
- IEP-VS warns of a different technical use: in mathematical logic a formula is "valid" if true under all admissible interpretations, and a calculus is "sound" if all its theorems are semantically valid. [source: IEP-VS]
- SEP-CL (Shapiro): "An argument is valid if there is no interpretation (in the semantics) in which its premises are all true and its conclusion false. This reflects the longstanding view that a valid argument is truth-preserving." Also: "an argument is valid if it is not possible for its premises to all be true and its conclusion false." [source: SEP-CL]
- SEP-LC: contemporary analyses take logical consequence "to be both necessary and formal"; its summary formula: "logical consequence as necessary truth preservation in virtue of form." Opening example: "If we charge high fees for university, only the rich will enroll. We charge high fees for university. Therefore, only the rich will enroll." — valid "if we do not equivocate". [source: SEP-LC intro, §3.1]
- SEP-Argn: "Valid deductive arguments are those where the truth of the premises necessitates the truth of the conclusion: the conclusion cannot but be true if the premises are true... A valid argument whose premises are also true is a sound argument." Gloss: "in all possible worlds where the premises hold, the conclusion will also hold." [source: SEP-Argn §2.1]
- SEP-LF: in valid inferences "a thinker takes no epistemic risk by endorsing the conditional claim that the conclusion is true if the premises are true... Any risk of error lies with the premises, as opposed to the reasoning." Examples of secure inferences: "(1) John danced if Mary sang, and Mary sang; so John danced." "(3) The detective is in the garden; so someone is in the garden." Risky: "(4) John danced if Mary sang, and John danced; so Mary sang." "(5) Every feathered biped is a bird, and Tweety is a feathered biped; so Tweety can fly." [source: SEP-LF intro]
- Which kind of necessity? SEP-LC: metaphysical impossibility of true premises/false conclusion is "plausibly ... a necessary condition" but on most accounts "not a sufficient condition": "x is water. Therefore, x is H2O" is necessarily truth-preserving (if Kripkean a posteriori necessities are accepted) but "seems a long way from being deductively valid" since it took empirical discovery. Conceptual/analytic necessity runs into Quine's critique of the analytic/synthetic distinction; apriority does not exclude "Peter is Greg's mother's brother's son. Therefore, Peter is Greg's cousin." [source: SEP-LC §1]
- Model theory: the modern model-theoretic definition "traces back to Tarski (1936)", building on his 1935 truth definition: "an argument is valid if in any model in which the premises are true... the conclusion is true too." Called "one of the most successful mathematical explications of a philosophical concept to date." Etchemendy (1990) distinguished representational and interpretational readings of models and raised problems for each. [source: SEP-LC §3.1]
- Ancient definitions: Aristotle's deduction (*sullogismos*): "speech (*logos*) in which, certain things having been supposed, something different from those supposed results of necessity because of their being so" (Prior Analytics I.2, 24b18–20, Robin Smith's translation). Smith: "This corresponds to a modern notion of logical consequence." Premise = *protasis*; conclusion = *sumperasma*; "results of necessity" = *ex anankês sumbainein*. [source: SEP-AL §5.1] (The *Aristotle* chapter already quotes this definition.)
- Stoic definitions: an argument is "a system of at least two premises and a conclusion" (D.L. 7.45); every premise but the first introduced by "now" or "but", conclusion by "therefore". "An argument is valid if the (Chrysippean) conditional formed with the conjunction of its premises as antecedent and its conclusion as consequent is correct (S. E. PH 2.137; D. L. 7.77). An argument is 'sound' (literally: 'true'), when in addition to being valid it has true premises." [source: SEP-AncLog §5.3] — i.e., the valid/sound distinction is Stoic in origin.

## 4. The counterexample method

- SEP-LC: "The model-centered approach to logical consequence takes the validity of an argument to be absence of counterexample." One way to give a counterexample "is to provide an argument of the same form for which the premises are clearly true and the conclusion is clearly false. Another way ... is to provide a circumstance in which the premises are true and the conclusion is false." [source: SEP-LC §3.1]
- Aristotle used it: "Aristotle proves invalidity by constructing counterexamples. This is very much in the spirit of modern logical theory: all that it takes to show that a certain form is invalid is a single instance of that form with true premises and a false conclusion." He gave pairs of term-triplets (one making premises and a universal affirmative "conclusion" true, the other a universal negative). [source: SEP-AL §5.3]
- IEP-VS invalid-form examples: "All basketballs are round. The Earth is round. Therefore, the Earth is a basketball." and "All popes reside at the Vatican. John Paul II resides at the Vatican. Therefore, John Paul II is a pope." — same invalid form "All A's are F; X is F; Therefore, X is an A"; the second has true premises and a true conclusion but is still invalid. [source: IEP-VS]
- WP Affirming the consequent counterexample: "If someone lives in San Diego, then they live in California" + "lives in California" does not give San Diego. [source: WP-Affirming_the_consequent]
- Parody / same-form method in a historical case: SEP-Ont formalises Gaunilo's "perfect island" parody of Anselm's *Proslogion* II argument step for step, and states: "Since the arguments have the same logical form, either they are both valid or they are both invalid. If they are both invalid, then the Proslogion II argument fails. If they are both valid, then, if the Proslogion II argument is to succeed, there must be a premise pair concerning which atheists and agnostics suppose that the premise in the first argument is true but the premise in the second argument is false." Also: "particular parodies are tied to particular formulations"; if islands do not admit of maximal greatness the parody can be rerun with "that score on this test than which no greater score on this test can be conceived". [source: SEP-Ont §11] (The *Early Scholasticism* chapter already tells the Gaunilo story; cross-reference it.)
- Note: "counterexample" in *What Is Philosophy?* is used for counterexamples to universal claims (Gettier). This chapter's use — a counterexample to an argument FORM — is distinct; the writer should flag the difference.

## 5. Logical form; formal vs material validity

- IEP-VS: "according to the dominant understanding among logicians, the validity or invalidity of an argument is determined entirely by its logical form. The logical form of an argument is that which remains of it when one abstracts away from the specific content of the premises and the conclusion, that is, words naming things, their properties and relations, leaving only those elements that are common to discourse and reasoning about any subject matter, that is, words such as 'all,' 'and,' 'not,' 'some,' and so forth." Represented by replacing content words with letters. [source: IEP-VS]
- IEP-VS same-form example: "All tigers are mammals. No mammals are creatures with scales. Therefore, no tigers are creatures with scales." and "All spider monkeys are elephants. No elephants are animals. Therefore, no spider monkeys are animals." share "All A are B; No B are C; Therefore, No A are C" — both valid; first sound, second unsound. [source: IEP-VS]
- Dissent (IEP-VS): "My table is circular. Therefore, it is not square shaped." and "Juan is a bachelor. Therefore, he is not married." seem impossible to have true premise/false conclusion, but surface form "x is F; therefore x is not G" is not valid. Responses: add implicit premises ("controversial"), call the necessity non-logical, or reanalyse the form ("x is F and not G and H"). [source: IEP-VS]
- Grammatical form misleads: "Tony is a ferocious tiger" vs "Clinton is a lame duck" (only the first licenses "is a tiger"); "The King and Queen are visiting dignitaries" is ambiguous in form. Hence logicians use "artificial logical languages in which logical form and grammatical form coincide" — "formal logic" or "symbolic logic". [source: IEP-VS]
- SEP-LC formal vs material: "Peter is Greg's mother's brother's son. Therefore, Peter is Greg's cousin" is "a material consequence and not a formal one, because to make the step ... we need more than the structure or form of the claims involved: we need to understand their contents too." Formality is "the strongest and most widespread proposal" for narrowing logical consequence. [source: SEP-LC §2]
- SEP-LC: inference schemes display form; "Aristotle's syllogistic is a proud example" (Ferio: No F is G; Some H is G; therefore some H is not F). But schemes alone don't define formality: "x is y's mother's brother's son; therefore x is y's cousin" is a scheme every instance of which is valid; something must say which schemes are "properly formal" — the problem of logical constants. [source: SEP-LC §2]
- Three proposals for what formality is (SEP-LC §2): (a) total generality, e.g. Tarski's proposal (1986) that an operation is logical if invariant under permutations of objects (identity passes; "mother of" fails; negation passes, "JC believes that" fails); (b) total abstractness — logical words add structure, not content; (c) logic as constitutive norms for thought regardless of subject matter. [source: SEP-LC §2]
- SEP-LF: "One ancient idea is that impeccable inferences exhibit patterns that can be characterized schematically by abstracting away from the specific contents"; the Stoics used ordinal numbers for such patterns: "If the first then the second, and the first; so the second" etc. [source: SEP-LF §1]
- SEP-LF: Frege and Russell argued natural-language grammar "misleads us about the structure of the propositions we assert"; Russell's "The present king of France is bald" analysed as ∃x{K(x) ∧ ∀y[K(y) → y = x] ∧ B(x)} — false, not meaningless; Wittgenstein's *Tractatus* developed the idea. "the most common suggestion has been that certain inferences are secure by virtue of their logical form." [source: SEP-LF intro, §§4–5] Cross-ref *Frege, Russell and the Birth of Analytic Philosophy*.
- DEBATE (whether validity is purely formal): mainstream (IEP-VS "dominant understanding"; SEP-LC "necessary and formal") vs. dissenters and puzzles: material inferences (bachelor/unmarried; cousin), a posteriori necessities (water/H2O), the unsettled demarcation of logical constants (SEP-LC §2; Tarski "left the question of a principled" demarcation open — SEP-LC §3). [sources: IEP-VS; SEP-LC §§1–3]

## 6. Classical valid forms (with standard examples)

- Modus ponens (MP), *modus ponendo ponens*, "mode that by affirming affirms": If P then Q; P; therefore Q. [source: WP-Modus_ponens] In natural deduction it is the conditional-elimination rule ("This elimination rule is sometimes called 'modus ponens'"). [source: SEP-CL] Stoic first indemonstrable: "an argument that concludes from a conditional and its antecedent the consequent" (S.E. M 8.224–5; D.L. 7.80–1). [source: SEP-AncLog §5.4] Stoic example: "If it is day, it is light" (mode "If the 1st, the 2nd"). [source: SEP-AncLog §5.3]
- Modus tollens (MT), *modus tollendo tollens*, "mode that by denying denies": If P then Q; not Q; therefore not P. WP example: "If the dog detects an intruder, the dog will bark. The dog did not bark. Therefore, no intruder was detected." [source: WP-Modus_tollens] Stoic second indemonstrable: "concludes from a conditional and the contradictory of the consequent the contradictory of the antecedent"; Bobzien's example: "If it is day, it is not the case that it is night. But it is night. Therefore it is not the case that it is day." and the mode example "If it is day, it is light. But it is not the case that it is light. Therefore it is not the case that it is day." [source: SEP-AncLog §§5.3–5.4]
- Hypothetical syllogism (pure/"wholly hypothetical"): If P then Q; if Q then R; therefore if P then R. Theophrastus is credited with a system of "wholly hypothetical" syllogisms; in later antiquity, "possibly under Stoic influence", they were read as "If p, then q. If q, then r. Therefore, if p, then r." [source: SEP-AncLog §3.4; WP-Hypothetical_syllogism]
- Disjunctive syllogism (*modus tollendo ponens*, "mode that affirms by denying"): P or Q; not P; therefore Q. WP example: "I will choose soup or I will choose salad. I will not choose soup. Therefore, I will choose salad." [source: WP-Disjunctive_syllogism] = Stoic fifth indemonstrable ("from a disjunction and the contradictory of one of its disjuncts the other disjunct"). [source: SEP-AncLog §5.4]
- Constructive dilemma: If P then Q; if R then S; P or R; therefore Q or S — "the disjunctive version of modus ponens"; destructive dilemma is the disjunctive version of modus tollens. [source: WP-Constructive_dilemma]
- Reductio ad absurdum (proof by contradiction, indirect proof): establish a claim by showing its denial leads to absurdity or contradiction. WP examples: no smallest positive rational number (if q were smallest, q/2 would be smaller); Euclid *Elements* I.6 uses it; Plato's early dialogues turned refutation into the Socratic elenchus (Vlastos's analysis: interlocutor's thesis + agreed premises → contrary of thesis). In intuitionistic logic proof by contradiction is not universally valid. [source: WP-Reductio_ad_absurdum] Aristotle used "proof by impossibility" (reductio) to prove syllogistic moods (e.g. Baroco/Bocardo; "the contradictory of the desired conclusion" assumed). [source: SEP-AL §§5.2, 5.4] Stoic first *thema* is "an inference rule of the kind today called antilogism". [source: SEP-AncLog §5.4] Anselm's argument is usually read as a reductio (Periergia *Early Scholasticism*).
- Ex falso quodlibet ("explosion"): "anything at all follows from a pair of contradictory opposites"; in classical logic it is a derived rule. [source: SEP-CL]
- History of MP/MT: Theophrastus and Eudemus developed arguments "mixed from a hypothetical premise and a probative premise" which "were forerunners of modus ponens and modus tollens", in quantified form: "If something is F, it is G. a is F. Therefore, a is G." / "... a is not G. Therefore, a is not F." (Theophrastus frs. 111–112 Fortenbaugh). [source: SEP-AncLog §3.3] (WP-Modus_ponens says flatly that Theophrastus was "the first to explicitly describe" MP; SEP is more cautious: "forerunners". Use SEP's wording.)

## 7. Invalid forms (formal fallacies)

- Affirming the consequent: If P then Q; Q; therefore P. IEP-Fal (Dowden) definition: "If you have enough evidence to affirm the consequent of a conditional and then suppose that as a result you have sufficient reason for affirming the antecedent, your reasoning contains the Fallacy of Affirming the Consequent. This formal fallacy is often mistaken for Modus Ponens." Example: "If she's Brazilian, then she speaks Portuguese. Hey, she does speak Portuguese. So, she is Brazilian." [source: IEP-Fal]
- Denying the antecedent: If P then Q; not P; therefore not Q. IEP-Fal: "often mistaken for Modus Tollens". Example: "If she were Brazilian, then she would know that Brazil's official language is Portuguese. She isn't Brazilian; she's from London. So, she surely doesn't know this about Brazil's language." [source: IEP-Fal]
- SEP-LF's inference (4) "John danced if Mary sang, and John danced; so Mary sang" is affirming the consequent ("John might dance whenever Mary sings, but also sometimes when Mary doesn't sing"). [source: SEP-LF]
- Aristotle's "fallacy of the consequent" (Sophistical Refutations 5): "if A is, B necessarily is, men also fancy that, if B is, A necessarily is" (SR 5 169b3); example: it does not follow that "a man who is hot must be in a fever because a man who is in a fever is hot" (SR 5 169b19). "This fallacy is sometimes claimed as being an early statement of the formal fallacy of affirming the consequent." [source: SEP-Fal]
- Undistributed middle-type form "All A's are F; X is F; therefore X is an A" (IEP-VS basketball/pope examples) is invalid. [source: IEP-VS]
- Stoic note: the Stoic fourth indemonstrable ("Either p or q; p; therefore not q") is valid only because Stoic disjunction is exclusive; with inclusive "or" it is invalid. Theophrastus "recognized that the connective particle 'or' can be inclusive" (fr. 82A). [source: SEP-AncLog §§3.3, 5.4] (Hellenistic chapter already flags "Stoic 'or' excludes both".)
- SEP-IL example of AC in real life: a vodka advertisement analysed as an argument with "an inference which is an instance of the fallacy 'Affirming the Consequent'". [source: SEP-IL §3.1]

## 8. Aristotle's syllogistic (brief; cross-ref *Aristotle*)

- "Aristotle's logical works contain the earliest formal study of logic that we have." [source: SEP-AL §1]
- Four categorical forms (Smith's table): Every S is P (universal affirmative); No S is P (universal negative); Some S is P (particular affirmative); Some S is not P / Not every S is P (particular negative); plus "indefinite" forms. Traditional letters A, E, I, O (Smith's abbreviations Aab, Eab, Iab, Oab, predicate first). [source: SEP-AL §4.3]
- "In the terminology traditional since the middle ages, each of these combinations is known as a mood (Latin modus, 'way'...)". Medieval mnemonic names "are still widely used, and each is actually a mnemonic for Aristotle's proof of the mood". First-figure moods: Barbara (Aab, Abc ⊢ Aac), Celarent, Darii, Ferio — "Perfect". [source: SEP-AL §5.4]
- Prior Analytics I.4–6: Aristotle "systematically investigates all possible combinations of two premises in each of the three figures... The results he states are correct." He reduces all moods ultimately to Barbara and Celarent. [source: SEP-AL §§5.1, 5.5] (*Aristotle* chapter: "fourteen combinations yield a conclusion of necessity".)
- Kant "even held that nothing significant had been added to Aristotle's views in the intervening two millennia"; Aristotle's logic, not Chrysippus's, was transmitted to Arabic and Latin medieval traditions; "the works of Chrysippus have not survived". [source: SEP-AL §1]
- Consistency warning: the *Aristotle* chapter (and research notes ancient--aristotle.md) record that "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal" is NOT Aristotle's example (traced to J. S. Mill) and that Aristotle's syllogistic seems to exclude singular sentences. IEP-DI uses the Socrates example without attribution — the writer may use it as a textbook example but must not call it Aristotle's.

## 9. Stoic propositional logic (brief; cross-ref *Hellenistic Philosophy*)

- Diodorus Cronus and his pupil Philo (later 4th–mid 3rd c. BCE) "conceived of logic as a logic of propositions". Philo "may be credited with introducing truth-functionality into logic": a conditional "is false when and only when its antecedent is true and its consequent is false, and it is true in the three remaining truth-value combinations" — resembling material implication. Philo's and Diodorus's conditionals "lead to variants of the 'paradoxes' of material and strict implication—a fact the ancients were aware of (Sextus Empiricus M. 8.109–117)." [source: SEP-AncLog §4]
- Chrysippus (mid-3rd c. BCE), "the second major theorist of logic in antiquity", developed "a propositional logic, crowned by a deductive system". Said that "if the gods used any logic, it would be that of Chrysippus (D. L. 7.180)"; he "wrote over 300 books on logic". [source: SEP-AncLog §§1, 5] (Hellenistic chapter gives Chrysippus c. 280–206 BCE; keep consistent.)
- Stoic syllogistic: five kinds of indemonstrables plus four rules (*themata*); "An argument is a syllogism precisely if it either is an indemonstrable or can be reduced to one by means of the themata (D. L. 7.78)." "The Stoics explicitly acknowledged that there are valid arguments that are not syllogisms; but assumed that these could be somehow transformed into syllogisms." [source: SEP-AncLog §5.4]
- The five indemonstrables (meta-linguistic descriptions, S.E. M 8.224–5; D.L. 7.80–1): 1st from conditional + antecedent the consequent; 2nd from conditional + contradictory of consequent the contradictory of antecedent; 3rd from negated conjunction + one conjunct the contradictory of the other; 4th from disjunction + one disjunct the contradictory of the other; 5th from disjunction + contradictory of one disjunct the other. [source: SEP-AncLog §5.4]
- Bobzien: "The almost universal assumption among historians of logic that the Stoics represented their five (types of) indemonstrables by five modes is false and not supported by textual evidence." Modes used ordinal numbers ("If the 1st, the 2nd. But not: the 2nd. Therefore not: the 1st."). [source: SEP-AncLog §§5.3–5.4]
- Only the first and third themata survive; the first is an antilogism rule, the third a "cut-rule". [source: SEP-AncLog §5.4]

## 10. Indian inference: the Nyāya five-membered argument (brief; cross-ref *Classical Indian Schools*, *Logic across Traditions*)

- IEP-Ny (Dasti): "Concerning inference for polemical or didactic purposes, Nyāya employs a formal five-step argument illustrated by the following stock example. There is fire on the hill (the pratijñā, thesis). Because there is smoke on the hill (the hetu, reason or probans). Wherever there is smoke, there is fire; like a kitchen hearth and unlike a lake (the udāharaṇa, illustration of concomitance). This hill is likewise smoky (the upanaya, application of the rule). Thus, there is fire on the hill (the nigamana, conclusion)." [source: IEP-Ny]
- IEP-Ny: "In practice, the five-membered 'syllogism' is often truncated into three steps"; the inferential subject is the *pakṣa* (the hill). [source: IEP-Ny]
- Invariable concomitance = *vyāpti* ("pervasion"; also *a-vinā-bhāva*, "indispensability"): "one superstratum pervades another just in case wherever the second occurs the first occurs." [source: SEP-Ind] Faulty reasons = *hetvābhāsa*, "pseudo provers" or "logical defeaters". [source: IEP-Ny]
- Nyāya-sūtra by Gautama/Akṣapāda "(c. 2nd ce), the actual redaction of which is thought by some to date to the third century ce"; NS 1.1.32 takes the syllogism to have five parts; the *Caraka-saṃhitā* (CS 3.8.31) does too and gives the only early illustration (soul eternal / unproduced / like space). [source: SEP-Ind §3] (*What Is Philosophy?* already uses the Caraka example; this chapter should use the smoke/fire example to avoid repetition.)
- DEBATE (deductive or analogical?): Gillon: "The earliest known example of the canonical Indian syllogism is found in the Caraka-saṃhitā. The argument is clearly an analogical one. The earliest known deductive version of the syllogism is found in the Fāng biàn xīn lùn, or Upāya-hṛdaya ... A concise version of the deductive version is found in Vasubandhu's Vāda-vidhi." The example member names a particular case; later a universal statement ("wherever there is smoke...") is attached. [source: SEP-Ind §3.2]
- Dignāga distinguished "inference for oneself" (*svārtha-anumāna*, the cognitive process) and "inference for another" (*parārtha-anumāna*, its verbal expression); his canonical syllogism has thesis (*pakṣa*), reason and two examples (similar and dissimilar), but no application or conclusion. [source: SEP-Ind] (*Mahāyāna Philosophy* chapter covers Dignāga/Dharmakīrti's triple-mark reason; Tillemans warns formal validity is not discussed separately.)
- Nyāya did not sharply separate invalidity from false premises: both are *hetvābhāsa* (per Periergia *Classical Indian Schools*, consistent with IEP-Ny "Fallacies ... of false premises lead to hetv-ābhāsa").

## 11. Mohist disputation (*biàn*) (brief; cross-ref *Hundred Schools*)

- The Mohist Canons: by anonymous members of the Mohist school, Warring States period (479–221 B.C.), "Written and compiled most likely between the late 4th and mid 3rd century B.C." [source: SEP-MC]
- Disputation (*biàn* 辯) is "interpretable as 'dialectics,' 'distinction drawing,' 'discriminating,' or 'disputation'"; "comparable to the Greek notion of dialectics". Quoting the "Lesser Selection" (*Xiǎo Qǔ*): disputation clarifies "divisions between this (shì 是) and not-this (fēi 非)" and "points of sameness and difference". [source: SEP-MC §7]
- Fraser: "the Mohists' logical investigations focus on analogical reasoning, and indeed they tend to treat all argumentation as fundamentally analogical in nature. They do not investigate formal logic or deductive inference, nor formulate an explicit notion of logical consequence." They apply versions of excluded middle and non-contradiction, with notions of "permissibility" (*kě* 可) and "perversity" (*bèi* 悖). [source: SEP-MC §7]
- Four techniques of disputation in the Lesser Selection: analogy (*pì* 譬), "paralleling" (*móu* 侔), "pulling" (*yuán* 援: "You are so, how is it that I alone cannot be so?"), "pushing" (*tuī* 推). "Unlike a valid deductive argument, analogical distinction-drawing is not invariably reliable." [source: SEP-MC]
- Robbers example (form can mislead): critics argued "Robbers are people, so killing robbers is killing people"; the Lesser Selection replies these are cases of "this but not so": "robbers are people, but killing robbers is not killing people". A parallel: "White horses are horses" vs "blind horse" (a white horse is white all over; a blind horse is not blind all over). This shows the Mohists recognised that syntactically parallel sentences need not license parallel inferences — the same lesson as IEP-VS's "lame duck". [source: SEP-MC]
- The early Mohist "three standards" (origin/precedent of sage kings; what people see and hear; benefit in practice). [source: SEP-Moh] (Already in *What Is Philosophy?*.)

## 12. Reconstruction: charity, suppressed premises, mapping

- Principle of charity: "requires interpreting a speaker's statements in the most rational way possible and, in the case of any argument, considering its best, strongest possible interpretation". Simon Blackburn (Oxford Dictionary of Philosophy, 3rd ed. 2016, p. 79): "it constrains the interpreter to maximize the truth or rationality in the subject's sayings." [source: WP-Principle_of_charity]
- Named by Neil L. Wilson, "Substances without Substrata", *Review of Metaphysics* 12(4), June 1959, pp. 521–539 (WP: "gave the principle its name in 1958–59"); his use concerned the referent of a proper name ("Caesar"): "We select as designatum that individual which will make the largest possible number of Charles' statements true." [source: WP-Principle_of_charity]
- Quine (*Word and Object*, 1960) and Davidson gave other formulations. Davidson ("On the Very Idea of a Conceptual Scheme", 1974): "We make maximum sense of the words and thoughts of others when we interpret in a way that optimises agreement." [source: WP-Principle_of_charity] SEP-Dav (Malpas): Davidson's principle of charity ("he refers to it briefly elsewhere as the principle of 'rational accommodation'") "often appears in terms of the injunction to optimise agreement between ourselves and those we interpret", interpreting speakers as holding true beliefs (true by our lights) wherever plausible; it is needed because meaning and belief must be determined together. [source: SEP-Dav]
- Informal-logic version: SEP-IL: the principle of charity "favors an interpretation of an argument which makes the argument as credible as possible. In many situations, this can best be accomplished by assigning the argument a 'logically minimum' implicit premise, understood as the weakest component needed to successfully connect the argument's premises to its conclusion." [source: SEP-IL §3.1]
- Four versions listed by WP: the other uses words in the ordinary way; makes true statements; makes valid arguments; says something interesting (WP marks the source as unspecified "{{Who}}" — use cautiously). [source: WP-Principle_of_charity]
- Enthymeme (modern informal-logic sense): "An enthymeme is an argument which is presented with at least one component that is suppressed." IEP-Arg example: "You should believe that abortion is immoral. You're a Catholic" — suppressed premise "all Catholics should believe that abortion is immoral". "A premise that is suppressed is never a reason for a conclusion independent of another explicitly offered." [source: IEP-Arg §1] SEP-IL example: "The murderer was very strong, so George cannot be the murderer" assumes "George is not very strong". [source: SEP-IL §3.2]
- Aristotle's enthymeme: in the *Rhetoric* the enthymeme is "the body of proof", "the strongest of rhetorical proofs ... a kind of syllogism" (Rhet. I.1); paired with the example (*paradeigma*). [source: WP-Enthymeme]
- DEBATE (is Aristotle's enthymeme an incomplete syllogism?): Rapp: Rhet. I.2, 1357a7–18 (and II.22, 1395b24–26) say enthymemes often have few or fewer premises; this led to "the widespread understanding that Aristotle defines the enthymeme as a sullogismos in which one of two premises has been suppressed, i.e., as an abbreviated, incomplete syllogism. But certainly the passages mentioned do not attempt to give a definition of the enthymeme ... Properly understood, both passages are about the selection of appropriate premises, not about logical incompleteness." Long chains of deduction lose a public audience, "This is why Aristotle says that the enthymeme is and should be from fewer premises." Rapp also notes enthymemes from probabilities (*eikos*) and signs, and real vs fallacious enthymemes (Rhet. II.23–24). [source: SEP-AR §§6.4–6.5] So: "enthymeme = argument with a suppressed premise" is the later/modern sense; attributing that definition to Aristotle is contested.
- Argument mapping history: Richard Whately's *Elements of Logic* (1826) "gave probably the first form of an argument map" (a "Tree, or Logical Division"); John Henry Wigmore's evidence charts (*Principles of Judicial Proof*, 1913) for legal reasoning; Monroe Beardsley's *Practical Logic* (1950) gave a marking-up procedure (bracket and number statements; circle indicators; supply missing indicators; diagram with arrows) and the terms convergent, divergent and serial; Stephen N. Thomas (1973) introduced "linked"; Stephen Toulmin's *The Uses of Argument* (1958) gave another widely taught diagram. [source: WP-Argument_map; SEP-IL confirms Whately 1826 and Wigmore 1913]
- SEP-IL: informal logic standardises arguments with a "Key Component" table plus a diagram ("mapping"); implicit premises in square brackets; linked premises with "+". Toulmin (1958/2003) and Hamblin's *Fallacies* (1970) are "theoretical touchstones" for informal logic. [source: SEP-IL]

## 13. Paradoxes of material implication; ex falso; relevance logic

- Material implication p→q "is true whenever p is false or q is true". Paradoxes include p→(q→p) ("every proposition implies a true one"), ¬p→(p→q) ("a false proposition implies every proposition"), and (p→q)∨(q→r). Paradoxes of strict implication include (p&¬p)→q ("a contradiction strictly implies every proposition"). [source: SEP-RL intro]
- "Many philosophers, beginning with Hugh MacColl (1908), have claimed that these theses are counterintuitive." Relevance logicians locate the problem in irrelevance of antecedent to consequent. Classically valid but irrelevant: "The moon is made of green cheese. Therefore, either it is raining in Ecuador now or it is not." [source: SEP-RL intro]
- Variable-sharing principle: no A→B provable, and no inference valid, unless premises and conclusion share a propositional variable — a necessary, not sufficient, condition for a relevance logic. [source: SEP-RL intro]
- C. I. Lewis's argument for explosion: from p&¬p derive p, then p∨q (disjunction introduction), then ¬p, then q by disjunctive syllogism. "Mainstream relevance logicians block this argument by rejecting disjunctive syllogism. The rejection of disjunctive syllogism, however, has become one of the most controversial aspects of relevance logic." Neil Tennant's Core Logic accepts disjunctive syllogism. [source: SEP-RL §6]
- Anderson and Belnap's natural deduction system for relevant logic R. [source: SEP-RL §4] (Anderson & Belnap 1975 *Entailment* cited by SEP-CL.)
- SEP-CL: some logicians object to ex falso because the conclusion "may be irrelevant to any of the premises" (example: deriving both "A hot dog is a sandwich" and its negation — should we then deduce "The economy is sound"?). "Deductive systems that demur from ex falso quodlibet are called paraconsistent. Most relevant logics are paraconsistent." Dialetheists "hold that some contradictions are actually true". [source: SEP-CL]
- Wason task connection: WP notes some argue participants do not read "if... then" as the material conditional (Oaksford & Chater 1994; Stenning & van Lambalgen 2004). [source: WP-Wason_selection_task]

## 14. Validity vs persuasiveness; begging the question

- IEP-VS: a valid argument may have a false conclusion; flawed arguments can have true conclusions. [source: IEP-VS]
- IEP-Arg: on the structural account a reasoner must believe premises "independent of C"; "B: Bill will be at the party, because Bill will be at the party" is not an argument (fails independence), and if R wrongly believes a premise independent "R's argument would be defective by virtue of being circular". [source: IEP-Arg §1]
- IEP-Fal: begging the question is "A form of circular reasoning in which a conclusion is derived from premises that presuppose the conclusion"; "the arguer is essentially arguing by repeating the point." Example: Bullfighters Association president: "women shouldn't fight bulls because a bullfighter is and should be a man." "It is still an open question among logicians as to why some deductively valid arguments are considered to be begging the question and others are not." Some say the conclusion must be "psychologically new"; others look to the reasons for accepting the premises; others to burden of proof. [source: IEP-Fal]
- SEP-Fal: Whately's example of *petitio principii*: "to allow every man an unbounded freedom of speech must always be, on the whole, advantageous to the State; for it is highly conducive to the interest of the Community, that each individual should enjoy a liberty perfectly unlimited, of expressing his sentiments" — premise and conclusion "the very same proposition". "Neither of these versions of begging the question are faulted for their invalidity, so they are not charged with being non-sequitors". Aristotle discussed begging the question in *Prior Analytics* II.16. [source: SEP-Fal] → Begging the question = valid but useless; cross-ref *Informal Fallacies and Everyday Reasoning*.
- "Standard definition of fallacy" (Hamblin 1970): an argument "that seems to be valid but is not so"; Hansen notes it was less widely accepted than Hamblin implied. [source: SEP-Fal]
- Monotonicity/defeasibility and functions of argumentation (persuasion, theoretical, practical) are in SEP-Argn §§2–3.

## 15. Psychology of reasoning

- Wason selection task ("four-card problem"), devised by Peter Cathcart Wason in 1966 (chapter "Reasoning" in B. M. Foss ed., *New Horizons in Psychology*, Penguin 1966); standard journal citation Wason 1968, "Reasoning about a rule", *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology* 20(3): 273–281. "one of the most famous tasks in the study of deductive reasoning"; Manktelow (1999): "often been claimed to be the single most investigated experimental paradigm in the psychology of reasoning." The original used letters (vowels/consonants) and numbers (even/odd). [source: WP-Wason_selection_task]
- WP's version: cards show 3, 8, blue, red; rule "if a card shows an even number on one face, then its opposite face is blue"; correct answer: the 8 and the red card (MP and MT); turning 3 would be denying the antecedent, turning blue affirming the consequent. [source: WP-Wason_selection_task]
- "In Wason's study, not even 10% of subjects found the correct solution" (WP citing Wason 1977); replicated 1993 (Evans, Newstead & Byrne). Wason attributed errors partly to confirmation bias. [source: WP-Wason_selection_task] (Figure is WP's, from a secondary citation; present as "fewer than one in ten" with caution.)
- Content effects: Cosmides and Tooby (1992) found good performance when policing a social rule ("If you are drinking alcohol, then you must be over 18": check "16" and "drinking beer"), arguing for a cheater-detection mechanism; contested by Davies, Fetzer & Foster (1995), and others. [source: WP-Wason_selection_task]
- Johan van Benthem (2008) reports a psychologist saying he "had never met an experimental subject who did not understand the logical solution when it was explained to him, and then agreed that it was correct." [source: WP-Wason_selection_task]
- Wason: b. 22 April 1924, Bath; d. 17 April 2003, Wallingford; English psychologist. [source: WP-Peter_Cathcart_Wason]
- Belief bias: "the tendency to judge the strength of arguments based on the plausibility of their conclusion rather than how strongly they justify that conclusion" (Sternberg & Leighton 2004). Evans, Barston and Pollard, "On the conflict between logic and belief in syllogistic reasoning", *Memory & Cognition* 11(3): 295–306 (1983): participants tended "to reject valid arguments with unbelievable conclusions, and endorse invalid arguments with believable conclusions", while also showing logical competence (more acceptance of valid than invalid). [source: WP-Belief_bias; WS-3 confirms citation and that bias was stronger on invalid syllogisms]
- Mercier and Sperber, *The Enigma of Reason* (2017): interactionist account — reasoning's primary function is social (argumentation and "epistemic vigilance"); people "seem to be better at evaluating the quality of arguments proposed by others than at formulating high-quality arguments ourselves (Mercier 2018)". [source: SEP-Argn §5.3]

## 16. Worked examples for reconstruction (chosen from published Periergia chapters)

A. Anselm, *Proslogion* 2 (chapter *Early Scholasticism* exists, quotes Mann translation and SEP steps). SEP-Ont "first effort":
 (i) When the fool hears the words 'that than which no greater can be conceived' he understands those words. (Premise)
 (ii) Whatever is understood exists in the understanding. (Premise)
 (iii) (So) That than which no greater can be conceived exists in the understanding. (From i, ii)
 (iv) If that than which no greater can be conceived exists in the understanding, it can be conceived to exist in reality. (Premise)
 (v) That than which no greater can be conceived is greater if it exists in reality than if it exists only in the understanding. (Premise)
 (vi) It is impossible to conceive of something greater than that than which no greater can be conceived. (Premise)
 (vii) (So) That than which no greater can be conceived exists in reality. (From iii–vi)
 SEP-Ont: "The obvious difficulty with this representation is that it is not exactly clear how we are meant to get to (vii) from (iii), (iv), (v), and (vi)." Lesson: reconstruction exposes a gap needing a suppressed premise or reading as reductio; Gaunilo's island = same-form counterexample (see §4). Anselm's text quoted in *Early Scholasticism*: "that than which a greater cannot be conceived cannot be in the understanding alone" (Mann). Williams's alternative reconstruction (no "existence is greater" premise) is also in that chapter. [source: SEP-Ont §§6, 11]

B. Descartes's cogito as enthymeme? (*Descartes* chapter exists.) Reconstruction as syllogism: "everything that thinks exists; I think; so I exist" (Periergia *Descartes* chapter's gloss). Descartes, Second Replies (AT 7:140, CSM 2:100): "When someone says 'I am thinking, therefore I am, or I exist,' he does not deduce existence from thought by means of a syllogism, but recognizes it as something self-evident by a simple intuition of the mind." Margaret Wilson (1978, 56): "the claim that the cogito is an inference … is not equivalent to the claim that it is a syllogism." Hintikka (1962): performative, "existentially incoherent" to think "I do not exist". Newman: "modus ponens is self-evident, yet it contains an inference"; Curley: Descartes "consistently blurs the distinction between inferences and propositions"; Kenny: "what is from one point of view intuited is from another point of view deduced". Lesson: charity and supplying premises can distort an author's intent. [source: SEP-DesEp; WP-Cogito,_ergo_sum gives the Latin "existentiam ex cogitatione per syllogismum deducit"]

C. (Optional) Hume on miracles (*Berkeley and Hume* chapter exists, Section 10 of the *Enquiry*). SEP-HumeRel: Hume's "two related moves": evidence against a miracle "must always constitute a full proof — since we have uniform human experience in support of the laws of nature"; hence "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish" (SEP cites EU 11.13/116 — SEP-HumeRel's paragraph numbering; the Periergia chapter calls it Section 10; Section X "Of Miracles" is the standard section). This is a good prose-to-premises exercise, but the premises are evidential (not a deductive form); flag it as inductive/probabilistic.

D. Also usable: Mohist robbers argument (§11) as a case where parallel form misleads; IEP-Arg's keys example as an extended argument.

## Debates
- Is validity purely formal? Dominant view: yes (IEP-VS; SEP-LC "necessary and formal"); problems: material inferences (bachelor/unmarried; cousin), a posteriori necessities, and no agreed criterion for logical constants (Tarski's permutation-invariance proposal one candidate). [source: IEP-VS; SEP-LC §§1–2]
- Is there a coherent deductive/inductive distinction? Govier (1987); Shanahan (IEP-DI) proposes dropping it for an evaluative approach. [source: IEP-DI]
- Arguments vs explanations: structural vs pragmatic approaches; Thomas (1986): all explanations are arguments; same reasoning can be either depending on context. [source: IEP-Arg]
- Ex falso and disjunctive syllogism: classical logic accepts explosion; relevance logicians (Anderson and Belnap tradition) reject disjunctive syllogism to block Lewis's derivation — "one of the most controversial aspects of relevance logic"; Tennant's Core Logic keeps DS. [source: SEP-RL; SEP-CL]
- Aristotle's enthymeme: traditional "truncated syllogism" reading vs Rapp's view that Aristotle's "fewer premises" remark concerns premise selection for a lay audience, not logical incompleteness. [source: SEP-AR §6.4]
- Indian syllogism: analogical (Caraka) vs deductive versions (Upāya-hṛdaya, Vasubandhu), per Gillon; whether "logic" is the right word (Tillemans, per Periergia *Mahāyāna*). [source: SEP-Ind §3.2]
- Mohist logic: Fraser holds they treated argument as analogical and did not formulate logical consequence; they still "decisively refute" the claim that early Chinese thinkers ignored logic (Periergia *Hundred Schools*, quoting Fraser). [source: SEP-MC §7]
- Cogito: inference or intuition (Hintikka 1962 vs Wilson 1978; Newman, Curley, Kenny). [source: SEP-DesEp]
- Wason task: confirmation bias; content/social-exchange effects (Cosmides & Tooby 1992 vs Davies et al. 1995); whether subjects read "if" as material conditional (Oaksford & Chater 1994). [source: WP-Wason_selection_task]
- Function of reasoning: Mercier & Sperber's interactionist account. [source: SEP-Argn §5.3]
- Begging the question: why some valid arguments beg the question and others don't — psychological novelty vs dialectical context vs burden of proof. [source: IEP-Fal; SEP-Fal]
