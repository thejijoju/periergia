## 1. The problem and the map of positions
- "Free will" is "the canonical designator for a significant kind of control over one's actions"; the questions are whether it requires the freedom to do otherwise or the power of self-determination, and whether it is necessary for moral responsibility. [source: SEP-FW intro]
- Two common answers to "up to her in what sense?": (i) able to choose otherwise; (ii) she is the **source** of her action ("leeway" vs "sourcehood"). [source: SEP-FW §2.1]
- Many define free will as "the strongest control condition—whatever that turns out to be—necessary for moral responsibility" (Wolf 1990; Fischer 1994; Mele 2006). [source: SEP-FW §2.1]
- Responsibility as accountability: S deserves praise if the act goes beyond what can reasonably be expected, blame if it is morally wrong; Pereboom's **basic desert**: the agent "would deserve to be blamed or praised just because she has performed the action ... and not, for example, merely by virtue of consequentialist or contractualist considerations" (2014: 2). [source: SEP-FW §2.1; SEP-Skep §1]
- Positions (IEP's map): **compatibilists** (free will possible even if determinism true); **incompatibilists**, subdivided into **hard determinists** (determinism true, so no free will) and **libertarians** (determinism false, some agents free); also "free will deniers" and **pessimists** (free will impossible whether or not determinism holds; Broad 1952, G. Strawson 1994). Both compatibilism and incompatibilism are claims about possibility, not about whether we actually are free. [source: IEP-FW §3c]
- **Hard incompatibilism** (Pereboom 2001, 2014): "whatever the fundamental nature of reality, whether it is deterministic or indeterministic, we lack basic desert moral responsibility"; rejects both compatibilism and libertarianism. Hard determinism "has very few defenders today", largely because the standard interpretation of quantum mechanics casts doubt on universal determinism; Honderich still defends determinism. [source: SEP-Skep §§2.1–2.2]
- NB: IEP-FW calls Pereboom (2001) "a defense of hard determinism"; SEP-Skep (and Pereboom's own term) say **hard incompatibilism**. Use "hard incompatibilism". [source: IEP-FW §3c; SEP-Skep §2.2]
- Leeway incompatibilism (determinism removes the ability to do otherwise) vs source incompatibilism (determinism prevents one being the "ultimate source" of action). [source: SEP-Skep §2.1]
- Disputes about free will are "often a function of underlying disputes about the nature and value of moral responsibility". [source: SEP-FW §1.2]

## 2. Determinism, Laplace, fatalism (definitions)
- **Determinism** (Hoefer's "loose" definition): "Determinism is true of the world if and only if, given a specified way things are at a time t, the way things go thereafter is fixed as a matter of natural law." [source: SEP-Det §1]
- IEP version: "the thesis that the course of the future is entirely determined by the conjunction of the past and the laws of nature" (also "nomological determinism"); given P (state of the world, e.g. 100 million years ago) and L (laws), only one possible future. Determinism ≠ predictability. [source: IEP-FW §3a]
- Determinism is distinct from Leibniz's Principle of Sufficient Reason (PSR can be satisfied by God's will without physical determinism; and determinism leaves "Why were things just so at the beginning of time?" unanswered). [source: SEP-Det §1]
- **Fatalism vs determinism (Hoefer):** fatalism is "the thesis that all events (or in some versions, at least some events) are destined to occur no matter what we do"; the guarantee lies in "the will of the gods, or their divine foreknowledge, or some intrinsic teleological aspect of the universe", not in natural laws. One can have fate without deterministic laws and deterministic laws without anything fated. [source: SEP-Det §1]
- **Fatalism (Rice):** in ordinary use an attitude of resignation; philosophers use it for "the view that we are powerless to do anything other than what we actually do". Argued from logic/metaphysics ("logical fatalism"), from God ("theological fatalism"), or from causal determinism (then not usually called "fatalism"). [source: SEP-Fat intro]
- **Laplace.** The famous passage is in Laplace's *Essai philosophique sur les probabilités* (Wikipedia: "published in 1814"; SEP cites the 1820 edition, "Laplace 1820", with the Dover reprint of Truscott and Emory's English translation, 1951). Wikipedia says Laplace "first expressed it in 1773"; variations in Maupertuis (1756), Condorcet (1768), d'Holbach (1770); Boscovich proposed a similar image in 1758. Laplace "did not use the word 'demon'" (his word was *une intelligence*); "Laplace's Superman" is Reichenbach's label. [source: WP-Laplace's_demon; SEP-Det §1 and bibliography]
- Hoefer: "Laplace probably had God in mind as the powerful intelligence"; no finite or embedded intelligence could have the computing power to predict the actual future; chaos makes even limited prediction impossible for finite agents. [source: SEP-Det §1]
- Hoefer's vivid gloss: a demon who knew the world 100 years before my birth could predict every action and emotion of my life and "might smile condescendingly, as one who watches a marionette dance to the tugs of strings that it knows nothing about"; "We can't stand the thought that we are (in some sense) marionettes." [source: SEP-Det §1]
- Determinism "is a contingent matter" for most philosophers, so an empirical question. [source: IEP-FW §3b]

## 3. Logical fatalism: Aristotle's sea battle (*De Interpretatione* 9) — argument step by step
- Source: Aristotle (384–322 BCE), *De Interpretatione* ch. 9. Rice's reconstruction [source: SEP-Fat §1]:
  1. Either *p* is true or false, and either not-*p* is true or false; so *p* is true or not-*p* is true.
  2. In 1900 one person says a sea battle will take place on 1/1/2100; another says it will not.
  3. So one of them spoke truly.
  4. So either it was necessary in 1900 that a sea battle takes place on 1/1/2100, or necessary that it does not.
  5. The date of the prediction, and whether anyone made it, is irrelevant; so it is necessary at all times.
  6. Generalise: everything that happens, happens of necessity.
- "Necessity" here is not logical necessity but **inevitability**: no one has the power to prevent it. Aristotle accepts "What is, necessarily is, when it is; and what is not, necessarily is not, when it is not" (19a23–5), adding "But not everything that is, necessarily is". [source: SEP-Fat §1, §1.1]
- **Objection (the suspicious step):** the move from truth to necessity looks like the fallacy from "Necessarily (if p is true, p)" to "If p is true, necessarily p"; Rice thinks this suspicion "probably ill-founded" — the move more likely rests on a correspondence theory of truth plus the necessity of the present. [source: SEP-Fat §1]
- **Aristotle's solution (one reading; "more than one view")**: deny that the affirmation or negation of future contingents is already true — they are neither true nor false, i.e. reject **bivalence** for them. Another reading: he rejects the move from truth to necessity. [source: SEP-Fat §1.1]
- Aristotle also says that if everything happened of necessity "there would be no need to deliberate or to take trouble" (18b31–3). [source: SEP-Fat §7]
- IEP's "logical determinism" version: "Allison will take the dog for a walk next Thursday" was already true a million years ago; replies: show freedom compatible with future truths, or deny bivalence/excluded middle for future free actions. [source: IEP-FW §6b]
- Cross-ref *Formal Logic*: Łukasiewicz's three-valued logic (published chapter) was prompted by the sea battle.

## 4. The Idle (Lazy) Argument and the Stoics (cross-ref *Hellenistic Philosophy*)
- **Idle Argument** step by step [source: SEP-Fat §7, after Bobzien 1998]: (1) If it is fated that you will recover from this illness, you will recover whether or not you consult a doctor. (2) If it is fated that you will not recover, you will not recover whether or not you consult a doctor. (3) Either it is fated that you recover or that you do not. (4) So it is futile to consult a doctor.
- **Chrysippus's reply:** the conclusion does not follow, "because it may have been fated that you will recover as a result of seeing the doctor"; Rice: the reply "seems exactly right". [source: SEP-Fat §7] O'Keefe: Chrysippus distinguishes **simple** outcomes (e.g. that I will die, whatever I do) from **conjoined** ones (Oedipus's birth is conjoined with Laius and Jocasta having intercourse); recovery and seeking treatment are **"co-fated"** (Cicero, *On Fate* 30, LS 55S); "even if it's true ... that somebody will wrestle, it doesn't follow that they'll wrestle whether or not they have an opponent". [source: SEP-Anc §1.4]
- Stoic fate: an everlasting "ordering and sequence of causes"; "not the 'fate' of superstition, but that of physics" (Cicero, *On Divination* 1.125–6, LS 55L). God is the world, works like a craftsman within physical limits; God is "extremely powerful but not omnipotent". [source: SEP-Anc §1.4]
- Chrysippus (*On Providence* 4): fate is "a certain natural, everlasting ordering of the whole"; the interconnection "is inviolable". [source: IEP-Chr §4d]
- Chrysippus's dates: c. 280–206 BCE (SEP-Fat); c. 280–207 BCE (IEP-Chr); "If Chrysippus had not existed, neither would the Stoa." [source: SEP-Fat §7; IEP-Chr intro]
- The Stoics say actions brought about "through the animal" are "in the power" of the animal, but deny that we are free to choose between opposites (Alexander, *On Fate* ch. 13, LS 62G). [source: SEP-Anc §1.4]
- **Cylinder and cone/top** (Cicero, *On Fate* 42–3; LS 62C): "[Chrysippus] resorts to his cylinder and cone: these cannot begin to move without a push; but once that has happened, he holds that it is thereafter through their own nature that the cylinder rolls and the cone spins. 'Hence', he says, 'just as the person who pushed the cylinder gave it its beginning of motion but not its capacity for rolling, likewise, although the impression encountered will print and, as it were, emblazon its appearance on the mind, assent will be in our power.'" (SEP-Sto, LS translation). IEP-Chr's version reads "cylinder and spinning-top". The push is an "auxiliary and proximate" cause; the shape is responsible for rolling vs spinning; SEP adds that, given push and nature, "each solid could not do otherwise". SEP's worked example: a glutton and a non-glutton offered the same slice of carrot cake. [source: SEP-Sto §2.8; IEP-Chr §4d]
- **Dog tied to a cart** (Hippolytus, *Refutation of All Heresies* 1.21; LS 62A): "When a dog is tied to a cart, if it wants to follow it is pulled and follows, making its spontaneous act coincide with necessity, but if it does not want to follow it will be compelled in any case. So it is with men too: even if they do not want to, they will be compelled in any case to follow what is destined." Hippolytus attributes it to "Zeno and Chrysippus". [source: SEP-Anc §1.4; IEP-Chr §4d]
  - **Authorship disputed:** Bobzien argues it is by neither Zeno nor Chrysippus but comes from the Roman Stoa, probably interpreting verses of Cleanthes, and is only about the futility of revolting against fate (IEP gives "2001 354"). O'Keefe: as deployed by Chrysippus the "outcomes fixed whatever we decide" reading is "probably incorrect"; more likely the point is that resisting providence is futile and makes one miserable. [source: IEP-Chr §4d; SEP-Anc §1.4]
- SEP-FW: Chrysippus held actions "up to you" when they come about "through you" — when the determining factors are your own choices, not external circumstances; "the issue is not whether one's choices are determined (they are) but in what manner they are determined." [source: SEP-FW §1.1]
- Ancient vocabulary: no phrase that "should obviously be translated as 'free will'"; the Greeks ask what is "up to us" (*eph' hēmin*) or "depends on us" (*par' hēmas*). Lucretius's *libera voluntas* is often translated "free will", but whether it means that "is far from obvious". [source: SEP-Anc §2]
- Origin debate: Dihle finds the concept of free will first in Augustine; Michael Frede (2011) in the Stoic Epictetus (c. 55–c. 135 CE). [source: SEP-FW §1.1]

## 5. Epicurus and the swerve; Carneades; Alexander
- Epicurus wanted to avoid being "a slave to the 'fate' of the natural philosophers ... which involves an inexorable necessity" (*Letter to Menoeceus* 134, LS 20A); better to believe the old myths. [source: SEP-Anc §1.2; SEP-Epi]
- Epicurus and the Stoics both held bivalence about the future and causal determinism to be inter-entailing; Epicurus rejects both, the Stoics accept both. [source: SEP-Anc §§1.2, 1.4]
- **Swerve:** an occasional minute sideways atomic motion "at no fixed region of space or fixed time" (*DRN* 2.251–93, LS 20F, as quoted by O'Keefe); SEP-Luc paraphrases "at no specific time or place" (216–93). Not mentioned in Epicurus's extant texts; attested in later sources (Lucretius, Cicero *On Fate* 22). [source: SEP-Anc §§1.2, 2.4; SEP-Luc]
- Lucretius's argument (2.251–293): animals evidently act freely (racehorses straining at the opening gates show a delay; we can push against a jostling crowd); if atoms never swerved, they could not; so atoms swerve. The swerve also solves a cosmological problem (atoms falling in parallel could never collide; 2.216–50). [source: SEP-Anc §2.4; SEP-Luc; SEP-Epi]
- **Debate on what the swerve does:** (a) gives the ability to do otherwise (Purinton 1999); objection: random swerves would yield "random and blameless twitches" (O'Keefe's example: suddenly singing Queen's "We Are the Champions" instead of accepting a job). (b) Protects character development (Furley 1967; Bobzien 2000). Epicurus argues for agency against determinism in *On Nature* 25 apparently without the swerve, so "it [is] unclear how widely Epicurus relied on" it. [source: SEP-Anc §§2.4.1–2.4.2; SEP-Luc]
- IEP-Chr cites "As Hume argued in the Enquiry, one has no control over that which lacks a cause" against the swerve option. [source: IEP-Chr §4d]
- **Carneades** (Academic sceptic): distinguishes the truthmaker of a statement from the cause of an event; bivalence alone does not threaten freedom; our actions are caused by a "voluntary motion of the mind" whose nature is to be in our power (*On Fate* 24–5, LS 20E); but causal determinism *would* be incompatible with actions being in our power (*Fat.* 31, LS 70G); hence even the gods cannot foretell the future. [source: SEP-Anc §1.3]
- **Alexander of Aphrodisias** (*On Fate*) criticises the Stoics; holds that even given all shaping factors it remains open to the agent to do or not do; "Many scholars see Alexander as the first unambiguously 'libertarian' theorist of the will." [source: SEP-FW §1.1]
- Aristotle (*NE* III): voluntary acts have their origin "in us" with knowledge of particulars; "the man is the father of his actions as of children"; worry that this "pass[es] the buck". [source: SEP-FW §1.1; SEP-Anc §2.1]

## 6. Theological fatalism: foreknowledge (cross-ref *Augustine and Early Christian Philosophy*)
- **Basic Argument for Theological Fatalism** (Hunt & Zagzebski), with T = "you will answer the phone at 9 am tomorrow" [source: SEP-FK §1]:
  1. Yesterday God infallibly believed T.
  2. If E occurred in the past, it is now-necessary that E occurred (Necessity of the Past).
  3. So it is now-necessary that yesterday God believed T.
  4. Necessarily, if God believed T yesterday, then T (infallibility).
  5. If p is now-necessary and necessarily (p → q), then q is now-necessary (Transfer of Necessity).
  6. So it is now-necessary that T.
  7. If so, you cannot do otherwise than answer.
  8. So you cannot do otherwise.
  9. If you cannot do otherwise, you do not act freely (PAP).
  10. So you will not answer freely.
- "There is a consensus that this argument or something close to it is valid." Responses: deny (1) — Boethius and Aquinas (God not in time), or deny future contingent truth; deny (2) — **Ockham** (God's past beliefs belong to a part of the past not subject to the necessity of the past); (5) "rarely disputed", perhaps denied by Scotus and Molina; deny (9) — "arguably the key to the solution proposed by Augustine". Incompatibilist responses: deny infallible foreknowledge (cf. open theism) or deny libertarian free will. Nelson Pike (1965, "Divine Omniscience and Voluntary Action", *Philosophical Review* 74: 27–46) set off the modern debate. [source: SEP-FK §1]
- Key point: the problem is **infallibility**, not foreknowledge as such; ordinary fallible foreknowledge threatens nothing. [source: SEP-FK §1]
- Pike's version (Rice): God believed in 1900 that Jones would mow his lawn on 1/1/2000; Jones could refrain only if he could make God have had a false belief, or not have had that belief, or not have existed. [source: SEP-Fat §5]
- **Boethius** (c. 480–524), *Consolation of Philosophy* V.3: "If God foresees all things and cannot be mistaken in any way, what providence has foreseen will be, will necessarily happen..." (Marenbon's translation). Lady Philosophy's replies: the Modes of Cognition Principle ("everything that is known is grasped, not according to its own power, but rather according to the capacity of those who know it"); eternity is "the whole, simultaneous and perfect possession of unbounded life" (V.6); God knows all times as we know the present; **simple** necessity (the sun rises; a man will die) vs **conditional** necessity (I am necessarily walking when someone sees me walking). [source: SEP-Boe §6]
- **Debate on Boethius:** many modern commentators read Philosophy as spotting a modal scope fallacy; Marenbon reads it as Aristotle's necessity of the present (*De Int.* 19a23); "most contemporary interpreters" read it as an atemporal-God solution. Most scholars take Boethius as an incompatibilist, but Noble (2024) argues he thinks moral choice compatible with atemporal causal determination, and Rogers (2024) attributes "theological compatibilism". The book ends by making God's knowing "set the measure for all things", which may undercut the defence. [source: SEP-Boe §6] (Consistent with the published *Augustine* chapter.)
- Rice: Boethius and Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* I q.14 a.13) deny God existed/believed "in 1900"; problem: a timeless God's beliefs may be as fixed as past ones. [source: SEP-Fat §5.4]
- Augustine (354–430): scholars divide on whether he was a libertarian (MacDonald 1999, Stump 2006) or a compatibilist (Baker 2003, Couenhoven 2007); "true freedom" requires grace. [source: SEP-FW §1.1]
- IEP-FW **theological determinism** (distinct from foreknowledge): if God's willing x is necessary and sufficient for x, it is hard to see how we are free; links to Hell and the problem of evil (Helm 1994). [source: IEP-FW §6a]
- Robb: divine foreknowledge as a Frankfurt-style counterexample to PAP (God's knowing "a billion years ago" makes Kurt's theft inevitable without causing it) (Hunt 1996, 1999). [source: SEP-AP §3.4.1]

## 7. Indian traditions (cross-ref *Upanishads*, *Buddha*, *Jains, Ājīvikas…*)
- SEP-FW declines to cover non-Western traditions and points to Arindam Chakrabarti, "Free Will and Freedom in Indian Philosophies" (2017) and Kai Marchal and Christian Helmut Wenzel, "Chinese Perspectives on Free Will" (2017), both in *The Routledge Companion to Free Will*, ed. Kevin Timpe, Meghan Griffith and Neil Levy (New York: Routledge), pp. 389–404 and 374–88. [source: SEP-FW intro and bibliography]
- **Ājīvikas:** *ājīvika* from *ājīva*, "livelihood"; known for *niyati* (fate) and denial of free will. Buddhist and Jain sources "agree that Gosāla was a rigid determinist, who exalted Niyati to the status of the motive factor of the universe and the sole agent of all phenomenal change"; life "like a ball of pre-wrapped up string, which unrolls until it [is] done and then goes no further" (Lochtefeld's summary). Debate: Dundas suspects the Jains and Buddhists "deliberately distorted Ajivika doctrine for their own polemical purposes"; Bronkhorst: Gosāla taught asceticism can stop the influx of new karma but not destroy old karma. [source: WP-Ājīvika] (Matches the published *Jains, Ājīvikas…* chapter.)
- Jains are "severe critics ... of the fatalist theory according to which all things are fixed", because moral distinctions need an active self. Makkhali Gośāla is the Jain example of a soul reborn as a *nigoda* for disrespecting Mahāvīra. [source: SEP-Jain]
- **Buddhism:** karma defined by intention: "It is intention, monks, what I call action" (A III 415, IEP's rendering; Pāli *cetanā*); past karma conditions but does not dictate; "not fatalism". [source: research note ancient--early-buddhism.md, from IEP-Bud §5 and SEP-Bud; consistent with the published chapter]
- **Buddhist free-will debate (WS only):** Mark Siderits, "Paleo-compatibilism and Buddhist Reductionism", *Sophia* 47(1) (2008): 29–42 — responsibility compatible with determinism; the illusion of incompatibilism arises from mixing the conventional vocabulary of persons with the ultimate vocabulary of the parts persons reduce to (two truths). Rick Repetti (*Journal of Buddhist Ethics* 19, 2012) criticises it and notes Siderits presents it as a position extrapolated from Abhidharma, not necessarily his settled view. Charles Goodman argues for a Buddhist **hard determinism**. [source: WS-5] So "paleo-compatibilism" IS Siderits's term (verified only via search summary).
- **Bhagavad Gītā on action:** 2.47 (*karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana*) is usually rendered as having a right to action but never to its fruits; Wikipedia's (uncredited) rendering: "One has the right to perform their expected duty, / But not to the right to the fruits of action". The ideal of action without craving for fruits is later called *niṣkāma karma*, "a term not used in the Gita" (Fowler; equivalent to *karma-phala-tyāga*). [source: WP-Bhagavad_Gita] Ranganathan (IEP): Kṛṣṇa's placing outcomes beyond the individual's control "seems like an argument for hard determinism (Brodbeck 2004)" out of context, but fits a procedural ethics of devotion (*bhakti*). [source: IEP-Gita]
- Upanishadic karma: Yājñavalkya presents karma "more as a theory to promote good actions, than as a fatalistic doctrine" (Black) — published *Upanishads* chapter; not re-verified here.

## 8. Islamic theology (cross-ref *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes…*, *Falsafa*, *Jewish Philosophy*)
- **Qadariyya** (from *qadar*, "power"): originally a derogatory label for early theologians who rejected predestination and held humans have free will and are responsible, absolving God of responsibility for evil; earliest associated document the pseudepigraphic *Risāla* ascribed to Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, composed between 75/694 and 80/699. Some of their doctrines were adopted by the Muʿtazila. A hadith (Abū Dāwūd) calls them "the Magians of this community". [source: WP-Qadariyah]
- **Jabriyya** (from *ajbara*, "to compel"): humans have no choice; all actions compelled by God. Van Ess: largely a derogatory label, not an institutionalised school. Associated with Jahm ibn Ṣafwān (d. 746); the leaf-in-the-wind image. Muʿtazilites called the Ashʿarites "Jabriyya"; Ashʿarites called the Muʿtazilites "Qadariyya". [source: WP-Jabriyya; WP-Predestination_in_Islam]
- **Muʿtazila** (founded by Wāṣil ibn ʿAṭāʾ): principles include God's oneness and justice (*ʿadl*) and human freedom of action; if evil acts came from God's will, "punishment would have been meaningless"; it is "unthinkable" that God "would punish man for what He himself had commanded". They call themselves "people of monotheism and justice". [source: WP-Muʿtazilism; WP-Predestination_in_Islam] SEP-AIR: Muʿtazilites justify suffering by reasons (compensation, punishment, benefit, grace), "excluding human free actions"; God does what is best (*aṣlaḥ*). [source: SEP-AIR §5]
- **Ashʿarites and *kasb* (acquisition):** al-Ashʿarī (d. 935/6); early on he stressed God's absolute control: the universe is God's property, so God "can punish an innocent person". [source: SEP-AIR §5] Doctrine of *kasb*: all acts are created by God, but the human who performs one is responsible because he "acquires" it; freedom limited to deciding between possibilities God creates (Hughes 2013). Even Sunni Māturīdīs found *kasb* "obscure" and called Ashʿarites determinists (*mujbira*). [source: WP-Ash'arism; WP-Predestination_in_Islam] Al-Ghazālī's account: God creates in me a momentary power for an act, but the power does not cause it. [source: published *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes…* chapter, from SEP causation entry]
- Saadia defended freedom "on the Muʿtazilite pattern" (published *Jewish Philosophy* chapter).

## 9. China
- **Mohist "Condemning Fatalism"** (*Mozi* books 35–37, three versions): fatalism teaches that one's lot is predestined and effort useless, damaging wealth, population and order. Three tests: (1) history — sage kings brought order and tyrants turmoil under the same conditions, so outcomes depend on policy; (2) no one has ever seen or heard fate; (3) belief in fate makes people stop trying. Fraser: the Mohists "oddly ... overlook the obvious fatalist rejoinders" (that outcomes were predestined, that fate is not a physical object, that some are fated to be diligent). [source: SEP-Moh] Franklin Perkins, "The Moist Criticism of the Confucian Use of Fate" (*Journal of Chinese Philosophy*, 2008). [source: SEP-Moh bibliography]
- **Confucian *ming*:** *ming* = "mandate, decree, destiny"; originally the Zhou "Mandate of Heaven" (*tianming*) depending on the ruler's virtue; later also brute facts beyond human effort such as length of life: Analects 6.2, "Unfortunately, the time allotted to him (*ming*) was short, and he died" (Gardner's translation). Mencius: one "should willingly accept fate (*ming*), what is not within one's control" and devote effort to ethical pursuits (2B13, 7A2). [source: SEP-Men14 §1, §2] Mencius 7A2 includes "One who understands Destiny will not stand beneath a tottering wall" (Chinese Text Project wording) — i.e. accepting *ming* is not passivity. [source: WS-4; flag]
- Mohist-Confucian contrast is fair to draw, but Confucians were not fatalists in the Mohist caricature (Perkins's article title suggests the Mohists criticised a Confucian *use* of fate). [source: SEP-Moh bibliography; SEP-Men14]

## 10. Classical compatibilism: Hobbes and Hume (cross-ref *Hobbes*, *Berkeley and Hume*)
- Early-modern consensus (with Spinoza, Reid, Kant as clear exceptions; some add Descartes): free will has two aspects (freedom to do otherwise and self-determination), must ground responsibility, and **compatibilism** is true. [source: SEP-FW §1.2]
- "Classical compatibilism" two-step strategy: (1) the opposite of freedom is not determinism but external constraint — Hobbes: liberty is "the absence of all the impediments to action that are not contained in the nature and intrinsical quality of the agent" (1654); (2) deeper notions (willing what one wills) are incoherent — Locke: to ask whether a man is free to will is "to ask whether a man can will what he wills" (*Essay* II.xxi.25). Implication: free will actually *requires* determinism (Hume). [source: SEP-FW §1.2]
- **Hume, Treatise 2.3.2.1:** "Few are capable of distinguishing betwixt the liberty of spontaneity, as it is call'd in the schools, and the liberty of indifference; betwixt that which is oppos'd to violence, and that which means a negation of necessity and causes. The first is even the most common sense of the word; and as 'tis only that species of liberty, which it concerns us to preserve..." (T 2.3.2.1/407–8). [source: SEP-HFW §1]
- **Hume, Enquiry 8.23:** "a power of acting or not acting, according to the determinations of the will; that is, if we choose to remain at rest, we may; if we choose to move, we also may." This "hypothetical liberty" is "universally allowed to belong to every one, who is not a prisoner and in chains" (EU 8.23/95). He calls his treatment a "reconciling project with regard to the question of liberty and necessity". **NB: the spontaneity/indifference distinction is in the *Treatise*; in the *Enquiry* Hume "drops" it for "hypothetical liberty".** [source: SEP-HFW §1]
- Hume's "new definition of necessity": "the constant union and the inference of the mind" (T 2.3.1.4); confusing causation with compulsion is the root of incompatibilism. In the Treatise he suggests "liberty and chance are synonimous" (T 2.3.2.8). [source: SEP-HFW §1]
- Necessity is essential to morality: we blame character inferred from actions; uncaused acts couldn't be blamed (T 2.3.2.5–7). [source: SEP-HFW §2]
- Objections: Kant called this a "wretched subterfuge" (freedom of a clock moved by internal causes); compulsive internal desires (addict, kleptomaniac) show the internal/external line fails; hypothetical liberty doesn't give the categorical ability to choose otherwise. [source: SEP-HFW §1]
- Interpretive debate: "classical" reading (Hume as Schlick-style optimist) vs Paul Russell's "naturalistic" reading (moral sentiment central; close to P. F. Strawson). [source: SEP-HFW §§1–3]
- Hoefer: Hume argued "determinism is a necessary condition for freedom". [source: SEP-Det §6]
- Twentieth-century heirs: Moore 1912, Schlick 1930/1939, Ayer 1954; Schlick: "Freedom means the opposite of compulsion"; conditional analysis ("could have done otherwise if she had chosen"); objection (Chisholm 1964, Lehrer 1968, van Inwagen 1983): a compulsive agent satisfies the conditional but lacks the ability. [source: SEP-MR §1]
- Spinoza: necessitarian; "a free will skeptic", forerunner of modern sceptics; virtue its own reward (V P42). [source: SEP-FW §1.2]

## 11. The Consequence Argument — step by step (van Inwagen)
- Informal statement, van Inwagen, *An Essay on Free Will* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1983): "If determinism is true, then our acts are the consequences of the laws of nature and events in the remote past. But it is not up to us what went on before we were born, and neither is it up to us what the laws of nature are. Therefore, the consequences of these things (including our present acts) are not up to us." [source: SEP-MR §1, cited as 1983: 16; SEP-IA §5 gives near-identical wording ("consequence"/"it's") cited as 1983: 56 — **page and minor wording differ between the two SEP entries; quote SEP-MR's version and cite "1983, p. 16 (as quoted in SEP)", or paraphrase**]
- Earlier versions: Ginet 1966; van Inwagen 1975 ("The Incompatibility of Free Will and Determinism"); Wiggins 1973; Lamb 1977. The 1983 book gives three formal versions. [source: SEP-IA §5; WP-Consequence_argument] Van Inwagen (b. 21 Sep 1942; Notre Dame) coined "incompatibilism"; the book helped rehabilitate libertarianism. [source: WP-Peter_van_Inwagen]
- **Rule Beta version** ("Np" = "p and no one has, or ever had, any choice about whether p"; H = past state before any agents; L = laws; P = any true proposition) [source: SEP-IA §5]:
  1. □((H & L) ⊃ P) — definition of determinism
  2. □(H ⊃ (L ⊃ P)) — logic
  3. N(H ⊃ (L ⊃ P)) — Rule Alpha (from □p infer Np)
  4. NH — fixity of the past
  5. N(L ⊃ P) — Rule Beta (from Np and N(p ⊃ q) infer Nq), 3, 4
  6. NL — fixity of the laws
  7. NP — Rule Beta, 5, 6
- Van Inwagen's illustration of Beta: N(the sun explodes in 2000); N(if it explodes, all life ends); so N(all life ends). Beta is what distinguishes the argument from the "fatalist fallacy" (□(P ⊃ Q), P, therefore □Q). [source: SEP-IA §5]
- **Objection 1 (early compatibilists):** Beta is invalid if a conditional analysis of ability is correct (Gallois 1977; Foley 1979; Slote 1982). Reply: incompatibilists found Beta more plausible than any compatibilist analysis, and the simple conditional analyses had been rejected even by compatibilists. [source: SEP-IA §5]
- **Objection 2 (McKay and Johnson 1996, "A Reconsideration of an Argument against Compatibilism", *Philosophical Topics* 24: 113–22):** Beta entails **Agglomeration** (from Np and Nq infer N(p & q)), which is invalid: a fair coin is not tossed though someone could have tossed it; p = it does not land heads, q = it does not land tails; no one had a choice about either, but someone had a choice about "it lands neither heads nor tails". **Van Inwagen conceded Beta is invalid (2000).** [source: SEP-IA §5]
- **Repairs:** van Inwagen's N* ("p and no one can, or ever could, do anything such that if she did it, p might be false"), for which Agglomeration is valid; Finch & Warfield (1998) **Beta 2**: from Np and □(p ⊃ q) infer Nq, giving: N(L & H); □((L & H) ⊃ P); so NP. Also O'Connor 1993, Huemer 2000. "So it still looks as though the compatibilist is in trouble." [source: SEP-IA §5]
- **Lewis's reply (1981):** if determinism is true and I could have raised my hand, then if I had, either the past or the laws would have been different ("Different Past"/"Different Laws"); on Lewis's semantics, "Slightly Different Laws" (a small divergence just before the decision). The argument equivocates between "if I had raised my hand, the laws would have been different" (C1) and "I would thereby have *caused* the laws to be different" (C2); compatibilists are committed only to C1, which is no more incredible than our abilities depending on circumstances outside our control. [source: SEP-IA §5]
- Vihvelin's alternative explanation of the fixed past/open future: the direction of causation, not absolute necessity of the past (Horwich 1987; Lewis 1976). Hoefer: physics gives "no support ... for the idea that the past is 'fixed' in some way that the present and future are not". [source: SEP-IA §5; SEP-Det §6]

## 12. Frankfurt cases — step by step, with the dilemma defence
- **PAP:** "a person is morally responsible for what she has done only if she could have done otherwise" (cp. Frankfurt 1969); a standard presupposition of Greek, medieval and early modern thought, taken for granted by both sides until about fifty years ago. [source: SEP-AP intro]
- Frankfurt, "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility", *Journal of Philosophy* 66(23) (1969): 829–39; Frankfurt credits Nozick's unpublished lectures for similar examples. [source: SEP-AP §3.4.3; SEP bibliographies] Frankfurt (born David Bernard Stern, 29 May 1929 – 16 July 2023), Princeton 1990–2002; also *On Bullshit*. [source: WP-Harry_Frankfurt]
- Frankfurt's own words (1969), as quoted on Wikipedia: "Suppose someone (Black, let us say) wants Jones to perform a certain action. Black is prepared to go to considerable lengths to get his way, but he prefers to avoid showing his hand unnecessarily... Now suppose that Black never has to show his hand because Jones, for reasons of his own, decides to perform and does perform the very action Black wants him to perform. In that case, it seems clear, Jones will bear precisely the same moral responsibility for what he does as he would have borne if Black had not been ready to take steps to ensure that he do it." [source: WP-Frankfurt_cases]
- Fischer's neurosurgeon version (1982: 26): Black implants a device; if Jones shows an inclination to vote Democrat, it makes him decide to vote Republican; Jones decides on his own to vote Republican; he could not have done otherwise but seems responsible. [source: SEP-AP §3.4.3]
- **Step by step:** (1) If PAP is true, Jones is responsible only if he could have done otherwise. (2) Jones could not have done otherwise (Black). (3) Jones is responsible (he acted on his own reasons; Black was idle). (4) So PAP is false. Frankfurt's recipe: "conceive circumstances which make it inevitable that a person will perform some action but which do not bring it about that he performs it" (1983: 322). [source: SEP-AP §3.4]
- Precursor: **Locke's man locked in a room** who stays willingly (*Essay* II.xxi.10); Robb: not decisive, since the man could have done otherwise than *decide* to stay. [source: SEP-AP §3.4.2]
- **Objection — flickers of freedom:** Jones could have shown a different inclination, or could have refrained from voting Republican *on his own* (van Inwagen 1978; Naylor 1984; Rowe 1987); reply: such flickers are not "robust" enough to ground responsibility (Fischer 1994). [source: SEP-AP §4.2.2]
- **Objection — the dilemma defence** (Kane 1985: 51; 1996: 142–3, 191–2; Widerker 1995, "Libertarianism and Frankfurt's Attack on the Principle of Alternative Possibilities", *Philosophical Review* 104: 247–61; Ginet 1996; Wyma 1997): Black relies on a prior sign ("Pro-R"). Either the sign determines Jones's choice — then libertarians will not grant he is responsible — or it doesn't — then Jones could have chosen otherwise, "and a robust one at that". "Either way, the FSC fails as a counterexample to PAP." [source: SEP-AP §4.3.1]
- **Replies:** first horn — a causally idle factor cannot be relevant to responsibility, so even if Jones isn't responsible this isn't because he lacked alternatives (Fischer 1999). Second horn — Pereboom's **"Tax Evasion"** buffer case (2001: 19): a device would intervene only if a moral reason occurred with a certain force; none occurs; Joe decides on his own; Hunt's **blockage** cases; Mele & Robb (1998). Critics: blockage may amount to causal determination. "Not much consensus has emerged." [source: SEP-AP §§4.3.2, 5]
- Frankfurt later: his point was "that making an action unavoidable is not the same thing as bringing it about that the action is performed"; "[w]hen a person acts for reasons of his own ... the question of whether he could have done something else instead is quite irrelevant" (2006, 340). [source: SEP-MR §1]

## 13. Frankfurt's hierarchical theory; reasons-responsiveness; P. F. Strawson
- Frankfurt, "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person", *Journal of Philosophy* 68(1) (1971): 5–20. First-order desires (for actions) vs second-order desires (about desires); the will = effective first-order desire, moving one "all the way to action" (1971, p. 84); **second-order volitions** = wanting a desire to be one's will. Psychotherapist example: wants to feel a craving for narcotics to understand a patient, not to act on it. [source: SEP-Comp §4.2.1]
- **Unwilling addict**: second-order volition against the addictive desire, which nevertheless is her will — not free. **Willing addict**: embraces it — acts of her own free will even though she could not do otherwise. "One acts of her own free will if and only if her action issues from the will she wants." [source: SEP-Comp §4.2.1]
- **Wanton**: a being with desires that does not care which of them moves it to action; a **person** has second-order volitions. [source: WP-Harry_Frankfurt]
- Objections: regress/authority problem (one can be alienated from higher-order desires too; Watson 1975); **mesh/manipulation problem** — the mesh could be brainwashed in (Slote 1980; Fischer & Ravizza 1998). [source: SEP-Comp §4.2.2; IEP-FW §2b]
- **Reasons-responsiveness:** Fischer and Ravizza, *Responsibility and Control: A Theory of Moral Responsibility* (Cambridge UP, 1998), "the most influential articulation". Responsibility requires **guidance control** (not regulative control): the mechanism that actually issues in the action is "his own, reasons-responsive mechanism" (1998, 39); **moderate** reasons-responsiveness: "an understandable pattern of (actual and hypothetical) reasons-receptivity" (1998, 71); ownership adds a historical condition. Hypnosis or irresistible urges: "his behavior would be the same, no matter what reasons there were" (37). [source: SEP-MR §2.3] Fischer's view = "semi-compatibilism". [source: SEP-Skep §2]
- **P. F. Strawson, "Freedom and Resentment"** (*Proceedings of the British Academy* 48 (1962): 187–211; IEP dates it 1963). Reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation, love, forgiveness) respond to "the quality of others' wills towards us"; "how much it matters to us, whether the actions of other people ... reflect attitudes towards us of goodwill, affection, or esteem on the one hand or contempt, indifference, or malevolence on the other". **Excuses** (accident, pushed) vs **exemptions** (children, severe disorder: the "objective attitude", seeing someone as "an object of social policy", "to be managed or handled or cured or trained"). Terminology "excuses"/"exemptions" is Watson's (1987). [source: SEP-MR §2.2.1; IEP-FW §5c]
- Strawson's two claims: (a) our commitment to interpersonal relationships is "too thoroughgoing and deeply rooted for us to take seriously the thought that" a general theoretical conviction could abolish them; (b) determinism would not show everyone excused or exempt: "it cannot be a consequence of any thesis which is not itself self-contradictory that abnormality is the universal condition". [source: SEP-MR §2.2; IEP-FW §5c]
- Objections: determinism might show normal humans incapacitated in a relevant way (Russell 1992); we could give up the attitudes (G. Strawson 1986; Nelkin 2011); response-dependence worry (Watson: "It is not that we hold people responsible because they are responsible"). Most compatibilists abandoned Hobbes-style consequentialist justifications "in the wake of" Strawson's "landmark essay". [source: SEP-MR §2.2.2; SEP-FW §1.2]
- Three "faces" of responsibility: attributability, answerability, accountability (Watson 1996; Shoemaker 2011). [source: SEP-FW §2.1]

## 14. Libertarianism: event-causal (Kane) and agent-causal (Chisholm, O'Connor)
- **Event-causal**: the compatibilist's requirements plus nondeterministic causation of the action by agent-involving events. Two objections: **luck** (indeterminism reduces control) and **enhanced control** (indeterminism adds nothing of value). [source: SEP-IT §2]
- **Kane** (Robert Hilary Kane, 25 Nov 1938 – 20 Apr 2024; Texas at Austin), *The Significance of Free Will* (Oxford UP, 1996): free action requires **ultimate responsibility** (UR); somewhere in the history of one's character there must be undetermined, regress-stopping **"self-forming actions"** (SFAs; also "self-forming willings"). In moral conflict, an indeterminate effort of will to resist temptation; Kane likens its indeterminacy to a microphysical particle's ("[t]he choice one way or the other is undetermined because the process preceding and potentially terminating in it ... is indeterminate", 1996: 128); "plurality conditions": whichever way she chooses, she chooses for reasons, voluntarily, and could have chosen the other. [source: SEP-IT §2.3; WP-Robert_Kane]
- **Luck objection (step by step)** [source: SEP-IT §2.2]: (1) If there was a chance, at the moment of choice, that she would decide otherwise, there is a possible world identical up to that moment where she does. (2) Nothing about her, or the world, prior to the decision accounts for the difference. (3) So the difference is "just a matter of luck". (4) So she cannot be responsible for the decision. (Versions: Almeida & Bernstein 2003; Ekstrom 2000; Haji; Levy 2011; Strawson 1994; Mele discusses but rejects.)
- **Kane's replies:** exact sameness is undefined for indeterminate efforts (contested: Clarke, O'Connor); the **glass tabletop** case — a man who tries to shatter a glass tabletop is responsible if he succeeds even if success was undetermined; **dual efforts** — the agent simultaneously tries to make both choices, so either outcome is something she was trying to do. Objections to dual efforts: not how torn decisions feel (Balaguer 2010; Ekstrom 2003; Lemos); not clearly rational. Other replies: contrastive explanation by reasons (Franklin 2018); Shabo (2020) demands a "deciding factor". [source: SEP-IT §§2.2–2.3]
- **Rollback argument:** van Inwagen ("Free Will Remains a Mystery", 2000, in Tomberlin ed., *Philosophical Perspectives* 14: 1–19) imagines God rolling back history to before an undetermined decision and replaying it; outcomes would vary, and nothing the agent does ensures which, so the outcome is a matter of luck (also called the "Mind" or "Luck" argument; van Inwagen 1983 ch. 4). Replies: control is exercised at the time of the choice in bringing it about (Clarke 2005; O'Connor 2007); non-contrastive explanation. [source: SEP-Skep §2.4; SEP-FW §3.1; SEP-IA bibliography]
- **Agent-causal**: the agent, a persisting substance, causes the decision, and is "in a strict and literal sense an originator ... an uncaused cause". Early modern: Berkeley (1710), Reid (1788); recent: Chisholm (1966–78), Clarke, O'Connor (1995–2009; *Persons and Causes: The Metaphysics of Free Will*, OUP 2000), Rowe, Steward (2012), Taylor. [source: SEP-IT §3; bibliographies]
- Chisholm, "Human Freedom and the Self", Lindley Lecture, University of Kansas, 1964. [source: SEP bibliographies] Reid: "If the person was the cause of that determination of his own will, he was free in that action" (1788, IV.i). [source: SEP-FW §1.2]
- Luck "again" for agent causation (Leo agent-causes a decision to tell the truth; in another world, to lie; "just a matter of luck") (Haji 2004; Mele 2005, 2006; van Inwagen 1983: 145, 2000); reply: the difference is how Leo exercises his free will. [source: SEP-IT §3.2]
- Pereboom's **"wild coincidences"** objection: if agent-caused choices matched quantum statistical laws (e.g. probability 0.32), conformity would be an incredible coincidence (2014, 67); replies: Clarke 2010, O'Connor 2009a, Runyan 2018. [source: SEP-FW §3.1]
- Scotus (1265/66–1308): "the stoutest defender in the medieval era of a strongly libertarian conception of the will"; "nothing other than the will is the total cause" of its activity. Aquinas: interpreters divide (compatibilist vs libertarian). [source: SEP-FW §1.1] (Consistent with the published *Thomas Aquinas* and *Scotus, Ockham* chapters.)

## 15. Manipulation: Pereboom's four-case argument and Mele's zygote — step by step
- Pereboom, *Living Without Free Will* (Cambridge UP, 2001), ch. 4; also 1995, 2014 (*Free Will, Agency, and Meaning in Life*, OUP 2014). Pereboom b. 6 Feb 1957, Pesse, Netherlands. [source: SEP-FW §2.4; WP-Derk_Pereboom]
- **Four cases** (Professor Plum kills White, satisfying all leading compatibilist conditions) [source: SEP-MR §3.9; SEP-Skep §2.2]:
  - Case 1: Plum is "created by neuroscientists, who ... manipulate him directly through the use of radio-like technology" (2001, 112).
  - Case 2: neuroscientists "programmed him at the beginning of his life" so that his reasoning is often egoistic; he has "the general ability to regulate his actions by moral reasons" but is causally determined to decide to kill; "he does not decide as he does because of an irresistible desire" (2014a: 77).
  - Case 3: his character is determined by rigorous socio-cultural training (upbringing).
  - Case 4: Plum is an ordinary human in a deterministic universe.
- **Argument:** (1) Plum is not responsible in Case 1. (2) There is no relevant difference between adjacent cases. (3) So he is not responsible in Case 4. (4) Case 4 is just ordinary determinism, so if determinism is true no one is responsible in the basic-desert sense. Challenge to compatibilists: find a principled difference between adjacent cases. [source: SEP-MR §3.9; SEP-Skep §2.2]
- **Replies:** soft-line (find a difference, e.g. an intentional manipulator; Demetriou/Mickelson 2010, Fischer 2004, Mele 2005) — problem: manipulation cases can be revised to remove the difference, e.g. versions with no external agent; hard-line (McKenna 2008): Plum *is* responsible in all four cases — problem: conflicts with strong intuitions. [source: SEP-Skep §2.2; SEP-MR §3.9]
- The four-case argument is strictly an argument for incompatibilism; combined with Pereboom's anti-libertarian arguments it motivates scepticism. [source: SEP-MR §3.9]
- **Mele's zygote argument** (2006, ch. 7; 2019): goddess Diana creates zygote Z in Mary in a deterministic world so that Ernie will, 30 years later, do A; Ernie's duplicate Bert arose naturally. If Ernie is not free, neither is Bert. Vihvelin's reply: why not reason the other way, from Bert's freedom to Ernie's? [source: SEP-IA §3.2; SEP-FW §2.4]
- Manipulation arguments as the source incompatibilist's answer to Frankfurt: determinism is not an idle Black but "part of the 'actual sequence'". [source: SEP-IA §3.2]

## 16. Galen Strawson's Basic Argument — step by step
- G. Strawson (b. 1952; son of P. F. Strawson), "The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility", *Philosophical Studies* 75(1–2) (1994): 5–24; also *Freedom and Belief* (1986; rev. 2010). [source: SEP bibliographies; WP-Galen_Strawson]
- Short form: (1) Nothing can be *causa sui*. (2) To be truly or ultimately morally responsible one would have to be *causa sui*, at least in certain crucial mental respects. (3) So no one can be truly or ultimately morally responsible. [source: SEP-Skep §2.3]
- Expanded form (Strawson 2011, as given by Caruso) [source: SEP-Skep §2.3]: (1) We are interested in actions done for reasons. (2) What one does for a reason is a function of how one is, mentally speaking. (3) So to be truly responsible for how one acts, one must be truly responsible for how one is mentally, in some respects. (4) That requires having consciously chosen to be that way, and succeeded. (5) But one can choose how to be only in the light of existing principles of choice P1. (6) To be responsible for the choice one must be responsible for P1, so must have chosen P1 in the light of P2... (7) An infinite regress; true self-determination is impossible. (8) So true moral responsibility is impossible.
- Strawson's target notion: responsibility "of such a kind that, if we have it, then it makes sense to suppose that it could be just to punish some of us with (eternal) torment in hell and reward others with (eternal) bliss in heaven" (2011: 43). [source: SEP-Skep §2.3]
- Antecedent: Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil* §21 (1886): the *causa sui* is "the best self-contradiction that has been conceived so far"; to pull oneself "up into existence by the hair, out of the swamps of nothingness" "with more than Baron Munchhausen's audacity". [source: SEP-Skep §2.3]
- Clarence Darrow (1924): "He was not his own father; he was not his own mother... He did not make himself. And yet he is to be compelled to pay." (Leopold and Loeb defence.) [source: SEP-IA §4]
- **Replies:** freedom is primarily a feature of actions, not characters (Mele 1995: 221ff.); agent-causal theorists say the agent's antecedent state explains without being the complete cause (Clarke 2003); freedom and responsibility come in degrees and grow over time (Mele; O'Connor 2009b); possibility of self-creation (Bernstein 2005; Kane 1996). Defenders: Istvan 2011, Parks 2009. [source: SEP-FW §3.1; SEP-Skep §2.3]
- Neil Levy's **Luck Pincer** (2011): actions are subject to present luck or constitutive luck (Nagel 1979) or both; either way responsibility-level control is undermined; aimed at compatibilists too. [source: SEP-Skep §2.4]

## 17. Neuroscience: Libet, Wegner, and what they do not show
- Readiness potential (*Bereitschaftspotential*) discovered by Hans Helmut Kornhuber and Lüder Deecke, Freiburg, first recorded 1964, full publication 1965. [source: WP-Bereitschaftspotential]
- **Libet et al. 1983**: Benjamin Libet, Curtis A. Gleason, Elwood W. Wright and Dennis K. Pearl, "Time of Conscious Intention to Act in Relation to Onset of Cerebral Activity (Readiness-Potential): The Unconscious Initiation of a Freely Voluntary Act", *Brain* 106(3): 623–42. Subjects flexed a wrist or finger whenever they chose, reporting the moment of awareness by the position of a dot circling an oscilloscope "clock" (marked intervals c. 43 ms per WP). [source: SEP bibliographies; WP-Benjamin_Libet]
- Findings (Caruso's summary): conscious intention (W) "came 200 milliseconds before the motor act, but 350-400 milliseconds after readiness potential (RP)"; Libet and others concluded conscious intention "cannot be the true cause of action because it comes too late". [source: SEP-Skep §2.5] WP-Bereitschaftspotential: the BP started "about 0.35 sec earlier" than reported awareness.
- Libet (1916–2007) himself kept a conscious **veto** ("free won't"). [source: WP-Benjamin_Libet; WP-Bereitschaftspotential]
- **What it does not show (objections):** no direct way to tell which neural event corresponds to which conscious state; the RP may be an urge or the beginning of forming an intention, not a decision — Mele, *Effective Intentions: The Power of Conscious Will* (OUP, 2009); the "impossible demand" of the timing task (Levy 2005); the unusual design — arbitrary, spontaneous movements, not deliberate choices (Nahmias 2002, 2011); irrelevance to responsibility (Levy 2014); timing relies on introspective clock reports (Dennett). "These objections have led many contemporary philosophers (including many skeptics) to reject the neuroscientific argument" (e.g. Pereboom & Caruso). [source: SEP-Skep §2.5; WP-Benjamin_Libet]
- **Schurger, Sitt and Dehaene 2012** ("An accumulator model for spontaneous neural activity prior to self-initiated movement", *PNAS* 109(42): E2904–E2913): the RP in Libet tasks may be produced by spontaneous subthreshold fluctuations in neural activity crossing a threshold, rather than an unconscious goal-directed decision. [source: WP-Neuroscience_of_free_will] Schurger, Hu, Pak and Roskies, "What Is the Readiness Potential?" (*Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 25(7), 2021): review; "After extensive subsequent study, neuroscientists are uncertain what it signifies." [source: SEP-FW §3.1; SEP-Neth §2.3.1]
- Soon et al. 2008 (fMRI): predicted left/right button choice up to 10 s before awareness with about 60% accuracy (12 subjects); later replicated from decision history alone. [source: WP-Neuroscience_of_free_will]
- **Wegner**, *The Illusion of Conscious Will* (MIT Press, 2002): the experience of conscious willing is "an illusion"; surveys unconscious influences, induced false feelings of authorship, and disorders where people act purposively while denying they are doing so. Daniel Wegner (1948–2013). [source: SEP-FW §3.1; WP-Daniel_Wegner]
- Belief manipulation: Vohs and Schooler (2008) reported that reading anti-free-will text increased cheating; "this study has failed to be replicated". [source: SEP-Neth §2.3.1]
- Situationist/automaticity research (Bargh, Wilson, Kahneman) poses a "shrinking agency" threat even if Libet does not; Baumeister: "free will is at best an occasional phenomenon" (2008: 17). [source: SEP-Skep §2.5]

## 18. Quantum indeterminacy
- Popular view: QM is indeterministic; Hoefer: "partly mistaken and/or misleading. Ironically, quantum mechanics is one of the best prospects for a genuinely deterministic theory"; the Schrödinger equation is deterministic; collapse interpretations are indeterministic; Everettian and Bohmian versions deterministic. [source: SEP-Det §4.4]
- Clarke et al.: "there is nothing approaching a consensus on how to interpret" QM; "Perhaps the best that can be said here is that there is currently no good evidence that determinism is true"; even less evidence for indeterminism located exactly where libertarians need it. [source: SEP-IT §4]
- Honderich: even with micro-indeterminacy there is likely "determinism-where-it-matters" ("near determinism"). [source: SEP-Skep §2.1; IEP-FW §3b]
- The luck objection shows randomness alone doesn't give control (see §14); the swerve parallel (SEP-Luc; SEP-Epi).

## 19. Surveys and folk intuitions
- **PhilPapers Survey 2020 (WS only):** free will — compatibilism 59.16%, libertarianism 18.83%, no free will 11.21%, other 13.54% (N = 1,758 after exclusions; target population of professional philosophers). 2009 survey: compatibilism 59.1%, libertarianism 13.7%, no free will 12.2%, other 14.9% (N = 931). PhilPapers warns against direct comparison (different populations). [source: WS-1, summarising https://survey2020.philpeople.org/survey/results/4838 — host blocked here; treat as second-hand]
- **Nahmias, Morris, Nadelhoffer and Turner**, "Surveying Freedom: Folk Intuitions about Free Will and Moral Responsibility", *Philosophical Psychology* 18(5) (2005): 561–84: in deterministic scenarios most participants judged that agents could act freely and be morally responsible — suggesting ordinary people are not natural incompatibilists. [source: SEP-Comp bibliography for citation; WS-2 for the finding — exact percentages NOT verified]
- **Nichols and Knobe**, "Moral Responsibility and Determinism: The Cognitive Science of Folk Intuitions", *Noûs* 41(4) (2007): 663–85: abstract questions about a deterministic universe yield mostly incompatibilist answers; concrete, emotionally vivid cases (Bill, who kills his family) yield compatibilist ones. One summary: 14% in the abstract condition said people could be fully responsible vs 72% for Bill. [source: SEP-Comp bibliography; WS-3 — figures second-hand, sources disagree (84% vs 86% incompatibilist in abstract condition); avoid exact numbers or hedge]

## 20. Kant (recap only; cross-ref *Kant*)
- Kant, with Reid and Spinoza, is a clear early-modern exception to compatibilism; he and Reid argued freedom requires that one "could have willed differently", and that the agent rather than his desires cause free choices. [source: SEP-FW §1.2] Published *Kant* chapter: third antinomy; free as things in themselves; "fact of reason".

## Debates
- **Was Aristotle denying bivalence?** "More than one view": Rice uses the bivalence-denial reading but notes the alternative that Aristotle rejects the truth-to-necessity step. [source: SEP-Fat §1.1]
- **Who wrote the dog-and-cart simile?** Hippolytus says Zeno and Chrysippus; Bobzien: Roman Stoa, maybe glossing Cleanthes; O'Keefe: if Chrysippean, it means resisting fate is futile, not that outcomes are fixed regardless of action. [source: IEP-Chr; SEP-Anc]
- **What is the swerve for?** Ability to do otherwise (Purinton) vs character development (Furley, Bobzien) vs narrow; Epicurus may not have relied on it much. [source: SEP-Anc §2.4; SEP-Luc]
- **Origin of the concept of free will:** Dihle (Augustine) vs Frede (Epictetus). [source: SEP-FW §1.1]
- **Augustine and Aquinas: libertarians or compatibilists?** [source: SEP-FW §1.1]
- **Boethius:** incompatibilist (majority) vs Noble, Rogers (2024); fallacy reading vs necessity-of-the-present reading vs atemporal-God reading. [source: SEP-Boe §6]
- **Hume:** classical (Schlick-like) vs naturalistic (Russell; Strawson-like) interpretation. [source: SEP-HFW]
- **Consequence Argument:** Beta invalid (McKay–Johnson; conceded by van Inwagen 2000) but repaired (N*, Beta 2); Lewis's local-divergence/equivocation reply. [source: SEP-IA §5]
- **Frankfurt cases:** dilemma defence (Kane, Widerker, Ginet) vs buffer/blockage cases (Pereboom, Hunt, Mele & Robb); flickers of freedom; "not much consensus". [source: SEP-AP]
- **Manipulation:** hard-line (McKenna) vs soft-line replies. [source: SEP-Skep §2.2]
- **Libet:** decision vs urge vs stochastic accumulation (Schurger); relevance to deliberate choices. [source: SEP-Skep §2.5; SEP-FW §3.1]
- **Ājīvikas:** genuine fatalists vs distorted by rivals (Dundas, Bronkhorst). [source: WP-Ājīvika]
- **Buddhism:** Siderits's paleo-compatibilism vs Goodman's hard determinism; Repetti's critique. [source: WS-5]
- **Kasb:** a real middle way vs disguised determinism (Māturīdī critics called Ashʿarites *mujbira*). [source: WP-Predestination_in_Islam]
- **Kane's dual efforts:** phenomenologically and rationally doubtful (Balaguer, Ekstrom, Lemos). [source: SEP-IT §2.3]
