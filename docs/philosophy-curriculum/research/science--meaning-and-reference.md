## 1. Frege: sense and reference (go deeper than PUB-Birth)

- "Über Sinn und Bedeutung" appeared in *Zeitschrift für Philosophie und philosophische Kritik* 100 (1892): 25–50; translated as "On Sense and Reference" by M. Black in Geach and Black (eds.), *Translations from the Philosophical Writings of Gottlob Frege* (3rd ed. 1980), pp. 56–78. [source: SEP-Frege bibliography, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/frege/] The companion paper "Über Begriff und Gegenstand" ("On Concept and Object") is 1892b. [source: SEP-Frege]
- The distinction was "first outlined, albeit briefly" in "Funktion und Begriff" (1891), made first for mathematical equations such as "4 × 2 = 11 – 3", where contemporaries were wary of reading "=" as identity; Frege held that both sides stand for one number, the number eight, determined or presented differently. [source: IEP-Frege, https://iep.utm.edu/frege/] IEP-FregeLang adds that although the words *Sinn* and *Bedeutung* appear already in the *Foundations* of 1884, "the distinction itself … do[es] not occur in Frege's writings before 1891". [source: https://iep.utm.edu/freg-lan/]
- SEP frames the paper as addressing **two puzzles**: (1) identity statements; (2) propositional-attitude reports, where the "Principle of Identity Substitution" (from S(n) and n = m, infer S(m)) seems to fail: "John believes that Mark Twain wrote *Huckleberry Finn*; Mark Twain = Samuel Clemens; therefore John believes that Samuel Clemens wrote *Huckleberry Finn*" is invalid. SEP notes Frege "is generally credited with identifying" the second puzzle "even though he didn't quite describe the puzzle in the terms used below". [source: SEP-Frege]
- Frege's examples as reported by SEP and IEP include "117 + 136 = 253", "the morning star is identical to the evening star", "4" and "8/2", "Pegasus", "the most powerful Greek god". IEP lists "Romulus", "Odysseus", "the least rapidly converging series", "the present King of France" as expressions with sense but no reference, and says sentences containing them "express thoughts, but are neither true nor false". [source: SEP-Frege; IEP-Frege] (On whether Frege's own text used "Hesperus/Phosphorus" see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Sense defined: the sense of an expression "accounts for its cognitive significance—it is the way by which one conceives of the denotation of the term" (SEP); IEP: the sense is "the 'mode of presentation' or cognitive content associated with the expression in virtue of which the reference is picked out". [source: SEP-Frege; IEP-Frege]
- **Indirect (oblique) contexts.** In attitude reports a term "denotes its ordinary sense"; IEP calls these "secondary references" and "oblique contexts"; contexts also include "it is informative that…" and "… is analytically true". Frege thereby keeps Leibniz's law intact: substitution fails only because the names no longer co-refer in that context. [source: SEP-Frege; IEP-Frege] The whole embedded sentence then refers to a thought, not a truth-value. [source: IEP-FregeLang]
- Compositional picture: in "John loves Mary", names and the whole sentence have both sense and reference; the predicate's reference is a function ("concept") from objects to truth-values; "there are only two truth values, [but] … an infinite number of thoughts". [source: SEP-Frege] Predicates are "unsaturated" (*ungesättigt*); "( ) has a heart" and "( ) has a kidney" (IEP's example) arguably have the same reference but different senses. [source: IEP-Frege]
- Senses are objective, interpersonal and language-independent: different people, and expressions in different languages, can express the same sense; they exist in a timeless "third realm". [source: IEP-Frege]
- **Aristotle example.** Frege implies that someone might attach to "Aristotle" the sense "the pupil of Plato and teacher of Alexander the Great"; IEP stresses senses "are not linguistic items" and "it is not even necessary that the sense of the name be expressible by some descriptive phrase". [source: IEP-Frege] (Important for the debate over whether Frege was a descriptivist.)
- **Tone/colouring.** Frege separated sense from *Färbung* or *Duft* ("colouring", "scent"), usually called "tone" in English. [source: WP-Sense_and_reference]
- **Telescope analogy** (WS only, weaker): Frege compares the Moon to the reference, the real image in the telescope to the sense, and the observer's retinal image to the subjective idea; the telescope image is "one-sided and dependent on the standpoint of observation" but "objective, inasmuch as it can be used by several observers". [source: WS-3 summarising translations at https://www2.lawrence.edu/fast/ryckmant/On%20Sense%20and%20Reference.pdf and https://www3.nd.edu/~jspeaks/courses/mcgill/415/fall2003/Frege-sense-reference.pdf] Use as paraphrase only.
- **Translations of *Bedeutung*.** IEP-Frege lists English versions titled "On Sense and Meaning" (*Collected Papers* [CP] 157–77), "On *Sinn* and *Bedeutung*" (*Frege Reader* [FR] 151–71) and "On Sense and Reference" (TPW 56–78). [source: https://iep.utm.edu/frege/] "Meaning" was chosen as standard for Blackwell editions in the early 1970s (Beaney: at a meeting attended by Dummett, Geach, Kneale, Roger White and a Blackwell representative) on the principle of "exegetical neutrality". [source: WP-Sense_and_reference] IEP-FregeLang dates the Blackwell decision to 1970 and gives the two main alternatives as "reference" (Dummett, who also distinguishes "reference" for the relation and "referent" for the object) and "significance" (Tugendhat 1970), the latter because "referent" is misleading for predicates. [source: https://iep.utm.edu/freg-lan/] SEP's Zalta prefers "denotation" because "reference" also names what a *person* does with language. [source: SEP-Frege notes, n. 5] The 1949 title "On Sense and Nominatum" appeared in Herbert Feigl and Wilfrid Sellars (eds.), *Readings in Philosophical Analysis* (Appleton-Century-Crofts), pp. 85–102 (WS: PhilArchive record lists Feigl as *editor*; translator not confirmed). [source: WS-5, https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/FREOSA-6]
- **Debate on Stoic sources.** Susanne Bobzien (2021) argued, with cumulative evidence but "no smoking gun", that Frege borrowed heavily and without attribution from Stoic ideas as reported in Prantl's *Geschichte der Logik im Abendland* (1855–1870) and Diogenes Laertius: Stoic *lekta* vs *Sinn*, "incomplete" (*ellipē*) vs *ungesättigt*, *axiōmata* vs *Gedanken*. Replies include Rami, Gabriel & Hüser 2024 and Gahér 2021. SEP adds this does not touch Frege's formal achievement. [source: SEP-Frege §3.3]
- Frege lived 8 Nov 1848 (Wismar) – 26 Jul 1925 (Bad Kleinen). [source: WP-Gottlob_Frege]

## 2. Mill and the "tag" theory (the alternative to sense)

- Millianism: "a name's meaning is simply its referent"; SEP-Names cites J. S. Mill, *A System of Logic* (1843: 34, 36–37); SEP-Ref cites "Mill (1867)" (a later edition) — use 1843 for first publication. [source: SEP-Names; SEP-Ref]
- **Ruth Barcan Marcus** (2 Aug 1921 – 19 Feb 2012) proposed in "Modalities and Intensional Languages" (*Synthese*, 1961) that proper names are mere "tags", whose meaning "is exhausted by this referential function"; consequences include the necessity of identity statements between co-referring names. She formally proved the necessity of identity in 1946/47 (*Journal of Symbolic Logic* 12 (1947): 12–15). [source: WP-Ruth_Barcan_Marcus; SEP-Ref §2.2] Timothy Williamson distinguishes her direct-reference idea from Kripke's rigidity, since rigidified descriptions are rigid but still descriptive. [source: WP-Ruth_Barcan_Marcus, quoting Williamson's 2008 tribute]
- Priority controversy: Quentin Smith (Synthese 104, 1995) claimed some ideas of *Naming and Necessity* were first presented by Marcus (a 1969 lecture Kripke attended); Stephen Neale ("No Plagiarism Here", *TLS*, 9 Feb 2001) and Scott Soames argued he is mistaken. [source: WP-Naming_and_Necessity; WP-Ruth_Barcan_Marcus] Present as a named controversy, with the majority view against Smith.

## 3. Russell's descriptions: the pieces PUB-Birth left out

- "On Denoting", *Mind* 14(4), October 1905, pp. 479–493. Denoting phrases "never have any meaning in themselves". [source: WP-On_Denoting]
- Russell's general analysis: "C (the father of Charles II)" implies there is one and only one x such that x begot Charles II, and C(x); in modern notation ∃x(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y = x) ∧ Cx). [source: WP-On_Denoting]
- **Primary vs secondary occurrence** = wide vs narrow scope. Russell: "Thus all propositions in which 'the King of France' has a primary occurrence are false: the denials of such propositions are true, but in them 'the King of France' has a secondary occurrence." [source: WP-On_Denoting, quoting Russell]
- Fictional names such as "Apollo" treated as abbreviated descriptions denoting nothing. [source: WP-On_Denoting]
- *De re / de dicto* via scope: "George wondered whether the Morning Star is the Evening Star" has a reading where the description takes wide scope ("The star x that appears in the morning is such that George wondered whether x is identical to the star that appears in the evening") and one where both descriptions do (George "has gone mad and is in fact wondering about the law of identity"). [source: SEP-Desc §3.2]
- Russell's methodological line "A logical theory may be tested by its capacity for dealing with puzzles" (WS: placed at pp. 484–5 of the original). [source: WS-4, https://www3.nd.edu/~jspeaks/courses/mcgill/415/russell-descriptions.html]
- Strawson's later concession: Strawson (1964) "came to doubt whether the entailment vs. presupposition debate could be settled by 'brisk little formal argument[s]'". [source: SEP-Desc §5.1]
- **Donnellan (1966)**, "Reference and Definite Descriptions", *Philosophical Review* 75(3), July 1966. Attributive: Detective Brown, seeing Smith's mutilated corpse, says "The murderer of Smith is insane" — whoever did it. Referential: pointing at Jones behaving strangely in the dock, "The murderer of Smith is insane" — said of Jones "whether or not Jones is the actual murderer". Donnellan said Russell fits attributive uses only; "it is not clear whether Donnellan himself intended to endorse a lexical ambiguity". [source: SEP-Desc §5.2; WP-Keith_Donnellan] Donnellan: 25 Jun 1931 – 20 Feb 2015, mostly at UCLA. [source: WP-Keith_Donnellan]
- **Kripke (1977) reply**, step by step: (i) Grice's distinction between what is said and what is meant (his recommendation letter praising only punctuality and handwriting); (ii) referential uses = speaker's reference vs semantic reference; (iii) the same split occurs with names — seeing Johnson raking leaves and taking him for Jones, "Jones is really working up a sweat today" says something about Jones but communicates something about Johnson, yet no one thinks "Jones" is ambiguous; (iv) so do not posit an ambiguity in "the" ("a kind of philosophical cheat"). [source: SEP-Desc §5.2]
- Residual problems: Hornsby (1977) misdescription case; Ludlow and Segal's (2004) "residue of the problem of misdescription" (several murderers, all mad cultists); the incompleteness argument ("the table is covered with books"), which Kripke (1977) allowed "might be enough of a problem to force us to accept referential interpretations". [source: SEP-Desc §§5.2–5.3]
- Peter Ludlow (SEP) called "On Denoting" "*the* paradigm of philosophy"; Ramsey's phrase was "that paradigm of philosophy". [source: WP-On_Denoting]

## 4. Kripke on names: the modal argument and its replies (main step-by-step argument)

- Kripke: born 13 Nov 1940, died 15 Sep 2022 (Plainsboro, New Jersey). Lectures at Princeton on 20, 22 and 29 January 1970; transcript in Davidson and Harman (eds.), *Semantics of Natural Language* (Dordrecht: Reidel, 1972), pp. 253–355, 763–69; book, Harvard UP 1980. [source: WP-Naming_and_Necessity; WP-Saul_Kripke] 1973 John Locke Lectures *Reference and Existence* continue the work (fictional names). [source: WP-Naming_and_Necessity]
- Kripke attributes variants of descriptivism to Frege, Russell, Wittgenstein and Searle. [source: WP-Naming_and_Necessity] Wittgenstein PI §79 ("Moses" — the man who led the Israelites… or etc.) is a standard cluster-style text. [source: SEP-Names §2.9] Searle's "Proper Names" (*Mind* 1958) proposed the cluster theory; Strawson (1959: 191–192) the community-composite version ("some reasonable proportion of these propositions"). [source: SEP-Names §2.6]
- **Kripke's six theses** of the cluster theory he criticised (written on the board, reproduced at 1980 p. 71): (1) to every name 'X' corresponds a cluster of properties φ such that A believes 'φX'; (2) one or some conjointly are believed by A to pick out an individual uniquely; (3) if most, or a weighted most, of the φ's are satisfied by one unique object y, y is the referent; (4) if the vote yields no unique object, 'X' does not refer; (5) 'If X exists, then X has most of the φ's' is known a priori by the speaker; (6) it expresses a necessary truth (in the speaker's idiolect); plus (C) non-circularity. [source: WP-Naming_and_Necessity]
- Kripke's definition of rigidity, in SEP's words: a rigid designator "designates just the object it actually designates in all possible worlds in which that object exists, and it designates nothing else in any possible world" (the 1980 p. 48 wording was not retrieved; see NOT VERIFIED). [source: SEP-Rigid §1.1] *De jure* vs *de facto* rigidity: "the successor of 2" is rigid de facto; a name is rigid de jure. [source: SEP-Rigid]

### The modal argument, step by step (SEP-Names §2.4, SEP-Rigid §2.2.3, SEP-Ref §2.1)
1. Suppose descriptivism: "Socrates" means "the teacher of Plato and Xenophon" (or "Petrarch" means "the famous humanist most closely associated with the Italian Renaissance").
2. Synonyms should be substitutable *salva veritate* in modal contexts.
3. "The teacher of Plato and Xenophon might not have been human" sounds true (Plato and Xenophon might have been raised by wolves); "Socrates might not have been human" is false. Likewise "Petrarch might not have been famous" is true, but "The famous humanist … might not have been famous" has a false reading. (Kripke 1980: 40–42; Searle 1958: 172 already noted the contingency of "Socrates taught Plato".)
4. So the name and the description differ in modal profile: the name is rigid, the description is not.
5. Therefore the name does not mean the description. [source: SEP-Names §2.4; SEP-Rigid §2.2.3.1]

**Reply 1 — wide scope ("widescopism"; Dummett 1973/1981 ch. 9, later Sosa 2001, Hunter 2005; cf. Soames 1998).** The sentence is ambiguous; on the reading where the description takes wide scope ("For some x, x is the famous humanist … and it might have been the case that x has never been famous") it is true. Names just "gravitate towards the widest possible scope". [source: SEP-Names §2.4; SEP-Rigid §2.2.3.1]
**Kripke's counter (1980 pp. 11–15, the Preface):** (a) Some sentences have no modal operator: evaluating "Petrarch is never famous" at a world where Petrarch dies as an infant, it seems true, but "The famous humanist … is never famous" is false at every world. (b) We can deliberately put the name inside the operator: "It might have been the case that: Petrarch never became famous." SEP: these arguments "seem to have persuaded most of his audience"; Dummett "responded by making some modifications (1981, pp. xvii–xix)". [source: SEP-Rigid §2.2.3.1]
**Reply 2 — rigidified descriptions.** "The famous humanist most closely associated, *in α* [the actual world], with the Italian Renaissance", or "the *actual* F", is rigid de jure; so rigidity does not by itself refute descriptivism. [source: SEP-Rigid §2.2.1; SEP-Ref §2.1] Counter: the epistemic and semantic arguments remain (a speaker can refer to Petrarch while thinking Valla was the leading humanist). [source: SEP-Rigid §2.2.3.2]
**Reply 3 — assertoric content vs ingredient sense (Dummett 1991; Stanley 1997a, 1997b, 2002).** Sentences can say the same thing yet embed differently, as "now"/"here"/"in α" variants show; Evans's descriptive name "Julius" (rigid for whoever invented the zip) — Evans: such pairs "have the same content, despite their modal differences" (1979, p. 187 n. 10). Stanley: "the propositional content of a sentence in a context is not its modal content". Critics: Everett 2005, King 2007, Shieh 2001, Soames 2005; defenders: Ninian 2012, Stalnaker 2014, Yalcin 2014. [source: SEP-Rigid §2.2.3.2–3]
**Reply 4 — role readings** (Rothschild 2007): descriptions can denote roles ("the role of teacher to Plato and Xenophon might have been filled by a non-human"). [source: SEP-Names §2.4]

- **Epistemic argument:** if "Socrates" meant "the teacher of Plato", "Socrates taught Plato" would be a priori; Searle's cluster retreat makes only the disjunction a priori; Kripke (1980: 66–67) replies that experts can posit a historical figure behind a legend who shares none of the legend's attributes. [source: SEP-Names §2.6]
- **Semantic argument:** Feynman ("a physicist") and Gödel/Schmidt (already in PUB-1950); Kripke's further point that reference depends on the person from whom one actually learned the name (1980: 90–91; Geach 1969; Donnellan 1970). [source: SEP-Names §2.6; SEP-Ref §2.1] Columbus example: speakers' only beliefs ("first European in North America", "first to believe the earth was round") are false, yet they refer to Columbus. [source: WP-Causal_theory_of_reference]
- **Causal picture**, adumbrated "at roughly the same time" by Geach (1969), Donnellan (1970) and Kripke (1972): reference fixing by dubbing, then reference borrowing along a chain; borrowers "needn't be able to identify any of the lenders". Kripke's caveat: re-using a name (SEP's example: calling a hedgehog "Napoleon") is a new use, because of the speaker's intention; so "not every sort of causal connection to previous use suffices". [source: SEP-Ref §2.2] Causal grounding and rigidity come apart both ways ("Joy" for the most joy-filled individual is rigid but not causally grounded; "Originalowner" is grounded but not rigid). [source: SEP-Rigid §2.2.2]
- Evans (12 May 1946 – 10 Aug 1980; *The Varieties of Reference*, 1982): "The Causal Theory of Names", *Proc. Aristotelian Society* Supp. Vol. 47 (1973): 187–208; besides Madagascar (in PUB-1950), objected that on simple versions even a name "briefly overheard in a train or café" would refer to its original bearer. [source: WP-Gareth_Evans_(philosopher); SEP-Ref bibliography; WP-Causal_theory_of_reference]

## 5. Putnam, Burge and externalism (beyond Twin Earth)

- Twin Earth appears in Putnam's "Meaning and Reference" (*Journal of Philosophy* 70 (1973): 699–711) and "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975), reprinted in *Philosophical Papers* vol. 2, *Mind, Language and Reality* (Cambridge UP, 1975). [source: WP-Twin_Earth_thought_experiment; SEP-Ext bibliography] Putnam: 31 Jul 1926 – 2016. [source: WP-Hilary_Putnam]
- Putnam's three cases: Twin Earth, **aluminium–molybdenum**, and **beech–elm**. [source: WP-Twin_Earth_thought_experiment] In the elm case a speaker who cannot tell an elm from a beech still has an "elm" whose extension is all and only elms (WS). [source: WS-2, https://www.uvm.edu/~lderosse/courses/lang/Putnam_TwinEarth.pdf]
- **Division of linguistic labour**, "analogous to Adam Smith's economic division of labor": reference of natural-kind terms is fixed by experts (zoologists for "lion", botanists for "elm", chemists for "table salt"). [source: WP-Hilary_Putnam]
- **Meaning vector**: (1) the extension (e.g. H₂O); (2) the "stereotype" (e.g. transparent, colourless); (3) semantic markers (natural kind, liquid); (4) syntactic markers (concrete noun, mass noun). Putnam: meaning changes only if the reference, not the stereotype, changes. [source: WP-Hilary_Putnam]
- SEP's reconstruction: you and your molecule-for-molecule twin utter "Water is wet"; truth-conditions differ (water vs "twater"); "what occurs inside the head is … not sufficient to determine or fix everything that occurs in the mind". McGinn (1977) and Burge (1979) extended the point to attitudes. Individuation is not location (Davidson's sunburn). [source: SEP-Ext §3.1]
- Burge, "Individualism and the Mental", *Midwest Studies in Philosophy* 4 (1979): 73–121. A patient believes he has arthritis in his thigh; the belief is an arthritis-belief and false (arthritis afflicts joints only); in a counterfactual community where "arthritis" covers his ailment ("tharthritis") he has a true belief that is *not* an arthritis-belief. Differences stem from linguistic practice, not the natural environment, and Burge, "unlike Putnam, explicitly endorses" the extension to attitudes. [source: SEP-Ext §3.1; WP-Tyler_Burge] Burge, born 1946, UCLA. [source: WP-Tyler_Burge] Burge's "Other Bodies" (1982) argued the twins' mental states differ; Putnam later agreed. [source: WP-Twin_Earth_thought_experiment]
- Objections: Boghossian's "Dry Earth" (1997) and the self-knowledge problem; Crane (1991) and Wikforss (2001): the patient has only the concept *tharthritis*; Searle: "water" could cover anything sufficiently water-like; Dennett's "intuition pumps"; experimental philosophers on culturally variable intuitions (Mallon et al. 2009); Gertler (2012) doubts the debate is well defined. [source: SEP-Ext §3.2; WP-Twin_Earth_thought_experiment]
- Narrow content: Fodor (1987) as functions from contexts to truth-conditions; Stalnaker (1989) and Block (1991) object it is not content at all. [source: SEP-Ext §3.3]

## 6. Two-dimensionalism (briefly)

- Introduced for indexicals (Kaplan's *character* vs *content*: "I" has constant character, varying content) and tense/modal logic. [source: SEP-2D §1.1]
- Generalised 2D (Lewis, Jackson, Chalmers) claims an a priori aspect of meaning. Jackson (1998): consider worlds "as actual" vs "as counterfactual". If Earth is actual, water is H₂O in every counterfactual world; if Twin Earth is actual, water is XYZ. A-intension (diagonal) = naïve reference-fixing criterion; C-intension = the modal profile. Externalists recognise only the C-intension as content. Stalnaker's "metasemantic" use avoids a priori truth-conditions. [source: SEP-2D §2.2.2, intro]

## 7. Indian theories of word meaning

### Grammarians
- Pāṇini: dated variously from 6th–5th to 4th century BCE; Cardona (1997): evidence "strongly supports a date no earlier than 400 BCE"; von Hinüber (1989) and Falk (1993) place him mid-4th century BCE; tradition places him at Śalātura (likely Gandhāra). [source: WP-Pāṇini] SEP-LangInd simply writes "Pāṇini (400 bce)". [source: SEP-LangInd §2]
- **Pāṇini's argument (Aṣṭādhyāyī 1.2.64)**: if words referred only to particulars, someone who tied up one cow for a sacrifice in response to a Vedic command would be in error tying up a different cow in a later ritual, since the command could refer only to one specific animal. [source: SEP-Lit §2.1]
- Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya* (mid-2nd century BCE) reports **Vyāḍi** (words like "cow" denote individuals) vs **Vājapyāyana** (they denote the generic property *ākṛti*, cowness); Patañjali concludes both belong to word-meaning, the dispute being which is understood first. [source: SEP-LangInd §2; SEP-Lit §2.1; WP-Patanjali for date] Matilal's "brave-man" (*vīra-puruṣaḥ*) illustration: Vājapyāyana says the two attributes share one substratum; Vyāḍi replies "brave" refers to the brave man but is used *because of* his braveness. [source: SEP-Lit §2.1]
- Kātyāyana, first *vārttika*: "While the relationship between words and meanings is established on the basis of the usage of specific words to denote specific meanings in the community of speakers, the science of grammar only makes a regulation concerning the religious merit produced by the linguistic usage" (Deshpande's tr.). Patañjali reads "established" (*siddha*) as eternal (*nitya*), not created (*kārya*) — for Sanskrit only; vernaculars were *apaśabda/apabhraṃśa*. [source: SEP-LangInd §2]
- **Sphoṭa before Bhartṛhari:** Patañjali is the first known to use *sphoṭa* (for Kātyāyana's "true sounds which are fixed") and *dhvani* ("uttered sounds"); the length/speed of a sound belongs to *dhvani*. Kātyāyana's puzzle: sounds perish as soon as uttered, so no two coexist to form a word; Patañjali: the mind pulls together impressions of the uttered sounds into a word-image. [source: SEP-LangInd §2] Pāṇini (6.1.123) names a predecessor Sphoṭāyana. [source: WP-Sphoṭa]
- **Bhartṛhari** (Deshpande: "400 ce"; WP: fl. 5th–6th c., "assumed … between 485 and 540 CE", or 450–510 for those separating him from the poet; distinct from the poet-king of the *Śatakatraya* according to K. A. S. Iyer and Matilal): *Vākyapadīya* (also *Trikāṇḍī*), three books (Brahma-, Vākya-, Pada-kāṇḍa), about 635 verses (WP). [source: SEP-LangInd §2; WP-Bhartṛhari] Give a date range, not a single year.
- Bhartṛhari's doctrines: language is the ultimate principle (*śabdabrahman*); the sentence is the given unit, and words, stems and affixes are abstractions (*apoddhāra*) that are "imagined" (*kalpita*) (VP III.14.75–76), useful for instruction (VP II.238); the sentence and its meaning are an "undivided singularity" grasped in a flash (*pratibhā*). VP II.10: "Just as stems, affixes etc. are abstracted from a given word, so the abstraction of words from a sentence is justified." [source: SEP-LangInd §§2, 6] Bhartṛhari's position was "generally unacceptable" to Mīmāṃsā, Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika and later grammarians (Kauṇḍabhaṭṭa, Nāgeśabhaṭṭa), who used *ākāṅkṣā* (mutual expectancy), *yogyatā* (compatibility) and *āsatti* (contiguity). [source: SEP-LangInd §2]
- For grammarians, meaning is a "projection of intellect" (*bauddhārtha*): "hare's horn" and "son of a barren woman" are meaningful; truth and meaningfulness are not equated — a "middle path" between Nyāya/Mīmāṃsā realism (*bāhyārthavāda*) and Buddhist conceptual construction (*vikalpa*). [source: SEP-LangInd §4]
- Three levels of speech (*paśyantī*, *madhyamā*, *vaikharī*). [source: WP-Bhartṛhari] (WP-Sphoṭa mislabels the levels; see NOT VERIFIED.)

### Mīmāṃsā vs Nyāya
- Mīmāṃsā: meanings are natural, not conventional; words signify only (eternal) universals; words themselves are eternal ("If one utters the word 'chair' ten times, is one uttering the same word ten times?"); so the relation is eternal. [source: SEP-LangInd §3]
- Precise positions on "bull": Mīmāṃsā — primary meaning is the universal (*jāti*, bull-ness), the individual understood "secondarily and subsequently"; *Kevalavyaktivāda* — the individual alone is primary; Nyāya — "an individual object qualified by a generic property (*jāti-viśiṣṭa-vyakti*), both being perceived simultaneously". [source: SEP-LangInd §4]
- **Nyāya argument against a natural word–object relation**: if it were natural, hearing "*agni*" (fire) or "*asi*" (sword) would burn or cut one's mouth; so the relation is a convention (*saṃketa*) established by God's will ("let this word refer to this thing"). Naiyāyikas held that vernacular words merely remind hearers of Sanskrit words. [source: SEP-LangInd §§3–4]
- **Kumārila** (fl. 7th c.; WP "roughly 7th century"; possibly south Indian): realism about universals — one perceives *go-tva*, "being a cow"; argues by *arthāpatti* (presumption) that universals must be posited as word referents; avoids Nyāya's inherence regress by a quasi-Jaina perspectivalism on which universal and particular are aspects of one thing (*Ślokavārttika*, ākṛtivāda 8 ff.). He rejected sphoṭa, explaining understanding by memory of discrete phonemes, and held phonemes too are eternal. [source: SEP-Kum §§1.2, 3.1–3.3; WP-Kumārila_Bhaṭṭa]
- Arnold contrasts Kumārila's "abstract objectivism" with Dharmakīrti's apoha as "individualistic subjectivism" (Volosinov's terms). [source: SEP-Kum §3.1]
- **Sentence meaning.** *Abhihitānvayavāda* (Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā and Nyāya; also Kātyāyana and Patañjali): words convey unconnected meanings, then connected (Brough 1953: a sentence is "a series of expressed word-meanings"; Kumārila: "as the words are uttered in a sentence, each word performs its task of expressing its meaning, and the sentence is the summation of these meanings"). *Anvitābhidhānavāda* (Prabhākara): words already convey contextually inter-connected meanings (Brough: "the expressed meaning of a series [of words]"). [source: SEP-LangInd §6; SEP-Kum §3.2] Arnold glosses Prabhākara as holding "complete sentences represent the basic unit of meaning"; Deshpande instead says, for Prabhākara, "the sentence-meaning is not different from a simple addition of these inherently inter-connected word-meanings" — present both glosses (see Debates). Many Westerners describe abhihitānvaya as "compositional" (WP). [source: SEP-Kum; SEP-LangInd; WP-Prabhākara]
- Prabhākara's date: SEP-Kum "flourished around 700 … sometimes said to have been Kumārila's student"; WP-Prabhākara "active c. 6th century"; WP-Mīmāṃsā "8th century". Give a range (c. 6th–8th c.) or follow SEP. [source: SEP-Kum; WP-Prabhākara; WP-Mīmāṃsā]
- Levels of meaning: primary (*śakti/abhidhā*), secondary (*lakṣaṇā*; "*gaṅgāyāṃ ghoṣaḥ*", a cowherd colony "on the Ganges" = on its bank), suggested (*vyañjanā*, developed by Ānandavardhana; the night-blind mother-in-law verse), intended (*tātparya*). [source: SEP-LangInd §§4, 7]
- Deshpande: "The modern distinction of 'sense' versus 'reference' is somewhat blurred in the Sanskrit discussions"; *artha* has been rendered "sense", "reference", "denotation", "connotation", "designatum", "intension", none adequately. The Navya-Nyāya term *śakyatāvacchedaka* / *pravṛttinimitta* (e.g. potness for "pot") is "the necessary and sufficient condition for a given word being used to refer to that entity". [source: SEP-LangInd §§4, 7]
- Why the schools differed (Deshpande): poetics wanted poetic meaning; grammarians cognition; Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika a true picture of reality; Mīmāṃsā an authorless, intentionless Veda; Buddhists to show how language fails to portray reality. [source: SEP-LangInd §8]

### Buddhist apoha: Dharmakīrti's causal theory (new material)
- Dignāga c. 480–c. 540; Dharmakīrti 6th or 7th c. (Frauwallner 600–660, "short of conclusive"). [source: SEP-Dhk]
- Particulars are "ineffable", "not an object of words"; grammatical distinctions (subject/predicate, property/possessor) have no ontological counterpart (PV I.61). [source: SEP-Dhk §2.2]
- Tillemans distinguishes Dignāga's "top-down" (descriptive) apoha from Dharmakīrti's "bottom-up" **causal** version: "a causal chain from particular things to perceptions to thoughts and to the utterances of words—in short we have a type of causal theory of reference". Like Putnam, it extends to kinds, not just proper names. Reference is fixed "at the time of [establishing] the linguistic convention" (*saṃketakāle*) by dubbing a sample "This is blue" — "a purely causal event not dependent on any descriptive content" — and then preserved in a convention "from speaker to speaker", with later speakers relying on habits (*vāsanā*) "at the time of using [language] conventions" (*vyavahārakāle*). Tillemans: "much like the two-fold process of reference-fixing and reference-borrowing that figure in causal theories of reference". [source: SEP-Dhk §2.2.2]
- Open questions Tillemans flags: what role remains for the double negation; and whether "having the same powers" reinstates a universal. [source: SEP-Dhk §2.2.2]
- Modern interpretations: Matilal (1971) and Herzberger (1975, "resourceful nominalism") read apoha top-down; Siderits (1995, 2005) distinguishes two negations (choice and exclusion); Hale (2011) says apoha runs against compositionality; parallel with Saussure's differences (D'Ottavi 2010). [source: SEP-Dhk §2.2.1]
- Later apoha: Ratnakīrti's *Apohasiddhi* (SEP-Ratna, consulted only).

## 8. Chinese debates on names (brief; avoid repeating PUB-Logic / PUB-Hundred)
- Later Mohist Canon A78: three kinds of names — unrestricted (*da*, "thing": "any object necessarily requires this name"), classifying (*lei*: "naming something 'horse' is classifying it"), and private (*si*: "naming someone 'Zang' is 'private'", a "name which stays confined to this object"). Lucas (2020) maps them to variables, predicates and individual constants. [source: SEP-CLL]
- Willman: the Mohists' "primary interest is with naming and reference, and only consequentially are they drawn into reflections on meaning". [source: SEP-CLL]
- Mozi (Book 47, "Valuing Duty"): the blind can say "What's bright is white, and what's dark is black", but "collect white and black things together and make the blind select from among them, and they cannot know … it's not by their naming, it's by their selecting" (Fraser's tr.). Knowledge-of is the ability to "distinguish the referent(s)" of a name. Later Canons distinguish knowledge of names, of things, of their relation, and of how to act. [source: SEP-Moh §4]
- Xunzi, "Rectifying Names" (22.2g): "Names have no inherent appropriateness. We designate them [by some word] in order to name them"; Goldin: "the primary concern of the chapter is morality, not linguistics" (citing Fraser 2016); the king fixes names (22.1c); "You can make up the word for 'reality', but you cannot make up reality." [source: SEP-Xun §7] IEP: "a modified conventionalism … once usage was determined by convention, to depart from it is wrong", yet not nominalism. [source: IEP-Xunzi] (Contrast with Mīmāṃsā's eternal relation and Nyāya's divine convention.)
- Gongsun Longzi: only "White Horse", "Indicating Things" and a bit of another dialogue are probably genuine pre-Han (Graham); Hansen's "one name, one thing" reading of the problem of compounds (*niu ma* "oxen-and-horses" = sum vs *bai ma* "white horse" = intersection); Xunzi "solved it by explicitly rejecting the one-name-one-thing principle". [source: SEP-SN §6]
- Confucius, *Analects* 13.3 (Legge): "If names be not correct, language is not in accordance with the truth of things…". [source: WP-Rectification_of_names]

## Debates
- **Was Frege a descriptivist?** IEP: reading sense as descriptive information is the post-Russell interpretation that "makes Frege a forerunner" of descriptivism; but senses "are not linguistic items" and need not be expressible by a description; Searle, Dummett "and others" have reworked sense to evade Kripke. [source: IEP-Frege] SEP-Names notes (May 2006) dispute over whether Frege let a name's sense vary between speakers (SEP-Frege §3.2). Name it as open.
- **Frege's Stoic sources** (Bobzien 2021 vs Rami, Gabriel & Hüser 2024; Gahér 2021). [source: SEP-Frege §3.3]
- **Translating *Bedeutung***: meaning (Blackwell, exegetical neutrality) vs reference (Dummett) vs significance (Tugendhat) vs denotation (Zalta). [source: IEP-FregeLang; SEP-Frege n. 5; WP-Sense_and_reference]
- **Russell vs Strawson vs Donnellan vs Kripke** on descriptions (truth-value gaps; referential/attributive as semantic ambiguity or pragmatics). [source: SEP-Desc]
- **Wide-scope and rigidified-description replies to Kripke**, and Dummett/Stanley's assertoric content (see §4). [source: SEP-Rigid]
- **Marcus/Kripke priority** (Smith vs Neale, Soames). [source: WP-Naming_and_Necessity]
- **Externalism**: Boghossian, Crane, Searle, Fodor's narrow content, Gertler. [source: SEP-Ext]
- **Indian universals vs individuals**: Vyāḍi vs Vājapyāyana, Patañjali's compromise; Mīmāṃsā (*jāti* primary) vs *Kevalavyaktivāda* vs Nyāya (*jāti-viśiṣṭa-vyakti*). [source: SEP-LangInd; SEP-Lit]
- **Eternal vs conventional**: Mīmāṃsā (eternal, authorless) vs Nyāya (God's convention; *agni/asi* argument) vs Buddhists (human convention, *saṃketa*) vs Xunzi (convention fixed by the king). [source: SEP-LangInd; SEP-Kum; SEP-Dhk; SEP-Xun]
- **Prabhākara's anvitābhidhāna**: "sentences are basic" (Arnold, SEP-Kum) vs "words convey already-connected meanings whose sum is the sentence-meaning" (Deshpande, SEP-LangInd). The writer should state the doctrine in Deshpande's words and note Arnold's holistic gloss.
- **Is apoha top-down or causal?** Dignāga vs Dharmakīrti (Tillemans); modern readings Matilal, Herzberger, Siderits, Hale. [source: SEP-Dhk]
- **White Horse** interpretations (universals — now broadly rejected among European/American scholars; part–whole; extension of compounds; use/mention; comic spoof). [source: SEP-SN]
