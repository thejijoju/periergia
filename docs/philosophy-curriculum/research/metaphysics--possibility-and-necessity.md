## 1. The problem; kinds of modality (worked examples)
- Modal claims say what could, must or could not be. Kment's list of ten "cannot" sentences, each true on a different reading: "No one can be both a bachelor and married" (meaning); "You could not have been born of different parents" (your nature); "Nothing can travel faster than light" (law of nature); "One cannot get from London to New York in less than one hour" (technology); "You cannot leave the palace (The doors are locked)"; "You cannot promise to come and then stay at home (It's wrong)" (morality); "...cover letter with 'hey guys'" (etiquette); "You cannot castle if your king is in check" (rules of chess); "You cannot deduct your holidays from your taxes" (law); "Fred cannot be the killer (The evidence shows that he's innocent)" (evidence). [source: SEP-Var intro]
- Kment's Caesar/Cleopatra dialogue: Caesar: "The doors were unlocked. I could have left the palace." Cleopatra: "True. But then again, you couldn't have left the palace. That would have been wrong, given that you promised to meet me here." — one can accept a modal claim in one sense and reject it in another. [source: SEP-Var intro]
- Standard definitions: p is **logically** possible iff consistent with the laws of logic, logically necessary iff it follows from them; **physically** possible iff consistent with the laws of nature, physically necessary iff it follows from them; **metaphysically** possible iff true in at least one possible world, necessary iff true in all (alternatively: consistent with / following from the "laws of metaphysics", Kment 2014, 2021). [source: SEP-Epi §1]
- Necessary = true in all possible worlds; possible = true in some; **contingent** = true in some but not all. [source: SEP-Var §1]
- **Nesting model**: what is physically possible is metaphysically possible, and what is metaphysically possible is logically possible; conversely logical necessity ⊂ metaphysical ⊂ physical necessity. Metaphysical modality is often called the "widest", "strongest", "absolute" modality (Kripke 1980; Lewis 1986; Stalnaker 2003; van Inwagen 1998; Hale 2013; Williamson 2016) — labels that have been criticised (Clarke-Doane; Mallozzi). Some deny metaphysical modality is distinct: Chalmers (2002) "modal monism"; Shoemaker (1998) makes it coincide with physical; Priest (2021) sceptical. [source: SEP-Epi §1]
- **Epistemic** modality ("Fred must have stolen the book", "Mary cannot be in London (she would have called me)"): P is epistemically necessary for A iff A's evidence plus ideal reasoning rules out not-P; agent-relative. [source: SEP-Var §1]
- **Alethic vs non-alethic**: alethic modalities obey "if necessarily p then p" (axiom T); **deontic** modality (obligation/permission) does not, since "even if one is obligated to do p, they might not do p". Kment: "It is both morally and legally necessary ... that no murders are committed, even though murders are in fact being committed." [sources: SEP-Epi §1; SEP-Var §3]
- Deontic logic uses O for "it is obligatory that"; the T axiom (OA → A) "is clearly not appropriate" since "what ought to be is not always the case"; the weaker axiom (D), OA → PA, is accepted by most deontic logicians. [source: SEP-ML §3] (Modal logic "broadly" covers logics of belief, tense, obligation. [source: SEP-ML intro])
- Nomic necessity debate: **necessitarians** (Swoyer 1982; Shoemaker 1980, 1998; Ellis 2001; Bird 2005) hold the laws metaphysically necessary; **contingentists** deny it, many (e.g. Fine 2002) positing a weaker "natural or nomic necessity". Arguments for necessitarianism: from causal essentialism (Shoemaker) and from counterfactual robustness ("No emerald has ever decorated a royal crown" is not robust). Contingentists reply that laws are known a posteriori and their negations are conceivable; necessitarians answer with Kripke's necessary a posteriori or the "schmass" reply. [source: SEP-Var §2]
- Fine's suggestion: logical necessity = metaphysical necessity "because it is true in virtue of the natures of the logical constants" (Fine 1994, 2005); mathematical necessity similarly by restriction. [source: SEP-Var §3]

## 2. De re and de dicto (Quine's planets)
- De dicto = modality "of the dictum/statement" ("Necessarily, all dogs are mammals"); de re = modality "of the thing", a property ascribed to a specific individual in a modal context ("Algol is a dog essentially; a pet accidentally"). De re truth conditions need **transworld identity** (the same individual in many worlds). [source: SEP-PW §1.3]
- **Quine's argument (1943)**: from "The number of planets = 9" and "9 is necessarily greater than 7" (understood as "'9 is greater than 7' is analytic"), "The number of planets is necessarily greater than 7" does not follow, since "The number of planets is greater than 7" is not analytic. So substitutivity of identicals fails in modal contexts; Quine also held quantification into modal contexts meaningless. [source: SEP-RBM §4]
- **Reply (Smullyan 1947, 1948; endorsed by Barcan Marcus 1948; anticipated though not endorsed by Church 1942; later Fitch 1949)**: "the number of planets" is a description, not a name, so treat it Russell's way: it has two readings — the true "There is a unique thing that numbers the planets and it is necessarily greater than 7" (de re) and the false "Necessarily there is a unique thing that numbers the planets and it is greater than 7" (de dicto). [source: SEP-RBM §4]
- SEP-EA's version of the same anti-essentialist point uses "the number of Martian moons" (two) vs "one plus one"; and Quine's mathematical cyclist (*Word and Object* 1960, pp. 195–200): qua mathematician rational but not two-legged, qua cyclist two-legged but not rational — "it makes no sense" to ask whether he is essentially rational independently of a description. Essentialist reply: intuitively the very object two could not have failed to be greater than one (true de re, false de dicto). [source: SEP-EA §1.5] (NB SEP-EA mixes "Martian moons" and "Martian planets" in one paragraph — a slip; use "moons".)
- Quine's definition of essentialism: "the doctrine that some of the attributes of a thing (quite independently of the language in which the thing is referred to, if at all) may be essential to the thing, and others accidental" (1953b/1976, pp. 175–6). [source: SEP-EA §1.3]
- Quine's scepticism stood in "a long line of philosophers dating back at least to David Hume" sceptical that modality is objective; Lewis "parted ways dramatically with his mentor" on this. [source: SEP-PW §2.1.3]

## 3. Aristotle and the ancient paradigms (recap; cross-ref *Aristotle*, *Logic across Traditions*)
- Knuuttila: four ancient modal paradigms — the frequency ("statistical") interpretation, possibility as potency, diachronic (antecedent) modalities, and possibility as non-contradictoriness; none involved simultaneous alternatives. [source: SEP-Med intro]
- **Statistical model**: "what is necessary is always actual, what is impossible is never actual and what is possible is at least sometimes actual"; term introduced by Oscar Becker (1952), applied historically esp. by Hintikka (1973, *Time and Necessity*). Aristotle did not define modal terms so, but examples are found (e.g. *Metaphysics* IX.10); interpreters differ (Hintikka, Sorabji, Waterlow, Gaskin). [source: SEP-Med §1]
- **Principle of plenitude** (Lovejoy, *The Great Chain of Being*, 1936): ancient habit of thinking that all generic possibilities will be actualised — in Plato, Aristotle, the Stoics and Plotinus. [source: SEP-Med §1]
- Aristotle's non-contradiction test (*Prior Analytics* I.13 / *Metaphysics* IX.3, 1047a24–6): "What is possible is that for which, if the actuality of what is said to have the possibility obtains, then nothing impossible will result." [source: SEP-Dio §2.2] (translator not named)
- The **Megarians** (*Metaphysics* IX.3, 1046b29–32): "something is possible only when it is actual ... someone who is not building cannot build, but someone who is building can when building." Aristotle objected that this rules out change (one who is sitting could never stand). [source: SEP-Dio §2.2]
- **Sea battle** (*De Interpretatione* 9, 18b23 ff.): "Tomorrow there will be a sea-battle" / "Tomorrow there will not be a sea-battle" — is one true today? How to distinguish what *will* happen from what *must*? Interpretation is contested (e.g. Łukasiewicz's three-valued reading). Aristotle says "what is, necessarily is when it is" but this does not make it necessary without qualification. [sources: SEP-FC §2, §4.1; SEP-Med §1] — Cross-ref *Free Will and Determinism* (not yet written; *Aristotle* chapter does not cover the sea battle).
- Aristotle's modal syllogistic: "no generally accepted historical reconstruction which would make it a coherent theory" (Knuuttila). Cross-ref *Logic across Traditions* (Malink vs Bobzien already given there). [source: SEP-Med §1]

## 4. Diodorus Cronus and the Master Argument (ARGUMENT 1)
- Diodorus Cronus, born at Iasos in Caria; died c. 284 BCE (SEP-Dio; WP: "died c. 284 BC", citing Dorandi 1999). SEP-FC gives "ca. 340–280 B.C.E."; SEP-Med "fl. 300 BCE". Studied with Apollonius Cronus; nickname "Cronus" perhaps "old codger" (SEP-Dio) / "old fogey" (WP). [sources: SEP-Dio §1; WP-Diodorus_Cronus; SEP-FC §2]
- His five daughters — Menexene, Argeia, Theognis, Artemisia, Pantacleia — "are all said to have been logicians". [source: SEP-DS §1; WP gives spellings Argia, Artemesia, Pantaclea, citing Clement, *Stromata* iv.19] (women in logic — usable)
- Philo (the Dialectician) studied with Diodorus. [source: SEP-DS §1] (consistent with *Hellenistic Philosophy* "his pupil Philo")
- Name: *ho kurieuôn logos*, "master" or "ruling" argument. Not to be confused with Berkeley's "Master Argument" (a later nickname; *Berkeley and Hume* already uses that term). [source: WP-Diodorus_Cronus]
- **Diodorus's definitions** (Boethius, *On Int.* 2.II.234–5): "Possible is that which either is or will be <true>; impossible that which is false and will not be true; necessary that which is true and will not be false; non-necessary that which either is false already or will be false." Not every proposition is necessary or impossible: "It is day" is possible and non-necessary. Hellenistic philosophers saw his notions as jeopardising freedom: if "Dio goes to Corinth" never will be true, it is impossible. [source: SEP-DS §4.2]
- **The text** (Epictetus, *Discourses* II.19.1), Bobzien's rendering: "The Master argument seems to have been developed from the following starting points: There is a general conflict between the following three <statements>: (I) every past true <proposition> is necessary; and (II) the impossible does not follow from the possible; and (III) something is possible which neither is true nor will be true. Being aware of this conflict, Diodorus used the plausibility of the first two <statements> in order to show that (IV) nothing is possible that neither is nor will be true." [source: SEP-DS §5]
- Duncombe's rendering: "...these three conflict with each other: Every past truth is necessary; the impossible does not follow from the possible; there is a possible truth which neither is true nor will be. When he saw this conflict, Diodorus used the persuasiveness of the first two premises to establish that nothing is possible that neither is true nor will be" (Epictetus, *Discourses* 2.19.1–2 = Döring 131). [source: SEP-Dio §2.1]
- **Step by step**: MA1 every past truth is necessary; MA2 the impossible does not follow from the possible; MA3 there is a possible truth which neither is nor will be true. The three are inconsistent; Diodorus kept MA1 and MA2 and denied MA3, concluding (D) "All possible truths are either true or will be true". [source: SEP-Dio §2.1]
- **Worked example** (Duncombe, "tentative, non-technical"): I did not train in gymnastics as a child, so I am not and never will be an Olympic gymnast. Suppose it possible that I will be one. That implies I trained from childhood. But since I did not, it is by now impossible that I trained (MA1). So an impossibility would follow from a possibility, contrary to MA2. So it is not possible that I will be an Olympic gymnast. [source: SEP-Dio §2.1]
- **Replies (who denied what)**: Panthoides, Cleanthes and Antipater of Tarsus used II and III to reject I; Chrysippus accepted I and rejected II (Epictetus II.19.2–5; Cicero, *De fato*). [sources: WP-Diodorus_Cronus; SEP-Dio §2.1 (Panthoides rejected MA1, Chrysippus MA2)]
- The original argument is lost: "Despite being widely known in antiquity, the argument has not come down to us"; people discussed it over dinner (Epictetus II.19.8); no ancient logician thought it invalid; most modern reconstructions (pioneered by Arthur Prior 1955) treat the propositions as temporally indefinite and "follows" as logical consequence. [sources: SEP-DS §5; SEP-Dio §2.1]
- Taken as fatalism (Alexander, *In An. Pr.* 183.34–184.10), as an argument for Diodorus's account of possibility, or as a sophism to be solved (Epictetus II.18.17) — "not exclusive options". Possible antecedent: the Megarian view in *Metaphysics* IX.3. [source: SEP-Dio §§2.1–2.2]
- Diodorus's modal theory inspired Arthur Prior's tense logic. [source: SEP-DS §1]
- Stoics rejected the Master Argument though they were determinists about fate. [source: SEP-Med §1]

## 5. Medieval: from frequency to synchronic alternatives (recap; cross-ref *Scotus, Ockham*)
- Boethius transmitted the frequency and potency models to the early Middle Ages. [source: SEP-Med §2]
- The new paradigm of modality as **simultaneous alternatives** entered Western thought in early-12th-century discussions influenced by Augustine's conception of God choosing between alternative histories (Abelard, Gilbert of Poitiers). [source: SEP-Med intro, §2]
- **Scotus**: "I do not call something contingent because it is not always or necessarily the case, but because its opposite could be actual at the very moment when it occurs" (*Ord.* I.2.1.1–2, 86) — a denial of the necessity of the present and of the frequency account. A logical possibility is something "to which to be is not repugnant", though it may not be compossible with other possibilities; possibilities would be what they are "even if there were no God" (Ord. I.7.1). [source: SEP-Med §4] (*Scotus, Ockham* chapter already gives "synchronic contingency" for the will; do not repeat.)
- **Debate**: Knuuttila presents Scotus as the innovator of a modal semantics of alternatives; the thesis of Scotus's innovativeness is criticised in Pasnau (2020, "Medieval Modal Spaces"); commentators also divide on whether Scotus's possibilities are independent of God as such (Knuuttila 1996) or only formally (Normore 2003; Hoffmann 2009). [source: SEP-Med §4]
- Buridan distinguished "four grades of necessity" and used a notion of possibility *secundum imaginationem*. [source: SEP-Med §4]

## 6. Islamic: Avicenna's necessary existent; kalām alternatives; al-Ghazālī (cross-ref *Falsafa*, *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes*)
- Avicenna: an existent is either necessary in itself (*wāǧib*/*wājib*) or possible in itself (*mumkin*) — every existent except the First Principle is possible in itself; "necessarily existent" = *al-wāǧib al-wuǧūd*, "possibly existent" = *al-mumkin al-wuǧūd* (*Ilāhiyyāt* I.6). The modality depends on essence; what is possible in itself but exists is necessary through another (its cause); "what is possible is always and inevitably an effect", the Necessary Existent has no cause and no "why". [source: SEP-Av §4.1] (Consistent with *Falsafa*, which gives the proof step by step: recap only.)
- Avicenna's modality "follows the statistical model of Aristotle": fire necessarily burns cotton because "Fire causes cotton to combust" was, is and always will be true. [source: SEP-Gh §7]
- **Ashʿarite kalām** "does not share the statistical model ... and developed an understanding that is closer to our modern view of the modalities as referring to synchronic alternative states of affairs" (Griffel, citing Knuuttila 1998). God is the particularising agent (*mukhaṣṣiṣ*); particularisation (*takhṣīṣ*) "includes implicitly an understanding of possible worlds that are different from this". [source: SEP-Gh §7]
- **Al-Juwaynī** (al-Ghazālī's teacher), *Creed for Niẓām al-Mulk*: every sound thinker knows "the possibility of what is possible, the necessity of what is necessary, and the impossibility of what is impossible"; seeing a building, one knows it is "a possibility that comes into being (*min jawāz ḥudūthihī*)", its actual state "from among its possible states (*jāʾizāt*)" and "not impossible in the intellect had it not been built" — a synchronic alternative; this is possibility/contingency (*imkān*). [source: SEP-Gh §7] (So *jawāz* = admissibility/possibility is verified in this sense.)
- Al-Ghazālī: the connection of cause and effect is contingent because an alternative is conceivable: "it is within divine power to create satiety without eating, to create death without a deep cut in the neck ..." (*Incoherence*, trans. Marmura, 166). Griffel: a world where fire does not burn cotton "can be conceived in our minds, which means it is a possible world. God, however, did not choose to create such an alternative possible world"; for al-Ghazālī modalities exist only as judgements in the mind, not in reality; Kukkonen (2000, 488–9): he shifts the presumption "from the plane of the actualized reality to the plane of mental conceivability". [source: SEP-Gh §7]
- Kukkonen, "Possible Worlds in the *Tahâfut al-falâsifa*: Al-Ghazâlî on Creation and Contingency", *Journal of the History of Philosophy* 38 (2000): 479–502. Knuuttila: "The idea of divine choice between alternatives was absent in Avicenna and Averroes, but it was defended by al-Ghazali". [source: SEP-Med §2 and bibliography]
- Al-Ghazālī also held that this universe is the best of all possible worlds: "there is in possibility nothing more wondrous than what is" (*laysa fī l-imkān abdaʿ mimmā kān*) (*Iḥyāʾ*), provoking a long debate (Ormsby 1984) — a striking parallel to Leibniz (comparison is ours; hedge). Unlike Avicenna, God "chooses between alternatives". [source: SEP-Gh §6]

## 7. Leibniz (recap only; cross-ref *Leibniz*) and Spinoza (cross-ref *Spinoza*)
- A world is "a collection of finite things"; "the universe is only a certain kind of collection of compossibles" (to Bourguet, G III 573). Substances are **compossible** iff no contradiction among the predicates derivable from their complete concepts (Look's example: Don and Ron cannot both be *the tallest man* at t). A possible world also includes laws of nature (to Arnauld, 14 July 1686). [source: SEP-LMod §1]
- Leibniz "had all the tools at his disposal" for possible-worlds semantics but did not develop it explicitly; his necessity/contingency account rests on **infinite analysis** (necessary truths reduce to identities; contingent ones need analysis "to infinity through reasons for reasons ... understood completely by God, who alone traverses the infinite series in one stroke of mind"). [source: SEP-LMod §2] (Same quotation already in *Leibniz*.)
- Truths of reasoning (resolvable by analysis into identities; Principle of Contradiction) vs truths of fact (no finite analysis; reason lies outside the series; PSR). [source: SEP-Lz §6.2]
- Leibniz famously held "maximal essentialism" (every property essential); individual essences purely qualitative. [source: SEP-EA §1.4] (NB the *Leibniz* chapter treats this as a debated reading ("Some read him as holding..."; others counterparts) — keep the hedge.)
- Merely possible individuals exist in the divine mind: "In the region of the eternal verities are found all the possibles" (*Theodicy* §189). By contrast Spinoza (*Ethics* Ip33) holds there are no unactualised possibles. [source: SEP-LMod]
- Spinoza: "in nature there is nothing contingent" (Ip29); "things could have been produced by God in no other way, and in no other order than they have been produced" (Ip33); modality was the "principal foundation" of the *Ethics* (Ep75). Read as **necessitarianism** ("The actual world ... is the only possible world"), but interpreters disagree whether he was a necessitarian about modes. [source: SEP-SpM intro] (*Spinoza* chapter already has Curley vs Garrett; cross-ref.)

## 8. Possible-worlds semantics (brief; cross-ref *Formal Logic*)
- Possible worlds "failed to gain any real traction among philosophers until the 1960s", when invoked for modal logic. Before that, systems existed (Lewis and Langford 1932) without a semantics comparable to Tarski's, which fed Quine's scepticism. [source: SEP-PW §1]
- Lineage: "traced most clearly back to the work of Carnap (1947), its basic development culminating in the work of Hintikka (1957, 1961), Bayart (1958, 1959), and Kripke (1959, 1963a, 1963b)". Kanger, *Provability in Logic* (1957), is in SEP-PW's bibliography. Bayart (working largely independently) proved soundness and completeness of first-order S5; "surprisingly unfamiliar". [sources: SEP-PW §1.2; SEP-PWn n.5] (*Formal Logic* says "often credited to Saul Kripke's papers of 1959 and 1963, but Kanger published similar ideas in 1957, and the origins are still discussed" — consistent; this chapter may add Hintikka, Bayart, Carnap.)
- Kripke 1959 = "A Completeness Theorem in Modal Logic", *Journal of Symbolic Logic* 24(1): 1–14; 1963b "Semantical Considerations on Modal Logic", *Acta Philosophica Fennica* 16. [source: SEP-PW bibliography] Written at 17, published a year later. [source: WP-Saul_Kripke]
- Accessibility relation; S5 etc. [source: SEP-ML §6] (already in *Formal Logic*.)
- Ruth Barcan (Marcus) (1921–2012): first published construction of quantified modal logic (1946); proved the necessity of identity in 1947 (structure resembles Kripke's 1971 proof); in 1961 "Modalities and Intensional Languages" called proper names "tags": "This tag, a proper name, has no meaning. It simply tags." Whether she then anticipated the direct-reference theory of ordinary names is "neither an unqualified yes nor a simple no" (Ballarin). [source: SEP-RBM §§1, 2.3, 4.3] (Quentin Smith's claim of anticipation is reported in WP-Naming_and_Necessity; *Analytic Philosophy since 1950* already calls it controversial and points here.)

## 9. What are possible worlds? Lewis's modal realism (ARGUMENT 2)
- David Lewis (28 Sept 1941 – 14 Oct 2001). [source: WP-David_Lewis_(philosopher)]
- Counterfactuals (1973), 84: "I believe, and so do you, that things could have been different in countless ways. But what does this mean? Ordinary language permits the paraphrase: there are many ways things could have been besides the way they actually are. ... taking the paraphrase at its face value, I therefore believe in the existence of entities that might be called 'ways things could have been.' I prefer to call them 'possible worlds.'" [source: SEP-DL §6]
- *On the Plurality of Worlds* (Blackwell, 1986) grew out of his 1984 John Locke lectures; chapter 1 "A Philosophers' Paradise". [source: SEP-DL §6]
- **Argument step by step (Lewis 1986 ch. 1, as reconstructed by Weatherson/Parent)**: (1) Quantifying over possibilities makes our best theories of four things much better: modality (contingent existence, modal comparatives, supervenience), closeness (counterfactuals), content (belief as sets of possibilities, following Stalnaker 1984) and properties (sets of possibilia). (2) We should believe in the entities our best theories need — "an extended indispensability argument" (cf. unobservables in science). (3) So there are possible worlds. (4) Abstract ("ersatz") substitutes fail (ch. 3). (5) So the worlds are concrete. "Lewis does not say that possibilities are absolutely indispensable, only that they make our philosophical theories so much better". [sources: SEP-DL §§6.1, 6.3; IEP-MM §2]
- **The theory**: a world is a maximal spatiotemporally connected object; other worlds are as concrete as ours, isolated (no spatiotemporal or causal relation to ours); "actual" is **indexical**, like "here" or "now" — the actual world is simply ours. Plenitude via **recombination**: "anything can coexist with anything" (dragon and unicorn) and anything can fail to coexist with anything ("the Humean denial of necessary connections"; talking head). Lewis 1986, 1: "The world we live in is a very inclusive thing...". [source: SEP-PW §§2.1.1–2.1.4]
- Payoff: a genuine **reduction** — modal operators become quantifiers over worlds, worlds defined in non-modal terms. Lewis: no other theory "explains so much so economically". He distinguishes quantitative from qualitative parsimony: he multiplies entities, not kinds. [sources: SEP-PW §2.1.5; IEP-MM §2]
- **Counterpart theory** (Lewis 1968, "Counterpart Theory and Quantified Modal Logic", *Journal of Philosophy* 65: 113–126): individuals are world-bound; a counterpart is something in another world that resembles you closely (and nothing there resembles you more, roughly); "Algol could have been a stray" = some counterpart of Algol is a stray; □Fa = all a's counterparts are F. The counterpart relation is vague and context-sensitive — so "Could Malloy have been brought by a stork?" gets different answers in different contexts; motivated also by the "problem of accidental intrinsics" (Malloy cannot have two heights). [sources: SEP-DL §6.4; SEP-PW §2.1.3; SEP-LM §8]
- **Objection 1: the incredulous stare.** Lewis concedes his theory "does disagree, to an extreme extent, with firm common sense opinion about what there is" (1986, 133), but concludes the worlds "ought to be accepted as true. The theoretical benefits are worth it" (135). Section 2.8 of the book is titled "The Incredulous Stare" (p. 133). [sources: SEP-PW §2.1.5; IEP-MM §2; WS-4]
- **Objection 2: the Humphrey objection** (Kripke 1980, 45 n.13): on Lewis's view, when we say Humphrey might have won the 1968 election we are "not talking about something that might have happened to Humphrey, but to someone else". Argument from concern: Humphrey cares that *he* could have won, not that someone like him wins elsewhere. [sources: SEP-LM §8.2; SEP-Fic §4; SEP-RD §2.1.1]
- **Lewis's reply**: on his view "it is indeed Humphrey himself who might have won, in virtue of having a winning counterpart" (1986, 194–6, 246); objects are represented as existing at many worlds though not literally parts of them. Lewis: "The philosophers' chorus on behalf of 'trans-world identity' is merely insisting that, for instance, it is Humphrey himself who might have existed under other conditions, … who might have won the presidency ... All that is uncontroversial. The controversial question is how he manages to have these modal properties" (1986, 198). Also: any theory of worlds faces a version of the concern objection (1986, 195–7). Rosen (1990): the objection shows at most a "pragmatic" cost. [sources: SEP-LM §8.2; SEP-TWI; SEP-Fic]
- Related "irrelevance" objection (Salmon 1988; Plantinga 1987): what happens to someone else in another world seems irrelevant to what Socrates could have done; Menzel calls it "arguably question-begging". [sources: SEP-PWc; IEP-MM §2]
- Other objections: island universes (Lewis must deny two spatiotemporally unconnected universes in one world; 1986, 66ff; Bricker 2001); epistemic access (we cannot causally interact with other worlds; Lewis: modal knowledge from tacit grasp of recombination); circularity (Lycan: "world" means "possible world"); paradoxes (Forrest & Armstrong 1984 on recombination; Kaplan/Davies on cardinality); Forrest 1982 inductive scepticism; Adams 1974 moral consequences — each with Lewis's reply in SEP-DL §6.2. [sources: SEP-PWc; IEP-MM §2; SEP-DL §6.2]

## 10. Ersatzism / actualism (abstractionism) and Lewis's objections
- **Abstractionism**: worlds are abstract — total ways things could be. Adams (1974) "Theories of Actuality" (*Noûs* 8): maximal consistent sets of propositions ("world stories"); Fine (1977, following Prior): maximal consistent propositions; Plantinga (*The Nature of Necessity*, Clarendon Press 1974; "Actualism and Possible Worlds", 1976): maximal possible **states of affairs**; Stalnaker (1976, "Possible Worlds", *Noûs* 10): ways the world might have been. Carnap's 1947 maximal consistent sets of sentences were an early version. [sources: SEP-PW §2.2.1; IEP-MM §3; SEP-PA §4.4]
- Plantinga: SOAs are primitive; a world = a possible and total SOA; the actual world is the one that obtains; individuals exist in many worlds (transworld identity), no counterparts needed; "haecceities" like *being Plantinga* exist necessarily even if unexemplified. [sources: SEP-PW §2.2.1; SEP-PA §4.2]
- **Actualism** = "everything that there is ... is actual"; most abstractionists are actualists; possibilism denies it. [source: SEP-PW §2.2.3]
- The price: abstract worlds are defined in irreducibly modal terms ("possible", "consistent"), so no reduction of modality; abstractionists reply that their goal is the converse — to understand worlds in terms of modality. [source: SEP-PW §2.2.2]
- **Lewis's objections (Plurality ch. 3)**: *linguistic* ersatzism (1) takes modality as primitive, (2) cannot distinguish qualitatively indiscernible possibilities, (3) cannot allow "alien" properties; *pictorial* ersatzism either too coarse or as ontologically costly as concrete worlds; *magical* ersatzism (defined negatively) is mysterious. Weatherson: the primitivists Adams (1974), Plantinga (1974) and Stalnaker (1976) arguably count as "magicians". [source: SEP-DL §6.3]
- Plantinga (1974, 115f.) and Salmon complained that on Lewis's view everything has all its properties essentially — SEP-LM calls this an early misunderstanding. [source: SEP-LM §8.2]

## 11. Modal fictionalism (brief)
- Gideon Rosen, "Modal Fictionalism", *Mind* 99 (1990): 327–354 — first developed fictionalism proper. "Possibly p" is true iff according to the fiction of possible worlds (Lewis's theory) p; compare "According to the Holmes stories, Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street". Advantages: avoids the incredulous stare and the epistemic problem. Problems: commitment to stories; Lewis's theory says modal realism is necessary (answered by noting Lewis 1969-style "there are many worlds" is true at no world); incompleteness where Lewis is silent (Rosen: leave such claims without truth-value). Rosen (b. 1963), Princeton. [sources: IEP-MM §4; SEP-Fic; WP-Gideon_Rosen]
- Fictionalism is usually fictionalist about *worlds*, not about "it is possible that there be a tail-less kangaroo". [source: SEP-Fic intro]

## 12. Kripke: rigid designation, necessary a posteriori, essentialism (cross-ref *Analytic Philosophy since 1950*, *Reason, Experience*)
- Saul Kripke (13 Nov 1940 – 15 Sept 2022). *Naming and Necessity* lectures transcript first published 1972 in Davidson & Harman (eds.), *Semantics of Natural Language*; book 1980 (Harvard). [sources: WP-Saul_Kripke; WP-Naming_and_Necessity; SEP-PW bibliography]
- Rigid designator: "designates the same object in all possible worlds in which that object exists and never designates anything else"; Kripke coined the term. Descriptions ("the brightest non-lunar object in the evening sky") are not rigid (Mars might have been brightest). [source: SEP-RD §1.1]
- Kripke: "When you ask whether it is necessary or contingent that Nixon won the election, you are asking the intuitive question whether in some counterfactual situation this man would in fact have lost the election" (1972, 44). [source: IEP-MI §5a]
- Two notions of rigidity: Kripke 1971 "in any possible world where the object in question does exist ... we use the designator ... to designate that object"; but 1980, 78: "'Hitler' refers here, still rigidly, to something that would not exist" ("obstinate" rigidity, Salmon). [source: SEP-RD §1.2]
- Kripke conditional / deduction model: (K1) If Hesperus = Phosphorus, then necessarily Hesperus = Phosphorus (known "by a priori philosophical analysis", 1971: 180; 1980: 108); (K2) Hesperus = Phosphorus (empirical); (K3) Necessarily Hesperus = Phosphorus, a posteriori. [source: SEP-Epi §2.3]
- Examples of the necessary a posteriori: "Mark Twain (if he exists) is Samuel Clemens"; "If gold exists, then it has atomic number 79"; "If this desk exists, it is made of wood". Contingent a priori: Evans's "Julius" (and Kripke's metre stick, already in *Analytic*). [source: SEP-Var §1.1]
- **Origin essentialism**: an object could not have had a radically different origin; Kripke (1972/1980, pp. 112–114) endorsed it, mentioning both a "branching conception of possible worlds" and "constitutional sufficiency principles". Defended by Salmon (1981), Forbes (1985), McGinn (1976). Sortal essentialism: Socrates could not have been "a credit card account". [sources: SEP-EA §1.4; SEP-EAo]
- **Salmon's sufficiency argument (table)**: (Compossibility) if table a is originally made from matter m, and a table could be made from non-overlapping matter m*, then possibly a is made from m and some table b from m*; (Origin Uniqueness) no table has two wholly different material origins; (Sufficiency) any possible table originally made from m* is b; so (with the necessity of distinctness) a could not have been originally made from m*. Salmon himself admits this version of Sufficiency is false (the "tolerance" and "recycling" problems), seeking a modified version. [source: SEP-EAo §1]
- Kripke on the table illusion: imagining this wooden table made of ice "is not to imagine this table as made of…ice, but to…imagine another table, resembling this one in all the external details made of…ice" (1972, 114); the false statement should be replaced by an "appropriate corresponding qualitative statement" ("two distinct bodies might have occupied ... the very positions actually occupied by Hesperus-Phosphorus-Venus", 143). [source: IEP-MI §4]
- Kripke denies such a redescription for pain without C-fibre firing, since to be a pain is to be felt as pain — his anti-materialist use. [source: IEP-MI §4] Objections (Duffy): non-uniform (Nixon intuitions are about Nixon himself; table intuitions not about the table); "person in the know" still feels the illusion. [source: IEP-MI §5]
- Natural kinds: cats are necessarily animals; water necessarily H2O (Kripke 1972/1980; Putnam 1975) — necessary a posteriori connections between properties. [source: SEP-EA §1.4]
- Queen Elizabeth II example: Kripke asks whether she could have been born of different parents (e.g. Mr and Mrs Truman) and answers that a child of theirs, however similar, would not be her; responds to Timothy Sprigge. [source: WS-2 only — secondary summaries; the page range 112–114 is confirmed by SEP-EA for origin essentialism. Treat the Truman detail as secondary.]

## 13. Fine, "Essence and Modality" (1994) (ARGUMENT 3)
- Kit Fine (b. 26 March 1946), NYU. [source: WP-Kit_Fine] Paper: "Essence and Modality: The Second Philosophical Perspectives Lecture", *Philosophical Perspectives* 8 (1994): 1–16. [source: SEP-EA bibliography]
- Two senses of "essential": **modal** (a property an object must have) vs **"whatness"** (a property that says what the object is; term from Almog 1991). The modal sense dominated the second half of the 20th century; the whatness sense is "dominant among analytic metaphysicians today". [source: SEP-EA intro]
- **Argument step by step**: (1) The modal account (MAW): P is part of o's essence iff necessarily o has P (or: has P if o exists). (2) Necessarily, if Socrates exists, he belongs to {Socrates}, the set whose only member is Socrates. (3) So by MAW, belonging to {Socrates} is essential to Socrates. (4) But intuitively it is no part of what Socrates is (nor is being distinct from the Eiffel Tower, nor being such that 2 + 2 = 4). (5) Yet having Socrates as a member *is* essential to {Socrates} — an **asymmetry** that necessity (symmetric here) cannot capture. (6) So essence is not a modal notion. Second movement: reverse the order — metaphysical necessities are true in virtue of essences (real definitions). [sources: SEP-EA §3; SEP-Epi §5]
- "Finean essentialism" (FE): "Metaphysical necessities are true in virtue of (or: are grounded in) essentialist truths"; defended by Lowe, Hale (2013), Kment, Rosen 2015 and others; criticised by Della Rocca, Correia 2007, Wildman, Leech 2018, Mackie 2020. [source: SEP-Epi §5]
- **Replies**: (I) take whatness essence as primitive (Fine 1995, Correia, Lowe, Koslicki, Hale); (II) improved modal accounts adding a "not cheap" condition — joint-carving (Cowling 2013, Wildman 2013) or intrinsic (Denby 2014; this removes the singleton case since Socrates is not intrinsically a member of {Socrates}); (III) non-modal accounts. Against the other direction: Mackie (2020), Leech (2021). [source: SEP-EA §§3–4]
- **Historiographical debate**: Fine claimed the modal account had become "the usual" view "in the last twenty years or so" without naming anyone; later writers called it "widespread", "once the dominant account"; SEP-EA authors adopt "a neutral" stance on whether Kripke and Plantinga actually held it. [source: SEP-EA §3]

## 14. Modal epistemology: conceivability as a guide to possibility (ARGUMENT 4)
- Central question: "How can we come to know, or be justified in believing, what is necessary, possible, contingent, essential, and accidental?" Access vs Navigation questions (Vaidya & Wallner 2021). Necessity-first vs possibility-first (Hale 2002). Modal rationalism vs empiricism. [source: SEP-Epi intro, §2]
- **Hume**: "'Tis an establish'd maxim in metaphysics, that whatever the mind clearly conceives includes the idea of possible existence, or in other words, that nothing we imagine is absolutely impossible" (T 1.2.2.8; SBN 32). Hume uses it e.g. to argue that "whatever has a beginning has also a cause of existence" is "neither intuitively nor demonstrably certain" (T 1.3.3.2–3). Scholars debate whose conceiving counts (ordinary or ideal) and whether Hume also accepts the converse "Inconceivability Principle" (suggested by the same paragraph). [source: IEP-HI §6; wording also confirmed WS-1]
- **Descartes** (Sixth Meditation, AT VII 78, CSM II 54): "everything which I clearly and distinctly understand is capable of being created by God so as to correspond exactly with my understanding of it", so understanding one thing apart from another shows they can be separated "at least by God"; clear and distinct ideas of himself as thinking, non-extended and body as extended, non-thinking ⇒ "I am really distinct from my body, and can exist without it". [sources: IEP-HI §6 (first clause); IEP-DMB §3] Skirry: he "has not adequately eliminated the possibility of minds being extended things like brains". Cross-ref *Descartes* (argument already set out; recap only).
- Descartes vs Hume: for Descartes, clear and distinct conceptions come from the pure intellect; Hume's principle is about clear imagination. [source: IEP-HI §6]
- **Yablo (1993)**, "Is Conceivability a Guide to Possibility?", *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 53(1): 1–42: p is conceivable for X if X can imagine a world X takes to verify p; conceivability gives *defeasible* evidence ("[T]o imagine an X is thereby to enjoy the appearance that an X could exist", 30); analogy with perception, which is fallible yet justifying. Yablo born 1957, MIT. [sources: SEP-Epi §4.1; WP-Stephen_Yablo]
- **Chalmers**: *The Conscious Mind* (OUP, 1996); "Does Conceivability Entail Possibility?" in Gendler & Hawthorne (eds.), *Conceivability and Possibility* (Clarendon, 2002), 145–200. Weak Modal Rationalism: ideal, positive, primary conceivability *entails* primary possibility. Distinctions: prima facie vs ideal; positive (coherently imagining a scenario) vs negative (cannot rule out a priori); primary (considered as actual: "the liquid drinkable stuff in lakes and rivers" could have been XYZ) vs secondary (considered counterfactually: water is H2O in every world). "Water is H2O" is secondarily necessary but primarily contingent. "Modal monism": one space of worlds ("these worlds are all first-class metaphysical possibilities", 2002: 165). Chalmers b. 20 April 1966. [sources: SEP-Epi §4.1; WP-David_Chalmers]
- **Zombie argument** (simplest form): (1) Zombies are conceivable. (2) Whatever is conceivable is possible. (3) Therefore zombies are possible. "Clearly ... valid"; both premises problematic. Kripke used a similar argument (1972/80); versions by Nagel 1974, Levine 2001, Stoljar. [source: SEP-Zom §3]
- **Objection (a posteriori physicalism)**: conceivability is epistemic, possibility metaphysical; given psychophysical identities it is a posteriori that any physical duplicate of our world has the same sensory states (Hill & McLaughlin 1999; Loar; Block & Stalnaker 1999). **Chalmers's reply**: a dilemma — either primary conceivability of zombies entails their secondary possibility (materialism false) or Russellian monism is true. **Parity objection**: anti-zombies (physical duplicates made conscious by the physical facts; Frankish 2007) are equally conceivable, so (2) yields a contradiction; reply: deny anti-zombies are conceivable (Chalmers 2010). [source: SEP-Zom §§5.1, 6] Cross-ref *The Mind–Body Problem* / *Consciousness* for the full debate.
- **Water counterexample and Kripke's explanation**: before learning Hesperus = Phosphorus one may find it conceivable that one exists without the other — a modal error (Yablo's challenge). Kripke's similarity account: what we really conceive is a qualitatively similar situation. [sources: SEP-Epi §4.1; IEP-MI §4]
- **Williamson** (*The Philosophy of Philosophy*, Blackwell 2007): modal knowledge is "a special case" of knowledge of counterfactuals; "the ordinary cognitive capacity to handle counterfactual conditionals carries with it the cognitive capacity to handle metaphysical modality" (2007: 136). □A iff (¬A □→ ⊥); ◇A iff ¬(A □→ ⊥). Rock example: "If the bush had not been there, the rock would have ended in the lake" (2007: 142); gold: "If we know enough chemistry, our counterfactual development of the supposition that gold is [not] the element with atomic number 79 will generate a contradiction" (2007: 164). Objection: Goodman's problem of cotenability (which background facts to hold fixed). Williamson b. 6 Aug 1955. [sources: SEP-Epi §4.2; WP-Timothy_Williamson]
- Other routes: essence-based (Hale, Lowe, Kment); similarity/induction (Roca-Royes: "Twin-Messy"); perception of potentialities (Barbara Vetter 2015, 2021, 2023: we "literally see potentialities", modal knowledge "distinctively empirical"). [source: SEP-Epi §§5–7]
- Epistemology of worlds: Lewis's view faces the Benacerraf-style problem of knowing about causally isolated worlds. [source: IEP-MM §2]

## 15. Indian and Chinese traditions (hedged)
- **Dharmakīrti** (dates disputed: Frauwallner 600–660 CE; some now earlier, 6th c.): causal "connection by essential natures" (*svabhāva-pratibandha*) guarantees a "nexus where [the effect] will not be without [the cause]" (*avinābhāvaniyama*); real smoke must come from fire, otherwise it is "pseudo-smoke"; a "termite tower" with fire's causal powers would be on fire. [source: SEP-Dhk §1.4]
- **Crucial hedge (Tillemans)**: "there certainly is no development of a modal logic of necessity and possibility and talk of possible worlds in Dharmakīrti, nor in Indian philosophy generally for that matter"; key terms are *niścaya* (certainty) and *avyabhicāra* (non-deviation); "A more likely interpretation" reads them epistemically — justified confidence about the actual world — "not ... metaphysical necessity throughout worlds". Partly because Indian philosophy has "no truck with propositions" as mind-independent abstract entities (note 25, crediting Mark Siderits). Others (Dunne 2004; Iwata 2004) use "necessity". [sources: SEP-Dhk §1.4; SEP-Dhkn n.25] Dharmakīrti vs Hume on causation compared (SEP-Dhk §1.5); cross-ref *Causation*, *Induction*, *Logic across Traditions*.
- **Navya-Nyāya / Gaṅgeśa**: *tarka* = "hypothetical reasoning", not itself a knowledge source but able "to shift the balance of what it is rational to accept"; mainly helps grasp pervasions (*vyāpti*). It reveals "a fallacy or defeater" for ∼p (*pratikūla-tarka*) or supports (*anukūla-tarka*, e.g. economy). Ten types: self-dependence, mutual dependence, circularity, infinite regress, unwanted consequence (contradiction), being presupposed, hasty generalisation, differentiation failure, lightness, heaviness (Bagchi 1951). Example: doubting that smoke is pervaded by fire, then why does the doubter "resort to fire for smoke, to food to allay hunger"? [source: SEP-Gan §5.2] NB: Phillips calls *tarka* "hypothetical", not "counterfactual" — use "hypothetical/suppositional reasoning".
- Nyāya's four kinds of **absence**: prior absence (the pot the potter contemplates), destruction, mutual absence (a pot is not a cloth), and "absolute"/locatable absence (my glasses not on my desk), each with a "counterpositive". [source: SEP-Gan §2]
- **Mohist Canons**: the Mohists used concepts of logical "permissibility" (*kě* 可) and "perversity" (*bèi* 悖) "intertwined with a rough notion of logical consistency", plus versions of excluded middle and non-contradiction, applied to predicating terms of objects, not to propositions; Canon A73: "it is impermissible for both to be impermissible" (ox / non-ox). They did not formulate an explicit notion of logical consequence. [sources: SEP-MC §8; SEP-MCn n.25]
- *Bì* 必 ("necessary, must"): Fraser — the Mohist notion "does not appear to distinguish between logical necessity, causal necessity or constant conjunction, epistemic certainty, and various forms of obligation"; Graham's claim that "disputation" yields necessary a priori knowledge is rejected by Fraser. [source: SEP-MCn n.14; WS-3 pointed to it]

- "it is Humphrey himself who might have existed under other conditions, … who might have won the presidency, who exists according to many worlds and wins according to some of them. All that is uncontroversial. The controversial question is how he manages to have these modal properties." — Lewis, *Plurality* (1986), 198 [source: SEP-TWI]
- "this is not to imagine this table as made of…ice, but to…imagine another table, resembling this one in all the external details made of…ice." — Kripke, "Naming and Necessity" (1972), 114 [source: IEP-MI §4]
- "This tag, a proper name, has no meaning. It simply tags." — Ruth Barcan Marcus, "Modalities and Intensional Languages" (1961), 310 [source: SEP-RBM §4.3]
- "the ordinary cognitive capacity to handle counterfactual conditionals carries with it the cognitive capacity to handle metaphysical modality." — Williamson, *The Philosophy of Philosophy* (2007), 136 [source: SEP-Epi §4.2]
- "[T]o imagine an X is thereby to enjoy the appearance that an X could exist" — Yablo (1993), 30 [source: SEP-Epi §4.1]
- "What is possible is that for which, if the actuality of what is said to have the possibility obtains, then nothing impossible will result." — Aristotle, *Metaphysics* IX.3, 1047a24–6 (translator unnamed; Duncombe's) [source: SEP-Dio §2.2]

## Debates
- Was Aristotle a "statistical" modal theorist? Hintikka (1973) yes, in places; others (Sorabji, Waterlow, Gaskin) see unrealised singular possibilities or diachronic modality. [source: SEP-Med §1]
- How the Master Argument ran: four families of reconstruction (temporally definite vs indefinite propositions; "follows" as temporal succession vs logical consequence); most follow Prior. Whether it argues for fatalism, defines possibility, or is a sophism. [sources: SEP-DS §5; SEP-Dio §2.1]
- Scotus's originality (Knuuttila vs Pasnau 2020). [source: SEP-Med §4]
- Al-Ghazālī: possible worlds reading (Kukkonen 2000; Griffel) — note Frank (1992) read al-Ghazālī as closer to Avicenna's determinism; Griffel disagrees. [source: SEP-Gh §6]
- Leibniz: maximal essentialism vs counterparts (see *Leibniz*); "all the tools" but no explicit semantics. [sources: SEP-LMod §2; SEP-EA §1.4]
- Concrete (Lewis) vs abstract (Plantinga, Adams, Stalnaker) vs combinatorial (Armstrong) vs fictional (Rosen) worlds; reduction vs primitive modality. [sources: SEP-PW §2; IEP-MM]
- Counterparts vs transworld identity: Humphrey objection and Lewis's reply. [sources: SEP-LM §8.2; SEP-TWI]
- Essence → modality (Fine) or modality → essence; whether anyone actually held the modal account. [source: SEP-EA §§3–4]
- Does conceivability entail possibility (Chalmers), give defeasible evidence (Yablo), or fail as a guide (Hill & McLaughlin; Block & Stalnaker)? [sources: SEP-Epi §4.1; SEP-Zom §5]
- Laws of nature: necessitarian vs contingentist. [source: SEP-Var §2]
- Whether Dharmakīrti's "must" is metaphysical necessity (Dunne; Iwata) or epistemic certainty about the actual world (Tillemans). [source: SEP-Dhk §1.4]
- Spinoza a necessitarian about modes? [source: SEP-SpM]
