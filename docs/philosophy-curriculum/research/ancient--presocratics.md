# Verified facts — The Presocratics: from Myth to Argument (researched 18 November 2026)

Node: `philosophy/history-of-philosophy/ancient-beginnings/presocratics`

Required coverage (seed summary): the first Greek thinkers who explained the world by principles rather than gods, and gave reasons a rival could dispute: the Milesians, Pythagoras, Heraclitus, Parmenides and the problem of change, Zeno's paradoxes, Empedocles, Anaxagoras and the atomists; and how their ideas survive only in fragments quoted by later writers.

## 1. The term "Presocratic" and the scope of the group
- The Presocratics were "6th and 5th century BCE Greek thinkers who introduced a new way of inquiring into the world". [source: SEP-Pre]
- The term "Presocratic" was "coined in the eighteenth century" and "made current by Hermann Diels in the nineteenth", to contrast Socrates (moral problems) with predecessors supposedly concerned with cosmology. WP-Pre-Socratic_philosophy names J. A. Eberhard as first using *vorsokratische Philosophie* in the late 18th century (citing Laks & Most 2018). [source: SEP-Pre; WP-Pre-Socratic_philosophy]
- Problems with the label (SEP-Pre): strictly chronological use is inaccurate, since "the last of them were contemporaneous with Socrates and even Plato"; several explored ethics; the term may suggest inferiority. Curd's working definition: "the early Greek thinkers who were not influenced by the views of Socrates, whether his predecessors or contemporaries". Some scholars now avoid the term; Laks and Most prefer "early Greek philosophy". [source: SEP-Pre; WP-Pre-Socratic_philosophy]
- They would not have called themselves "philosophers" in the later sense: Heraclitus' word *philosophos* (B35, "those who are lovers of wisdom must be inquirers into many things") lacks the special sense it gets in Plato and Aristotle. [source: SEP-Pre]
- Their inquiries covered what we call physics, chemistry, geology, meteorology, astronomy, embryology and psychology, as well as theology, metaphysics, epistemology and ethics; the Milesians did not separate "scientific" from "philosophical" questions. [source: SEP-Pre]
- Aristotle (Metaphysics I, Physics I, De Anima I, On Generation and Corruption I) treats them as his predecessors and traces a continuity of subject and method from them to himself, while judging them "unsuccessful and even amateurish"; his reading is shaped (anachronistically) by his own four-cause scheme. [source: SEP-Pre]
- Curd: "Perhaps the fundamental characteristic is the commitment to explain the world naturalistically, in terms of its own inherent principles." [source: SEP-Pre]
- Aristotle called them *physiologoi* ("inquirers into nature"), as against *theologoi*. [source: WP-Pre-Socratic_philosophy; SEP-Pre §9 ("physiologoi, or 'inquirers into nature,' as Aristotle called them")]

## 2. How the evidence survives: fragments, testimonia, doxography
- "Most of them wrote at least one 'book' (short pieces of prose writing, or, in some cases, poems), but no complete work survives." We depend on later philosophers, historians and compilers for "disconnected quotations (fragments) and reports about their views (testimonia)". The chain of transmission often depends on "Hippias, Aristotle, Theophrastus, Simplicius, and other ancient philosophers who did have direct access", and every source used the material "selectively". [source: SEP-Pre §1]
- **Diels–Kranz (DK).** Hermann Diels, *Die Fragmente der Vorsokratiker*, first published 1903, revised three times by Diels, then a 5th edition (1934–37) by Walther Kranz and a 6th edition (WP-Hermann_Alexander_Diels: 1952; SEP-Her note 1: 1951; SEP-PythM bibliography: 1952 — say "1951–52"). Later editions are mainly reprints. Original Greek/Latin with German translation. [source: WP-Diels–Kranz_numbering; WP-Hermann_Alexander_Diels; SEP-Pre]
- **How DK numbers work.** Each thinker has a chapter number (Heraclitus 22, Anaxagoras 59); "A" = testimonia (reports of life and thought), "B" = passages the editors take to be direct quotations ("fragments"), "C" = imitations. So DK59B12.3 = line 3 of Anaxagoras fragment 12; DK22A1 = testimonium 1 on Heraclitus. [source: SEP-Pre intro; SEP-Emp]
- DK chapter numbers used in this chapter: Thales 11, Anaximander 12, Anaximenes 13, Pythagoras 14, Xenophanes 21, Heraclitus 22, Parmenides 28, Zeno 29, Melissus 30, Empedocles 31, Philolaus 44, "The Pythagorean School" 58, Anaxagoras 59, Leucippus 67, Democritus 68 (DK chapters 1–4 are Orpheus, Musaeus, Epimenides, Hesiod). [source: WP-Diels–Kranz_numbering table]
- **Laks–Most (LM).** In 2016 André Laks and Glenn W. Most published *Early Greek Philosophy* (Loeb Classical Library, 9 volumes, Harvard UP), Greek with English translation (French version, *Les débuts de la philosophie*, Fayard, 2016). LM sections: P (person/life), D (doctrine, direct quotations in bold), R (reception, including forgeries). E.g. LM25D27 = DK59B12. SEP-Emp: "It is now essential to consult this monumental work ... in addition to Diels-Kranz." [source: SEP-Pre; SEP-Emp]
- **Doxography.** The term (from Greek *doxa*, "opinion") derives from the Latin neologism *doxographi* coined by Diels for his *Doxographi Graeci* ("Greek Doxographers", 1879). [source: SEP-Dox; WP-Doxography]
- **Theophrastus** (c. 371 – c. 287 BCE), Aristotle's pupil and successor: Diogenes Laertius' catalogue lists a work "*Physikôn Doxôn*, sixteen books". Diels read the title as *Physikôn Doxai* ("Tenets of the Natural Philosophers"); many scholars now prefer *Physikai Doxai* ("Tenets in Natural Philosophy"). Fragments of it on the principles (Thales' water, Heraclitus' fire etc.) survive mostly via Simplicius. Diels believed this work was the foundation of the later doxographical tradition. [source: SEP-Dox; WP-Theophrastus for dates]
- **Aetius**: an otherwise unknown compiler of *Placita* ("Tenets"), mentioned by Theodoret; dated "late 1st or perhaps early 2nd c. CE" (SEP-Dox; WP-Aetius gives "1st- or 2nd-century AD"). Diels reconstructed Aetius from ps.-Plutarch's *Placita* (an epitome of Aetius), Stobaeus' *Anthology* (5th c. CE) and Theodoret. Diels's further postulated source, the *Vetusta Placita*, "is Diels' invention and is generally disregarded" (WP) / "has been shown to be in need of revision" (SEP-Dox, which says the Aetius hypothesis "has proved to be tenable"). [source: SEP-Dox; WP-Aetius_(philosopher)]
- **Simplicius** (c. 480 – c. 540 CE), Neoplatonist commentator on Aristotle. He "appears to have possessed a good copy" of Parmenides' poem and transcribed fr. 8.1–52, introducing it: "Even if one might think it pedantic, I would gladly transcribe in this commentary the verses of Parmenides on the one being, which aren't numerous, both as evidence for what I have said and because of the scarcity of Parmenides' treatise." Thanks to him "we appear to have the entirety of Parmenides' major metaphysical argument". He also quotes Zeno's words (B1–B3) and preserves Anaximander's one fragment (after Theophrastus). Huggett: he wrote "a thousand years after Zeno" but "apparently possessed at least some of his book". [source: SEP-Parm §1; SEP-ZenoP; SEP-Pre §2; WP-Simplicius_of_Cilicia for dates]
- **Diogenes Laertius** (fl. 3rd century CE), *Lives and Opinions of Eminent Philosophers*: "a primary source for the history of ancient Greek philosophy", but his reputation is controversial "because he often repeats information from his sources without critically evaluating it". SEP authors repeatedly treat his biographies as legend-laden (e.g. Empedocles' Life "much of this is a romantic confection"; Heraclitus' biography "stories apparently invented to illustrate his character as inferred from his writings"). [source: WP-Diogenes_Laertius; SEP-Emp; SEP-Her §1]
- Other key transmitters: Plato, Aristotle, Plutarch, Sextus Empiricus, Clement of Alexandria and Hippolytus (Christian writers), Stobaeus. Xenophanes' theological fragments were quoted by Clement (B14–16, B23), Sextus (B11, 12, 24) and Simplicius (B25, 26); his drinking-party poems by Athenaeus. [source: WP-Pre-Socratic_philosophy; SEP-Xen §1]
- New evidence still appears: the **Derveni Papyrus** (found 1962 in a tomb in northern Greece; probably second half of 4th c. BCE), a commentary on an Orphic poem whose author knew contemporary philosophy and quotes Heraclitus (B3 + B94); the **Strasbourg papyrus** of Empedocles (identified 1994 in Strasbourg; a 1st- or 2nd-century CE papyrus from Upper Egypt) with previously unknown lines (published Martin & Primavesi 1999). [source: SEP-Pre §1; SEP-Her §8; SEP-Emp §4]
- Scale of the losses: Parmenides' poem was perhaps about 800 lines, of which about 160 survive; Heraclitus' fragments number "over one hundred"; DK gives Xenophanes 45 fragments; only one fragment of Anaximander survives. [source: SEP-Parm §1; SEP-Her §1; SEP-Xen §1; IEP-Anaxi §1]
- Zeno: "almost none of these paradoxes are quoted in Zeno's original words ... but in paraphrase"; the motion paradoxes come to us from Aristotle (Physics), and the traditional names are Aristotle's. [source: SEP-ZenoP; SEP-Pre note 6]

## 3. Setting: Ionia, Italy, and contact with the Near East
- Miletus is "a city in Ionia, on the west coast of what is now Turkey". [source: SEP-Pre §2]
- Persians conquered the Ionian towns c. 540 BCE; the Ionian revolt began 499 BCE. Heraclitus' Ephesus "was subject to Persian rule in his lifetime". [source: WP-Pre-Socratic_philosophy; SEP-Her §1]
- The scene shifted west: Pythagoras moved from Samos to Croton (southern Italy); Parmenides and Zeno were from Elea (south of Naples, modern Velia); Empedocles from Acragas (Agrigento) in Sicily. Later Anaxagoras is "the earliest philosopher to have resided in Athens". [source: SEP-Pre §§4–5; SEP-Emp §1; SEP-Anax]
- Near Eastern contact (see Debates): IEP-Pre: "there is great evidence to suggest that the Babylonians ... contributed significantly to ancient Greek knowledge of astronomy and mathematics"; Anaximander's gnomon and twelve-hour day were "probably" learned from Babylon (Graham's Texts). Ionian towns, especially Miletus, traded closely with Egypt and Mesopotamia. [source: IEP-Pre; WP-Pre-Socratic_philosophy]
- Global frame (optional): Karl Jaspers coined "Axial Age" in 1949 (*Vom Ursprung und Ziel der Geschichte*) for parallel changes in thought in Persia, India, China, the Levant and the Greco-Roman world c. 8th–3rd c. BCE; "the historical validity of the Axial Age is disputed". Use only as a cross-reference to the Upanishads, Buddha and Confucius chapters. [source: WP-Axial_Age]

## 4. The Milesians
### Thales of Miletus (DK 11)
- Dates: c. 620 – c. 546 BCE (IEP-Thal); WP gives c. 626/623 – c. 548/545 BCE. These rest on Apollodorus' chronicle (2nd c. BCE), which assumed a person's *acme* at 40; Diogenes Laertius quotes Apollodorus that Thales died aged 78 in the 58th Olympiad (548–545 BCE). Curd: "He seems to have lived around the beginning of the 6th c. BCE." [source: IEP-Thal; WP-Thales_of_Miletus; SEP-Pre §2]
- Aristotle names Thales the first to inquire into "causes and principles" and says Thales declared water the first cause (Metaphysics 983b27–33; also 983b20ff.), adding that "that from which they come to be is a principle of all things" (983b24–25; DK11A12); he guesses Thales chose water because of its role in coming-to-be, nutrition and growth. Aristotle's comments "do not sound as if they were based on first-hand knowledge", and "the doxographical reports say that Thales did not write a book" (some ancient reports credit him with two treatises, On the Solstice and On the Equinox; Diogenes Laertius doubted the *Nautical Star-guide*). [source: SEP-Pre §2; IEP-Thal]
- Thales held that the earth rests on water (Aristotle, De Caelo 294a28–30). [source: IEP-Thal]
- Thales reportedly said the lodestone and amber have souls, and that "all things are full of gods" (Aristotle, De Anima 411a7–8; DK11A22). [source: SEP-Pre §2; IEP-Thal]
- **Eclipse.** Herodotus (writing about 150 years later) says Thales predicted the eclipse that halted a battle between the Lydians and Medes; the only matching eclipse is 28 May 585 BCE. Modern scholars are sceptical: Curd says the report "almost certainly" is not correct; WP: the Babylonians "were far from being able to predict the local conditions of solar eclipses at that point" (citing Neugebauer 1969). IEP-Thal (O'Grady) is more credulous and discusses the Saros cycle. Writer: present as a tradition, not a fact. [source: SEP-Pre §2; WP-Eclipse_of_Thales; IEP-Thal]
- Tradition also says Thales introduced geometry into Greece from Egypt (DK 11A11). [source: SEP-Pre §2]
- Aristotle's anecdote (Politics 1259a6–23): Thales foresaw a bumper olive crop and rented all the olive presses of Miletus and Chios, to answer those who mocked philosophy's poverty. [source: IEP-Thal]
- Plato's anecdote (Theaetetus): Thales, gazing at the stars, fell into a pit and a Thracian servant girl laughed at him — DK 11A9. [source: WP-Diels–Kranz_numbering]

### Anaximander of Miletus (DK 12)
- Dates: c. 610 – 546 BCE (IEP-Anaxi; WP same). Said to be Thales' "successor and pupil" (Simplicius), though "the ancient tradition that the three were related as master and pupil may not be correct". [source: IEP-Anaxi; WP-Anaximander; SEP-Pre §2]
- "The first who dared to write a treatise in prose", traditionally called *On Nature*; credited with introducing the gnomon, a model sphere of the heavens, and the first map of the inhabited world (DK 12A1, A6). [source: IEP-Anaxi §1; SEP-Pre §2]
- **The one fragment (DK12B1)**, in Simplicius, Commentary on Aristotle's Physics 24.13ff. (= DK12A9 + B1), Curd's translation: "... the indefinite (to apeiron) is both principle (archē) and element (stoicheion) of the things that are ... He says that it is neither water nor any other of the so-called elements, but some other indefinite (apeiron) nature, from which come to be all the heavens and the worlds in them; and those things, from which there is coming-to-be for the things that are, are also those into which is their passing-away, in accordance with what must be. For they give penalty (dikê) and recompense to one another for their injustice (adikia) in accordance with the ordering of time—speaking of them in rather poetical terms." The extent of the actual quotation is disputed; it is "widely agreed" that the sentence "For they give penalty..." is Anaximander's own; the words about "elements" are Simplicius'. [source: SEP-Pre §2 and note 1]
- *Apeiron* = "the Boundless/Unlimited/Indefinite"; scholars dispute whether it is spatially or temporally unlimited, qualitatively indefinite, or inexhaustible. [source: IEP-Anaxi §2]
- Ps.-Plutarch (DK12A10): "Something productive of hot and cold was separated off from the eternal at the genesis of this world and from this a sphere of flame grew around the air around the earth like the bark around a tree." [source: SEP-Pre §2]
- Earth's stability (Aristotle, De Caelo 295b10ff., DK12A26): the earth stays where it is "because of equality ... For that which is situated in the center and at equal distances from the extremes, has no inclination whatsoever to move up rather than down or sideways". Often called the first known argument from the principle of sufficient reason; Aristotle mocked it (the hair pulled equally from both sides; the man equally hungry and thirsty). [source: IEP-Anaxi]
- For the argument for the *apeiron* see §13.

### Anaximenes of Miletus (DK 13)
- Dates: flourished mid-6th c. BCE, died about 528 BCE (IEP-Anaxm); WP gives c. 586/585 – c. 526/525 BCE. [source: IEP-Anaxm; WP-Anaximenes_of_Miletus]
- Basic stuff: air. Theophrastus (via Simplicius, DK13A5) speculates he chose air because a principle must be neutral but not so lacking in properties that it seems to be nothing. Mechanism: rarefaction and condensation — "Rarified, air becomes fire; more and more condensed, it becomes progressively wind, cloud, water, earth, and finally stones." Plutarch's breath example (DK13B1): blowing through compressed lips gives cool air, through relaxed lips warm air. [source: SEP-Pre §2]
- Anaximenes "may have thought of air as capable of directing its own development, as the soul controls the body (DK13B2)". [source: IEP-Anaxm]
- Debate on "material monism": Aristotle's reading (one persisting stuff) is accepted by Barnes, Guthrie, Sedley; Graham and Mourelatos argue the Milesians were not material monists in Aristotle's sense (the originating stuff is transformed, not persisting). [source: SEP-Pre §2]

## 5. Xenophanes of Colophon (DK 21) — the critique of the gods
- Dates: c. 570 – c. 478 BCE (IEP-Pre; WP). Diogenes Laertius (DK 21A1): born in Colophon (Ionia), flourished in the 60th Olympiad (540–537 BCE); exiled, he lived in Sicily (Zancle, Catana) and is linked with Elea. His own B8 says he "tossed about the Greek land" for sixty-seven years, starting at twenty-five. A travelling rhapsode who wrote in hexameters, elegiacs and iambics. [source: SEP-Xen §1; WP-Xenophanes]
- B11: "Homer and Hesiod have attributed to the gods all sorts of things that are matters of reproach and censure among men: theft, adultery, and mutual deception." (Lesher's translation in SEP-Xen) [source: SEP-Xen §2]
- B14, B15, B16 — see Quotations.
- B23: "One god greatest among gods and men, not at all like mortals in body or in thought." Lesher: often read as "a pioneering expression of monotheism", but the plural "gods" in the same line makes that reading "problematic"; possibly "the one greatest god". B24–26: the god sees, thinks and hears as a whole, shakes all things by the thought of his mind, and remains in the same place. [source: SEP-Xen §3; SEP-Pre §3]
- B32 (rainbow), Curd: "And she whom they call Iris, this too is by nature cloud / purple, red, and greeny yellow to behold." Lesher: "for Xenophanes, 'she' is really an 'it'"; Iris was the messenger goddess. [source: SEP-Pre §1, §3; SEP-Xen §5]
- Fossils: Hippolytus (A33) credits him with a theory of alternating world-wide flood and drought prompted by fossils of sea creatures found inland (Syracuse, Paros, Malta). [source: SEP-Xen §5]
- B18 (Curd): "Indeed not even from the beginning did the gods indicate all things to mortals, but, in time, inquiring, they discover better." B34 — see Quotations; whether its scepticism is global or limited is controversial. [source: SEP-Pre §3; SEP-Xen §6]
- Plato (Sophist 242d) speaks of the "Eleatic tribe, beginning from Xenophanes"; Lesher thinks the picture of Xenophanes as founder of the Eleatic school rests largely on this and on Aristotle (Metaphysics 986b18). [source: SEP-Xen §7]
- Lesher compares (no direct influence claimed) Feuerbach's critique of religion as projection and Freud's "wish-fulfilment" analysis. [source: SEP-Xen §7]

## 6. Pythagoras and the Pythagoreans (DK 14, 58; Philolaus 44)
- Dates: c. 570 – c. 490 BCE (SEP-Pyth); WP gives c. 570 – c. 495 BCE. Grew up on Samos; per Aristoxenus left at 40 when the tyranny of Polycrates (came to power c. 535) became unbearable; emigrated to Croton c. 530 BCE; violence against the Pythagoreans in Croton c. 510 BCE; fled to Metapontum, where he died around 490 BCE. [source: SEP-Pyth §3; WP-Pythagoras]
- Travels to Babylonia, Phoenicia and Egypt are reported; "to some extent" an attempt to claim eastern wisdom for him, and some scholars (Zhmud) reject them, but Herodotus (II.81) and Isocrates connect him with Egypt. [source: SEP-Pyth §3]
- He wrote nothing; books later ascribed to him were forgeries. [source: SEP-Pyth §3]
- Early evidence makes him famous not for mathematics but as an expert on the soul's fate after death (immortality and reincarnation, including into animals), on religious ritual, as a wonder-worker ("a thigh of gold", in two places at once), and as founder of a strict way of life (diet, ritual, self-discipline). Dicaearchus' four well-known doctrines: the soul is immortal; it transmigrates into other kinds of animals; events recur in cycles; all animate beings are kin. [source: SEP-Pyth intro, §5]
- Xenophanes (B7) mocked his claim to recognise a dead friend's soul in a barking dog; Heraclitus (B40, B129) called his learning *polymathy* and "an evil conspiracy"; Graham's SEP-Her says Heraclitus "called Pythagoras a fraud". [source: SEP-Xen §2; SEP-Pyth §3; SEP-Her §2]
- **The theorem.** No early source (Plato, Aristotle, their pupils) connects him with it; the story rests on two lines of verse by an Apollodorus ("When Pythagoras found that famous diagram, in honor of which he offered a glorious sacrifice of oxen..."), reported by Proclus (5th c. CE) almost a thousand years later. "There is not a jot of evidence for a proof by Pythagoras." Babylonians knew the rule and could generate Pythagorean triples long before (Høyrup). [source: SEP-Pyth §5]
- The musical ratios (octave 2:1, fifth 3:2, fourth 4:3): the blacksmith's-hammer stories "are clearly false"; verification traceable to Hippasus; the ratios are clearly in Philolaus' fragments. [source: SEP-Pyth §5]
- The story that he coined the word "philosophy" goes back to Heraclides of Pontus; Burkert (1960) showed the conception is Platonic and cannot belong to the historical Pythagoras. [source: SEP-Pyth §4]
- Later split: *acusmatici* (followers of the precepts, *acusmata*, "things heard") versus *mathematici* (including Philolaus and Archytas). Philolaus (2nd half of 5th c.): "Nature in the cosmos was fitted together out of unlimiteds and limiters" (DK44B1); "All things that are known have number; for without this nothing whatever could possibly be thought of or known" (DK44B4); he displaced the earth from the centre in favour of a central fire. [source: SEP-Pre §5]
- Consensus vs challenge: the consensus (Burkert 1972, Huffman) that the mathematical cosmology is post-Pythagoras "has received strong challenges" (Zhmud). [source: SEP-Pyth intro; SEP-Pre §5]

### Women associated with the Pythagoreans (with caveats)
- "Fourth-century evidence shows that Pythagoreanism gave an unusually large role to women for an ancient philosophical school." "Women were probably more active in Pythagoreanism than any other ancient philosophical movement. The evidence is not extensive." [source: SEP-PythM intro, §3.3]
- Iamblichus' *On the Pythagorean Life* (4th c. CE) ends with a catalogue of 218 men and 17 women Pythagoreans; often thought to rest on Aristoxenus (4th c. BCE), though Dutsch 2020 reads it sceptically. Eleven of the women are identified as wife, daughter or sister of a man. [source: SEP-PythM §3.1, §3.3]
- **Theano**: the most famous name; in Iamblichus' list the wife of Brontinus, elsewhere the wife, daughter or pupil of Pythagoras (Diogenes Laertius VIII 42). Dicaearchus (4th c. BCE) says Pythagoras had women as well as men followers and that Theano "became famous", without calling her anyone's wife. "We have little reliable evidence about her." Works were later forged in her name (one fragment *On Piety*, apophthegms, letters). Claims that she took over the school after Pythagoras' death: WP says "the evidence is overwhelmingly clear that was not the case" (citing Deakin 2013). [source: SEP-PythM §3.3, §4.2; WP-Theano_(philosopher)]
- **Timycha**: reportedly bit off her tongue rather than reveal Pythagorean secrets to the tyrant Dionysius (story via Neanthes; Iamblichus VP 189–194). [source: SEP-PythM §3.3]
- Writings ascribed to Pythagorean women (Theano, Periktione, Phintys, Melissa, Myia) are pseudepigrapha, probably mostly 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE; we cannot tell whether written by women or men under female pseudonyms (Huizenga 2013). [source: SEP-PythM §4.2]

## 7. Heraclitus of Ephesus (DK 22)
- Active around 500 BCE (SEP-Her); traditional floruit 69th Olympiad (504–501 BCE), possibly a synchronism with Darius (Kahn); IEP-Pre gives c. 540 – c. 480 BCE. [source: SEP-Her; WP-Heraclitus; IEP-Pre]
- Wrote a single book, said to have been deposited in the temple of Artemis at Ephesus; Theophrastus thought it seemed "half-finished". Diogenes Laertius' biographical stories are largely invented from the fragments. Over one hundred fragments survive. [source: SEP-Her §1]
- Logos (account, word, statement, argument): B50 (Graham) "Having harkened not to me but to the Word (Logos) it is wise to agree that all things are one." B1 opens his book: "Of this Word's being forever do men prove to be uncomprehending, both before they hear and once they have heard it..." [source: SEP-Her §2; SEP-Pre note 3]
- Unity of opposites, e.g. B61 "Sea is the purest and most polluted water: for fish drinkable and healthy, for men undrinkable and harmful"; B60 "the road up and down are one and the same"; B88. Graham argues these do not commit him to contradiction (the qualifiers are understood). [source: SEP-Her §3.2; SEP-Pre §3]
- B30 (Graham): "This world-order [kosmos], the same of all, no god nor man did create, but it ever was and is and will be: everliving fire, kindling in measures and being quenched in measures." Graham: the first extant Greek text to use *kosmos* to mean "world". [source: SEP-Her §4]
- B53: "War is father of all and king of all; and some he manifested as gods, some as men; some he made slaves, some free." B80: "We must recognize that war is common, strife is justice, and all things happen according to strife and necessity" — possibly a criticism of Anaximander's cosmic justice. [source: SEP-Her §4]
- B119 *êthos anthrôpôi daimôn*: "The character of man is his guardian spirit." [source: SEP-Her §2]
- B40 (Graham): "Learning many things does not teach understanding. Else it would have taught Hesiod and Pythagoras, as well as Xenophanes and Hecataeus." [source: SEP-Her §5]
- Plato and Aristotle read him as denying the law of non-contradiction; the Stoics read in him a periodic world-conflagration, which B30 seems to contradict (Hegel noticed). [source: SEP-Her §4, §7; SEP-Pre §3]
- Epithets "the dark/the obscure"; later "the weeping philosopher". [source: WP-Heraclitus]
- River fragments: see Quotations and Debates.

## 8. Parmenides of Elea (DK 28)
- Dates: Plato's *Parmenides* makes him about 65 when Socrates was "quite young" (about 20), implying birth about 515 BCE (Palmer) / "ca. 510" (Curd); Apollodorus (via Diogenes Laertius) put his floruit in the 69th Olympiad (504–500), implying birth c. 540 BCE — a scheme known to be artificial. Active early-to-mid 5th c. BCE. Speusippus reports he gave Elea its laws. A 1st-c. CE portrait head inscribed "Parmeneides, son of Pyres, Ouliadês, Natural Philosopher" was found at Elea in the 1960s. [source: SEP-Parm §1; SEP-Pre §4; WP-Parmenides]
- One work: a poem in epic hexameters (title "On Nature" probably not authentic), perhaps 800 lines, about 160 surviving; fr. 8 has 62 verses. [source: SEP-Parm §1]
- Proem (fr. 1): a young man (*kouros*) carried by chariot, led by the daughters of the Sun, to "the halls of Night", where a goddess welcomes him. Palmer: not an allegory but "a topographically specific description of a mystical journey", with precedents in Hesiod and in "the Babylonian mythology of the sun god's abode"; Parmenides casts himself as an initiate. [source: SEP-Parm §2.1]
- Two-part revelation: the "Way of Conviction"/"Truth" (*Alêtheia*) and "the beliefs of mortals" (*Doxa*), a cosmology the goddess calls "deceptive" (fr. 8.50–52). The Doxa was originally the greater part of the poem; only perhaps 10% of it survives. [source: SEP-Parm §2.2, §2.4]
- Cosmological contributions: first to posit a plurality of physical principles (light and night); recognised that the moon shines by light reflected from the sun (frs. 14, 15); credited with identifying the morning and evening star as Venus (Aët. 2.15.4); Curd adds that he "seems to have been the first Presocratic to claim ... that the earth is spherical". IEP-Pre notes the Babylonians already knew morning and evening star were the same. [source: SEP-Parm §2.4; SEP-Pre §4; IEP-Pre]
- Palmer's conclusion: Parmenides was "the first philosopher rigorously to distinguish what must be, what must not be, and what is but need not be", and may be seen "as the founder of metaphysics or ontology as a domain of inquiry distinct from theology". [source: SEP-Parm §3.5]
- Argument against coming-to-be/change: see §13. Interpretations: see Debates.

## 9. The later Eleatics: Zeno and Melissus
- **Zeno of Elea (DK 29)**: Plato's *Parmenides* makes him nearly 40 when Socrates was about 20, so born about 490 BCE; WP gives c. 490 – c. 430 BCE. Close associate of Parmenides; Diogenes Laertius' stories of a plot against a tyrant are unverifiable. Plutarch says Pericles heard him. [source: SEP-ZenoE §1; SEP-ZenoP; WP-Zeno_of_Elea]
- A red-figure drinking cup from Falerii (mid-5th c. BCE; Rome, Villa Giulia inv. 3591) showing a hero racing ahead of a tortoise "has every appearance of being the first known 'response' to the Achilles paradox". [source: SEP-ZenoE §1]
- Proclus reports a work of "forty arguments"; Huggett: "40 'paradoxes of plurality'", of which only two definitely survive (a third probably); plus Aristotle's four arguments against motion (Dichotomy, Achilles, Arrow, Stadium) and two more (Place; Millet Seed). IEP-Zeno: "Zeno probably created forty paradoxes, of which only ... ten are known." [source: SEP-ZenoE §1; SEP-ZenoP; IEP-Zeno]
- Plato's Zeno says his book was "a sort of support (boêtheia tis) for Parmenides' doctrine against those attempting to ridicule it", paying back those who say "the many are" "with the same results and worse" (Prm. 128c6–d6), written "in a contentious spirit when I was still young". [source: SEP-ZenoE §3]
- Aristotle (in his lost *Sophist*) called Zeno the inventor of dialectic (D.L. 8.57); Plato's Phaedrus calls him the "Eleatic Palamedes". [source: SEP-ZenoE §3]
- Aristotle reports that the argument from complete divisibility convinced the atomists that there must be indivisible magnitudes (GC 316b34). [source: SEP-ZenoP §5]
- Diogenes the Cynic reportedly answered by standing up and walking (Simplicius, in Phys. 1012.22) — Huggett: this "would not impress Zeno". [source: SEP-ZenoP §3.1]
- **Melissus of Samos (DK 30)**: fl. ca. 440 BCE (Curd); commanded the Samian fleet that defeated the Athenians in 440 BCE (Plutarch, Life of Pericles 26). Argued explicitly that what-is is one and unlimited ("if there were two ... they would be 'limited against each other'", DK30B6) and denied the void and motion; Aristotle dismissed him as simple-minded. [source: SEP-Pre §6; WP-Melissus_of_Samos]

## 10. The pluralists: Empedocles and Anaxagoras
### Empedocles of Acragas (DK 31)
- Born early 5th c. BCE; Aristotle says he died aged sixty; WP gives c. 494 – c. 434 BCE (fl. 444–443). Aristocratic family (grandfather an Olympic victor). Tradition credits him with democratic sympathies (contested). [source: SEP-Emp §1; WP-Empedocles]
- Wrote in hexameter verse (adopting Parmenides' metre). Traditionally two poems, *On Nature* (*Physics*) and *Purifications*; whether there was one poem or two is disputed (Primavesi for two; Inwood, Osborne against); the Strasbourg papyrus shows cosmology and religion "inextricably intertwined". [source: SEP-Pre §7 and note 9; SEP-Emp §4]
- Four "roots" (earth, water, air, fire) and two forces, Love and Strife; Aristotle credits him with being the first to distinguish the four elements clearly (Metaph. 985a31–3). Both forces mix and separate (Love unites unlikes, Strife segregates them). [source: SEP-Emp §2.1; SEP-Pre §7]
- B17.1–2 (Laks–Most, as in SEP-Emp): "Twofold is what I shall say: for at one time they grew to be only one / Out of many, at another time again they separate to be many out of one." [source: SEP-Emp §2.1]
- B17 also echoes Parmenides against coming-to-be: "For if they perished entirely, they would no longer be. / And this whole here, what could increase it, and coming from where?" (B17.31–32 approx.; SEP-Emp prints B17.27–35). [source: SEP-Emp §2.1]
- B112 (Laks–Most): "I, who for you am an immortal god, no longer mortal, I go among you, honored". Like the Pythagoreans he taught transmigration of *daimōns* and dietary rules. [source: SEP-Emp §3; SEP-Pre §7]
- Death: "We can be sure that he did not make the fiery leap into Aetna, as was widely held in antiquity" (Kingsley, citing Chitwood 1986). [source: SEP-Emp §1]
- Kingsley: Empedocles has long been seen "as a figure moving between mythos and logos, religion and science". [source: SEP-Emp intro]

### Anaxagoras of Clazomenae (DK 59)
- 500–428 BCE (SEP-Anax). Diogenes Laertius: aged twenty when Xerxes crossed the Hellespont (480 BCE). "The earliest philosopher to have resided in Athens", for twenty to thirty years; friend and protégé of Pericles. Charged with impiety (he called the sun a red-hot stone), fled to Lampsacus, where he died in 428 and was honoured with a children's festival. Dates of his arrival in Athens and of the trial are disputed (proposals range from 480 to 456 for arrival). His book could be bought for a drachma (Plato, Apology). [source: SEP-Anax §1]
- First Greek to give the correct account of eclipses; a meteorite "the size of a wagon" fell at Aegospotami soon after he proposed that heavy stones exist in the heavens, and he gained notoriety for "predicting" it (DK 59A11). [source: SEP-Anax intro]
- B17 (Curd): "The Greeks do not think correctly about coming-to-be and passing-away; for no thing comes to be or passes away, but is mixed together and dissociated from the things that are. And thus they would be correct to call coming-to-be mixing-together and passing-away dissociating." [source: SEP-Pre §7]
- B1: "All things were together, unlimited both in amount and in smallness..." B11 (Sisko/Curd): "In everything there is a share of everything except noûs, but there are some things in which noûs too is present." Mind (*nous*) sets the mixture rotating; the vortex separates out the world. [source: SEP-Pre §7; SEP-Anax §4]
- Aristotle (Metaph. I.3 984b15): when Anaxagoras said *nous* is the cause of the cosmos and its order, "he appeared as a sober man among the random chatterers who preceded him"; yet Plato and Aristotle were disappointed his theory was not properly teleological. [source: SEP-Anax epigraph; SEP-Pre §7]
- Zeno's principle of limitless divisibility was accepted by Anaxagoras and rejected by the atomists. [source: SEP-ZenoE §3]

## 11. The atomists: Leucippus and Democritus
- **Leucippus (DK 67)**: born variously at Elea, Abdera or Miletus; lived in the 5th c. BCE; dates unknown. Diogenes Laertius makes him a student of Zeno. His very existence was disputed in 19th-c. scholarship; Epicurus seems to have denied there was a Leucippus; Aristotle ascribes the foundation of atomism to him. Surviving quotation (DK67B2): nothing happens in vain (*matên*) but everything from *logos* and by necessity. [source: SEP-Leu]
- **Democritus (DK 68)**: born about 460 BCE, of Abdera (some reports Miletus); "forty years younger" than Anaxagoras (DK68A1); WP gives c. 460 – c. 370 BCE. Known in antiquity as the "laughing philosopher" (from his ethic of cheerfulness, *euthumiē*). Diogenes Laertius lists many works; authenticity of the ethical sayings in Stobaeus is debated. [source: SEP-Dem §1; SEP-Pre §8; WP-Democritus]
- Doctrine: atoms (*atomos*, "uncuttable") infinite in number, varying in shape and size, solid, ungenerated and indestructible, moving in an infinite void; differ (Aristotle, Metaph. 985b4–20 = DK67A6) in shape, arrangement and position — Aristotle's letter analogy: A differs from N in shape, AN from NA in arrangement, N from Z in position. Whether early atoms had weight is debated. [source: SEP-Dem §2; SEP-Leu §2; SEP-Pre §8]
- The atomists "deliberately embraced an apparent contradiction, claiming that 'what is not' exists": the void exists "no more" (*ou mallon*) than body does (DK67A6). [source: SEP-Dem §2; SEP-Leu §2]
- Ancient sources and most modern scholars read atomism as a response to Parmenides (challenged by Osborne 2004); some read the indivisible minimum as an answer to Zeno (Hasper 2006). Leucippus reportedly accepted Melissus' premise that motion requires void, but reasoned backwards: since there is motion, there is void (DK67A7). [source: SEP-Dem §2; SEP-Leu]
- B125/B9 and B117, B11: see Quotations. Possible influence on Epicurus a century later. [source: SEP-Pre §8]

## 12. Diogenes of Apollonia and the end of the period (brief)
- Diogenes of Apollonia (active after 440 BCE) revived Anaximenes' air as the single substance and argued for it: only things alike can affect one another, so all things must be forms of one thing (DK64B2); he required that "anyone beginning a logos (account) ought to present a starting principle (archē) that is indisputable" (DK64B1). Theophrastus called him the last of the *physiologoi*. [source: SEP-Pre §9]
- Gorgias' *On Nature, or On What-Is-Not* argued nothing meets Parmenides' requirements; the Sophists overlap with the last Presocratics (cross-refer to the Socrates and the Sophists chapter). [source: SEP-Pre §9]

## 13. Arguments, set out precisely

### A. Anaximander: why the principle is not water (or any element)
Text: Aristotle, Physics 3.5, 204b25–29 (DK12A16), as translated in IEP-Anaxi: "Some make this (namely, that which is additional to the elements) the Boundless, but not air or water, lest the others should be destroyed by one of them, being boundless; for they are opposite to one another (the air, for instance, is cold, the water wet, and the fire hot). If any of them should be boundless, it would long since have destroyed the others; but now there is, they say, something other from which they are all generated." Aristotle does not name Anaximander here; Couprie says the argument "probably goes back to Anaximander". Simplicius (in Curd's translation of Phys. 24.13ff.) gives the same motive: "having seen the change of the four elements into each other, he did not think it fit to make some one of these underlying subject" — but Curd notes these words are Simplicius' comment, not Anaximander's. [source: IEP-Anaxi §2c; SEP-Pre §2 and note 1]
- P1. The source of all things must be boundless (unlimited in amount, so that coming-to-be never runs out).
- P2. The familiar stuffs (water, air, fire...) are opposites: they act against and "destroy" one another.
- P3. If one of these opposites were boundless, it would by now ("long since") have overwhelmed and destroyed the others.
- P4. But the opposites all still exist.
- C. So the boundless source is none of the opposites but "something other", from which they are all generated: the *apeiron*.
- Companion argument (Physics 203b6–10, DK12A15): everything either has an origin or is an origin; the Boundless has no origin (else it would have a limit); it is unborn and immortal. Couprie: "more like a string of associations and word-plays than ... a formal argument". [source: IEP-Anaxi §2a]
- Objection (implicit in later thought, e.g. Anaximenes): an utterly indefinite stuff explains too little — Theophrastus says Anaximenes chose air because a principle must be neutral "but not so lacking in properties that it seems to be nothing at all". Possible reply: the *apeiron* is not propertyless but "something productive of hot and cold" (DK12A10), with motion innate. [source: SEP-Pre §2]
- Significance: Couprie: "he did not just utter apodictic statements, but also tried to give arguments. This is what makes him the first philosopher." The overview's paraphrase ("why should one element dominate its opposites?") fits this argument. [source: IEP-Anaxi §2]

### B. Parmenides against coming-to-be (and change)
Texts: fr. 2, fr. 3, fr. 6–7, fr. 8.5–21 (translations in Quotations). Reconstruction (following the logic as summarised by Curd and Palmer):
- P1. There are only two routes of inquiry for understanding: that [it] is and cannot not be; and that [it] is not and must not be (fr. 2).
- P2. The second is "wholly without report": you could neither know nor point out what is not (fr. 2.7–8); "for the same thing is for thinking and for being" (fr. 3, Curd).
- P3. So what is not cannot be said or thought (fr. 8.8–9: "for not to be said and not to be thought / is it that it is not").
- P4. If what-is came to be, it came either from what is not or from what is. Not from what is not (P3). Not from what is (it would already be). And "what need could have aroused it / later rather than before, beginning from nothing, to grow?" (fr. 8.9–10; Palmer calls this an argument "based on the principle of sufficient reason").
- C1. "Thus it must either be altogether or not at all"; "generation is extinguished and destruction unheard of" (fr. 8.11, 8.21).
- Further: what-is is "whole and uniform" (no more of it here, less there: fr. 8.22–25), "still"/motionless (8.26–33), and "perfect", "like the bulk of a well-rounded globe" (8.42–49). Mortals who rely on "aimless sight and echoing hearing" are told to "judge by reason" (fr. 7). [source: SEP-Parm §§2.2–2.3; SEP-Pre §4]
- Main objection 1 (Russell, Owen): the argument trades on the problem of negative existentials — Russell's paraphrase: "When you think, you think of something ... whatever can be thought of or spoken of must exist at all times. Consequently there can be no change" (Russell 1945, 49); the problem is now often called "Parmenides' paradox". On this view the argument confuses talking about a non-existent thing with talking about nothing. [source: SEP-Parm §3.2]
- Main objection 2 (the atomists): deny the premise that what is not cannot be: the void ("what is not") exists "no more" than body, and change is rearrangement of unchanging atoms in the void. Empedocles and Anaxagoras accept "no coming-to-be" but make change mixture and separation of permanent stuffs. [source: SEP-Dem §2; SEP-Anax; SEP-Emp]
- Reply on Parmenides' behalf (Palmer's modal reading): "what is not" is shorthand for "what is not and must not be", i.e. the impossible; one genuinely cannot conceive or point out a round square, so fr. 2.7–8 makes "a perfectly acceptable point about the inconceivability of what necessarily is not" (an early inference from inconceivability to impossibility), and Parmenides "has not fallen prey" to the negative-existentials paradox. On this reading the argument concerns what *must* be; contingent changing things still exist (as described in the Doxa) but cannot be objects of unwavering understanding. [source: SEP-Parm §3.5]
- Caution: whether Parmenides concluded that change and plurality are *illusions* is exactly what is disputed (see Debates). Do not state it as fact.

### C. Zeno's Dichotomy (and the Achilles)
Text: "The first asserts the non-existence of motion on the ground that that which is in locomotion must arrive at the half-way stage before it arrives at the goal." (Aristotle, Physics 239b11, as quoted in SEP-ZenoP) [source: SEP-ZenoP §3.1]
- P1. To run from A to B, the runner must first reach the halfway point; before that, the quarter point; and so on without end.
- P2. So the run requires completing infinitely many finite sub-runs.
- P3. Completing infinitely many finite runs takes an infinite time / can never be finished (Curd: "The argument assumes that it is impossible to pass an infinite number of points in a finite time").
- C. The runner never reaches B; since nothing depends on the distance or the runner, no motion is possible.
- Method: a *reductio*: suppose the opponents' view (here that a distance is infinitely divisible) "for argument's sake" and derive absurdity. [source: SEP-ZenoP §1.1, §3.1; SEP-Pre §6]
- Achilles version (Simplicius in Phys. 1014.10): before overtaking, the pursuer must first reach the point the fleeing tortoise started from, by which time the tortoise has moved on, and so on for ever. Huggett's numbers: Achilles 1 m/s, tortoise 0.1 m/s, 0.9 m head start: catch-ups of 0.9 m, 0.09 m, 0.009 m...; he should catch it after 1 s at 1 m. [source: SEP-ZenoP §3.2]
- Objection 1 (Aristotle): divide the time as you divide the distance — each sub-run has its own fraction of a finite total time. Aristotle saw (Physics 263a15) this was not the end of the matter, and added his distinction between a potentially infinite (undivided, continuous) run and an actually infinite series of distinct runs. [source: SEP-ZenoP §3.1]
- Objection 2 (the modern "Standard Solution": Russell, Grünbaum, Salmon): some infinite series have finite sums — 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... = 1, made rigorous by Cauchy in the 19th century; Cantor's theory of the transfinite makes the infinite series of points respectable. IEP-Zeno: the Standard Solution "implies Achilles's path contains an actual infinity of places and parts, but Zeno was mistaken to assume this is too many". [source: SEP-ZenoP §1.2, §2.2, §3.1; IEP-Zeno]
- Reply / remaining worry: (a) a pluralist must not just say the sum "might be" finite but give a rigorous theory of infinite sums (Huggett's example 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 ... ); (b) "supertasks" (Black 1950–51, "infinity machines"): can any infinite series of actions really be completed?; (c) whether physical space and time really have the structure of the mathematical continuum is an empirical question — quantum gravity may say not. [source: SEP-ZenoP §2.2, §5]
- The Arrow (Aristotle Physics 239b30; D.L. ix.72 "What is in motion moves neither in the place it is nor in one in which it is not"): at each instant the arrow occupies a space equal to itself, so is at rest; time is composed of instants; so the arrow never moves. Modern reply: velocity at an instant is not distance/time over that instant (0/0); motion is being at different places at different times (the "at-at" theory; Bergson thought it absurd: "movement is composed of immobilities"). [source: SEP-ZenoP §3.3]
- Modern reading (SEP-Pre): the Achilles and Dichotomy are reductios of the view that space and time are continuous; the Stadium and Arrow of the view that they are discrete. [source: SEP-Pre §6]

### D. Diogenes of Apollonia (optional short example of an explicit argument)
- Only like can affect like; things evidently interact; so all things are forms of one thing (DK64B2). [source: SEP-Pre §9]

## 14. Influence and legacy (brief)
- Plato and Aristotle: Plato's Forms are described in language echoing Parmenidean Being (Symposium 210e–211b, Phaedo 78d, 80b); Plato used Cratylus' Heraclitus as a model for the sensible world and Parmenides for the intelligible. [source: SEP-Parm §3.4; SEP-Her §7]
- The Stoics drew on Heraclitus' physics; Aenesidemus read him as a proto-sceptic; ancient sceptics claimed Xenophanes as a founder. [source: SEP-Her §7; SEP-Xen §6]
- Zeno's arguments shaped Aristotle's theory of the continuum and the sophists' "antilogic" (Protagoras, Gorgias). [source: SEP-ZenoE §3]
- Nietzsche admired them ("tyrants of the spirit"); Heidegger found the roots of his thought in Anaximander, Parmenides and Heraclitus; Popper, "Back to the Presocratics" (1958). [source: WP-Pre-Socratic_philosophy]

## Debates
- **Was it "from mythos to logos"?** The formula is Wilhelm Nestle's *Vom Mythos zum Logos* (1940; 2nd ed. 1942) [source: WP-Wilhelm_Nestle]. Curd's SEP entry states the case for a break: Hesiod's "god-saturated" world versus the Presocratic *kosmos*, "an ordered natural arrangement that is inherently intelligible and not subject to supra-natural intervention", with Xenophanes' Iris-as-cloud as the "striking example"; Lesher calls B32 the clearest expression of the Ionian "intellectual revolution" [source: SEP-Pre §1; SEP-Xen §5]. But Curd also says "scholars disagree about the extent of the divergence between the early Greek philosophers and their non-philosophical predecessors" [source: SEP-Pre §1]. Evidence against a clean break, all in the sources: Thales' "all things are full of gods"; Anaximander's cosmic "justice" and "penalty"; Heraclitus' divine *logos* "willing and unwilling to be called by the name of Zeus" (B32); Xenophanes' "one greatest god"; Parmenides' poem as a revelation by a goddess, modelled on initiation into mysteries; Empedocles as "an immortal god" and "a figure moving between mythos and logos"; the Pythagorean way of life [source: SEP-Pre §§2–3; SEP-Parm §2.1; SEP-Emp]. Scholarly positions: F. M. Cornford, *From Religion to Philosophy* (London, 1912), argued Presocratic speculation grew directly out of earlier religious thought; J.-P. Vernant (1957) rejected a Greek "miracle" and tied the novelty to the polis [source: WS: https://press.princeton.edu/node/7105 ; https://erevistas.uca.edu.ar/index.php/STY/article/download/440/434/0 — search summaries only]; Richard Buxton (ed.), *From Myth to Reason? Studies in the Development of Greek Thought* (Oxford UP, 1999) collectively questions the polarity, with Glenn Most's chapter "From logos to mythos" (pp. 25–47) [source: WS: https://academic.oup.com/book/47052 ; BMCR 2000.07.08 summary]; Gregory (2013) on the Presocratics and the supernatural [source: SEP-Pre bibliography]. Jonathan Barnes (1987) by contrast says they "changed the course of human thought from myth to philosophy and science" [source: WP-Pre-Socratic_philosophy]. Writer: present as a live debate; a fair formulation is that the novelty lies less in abandoning gods than in giving *reasons* open to criticism (cf. Couprie on Anaximander; Diogenes of Apollonia's demand for an "indisputable" *archē*).
- **Near Eastern and Egyptian influence.** Sources supporting real influence: Babylonian astronomy and mathematics (IEP-Pre); Babylonians knew the "Pythagorean" rule and triples long before Pythagoras (Høyrup, in SEP-Pyth); Parmenides' proem has precedent in "Babylonian mythology of the sun god's abode" (SEP-Parm); Burkert 2008 surveys influence of "the myths, astronomy, and cosmogony of the Babylonians, Persians, and Egyptians" (SEP-Pre); Greek tradition itself connected Thales and Pythagoras with Egypt (SEP-Pre §2; SEP-Pyth §3). Caveats: reports of travels were partly a later attempt to claim "the ancient wisdom of the east" (SEP-Pyth); M. L. West, *Early Greek Philosophy and the Orient* (Oxford: Clarendon, 1971) argued for strong (incl. Iranian) parallels; one survey says his work "is not widely accepted" [source: WS: https://zenon.dainst.org/Record/000177026/Description ; https://www.iranicaonline.org/?p=2955 — summaries only]. Stronger claims: George G. M. James, *Stolen Legacy* (1954), argued Greek philosophy was stolen from Egypt; it is "generally ... considered pseudohistory" (Lefkowitz and others) and contains chronological impossibilities (e.g. on Democritus and Anaxarchus) [source: WP-George_G._M._James]. Martin Bernal, *Black Athena* (vol. 1 1987, vol. 2 1991, vol. 3 2006), argued for Afroasiatic roots of Greek civilisation; "heavily criticised and rejected by the majority of academics" [source: WP-Black_Athena]. Writer: distinguish documented borrowing (astronomy, mathematics, myth motifs) from the unsupported "theft" thesis, and note that the *argumentative* form is what scholars generally treat as new; the History overview's Greece chapter already raises Bernal.
- **What Parmenides meant (strict monism or not).** Palmer's typology: (1) strict monism (Guthrie 1965): only one unchanging thing exists; the sensible world is "a deceitful show"; (2) logical-dialectical (Owen 1960, Barnes, Kirk–Raven–Schofield 1983): driven by logic of "what can be thought and talked about"; cosmology "no more than a dialectical device"; (3) meta-principle (Mourelatos 1970, Nehamas, Curd's "predicational monism"): conditions any fundamental entity must meet, compatible with many such entities; (4) the ancient "aspectual"/"generous monist" view (Plato, Aristotle, Theophrastus, Plutarch, Simplicius): one in account, many in perception; (5) Palmer's modal interpretation (what must be vs what is but need not be). Curd: the nature of "what-is" is "a highly contentious subject" (existence, object of inquiry, essential being). Recent scholars (Palmer, Cordero, Graham, Mourelatos, Bryan, Johansen) treat the Doxa as Parmenides' own serious cosmology. [source: SEP-Parm §3; SEP-Pre §4 and note 5]
- **Did Parmenides set the agenda for all later Presocratics?** The idea that his arguments made "the phenomenon of change ... the central preoccupation of subsequent Presocratic natural philosophers is a commonplace of modern historical narratives. Unfortunately, this notion has no real ancient authority" (Palmer). Yet SEP-Dem/SEP-Leu: ancient sources describe atomism as a response to Parmenides, "how its motivation is generally interpreted by scholars today" (challenge: Osborne 2004). Present both. [source: SEP-Parm §3.4; SEP-Dem §2]
- **Zeno's purpose.** Conventional view: defence of Parmenides' monism (from Socrates' words in Plato's *Parmenides* 128a–b). Palmer: this is "unjustifiably selective"; Plato's Zeno only offers "a sort of support" by showing pluralism has worse consequences; Barnes: "Zeno mocked the mockers ... He did not have the serious metaphysical purpose of supporting an Eleatic monism." Older view (Tannery 1885) that the target was a Pythagorean number-atomism, rejected mid-20th c. (Vlastos) but still defended (Matson 2001). [source: SEP-ZenoE §3; SEP-ZenoP §5]
- **Are Zeno's paradoxes solved?** The "received view" (Russell, Grünbaum, Salmon) holds 19th-c. mathematics (Cauchy, Cantor, Dedekind) resolves them; challenges: supertasks (Black), whether real space-time is a continuum (quantum gravity), non-standard analysis (Robinson; either system "equally successful"), recent historical work (Sattler 2020; Reese et al. 2022). [source: SEP-ZenoP §§1.2, 5]
- **Heraclitus and flux.** Plato's reading (everything changes; you cannot step twice) vs Graham's (following Reinhardt, Kirk 1954, Marcovich 1967): only B12 is genuine, and it says rivers stay the same *by* their waters changing — constancy through change. "The Platonic reading still has advocates (e.g. Tarán 1999)." Also: material monist (Aristotle, Barnes) vs process philosopher. Barnes: "*Panta rhei*, 'everything flows' is probably the most familiar of Heraclitus's sayings, yet few modern scholars think he said it." [source: SEP-Her §3; WP-Heraclitus]
- **The Milesians as "material monists"** (Aristotle, Barnes, Guthrie) vs Graham/Mourelatos (originating stuff transformed, no persisting substrate). [source: SEP-Pre §2]
- **Pythagoras as mathematician?** Burkert/Huffman: no early evidence; a religious and ethical teacher who honoured number; Zhmud and others challenge this consensus. [source: SEP-Pyth]
- **Empedocles: one poem or two?** Primavesi (two) vs Inwood, Osborne (one); Strasbourg papyrus complicates. [source: SEP-Pre note 9; SEP-Emp §4]
- **Xenophanes: monotheist? sceptic?** Monotheism reading "problematic" (Lesher); B34 read as global scepticism by ancient sceptics, but recent readings prefer limited scepticism or fallibilism (Lesher 1992, 1994). [source: SEP-Xen §§3, 6; SEP-Pre §3]

  - "Sometime around the sixth century BCE, in the commercial Greek cities of Ionia ... explaining the world by natures and principles rather than by the biographies of gods, and ... offering reasons that a rival could dispute" — consistent with SEP-Pre and IEP-Anaxi (but see mythos/logos caveats: the gods do not disappear).
  - "Thales says water underlies everything" — slightly stronger than the evidence: Aristotle reports water as *archē*/first cause; whether Thales meant everything *is now* water (persisting substrate) is disputed (SEP-Pre §2). Suggest "Thales made water the origin of things".
  - "Anaximander counters with the boundless (apeiron) and adds an argument (why should one element dominate its opposites?)" — consistent with Physics 204b25–29, but Aristotle does not name Anaximander there; attribution "probable" (IEP-Anaxi).

  - "Parmenides, in the tradition's first knock-down deductive argument, reasons that change and plurality are impossible — being cannot come from non-being — bequeathing to every successor the task ... save the appearances of change" — **partly contradicted**: that he denied the reality of change and plurality is the strict-monist reading, now one of several (SEP-Parm §3); Palmer says the "commonplace" that Parmenides made change the central problem for successors "has no real ancient authority". (Atomism as a response to Parmenides is, however, the standard reading per SEP-Dem.)

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use
- "Panta rhei" / "everything flows" as a quotation of Heraclitus — not a fragment; Barnes: "few modern scholars think he said it"; first ascribed in this exact form by Simplicius (6th c. CE) per WP-Heraclitus. Use only as a later slogan.
- "You cannot step into the same river twice" as Heraclitus' own words — it is Plato's (Cratylus 402a) and Plutarch's (B91) paraphrase; the likely genuine fragment is B12 (Graham, following Kirk and Marcovich). Use with attribution.
- "Thales predicted the eclipse of 585 BCE" as fact — a tradition from Herodotus; Curd: "almost certainly" not correct; Babylonians could not then predict local solar eclipses (WP-Eclipse_of_Thales).
- That Pythagoras proved (or discovered) the Pythagorean theorem; that he discovered the musical ratios with blacksmiths' hammers; that he coined the word "philosophy" — all rejected or unsupported (SEP-Pyth §§4–5).
- That Theano led the Pythagorean school after Pythagoras' death, or that any surviving writing is genuinely hers — unsupported (SEP-PythM; WP-Theano).
- That Empedocles died by leaping into Etna — "We can be sure that he did not" (SEP-Emp).
- Xenophanes as straightforward "first monotheist" — reading called "problematic" (SEP-Xen §3).
- That Zeno wrote "40 paradoxes" as a firm fact — reported by Proclus (and Elias) for a later work of uncertain fidelity (SEP-ZenoE §1; IEP-Zeno). Say "reportedly forty".
- Precise birth/death years for Thales, Anaximander, Anaximenes, Heraclitus, Parmenides, Empedocles — all derive from ancient chronographers (Apollodorus' 40-year *acme* convention) or Plato's dramatic dating; always give "c." and the alternatives listed above.
- Plato's *Sophist* "parricide" reply to Parmenides (the Eleatic Visitor's "what is not" as "the different") — not verified in this session (SEP page not reachable at the guessed URL); omit or verify separately.
- G. E. R. Lloyd's position in the mythos/logos debate — not found in this session's sources.
- Any page numbers or contents of Buxton 1999, Burkert 2004, West 1971 beyond what is given above (only search-summary evidence; BMCR not reachable).
- Leucippus' dates beyond "5th c. BCE"; and that he was really Zeno's pupil (a story SEP-Leu explains as an inference from his response to Zeno).
