## 1. Setting: the śramaṇas and "rejecting the Vedas"
- Classical Indian systems "either admitted or denied the infallibility of the Vedas". Upholding Vedic authority: Vedānta, Mīmāṃsā, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika. Challenging it: "Cārvāka materialism and various schools of Buddhism and Jainism". [source: SEP-Nat §1]
- IEP-Jain: Mahāvīra's doctrines, like the Buddha's, "were formulated as a reaction to and rejection of the Brahmanism"; Jainism, Buddhism and the Cārvāka materialists "were regarded as the unorthodox darsanas, because they taught that the Vedas and Upanisads, and hence the brahman caste, had no authority". [source: IEP-Jain intro]
- *Nāstika* is used by almost all schools "as a critical term" for a school that has breached what is acceptable; its "greatest recipient" is the Cārvāka. [source: IEP-Mat §4] (Consistent with the What Is Philosophy? notes: the āstika/nāstika classification is "a scholarly construct" and lists vary.)
- Mahāvīra and the Buddha both belong to the śramaṇa movements of "Greater Magadha" in the period of the second urbanisation; both lived under kings Bimbisāra and Ajātaśatru of Magadha. [source: WP-Mahavira, note citing Dundas 2002]
- At Mahāvīra's time (fifth–sixth centuries BCE) wandering mendicant teachers practised *vibhajya*: instead of answering philosophical questions one-sidedly, the teacher "analyz[ed]" them, showing their presuppositions and possible meanings. [source: SEP-Jain §3.4.1]

## 2. Mahāvīra, Pārśva and the tīrthaṅkaras
- Mahāvīra ("the Great Hero"), birth name Vardhamāna, is for Jains the 24th and last Jina/tīrthaṅkara of the current cosmic period; "the only extant teaching is that of Mahāvīra". [source: SEP-Jain intro & §2.5; WP-Mahavira]
- **Dates (give as ranges).** Traditional: born 599 BCE (both sects); died 527 BCE (Śvetāmbara) or 510 BCE (Digambara). SEP gives "traditional dates 599–527/510 BCE". [source: SEP-Jain intro; WP-Mahavira]
  - The Jain *Vīra Nirvāṇa Saṃvat* era starts 527 BCE; Rapson judged the king list it rests on unreliable. [source: WP-Mahavira]
  - From Hemacandra's *Pariśiṣṭaparvan* some historians date birth 549 BCE and death 477 or c. 468–467 BCE. [source: WP-Mahavira]
  - Scholars accepting the "short chronology" for the Buddha (c. 480–400 BCE) put Mahāvīra up to a century later: Dundas suggests he died "around 425 BCE, or a few years after"; Jeffery Long gives "c.499–427 BCE". [source: WP-Mahavira citing Dundas 2002 p. 24, Long 2020 p. 206]
  - Historians: "6th or 5th century BCE"; historicity "well-established and not in dispute". [source: WP-Mahavira] SEP-Jain calls him "an older contemporary of the Buddha" (the Buddha, younger, may have reached nirvāṇa a few years later: WP-Mahavira note).
  - Safe wording for the chapter: "traditionally 599–527 BCE; most historians now place him in the fifth century BCE."
- Birthplace: Kuṇḍagrāma (near Vaiśālī, Bihar; exact site disputed); death/nirvāṇa at Pāvāpurī (Bihar). SEP: "in Magadha, South of modern Bihar". [source: WP-Mahavira; SEP-Jain intro]
- Life (tradition): left home at about 28 or 30; 12½ years of severe asceticism; attained *kevala-jñāna* (omniscience); preached about 30 years. [source: WP-Mahavira]
- His biography is "very close to that of" the Buddha (left a royal family, became an ascetic, founded a community of mendicants and laity); "one notable difference being that he follows a tradition that was existing long before him". [source: SEP-Jain §2.5]
- **Pārśva (Pārśvanātha)**, the 23rd tīrthaṅkara, is "the earliest Jain tīrthaṅkara who is acknowledged as a historical figure". Tradition: born 273 years before Mahāvīra (c. 872–772 BCE). Historians: c. 8th–7th (or 8th–6th) century BCE; Dhaky argues Pārśva "may have passed away only a few decades before" Mahāvīra began preaching. [source: WP-Pārśvanātha; WP-Mahavira]
  - Mahāvīra is seen by historians as reviving and reforming an earlier (proto-)Jain community "likely" led by Pārśva; Winternitz: a reformer of the *Nigaṇṭhas* ("fetter-less") known from Buddhist texts. The Ācārāṅga says Mahāvīra's parents followed Pārśva. [source: WP-Mahavira; WP-Pārśvanātha]
  - Pārśva's *cāturyāma* "fourfold restraint" (non-violence, non-lying, non-stealing, non-possession) vs Mahāvīra's five great vows (adding celibacy). Jacobi accepted Pārśva's historicity partly because the four restraints appear in Buddhist texts (e.g. the Samaññaphala Sutta attributes them to the Nigaṇṭha). The Uttarādhyayana has Keśin (Pārśva's follower) and Gautama (Mahāvīra's disciple) discuss the difference. Digambaras read "fourfold" differently (Jaini: "four modalities"). [source: WP-Pārśvanātha]
- **Tīrthaṅkara** = "ford-maker": one who makes a *tīrtha*, a crossing over the river of rebirth. Each cosmic half-cycle has 24; the first of the current one is Ṛṣabha (Ādinātha). Liberated Jinas "know all, but are by essence unable to influence the course of action of the world". [source: SEP-Jain §2.5; WP-Tirthankara]
- Jains hold the teaching to be eternal, without founder; "the Jainism of this age can be traced back to Mahavira". [source: IEP-Jain intro]
- Jainism today: "between four and five million followers". [source: WP-Jainism]

## 3. Sources and the Śvetāmbara/Digambara split
- Jain philosophy is written in Ardhamāgadhī, Jaina Māhārāṣṭrī, Śaurasenī, Sanskrit, Apabhraṃśa, Kannada, Tamil, Gujarati, Hindi and others; Sanskrit became the lingua franca of inter-school debate "at the turn of the common era". [source: SEP-Jain intro]
- **Ācārāṅga Sūtra** ("Canonical Text on Behavior"): "one of the most ancient Jaina texts", in early Ardhamāgadhī, c. 4th–3rd centuries BCE (SEP). WP gives first book c. 5th–4th c. BCE, second book late 4th–3rd c. BCE. [source: SEP-Jain §1.2; WP-Ācārāṅga_Sūtra] Other canonical datings in SEP: Sthānāṅga 3rd–2nd c. BCE; Uttarādhyayana 2nd c. BCE–1st c. CE; Bhagavatī Sūtra old parts 5th–2nd c. BCE. Canonical literature overall: "3rd century BCE—6th century CE". [source: SEP-Jain]
- **Tattvārthasūtra** ("Treatise on What There Is"), associated with Umāsvāmin/Umāsvāti, "probably written by multiple authors around 150–400 CE"; "the first work and main representative of classical Jaina philosophy" and the oldest Jain treatise in Sanskrit. [source: SEP-Jain §1.3] WP: 2nd–5th c. CE, 350 sūtras in 10 chapters, accepted by both Digambaras and Śvetāmbaras; its sūtra *parasparopagraho jīvānām* ("souls render service to one another") is the motto of Jainism. [source: WP-Tattvartha_Sutra] The auto-commentary (*Bhāṣya*) is dated by SEP to 400–450 CE.
- **Kundakunda**: not a single author but "the name that stands for the collective authorship" of a Prakrit (Jaina Śaurasenī) tradition composed 3rd–8th c. CE around Karnataka (Balcerowicz 2017), "as influent as the TS". Central teaching of the *Samayasāra*: "karmic matter is never genuinely mixed with the self". [source: SEP-Jain §2.4] WP: traditional date 2nd–3rd c.; scholarly layers 350–400 up to 950–1150 CE; distinction between *niścaya-naya* (ultimate) and *vyavahāra-naya* (mundane) perspective. [source: WP-Kundakunda]
- **Śvetāmbara canon**: 45 texts, mostly Ardhamāgadhī, codified at the Council of Valabhī under Devardhigaṇi, 454 CE (WP-Śvetāmbara; other sources give the 5th century without a year). The 12th Aṅga (Dṛṣṭivāda, with the 14 Pūrvas) is lost according to both sects. [source: WP-Śvetāmbara; WP-Jainism]
- **Digambaras** reject the Śvetāmbara canon, holding Mahāvīra's words "neither survive nor could be recorded"; their oldest text is the *Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama* (from Dharasena's teaching, c. 2nd century). [source: WP-Digambara]
- **The split.** *Digambara* = "sky-clad" (monks naked); *Śvetāmbara* = "white-clad". [source: WP-Digambara]
  - Digambara account: a 12-year famine under Candragupta Maurya (c. 4th c. BCE); Bhadrabāhu led monks south to Karnataka; those who stayed under Sthūlabhadra took to white robes. Śvetāmbara account (*Viśeṣāvaśyaka Bhāṣya*, 5th c.): the Digambara sect arose 609 years after Mahāvīra's nirvāṇa, founded by Śivabhūti. [source: WP-Jainism; WP-Digambara]
  - Scholarly view: "Most modern scholars, such as Padmanabh Jaini and Paul Dundas, conclude that the schism was not a single 'event' but a gradual hardening of differences over several centuries"; inscriptions from as early as the 1st century CE already show the division; often formally associated with the 5th-century Valabhī council. [source: WP-Jainism] Treat both traditional stories as sectarian.
  - WP-Digambara contains partisan wording ("Digambaras are the original followers of Mahavira") — not to be used.

## 4. Jain metaphysics: jīva, ajīva, karma as matter
- Shared principles: "Jaina metaphysics is an atomist and dualist conception of the world"; it focuses on the self, karmic matter and their association. [source: SEP-Jain intro]
- TS 1.1: "The path to liberation is constituted by right view (darśana), right knowledge, and right conduct." (the "three jewels"). [source: SEP-Jain §1.3, translation as given in SEP]
- **Seven categories (tattvas)**, TS 1.4: self (*jīva*), non-self (*ajīva*), inflow of karmic matter (*āsrava*), bondage (*bandha*), stoppage (*saṃvara*), gradual dissociation/destruction (*nirjarā*), liberation (*mokṣa*). Five of the seven "are not ontological ones" but describe how the self relates to what is not conscious. [source: SEP-Jain §1.3]
- **Six substances**: selves plus five non-self substances: medium of motion (*dharma*), medium of rest (*adharma*), space (*ākāśa*), matter (*pudgala*); time (*kāla*) is a substance "only in some textual traditions" (TS 5.38). [source: SEP-Jain §1.3, §2.1; IEP-Jain §1 gives the same six]
  - *Dharma* here means the medium of motion, not virtue — "an instance of a Jaina tendency to physicalize the philosophical and ethical concepts"; "as water is the condition of the motion of fish". [source: SEP-Jain §2.1]
  - Space includes the world (*loka*) and an empty beyond (*aloka*). The inhabited universe is often pictured as a standing man (IEP: "arms akimbo"; SEP: "Around the 16th c., the world is presented in the form of a human being"). [source: IEP-Jain §1; SEP-Jain §2.5]
- **The self (jīva)**: "unobstructed consciousness"; TS 2.8 "The defining characteristic of the self is experience (upayoga)". Selves are many ("as many selves as there are living beings"), eternal, active (Jains need an active self "because moral distinctions lose their value on the hypothesis of the passivity of the soul", against Sāṃkhya). The self is co-extensive with its body, expanding and contracting like a lamp's light in a pot or a room. Jains criticise both the Buddhist no-self view and the Brahmanical view that the individual self is ultimately one with the universal self. [source: SEP-Jain §§1.4, 2.1, 2.2]
- Some selves are "unfit" (*abhavya*) for liberation: "When a self is unfit, no amount of good act can change this fact." [source: SEP-Jain §2.1; WP-Jainism]
- **Karma as subtle matter**: "karma is in Jainism conceived as a subtle type of matter that fills all cosmic space"; passions "act as a glue", making karmic matter stick to the self. SEP's illustration: a red-tinted mirror (*rāga* means both "red" and "passion") distorts one's reflection. [source: SEP-Jain §1.4]
  - Eight types of karmic matter (TS 8.5): four "harming" (deluding, knowledge-obscuring, perception-obscuring, obstacle) and four "non-harming" (feeling, name, life-span, clan/status). [source: SEP-Jain §2.1]
  - IEP: since karma is material, "all karma, both good and bad, leads to rebirth"; "No karma can help a person achieve liberation". Release is "purging the soul of all karma, good and bad". [source: IEP-Jain §§1, 3]
  - Jain karma theory includes calculations of masses and durations (e.g. Nemicandra's *Gommaṭasāra*, 10th c.); TS 8.15: bondage to life-karma lasts up to 33 "ocean-measured periods". [source: SEP-Jain §§2.1, 2.4]
- **No creator god**: atoms "have always been there and their association follows natural laws"; Jain and Buddhist philosophers produced "a lengthy tradition of refutations" of the Nyāya arguments for God, because a world-controlling consciousness would undermine ethical responsibility. IEP: "frequently said to be atheistic. What is denied is a creator god"; gods exist but are subject to karma. [source: SEP-Jain §2.2; IEP-Jain §1]
- Jain atomism: atoms combine according to rules (a "viscid" atom combines with a "dry" one, TS 5.32), giving a "natural forces" account, in contrast to Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika's appeal to God and *adṛṣṭa*. [source: SEP-Nat §2.2]
- Cosmic time: the wheel of time has descending and ascending half-cycles of six periods; we are now in the fifth descending period, in which liberation is no longer possible in our region. [source: SEP-Jain §2.5]
- **Senses taxonomy**: one-sensed beings (touch only: plants and "elemental" earth-, water-, fire-, air-bodies, so there are souls in stones and metals); two-sensed (worms: + taste); three-sensed (ants: + smell); four-sensed (bees, flies: + sight); five-sensed (humans, fish, birds, quadrupeds, gods, hell-beings: + hearing), some with mind (*manas*). Only humans can be liberated ("a reasoned anthropocentrism"). [source: SEP-Jain §§1.2, 2.3; IEP-Jain §1 agrees in outline]
- SEP notes the Jain conception of Gośāla: a famous example of a human self falling to the lowest life-form (*nigoda*) "is that of Makkhali Gośāla, leader of the heretic Ājīvika movement, who was disrespectful and challenged Mahāvīra". [source: SEP-Jain §2.3] (Useful as evidence of hostile sources.)

## 5. Ahiṃsā: its extent, intention, asceticism, sallekhanā
- "A peculiarity of Jainism is to essentially associate these renunciatory liberating practices with the imperative of non-violence (ahiṃsā)", grounded in the belief that each living being embodies "a permanent divine-like self". [source: SEP-Jain §1.2]
- Classifications of violence include direct acts, delegated acts and "the bare fact of permitting a harmful act"; acts may be physical, verbal or mental. "This version of non-violence is so extreme that one's restrained action is very close to one's mere absence of act, since even breathing, drinking water, or walking can prove harmful." [source: SEP-Jain §§1.2, 2.3]
- Practices: no meat, no unfiltered water, careful walking, meditation, fasting, celibacy, non-possession "up to the nudity of some monks". [source: SEP-Jain §1.2] Digambara monks carry a peacock-feather broom (*picchī*) to move insects; Śvetāmbara ascetics wear a small mouth-cloth. [source: WP-Digambara; WP-Ahimsa_in_Jainism]
- Lay vs monastic: lay Jains should avoid harming beings with two or more senses; monastics also one-sensed beings, so they cannot cook; a redistribution of merit lets lay donors support them. Laity take the "minor vows" (*aṇuvrata*). [source: SEP-Jain §2.3; WP-Ahimsa_in_Jainism]
- Five great vows of monks: *ahiṃsā*, *satya* (truthfulness), *asteya* (not taking what is not given), *brahmacarya* (chastity), *aparigraha* (non-attachment/non-possession). [source: IEP-Jain §3; WP-Sallekhana]
- **From act to intention (a historical shift)**: "in early canons, violent acts are the only direct factors of bondage; while in later canonical texts, passions came to play an important role, which means that one's intention to harm is valued"; non-intentional harm "can be forgiven" (Johnson 1995). [source: SEP-Jain §1.2] Dundas quotes Jinabhadra (7th c.): "It is the intention that ultimately matters… a man does not become a killer only because he has killed… a doctor has to cause pain but is still non-violent". [source: WP-Ahimsa_in_Jainism, citing Dundas]
- SEP-Jain also says the difficulty of these norms is "one of the fields in which the many-sided perspectival stance of Jaina philosophers emerged": an act harmful from one perspective may be non-harmful from another. [source: SEP-Jain §1.2]
- Ācārāṅga, as quoted in SEP: causing violence to earth-bodied beings "is the knot of bondage; it, in fact, is the delusion; it, in fact, is the death; it, in fact, is the hell" (ĀS 1.1.25). [source: SEP-Jain §1.2; translator not named in SEP — SEP's bibliography lists Jacobi 1884]
- **Sallekhanā**: ritual fast to death by gradually reducing food and drink; open to ascetics and householders; Jain scholars deny it is suicide "because it is not an act of passion, nor does it employ poisons or weapons". Appropriate in old age, calamity, famine, incurable disease. *Ratnakaraṇḍa-śrāvakācāra* 127–128 describes giving up solid food, then milk and whey, then hot water. [source: WP-Sallekhana] SEP explains its logic: the last volitions at death influence the next life's life-karma, so Jains "seek a good death… free from fears and desires". [source: SEP-Jain §2.4]
  - In 2015 the Rajasthan High Court banned it as suicide; in 2016 the Supreme Court of India stayed that decision. [source: WP-Sallekhana; SEP-Jain mentions "legal and cultural turmoil in recent years"]
  - IEP-Jain: "the ideal way to end one's life, for a Jain, is to sit motionless and starve to death"; Mahāvīra "and other great Jain saints, are said to have died this way". [source: IEP-Jain §3] (Attribute; tradition, not history.)
- Objection and reply (IEP): the code looks self-interested, aimed at the agent's liberation; Webb replies that the same holds of any religion-based code, and the law of karma is what links morality to personal advantage. [source: IEP-Jain §3]

## 6. The debate over women's liberation (strī-mokṣa)
- Digambaras "maintain that women cannot attain nirvana"; Śvetāmbaras hold that women (and eunuchs) can. [source: WP-Digambara]
- **Digambara argument**, step by step (from WP-Sexual_differences_in_Jainism and WP-Digambara):
  1. Complete non-possession, including of clothing, is "an essential component of the path to liberation" (Mahāvīra practised naked asceticism; both sects agree on that).
  2. Women cannot (socially and by monastic rule) go fully naked; both sects agree nudity is not permitted for nuns.
  3. So a woman's renunciation is only partial; the best a nun can achieve is rebirth as a man.
  - Supplementary arguments in Jain texts: women are "inherently weaker, passion-filled", and their bodies "generate and destroy life-forms within their sexual organs… thus repeatedly infringing nonviolence". [source: WP-Sexual_differences_in_Jainism]
  - A further argument (WS, second-hand): women cannot be reborn in the lowest (seventh) hell, so they lack the extreme capacity of action that, at the other pole, liberation needs. [source: WS-3, summarising Jaini's *Gender and Salvation* and study notes — NOT checked in a primary source; use only with attribution "Jaini reports"]
- **Śvetāmbara reply**: clothing is "merely… tools that enable the religious life of restraint", not possessions that attach; nudity is "deemed inappropriate" in the current cosmic age; nothing in scripture precludes women's liberation. They hold the 19th tīrthaṅkara, Mallī (Mallinātha), was a woman; Digambaras deny this. [source: WP-Sexual_differences_in_Jainism; WP-Jainism]
- The Śvetāmbara position is not simply egalitarian: Mallī's female birth is explained as a karmic result of deceit in a past life, and texts of both sects describe women as deceitful. [source: WP-Sexual_differences_in_Jainism]
- Scholarship: Padmanabh S. Jaini, *Gender and Salvation: Jaina Debates on the Spiritual Liberation of Women* (University of California Press, 1991), an anthology of six Jain texts on the question (c. 150–c. 1700 CE). Śākaṭāyana (Yāpanīya, 9th c.) is described as a defender of women's spiritual abilities. [source: WP-Digambara bibliography; WS-3]
- Nuns outnumber monks: a 1970s–80s survey counted about 125 Digambara monks and 50 nuns, against about 1,200 Śvetāmbara monks and 3,400 nuns (WP-Digambara); the *Kalpa Sūtra* says Mahāvīra's community had two and a half times as many female as male ascetics, and Candanbālā is said to have led 36,000 nuns (tradition). [source: WP-Digambara; WP-Sexual_differences_in_Jainism]

## 7. Jain epistemology
- Knowledge: TS lists four kinds of indeterminate perception and eight kinds of cognition: sense-cognition, testimonial cognition, mental (telepathic) cognition, "cosmic" (clairvoyant) cognition, absolute cognition (*kevala*), plus three faulty counterparts. [source: SEP-Jain §3.1] IEP gives the list as sense perception, testimony (including scripture), extra-sensory perception, telepathy and *kevala*, with inference added only by later thinkers, together with memory and *tarka*. [source: IEP-Jain §2]
- For Jains *pratyakṣa* ("direct") means directly present *to the soul* (mental, cosmic and absolute cognition), whereas sense-knowledge counts as mediated — the reverse of other schools' usage. [source: SEP-Jain §3.1]
- Testimony is authoritative only from a liberated, passionless teacher (*āpta*), against the Mīmāṃsā view that the Veda is authoritative because authorless. Samantabhadra (RKŚĀ 1.8): a teacher instructs as a drum sounds at a skilled touch, wanting nothing. [source: SEP-Jain §3.2]
- Inference: Jain logicians (from Siddhasena Mahāmati, 710–780 per SEP, and the Digambara Akalaṅka, 720–780) cut the Nyāya five-member argument to two necessary statements (thesis + reason). Against Dignāga's three conditions, they hold that a good reason is one "impossible otherwise" (*anyathānupapatti*). The necessary connection (*vyāpti*) cannot be known by perception, "since perception deals only with particulars, and… even the biggest list of particular instances would not suffice to reach certainty"; it is known by a special cognition, *tarka*. [source: SEP-Jain §3.3] (This is the Jain reply to the Cārvāka problem; see §11.)

## 8. Anekāntavāda, syādvāda (saptabhaṅgī), nayavāda
- **Anekāntavāda** = "the famous doctrine of the many-sidedness of things". Reality is "multifaceted (anekanta, or 'non-one-sided'), such that no one view can capture it in its entirety". [source: SEP-Jain §3.4.2; IEP-Jain §2]
- **Its metaphysical root (SEP)**: every existing thing is "at the same time persistent and changing"; TS 5.29: "Existence is endowed with production, decay and duration." A pot is already existent with respect to its substance (*dravya*, the clay), but formerly non-existent with respect to its modes (*paryāya*: shape, function). Substance and mode coexist "as a single discrete entity", a many-sided object of knowledge. This opposes the Vaiśeṣika (qualities separate from substances, linked by inherence) and the Buddhist view that only non-complex unities are real. Jains are **realists**: perspectives multiply "because the known object itself has many aspects". [source: SEP-Jain §§2.4, 3.4.2]
- **Early example (Bhagavatī Sūtra)**: asked whether the world/soul is eternal, Mahāvīra answers that the world is eternal ("it was, it is and it will be") and non-eternal (it cycles); the soul is eternal (it never ceased to exist) and non-eternal (it passes from hell-being to animal to human to god). That is, eternal with respect to substance, non-eternal with respect to modes. "Mahāvīra does not make contradictory predications, but he refutes extremes views… by making explicit the parameters". By contrast the Buddha "is known to refuse to answer questions of the sort" (Matilal 1981). [source: SEP-Jain §3.4.1]
- **Syādvāda and the sevenfold predication (saptabhaṅgī)**. Using *a* for any object and *F* for any predicate, with *syāt* ("perhaps", "from a perspective", "somehow") before each [source: IEP-Jain §2; SEP-Jain §3.4.3]:
  1. syāt *a* is F (exists);
  2. syāt *a* is not F (does not exist);
  3. syāt *a* is F and is not F;
  4. syāt *a* is inexpressible (*avaktavya*);
  5. syāt *a* is F and inexpressible;
  6. syāt *a* is not F and inexpressible;
  7. syāt *a* is F, is not F, and is inexpressible.
  - Samantabhadra's *Āptamīmāṃsā* 14 is "amongst the first to teach" the first four: an entity is "somehow" being, non-being, both, and indescribable, "in accordance with the speaker's intention and not in an absolute fashion". Āmī 15: something "is" with respect to its own form, substance, place, time and state, and "is not" with respect to what is alien to it — "the not-being blue is part of the essence of the red pot". Inexpressibility arises when existence and non-existence are considered *simultaneously*. Haribhadra's *Anekāntajayapatākā* spends most effort on the sense of existence and non-existence. [source: SEP-Jain §3.4.3]
  - Early Jain works (esp. TS) indicate that for any object and predicate all seven are true; they "are not to be understood as seven truth-values, since they are all seven thought to be true". [source: IEP-Jain §2]
  - Note on order: both IEP and SEP give the order above (inexpressible fourth). Some presentations put "inexpressible" third; not verified here, so follow this order.
- **Objections and replies (IEP)**: (1) Śaṅkara, among others, charged inconsistency: forms 3 and 7 conjoin a proposition and its negation, which "can never be even assertible". Reply: read *syāt* as "from a perspective"; from one perspective *a* is F, from another not-F, and one person may assert both. (2) "Perhaps the deepest problem": self-defeat. If anekāntavāda itself is only "perhaps" true, Jains must allow "Perhaps reality is not multifaceted"; "What begins as a laudable fallibilism ends as an untenable relativism" (Webb's verdict, to be attributed). [source: IEP-Jain §2] SEP's reading answers the relativism charge differently: the perspectives are grounded in a realist ontology of substance and modes, not in the knower's whim. [source: SEP-Jain §3.4.2] A Jain reply recorded on WP: the "perhaps" applies to the nature of reality, not to ethics; the five vows are strict and "there is no 'perhaps' about them". [source: WP-Digambara, citing Dundas] Present this as a named debate.
- **Nayavāda (doctrine of perspectives)**: "a meta-epistemology", classifying the standpoints from which the complex object is known, "from the most inclusive to the most particular". Developed by Siddhasena Mahāmati, Akalaṅka and commentators (Siddharṣigaṇi, 900 CE; Prabhācandra). The seven *nayas* and the rival schools Jains saw as "erroneous radicalization" of each [source: SEP-Jain §3.4.4]:
  1. *naigama* (comprehensive: unity and difference, but not at once) — overdone by Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika;
  2. *saṃgraha* (synthesising: unity) — overdone by Advaita Vedānta and Sāṃkhya;
  3. *vyavahāra* (empirical, everyday difference) — overdone by the **Cārvākas**;
  4. *ṛjusūtra* (the actual, present moment) — overdone by the Buddhists;
  5–7. *śabda* (grammatical), *samabhirūḍha* (etymological), *evaṃbhūta* ("actual etymological") — word-centred, overdone by the Grammarians.
  - Pūjyapāda (*Sarvārthasiddhi*): like threads woven into cloth, the perspectives serve a purpose only together. SEP: nayavāda "seems to be the Jaina reaction to philosophical and religious pluralism in classical India"; it shows "how each could contribute to the whole picture" rather than flattening them. Many scholars see the many-sidedness doctrines as Jainism's "major contribution to philosophy". [source: SEP-Jain §3.4.4]
  - IEP's simpler gloss: "any judgment is true only from the viewpoint or perspective of the judge, and ought to be so expressed". [source: IEP-Jain §2]
- **Blind men and the elephant — origin**:
  - The oldest datable version is **Buddhist**: *Tittha Sutta*, Udāna 6.4 (Khuddaka Nikāya, Pali Canon). Monks hear rival ascetics quarrelling over whether the world is eternal, whether the soul is the same as the body, etc.; the Buddha tells of a king who has blind men feel an elephant (head = pot, ear = winnowing basket, leg = post…) and they come to blows. The Buddhist moral is against dogmatic quarrelling sectarians, not for pluralism. [source: WP-Tittha_Sutta; WP-Blind_men_and_an_elephant]
  - Jain use is attested in **medieval** texts to illustrate anekāntavāda and syādvāda: Vidyānanda's *Tattvārthaślokavārttika* (9th c.) and Malliṣeṇa's *Syādvādamañjarī* (13th c.). [source: WP-Blind_men_and_an_elephant] IEP-Jain calls it "the famous story" illustrating anekānta.
  - Śaṅkara also mentions it (commentary on Chāndogya Upaniṣad 5.18.1). Later Sufi (Rumi) and Bahá'í versions; John Godfrey Saxe's 19th-century poem popularised it in English. [source: WP-Blind_men_and_an_elephant]
  - The parable's Jain moral differs from the Buddhist: each blind man is partly right (each grasps a real aspect), not simply wrong.

## 9. The six teachers of the Sāmaññaphala Sutta (DN 2)
- The *Samaññaphala Sutta* ("The Fruit of the Contemplative Life") is the 2nd discourse of the Dīgha Nikāya. King Ajātasattu of Magadha reports to the Buddha the answers of six famous śramaṇa teachers to his question about the visible fruit of the ascetic life, finding each answer like being asked about a mango and answered with a breadfruit. It is a Buddhist text, so these are hostile summaries. [source: WP-Samaññaphala_Sutta]
- **Pūraṇa Kassapa**: *akiriyavāda* (non-action, no moral efficacy): killing, stealing, adultery, lying bring no evil; "If with a razor-edged disk one were to turn all the living beings on this earth to a single heap of flesh… there would be no evil from that cause"; gifts and sacrifice bring no merit. [source: WP-Purana_Kassapa, quoting Thanissaro's DN 2 translation]
- **Makkhali Gosāla**: there is no cause or condition for the defilement or purification of beings; beings are impelled by fate (*niyati*), circumstance and nature; fools and wise alike wander through a fixed span of (8,400,000) great aeons and then make an end of suffering, as a thrown ball of string unwinds to its end. [source: WS-1, WS-2 summaries of DN 2 translations; WP-Ājīvika. Exact wording NOT verified against a translation; paraphrase only.]
- **Ajita Kesakambalī** ("of the hair blanket"): materialism/annihilationism. Quoted on WP: "There is no such thing as alms or sacrifice or offering. There is neither fruit nor result of good or evil deeds. A human being is built up of four elements. When he dies the earthly in him returns… to the earth, the fluid to the water, the heat to the fire, the wind to the air, and his faculties pass into space… Fools and wise alike, on the dissolution of the body, are cut off, annihilated, and after death they are not." [source: WP-Ajita_Kesakambali — translator not named on WP; wording resembles Rhys Davids' 1899 *Dialogues of the Buddha*, NOT verified]. The Brahmajāla Sutta associates him with *ucchedavāda* (annihilation) and identity of soul and body. He is often treated as a forerunner of Lokāyata; IEP-Mat even names him with "Cārvāka" as co-founder (a weak claim). [source: WP-Ajita_Kesakambali; IEP-Mat intro]
- **Pakudha Kaccāyana**: seven eternal, uncreated, non-interacting substances (earth, water, fire, wind, pleasure, pain, soul); hence "When one cuts off [another person's] head, there is no one taking anyone's life. It is simply between the seven substances that the sword passes." [source: WP-Pakudha_Kaccayana, quoting Thanissaro]
- **Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta** = Mahāvīra; DN 2 attributes to him the "fourfold restraint" (*cātuyāma-saṃvara*), which scholars link to Pārśva's tradition. [source: WP-Pārśvanātha]
- **Sañjaya Belaṭṭhaputta**: refusal to commit: "I don't think so. I don't think in that way. I don't think otherwise. I don't think not. I don't think not not." The Brahmajāla calls this *amarāvikkhepa*, "eel-wriggling". He is said to have been the first teacher of Sāriputta and Moggallāna. [source: WP-Sanjaya_Belatthiputta, quoting Thanissaro] A recent study (Chakravarty 2021) compares Sañjaya's agnosticism with Mahāvīra's anekāntavāda.
- Kizil Caves (Xinjiang, 4th c. CE) paint Mahāvīra among the "Six Heretical Teachers". [source: WP-Mahavira caption]

## 10. Makkhali Gosāla and the Ājīvikas
- *Ājīvika* derives from *ājīva*, "livelihood, mode of life"; the school is known for the doctrine of *niyati*, "fate", and the claim that there is no free will. [source: WP-Ājīvika]
- **Known only through opponents**: Ājīvika scriptures are lost; "All of the available information about Gosala and about the Ājīvika movement generally comes from Buddhist and Jain sources", "regarded by most scholars as… coloured by sectarian hostilities". Main sources: the Jain *Bhagavatī Sūtra* and Buddhaghosa's commentary on DN 2; also the Sandaka Sutta, Vāyu Purāṇa, and later Tamil texts (*Maṇimēkalai*, *Nīlakēci*). [source: WP-Makkhali_Gosala; WP-Ājīvika]
- Gosāla (Pali Makkhali; Skt. Maskarin Gośāla; Jain Prakrit Gosāla Maṅkhaliputta): contemporary of Mahāvīra and the Buddha. Name "cowshed" (*gosālā*), allegedly from his birth in a cowshed near Sāvatthī. Basham dates his death to 484 BCE (from the *Mahāvaṃsa*). Charpentier and others argue the Ājīvika tradition predates him. [source: WP-Makkhali_Gosala; WP-Ājīvika]
- **Bhagavatī account**: Gosāla was Mahāvīra's companion/disciple for six years; they split; the uprooted sesame plant that re-rooted and fulfilled Mahāvīra's prediction is said to have convinced Gosāla of reanimation and fate. Later he claimed to be a different, reanimated soul inhabiting Gosāla's body; Mahāvīra called this sophistry; the Bhagavatī records factions "coming to blows". The narratives' episodes "reflecting poorly on Gosala" are "another likely indication of sectarian bias", though some may be adapted Ājīvika stories. [source: WP-Makkhali_Gosala; WP-Ājīvika]
- **Doctrine as reported**: Buddhist and Jain sources "agree that Gosāla was a rigid determinist, who exalted Niyati to the status of the motive factor of the universe and the sole agent of all phenomenal change"; suffering and its ending are "without cause or basis". Lochtefeld's summary: life is "like a ball of pre-wrapped up string, which unrolls until it [is] done". Yet Ājīvikas lived as naked ascetics. [source: WP-Ājīvika]
- **The puzzle and the revisionist reading (a debate)**: Dundas: "it seems doubtful whether a doctrine which genuinely advocated the lack of efficacy of individual effort could have formed the basis of a renunciatory path"; "the suspicion must be that the Jains and Buddhists deliberately distorted Ajivika doctrine". Bronkhorst: Gosāla taught that asceticism can stop the inflow of new karma but cannot destroy old karma — which explains why Ājīvikas practised austerities and why their popularity could rival Jainism and Buddhism. Others hold that what Jain and Buddhist sources agree on can be trusted, since those two were rivals of each other too. [source: WP-Ājīvika]
- Another reading: Gosāla combined *niyati*, *svabhāva* (nature), *saṅgati* (circumstance) and perhaps *pariṇāma* (development); all beings are destined for final purification (*saṃsāra-suddhi*). [source: WP-Ājīvika] SEP-Nat pairs "the reductionist Cārvākas and the nihilist Ājīvika-s" as deniers of causal connection. [source: SEP-Nat §2.3]
- Tamil texts report an Ājīvika atomism: seven *kāyas* (earth, water, fire, air, joy, sorrow, life), uncreated and "barren", made of indivisible atoms combining by fixed cosmic rules. [source: WP-Ājīvika] (Note the parallel with Pakudha's seven substances in DN 2.)
- **Patronage**: height of popularity under the Maurya emperor Bindusāra (4th–3rd c. BCE; the Buddhist *Aśokāvadāna* says Bindusāra and his queen were believers). Aśoka dedicated caves in the Barabar hills (Bihar) to the Ājīvikas in his 12th and 19th regnal years (c. 257 and c. 250 BCE; WP also gives 258/251 on a 269 BCE coronation): e.g. "This Banyan-cave was given to the Ajivikas by King Priyadasin, crowned twelve years". His 7th Pillar Edict names officials for the Saṃgha, Brahmins and Ājīvikas. The *Aśokāvadāna* also claims Aśoka massacred some 18,000 Ājīvikas in Puṇḍravardhana — a hostile Buddhist legend at odds with his own inscriptions. [source: WP-Ājīvika]
- **Disappearance**: after the Mauryas, only occasional mentions; the word "Ājīvikas" in several Barabar inscriptions was later chiselled out (probably before the 5th c. CE, while Brāhmī was still readable). In the south, at least 17 inscriptions (from c. 446 CE to 1346 CE, near Kolar, Karnataka) record a tax on "Ācuvas" (probably Ājīvikas, though Hultzsch and Hoernlé thought this meant Jains). Basham thinks remnants merged into other sects and may have survived to the 15th century. The 14th-century *Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha* does not mention them. [source: WP-Ājīvika]
- Basham's *History and Doctrines of the Ājīvikas* (London: Luzac, 1951) remains the standard study; Balcerowicz, *Early Asceticism in India: Ājīvikism and Jainism* (Routledge, 2015). [source: WP-Ājīvika; WP-Makkhali_Gosala]

## 11. Cārvāka / Lokāyata: names, founder, sources
- Names: *Lokāyata* ("prevalent among the people" per Chattopadhyaya; dictionary sense "directed towards the world, worldly"); *Cārvāka* (etymology uncertain: Hemacandra derives it from *carv*, "to chew"; others from *cāru-vāk*, "agreeable speech"; or eponymous); *Bārhaspatya*, after Bṛhaspati. [source: WP-Charvaka; IEP-Mat intro]
- The meaning of "Lokāyata" changed: in Kauṭilya's *Arthaśāstra* it is one of three *ānvīkṣikīs* (with Sāṃkhya and Yoga) and seems to mean logic/debate, not anti-Vedic materialism; in the *Divyāvadāna* (c. 200–350 CE) a "technical logical science"; Śaṅkara and Śāntarakṣita use it for materialism. [source: WP-Charvaka] IEP-Mat says it signified materialism only "between the 6th and 8th century C.E." [source: IEP-Mat intro]
- **Bṛhaspati** is the traditional (perhaps mythical) founder; the base text, the *Bṛhaspati-sūtra* (Bārhaspatya-sūtras), is lost, known only in quoted fragments. IEP-Mat: "at the least… the mythical authority on Indian Materialism and at most the actual author of the since-perished Bṛhaspati Sῡtra". [source: IEP-Mat intro; WP-Brihaspati; WP-Charvaka]
- Bhattacharya collected the fragments: "Cārvāka Fragments: A New Collection", *Journal of Indian Philosophy* 30.6 (2002): 597–640. Commentators on the base text whose fragments survive: Kambalāśvatara, Purandara, Aviddhakarṇa, Udbhaṭa (about 30 fragments). [source: WP-Brihaspati bibliography; WS-9]
- Opening aphorisms as quoted in the scholarship (Bhattacharya 2002 pp. 603–604): *pṛthivy āpas tejo vāyur iti tattvāni* ("earth, water, fire, air: these are the principles"); *tebhyaś caitanyam* ("from them, consciousness"); *kiṇvādibhyo madaśaktivat* ("like the power of intoxication from ferments, etc."). [source: WS-9 — second-hand; translation mine from the cited glosses, check before quoting]
- **Known mostly through opponents**: "There are no existing works that serve as the doctrinal texts for the Lokāyata". Chatterjee and Datta: "we do not find any systematic work on materialism… But almost every work of the other schools states, for refutation, the materialistic views". [source: IEP-Mat §3; WP-Charvaka]
- Bhattacharya (2009): the Cārvāka as a system "flourished in ancient India between the eighth and twelfth centuries CE" and then disappeared; a pre-Cārvāka materialism also existed. WP: "no continuity in the Charvaka tradition after the 12th century". [source: WP-Ramkrishna_Bhattacharya; WP-Charvaka]
- Hostile representations include the *Mahābhārata* (12.39, a demon named Cārvāka disguised as a brahmin, burnt by the brahmins' mantras), the *Rāmāyaṇa* (Jābāli's nāstika speech, 2.108, refuted by Rāma), and Sanskrit plays (*Prabodhacandrodaya*, *Āgamaḍambara*). [source: WP-Charvaka]
- Jain doxography: Haribhadra (8th c.) includes Lokāyata among six *darśanas* in his *Ṣaḍdarśanasamuccaya*: "no God, no samsara, no karma, no duty, no fruits of merit, no sin". [source: WP-Charvaka]
- Akbar's 1578 symposium included Cārvāka thinkers per Abu'l-Fazl's *Ā'īn-i Akbarī* (contested evidence; WP-Charvaka; treat cautiously).

## 12. Mādhava's Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha (a hostile source)
- The *Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha* ("Compendium of all views"), 14th century, traditionally attributed to Mādhava (Mādhavācārya, usually identified with Vidyāraṇya, head of the Śṛṅgeri maṭha c. 1374/80–1386). Authorship is debated: Thakur (1961) argued the author was Cannibhaṭṭa, working with Sāyaṇa and Mādhava. [source: WP-Vidyaranya]
- It sets out 16 systems in an ascending series culminating in Śaṅkara's Advaita; the Cārvāka comes **first, i.e. lowest**. [source: WP-Vidyaranya, citing Radhakrishnan] It is "one of the very few sources" for Lokāyata, but "some of these arguments… may be a mere caricature". [source: WP-Vidyaranya] SEP-Epi cites it as the source of the standard Cārvāka argument against inference. [source: SEP-Epi §2]
- Famous Cārvāka verse it quotes (Cowell & Gough translation): "While life is yours, live joyously; / None can escape Death's searching eye: / When once this frame of ours they burn, / How shall it e'er again return?" [source: WP-Charvaka block quote; WS-7 confirms it is in Cowell & Gough's translation, Trübner's Oriental Series, 1882 per that summary]
- It also has the Cārvākas say: "while life remains, let a man live happily, let him feed on ghee even though he runs in debt", and calls the Vedas incoherent, self-contradictory and a livelihood for priests. [source: WP-Charvaka]
- Bhattacharya: the hedonism and immorality charges come from opponents; "no authentic Carvaka aphorism" supports the view that "all materialists are nothing but sensualists", and key hedonist passages are missing in many manuscripts. [source: WP-Charvaka; WP-Ramkrishna_Bhattacharya]

## 13. Cārvāka doctrine: metaphysics and ethics
- Only four elements (earth, water, fire, air); the Cārvākas "reject atomism, however, since they refuse to admit any imperceptible thing", including God, soul and *ākāśa*. [source: SEP-Nat §2.3]
- Consciousness emerges from the elements combined in the right proportion, though none has it alone — "like emergence of red colour from the chewing of beetle-nut and certain other ingredients"; the self "is nothing but conscious material body". Major interpreters call this reductionist; Ganeri reads it as non-reductive supervenience. [source: SEP-Nat §2.5] Adamson and Ganeri (2020) compare it to J.S. Mill's emergentism. [source: WP-Charvaka]
- IEP-Mat gives three arguments for body as the substratum of consciousness (via K.K. Mittal 1974): no disembodied consciousness is observed; sensation and perception arise only as conditioned by the body; medicine shows certain foods improve intellect. Two sub-views: no soul apart from body; or a material (air/breath) soul that perishes with the body. Critics charge the fallacy of moving from "the soul cannot be known" to "the soul does not exist". [source: IEP-Mat §3b]
- **Svabhāvavāda** (naturalism, "the view of individual natures", R. Bhattacharya 2002): things have their properties by nature — fire hot, water cold, thorns sharp — so causal analysis is "redundant"; some associate this with *ākasmikatāvāda*, the fortuitous generation of events. Verse: "The fire is hot, the water cold, refreshing cool the breeze of morn; / By whom came this variety? from their own nature was it born." [source: SEP-Nat §2.3; WP-Charvaka block quote, translator unnamed]
  - Udayana's reply (*Nyāyakusumāñjali* I.4–5): an event that occurs only at some times (*kādācitka*) must be conditional on something, so must have a cause; if an effect did not depend on a cause it "could have occurred at any time, in fact at all times". SEP's author adds that Cārvākas "may find an ally in quantum physical talk" about spontaneous decay. [source: SEP-Nat §2.3]
- "Only Cārvāka hints at the causal closure of the physical world." In SEP-Nat's taxonomy, the Cārvākas are "hard naturalists", Advaitins non-naturalists. [source: SEP-Nat §1]
- Denied: rebirth, an extracorporeal soul, efficacy of ritual, heaven and hell, karma, merit and demerit. [source: WP-Charvaka; IEP-Mat]
- Ethics: commonly reported as egoistic hedonism (IEP-Mat §4); Bhattacharya and Riepe contest this ("Cārvākas hold truth, integrity, consistency, and freedom of thought in the highest esteem", Riepe 1964, quoted in WP-Charvaka).

## 14. THE ARGUMENT: only perception; no inference (step by step)
Main sources: SEP-Epi §2; SEP-Jain §3.3; SEP-Nat §2.3; WP-Charvaka; WS-6 (Cowell's SDS translation, second-hand).
- **Background: inference needs vyāpti.** In the stock example, one infers fire on a hill from smoke because smoke is invariably accompanied by fire (*vyāpti*, "pervasion": wherever smoke, there fire). An inference transmits certainty "based on the 'invariable concomitance' (vyāpti)". [source: SEP-Jain §3.3; SEP-Nat §3]
- **The Cārvāka argument** (reconstruction; SEP-Epi: "Inference depends upon generalizations which outstrip perceptual evidence, everything F as a G. No one can know that"):
  1. Inference is a source of knowledge only if the universal concomitance it relies on (all smoke is accompanied by fire) is known.
  2. Perception grasps only particular, present cases; it cannot cover all past, future and distant instances. (Jain logicians concede: "perception deals only with particulars, and… even the biggest list of particular instances would not suffice to reach certainty", SEP-Jain §3.3.)
  3. Nor can we perceive that there is no hidden condition (*upādhi*) on which the concomitance depends (fire produces smoke only with wet fuel); "the absence of conditions… can not be established beyond doubt by perception". [source: WP-Charvaka; WS-6]
  4. The concomitance cannot be known by another inference, since that inference needs its own concomitance, and so on — an infinite regress (Cowell's SDS translation, via WS-6).
  5. Nor by testimony, which "presupposes that a speaker would tell the truth and thus is subject to the same criticism"; likewise comparison and the rest. [source: SEP-Epi §2]
  6. So no universal concomitance can be established, and inference is not a valid source of knowledge; perception alone is.
  7. So claims resting only on inference or scripture — God, the soul surviving death, karma, heaven — are not known. SEP-Epi notes the motive: "With an eye to the alleged power of inference to prove the existence of God or personal survival". [source: SEP-Epi §2]
- **What this argument is**: SEP-Epi calls it "The Cārvāka argument identifying the problem of induction" (compare Hume — the comparison with Locke and Hume is made in IEP-Mat §3a).
- **Objection 1: performative self-refutation / pragmatic reply (Nyāya).** "The standard response is pragmatic. We could not act as we do if we could not rely on inference"; "The skeptic himself relies on such generalizations when he opens his mouth to voice his skepticism, by using words with repeatable meanings" (Gaṅgeśa, *Tattvacintāmaṇi*, inference chapter). [source: SEP-Epi §2] Gaṅgeśa: someone who doubted that smoke comes from fire should not, as he does, "resort to fire for smoke… to food to allay hunger, and to speech to communicate" (Phillips's translation). [source: SEP-Epi §8] Udayana's maxim (*Nyāyakusumāñjali*): doubt is limited by practical contradiction (*vyāghāta*), and *tarka* is the limit of doubt; a doubt about the future can itself only arise by inference. [source: WS-8, second-hand; verse numbering varies by edition]
  - Also: to deny inference the Cārvāka must himself infer (e.g. that others are wrong, that the soul does not exist). Akalaṅka: the materialist cannot prove there is no omniscient being, since perception's scope cannot show that no other proof exists, and "inference… has no validity [for him]". [source: SEP-Jaya §1.1]
- **Objection 2: fallibilism, not scepticism (Buddhist and Nyāya).** "The Cārvāka argument… is turned by both Buddhist and Nyāya philosophers into an argument for fallibilism about inference": inferences can rest on pseudo-reasons (*hetvābhāsa*), but "the very concept of a fallacy… presupposes that of the veritable reason". [source: SEP-Epi §2]
- **Objection 3: grounding the concomitance.** Buddhists (Dharmakīrti's tradition) ground it in identity of nature or causality — "invariable concomitance is easily cognisable by means of identity" (e.g. a śiṃśapā is necessarily a tree) (Cowell's SDS translation, via WS-6; the Sissoo/śiṃśapā example also in SEP-Jain §3.3). Jains: it is known by a special cognition, *tarka*, which grasps the universal in the particular. [source: SEP-Jain §3.3] Nyāya: knowledge of pervasion comes through generalisation from positive and negative cases, presupposing perceivable similarity. [source: SEP-Epi §7.1]
- **Reply on the Cārvāka side (a debate about what they held).** The later Cārvāka Purandara (fl. c. 8th c.), in a fragment preserved by Kamalaśīla, says that inference "well known in the world" is accepted even by the Cārvākas; what they reject is inference used beyond the everyday path, to the imperceptible. [source: WS-6/Purandara search, second-hand] Bhattacharya argues the Cārvākas "did admit inference in so far as it was grounded on perception". [source: WP-Ramkrishna_Bhattacharya] IEP-Mat similarly: they accepted probable, mundane inference (smoke → probably fire) but refused inferences "about what has never been perceived, namely god or the after-life". [source: IEP-Mat §3a] So the textbook slogan "perception is the only pramāṇa" (SEP-Epi; Mādhava) may be the opponents' simplification. State as a debate: Mādhava/SEP-Epi vs Bhattacharya/Purandara.
- **Note for the reader**: SEP-Epi's opening contrasts this "distinctive" Indian scepticism with the Madhyamaka type (Nāgārjuna's regress of pramāṇas). [source: SEP-Epi §2]

## 15. Jayarāśi Bhaṭṭa and the Tattvopaplavasiṃha
- Dates: "most likely flourished between 800–840 probably in Southern India" (Balcerowicz). Earlier proposals: 8th c. (Saṁghavī & Pārīkh 1940); 725–825 (Saṁghavī 1941, accepted by Franco 1987); 770–830 (Franco 1994, from apparent allusions to Dharmottara, c. 740–800). Balcerowicz places him after Akalaṅka (c. 720–780) and before Vidyānanda (c. 850), the first to mention him. [source: SEP-Jaya §1.1] SEP-Perc: "c. 8th century CE". Give "8th–9th century".
- The title: *Tattvopaplavasiṃha*, "The Lion of the Dissolution of [all] Categories" or "The Lion [Destroying] the Delusion of Categories" (also rendered "The Lion which Annihilates [all] Principles"). [source: SEP-Jaya intro, §1.3]
- The single palm-leaf manuscript was found in 1926 in a library at Patan (Gujarat) by Sukhlāljī Saṁghavī and Rasiklāl C. Pārīkh and published in 1940 (Gaekwad Oriental Series 87). No commentary survives. [source: SEP-Jaya §1.3, bibliography]
- Franco (1994): "Tattvopaplavasimha is the only Lokayata text which has been discovered so far". [source: WP-Jayarāśi_Bhaṭṭa]
- **Main claim**: knowing objects depends on valid means of knowledge (*pramāṇas*), and establishing those depends on proper definitions; but testing definitions requires prior knowledge of real objects. TUS 1 (Balcerowicz's translation): "To establish cognitive criteria… depends on proper definitions. Further, to establish objects of cognitive criteria depends on cognitive criteria. When proper definitions are absent, how is it possible that one would treat both the cognitive criteria and their objects as genuinely real?" — "We land in vicious circle". [source: SEP-Jaya §2.2]
- He examines the definitions of perception, inference, presumption, analogy, absence, equivalence, tradition and testimony (reducible to three), mainly in Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya, Mīmāṃsā, Buddhist and Jain authors. No definition of perception reliably separates genuine perception from mirage, illusion or dream. Inference rests on perception, on an undefinable notion of causality, and on universals, which do not exist; particular-to-particular relations cannot ground general inferences; induction "cannot be demonstrated to be universally valid". Testimony needs other pramāṇas and a criterion of reliability. [source: SEP-Jaya §2.2]
- **Method**: a reductio (*prasaṅga*) borrowed from Madhyamaka (Nāgārjuna, c. 150 CE): list every conclusion an opponent's thesis T admits, refute each, so reject T — without committing himself to not-T. Example: if the universal exists, it is either different from its individuals, not different, or both; each fails; so there is no universal. Nyāya would class this as *vitaṇḍā* (refutation-only debate). [source: SEP-Jaya §2.1]
- He is a "qualified sceptic", a methodological sceptic; unlike Descartes he seeks no foundations and "does not really cast doubt on what comes to us from the senses": the point is that "given our present definitions… there is no way to determine which of our sense-perceptions are true". Balcerowicz compares him with Sextus's Pyrrhonist "perpetual investigator". [source: SEP-Jaya §§2.1–2.2]
- **Positive views** (reconstructed by Balcerowicz): no karmic retribution, afterlife or supernatural; "the worldly path should be followed" (ethics = what people agree to); consciousness from the combination of elements; universals do not exist; macroscopic material objects exist; demons, atoms and God are fictions. [source: SEP-Jaya §2.3]
- **Affiliation (a debate)**: Saṁghavī & Pārīkh, Franco, Balcerowicz: within the Cārvāka tradition (Franco: "a radical change from a rather primitive materialism to a highly sophisticated form of scepticism"). He calls Bṛhaspati "Honourable Bṛhaspati" and in his colophon claims to add arguments that "escaped the attention of the preceptor of gods". Against: Chattopadhyaya (one cannot be both materialist and sceptic), Werner (1995), and Ramkrishna Bhattacharya (2009): "Jayarāśi was not a Cārvāka/Lokāyata", rather an idealist (solipsist). Others (Matilal, Solomon, Gokhale, Mills) call him a sceptic loosely or not at all affiliated. [source: SEP-Jaya §1.4]
- Reception: largely ignored by other schools; Jain thinkers Vidyānanda and Anantavīrya and the Naiyāyika Bhāsarvajña engaged him; Malliṣeṇa (c. 1229): "A refutation of all cognitive criteria in details should be consulted from the Tattvôpaplava-siṁha". [source: SEP-Jaya §1.3]
- Translations: Franco 1987 (first chapter, with edition); Esther Solomon 2010; V.N. Jha 2013 (complete). [source: SEP-Jaya bibliography]

## Debates
- **Mahāvīra's dates**: traditional 599–527/510 BCE vs fifth-century datings tied to the Buddha's short chronology (Dundas: death c. 425 BCE; Long: c. 499–427). [source: WP-Mahavira; SEP-Jain]
- **Pārśva and Mahāvīra**: four restraints vs five vows; Jacobi/Śvetāmbara reading vs Digambara reading (Jaini: "four modalities"). [source: WP-Pārśvanātha]
- **The schism**: Digambara famine story vs Śvetāmbara Śivabhūti story vs scholarly "gradual hardening" (Jaini, Dundas). [source: WP-Jainism]
- **Women's liberation**: Digambara (nudity required; women's bodies and natures) vs Śvetāmbara (clothes are tools; Mallī was a woman). Jaini 1991 collects the texts. [source: WP-Sexual_differences_in_Jainism; WP-Digambara]
- **Syādvāda — contradiction or perspectivism?** Śaṅkara's inconsistency charge vs the "from a perspective" reading; Webb's self-defeat/relativism charge vs SEP's realist grounding in substance and modes. [source: IEP-Jain; SEP-Jain]
- **Intention vs act in Jain ethics**: early canon counts acts; later texts count intention (Johnson 1995). [source: SEP-Jain §1.2]
- **How far to trust the hostile sources on the Ājīvikas**: Dundas and Bronkhorst (distorted; Gosāla allowed asceticism to stop new karma) vs those who trust what Jain and Buddhist sources share. [source: WP-Ājīvika]
- **Did Cārvākas reject all inference?** Mādhava, SEP-Epi's summary vs Purandara's fragment, Bhattacharya and IEP-Mat (they accepted inference grounded in perception and rejected inference to the imperceptible). [source: SEP-Epi; WP-Ramkrishna_Bhattacharya; IEP-Mat; WS]
- **Were the Cārvākas hedonists?** Opponents' portrait (SDS; IEP-Mat calls the ethics egoistic hedonism) vs Bhattacharya and Riepe. [source: WP-Charvaka; IEP-Mat §4]
- **Was Jayarāśi a Cārvāka?** Franco, Balcerowicz (yes, a sceptic within it) vs Chattopadhyaya, Werner, Bhattacharya (no). [source: SEP-Jaya §1.4]
- **Cārvāka consciousness**: reductionist (most interpreters) vs Ganeri's non-reductive supervenience. [source: SEP-Nat §2.5]
