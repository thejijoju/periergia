## Framing: not just "reaction to the West"
- SEP-JP's framing: when Western philosophy arrived "in full force in the mid-nineteenth century", the Japanese coined *tetsugaku* for it rather than reusing a word such as *tetsujin* ("wise people"). They were "distinguishing two species of understanding": scholarly detachment (*tetsugakusha*, "wisdomologists") versus engaged, transformative knowing (*tetsujin*). The image: geologists versus master potters understanding clay. [source: SEP-JP §1]
- SEP-JP: Japan's "fourth" fountainhead of philosophy is Western academic philosophy (post-1868), one source among five (Shintō, Confucianism, Buddhism, Western philosophy, *bushidō*). The *wakon yōsai* formula, "Japanese spirituality and Western ingenuity", allocated Western philosophy to the "ingenuity" side. [source: SEP-JP §§3.4, 4.4]
- Halbfass and "Neo-Vedanta": Halbfass defined "Neo-Hinduism" as "the adoption of Western concepts and standards and the readiness to reinterpret traditional ideas in light of these new, imported and imposed modes of thought". He traced the term to Bengal, used by both Christian missionaries and Hindu traditionalists against the Brahmos, with an "obvious" polemical undertone (Halbfass 1995, 2007). [source: WP-Neo-Vedanta]
- Wilhelm Halbfass (11 May 1940 – 25 May 2000), German Indologist at the University of Pennsylvania 1982–2000. *Indien und Europa* (1981), English *India and Europe: An Essay in Understanding* (SUNY 1988). [source: WP-Wilhelm_Halbfass]
- Pre-colonial roots: Andrew Nicholson (*Unifying Hinduism*, 2010) argues that thinkers between the 12th and 16th centuries already treated the Upaniṣads, epics, Purāṇas and "six systems" as "a single whole". So neo-Vedānta's unifying move "has deeper origins in the Muslim period of India", and Vijñānabhikṣu (16th c.) influenced Vivekananda. [source: WP-Neo-Vedanta]
- Bhushan and Garfield, *Minds Without Fear: Philosophy in the Indian Renaissance* (OUP, 2017), argue against the view that Indian philosophy "proper" must be written in Sanskrit, and reject the premise that English was "perforce an alien medium for doing Indian philosophy". It covers Hindu and Muslim, academic and public philosophers; ch. 8 is "Theorizing Svaraj". [source: WS-12 (NDPR review, publisher listings)]
- Gilroy: *The Black Atlantic: Modernity and Double Consciousness* (1993; Harvard UP and Verso). It describes a Black Atlantic culture crossing African, American, British and Caribbean cultures, with the slave ship as image of a position "between two (or more) lands". [source: WP-The_Black_Atlantic] SEP-DC: Gilroy "extends Du Boisian double-consciousness both beyond the American context and at odds with an essentialist understanding of race". The global-diasporic theme would come "to illuminate the experience of post-slave populations in general" (Gilroy 1993: 126). [source: SEP-DC §3]
- SEP-AF: "Africana philosophy" is "a third-order, metaphilosophical, umbrella-concept" for the philosophizing of peoples African and of African descent on the continent and in the diasporas, recognized by professional bodies including the APA "starting in the 1980s"; it "does not refer to a particular philosophy". [source: SEP-AF intro]
- Walter D. Mignolo (b. 1 May 1941), Argentine semiotician at Duke, is "one of the founders of the modernity/coloniality critical school of thought" (decoloniality, "border thinking", "pluriversality"). [source: WP-Walter_Mignolo]
- Liberal empire as backdrop: SEP-COL (after Uday Mehta 1999) says liberal imperialism came from universalism plus a "developmental history" in which capacities for self-government emerge only at a certain "stage of civilization". Mill, "a life-long employee of the British East India Company", called despotism over "barbarians" legitimate as a means to improvement, but listed four reasons foreigners govern colonies badly (*Considerations on Representative Government*, 1861). [source: SEP-COL §3]
- IEP-CP distinguishes comparative philosophy (setting sources from different traditions into dialogue) from area-studies philosophy and "world philosophy" (constructive synthesis). [source: IEP-CP]

## India: Rammohun Roy and the Brahmo movement
- Ram Mohan Roy, 22 May 1772 (c.) – 27 September 1833, died of meningitis at Stapleton, Bristol. Raja title given by Mughal emperor Akbar II, as whose envoy he went to England in 1830 (sailed November 1830). [source: WP-Raja_Ram_Mohan_Roy; WP-Brahmo_Samaj]
- Works: *Tuhfat-ul-Muwahhidin* ("A Gift to Monotheists"; Persian, Arabic introduction), 1804. Atmiya Sabha founded 1814. *The Precepts of Jesus* (1820), followed by "Appeals to the Christian Public" (1820s) against Trinitarian Christianity and the Serampore Baptist missionaries. He was assisted by Unitarians and co-founded the Calcutta Unitarian Society. [source: WP-Raja_Ram_Mohan_Roy]
- Brahmo Sabha: first assembly 20 August 1828, at the house of Firingi Kamal Bose in north Calcutta, "open to all people irrespective of religion, caste, creed". Premises inaugurated 23 January 1830. [source: WP-Brahmo_Samaj]
- Roy "preached the unity of God, made early translations of Vedic scriptures into English" and presented a "pure" monotheistic Vedānta of the Upaniṣads. His beliefs drew on monistic Hinduism, Islam, eighteenth-century Deism, Unitarianism and Freemasonry (Doniger 2014). [source: WP-Raja_Ram_Mohan_Roy]
- Published Upaniṣads chapter (consistent): Roy's English Kena (1816) and Bengali versions served his campaign against idolatry and caste.
- **Sati abolition (how sources describe Roy's role).** The Bengal Sati Regulation (Regulation XVII of 1829) was passed by Governor-General Lord William Bentinck in Council on 4 December 1829. "The most prominent campaigners to end the practice … were led by British Christian evangelists, such as William Carey, and Hindu reformers such as Ram Mohan Roy." Opposition came from Radhakanta Deb and the Dharma Sabha, who appealed to the Privy Council; the ban was upheld, "four of the seven privy councillors supporting it". [source: WP-Bengal_Sati_Regulation,_1829] Roy went to Britain in 1830 partly "to ensure that … [the] Regulation … was not overturned". He petitioned against sati in 1829. [source: WP-Raja_Ram_Mohan_Roy] **Safe wording:** Roy was a leading Indian campaigner. Abolition was a British regulation that followed campaigns by both missionaries and reformers. Do not say Roy "abolished" sati.
- The Regulation's own preamble makes Roy's kind of scriptural argument: sati "is nowhere enjoined by the religion of the Hindus as an imperative duty; on the contrary a life of purity and retirement on the part of the widow is more especially and preferably inculcated". [source: WP-Bengal_Sati_Regulation,_1829 (text of Reg. XVII)]
- Debendranath Tagore (15 May 1817 – 19 January 1905) founded the Tattvaranjini (soon Tattwabodhini) Sabha on 6 October 1839. He took charge of the Brahmo Samaj in 1842 and formally entered its trust with twenty others in 1843. He compiled *Brahmo Dharma* (1848; English tr. Hem Chandra Sarkar 1928) and founded the Santiniketan ashram (land bought 1867 per WP; prayer hall 1863). [source: WP-Debendranath_Tagore; WP-Brahmo_Samaj] De Michelis (via WP): Debendranath "questioned central Hindu beliefs like reincarnation and karma, and rejected the authority of the *Vedas*". [source: WP-Neo-Vedanta]
- Keshab (Keshub) Chandra Sen (19 November 1838 – 8 January 1884) broke with Debendranath in November 1865 over Christian elements, forming the Brahmo Samaj of India (1866), the "First Schism". His 1866 address "Jesus Christ, Europe and Asia" proclaimed that "India would be for Christ alone". The Cooch Behar marriage of his under-age daughter led to the breakaway Sadharan Brahmo Samaj (1878). He announced the "New Dispensation" (Nava Vidhan) in 1881, claiming "all the established religions of the world are true". He met Ramakrishna in 1876. [source: WP-Keshub_Chandra_Sen]

## India: Dayananda and the Arya Samaj
- Dayananda Saraswati (born Mool Shankar, 12 February 1824, Tankara, Gujarat – 30 October 1883, Ajmer). [source: WP-Dayananda_Saraswati]
- Arya Samaj founded 10 April 1875 in Bombay "with ten principles", which were "finally settled in 1877 in Lahore". It holds "the infallible authority of the Vedas". [source: WP-Arya_Samaj]
- *Satyarth Prakash* ("The Light of Truth"), Hindi, 1875 (revised second edition written 1882–83, published 1884), in fourteen chapters. Chapters 11–14 criticise Indian sects, Cārvāka, Buddhism and Jainism, Christianity and Islam. First English translation 1908. [source: WP-Satyarth_Prakash; WP-Dayananda_Saraswati]
- Doctrine: the four Vedas are the "only true uncorrupted" source. Other texts (Upaniṣads, Brāhmaṇas, darśanas) are accepted only so far as they agree with the Vedas, and the Purāṇas are rejected. He opposed idol worship, birth-based caste and untouchability, and supported women's education. Slogan: "Back to the Vedas". [source: WP-Dayananda_Saraswati]
- Contrast drawn by Rambachan (via WP): "The Brahmo Samaj strived towards monotheism, while no longer regarding the Vedas as sole religious authority … Other groups, like the Arya Samaj, strived toward a revival of Vedic authority." [source: WP-Neo-Vedanta]
- Further reading noted by WP: N. A. Salmond, *Hindu Iconoclasts: Rammohun Roy, Dayananda Sarasvati, and Nineteenth-Century Polemics against Idolatry* (2004). [source: WP-Dayananda_Saraswati]

## India: Vivekananda
- Born Narendranath Datta, 12 January 1863, Calcutta; died 4 July 1902, Belur Math. [source: WP-Swami_Vivekananda]
- Joined Keshab Sen's Nava Vidhan (1880), then the Sadharan Brahmo Samaj. First met Ramakrishna (18 February 1836 – 16 August 1886) probably in November 1881 (exact date unknown). Took monastic vows Christmas Eve 1886 at Antpur. Wandering monk from 1888. Reached Chicago 30 July 1893. [source: WP-Swami_Vivekananda; WP-Ramakrishna]
- The World's Parliament of Religions ran 11–27 September 1893 at the Art Institute of Chicago, part of the World's Columbian Exposition. Vivekananda spoke on the opening day (11 Sept., "Response to Welcome"), gave his "Paper on Hinduism" on 19 Sept., and spoke at the final session on 27 Sept. [source: WP-Swami_Vivekananda_at_the_Parliament_of_the_World's_Religions]
- Opening words: "Sisters and Brothers of America" (WP prints "Sisters and brothers of America!"). The *Complete Works* text continues: "It fills my heart with joy unspeakable to rise in response to the warm and cordial welcome which you have given us." The newspaper reports of 11 Sept. 1893 differ ("grand words of welcome given to us by you"). [source: WP-Swami_Vivekananda; WS-1] A two-minute standing ovation from about seven thousand is reported from a "sectarian biography" (S. N. Dhar 1975), so treat it as tradition. [source: WP-Swami_Vivekananda, note]
- Ramakrishna Mission founded 1 May 1897, Calcutta. *Raja Yoga* published 1896. Vedanta Society of New York founded during his Western tour. [source: WP-Swami_Vivekananda]
- Thought: he took Śaṅkara's Advaita as the essence of Hinduism, but followed Ramakrishna in seeing Brahman as both *saguṇa* and *nirguṇa*. He read Vedānta as grounding *śivajñāne jīver sevā*, service to human beings as manifestations of the divine (the "practical" or socially engaged side). He held all sects and religions to be paths to one goal. [source: WP-Swami_Vivekananda; WP-Neo-Vedanta]
- Agehananda Bharati: "modern Hindus derive their knowledge of Hinduism from Vivekananda, directly or indirectly". [source: WP-Swami_Vivekananda]

## India: K. C. Bhattacharyya, Gandhi, Tagore
- Krishnachandra Bhattacharyya (12 May 1875, Serampore – 11 December 1949), University of Calcutta. *Subject as Freedom* (1930) is "almost universally regarded" as "the most important philosophical treatise composed in India during the period of British rule" (WP's wording). He synthesised neo-Vedāntin, Kantian and Husserlian ideas. [source: WP-Krishna_Chandra_Bhattacharya]
- "Svaraj in Ideas": an appeal for freedom from "cultural subjection", "ordinarily of an unconscious character", which "implies slavery from the very start". WP: "delivered in Candranagar in October 1931", unpublished in his lifetime. **Date disputed:** Scroll.in says 1929, as the Sir Ashutosh Memorial Lecture at Hooghly Mohsin College, Chandernagore. The prompt's "1928" is also current in the literature. Publication in *Visva-Bharati Quarterly* vol. 20 (1954) is suggested by one source only. **Say "a lecture of the late 1920s or 1931, published posthumously".** [source: WP-Krishna_Chandra_Bhattacharya; WS-3]
- Gandhi (2 October 1869 – 30 January 1948). *Hind Swaraj or Indian Home Rule*: written 1909 in Gujarati aboard SS *Kildonan Castle* (London to South Africa). It is his only work he translated into English himself. The Gujarati version was banned by the British authorities in India (1910). [source: WP-Hind_Swaraj_or_Indian_Home_Rule; WP-Mahatma_Gandhi]
- *Hind Swaraj* is a dialogue between "Reader" and "Editor". Themes: (1) "Home Rule is Self Rule": some "want English rule without the Englishman … This is not the Swaraj I want"; (2) passive resistance, "the force of love and pity is infinitely greater than the force of arms"; (3) *swadeshi*: "You can govern us only so long as we remain the governed"; (4) rejection of modern civilisation: "India is being ground down, not under the English heel, but under that of modern civilization", and Western civilisation is "such that one has only to be patient and it will be self-destroyed". [source: WP-Hind_Swaraj_or_Indian_Home_Rule (quoting Gandhi's English text)]
- Rabindranath Tagore (7 May 1861 – 7 August 1941), Nobel Prize in Literature 1913. [source: WP-Rabindranath_Tagore]
- Tagore–Gandhi debate: Tagore opposed the Non-cooperation Movement in "The Call of Truth" (1921, *Modern Review*); Gandhi replied in "The Great Sentinel" (*Young India*, 13 Oct 1921). Tagore criticised the "cult of the charkha" (1925) and Gandhi answered in "The Poet and the Charkha". After the Bihar earthquake of 15 January 1934, Gandhi called it "a divine chastisement" for the sin of untouchability, and Tagore rejected that reading (published with Gandhi's reply, *Young India*/Harijan, Feb 1934). [source: WS-4 (book chapter bibliography + secondary)] WP: Tagore sought "to moderate Gandhi's Swaraj protests", and Roy's English-education legacy "sparked debate between" them. [source: WP-Rabindranath_Tagore; WP-Raja_Ram_Mohan_Roy]

## India: caste critics and women reformers
- Jotirao (Jyotirao) Phule (11 April 1827 – 28 November 1890), of the Mali (Shudra) caste. He opened a girls' school in Pune with Savitribai Phule in 1848 and founded the Satyashodhak Samaj on 24 September 1873. *Gulamgiri* ("Slavery"), Marathi with an English preface, 1873, is called a "seed text" for anti-Brahmanical consciousness (Kashyap). He recast the Aryan-invasion theory, with Aryans as conquerors who instituted caste, and took King Bali as hero. [source: WP-Jyotirao_Phule; WP-Gulamgiri] Consistent with the published Modern South Asia History chapter.
- Pandita Ramabai Sarasvati (23 April 1858 – 5 April 1922): the first woman awarded the titles *Paṇḍitā* and *Sarasvatī* (University of Calcutta). Keshab Sen gave her a copy of the Vedas. She gave evidence to the Hunter Commission (1882) and converted to Christianity in England (1883). *The High Caste Hindu Woman* (1887). Sharada Sadan for child widows, Pune, 1889. Mukti Mission, Kedgaon. Opposed by Tilak in *Kesari*. [source: WP-Pandita_Ramabai]
- B. R. Ambedkar (14 April 1891, Mhow – 6 December 1956). Mahar (Dalit). Columbia PhD 1927 and LSE DSc 1923 (doctorates "in 1927 and 1923, respectively"). "Came under the influence of John Dewey" at Columbia. Converted to Buddhism at Nagpur on 14 October 1956 with about half a million followers. [source: WP-B._R._Ambedkar]
- *Annihilation of Caste* (1936): an undelivered address for the Jat-Pat-Todak Mandal, Lahore. The invitation letter was dated 12 December 1935, and the invitation was withdrawn over the text. He self-published it. Gandhi responded in July 1936 in *Harijan* ("A Vindication of Caste"). 2nd ed. 1937: *Annihilation of Caste: With a Reply to Mahatma Gandhi*. 3rd ed. 1944 added "Castes in India" (1916). [source: WP-Annihilation_of_Caste]

## Muslim modernism
- Sayyid (Syed) Ahmad Khan (17 October 1817, Delhi – 27 March 1898, Aligarh). Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College founded 1875 (began 24 May 1875); it became Aligarh Muslim University in 1920. He wrote *The Causes of the Indian Revolt* after 1857, began a *Tafsir* of the Qur'an in 1877 (vol. 1, 1880), and was a "theistic evolutionist" reader of Darwin. [source: WP-Syed_Ahmad_Khan; WP-Aligarh_Muslim_University]
- His principle (single secondary source): since creation is God's work and religion is God's word, "there cannot be any contradiction between the two"; conformity with nature is "the touchstone of a true religion". [source: WS-11 (chapter on him in M. M. Sharif ed., *A History of Muslim Philosophy*, hosted at al-islam.org)]. Usable as paraphrase with caution.
- Jamāl al-Dīn al-Afghānī (1838/39 – 9 March 1897). Pan-Islamist. With ʿAbduh he published *al-ʿUrwa al-wuthqā* ("The Indissoluble Link") in Paris in 1884. *Al-Radd ʿalā al-dahriyyīn* ("Refutation of the Materialists"), 1881. [source: WP-Jamal_al-Din_al-Afghani]
- Renan–Afghānī debate: Ernest Renan (1823–1892) lectured on "L'Islamisme et la science" at the Sorbonne on 29 March 1883. Al-Afghānī replied in a letter to the *Journal des Débats* published 18 May 1883, written in Arabic and translated into French. He conceded that Islam, like all religions, had stifled science, but defended the Arab contribution to science; Renan rejoined. [source: WS-5; Renan dates WP-Ernest_Renan]
- Muḥammad ʿAbduh (1849 – 11 July 1905): student of al-Afghānī at al-Azhar. Exiled 1882. Grand Mufti of Egypt 1899–1905. *Risālat al-Tawḥīd* ("The Theology of Unity"). He was a central figure of the Nahda. [source: WP-Muhammad_Abduh]
- Muhammad Iqbal (9 November 1877, Sialkot – 21 April 1938). Studied at Trinity College, Cambridge (from 1905) and Heidelberg (1907, where Emma Wegenast taught him Goethe, Heine and Nietzsche). PhD at Munich: *The Development of Metaphysics in Persia* (thesis year 1908 per WP infobox; the Nietzsche notes give PhD 4 Nov 1907). [source: WP-Muhammad_Iqbal]
- *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam*: lectures in Madras, Hyderabad and Aligarh, published as *Six Lectures* (Lahore, 1930). The 1934 Oxford edition added a seventh, "Is Religion Possible?" (given in London to the Aristotelian Society, 1932). Lecture VI, "The Principle of Movement in the Structure of Islam", presents *ijtihād* as Islam's principle of movement. Delivery dates are given as 1928–29 or 1929–30. [source: WP-The_Reconstruction_of_Religious_Thought_in_Islam; WS-14]
- Iqbal and Nietzsche: Nicholson, translating *Asrar-i Khudi* (1915) in 1920, said Iqbal owed much to Nietzsche and Bergson; Iqbal denied it. [source: published Nietzsche chapter + its notes; not re-verified here beyond WP-Muhammad_Iqbal on Heidelberg and Nicholson's translation]

## Japan
- Meiji Restoration 1868. Meiji Constitution 1889. "Meiji Enlightenment": attraction to Comte's positivism, Mill's utilitarianism, Rousseau and *On Liberty*. German philosophy, Kant above all, became dominant through ties between Tokyo Imperial University and Germany. [source: SEP-JP §§3.4.1, 4.4]
- Nishi Amane (7 March 1829 – 30 January 1897). Sent to Leiden in 1862 (departed 1863) with Tsuda Mamichi, under Simon Vissering; returned 1865. Wrote *Hyakugaku renkan* (an encyclopedia on Comte's model). Helped draft the 1873 Conscription Ordinance. *Hyakuichi shinron* (1874) rejected Confucian ethics as no longer appropriate. [source: WP-Nishi_Amane]
- *Tetsugaku* (哲学): coined by Nishi "sometime in 1867 or earlier" for Dutch *filosofie*, "although it only occurred in a publication for the first time in 1874". Earlier he used *kitetsugaku* (希哲學, "study of admiring wisdom", 1861–62, 1870–71), with *ki* drawn from Zhou Dunyi. He explained its etymology in *Seisei hatsuun* (1873). It became the word in Chinese (*zhéxué*), Korean and Vietnamese. Nishi used it to separate Western philosophy from Eastern thought. [source: WP-Nishi_Amane] Consistent with the published *What Is Philosophy?* chapter.
- Other Nishi neologisms are claimed by WP only in general ("many of these philosophical words are currently in use"); no specific list was verified.
- Meirokusha (Meiji 6 Society): proposed by Mori Arinori in 1873 (Meiji 6) and formally founded 1 February 1874. Original members: Mori, Nishimura Shigeki, Fukuzawa Yukichi, Katō Hiroyuki, Mitsukuri Rinshō, Mitsukuri Shūhei, Nakamura Masanao, Nishi Amane, Tsuda Mamichi, Sugi Kōji. Its *Meiroku zasshi* stopped after the 1875 Press Ordinance and Libel Law. English tr.: Braisted, *Meiroku Zasshi: Journal of the Japanese Enlightenment* (Harvard, 1976). [source: WP-Meirokusha] (WP-Nishi_Amane says "In 1873 … helped to found". Use "1873–74".)
- Fukuzawa Yukichi (10 January 1835 – 3 February 1901). Founder of Keiō Gijuku (named 1868) and the newspaper *Jiji shinpō*. *Seiyō jijō* (*Conditions in the West*, 1866–70; about 250,000 copies). *Gakumon no susume* (*An Encouragement of Learning*, pamphlets 1872–76). *Bunmeiron no gairyaku* (*An Outline of a Theory of Civilization*, 1875). [source: WP-Fukuzawa_Yukichi]
- The opening line of *Gakumon no susume* is framed as a saying: 「天は人の上に人を造らず人の下に人を造らず」と言えり, where *to iu eri* = "it is said". It is commonly rendered "It is said that heaven does not create one man above or below another man". First English translation: David A. Dilworth and Umeyo Hirano (1969); exact 1969 wording not seen. [source: WS-10; WP gives "Heaven never created a man above another nor a man below another"]
- "Datsu-A ron" ("Leaving Asia"): an unsigned editorial in *Jiji shinpō*, 16 March 1885. It was "probably" by Fukuzawa and was included in his collected works in 1933. Shinya Ida's 1996 forensic-linguistic study concluded the author was likely Yoshio Takahashi *or* Fukuzawa. It drew no comment in 1885, was effectively forgotten until the 1950s, and was made notorious by 1967 paperbacks. Key line: "It is better for us to leave the ranks of Asian nations and cast our lot with civilized nations of the West." [source: WP-Datsu-A_Ron]
- Nakae Chōmin (8 December 1847 – 13 December 1901), pen name of Nakae Tokusuke. In France 1871–74 (Iwakura Mission). Translated Rousseau's *Social Contract* and popularised Rousseau. Founded *Tōyō jiyū shinbun* (1881). Elected to the Diet in 1890. [source: WP-Nakae_Chōmin] *Sansuijin keirin mondō*, *A Discourse by Three Drunkards on Government* (1887): a dialogue between a Western-minded "Gentleman of Western Learning", a "Champion" of samurai/expansionist values and the drinker Master Nankai. English tr. Nobuko Tsukui (Weatherhill, 1984; Shambhala reprint 1992; intro. Jeffrey Hammond). [source: WS-7] The 1882 classical-Chinese rendering (*Min'yaku yakkai*) was not verified.
- Inoue Tetsujirō (1 February 1855 – 1944): "Japan's first native born professor of philosophy" at Tokyo Imperial University. Used his prestige "to lend philosophical support to the kokutai theory against liberal democracy" and to make *bushidō* a National Morality for schools. Criticised utilitarian ethics. [source: SEP-JP §4.4.2] Compiled the *Tetsugaku jii* (*Dictionary of Philosophy*, 1881; 1884; 1912). Wrote a commentary on the Imperial Rescript on Education (1890) and condemned Christianity as disloyal to the *kokutai*. [source: WP-Inoue_Tetsujirō]
- Inoue Enryō (18 March 1858 – 6 June 1919): Shin Buddhist priest. First and only philosophy major at Tokyo University in 1881. Founded the Society of Philosophy (1884). His 1885 "Philosophy Ceremony" honoured Buddha, Confucius, Socrates and Kant as the "Four Sages". He founded the Philosophy Academy (Tetsugakkan, 1887; later Tōyō University). His *Prolegomena to a Living Discourse on Buddhism* (1887) carried the slogan of protecting the country and loving the truth. He also founded "Mystery Studies" (*yōkaigaku*). [source: WP-Inoue_Enryō]
- Okakura Kakuzō (Tenshin) (14 February 1863 – 2 September 1913). Wrote in English. *The Ideals of the East with Special Reference to the Art of Japan* (1903) opens "Asia is one." *The Awakening of Japan* (1904): "the glory of the West is the humiliation of Asia". *The Book of Tea* (1906). He stayed in India and talked with Vivekananda and Tagore. [source: WP-Okakura_Kakuzō]
- Nishida Kitarō (19 May 1870 – 7 June 1945). Began *An Inquiry into the Good* (*Zen no kenkyū*) in 1905 and published it in 1911 (Kōdōkan). He used it to secure a post at Kyoto Imperial University in 1910, becoming professor in 1914. [source: SEP-NK §1; WP-An_Inquiry_into_the_Good] English: Masao Abe and Christopher Ives, Yale UP, 1990. [source: SEP-NK bibliography]
- Pure experience: experience in its original form "precedes the differentiation into subject experiencing and object experienced". "The moment of seeing a color or hearing a sound" is prior to judgment (Nishida 1911c: 3, Abe/Ives tr.). Recapturing that unitary foundation "is what Nishida means by the Good". It shows "the influence of William James, Ernst Mach", but stresses non-individuated experience. [source: SEP-NK §2.1]
- The name "Kyoto School" (*Kyōto-gakuha*) comes from Tosaka Jun's 1932 essay "The Philosophy of the Kyoto School", a Marxist critique. [source: SEP-KS]
- China (brief): Yan Fu (8 January 1854 – 27 October 1921) became famous after the defeat of 1894–95. He translated Huxley's *Evolution and Ethics* as *Tianyan lun* (1896–98), Smith (1901), Spencer's *Study of Sociology* (1903), Mill's *On Liberty* as "On the Boundary between the Self and the Group" (1903) and *A System of Logic* (1903), and Montesquieu (1904–09). His *Tianyan lun* preface names "three difficulties in translation: faithfulness, expressiveness, and elegance" (信達雅). First principal of Peking University (1912). He wrote in classical Chinese (IEP-MCP). [source: WP-Yan_Fu; IEP-MCP §2a] Liang Qichao (23 February 1873 – 19 January 1929, WP; IEP-MCP says 1873–1930) joined Kang Youwei's Hundred Days' Reform (1898) and fled to Japan for 14 years. [source: WP-Liang_Qichao]

## Latin America: positivism
- SEP-LAP: in nation-building after independence "the ideology of choice was a version of positivism". The motto "order and progress", "which graces the Brazilian flag", explains its appeal. Positivism "became the official state philosophy of several nations" and was "even used to justify dictatorial regimes, as in the case of Mexico". It mixed Comte, Spencer and Haeckel. Its hegemony ran from mid-19th c. to the first decade of the 20th, and by 1930 remaining positivists were "museum pieces". [source: SEP-LAP §1]
- Comte (19 January 1798 – 5 September 1857). From 1847 the motto "Order and Progress" became "L'amour pour principe, l'ordre pour base et le progrès pour but" ("Love as principle, order as basis, progress as end"). Law of three stages: theological, metaphysical, positive. SEP-C: "The best known case is that of Latin America: Brazil, which owes the motto on its flag … to Comte, and Mexico". Comte's tomb at Père-Lachaise was erected by his Brazilian followers. [source: SEP-C §1; WP-Auguste_Comte]
- Brazil: the Republic was proclaimed 15 November 1889, and the flag adopted 19 November 1889 (Decree No. 4). The design was by Raimundo Teixeira Mendes, with Miguel Lemos, Manuel Pereira Reis and Décio Villares. A provisional US-style flag (Ruy Barbosa) flew 15–19 Nov. [source: WP-Flag_of_Brazil] Positivist Church of Brazil organised by Miguel Lemos and Teixeira Mendes in 1881; Temple of Humanity (Rio) 1897. [source: WP-Religion_of_Humanity] Teixeira Mendes (5 January 1855 – 28 June 1927) led the Church after 1903. [source: WP-Teixeira_Mendes] Positivism spread among army officers through the military schools. [source: published Latin America after Independence chapter, consistent]
- Mexico: Gabino Barreda (1818–1881) studied medicine in Paris 1847–51, where he encountered Comte. His "Oración cívica" (1867) read Mexican history through the three stages, with the Liberal victory grounded in positivism. He founded the Escuela Nacional Preparatoria (opened 1868) under Juárez, with a positivist curriculum that eliminated theology. [source: SEP-MX §7; IEP-LA §3c; WP-Gabino_Barreda] His motto, an adaptation of Comte's: "Liberty as the means; order as the base; progress as the end" (IEP's English). [source: IEP-LA §3c]
- Second generation: Justo Sierra (1848–1912) used Spencer, and his *Evolución política del pueblo mexicano* (1902) "was used by some to justify the ongoing dictatorship" of Porfirio Díaz. Francisco Bulnes (1847–1924). The *científicos* were Díaz's technocratic advisers (José Yves Limantour, Treasury 1893–1911, was their leader). IEP-LA: Sierra later "cast doubt upon both positivism and the dictatorship". [source: SEP-MX §7; IEP-LA §3c; WP-Científico] Díaz ruled 1876–1911 with interludes (published Latin America after Independence chapter, consistent).
- Leopoldo Zea (30 June 1912 – 8 June 2004), student of José Gaos. *El positivismo en México* (1943, his master's thesis) made him famous. English *Positivism in Mexico*, tr. Josephine H. Schulte (Texas, 1974). [source: WP-Leopoldo_Zea_Aguilar; SEP-MX §8.5; IEP-LA bibliography]

## Latin America: Argentina, Martí, Rodó, the anti-positivists
- Domingo Faustino Sarmiento (1811–1888; president of Argentina 1868–74). *Facundo* (first serialised 1845 in *El Progreso*, Chile, in exile). Original title *Civilización i barbarie: Vida de Juan Facundo Quiroga*. An attack on Rosas: civilisation = Europe, cities, Unitarians; barbarism = countryside, gauchos, caudillos. English: Mary Mann (1868), Kathleen Ross (UC Press, 2003). [source: WP-Facundo; WP-Domingo_Faustino_Sarmiento]
- Juan Bautista Alberdi (29 August 1810 – 19 June 1884). *Bases y puntos de partida para la organización política de la República Argentina* (1852) shaped the 1853 Constitution. He argued for a "possible" republic before a "true" one, and for massive northern-European immigration: "to govern is to populate" (*gobernar es poblar*). He quoted approvingly Bolívar's dictum that the new states "need kings with the name of president". He favoured work and commerce, where Sarmiento favoured schooling. [source: SEP-LIB §5]
- Francisco Bilbao (Chilean, 1823–1865; WP gives both 9 and 19 January for his birth) was "one of the first to advocate for the concept of Latin America", against "Yankee individualism". *La sociabilidad chilena* (1844), *América en peligro* (1862). [source: WP-Francisco_Bilbao]
- José Martí (28 January 1853, Havana – 19 May 1895, Dos Ríos). "Nuestra América" appeared in *La Revista Ilustrada* (New York) in January 1891 (WP: 1 January; other sources 10 January) and in *El Partido Liberal* (Mexico) on 30 January 1891. [source: WP-José_Martí; WS-9]
- IEP-LA: Martí was "one of the earliest critics of positivism". Positivists explained Latin American "backwardness" by the biological backwardness of races. Martí blamed international policies, gave "indios, mestizos, negros, and mulatos a place alongside criollos", and warned against imitating both Europe and the US. [source: IEP-LA §3c]
- Rodó: José Enrique Rodó (15 July 1871 – 1 May 1917), Uruguayan. *Ariel* (1900) is a "secular sermon to Latin American youth" in Prospero's voice. Ariel (idealism, art, spirit) is set against Caliban (utilitarian materialism associated with the US), with a warning against *nordomanía*. Written after 1898. IEP-LA dates the "generation of 1900" from it. English tr. Margaret Sayers Peden (Texas, 1988). [source: WP-Ariel_(essay); WP-José_Enrique_Rodó; IEP-LA §4a and bibliography]
- Ateneo de la Juventud: founded 28 October 1909 in Mexico City and renamed Ateneo de México in 1912. It grew out of Antonio Caso's 1909 lectures on the history of positivism at the Preparatoria. Members included Caso, Vasconcelos, Alfonso Reyes and Pedro Henríquez Ureña. [source: WP-Mexican_Youth_Athenaeum] They rejected the positivist views that humanity is moved by selfish calculation and the universe by deterministic law, holding humans "free, creative, and spiritual". [source: SEP-MX §8.1]
- Antonio Caso (19 December 1883 – 6 March 1946): *La existencia como economía, como desinterés y como caridad* (1919), with three levels of existence (economic, aesthetic/disinterested, charitable). [source: SEP-MX §8.1; WP-Antonio_Caso_Andrade]
- José Vasconcelos (28 February 1882 – 30 June 1959). Rector of the National University (to 12 Oct 1921). First Secretary of Public Education (28 Sept 1921 – 27 July 1924). Coined the UNAM motto "Por mi raza hablará el espíritu". [source: WP-José_Vasconcelos; WP-La_raza_cósmica] *La raza cósmica* (1925): Latin America as the source of a mixed "cosmic race" synthesising the four races and leading humanity to the aesthetic stage. [source: SEP-MX §8.1] He attacked Dewey as a vehicle of US imperialism in *De Robinson a Odiseo* (1935). [source: SEP-MX] English: *The Cosmic Race*, tr. Didier T. Jaén (Johns Hopkins, 1997). [source: IEP-LA bibliography]
- SEP-LAP names the "generation of founders" (Romero's term; Miró Quesada's "patriarchs"): Korn, Deústua, Vasconcelos, Caso, Molina, Vaz Ferreira, Farias Brito. [source: SEP-LAP §2.1]

## The Black Atlantic
- Frederick Douglass (c. 1817/1818 – 20 Feb 1895). *Narrative* (1845), *My Bondage and My Freedom* (1855). In 1851 he broke with Garrison's view that the Constitution was pro-slavery, persuaded by Gerrit Smith, Lysander Spooner and others. [source: SEP-FD §§1, 4]
- "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?": delivered 5 July 1852 at Corinthian Hall, Rochester, NY, to the Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society. It was printed as a 40-page pamphlet and is in *The Frederick Douglass Papers*, ser. 1, vol. 2 (1982). [source: WP-What_to_the_Slave…] It set out his positions: slavery is contrary to natural law, blacks are self-evidently human, slavery is inconsistent with the Constitution, republicanism and Christianity, and it should be resisted. [source: SEP-FD §4]
- Douglass's "The Claims of the Negro Ethnologically Considered" (1854) answered racial science. [source: SEP-AF §4]
- Martin Delany (6 May 1812 – 24 January 1885). Co-published *The North Star* with Douglass from 1847. Admitted to Harvard Medical School in 1850 and dismissed within weeks after white students protested. *The Condition, Elevation, Emigration, and Destiny of the Colored People of the United States* (1852) argued for emigration (first to South America, later Africa). He joined the Union army (Major). Novel *Blake* (1859–62). [source: WP-Martin_Delany; SEP-AF]
- Alexander Crummell (1819 – 10 September 1898). Studied at Queens' College, Cambridge. Spent twenty years in Liberia and Sierra Leone (professor of English and moral philosophy at Liberia College). *The Future of Africa* (1862). Principal founder of the American Negro Academy (1897–1924), which initially excluded women "largely" due to his objections. [source: SEP-AC; SEP-AF]
- Edward Wilmot Blyden (3 August 1832, St Thomas, Danish West Indies – 7 February 1912, Freetown). Refused by Rutgers and two other seminaries in 1850 because of his race, he emigrated to Liberia (1850). Professor of Greek and Latin at Liberia College (1861), its president 1880–84, and Liberian Secretary of State 1864–65. Directed Muslim education in Sierra Leone 1901–06. Called "father of Pan-Africanism". One of the first to articulate an "African Personality". *Christianity, Islam and the Negro Race* (London, 1887; 2nd ed. 1888) argued Islam was more unifying for Africans than Christianity. *The Jewish Question* (1898) supported Zionism as a model. [source: WP-Edward_Wilmot_Blyden] SEP-AF lists him among emigrationists; emigration was often tied to "civilizing" missionary aims. [source: SEP-AF]
- Anténor Firmin (18 October 1850 – 19 September 1911), Haitian. In exile in Paris he was elected to the Société d'Anthropologie de Paris in 1884; in his four years there he rose to speak only twice, both times silenced by racist comments. *De l'égalité des races humaines (anthropologie positive)* (Paris, 1885) is a rebuttal of Gobineau's *Essai sur l'inégalité des races humaines* (1853–55) and of Paul Broca. It attacks craniometry and the cephalic index, argues for Black Africans in pharaonic Egypt and cites the Haitian Revolution. It is a positivist (Comtean) work. English: *The Equality of the Human Races (Positivist Anthropology)*, tr. Asselin Charles (2000). He is "the first Black anthropologist" (WP) and influenced Price-Mars, Herskovits and Boas. [source: WP-Anténor_Firmin; WP-The_Equality_of_the_Human_Races; WP-An_Essay_on_the_Inequality_of_the_Human_Races]
- Anna Julia Cooper (born enslaved in Raleigh, NC, 10 August 1858 — "some sources date her birth in 1859" — died 27 February 1964). Oberlin BA 1884, MA 1887. *A Voice from the South: By a Black Woman of the South* (1892) is described as the first book-length Black feminist text (Guy-Sheftall). Her Sorbonne doctorate (1925) was *L'attitude de la France à l'égard de l'esclavage pendant la Révolution*, tr. Frances Richardson Keller as *Slavery and the French Revolutionists*. It analyses the French and Haitian Revolutions, written during the US occupation of Haiti (1914–35). She was the fourth African American woman to earn a PhD, at age 66 per WP (SEP-AF says 65). [source: SEP-AJC; WP-Anna_J._Cooper; SEP-AF]
- Ida B. Wells(-Barnett) (16 July 1862 – 25 March 1931). Born enslaved in Holly Springs, MS. The 1892 Memphis lynching of the People's Grocery owners started her investigations. *Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases* (1892) and *The Red Record* (1895, with an introductory letter from Douglass). [source: WP-Ida_B._Wells; SEP-AF]
- Booker T. Washington: Atlanta Exposition speech, 18 September 1895, Cotton States and International Exposition ("Cast down your bucket where you are"). It accepted segregation for economic advancement and was called the "Atlanta Compromise". *Up From Slavery* (1901). Died 1915. The Du Bois–Washington public debate ran from 1903 to 1915. [source: WP-Atlanta_Exposition_Speech; WP-Atlanta_Compromise; SEP-AF]
- W. E. B. Du Bois (23 February 1868 – 27 August 1963, Accra). Fisk BA 1888. Harvard BA cum laude in philosophy 1890 (courses with William James, Santayana and F. G. Peabody, 1889–90). Studied in Berlin 1892–94 under Schmoller. Harvard PhD in history 1895, "the first black to receive a PhD from Harvard". *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899); *Souls* (1903); NAACP and *The Crisis* (1910). Became a Ghanaian citizen in 1963 and died on the eve of the March on Washington. [source: SEP-DB chronology]
- *The Souls of Black Folk: Essays and Sketches*: A. C. McClurg & Co., Chicago, 18 April 1903. Chapter 1, "Of Our Spiritual Strivings", is a modified version of "Strivings of the Negro People", *Atlantic Monthly*, August 1897, where he first used "double-consciousness". The terms "double-consciousness" and "second-sight" "seem not to have been used in print by Du Bois after 1903". [source: WP-The_Souls_of_Black_Folk; SEP-DC §2]
- "The Conservation of Races" (1897): the second Occasional Paper of the American Negro Academy. It concedes physical science finds "at least two, perhaps three, great families", but holds there are eight "spiritually distinct races", caused by history, law, religion and "habits of thought" rather than biology. [source: SEP-DB §2.2; SEP-AF]
- Pan-Africanism: Henry Sylvester Williams (Trinidad) called the first Pan-African Conference, London, 1900. Pan-African Congresses followed in 1919 (Paris, 19–21 Feb., organised by Du Bois, Ida Gibbs Hunt, Edmund Fredericks and Blaise Diagne; 57 delegates from 15 countries), 1921 (London/Brussels/Paris), 1923 (Lisbon/London), 1927 (New York) and 1945 (Manchester). [source: WP-Pan-African_Congress; SEP-AF lists 1919, 1921, 1923, 1927, 1945]

## Key arguments (step by step)

### A. Rammohun Roy's scriptural case against sati (and Mani's objection)
1. A Hindu practice is binding only if authoritative scripture enjoins it as a duty.
2. The authoritative texts do not enjoin widow-burning as an "imperative duty". They prefer "a life of purity and retirement" for the widow.
3. So sati is not a religious duty. What is not a duty and destroys life may be prohibited without violating Hindu religion.
- This is the argument written into the 1829 Regulation's preamble [source: WP-Bengal_Sati_Regulation,_1829]. It answered the Dharma Sabha's objection that a ban broke the guarantee of non-interference with religion; the Privy Council upheld the ban 4–3 [same source].
- **Objection (Lata Mani, *Contentious Traditions: The Debate on Sati in Colonial India*, UC Press, 1998):** both sides argued over scripture and the definition of "Hindu tradition", and the burned women were "marginal to the debate". Eyewitness testimonies addressed the material hardships of widowhood, which the scriptural framing ignored. Colonial law relied on pandits' readings of contradictory texts. [source: WS-13 (publisher synopsis and review)]
- **Reply on Roy's behalf** (not sourced as a scholar's reply; present as a possible response): the scriptural argument was the one that could move both the colonial state, which had pledged non-interference, and orthodox opinion. Roy also wrote on "Ancient Female Rights" (1822). [source for the tract: WP-Raja_Ram_Mohan_Roy]

### B. Rambachan's critique of Vivekananda (scripture versus experience)
1. In classical Advaita (Śaṅkara), *śruti* (the Upaniṣads) is the *pramāṇa*, the valid means of knowing Brahman. Understanding it (*anubhava*) removes ignorance and liberates.
2. Vivekananda made personal experience, above all *samādhi*, the primary means and final authority, with the Vedas secondary.
3. So his Advaita is not Śaṅkara's but a reinterpretation, and a weaker one: experience claims, unlike the texts, cannot be publicly checked (Rambachan 1991, 1994).
[source: WP-Neo-Vedanta "Sruti versus experience", citing Rambachan 1984, 1991, 1994; published Vedānta chapter is consistent]
- **Objection/reply 1 (James Madaio, "Rethinking Neo-Vedānta", *Religions* 2017):** the reduced centrality of scripture and the emphasis on direct experience "are already present in medieval Advaitic literature such as the *Vivekacūḍāmaṇi*" (VCM 59, 474). The yogic Advaita of texts like the *Yoga Vāsiṣṭha* also shaped Vivekananda, so calling him a rupture overstates his novelty. [source: WP-Swami_Vivekananda; WP-Neo-Vedanta]
- **Objection 2 (Ayon Maharaj/Swami Medhananda):** "Neo-Vedanta" is misleading. It is a vague umbrella, it "misleadingly implies novelty", and it is "indelibly colored by … Paul Hacker's polemical use of the term". Hacker borrowed "neo-Hinduism" from the Jesuit Robert Antoine (1914–1981) and treated neo-Advaitins, in Halbfass's words, as "dialogue partners with a broken identity". [source: WP-Neo-Vedanta]

### C. Ambedkar: caste is not a division of labour
1. Defenders say caste is a (useful) division of labour.
2. But a division of labour is efficient only when work follows choice and aptitude.
3. Caste assigns work by birth, involuntarily, and forbids changing it. It is "not merely a division of labour but a division of labourers", graded in hierarchy.
4. So it produces forced occupation, aversion to work and inefficiency, and it destroys "public spirit", "public charity" and "public opinion".
5. Caste rests on religious notions taught by the *śāstras*. So inter-dining and inter-marriage are not enough; one must "destroy the religious notions on which Caste was founded".
[source: WP-Annihilation_of_Caste (summary and quoted lines)]
- **Objection (Gandhi, *Harijan*, July 1936, "A Vindication of Caste"):** he defended Ambedkar's right to speak and condemned the Mandal. Per standard accounts he distinguished an ideal *varṇa* from caste; that detail is not verified here. **Reply:** Ambedkar's 2nd edition (1937) printed a reply to Gandhi. [source: WP-Annihilation_of_Caste] Ambedkar also charged that Gandhi wrote against caste in English but defended it in Gujarati (published Modern South Asia chapter).

### D. Du Bois's definition of race and Appiah's objection
1. Natural science finds at most two or three physically distinct "families" of humans.
2. Yet history shows spiritually distinct groups with their own "message" for humanity.
3. These differences are caused by history, law, religion and habits of thought, not by biology.
4. So race should be defined socio-historically: "a vast family of human beings, generally of common blood and language, always of common history, traditions and impulses, who are both voluntarily and involuntarily striving together for the accomplishment of certain more or less vividly conceived ideals of life" (1897a, 53).
5. Such races should be "conserved" so that each can make its contribution.
[source: SEP-DB §2.2]
- **Objection (K. Anthony Appiah, "The Uncompleted Argument: Du Bois and the Illusion of Race", 1985; *In My Father's House*, 1992):** (i) the criteria do not individuate the eight races. (ii) "Common history" covertly relies on biological common ancestry, which makes it circular. (iii) The logic "leads naturally to the final repudiation of race as a term of difference". "The truth is that there are no races." [source: SEP-DB §2.2.3]
- **Replies:** Lucius Outlaw (1996) reads the definition as a cluster concept, not necessary-and-sufficient conditions. Paul Taylor (2000) and Ronald Sundstrom (2003) say Appiah misconstrues "common history" and defend race as a social kind. Robert Bernasconi (2009) says the debate "imposes an alien question"; the essay's aim was "to give hope to blacks at a time when scientists were questioning their future", in dialogue with Blyden, Crummell and Douglass. [source: SEP-DB §2.2.3]

### E. Douglass: slaveholders' laws presuppose the slave's humanity
1. Apologists claim blacks are beasts or sub-human, so slavery is no injustice.
2. Yet slave states pass laws punishing slaves for rebellion and other free acts, forbid teaching them to read, and encourage their Christianisation while banning their gatherings.
3. Such laws only make sense if slaves are responsible, rational, moral agents.
4. So slaveholders' own practice shows they know slaves are human ("guilty knowledge"), and the "beast" claim is hypocrisy. Douglass found it absurd even to need to argue this.
[source: SEP-FD §3, on the 1852 speech (SFD: 55–92)]

### F. Others available (brief)
- Gandhi's "Home Rule is Self Rule": if independence merely replaces English rulers with Indians running the same modern civilisation, India becomes "Englishtan". True swaraj needs self-rule and a rejection of modern civilisation. [source: WP-Hind_Swaraj…]
- Barreda's positivist history: history passes through theological, metaphysical and positive stages. Mexico's civil wars were the clash of the first two, and the Liberal (Juárez) victory opens the positive stage, so education must be scientific. True freedom is constrained by law, like a falling body. [source: SEP-MX §7]
- Firmin's anti-craniometry argument: a race-ranking must use a reliable measure of intelligence. Brain size and cephalic index fail as such measures, and "none are useful metrics", so the ranking collapses. Egypt and Haiti are counter-evidence. [source: WP-The_Equality_of_the_Human_Races]

## Debates
- **"Neo-Vedanta" as a label** — Hacker (pejorative, borrowed from Antoine) and Halbfass ("adoption of Western concepts") versus Madaio and Maharaj (continuity with medieval Advaita; the label is misleading). Richard King calls the spread "Vedanticisation". [source: WP-Neo-Vedanta]
- **"Bengal Renaissance"** — revered with nostalgia through the 19th–20th centuries; "due to a new focus on its colonialist origins, a more critical view emerged in the 1970s" (Sumit Sarkar). Led "predominantly" by upper-caste Bengali Hindus, though Bengali Muslims (Rokeya Sakhawat Hussain, Kazi Nazrul Islam) played a role. Kopf, *British Orientalism and the Bengal Renaissance* (1969). [source: WP-Bengal_Renaissance]
- **Sati debate** — Mani's revision (above); "colonialist as well as nationalist historiography" revised. [source: WS-13]
- **Datsu-A ron authorship and significance** — Ida (1996): Takahashi or Fukuzawa; Hirayama (2004): ignored until the 1950s; Banno (1981) reads it as a reaction to the failed 1884 Gapsin coup in Korea. [source: WP-Datsu-A_Ron]
- **Tagore versus Gandhi** — non-cooperation (1921), the charkha (1925), and the earthquake as divine punishment (1934). [source: WS-4]
- **Positivism's meaning in Latin America** — Comte was adapted (Barreda turned "order" into liberal "liberty as the means"), Spencerised (Sierra, Bulnes) and used to justify Díaz. Zea's 1943 study read Mexican positivism historically (WP: in the context of neocolonialism). The thesis that it served as the ideology of a rising bourgeoisie is NOT verified here; see below. [source: IEP-LA; SEP-MX; WP-Leopoldo_Zea_Aguilar]
- **Is there an authentic Latin American philosophy?** — Zea ("La filosofía americana como filosofía sin más", 1969) versus Salazar Bondy (not checked here); SEP-LAM treats authenticity as Zea's main concern. [source: SEP-LAM; SEP-MX §8.5]
- **Vasconcelos's *raza cósmica*** — anti-racist intent (a refutation of social Darwinism) versus critics. Palacios says Vasconcelos advocated a "eugenics of aesthetics", and the book has passages such as "the Chinese … multiply like rats"; Juárez says it upheld "Nordic" systems. [source: WP-La_raza_cósmica]
- **Washington versus Du Bois** — accommodationist gradualism versus immediate civil and political rights. SEP-AF suggests Washington's accommodation may have "masked surreptitious opposition". [source: SEP-AF]
- **Double consciousness's sources** — Emerson's "The Transcendentalist" (1842) and Romantic usage (Bruce 1992), the psychological literature on dual personality (Bruce cites James's *Principles*, 1890, which discussed such cases though James "does not seem to have used the term"), Hegel; Gilroy's diasporic extension. [source: SEP-DC §§2–3]
- **Du Bois's "Conservation"** — Appiah versus Outlaw, Taylor, Sundstrom, Bernasconi and Jeffers. [source: SEP-DB]
- **Iqbal and Nietzsche** — Nicholson versus Iqbal (see Nietzsche chapter).
- **Blyden's Islam thesis** — controversial on publication; at first some did not believe an African had written it. [source: WP-Edward_Wilmot_Blyden]

## Source contradictions found (record; do not propagate)
- WP-Raja_Ram_Mohan_Roy: "In 1828, he launched Brahmo Sabha with Debendranath Tagore". **Wrong**: Debendranath (b. 1817) joined only in 1842/43 (WP-Debendranath_Tagore; WP-Brahmo_Samaj). The 1828 funds came from his father Dwarkanath.
- WP-Bengal_Renaissance: "By 1829, Roy co-founded the Brahmo Sabha". Use 20 August 1828 (WP-Brahmo_Samaj).
- SEP-JP gives "Tanabe Hajime (1865–1962)". The birth year is almost certainly a typo for 1885; not verified here, so omit Tanabe's dates or check them.
- WP-Inoue_Tetsujirō: infobox death 7 November 1944, text 7 December 1944. Say "1944".
- Liang Qichao: died 19 January 1929 (WP) versus "1873–1930" (IEP-MCP). Use 1929.
- Comte's death: 1857 (SEP-C, SEP-LAP, WP) versus "1798–1859" (IEP-LA). Use 1857.
- Martí's birth: 28 January 1853 (WP, IEP-LA) versus "1854–1895" (SEP-LAP). Use 1853.
- Delany's death: 24 January 1885 (WP) versus "1812–1883" (SEP-AF). Use 1885.
- Barreda's birth: 1818 (SEP-MX, IEP-LA, WP-Gabino_Barreda) versus 1820 (WP-Científico). Use 1818.
- Antonio Caso: WP infobox birth 1861 versus text 19 December 1883 (SEP-MX: 1883). Use 1883.
