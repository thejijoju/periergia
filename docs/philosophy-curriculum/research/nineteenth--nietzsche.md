## 1. Life: childhood and education (1844–1869)
- Born **15 October 1844**, Röcken (Röcken bei Lützen), a village c. 20 miles SW of Leipzig, Prussian Province of Saxony; the birthday coincided with the 49th birthday of King Friedrich Wilhelm IV, after whom he was named (he later dropped "Wilhelm"). Father Carl (Karl) Ludwig Nietzsche (1813–1849), Lutheran pastor; mother Franziska, née Oehler (1826–1897). Grandfathers and uncle also Lutheran ministers. [source: SEP-LW §1; SEP-N §1; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- Father died **30 July 1849** of a "brain ailment" (SEP-LW; IEP: "brain hemorrhage"; WP: "brain disease … after a year of excruciating agony"); younger brother Ludwig Joseph died aged two, c. six months later (**4 January 1850**, SEP-LW). Family moved to **Naumburg**, household of mother, grandmother, two paternal aunts and sister **Elisabeth** (Therese Elisabeth Alexandra, 1846–1935). [source: SEP-LW §1; IEP §1; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- **Schulpforta** (Pforta) boarding school, 1858–1864, a former Cistercian monastery near Naumburg; friend Paul Deussen (later Sanskritist and Orientalist). WP: admitted on a scholarship for a pastor's son; the story that he was admitted on academic merit "has been debunked". [source: SEP-LW §1; IEP §1; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- **Bonn 1864**, theology and classical philology; abandoned theology after one semester ("to the anger of his mother") and lost his faith. June 1865 letter to Elisabeth: "if you wish to strive for peace of soul and pleasure, then believe; if you wish to be a devotee of truth, then inquire" (WP's translation; translator not named). [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche; IEP §1]
- Followed **Friedrich Wilhelm Ritschl** to **Leipzig, 1865**; essays on Theognis (published by Ritschl in *Rheinisches Museum*), Simonides, Diogenes Laertius; friendship with Erwin Rohde. [source: SEP-LW §1; IEP §1–2]
- **1865: found Schopenhauer's *World as Will and Representation* by chance in a Leipzig bookshop**, aged 21; 1866 read F. A. Lange's *History of Materialism*. Whether Schopenhauer's "cadaverous perfume" persists in the mature thought "remains a matter of scholarly debate" (SEP-LW). [source: SEP-LW §1; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- Military service 1867–68 (horse artillery, Naumburg); chest injury mounting a horse (March 1868 per WP). [source: SEP-LW §1; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- Met **Richard Wagner in November 1868** in Leipzig at the house of the Orientalist Hermann Brockhaus (husband of Wagner's sister Ottilie). They shared enthusiasm for Schopenhauer; Wagner was the same age Nietzsche's father would have been. [source: SEP-LW §1; IEP §1]

## 2. Basel (1869–1879)
- On Ritschl's recommendation, appointed to the chair of classical philology at **Basel**, began teaching **May 1869**, **aged 24** — "the youngest ever appointed to such a post" (SEP-N); WP: "still among the youngest of the tenured Classics professors on record". He had no completed doctorate or habilitation; Leipzig awarded him a doctorate (WP: honorary doctorate, March 1869). Ritschl's reference: "He will simply be able to do anything he wants to do" (Kaufmann 1954: 8, quoted SEP-N). IEP calls the post "Professor of Greek Language and Literature", appointed January 1869. [source: SEP-N §1; SEP-LW §1; IEP §2; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche; WP-Friedrich_Wilhelm_Ritschl]
- Inaugural lecture "Homer and Classical Philology" (May 1869). [source: IEP §2]
- **Citizenship:** on taking the Basel post he applied to give up his Prussian citizenship; revocation dated **17 April 1869**; he never acquired Swiss citizenship and remained **officially stateless** for the rest of his life. [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche; SEP-LW §1 ("stateless person (having given up his German citizenship, and not having acquired Swiss citizenship)")]
- **Franco-Prussian War:** served **August–October 1870** as a volunteer **medical orderly / hospital attendant** (SEP-LW: participated in the siege of Metz), caught **diphtheria and dysentery**. [source: SEP-LW §1; IEP §1; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche] (WP: Kaufmann "speculates" he also contracted syphilis then — speculation only.)
- Basel friends: theologian **Franz Overbeck** (1837–1905; lived five years in the same house; later rescued him in Turin) and historian **Jacob Burckhardt**, whose lectures he attended. [source: SEP-LW §1; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- **Wagner friendship:** frequent guest at Villa **Tribschen** near Lucerne (Wagner and Cosima); in 1869 called the friendship the "greatest achievement" of his life; helped plan Bayreuth, attended the first festival (1876) and was repelled by it; relations soured after 1876. [source: SEP-N §1; SEP-LW §1]
- **The break:** *Human, All Too Human* (1878), sent to the Wagners early 1878, "effectively ended their friendship"; Nietzsche later wrote that his book and Wagner's *Parsifal* libretto crossed in the mail "as if two swords had crossed" (EH III, HH, 5; Kaufmann tr. via SEP-N). SEP-LW calls Wagner "anti-Semitic" and says HH attacks him in a thinly disguised portrait of "the artist". IEP dates the final break to 1877. Late attacks: *The Case of Wagner* (1888), *Nietzsche contra Wagner* (1888). [source: SEP-N §1; SEP-LW §§1, 5; IEP §1]
- **Paul Rée** (1849–1901): met 1873; together in Sorrento autumn 1876 (Rée writing *On the Origin of Moral Sensations*, 1877, which the *Genealogy* later attacks). [source: SEP-LW §§1, 4; SEP-N §1]
- **Ill health and resignation:** migraines, eye trouble, vomiting; leave 1876–77; **resigned June 1879**, aged 34, with a pension. [source: SEP-LW §1; SEP-N §1; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche] **CONFLICT:** IEP says he was released from teaching in February 1876, that his university affiliation "officially ends in 1878", and that "by 1878" both sides agreed he should retire. Use **1879** (SEP ×2, WP).

## 3. The wandering years (1879–1888)
- Lived on his Basel pension, moving with the seasons: summers mostly in **Sils-Maria** (Upper Engadine, near St. Moritz), winters in **Nice, Genoa, Rapallo, Turin** and elsewhere; never stayed more than a few months anywhere. [source: SEP-LW §1; SEP-N §1; IEP §1; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- Secretary/copyist: former pupil **Heinrich Köselitz ("Peter Gast")**, who transcribed and proofread almost all his later work. [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche; WP-Heinrich_Köselitz]
- **Lou Salomé** (1861–1937), Russian student of philosophy and theology in Zurich: met in **Rome, April 1882** via Rée and Malwida von Meysenbug; the three planned an intellectual commune; Nietzsche proposed marriage (WP: three times per Salomé's own report, whose reliability Kaufmann doubted) and was refused; Elisabeth intrigued against the plan; by November 1882 Salomé and Rée had left him. Salomé's book *Friedrich Nietzsche in seinen Werken* (1894) first proposed the early/middle/late periodization; she later became an associate of Freud (met 1911). [source: SEP-LW §1; SEP-N §1; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche] IEP warns her book helped create the image of a lonely, self-immolating recluse that letters and daily-life evidence contradict.
- Wrote Part I of *Zarathustra* "in only ten days" at Rapallo (WP; Nietzsche's own claim in *Ecce Homo*). Part IV (1885) printed privately in about 40 copies. [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche; WP-Thus_Spoke_Zarathustra]
- 1883: failed to get a lecturing post at Leipzig — by his own account because of his "attitude towards Christianity and the concept of God". [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- 1886: broke with publisher Ernst Schmeitzner over his antisemitism (his writings "completely buried and in this anti-Semitic dump"); printed *Beyond Good and Evil* at his own expense. [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- 1887–88: correspondence with Hippolyte Taine and **Georg Brandes**, who in **1888 lectured on Nietzsche at the University of Copenhagen** — one of the first lecture series on him — and recommended he read Kierkegaard. [source: SEP-LW §1; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche; SEP-MP §4]

## 4. Collapse, illness, death (1889–1900)
- **3 January 1889, Turin:** mental breakdown in the street. Followed by the *Wahnzettel* ("madness notes") signed "Dionysus" or "the Crucified One"; e.g. to Burckhardt: "Wilhelm, Bismarck, and all anti-Semites abolished" (WP translation). Overbeck travelled to Turin and took him to a Basel clinic; then **Otto Binswanger's clinic in Jena**; from 1890 in his mother's care at Naumburg. [source: SEP-N §1; SEP-LW §1; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- **The horse story — treat as legend.** WP: "What happened remains unknown, but an often-repeated tale from shortly after his death states that Nietzsche witnessed the flogging of a horse at the other end of the Piazza Carlo Alberto, ran to the horse, threw his arms around its neck … and then collapsed." SEP-LW tells it but adds "this episode with the horse could be anecdotal"; IEP: "reportedly". WS-3 (LA Review of Books essay "Nietzsche's Horse", and others, second-hand): the only early written account is an anonymous 1900 newspaper interview with his landlord Davide Fino relaying what a policeman said; it has no fall; the beaten horse and tears appear only in a 1930s interview with Fino's son; no corroboration from Overbeck or Elisabeth. [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche; SEP-LW §1; IEP §1; WS-3 https://lareviewofbooks.org/blog/essays/nietzsches-horse/]
- **Cause of illness — debated, unresolved.** Original diagnosis by the Basel and Jena doctors: (tertiary) **syphilis**. Alternatives proposed: chloral hydrate and other drugs; hereditary brain disease from his father; manic-depressive illness with psychosis then vascular dementia (Cybulska); frontotemporal dementia (Orth and Trimble); **CADASIL** (hereditary stroke disorder); a slow-growing **right-sided retro-orbital meningioma** (proposed by Leonard Sax per WP). SEP-LW: "The exact cause … remains unclear." SEP-N (Anderson): "Recent work (Huenemann 2013) has convincingly argued that he probably suffered from a retro-orbital meningioma." [source: SEP-LW §1; SEP-N §1; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche] **Safe wording:** "long attributed to syphilis; many scholars now doubt that diagnosis, and a slow brain tumour is one leading alternative."
- Strokes 1898 and 1899; after his mother's death (1897) Elisabeth moved him to **Villa Silberblick, Weimar**. Died **25 August 1900**, Weimar, of pneumonia and a stroke; buried beside his father at the church in Röcken. Peter Gast's funeral oration: "Holy be your name to all future generations!" [source: SEP-LW §1; SEP-N §1; IEP §1; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- His manuscripts have been in the Goethe- und Schiller-Archiv, Weimar, since 1950. [source: SEP-LW §1]

## 5. Works and periods
Standard periodization (IEP §2; first proposed by Salomé, SEP-LW): juvenilia (before 1869); early 1869–76; middle 1877/78–82; late 1883–88.
- ***The Birth of Tragedy out of the Spirit of Music*** (January 1872, aged 27). Apollonian (order, form, individuation, "logical order") vs Dionysian (instinctual, wild, amoral, creative energy; its prime expression the tragic chorus). Greek tragedy fused both; European culture since Socrates became one-sidedly Apollonian; hope of rebirth through German music, especially Wagner. Schopenhauerian and Wagnerian in flavour. Reissued 1886 as *The Birth of Tragedy, Or: Hellenism and Pessimism* with "An Attempt at Self-Criticism", regretting its "metaphysics of art" and Kantian/Schopenhauerian formulas. [source: SEP-LW §2; SEP-N §1; IEP §2; WP-The_Birth_of_Tragedy]
- **Wilamowitz's attack:** Ulrich von Wilamowitz-Möllendorff (1848–1931), younger Schulpforta alumnus and later a leading classicist, published two polemics titled ***Zukunftsphilologie!*** ("Philology of the Future!") in 1872–73; called Nietzsche a disgrace to Schulpforta who should "gather tigers and panthers about his knees, but not the youth of Germany". Rohde and Wagner replied in Nietzsche's defence. Ritschl called the book "megalomania" (IEP) / "brilliant babbling" (*geistreiche Schwiemelei*, Danto via WP). Enrolments fell. In his memoirs at 80 Wilamowitz still defended his critique of Nietzsche's "rape of historical facts". [source: SEP-LW §1; SEP-N §1; IEP §1; WP-Ulrich_von_Wilamowitz-Moellendorff; WP-Friedrich_Wilhelm_Ritschl]
- "On Truth and Lies in a Nonmoral Sense" (1873, unpublished): truth "a mobile army of metaphors, metonyms, and anthropomorphisms" (SEP-LW's rendering); SEP-LW notes scholars disagree whether it is a keystone or a peripheral, non-representative fragment. [source: SEP-LW §2]
- ***Untimely Meditations*** (1873–76): four (of a projected thirteen) essays — *David Strauss, the Confessor and the Writer* (1873); *On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life* (1874); *Schopenhauer as Educator* (1874); *Richard Wagner in Bayreuth* (1876). Also translated *Unfashionable Observations*. [source: SEP-LW §2]
- ***Human, All Too Human*** (1878; supplements 1879, 1880; collected 1886 as *A Book for Free Spirits*): turn to aphorism and naturalistic, "historical philosophy"; original dedication to Voltaire (IEP). ***Daybreak*** (1881): "feeling of power"; Nietzsche later said it began his "crusade against morality" (EH). [source: SEP-LW §3; SEP-N §1; IEP §1]
- ***The Gay Science*** (*Die fröhliche Wissenschaft*, 1882; 2nd ed. 1887 with a new preface, Book V and an appendix of songs): title from Provençal troubadours; God is dead (§§108, 125, 343); amor fati (§276); eternal recurrence first presented (§341); §342 = opening of *Zarathustra*. [source: SEP-LW §3; SEP-N §§2, 3.2.2; WP-Thus_Spoke_Zarathustra]
- ***Thus Spoke Zarathustra: A Book for All and None*** (1883–85, four parts): introduces the *Übermensch* (substantially only in this work), the last man, will to power by name, eternal recurrence as Zarathustra's central teaching. Some 150,000 copies were printed by the German government and issued to soldiers in WWI (SEP-LW). [source: SEP-LW §§3–4; SEP-N §6.1; IEP §5]
- ***Beyond Good and Evil: Prelude to a Philosophy of the Future*** (1886): attacks dogmatic philosophers; "every great philosophy so far has been the personal confession of its author and a kind of involuntary and unconscious memoir" (BGE 6, Kaufmann via SEP-N); "Morality in Europe today is herd animal morality" (BGE 202, via SEP-MP); master and slave morality (BGE 260). [source: SEP-LW §4; SEP-N §6.2; SEP-MP §1]
- ***On the Genealogy of Morality: A Polemic*** (*Zur Genealogie der Moral: Eine Streitschrift*, 1887): preface and three treatises (see §7 below). [source: SEP-N §2; SEP-LW §4; WP-On_the_Genealogy_of_Morality]
- **1888:** *The Case of Wagner* (May–Aug); ***Twilight of the Idols, or How One Philosophizes with a Hammer*** (Aug–Sept; printed and released **January 1889** after the collapse, per WP) — the hammer is a diagnostic tuning-fork/percussion hammer for "sounding out" idols (SEP-LW); ***The Antichrist*** (Sept 1888; **published 1895**); ***Ecce Homo: How One Becomes What One Is*** (Oct–Nov 1888; **published 1908**), with chapters "Why I Am So Wise", "Why I Am So Clever", "Why I Write Such Good Books", "Why I Am a Destiny"; *Nietzsche contra Wagner* (Dec 1888). [source: SEP-LW §5; SEP-N §1 and bibliography; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]

## 6. The death of God
- "God is dead" appears at GS 108, 125 and 343 (SEP-N), and in *Zarathustra*. **Not mainly a claim that atheism is true**: in GS 125 the madman brings the news to a crowd of *atheists*; the point is that because "the belief in the Christian God has become unbelievable", everything "built upon this faith, propped up by it, grown into it", including "the whole of our European morality", is destined for "collapse" (GS 343, Kaufmann via SEP-N). The fitting response is "mourning and deep disorientation", not cheerful liberation. [source: SEP-N §2]
- GS 125 (the madman): runs into the marketplace with a lantern in the morning crying "I seek God!", is mocked, smashes his lantern, and concludes "I have come too early" — the event "is still on its way". [source: WP-God_is_dead]
- GS 108 ("New Struggles") opening: "After Buddha was dead, his shadow was still shown for centuries in a cave … God is dead; but given the way of men, there may still be caves for thousands of years in which his shadow will be shown." (Kaufmann tr.) [source: WP-God_is_dead]
- Precedents for the phrase: Lutheran hymn by Johann Rist ("Gott selbst liegt tot"), Hegel (*Phenomenology*; *Lectures on the Philosophy of Religion*), Jean Paul, Nerval, Heine, Mainländer. [source: WP-God_is_dead]
- Nihilism: Nachlass note (1887, WP §2 in the compilation): "What does nihilism mean? That the highest values devalue themselves. The aim is lacking; 'Why?' finds no answer." (quoted IEP §4; Kaufmann/Hollingdale tr.). SEP-N cites the same note as KSA 9[35] 12: 350. [source: IEP §4; SEP-N §3.2.2]
- Heidegger read "God is dead" as the end of Western metaphysics ("Nietzsche's Word: 'God is Dead'", 1943). [source: IEP §§4, 8]

## 7. The Genealogy and the critique of morality — ARGUMENT (step by step)
**Scope (Leiter, SEP-MP §1.1):** Nietzsche does not reject all "morality"; he attacks "morality in the pejorative sense" (MPS), defined by (a) three descriptive presuppositions — free will, transparency of the self, essential similarity of agents — and/or (b) norms that "harm the 'highest men' while benefiting the 'lowest'". What finally defines MPS is the normative agenda: "It is not error as error" he objects to (EH IV 7). The common targets are Christian and Kantian morality. [source: SEP-MP §1.1]

**First Treatise — "good and evil", "good and bad"; the slave revolt** (SEP-N §2):
1. There are two patterns of evaluation. **Good/bad** ("noble" or "master" morality, BGE 260): "good" first meant the socially high and then their characteristic excellences (courage, magnanimity, truthfulness); it is exclusive, arising from a "pathos of distance" (GM I 2); "bad" just means low, common. Dominant in Homeric, Greek and Roman culture.
2. **Good/evil**: the negative term "evil" is primary and condemns harm to others' welfare; "good" is derived from it as altruistic concern; the pattern is universal and egalitarian.
3. Today we mistake good/evil for "the moral manner of valuation as such" (GM Pref. 4) — but it is a *historical innovation*. So: how did it win?
4. Answer: a **"slave revolt in morality"** (GM I 10; BGE 260). The oppressed, powerless to strike back, developed ***ressentiment*** — a persistent, corrosive resentful hatred — and invented the concept *evil* to condemn their masters; "good" (humility, patience, harmlessness) came by negation. As the new values spread, even to the nobles, they achieved a "radical revaluation" (GM I 7), "the most spiritual" revenge.
5. Conclusion: our morality of altruism is, at root, "a vindictive effort to poison the happiness of the fortunate" (GM III 14 paraphrased by SEP-N), not dispassionate rational concern.
- Nietzsche's evidence (SEP-N): the "brimstone, hellfire, and damnation" genre in Christian writing (he quotes Tertullian, GM I 15); and how often moral indignation becomes free-floating vengefulness.
- Nietzsche locates the start of the revolt with the priestly people, the Jews (GM I 7), continued by Christianity; Roman good/bad vs Judaean good/evil; Renaissance, Reformation, French Revolution (WP summary). **Handle carefully**: SEP-N notes the *Genealogy* "freely deploys offensive anti-Semitic tropes (turned back, ironically, against anti-Semitic Christians themselves)". [source: WP-On_the_Genealogy_of_Morality; SEP-N §5]
- The "blond beast" appears in GM I (and II); WP notes Nietzsche's examples include Roman, Arabian, Germanic, Japanese nobilities (GM I 11). [source: SEP-LW §4; WP-On_the_Genealogy_of_Morality]
- "There is no 'being' behind doing … 'the doer' is merely a fiction added to the deed—the deed is everything" (GM I 13; WP's translation, translator not named — likely Kaufmann/Hollingdale; check before quoting). [source: WP-On_the_Genealogy_of_Morality]
- Scholarly controversy over exactly what the revolt was and how conscious/strategic: Bittner 1994, Reginster 1997/2021, Migotti 1998, Ridley 1998, Janaway 2007, Leiter 2015, etc. [source: SEP-N §2]

**Second Treatise — "guilt", "bad conscience"** (SEP-N §2; WP):
- German *Schuld* (guilt) is linked to *Schulden* (debts): guilt began in the creditor–debtor relation, where a debtor's failure entitled the creditor to compensation, including the pleasure of inflicting pain (GM II 5). The structure was transferred to wider norm-violations and then "purified and internalized", attaching a negative judgement to the guilty person's basic worth. [source: SEP-N §2; WP-On_the_Genealogy_of_Morality]
- Bad conscience: instincts that cannot discharge outward turn inward (WP summary; IEP: venom of slave morality "directed outwardly in ressentiment and inwardly in bad conscience").
- Christianity exploits the resulting need for redemption: God pays the debt by sacrificing his Son, so the sinner now owes God even more (GM II 21). [source: SEP-N §2]
- Bernard Williams (1993, "Nietzsche's Minimalist Moral Psychology"): purified guilt fits blame — "Blame needs an occasion—an action—and a target" (Williams 1993a: 10, quoted SEP-N). Reginster (2021) and others develop the self-punishment argument. [source: SEP-N §2]
- The "sovereign individual" with "the right to make promises" (GM II 2) as the "ripest fruit". [source: IEP §5; WP-On_the_Genealogy_of_Morality]

**Third Treatise — "What is the meaning of ascetic ideals?"** (SEP-N §§2, 5):
- Key aphorism (GM III 1, Clark/Swensen via SEP-N): the ascetic ideal's power expresses "the basic fact of the human will, its *horror vacui*: it needs a goal,—and it would rather will nothingness than not will."
- The ascetic priest reinterprets suffering as punishment: "every sufferer instinctively seeks a cause for his suffering … a guilty perpetrator"; the priest replies "you alone are to blame for yourself!" (GM III 15, via SEP-N). Asceticism is a self-defeating "self-medication" that makes the sick sicker (GM III 13, 16, 20–21). Targets include Schopenhauer's ethics.
- The treatise ends by turning on the **unconditional will to truth** itself as a form of asceticism (GM III 24–27): "the question of the value of truth". [source: SEP-N §§2, 3.2.3]
- Priests are "a group of weak people who shepherd even weaker people" (SEP-LW's paraphrase). [source: SEP-LW §4]

**Leiter's naturalist reconstruction of why MPS is bad** (SEP-MP §§1.2–1.3):
- Against free will: a free will would have to be *causa sui*, "the best self-contradiction that has been conceived so far" (BGE 21); and by the "Doctrine of Types" each person has a fixed psycho-physical constitution ("type-facts") that explains their beliefs and actions; conscious willing is epiphenomenal ("a thought comes when 'it' wishes, and not when 'I' wish", BGE 17).
- Against transparency: "all actions are essentially unknown" (D 116).
- Against similarity: people differ; Cornaro's diet example (TI VI 1) — one moral "diet" cannot be good for all.
- Core complaint: "the demand of one morality for all is detrimental to the higher men" (BGE 228); morality may be "the danger of dangers" if it prevents "the highest power and splendor possible to the type man" (GM Pref. 6). [source: SEP-MP]

**Main objection 1 — the genetic fallacy.** Showing where a belief or value came from does not show it is false or bad; to discredit a claim by its origin "when such an appeal to origins is irrelevant" is the genetic fallacy (IEP-F). So even if altruism grew from slave resentment, it might still be valuable. [source: IEP-F "Genetic"; WS-10 (Aeon essay: "showing that something has a bad origin isn't sufficient to show that it is bad")]
- **Replies:** (a) Nietzsche's critique does not rest on origins alone. Leiter: what he objects to is morality's present *effect* — that it harms higher types — and "it is not error as error" (SEP-MP §1.1, 1.3). (b) Anderson (SEP-N): Nietzsche's concern "is not so much to refute that view as to diagnose it"; the genealogy shows what psychological needs a value serves and whether it is self-defeating. (c) The preface says the aim is "a critique of moral values", "the value of these values themselves must be called into question" (GM Pref. 6, via WP) — origins are evidence about function, not a refutation. (d) A recent line (Queloz and Cueni, per WS-10 snippets only) even argues that Nietzsche criticises those who think bad origins debunk values. [source: SEP-MP; SEP-N §2; WP-On_the_Genealogy_of_Morality; WS-10]
**Main objection 2 — is it history or hypothesis?** Nietzsche himself calls Rée's accounts "hypotheses" and claims to offer real history instead (WP). Yet SEP-N calls the Second Treatise a "conjectural history" (GM II 21) and notes the slave-revolt story is "hard to accept" as history and even more as psychology of modern altruists. IEP: Nietzsche's picture of the Greeks is "partly derived from empirical evidence and partly confected in myth", and "the historical accuracy … is no more relevant to his philosophical schemata than … the actual signing of a material document is to a contractarian political theory". **Reply:** even as a hypothesis, it has explanatory power (Tertullian; vengeful indignation today) sufficient "to give pause to an open-minded reader" (SEP-N). [source: SEP-N §2; IEP §5; WP-On_the_Genealogy_of_Morality]
**Objection 3 — Nietzsche cannot condemn morality without a standard.** If values are "created" (GS 301) and he is an anti-realist, his own evaluations have no special authority. Leiter accepts this: Nietzsche "takes neither his positive vision, nor those aspects of his critique that depend upon it, to have any special epistemic status", which explains his rhetoric and "esoteric" moralizing; his argument for anti-realism is explanatory (moral facts are not needed in the best explanation of experience). Others read him as fictionalist (Hussain), skeptic (Berry), or constructivist/"subjective realist" (Richardson, Reginster, Anderson). [source: SEP-MP intro, §3.2; SEP-N §3.1]

## 8. Perspectivism and truth — ARGUMENT
- No single doctrine; contested what belongs under the heading. Positive and critical sides: past philosophers ignored their own perspectives (BGE 6); but multiplying perspectives aids knowledge. [source: SEP-N §6.2]
- **GM III 12** (Clark/Swensen via SEP-N): objectivity understood "not as 'disinterested contemplation' (which is a non-concept and absurdity), but rather as the capacity to have one's Pro and Contra in one's power, and to shift them in and out, so that one knows how to make precisely the difference in perspectives and affective interpretations useful for knowledge." [source: SEP-N §6.2] SEP-LW: GM III 12 is "one of Nietzsche's clearest expressions of 'perspectivism'" — no "God's eye" standpoint. [source: SEP-LW §4]
- The famous line in the same section — "There is only a perspective seeing, only a perspective 'knowing'" (wording found attributed to Kaufmann/Hollingdale) vs "There is *only* a perspectival seeing, *only* a perspectival 'knowing'" (other translations) — exact wording and translator **not confirmed** (WS-2). Quote the SEP passage above instead, or say "a perspectival seeing" without quotation marks.
- **"There are no facts, only interpretations"**: from the **Nachlass**, not a published book. SEP-LW: the 1880s notebooks "repeatedly state that 'there are no facts, only interpretations'". WS-1: the standard reference is notebook **late 1886–spring 1887, KSA 12, 7[60]** (also in the *Will to Power* compilation; section number **481** in Kaufmann's edition — not confirmed). [source: SEP-LW §6; WS-1] **Must be presented as an unpublished note.**
- BGE 22: Nietzsche says even physics is "interpretation", and anticipates the reply that this is "only interpretation" too — "so much the better" (IEP §3). [source: IEP §3]
- Sources of the idea: neo-Kantians F. A. Lange, Afrikan Spir; Ernst Mach; term "perspective" developed in an idealist version by Gustav Teichmüller (1882). Perspectives are rooted in affects and valuations, linking perspectivism to genealogy. [source: SEP-N §6.2]
**Reconstruction:** (1) All cognition is from some perspective shaped by sensory capacities, concepts, drives and affects. (2) There is no "view from nowhere" that removes all of these. (3) So "objectivity" cannot mean disinterested contemplation; it is the ability to use many perspectives. (4) Stronger versions: there are no facts independent of interpretation; truth is impossible.
**Objection — self-refutation:** if "there are no facts, only interpretations" or "there is no truth" is *true*, it refutes itself; and Nietzsche constantly asserts his own views as true and prizes honesty. SEP-LW notes he claims, "presumably as a truth", that truth is a mobile army of metaphors. [source: SEP-N §6.2; SEP-LW §2]
**Replies (SEP-N §6.2):** (a) some of his claims fall outside the scope of his denials of truth (Hales and Welshon 2000); (b) he distinguishes senses of "truth" — rejecting metaphysical truth while keeping ordinary truth (Schacht 1983; Anderson 1998, 2005); (c) **Clark (1990, 1998): developmental reading — Nietzsche gave up his "falsification thesis" late in his career**, so the mature perspectivism rejects only the "God's-eye" metaphysical notion of truth, not truth as such (see Clark–Nehamas exchanges 2015–18); (d) Gemes: perspectivism is not epistemology at all but psychology of drives; (e) Nietzsche himself (BGE 22) embraces the reflexive point. IEP adds that Clark's coherent theory of truth requires emphasizing some passages over others. [source: SEP-N §6.2; IEP §4] (Leiter is a naturalist reader allied with Clark — co-authored the 1997 Cambridge *Daybreak* introduction — but no specific Leiter position on perspectivism was verified here.)
- **Value of truth:** honesty is "our virtue, the last one left to us" (BGE 227); "How much truth does a spirit endure, how much truth does it dare? More and more that became for me the real measure of value" (EH Pref. 3, Kaufmann via SEP-N); "To what extent can truth endure incorporation? That is the question; that is the experiment" (GS 110). [source: SEP-N §3.2.3]

## 9. Will to power
- First explicit appearance: *Zarathustra* (1883–85); seeds in *Daybreak*'s "feeling of power" and GS 13. Zarathustra, "On Self-Overcoming": "Where I found a living creature, there I found will to power; and even in the will of the servant, I found the will to be master" (IEP's quotation, translation probably Kaufmann). [source: SEP-LW §3; IEP §6]
- A 2 (Kaufmann via SEP-N): "What is good? Everything that heightens the feeling of power in man, the will to power, power itself. What is bad? Everything that is born of weakness. What is happiness? The feeling that power is growing, that resistance is overcome." [source: SEP-N §3.2.1]
- Psychology "as the morphology and development-theory of the will to power" (BGE 23). [source: SEP-N §6.1]
- **Readings:** foundational metaphysics/cosmology (Heidegger, Jaspers, Richardson 1996); anti-essentialist (Nehamas, Poellner); psychological hypothesis (Kaufmann 1950, Soll, Clark & Dudrick); quasi-scientific conjecture (Schacht, Abel, Anderson); **Clark (1990, 2000): no claim about the world at all but an expression of Nietzsche's values**; Reginster (2006): a drive to **overcome resistance**, aimed against Schopenhauer's will to live; Richardson (2020): striving for growth in control. [source: SEP-N §6.1]
- **Published vs Nachlass:** the most cosmological statements (e.g. WP 1067, "This world is the will to power—and nothing besides!" per IEP context) are in the notebooks; SEP-LW: in the 1880s notebooks he "sometimes adopts a more metaphysical orientation towards the doctrines of Eternal Recurrence and the Will to Power". Four views on how much weight the Nachlass deserves: published works take precedence (classical); unpublished = more authentic (Heidegger); case-by-case; deconstructive (no rigid priority). [source: SEP-LW §6; IEP §6]
- Kaufmann's post-war campaign stressed self-overcoming and cultural excellence rather than domination of others; Anderson: right to add nuance but "threatens to sanitize" a view meant to challenge moral intuitions. [source: SEP-N §3.2.1]

## 10. Eternal recurrence
- Origin story: August 1881 near a "pyramidal block of stone" by **Lake Silvaplana** near Sils-Maria; first note headed "6000 feet beyond man and time" (EH; WP has the manuscript note "beginning of August 1881 in Sils-Maria, 6000 ft above sea level and much higher above all human regards"). [source: IEP §7; WP-Thus_Spoke_Zarathustra]
- **GS 341, "The greatest weight"** (Kaufmann tr., as quoted by IEP): "What, if some day or night a demon were to steal after you into your loneliest loneliness and say to you: 'This life as you now live it and have lived it, you will have to live once more and innumerable times more; and there will be nothing new in it, but every pain and every joy and every thought and sigh and everything unutterably small or great in your life will have to return to you, all in the same succession and sequence—even this spider and this moonlight between the trees, and even this moment and I myself. The eternal hourglass of existence is turned upside down again and again, and you with it, speck of dust!'" … "how well disposed would you have to become to yourself and to life to crave nothing more fervently than this ultimate eternal confirmation and seal?" [source: IEP §7]
- Nietzsche called it the "fundamental conception" of *Zarathustra* (EH III, Z 1). Published presentations are hypothetical (GS 341), elliptical, or metaphorical (Z III "On the Vision and the Riddle", "The Convalescent"). [source: SEP-N §6.3; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- **Cosmological reading** (early reception: Simmel, Löwith, Jaspers, Heidegger; recently Paul Loeb) vs **practical/ethical thought-experiment reading** (Magnus 1978; **Nehamas 1980, 1985**; **Clark 1990**; Reginster 2006; Anderson). Clark's analogy: like a spouse asking "would you marry me again?", the question demands an assessment of one's life. Objections to the cosmology: Simmel's counter-argument (1907); Soll 1973. Notebook attempts at proofs exist; IEP says they "present no serious challenge to a linear conception of time". Kaufmann thought the doctrine unworthy of serious reflection (IEP). [source: SEP-N §6.3; IEP §7]
- Anderson's account of the three features: recurrence of the *past* defeats future-bias; *sameness in every detail* blocks selective self-assessment; *eternity* rules out the Christian move of affirming earthly life only as a test redeemed by heaven (Reginster: eternity calls for joy). [source: SEP-N §6.3]
- Nehamas's three formulations (Life as Literature, 1985, p. 153 per WP): "My life will recur" (fatalistic); "may recur"; "If my life were to recur, then it could recur only in identical fashion." [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- **Amor fati** (GS 276, Kaufmann via SEP-N): "I want to learn more and more to see as beautiful what is necessary in things; then I shall be one of those who make things beautiful. *Amor fati*: let that be my love henceforth! … someday I wish to be only a Yes-sayer." Reginster: "Nietzsche regards the affirmation of life as his defining philosophical achievement." [source: SEP-N §3.2.2]

## 11. Übermensch and the last man
- *Übermensch* appears substantially only in *Zarathustra* and related texts; announced with fanfare early in the narrative then fades; debated whether central to his thought, whether a transition or a state, individual or community. [source: IEP §5; SEP-LW §4]
- Translation: Alexander Tille (1896) "Beyond-Man"; Thomas Common (1909) "Superman" (after Shaw's *Man and Superman*, 1903); Kaufmann preferred "overman", blaming "Superman" for confusion with the comic-book hero. *Mensch* = human being, not male. [source: WP-Übermensch]
- The **last man** (*der letzte Mensch*), in Zarathustra's Prologue: comfort-seeking, risk-averse, egalitarian; the crowd, offered him as a deterrent, cheer for him instead. IEP: the last man "blinks". [source: WP-Last_man; IEP §5]
- Nietzsche's other exemplars: free spirit, philosopher of the future, Goethe, Napoleon. [source: IEP §5; SEP-LW §5]

## 12. Style and aphorism
- Short numbered sections, often loosely connected; "I approach deep problems like cold baths: quickly into them and quickly out again" and "One does not only wish to be understood when one writes; one wishes just as surely not to be understood" (GS 381, Kaufmann via SEP-N). Lineage: Lichtenberg, Schopenhauer's *Parerga*, French moralistes (La Rochefoucauld). "I have many stylistic possibilities—the most multifarious art of style that has ever been at the disposal of one man" (EH III 4). Nehamas (1985) on variety of styles; Clark & Dudrick (2012) needed a book for 23 sections of BGE. [source: SEP-N §5]
- "Twilight" maxim: "I mistrust all systematizers" (TI "Maxims and Arrows" 26, cited by IEP); Kaufmann: he "had strong philosophic reasons for not having a system" (1950, quoted WP-Walter_Kaufmann). [source: IEP §3; WP-Walter_Kaufmann_(philosopher)]
- Anderson's hypothesis: the *Genealogy*'s vitriol is "audience partition" — designed to offend (and so protect) readers who cannot do without Christianity. [source: SEP-N §5]

## 13. Elisabeth, the Nachlass and *The Will to Power*
- **Bernhard Förster** (1843–1889): schoolteacher and leading antisemitic agitator (called Jews a "parasite on the German body"; co-founded the Deutscher Volksverein 1881). Married Elisabeth **22 May 1885**; Nietzsche did not attend. [source: WP-Bernhard_Förster; WP-Elisabeth_Förster-Nietzsche] (WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche says "In 1886 … married" — **conflict; use 1885.**)
- **Nueva Germania**, Paraguay: colonists left Hamburg **15 February 1887** (14 families); intended as an Aryan, antisemitic (and vegetarian) colony; founded 1887 (WP-Nueva_Germania gives 23 Aug 1887 in infobox but "1886" in text; SEP-LW "since 1886"). The colony failed; Förster killed himself with morphine and strychnine, San Bernardino, **3 June 1889**. Elisabeth returned to Germany in **1893**. The settlement still exists as a district of San Pedro department. [source: WP-Bernhard_Förster; WP-Elisabeth_Förster-Nietzsche; WP-Nueva_Germania; SEP-LW §1; IEP §8]
- Elisabeth took the name "Förster-Nietzsche"; founded the **Nietzsche Archive in 1894** (Naumburg), moved to Villa Silberblick, Weimar, after 1897; seized control of his literary remains; Overbeck was pushed out and Gast co-operated. She hired Rudolf Steiner to tutor her in her brother's philosophy; he gave up after a few months. Nominated four times for the Nobel Prize in Literature (1908–1923), always dismissed. [source: IEP §8; WP-Nietzsche_Archive; WP-Elisabeth_Förster-Nietzsche; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- ***The Will to Power***: a compilation from the notebooks by Elisabeth and **Peter Gast** (Köselitz), using a title Nietzsche had announced (GM III 27) for a work he later abandoned (a new plan titled *Revaluation of All Values* replaced it in late August 1888; drafts went into *Twilight* and *The Antichrist*). **First edition 1901 (483 sections); expanded 1906 (1067 sections).** First English tr. Anthony Ludovici, 1910; Kaufmann & Hollingdale tr. 1967. [source: WP-The_Will_to_Power_(manuscript); SEP-N §1 & bibliography; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- Assessments: "not well founded in Nietzsche's surviving plans" and "marred by Elisabeth's strong anti-Semitic commitments, which had been extremely distressing to Nietzsche himself"; leaves "a somewhat misleading impression" of the notebooks (SEP-N). **Montinari** called it a forgery ("*La 'Volontà di potenza' non esiste*" / "'The Will to Power' Does Not Exist"); fragments were cut and stitched. Elisabeth also suppressed passages (e.g. *Antichrist* §35 per WP). [source: SEP-N §1; WP-The_Will_to_Power_(manuscript); WP-Mazzino_Montinari; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- **Colli–Montinari critical edition:** Giorgio Colli (1917–1979) and his former pupil Mazzino Montinari (1928–1986) began, around the end of the 1950s, an Italian translation, then a new critical edition from the Weimar manuscripts, in chronological order including all notebooks: *Werke: Kritische Gesamtausgabe* (KGA, de Gruyter, Berlin, 1967 ff.) and the student *Kritische Studienausgabe* (KSA, 1980); also Adelphi (Italian) and Gallimard (French). Montinari co-founded *Nietzsche-Studien* (1972). [source: SEP-N bibliography; WP-Mazzino_Montinari; WP-Giorgio_Colli; WP-The_Will_to_Power_(manuscript)] (NB SEP-N misprints "Massimo Montinari"; the name is **Mazzino**. WP-The_Will_to_Power says the archives were in "Leipzig" — WP-Montinari and WP-Nietzsche_Archive say **Weimar**; use Weimar.)
- **DEBATE on Elisabeth's guilt:** the 1950s account (Kaufmann and others) — that she edited his work to fit racist, nationalist, Nazi themes — "has been the subject of debate in recent scholarship" (WP); one view says her selective editing aimed mainly to protect her brother and present herself as close to him; another places the distortion with the Nazis themselves. She joined the German National People's Party (1918), never the Nazi Party (WP). IEP: "editing his works … occasionally with an ominous political intent that (later researchers agree) corrupted the original thought." [source: WP-Elisabeth_Förster-Nietzsche; IEP §§1, 8] Biographies: Carol Diethe, *Nietzsche's Sister and the Will to Power* (2003); Ben Macintyre, *Forgotten Fatherland* (1992) (listed as WP sources; not checked further).

## 14. Nietzsche against antisemitism (verify carefully)
- IEP: Förster led the antisemitic movement "which Friedrich often ridiculed and unambiguously condemned, both in his published works and in private correspondences"; Yirmiyahu Yovel (1998) shows Nietzsche developed an attitude of **"anti-anti-Semitism"** after overcoming the prejudices of his youth. [source: IEP §8]
- SEP-LW: in most of his 1888 works he criticizes the anti-Semitic writers of his day (e.g. Renan in *The Antichrist*). [source: SEP-LW §5]
- Verified episodes: the 1886 break with publisher Schmeitzner (§3 above); the 1889 madness note "all anti-Semites abolished" (§4); **letter(s) to Theodor Fritsch, 1887**: Fritsch, antisemitic publisher, sent him issues of his *Antisemitic Correspondence*; Nietzsche thanked him for a glance at "the muddle of principles that lie at the heart of this strange movement" and asked not to be sent such writings again, lest he lose patience (WP-Theodor_Fritsch, citing a now-dead web page). Bataille (1937) quotes a scornful 1887 Fritsch letter mentioning Zarathustra's name in antisemites' mouths (WS-5). Exact dates (one is 29 March 1887 in some sources; Bataille also cites 21 May 1887) **not confirmed**. [source: WP-Theodor_Fritsch; WS-5]
- But SEP-N also notes that the *Genealogy* "freely deploys offensive anti-Semitic tropes (turned back, ironically, against anti-Semitic Christians)". The chapter should not present him as simply philosemitic. [source: SEP-N §5]

## 15. Nazi appropriation
- In the 1930s "aspects of Nietzsche's thought were espoused by the Nazis and Italian Fascists, partly due to the encouragement of Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche through her associations with Adolf Hitler and Benito Mussolini"; Nazi readers assembled passages "quite selectively". [source: SEP-LW §7]
- **Alfred Baeumler** (1887–1968): *Nietzsche, der Philosoph und Politiker* (1931, before the Nazis took power); edited Nietzsche editions in the Third Reich; Nazi ideologue, 1933 chair of philosophy and political pedagogy in Berlin; gave the inaugural lecture on the night of the 10 May 1933 book burning. [source: WP-Alfred_Baeumler; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche; IEP §8 ("National Socialist academic propagandists such as Alfred Bäumler")]
- **Hitler and the Archive:** WP: Hitler "was a frequent visitor to the Nietzsche museum in Weimar"; whether he read Nietzsche is debated. IEP: Hitler visited ceremoniously in the early 1930s and was entertained by Elisabeth with a reading from her late husband Bernhard's work. WS-9 (Klassik Stiftung Weimar materials, second-hand): she first met Hitler on 30 January 1932 at the Weimar premiere of a play co-written by Mussolini; visits 1932–1934; she gave him her brother's sword-cane (1933); he pledged money for a Nietzsche Memorial Hall (1934). **Hitler attended her funeral** (she died 8 November 1935). [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche; WP-Elisabeth_Förster-Nietzsche; IEP §8; WS-9] (Exact visit dates conflict among sources — say "several visits in 1932–34".)
- Counter-efforts in the 1930s: Karl Jaspers and Karl Löwith; Georges Bataille, "Nietzsche and the Fascists" (1937). IEP: "The stronger claim that Nietzsche's thought leads to National Socialism is even more problematic," and some Fascists themselves doubted the fit. Russell warned his aristocratic philosophy could only be realized by something like a Fascist or Nazi party (WP). Heidegger lectured on Nietzsche in Nazi Germany 1936–1940 (SEP-LW). [source: IEP §8; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche; SEP-LW §6]
- Earlier politics of reception: WWI soldiers given *Zarathustra*; Dreyfusards labelled "Nietzscheans" by the French antisemitic right; appeal to Zionists (Ahad Ha'am, Berdyczewski, Buber); Nietzschean Bolsheviks (Lunacharsky, Bogdanov); anarchists. [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]

## 16. Interpretive traditions
- **Brandes** (1888): "aristocratic radicalism", a label Nietzsche approved. [source: SEP-MP §4]
- **Heidegger**: lectures 1936–40, *Nietzsche* (2 vols, 1961); Nietzsche as "the last metaphysician", "the consummation of metaphysics"; will to power and eternal recurrence as his metaphysics; gave priority to the Nachlass. Challenged by Eugen Fink (1960) and others. [source: IEP §8; SEP-LW §6; SEP-N bibliography]
- **Kaufmann's rehabilitation**: *Nietzsche: Philosopher, Psychologist, Antichrist* (Princeton, **1950**); with Danto's *Nietzsche as Philosopher* (1965) opened serious English-language discussion after the Nazi association; Kaufmann (1921–1980, German-born, emigrated 1939, Princeton) translated most of the works. [source: SEP-LW §7; WP-Walter_Kaufmann_(philosopher); IEP bibliography]
- **French Nietzsche**: influential in French philosophy 1960s–80s (death of God, perspectivism, power); **Deleuze**, *Nietzsche et la philosophie* (1962) — Nietzsche as thinker of active and reactive forces, anti-Hegelian genealogist; **Foucault**, "Nietzsche, la généalogie, l'histoire" (1971) — genealogy rejects origins and teleology, uncovering "the play of dominations"; Derrida, *Spurs: Nietzsche's Styles* (1978/79); Sarah Kofman, *Nietzsche and Metaphor* (1972). [source: SEP-LW §7; IEP bibliography; SEP-N bibliography] Foucault borrowed "genealogy" from Nietzsche (consistent with the published Intellectual History chapter).
- **Analytic/naturalist**: Clark (1990), Leiter (*Nietzsche on Morality*, 2002; 2nd ed. 2015), Schacht, Reginster, Richardson, Katsafanas; IEP calls Schacht, Clark, Conway and Leiter a "backlash" against deconstructive readings. [source: IEP §8; SEP-N; SEP-MP]
- **Politics debate**: "Aristocratic Politics View" (Detwiler 1990, *Nietzsche and the Politics of Aristocratic Radicalism*) vs "Anti-Politics View" (Hunt 1985; Leiter: "no grounds for ascribing to him a political philosophy"); key texts A 56–57 (Laws of Manu) and BGE 257; Brobjer (1998) argues the Manu passages are not an endorsement; Nussbaum (1997) decries his "baneful influence". [source: SEP-MP §4]
- Ricoeur: with Marx and Freud a master of the "school of suspicion" (consistent with the published *Marx* chapter). [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]

## 17. Nietzsche on women
- Notorious remarks (e.g. BGE 231–239; in Z I "On Little Old and Young Women" the line about the whip — **wording not verified here**). In BGE 231 he prefaces his "truths about 'woman as such'" by saying they are "only—*my* truths" (WP's quotation, Kaufmann tr. presumed). [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche's_views_on_women]
- Feminist readings divide: Oliver & Pearsall (eds., *Feminist Interpretations of Friedrich Nietzsche*, 1998): his challenges to mind/body, reason/unreason hierarchies have been useful to feminist theory, but his remarks "confound attempts simply to proclaim Nietzsche a champion of feminism"; Kelly Oliver, *Womanizing Nietzsche* (1995); Derrida's *Spurs* on "woman" as figure for truth; Frances Nesbitt Oppel reads the misogyny as a rhetorical strategy exposing "woman-as-such" as a male construct; Cornelia Klinger: "like Schopenhauer a prominent hater of women", but he "at least relativizes" his statements. Contemporary Ida von Miaskowski recalled him as "sensitive, so natural and comradely" with women. [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche's_views_on_women; SEP-N bibliography; IEP bibliography]
- Women in his story: Lou Salomé, Malwida von Meysenbug (patron), Meta von Salis, Helene von Druskowitz (received a copy of Z IV), Cosima Wagner, Elisabeth. [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]

## 18. Reception beyond Europe
- **Muhammad Iqbal** (1877–1938): studied in Cambridge, Heidelberg and Munich (PhD 4 Nov 1907); read Nietzsche in Germany; Nietzsche's influence "most evidenced" in the Persian poem ***Asrar-i Khudi*** (*The Secrets of the Self*, **1915**; English tr. R. A. Nicholson, 1920, who said Iqbal's philosophy "owes much to Nietzsche and Bergson"). Iqbal himself insisted his doctrine of the Perfect Man (*insan-i kamil*) predated his reading of Nietzsche and that *Asrar* and Nietzsche "are diametrically opposed" (WS-6, via *Iqbal Review*). [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche; WP-Muhammad_Iqbal; WP-The_Secrets_of_the_Self; WS-6] (A famous Iqbal line on Nietzsche from *Javid Nama* — "his heart is a believer but his mind an infidel" — **not verified**.)
- **Lu Xun** (1881–1936): began reading Nietzsche in Tokyo after March 1906 and wrote essays influenced by him; "Diary of a Madman" (May 1918, *New Youth*) — whose form came from Gogol; he translated (part of) the Prologue of *Zarathustra* (date 1918/1920 unconfirmed); scholars (Arena) describe an ambivalent attraction and repulsion. [source: WP-Lu_Xun; WS-8]
- **Japan**: early "Nietzsche boom" around 1901, centred on **Takayama Chogyū**, who wrote articles praising Nietzsche (1900–01, e.g. "On the Aesthetic Life", 1901); **Watsuji Tetsurō**'s *Nīchie kenkyū* (*Study of Nietzsche*, 1913); **Nishitani Keiji**, *The Self-Overcoming of Nihilism* (*Nihirizumu*, 1949) — Nietzsche read through Buddhism; Graham Parkes (ed.), *Nietzsche and Asian Thought* (1991). [source: WP-Takayama_Chogyū; WP-Keiji_Nishitani; WS-7; SEP-N/SEP-MP bibliography (Parkes 1991)]
- Also: Russia (more Nietzsche publications in Russia 1888–1890s than any other country, per Prideaux via WP); Zionist thinkers; Yukio Mishima. [source: WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]
- Nietzsche on Indian texts: praised the *Laws of Manu* over the Bible in *The Antichrist* (A 56–57) — to attack Christianity; called Schopenhauer's pessimism a kind of European Buddhism; Nishitani and Parkes align him with Mahāyāna thought, Milne disagrees. [source: SEP-MP §4; WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche]

## Debates
- **How to read the Genealogy**: debunking critique (genetic-fallacy worry) vs diagnosis of function (Anderson, SEP-N) vs ideology critique freeing higher types (Leiter, SEP-MP) vs internal critique (Geuss, per WS-10) vs critique of debunking itself (Queloz & Cueni, WS-10 only). History or conjecture: SEP-N "conjectural history"; IEP "partly … confected in myth". [sources above]
- **Metaethics**: anti-realism (Leiter), skepticism (Berry), fictionalism (Hussain), constructivism/subjective realism (Richardson, Reginster, Anderson). [source: SEP-N §3.1; SEP-MP §3]
- **Will to power**: metaphysics (Heidegger, Richardson) vs psychology (Kaufmann) vs value expression (Clark) vs overcoming resistance (Reginster). [source: SEP-N §6.1]
- **Eternal recurrence**: cosmological (Löwith, Heidegger, Loeb) vs practical test (Nehamas, Clark, Reginster, Anderson). [source: SEP-N §6.3]
- **Perspectivism & truth**: self-refutation; replies Hales & Welshon, Schacht/Anderson, Clark developmental, Gemes psychological. [source: SEP-N §6.2]
- **Published works vs Nachlass**: four positions (SEP-LW §6); Heidegger favoured the Nachlass.
- **Politics**: aristocratic politics (Detwiler) vs no political philosophy (Hunt, Leiter); Nussbaum's critique. [source: SEP-MP §4]
- **Elisabeth's responsibility**: traditional (Kaufmann) vs revisionist (WP). [source: WP-Elisabeth_Förster-Nietzsche; IEP §8]
- **Schopenhauer's lasting influence**: "a matter of scholarly debate" (SEP-LW); Reginster reads will to power as a reply to Schopenhauer's will to live. [source: SEP-LW §1; SEP-N §6.1]
- **Biography and philosophy**: Heidegger and Kaufmann deny relevance; Jaspers, the French readers and Nehamas affirm it (IEP §8).
- **Illness**: syphilis vs meningioma vs CADASIL etc. (§4).

## Consistency with published Periergia texts
- *History of Philosophy* overview: "'God is dead,' the collapse of the entire Platonic-Christian framework of transcendent value … whether values can be *created* rather than discovered (genealogy of morals, will to power, eternal recurrence as a test of affirmation), all in a style calculated to resist system." **Consistent** with SEP-N (GS 343; GS 301 on value creation; practical reading of recurrence). Note the overview takes the "test of affirmation" reading of recurrence; the chapter should present it as the majority but contested reading (Loeb dissents).
- *Schopenhauer and Kierkegaard* (drafted on this branch): "Schopenhauer awakened Nietzsche" — **consistent** (1865 bookshop discovery; *Schopenhauer as Educator*). Its research notes say Brandes lectured on Kierkegaard in the 1870s — **consistent** with WP here (Brandes urged Nietzsche to read Kierkegaard).
- *Marx*: Ricoeur grouped Marx, Nietzsche and Freud as "masters of suspicion" — **consistent** (WP-Friedrich_Nietzsche).
- *German Idealism*: says Schelling's critique of Hegel "fed … Nietzsche (see *Nietzsche*)" — **not confirmed** by any source read for this chapter (none mentions Schelling as an influence on Nietzsche). Not a contradiction, but the Nietzsche chapter should not repeat it without a source.
- *The Presocratics*: Nietzsche "returned to them" — **consistent** (Basel lectures on the pre-Platonic philosophers; *Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks*, IEP §§1, 5; WP).
- *Intellectual History* (History subject): Foucault borrowed Nietzsche's term "genealogy" — **consistent** (IEP bibliography on Foucault 1971).
- *Utilitarianism*: cross-reference only; Nietzsche called Mill a "blockhead" (WP, via Russell) and attacks utilitarian hedonism (SEP-LW §3) — consistent with any contrast drawn.
