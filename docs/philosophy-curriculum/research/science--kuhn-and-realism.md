## 1. Kuhn: life and the road to *Structure*
- Thomas Samuel Kuhn was born in Cincinnati, Ohio, on 18 July 1922 and died on 17 June 1996 in Cambridge, Massachusetts, after two years of cancer of the throat and bronchial tubes. [source: IEP-KUHN; WP-Thomas_Kuhn]
- Graduated from Harvard summa cum laude in 1943; did radar research during the war at Harvard and then in Europe; master's in physics 1946, doctorate in physics 1949 (on an application of quantum mechanics to solid-state physics). [source: SEP-KUHN]
- Until 1956 he taught a science course for humanities undergraduates in James B. Conant's General Education in Science curriculum, built around historical case studies. [source: SEP-KUHN]
- The formative experience: reading Aristotle's physics, first bewilderment, then a "more or less sudden" ability to understand Aristotle undistorted by later science. IEP dates this to a summer day in 1947, when he realised he had been reading Aristotle's idea of motion through Newtonian categories. [source: SEP-KUHN; IEP-KUHN]
- First book: *The Copernican Revolution* (1957). In 1956 Harvard denied him tenure, the committee feeling that book was too popular in approach. [source: SEP-KUHN; IEP-KUHN]
- Moved to Berkeley in 1956 (history of science, in the philosophy department); full professor 1961. Colleagues included Stanley Cavell, who introduced him to Wittgenstein, and Paul Feyerabend, with whom he discussed a draft of *Structure*. [source: SEP-KUHN]
- *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* was published in 1962 in the series "International Encyclopedia of Unified Science", edited by Otto Neurath and Rudolf Carnap, and as a book by the University of Chicago Press. [source: SEP-KUHN; WP-The_Structure_of_Scientific_Revolutions]
- Opening sentence: "History, if viewed as a repository for more than anecdote or chronology, could produce a decisive transformation in the image of science by which we are now possessed." (1962/1970, 1) [source: SEP-KUHN]
- Kuhn called his work "history for philosophical purposes" (Kuhn 2000, 276). [source: SEP-KUHN]
- 1964 to Princeton (M. Taylor Pyne Professor of Philosophy and History of Science); 1983 Laurence S. Rockefeller Professor of Philosophy at MIT; retired from teaching 1991. President of the History of Science Society 1968–70; its Sarton Medal 1982. [source: SEP-KUHN; IEP-KUHN]
- Later books: *The Essential Tension* (1977, essays); *Black-Body Theory and the Quantum Discontinuity* (1978); *The Road Since Structure* (2000, ed. James Conant and John Haugeland); *The Last Writings of Thomas S. Kuhn* (2022, ed. Bojana Mladenović), which contains the unfinished book *The Plurality of Worlds: An Evolutionary Theory of Scientific Change* and the 1987 Shearman Memorial Lectures at University College London. [source: SEP-KUHN]
- Reception: *Structure* first interested social scientists. Wikipedia reports over one million copies sold and translations into sixteen languages (figures from Horgan 1991), and that in 1987 it was reported to be the twentieth-century book most cited in the arts and humanities in 1976–1983 (Garfield 1987). These are old figures; give them as "by 1991". A 50th-anniversary edition with an introductory essay by Ian Hacking appeared in April 2012. [source: SEP-KUHN; WP-The_Structure_of_Scientific_Revolutions]
- "Paradigm" and "paradigm shift" later became business buzzwords, "sometimes viewed as effectively devoid of content" (Wikipedia, citing Fulford 1999 and McFedries 2001). [source: WP-The_Structure_of_Scientific_Revolutions; WP-Paradigm_shift]

## 2. Kuhn's picture of science (*Structure*, 1962/1970)
- A mature science alternates between "normal" and "revolutionary" (or "extraordinary") phases, which differ in kind. Before the first paradigm comes a "pre-paradigm" period of competing schools with differing procedures, theories and even metaphysics; one school's impressive achievement draws adherents away from the others. [source: SEP-KUHN; WP-The_Structure_of_Scientific_Revolutions]
- **Normal science** is "puzzle-solving" (1962/1970, 35–42), like a crossword or chess problem: the solver expects a solution, success depends on her skill, and methods are familiar. During normal science scientists neither test nor try to confirm the guiding theories; anomalies are ignored or explained away. [source: SEP-KUHN]
- **Anomaly → crisis**: only an accumulation of troublesome anomalies, especially ones that undermine normal practice (e.g. doubts about a widely used instrument), produces a widespread loss of confidence, which Kuhn calls a "crisis" (1962/1970, 66–76). [source: SEP-KUHN]
- **Revolution**: replacement of the disciplinary matrix. No rules decide the significance of anomalies, so the choice is "not rationally compelled", leaving room for rational disagreement. Kuhn briefly mentioned extra-scientific factors (nationalities, personalities; 1962/1970, 152–3) but repudiated the idea that outcomes are fixed by socio-political factors. [source: SEP-KUHN]
- Persuasion across a revolution: older practitioners may never convert; younger ones often accept the new paradigm "through something like a religious conversion", with faith in its future puzzle-solving; Kuhn did not mean argument is unimportant, only insufficient. [source: IEP-KUHN]
- **"Kuhn-loss"**: a revolution may leave the new science without an explanation the old one had (1962/1970, 99–100). [source: SEP-KUHN]
- **Progress without truth**: revolutions increase puzzle-solving power (the new theory must keep pretty well all the predecessor's quantitative problem-solving power, 1970b, 20), but Kuhn denied any inference to nearness to truth (1962/1970, 170–1) and in 1970 denied that "nearness to the truth" makes sense (1970a, 206). Progress is evolutionary, not teleological: like Darwinian evolution, science moves away from problems rather than toward a fixed goal. [source: SEP-KUHN; SEP-INC]
- Textbooks make revolutions "invisible" by rewriting history so the current paradigm seems born mature. [source: IEP-KUHN]
- Kuhn's examples of paradigms: Aristotle's analysis of motion, Ptolemy's planetary computations, Lavoisier's use of the balance, Maxwell's mathematization of the electromagnetic field; and as texts, Ptolemy's *Almagest*, Lavoisier's *Traité élémentaire de chimie*, Newton's *Principia* and *Opticks* (1962/1970, 12, 23). [source: SEP-KUHN]
- Newtonian vs Einsteinian mass: "Newtonian mass is conserved; Einsteinian is convertible with energy" (1962/1970, 102); this is Kuhn's example against the view that Newton's theory is simply a special case of Einstein's. [source: SEP-KUHN]
- The pairs Kuhn treated as incommensurable include Newton vs his Cartesian and Aristotelian predecessors, and Lavoisier vs Priestley in chemistry (1962, 147–150). [source: SEP-INC]
- Paradigm as **exemplar** has three functions: it suggests new puzzles, suggests approaches to solving them, and is the standard for judging proposed solutions (1962/1970, 38–9). Similarity to exemplars cannot be reduced to rules; SEP (Bird) argues the charge of irrationalism is "wide of the mark", since recognising family resemblance is not rule-governed either but not irrational. [source: SEP-KUHN]
- Kuhn rejected the distinction between the context of discovery and the context of justification (1962/1970, 8). [source: SEP-KUHN]
- **Gestalt switch**: Kuhn compared changes in the scientist's perceptual world to seeing the duck-rabbit figure first as a duck and then as a rabbit, while admitting he was unsure whether this was only an analogy. He built on N. R. Hanson (1958) and Bruner and Postman's (1949) psychology experiments. IEP notes the analogy's limit: the gestalt viewer can stand outside and know it is the same drawing; the scientific community has "no answer sheet". [source: SEP-KUHN; IEP-KUHN]
- "Different worlds": "In a sense I am unable to explicate further, the proponents of competing paradigms practice their trades in different worlds… Practicing in different worlds, the two groups of scientists see different things when they look from the same point in the same direction" (1962/1970, 150). Kuhn denied any constructivist meaning; Paul Hoyningen-Huene later gave a neo-Kantian reading (a fixed world-in-itself, a changing phenomenal world). [source: SEP-KUHN]

## 3. Incommensurability
- "Incommensurable" means "having no common measure". The term goes back to Euclid (the side and diagonal of a square). Its application to successive theories was popularised in 1962 by both Kuhn and Feyerabend (Feyerabend's paper "Explanation, Reduction and Empiricism", 1962). The two were later labelled "the worst enemies of science" (Theocharis and Psimopoulos 1987). [source: SEP-INC; SEP-FEY]
- SEP-KUHN distinguishes (a) **methodological** incommensurability (different standards and different problems to be solved), (b) **observational** incommensurability (theory-dependence of observation), (c) **semantic** incommensurability: early (meaning change of terms like "mass"), later **taxonomic/lexical** (from the 1980s: a field's taxonomy of kinds, organised by a "no-overlap principle", cannot be mapped onto a successor taxonomy). Ian Hacking (1993): after a revolution the world of individuals is the same, but scientists work in a world of new kinds. [source: SEP-KUHN]
- In his last work (*The Plurality of Worlds*) incommensurability also isolates scientific specialties from each other at one time, as specialisation grows, and Kuhn draws on developmental psychology (children's innate capacities to track objects and kinds). [source: SEP-KUHN]
- Kuhn's five shared values for theory choice ("Objectivity, Value Judgment, and Theory Choice", in *The Essential Tension*, 1977, 320–39): accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness. They are constitutive of science but do not determine choice: they are imprecise, disputable in application and can conflict. Differences in applying them let "rational men… disagree" and spread the risk of novelty. [source: SEP-KUHN; IEP-SR]

## 4. Masterman, the 1965 London colloquium, and the 1969 Postscript
- The International Colloquium in the Philosophy of Science was held at Bedford College, London; IEP gives 13 July 1965 for Kuhn's session, jointly organised by the British Society for the Philosophy of Science and the LSE. A planned Kuhn–Feyerabend debate fell through when Feyerabend was ill; John Watkins took his place in a session chaired by Popper. Contributors included Popper, Margaret Masterman, Stephen Toulmin and L. Pearce Williams. [source: SEP-KUHN; IEP-KUHN]
- The papers, with contributions by Feyerabend and Lakatos, appeared as *Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge*, ed. Imre Lakatos and Alan Musgrave (Cambridge, 1970), the fourth volume of the colloquium's proceedings. Kuhn's pieces there: "Logic of Discovery or Psychology of Research?" (pp. 1–23) and "Reflections on my Critics" (pp. 231–78). [source: SEP-KUHN]
- Margaret Masterman (1910–1986), founder of the Cambridge Language Research Unit (1955) and a former student at Wittgenstein's 1933–34 lectures, read "The Nature of a Paradigm" (published 1970, pp. 59–89). She found "at least 21" senses of "paradigm" in *Structure*, grouped as metaparadigms, sociological paradigms and artefact/construct paradigms, and called the book "at once scientifically perspicuous and philosophically obscure", while praising Kuhn as "one of the outstanding philosophers of science of our time". [source: IEP-KUHN ("twenty-one senses"); WP-Margaret_Masterman; SEP-KUHN]
- The 2nd edition (1970) added a "Postscript—1969" splitting "paradigm" into the **disciplinary matrix** (symbolic generalisations, models, values: "the constellation of shared commitments", 1970a, 182) and **exemplars** (concrete problem-solutions). Kuhn called paradigms-as-exemplars "the most novel and least understood aspect of this book" (1970a, 187). The Postscript also first made his anti-realism explicit. [source: SEP-KUHN; IEP-KUHN]
- Early hostile reception: Dudley Shapere's review (*Philosophical Review* 1964) stressed relativist implications and set the agenda; critics charged irrationalism and relativism. [source: SEP-KUHN; IEP-KUHN]

## 5. Kuhn on relativism and the sociologists
- Kuhn treated the charges of irrationalism and (pernicious) relativism as his chief concerns and thought them inaccurate. Postscript: "That is not a relativist's position, and it displays the sense in which I am a convinced believer in scientific progress" (1970, p. 206, per secondary citations). He conceded relativism only if it means there is no theory-independent way to match a theory's ontology to nature. [source: IEP-KUHN; WS (McGrew 1994, ejap.louisiana.edu; page not checked against the book)]
- Later, Kuhn called the charge of relativism "trivial": evaluations are made within a lexicon, and he admitted his view looked idealist but "an idealism like none other". [source: IEP-KUHN]
- In "The Trouble with the Historical Philosophy of Science" (1992) Kuhn derided the view that in scientific "negotiations" all that counts is interests and power, aiming at the Strong Programme; he called his own work "pretty straight internalist" (2000, 287). [source: SEP-KUHN]
- SEP's interpretation (Bird): Kuhn rejected both "convergent" realism and the "extreme relativism" that denies any measure of progress; part of his fame is that "both his supporters and his detractors took his work to be more revolutionary (anti-rationalist, relativist) than it really was". By the end of the 1980s a "new realism" occupied the centre of philosophy of science. [source: SEP-KUHN]

## 6. Fleck: the forgotten precursor
- Ludwik Fleck (1896–1961), Polish-Jewish physician and microbiologist, born 11 July 1896 in Lvov (now Lviv), then in Austria-Hungary; assistant to typhus specialist Rudolf Weigl. [source: SEP-FLECK]
- *Entstehung und Entwicklung einer wissenschaftlichen Tatsache: Einführung in die Lehre vom Denkstil und Denkkollektiv* (Benno Schwabe, Basel, 1935); English *Genesis and Development of a Scientific Fact* (1979). Fleck had sent the manuscript to Moritz Schlick in 1933. A 1927 paper already used the terms "thought style" and "incommensurability". [source: SEP-FLECK]
- Core ideas: a **thought collective** (a community of people exchanging ideas) develops a **thought style**; facts are produced within thought styles; thought styles are often incommensurable. The book's case study is the history of the concept of syphilis and the Wassermann reaction. Quotation: "the expert is already a specially molded individual who can no longer escape the bonds of tradition and of the collective; otherwise he would not be an expert" (1935a, III). [source: SEP-FLECK]
- Wartime: in the Lvov ghetto from August 1941 he produced an anti-typhus vaccine; deported with wife and son to Auschwitz; December 1943 transferred to Buchenwald, where prisoners produced a typhus vaccine; liberated April 1945. Later head of microbiology at Maria Curie-Skłodowska University in Lublin, testified at the Nuremberg doctors' trial (SEP says 1948), emigrated to Israel 1957, died 5 June 1961. [source: SEP-FLECK]
- Kuhn read Fleck's book in 1949 and mentioned it in the Preface to *Structure*, but "remained silent about Fleck's theory of thought communities and thought styles". Between 1962 and 1966 the publisher sold not a single copy; the remaining 258 copies were pulped. [source: SEP-FLECK]
- Kuhn wrote the foreword to the 1979 University of Chicago Press English edition (ed. Thaddeus J. Trenn and Robert K. Merton; trans. Fred Bradley and Trenn), calling the book "a brilliant and largely unexploited resource". [source: WS (press.uchicago.edu, ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp)]
- Reception debate: in the English-speaking world Fleck is mostly seen as an unappreciated forerunner of Kuhn; German-speaking scholars see him as a highly original epistemologist who goes beyond Kuhn. Bruno Latour called him "the founder of sociology of science" (2005, 112). [source: SEP-FLECK]

## 7. Lakatos: research programmes
- Imre Lakatos, born Imre Lipsitz in Debrecen, Hungary, on 9 November 1922 to Jewish parents; his mother and other relatives died in Auschwitz. He changed his name to the Hungarian "Lakatos"; became a committed communist; fled Hungary in 1956 after the Uprising; Professor of Logic at the LSE; never became a British citizen and died stateless, suddenly of a heart attack on 2 February 1974, aged 51. [source: SEP-LAK; WP-Imre_Lakatos]
- A dark episode SEP discusses: in 1944 his resistance cell persuaded the 19-year-old Éva Izsák that it was her duty to commit suicide, since she might be captured and betray them. (Handle carefully and briefly if used.) [source: SEP-LAK]
- Key works: *Proofs and Refutations* (1963–4 articles; book 1976); "Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes" (FMSRP, 1970, in *Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge*); "The History of Science and Its Rational Reconstructions" (1971). [source: SEP-LAK]
- The unit of appraisal is not a single theory but a **research programme**: a sequence of theories sharing a **hard core** made refutation-resistant "by methodological fiat", surrounded by a **protective belt** of auxiliary hypotheses. The **negative heuristic** forbids directing modus tollens at the core; the **positive heuristic** suggests how to modify the belt. [source: SEP-LAK]
- "All theories, in this sense, are born refuted and die refuted. But are they [all] equally good?" (S&P: 4–5). [source: SEP-LAK]
- A programme is **progressive** if theoretically progressive (each new theory predicts novel facts) and empirically progressive (some of these are corroborated); **degenerating** if not (FMSRP: 33–34). Example: Halley's comet, whose return was predicted using Newtonian mechanics and duly appeared (SEP says the comet turned up "seventy-two years later"; see NOT VERIFIED, do not repeat that figure). Einstein's light-bending prediction is another. [source: SEP-LAK]
- Lakatos's example of a degenerating programme was Marxism: early bold predictions (absolute impoverishment of workers, first revolution in the most industrial country) failed, and auxiliary hypotheses were "cooked up after the event". [source: SEP-LAK]
- Lakatos charged that on Kuhn's view "scientific revolution is irrational, a matter for mob psychology" (FMSRP, 1970, p. 178 per secondary sources). [source: WS (several secondary sources; not checked against the text)]

## 8. Feyerabend: *Against Method*
- Paul Feyerabend, born in Vienna on 13 January 1924, died on 11 February 1994 at the Genolier Clinic overlooking Lake Geneva. He served in the Wehrmacht (Iron Cross, second class; lieutenant); during the 1945 retreat he was hit by three bullets while directing traffic, one in the spine, leaving him partially paralysed and walking with a crutch for life (WP). He then studied in Vienna, was influenced by Popper, taught at Bristol, then Berkeley from 1958/1960, and in the 1980s half at ETH Zurich. [source: SEP-FEY; WP-Paul_Feyerabend]
- He broke with Popper's school after an "epiphany" in letters of 17 December 1967, generalising from the limited validity of falsificationism to "anything goes" (the limited validity of all methodological rules). [source: SEP-FEY]
- *Against Method* (1975; revised 1988; 3rd ed. 1993, with a new introduction for the Chinese translation) was planned as one half of a debate with Lakatos; its dedication reads "To IMRE LAKATOS Friend and Fellow-Anarchist". Feyerabend: "I was to attack the rationalist position, Imre was to restate and defend it, making mincemeat of me in the process." Lakatos died before writing his reply. [source: SEP-LAK; SEP-FEY]
- Argument: there are no useful exceptionless rules of method; if one insists on a general rule that will not inhibit progress, the only one left is the useless "anything goes". The case study: Galileo, whom Feyerabend portrays as using rhetoric, propaganda and "epistemological tricks"; if Galileo was a great scientist, rules that would condemn his practice are defective. [source: SEP-FEY; SEP-LAK]
- Feyerabend's dilemma against Lakatos: if MSRP tells scientists to abandon degenerating programmes, it may kill programmes that would have recovered; if (as Lakatos replied) it only appraises and gives no advice, it is "scarcely distinguishable from 'Anything goes'". [source: SEP-LAK]
- His own gloss: "'anything goes' is not a 'principle' I hold… but the terrified exclamation of a rationalist who takes a closer look at history." On this reading, "anything goes" is a reductio of rationalism, not a recommendation. Some recent scholars read it as a positive pluralist proposal. [source: WP-Paul_Feyerabend; WP-Against_Method; edition/page uncertain, see NOT VERIFIED]
- He rejected political anarchism (his politics was "a mixture of liberalism and social democracy"). In *Science in a Free Society* (1978) he proposed separating science and state, with lay juries democratically controlling experts; a free society is one "in which all traditions have equal rights and equal access to the centers of power" (1978, 9). He also defended traditional medicine, astrology and voodoo as worth pursuing. [source: SEP-FEY]
- The Berkeley classroom: from 1964 "Mexicans, Blacks, Indians entered the university"; Feyerabend asked "Who was I to tell these people what and how to think?" (1978, 118). [source: SEP-FEY]
- After the backlash: "I often wished I had never written that fucking book" (*Killing Time*, 147). Feyerabend later regretted that *Against Method* had introduced rigid concepts of its own ("democracy", "tradition", "relative truth"). [source: SEP-FEY]
- Non-Western angle: Jamie Shaw, "Feyerabend, Funding, and the Freedom of Science: The Case of Traditional Chinese Medicine", *European Journal for Philosophy of Science* 11 (2021). [source: WP-Paul_Feyerabend]

## 9. Sociology of scientific knowledge (briefly)
- After Kuhn, sociologists including Barry Barnes, Steven Shapin and Harry Collins argued that scientific judgment is determined by social factors (Barnes 1977, Shapin 1982, Collins 1983). The Strong Programme (Barnes, David Bloor, Collins, Donald MacKenzie, Andrew Pickering, Shapin) studied how large-scale ideologies and professional interests settle controversies; landmark: Shapin and Schaffer, *Leviathan and the Air-Pump* (1985), on Boyle vs Hobbes. Laboratory studies: Latour and Woolgar, *Laboratory Life* (cited 1986 ed.), Karin Knorr Cetina (1981); Sharon Traweek (1988) compared Japanese and North American high-energy physics communities. [source: SEP-SOC]
- Bloor's four tenets (*Knowledge and Social Imagery*, 1976): causality, impartiality (true and false beliefs alike), symmetry (the same types of explanation for both), reflexivity (the theory applies to sociology itself). [source: WP-Strong_programme]

## 10. Realism vs anti-realism
- SEP-SR's three dimensions of realist commitment: metaphysical (a mind-independent world), semantic (theories taken literally, with truth values), epistemological (theories give knowledge of observables and unobservables). [source: SEP-SR]
- **No-miracles argument (Putnam)**: realism "is the only philosophy that doesn't make the success of science a miracle" (Putnam 1975a: 73, *Mathematics, Matter and Method*). J. J. C. Smart's *Philosophy and Scientific Realism* (1963) is an earlier source cited by SEP. [source: SEP-SR; SEP-CE]
- **Van Fraassen's Darwinian reply**: "I claim that the success of current scientific theories is no miracle. It is not even surprising to the scientific (Darwinist) mind. For any scientific theory is born into a life of fierce competition, a jungle red in tooth and claw. Only the successful theories survive—the ones which in fact latched on to actual regularities in nature." (1980, 40). [source: SEP-CE]
- **Base-rate objection** (Howson 2000; Lipton; Magnus & Callender 2004): inferring approximate truth from success ignores the unknown base rate of approximately true theories. Worrall replies that this rests on a misleading probabilistic formalisation. [source: SEP-SR]
- **Pessimistic (meta-)induction (Laudan)**: Larry Laudan, "A Confutation of Convergent Realism", *Philosophy of Science* 48 (1981) (pages given as 19–48 in SEP-SR and 19–49 in SEP-STR). History shows empirically successful theories whose central terms did not refer (19th-century ether, caloric, phlogiston), and failing theories whose terms did (18th-century chemical atomism, Prout's hypothesis, Wegener's continental drift), so reference is neither necessary nor sufficient for success. Precursors: Poincaré on the "bankruptcy of science" and theories "abandoned one after another" (neither endorsed the argument); Putnam (1978). [source: SEP-SR; IEP-SR]
- Laudan (16 Oct 1941 – 23 Aug 2022); his *Progress and Its Problems* (1977) treated science as problem-solving. [source: WP-Larry_Laudan]
- **Realist replies (selective realism)**: restrict the induction to mature, non-*ad hoc* theories; or commit only to the parts of theories that did the work: Kitcher's "working posits" vs "idle parts" (1993), Psillos (1999): "it is enough to show that the theoretical laws and mechanisms which generated the successes of past theories have been retained in our current scientific image". Objection: post hoc rationalising. Kyle Stanford's "problem of unconceived alternatives" (2006) is a historicised underdetermination argument. [source: SEP-SR]
- **Constructive empiricism (van Fraassen, *The Scientific Image*, 1980)**: "Science aims to give us theories which are empirically adequate; and acceptance of a theory involves as belief only that it is empirically adequate" (1980, 12), contrasted with realism: "Science aims to give us, in its theories, a literally true story of what the world is like; and acceptance of a scientific theory involves the belief that it is true" (1980, 8). A theory is empirically adequate if what it says about observable things and events is true, observed or not. Observability is unaided: one does not see cells through a microscope but an image; moons of Jupiter count as observable because astronauts could see them. Van Fraassen (born 5 April 1941) coined the term and is "widely credited with rehabilitating scientific anti-realism". [source: SEP-CE; IEP-SR; WP-Bas_van_Fraassen]
- Objections to CE (Churchland, Musgrave, Fine, 1985–86): the observable/unobservable line has no epistemic significance and is unprincipled selectivity. [source: IEP-SR]
- **Structural realism (Worrall)**: John Worrall, "Structural Realism: The Best of Both Worlds?", *Dialectica* 43 (1989), 99–124. From Fresnel's elastic-solid ether to Maxwell's electromagnetic field: "There was continuity or accumulation in the shift, but the continuity is one of form or structure, not of content" (1989: 117). This avoids the pessimistic induction while not making success a miracle. Worrall found the view in Poincaré. Epistemic SR (Worrall) vs ontic SR (James Ladyman 1998, Steven French: there may be no objects beyond structure). Objection: Newman's (1928) problem. Worrall born 27 Nov 1946, LSE. [source: SEP-STR; IEP-SR; WP-John_Worrall_philosopher]
- **Entity realism (Hacking; Cartwright)**: believe in unobservables we can causally manipulate to intervene elsewhere. Ian Hacking (Vancouver, 18 Feb 1936 – 10 May 2023), *Representing and Intervening* (1983), from an experiment at Stanford in which electrons and positrons were sprayed onto a superconducting niobium ball to change its charge, in a search for fractional charges (free quarks). Objection: whether one can coherently believe in entities while doubting the theories that describe them (Morrison 1990; Resnik 1994). Related "corroboration" argument: Hacking's dense bodies in red blood platelets seen with different microscopes. [source: SEP-SR; WP-Entity_realism; WP-Ian_Hacking; WS for niobium detail]
- **NOA (Arthur Fine)**: accept the certified results of science like homely truths; realists add a needless "REALLY", antirealists a needless reinterpretation. Fine's example: the realist Einstein saw quantum mechanics as degenerate, the instrumentalist Bohr as progressive, and history favoured Bohr. [source: IEP-SR]
- IEP (Liston) assessment of Kuhn: interpreted "wrongly, but with some justice given his sometimes incautious language" as a radical relativist; but "no SR-realist can accept the thesis, never abandoned by Kuhn, that there is no fact of the matter whether P or P* is correct". [source: IEP-SR]

## 11. Explanation
- **Deductive-nomological (DN) model**: Hempel and Oppenheim, "Studies in the Logic of Explanation", *Philosophy of Science* 15 (1948), 135–175. An explanation is a sound deductive argument; the explanandum ("sentence describing the phenomenon to be explained") must follow from the explanans; the explanans must be true and contain at least one law as an essential premise. Inductive-statistical (IS) variant: explanans confers high probability. Hempel: 8 Jan 1905 – 9 Nov 1997. [source: SEP-EXPL; WP-Carl_Gustav_Hempel]
- **Asymmetry counterexample (flagpole)**: the pole's height plus the sun's angle and laws of light yield the shadow's length (explanatory); the shadow's length plus the angle yield the height (meets DN, not explanatory). Usually credited to Sylvain Bromberger, "Why-Questions" (1966); IEP gives Bromberger's pendulum version (length explains period, not vice versa) and the barometer–storm case. Salmon (1989): "a flagpole of a certain height causes a shadow of a given length and thereby explains the length of the shadow… the shadow does not cause the flagpole". [source: SEP-EXPL; IEP-EXPL; WS (MIT memorial page; Glymour) for Bromberger attribution]
- **Irrelevance counterexamples**: Salmon (1971): "All males who take birth control pills regularly fail to get pregnant; John Jones is a male who has been taking birth control pills regularly; John Jones fails to get pregnant". Kyburg (1965): hexed salt dissolves. Scriven (1962): "The impact of my knee on the desk caused the tipping over of the inkwell" explains without citing a law. [source: SEP-EXPL]
- Van Fraassen's pragmatic theory: explanations answer why-questions relative to context; his tower-and-shadow story, where the tower's height *is* explained by its having been designed to cast a certain shadow. [source: SEP-EXPL]
- **Causal-mechanical model**: Wesley Salmon, *Scientific Explanation and the Causal Structure of the World* (1984). Causal processes can transmit a "mark" (a scuff on a baseball, a dent in a fender); pseudo-processes such as a moving shadow cannot. [source: SEP-EXPL]
- **Unificationist model**: Michael Friedman, "Explanation and Scientific Understanding" (*Journal of Philosophy* 1974); Philip Kitcher (esp. 1989). Explanation reduces the number of independent phenomena or argument patterns, as with Newton's unification of terrestrial and celestial motion and Maxwell's of electricity and magnetism. [source: SEP-EXPL]
- Later interventionist account: James Woodward, *Making Things Happen: A Theory of Causal Explanation* (2003). [source: SEP-EXPL bibliography]

## 12. Laws of nature
- The puzzle: "All gold spheres are less than a mile in diameter" (accidental) vs "All uranium spheres are less than a mile in diameter" (law-like, given uranium's critical mass) (van Fraassen 1989, 27). [source: SEP-LAWS]
- **Regularity/best-system (Mill–Ramsey–Lewis)**: laws are the generalisations in the true deductive systems with the best balance of simplicity and strength (Lewis 1973, 73), consistent with "Humean supervenience". [source: SEP-LAWS]
- **Universals (Dretske 1977, Tooley 1977, Armstrong 1978/1983)**: a law is a contingent necessitation relation between universals, N(F,G) (Armstrong 1983, 85). Van Fraassen's identification and inference problems. Lewis: N "can't enter into them just by bearing a name, any more than one can have mighty biceps just by being called 'Armstrong'" (1983, 366). [source: SEP-LAWS]
- **Cartwright, *How the Laws of Physics Lie* (Oxford, 1983)**: "Rendered as descriptions of fact, they are false; amended to be true, they lose their fundamental explanatory force" (from "Do the Laws of Physics State the Facts?", *Pacific Philosophical Quarterly* 61, 1980, 75). Newton's gravitation law alone gives the wrong force on bodies that are also charged; add "provided no other forces act" and it becomes true but applies only in idealised cases. She is anti-realist about fundamental laws but realist about entities via "inference to the most likely cause" (Perrin on Brownian motion, Rutherford's scattering). Critics: confuses lack of known interaction laws with their absence; F = ma combines forces (Wilson 1998). Nancy Cartwright born 24 June 1944. [source: SEP-LAWS; IEP-SR; WP-Nancy_Cartwright_philosopher]

## 13. Values in science
- **Value-free ideal (VFI)** and value-neutrality thesis: cognitive/epistemic values (accuracy, scope, simplicity…) are generally seen as benign; contextual (moral, social, political) values as threats. Cautionary cases: "Deutsche Physik" in Nazi Germany; Lysenkoism and the death in prison of Nikolai Vavilov in the USSR. [source: SEP-OBJ]
- **Rudner**, "The Scientist Qua Scientist Makes Value Judgments", *Philosophy of Science* 20 (1953), 1–6: scientists accept or reject hypotheses; no hypothesis is ever completely verified; so how much evidence is enough is "a function of the importance, in the typically ethical sense, of making a mistake in accepting or rejecting a hypothesis" (1953: 2). Example: a drug wrongly judged safe vs wrongly judged unsafe. [source: SEP-OBJ]
- Replies: Richard Jeffrey (1956), scientists should only assign probabilities, not accept; Isaac Levi (1960), community standards (e.g. 5% significance) fix acceptance. Hempel (1965: 91–92) used the term "inductive risk". [source: SEP-OBJ]
- **Heather Douglas** (born 21 March 1969): "Inductive Risk and Values in Science", *Philosophy of Science* 67 (2000), 559–579, extended inductive risk to choice of method, characterisation of data and interpretation. Case study: dioxin and cancer in rats (classifying tissue samples; extrapolating from high to low doses). *Science, Policy, and the Value-Free Ideal* (Pittsburgh, 2009): values may play an **indirect** role (deciding what counts as sufficient evidence) but not a **direct** one (as reasons in themselves). Criticism: the direct/indirect line may not be clear-cut. Defenders of the VFI include Hugh Lacey, Ernan McMullin, Sandra Mitchell; critics include Longino, Kitcher, Douglas. [source: SEP-OBJ; WP-Heather_Douglas_philosopher]
- **Helen Longino** (born 13 July 1944), *Science as Social Knowledge: Values and Objectivity in Scientific Inquiry* (Princeton, 1990): "contextual empiricism"; a method is "objective to the degree that it permits transformative criticism" (1990: 76), requiring avenues for criticism, shared standards, uptake of criticism and equality of intellectual authority. [source: SEP-OBJ; SEP-FEMSCI; WP-Helen_Longino]
- **Sandra Harding** (29 March 1935 – 5 March 2025): *The Science Question in Feminism* (1986), which distinguished feminist empiricism, standpoint and postmodernism; *Whose Science? Whose Knowledge?* (1991); **"strong objectivity"**: the knowers and the process of producing knowledge should be scrutinised by the same standards as the objects of knowledge, which "can be done only from the perspective of those whose lives have been marginalized" (2004a: 136); postcolonial work: *Is Science Multicultural? Postcolonialisms, Feminisms, and Epistemologies* (1998), *Sciences from Below: Feminisms, Postcolonialities, and Modernities* (2008), *Objectivity and Diversity* (2015); later Latin American decolonial science studies (2017; Harding and Mendoza 2021). [source: SEP-FEMSCI; SEP-FEMEP; SEP-OBJ; WP-Sandra_Harding]
- **Evelyn Fox Keller** (20 March 1936 – 22 Sept 2023): *A Feeling for the Organism: The Life and Work of Barbara McClintock* (1983); *Reflections on Gender and Science* (1985). Her example: gamete research shaped by casting the egg as passive (feminine) and the sperm as active (masculine), later corrected; "language can shape the thinking and acting of working scientists" (2001: 106). [source: SEP-FEMSCI; WP-Evelyn_Fox_Keller]
- Donna Haraway's "situated knowledges" (1988) and Patricia Hill Collins's "outsider-within" (1986) belong to standpoint theory. [source: SEP-OBJ; SEP-FEMSCI]
- SEP-FEMSCI notes that its own perspective is "predominantly Anglo-American" and leaves out issues of the global South. Uma Narayan is cited there on Western feminists' focus on the value-free ideal. [source: SEP-FEMSCI]

## 14. Beyond Europe: the Needham question and the dialogue of civilisations
- Joseph Needham (9 Dec 1900 – 24 Mar 1995), biochemist and historian, initiated *Science and Civilisation in China* (proposed to CUP in 1948, first volume 1954; initial collaborator Wang Ling; continued by the Needham Research Institute). [source: WP-Joseph_Needham; SEP-SOC]
- "The Needham Question", posed in *The Grand Titration* (1969): "Why did modern science, the mathematization of hypotheses about Nature, with all its implications for advanced technology, take its meteoric rise only in the West", when Chinese civilisation had been "much more efficient than occidental in applying" natural knowledge to practical needs. Needham himself stressed the rise of the bourgeoisie and capitalism in Western Europe. [source: WP-Joseph_Needham]
- Critics: Nathan Sivin (a collaborator) said the question resembles asking "why your name did not appear on page 3 of today's newspaper" (Sivin 1995); Andre Gunder Frank (*ReOrient*) charged residual Eurocentrism; Justin Lin (1995) offered an economic answer. [source: WP-Joseph_Needham]
- SEP-SOC: "how civilizational differences are expressed in scientific work" has "yet to give rise to extended philosophical reflection"; it notes that mathematics and astronomy of 10th–14th-century Islamic and South Asian scholars developed aspects of global science independently, and cites S. Irfan Habib and Dhruv Raina (eds.), *Situating the History of Science: Dialogues with Joseph Needham* (OUP New Delhi, 2001) and Arun Bala, *The Dialogue of Civilizations in the Birth of Modern Science*. [source: SEP-SOC]
- Bala's thesis: Chinese, Indian, Arabic and ancient Egyptian ideas played an indispensable role in the birth of modern science; one chapter revisits Needham. Palgrave Macmillan, 2006 (SEP-SOC's bibliography says 2008; Palgrave's own page gives ebook 2006, hardcover 2007, paperback 2008). [source: WS (Palgrave, TAMU catalogue, Project MUSE review); SEP-SOC]

## Argument step by step (recommended): no-miracles vs pessimistic induction
**No-miracles argument (Putnam 1975; reconstruction following SEP-SR §2.1 and IEP-SR §4):**
1. Our best scientific theories are extraordinarily successful: accurate predictions, including novel ones, and precise causal manipulation.
2. If these theories were far from the truth about the unobservable world, this success would be a miracle (an inexplicable coincidence).
3. We should prefer a non-miraculous explanation to a miraculous one (an inference to the best explanation; see *Induction, Probability and Inference to the Best Explanation*).
4. The best explanation of the success is that the theories are (approximately) true and their central terms refer.
5. So we should believe our best theories are approximately true.

**Objection 1, the pessimistic induction (Laudan 1981):**
1. Many past theories were empirically successful (the ether theory of light, the caloric theory of heat, phlogiston chemistry).
2. By current lights their central terms did not refer, so they were not even approximately true.
3. So success is no reliable sign of approximate truth (premise 4 above fails); and by induction, our current successful theories are probably false too.
(Also: failing theories, such as Wegener's continental drift before c. 1950, had terms that referred, so reference is not sufficient for success either.)

**Objection 2, van Fraassen's Darwinian dissolution:** success needs no truth-based explanation; theories are selected for success, as organisms for fitness ("a jungle red in tooth and claw", 1980, 40). Realist counter: that explains why we *have* successful theories, not why *this* theory succeeds (SEP-SR).

**Objection 3, base-rate fallacy** (Howson; Magnus & Callender): success raises the probability of truth only relative to an unknown base rate of true theories. The same complaint has been turned against the pessimistic induction (Lewis 2001; Lange 2002).

**Replies to the pessimistic induction:**
- Restrict it to *mature*, non-*ad hoc* theories making novel predictions, so many of Laudan's cases drop out.
- Selective realism: what earned the success was retained. Psillos and Kitcher: the "working posits"; Worrall: the structure (Fresnel's equations survive in Maxwell's theory, though the ether does not); Hacking/Cartwright: entities we can manipulate.
- Counter-reply: selective realists identify the "working" parts only in hindsight, by checking what survived (SEP-SR: the charge of rationalising "post hoc"); Stanford's unconceived alternatives.
- Kuhn's own position sits outside the dilemma: he rejected convergent realism and extreme relativism together; progress is increasing puzzle-solving power, not approach to truth (SEP-KUHN).

**Second argument option (shorter): Lakatos's modus tollens** (SEP-LAK): If ⟨hard core + auxiliaries⟩ then O; not-O; so not ⟨hard core + auxiliaries⟩. Logic does not say which conjunct to give up; the negative heuristic says the belt. The demarcation question becomes whether the sequence of adjusted theories predicts novel facts that are confirmed. Feyerabend's dilemma (§8) is the main objection.

## Debates
- **Was Kuhn a relativist/irrationalist?** Shapere (1964), Lakatos ("mob psychology", WS) and others said yes; Kuhn denied it (Postscript; 1977; 1992); SEP (Bird) and IEP (Liston) both say the radical reading was wrong but had "some justice" given his language. Hoyningen-Huene's neo-Kantian reading vs Wittgensteinian readings (Kindi; Sharrock and Read). [source: SEP-KUHN; IEP-SR]
- **Continuity in Kuhn**: did the later lexicon/taxonomic theory abandon *Structure*'s paradigms or deepen them? SEP: paradigms were dropped partly because they invited relativism charges, then the evolutionary picture returned in *The Plurality of Worlds* (2022). [source: SEP-KUHN]
- **How much Kuhn owed Fleck**, and whether Fleck went further: English-speaking vs German-speaking reception. [source: SEP-FLECK]
- **"Anything goes": reductio or positive proposal?** Most read it as a reductio of rationalism, following Feyerabend's own gloss; some recent work reads epistemological anarchism as a positive pluralism. Did Lakatos have an answer to Feyerabend's dilemma? Feyerabend suggested he would have; SEP (on the correspondence) says no trace of a counterargument exists. [source: WP-Against_Method; SEP-LAK]
- **Realism**: no-miracles vs pessimistic induction (above); epistemic vs ontic structural realism; whether entity realism is coherent; Fine's NOA as a rejection of the whole debate. [source: SEP-SR; SEP-STR; IEP-SR]
- **Explanation**: is causation the key (Salmon, Woodward) or unification (Friedman, Kitcher) or context (van Fraassen)? SEP-EXPL closes on whether a single model can work. [source: SEP-EXPL]
- **Laws**: Humean best-system (Lewis) vs necessitation (Armstrong–Dretske–Tooley) vs anti-realism (van Fraassen, Giere, Mumford) vs Cartwright's capacities. [source: SEP-LAWS]
- **Value-free ideal**: Lacey, McMullin, Mitchell defend a version; Longino, Kitcher, Douglas reject or reformulate it; feminist standpoint theory (Harding, Haraway, Wylie) rejects neutrality as an ideal. [source: SEP-OBJ]
- **Needham question**: a genuine question with economic/social answers (Needham, Lin) or a misconceived counterfactual (Sivin) or residual Eurocentrism (Frank, and implicitly Bala). [source: WP-Joseph_Needham; SEP-SOC]
