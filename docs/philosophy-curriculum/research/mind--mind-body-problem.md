## 1. Framing: what the problem is
- SEP-Dual: in its broadest form, the mind–body problem is "the problem of explaining how humans and other mentally endowed beings end up having, or seeming to have, these very different characteristics" (physical: size, weight, shape, colour, motion; mental: perceptual and emotional experience, beliefs, desires, agency, selfhood). In the narrower form that "takes centre stage after the emergence of mechanistic science in the seventeenth century", it is reconciling the apparent ontological disparity of mind and body "with the fact that they seem to interact closely". [source: SEP-Dual §1]
- Two component questions: the ontological question (what are mind and body; is one "nothing over and above" the other?) and the causal question (does each influence the other, and how?). Different aspects of the problem arise for consciousness, intentionality, agency, personal identity and selfhood. [source: SEP-Dual §1]
- Classic map: dualism, materialism and idealism (the triad is Wolff 1751); plus panpsychism as a recent fourth option. Materialism: mind, insofar as it exists, is fundamentally physical; idealism: matter is fundamentally mental; dualism: both real and fundamentally distinct. [source: SEP-Dual §1]
- **PhilPapers 2020 survey** (Bourget and Chalmers 2023) on the mind–body problem: 51% accept or lean towards materialism (physicalism), 22% dualism, 8% panpsychism; idealism was not an option on that question. Dualism is the second most popular answer. [source: SEP-Dual §1]
- Even opponents agree dualism is "intuitively appealing" (the "meta-problem": why dualism seems true). Infant psychology suggests dualism may be part of how humans naturally conceive themselves (Bloom etc., contested by Hodge 2008). [source: SEP-Dual §§1–2]
- **The mark of the mental (1): intentionality.** Brentano, *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (vol. 1, 1874): "Every mental phenomenon is characterized by what the Scholastics of the Middle Ages called the intentional (or mental) inexistence of an object, and what we might call, though not wholly unambiguously, reference to a content, direction toward an object (which is not to be understood here as meaning a thing), or immanent objectivity. Every mental phenomenon includes something as object within itself, although they do not do so in the same way. In presentation, something is presented, in judgment something is affirmed or denied, in love loved, in hate hated, in desire desired and so on. This intentional inexistence is characteristic exclusively of mental phenomena. No physical phenomenon exhibits anything like it." (English as quoted in SEP; the standard translation is Rancurello, Terrell and McAlister, Routledge 1973.) [source: SEP-Int §2; SEP-Bren §3.2 and bibliography]
- Caveat: in context Brentano offers this as one of six criteria to demarcate the subject matter of scientific psychology, not as a systematic theory of intentionality; whether "inexistence" means existence *in* the mind or directedness to possibly non-existent objects is debated (Crane 1998). [source: SEP-Bren §3.2; SEP-Int §2]
- **The mark of the mental (2): phenomenal character.** Most philosophers hold that "if minds are real, then two problems arise: the problem of intentionality and the problem of consciousness or conscious phenomenal experience", and that solving the first does not solve the second; consciousness is "what it is like" (Nagel 1974) to be a creature. Rorty (1979) doubted any single mark: "the attempt to hitch pains and beliefs together seems ad hoc—they don't seem to have anything in common except our refusal to call them 'physical'". [source: SEP-Int §10 "Is intentionality exhibited by all mental phenomena?"]
- Whether all mental states (e.g. pains, moods) are intentional is "very controversial". [source: SEP-Int]
- **"World knot"**: see NOT VERIFIED. Schopenhauer's *Weltknoten* ("world-knot") names the identity of the subject of willing with the subject of knowing (*Fourfold Root*); Günter Zöller's chapter "The World Knot" (in *Schopenhauer's Fourfold Root*, 2017) treats it so. Using it as a name for the mind–body problem is a loose later extension. [source: WS-5 — second-hand]

## 2. Descartes's arguments for dualism (cross-ref *Descartes*; *Possibility and Necessity*)
Already in *Descartes*: the *Discourse* argument and Hatfield's Batman/Bruce Wayne objection; the Sixth Meditation real-distinction argument in four quoted steps; Arnauld's triangle objection and reply; Skirry's verdict; "sailor in a ship"; Elisabeth's letters (dates 6 May, 21 May, 20 June, 28 June 1643); pineal gland 1640 quote; Lokhorst. Do not repeat; summarise and add the divisibility argument and modern framing.
- **Conceivability/separability argument (Meditation VI, AT VII 78; CSM II 54)**: "on the one hand I have a clear and distinct idea of myself, in so far as I am simply a thinking, non-extended thing [that is, a mind], and on the other hand I have a distinct idea of body, in so far as this is simply an extended, non-thinking thing. And accordingly, it is certain that I am really distinct from my body, and can exist without it" (CSM translation). [source: IEP-DescMB §3]
- Skirry's generalised form: (1) I have a clear and distinct idea of the mind as a thinking, non-extended thing; (2) I have a clear and distinct idea of body as an extended, non-thinking thing; (3) therefore the mind is really distinct from the body and can exist without it. The link from conceiving to possibility runs through God: whatever is clearly and distinctly understood can be created by God as understood; "the power of God makes Descartes' perceived logical possibility … into a metaphysical possibility". [source: IEP-DescMB §3]
- "Real distinction" is a technical term: a distinction between substances, each able to exist "with only the help of God's concurrence" (Principles I.60). [source: IEP-DescMB §1]
- SEP-Dual's modern reconstruction (the "disembodiment argument"): (1) It is conceivable that one's mind might exist without one's body. (2) If so, it is possible. (3) So it is possible. (4) So one's mind is a different entity from one's body. Premise (1) is supported by the doubt of Meditations I–II; premise (2) by the principle that what one clearly and distinctly conceives is possible. [source: SEP-Dual §4.2] (NB SEP-Dual says "Meditation V"; the passage is in Meditation VI — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- **Main objection**: conceivability is not a safe guide to possibility, since there are a posteriori necessities whose falsity seems conceivable (water/H2O); a materialist can also deny premise (1). [source: SEP-Dual §4.2] Skirry: "the main difficulty with Descartes' real distinction argument is that he has not adequately eliminated the possibility of minds being extended things like brains". [source: IEP-DescMB §3] (*Possibility and Necessity* already sets out the zombie version with Hesperus/Phosphorus objection and Chalmers's two-dimensional reply; cross-reference.)
- The Sixth Meditation is where Descartes argues positively from clear and distinct perception of essences, having in Meditation II "explicitly refrained" from concluding the distinction (AT 7:27). [source: SEP-D §3.4]
- **Divisibility argument (Meditation VI, AT VII 85–6; CSM II 59)**: "there is a great difference between the mind and the body, inasmuch as the body is by its very nature always divisible, while the mind is utterly indivisible. For when I consider the mind, or myself in so far as I am merely a thinking thing, I am unable to distinguish any parts within myself; I understand myself to be something quite single and complete…. By contrast, there is no corporeal or extended thing that I can think of which in my thought I cannot easily divide into parts; and this very fact makes me understand that it is divisible. This one argument would be enough to show me that the mind is completely different from the body…" (CSM; IEP gives AT VII 86–87). [source: IEP-DescMB §3]
  - Step by step (Skirry): (1) I understand the mind to be indivisible by its very nature. (2) I understand body to be divisible by its very nature. (3) Therefore the mind is completely different from the body (real distinction follows implicitly). Support: cut a pencil in half and you have two bodies; divide an "I" and you would have two selves, which is absurd. The properties are essential and contradictory, so one nature cannot have both. [source: IEP-DescMB §3]
  - Objection (Skirry): this version does not invoke clear and distinct perception, so outside Descartes's system "it is possible that Descartes is mistaken about the indivisibility of the mind", because the mind might require a brain and so be extended and divisible. [source: IEP-DescMB §3] (A Leibniz's-law reading: if mind = brain, they share all properties; brains are divisible; minds are not; so mind ≠ brain. The reply is the same: that we cannot *conceive* parts in the mind doesn't show it has none.)
- Descartes "shifts the focus" of dualism from intellect (the ancient and medieval basis for immateriality) to consciousness, which for him includes sensory awareness; and combined dualism with a mechanistic physics, making interaction "the leading objection to dualism". [source: SEP-Dual §2.4]
- Descartes's mind is a substance in the sense of existing independently (God aside) and is unitary (indeed indivisible); he does not obviously posit a Lockean substratum. [source: SEP-Dual §3.1]

## 3. Elisabeth's interaction objection and Descartes's reply (cross-ref *Descartes*; *Women Philosophers of the Early Modern Era*)
- Elisabeth of the Palatinate (of Bohemia), 26 December 1618 – 11 February 1680; correspondence began at her initiative in 1643 and ran until Descartes's death in early 1650; her extant philosophy is almost entirely this correspondence. [source: WP-Elisabeth_of_the_Palatinate; SEP-Eli §4]
- **First letter (May 1643), Lisa Shapiro's translation**: "tell me please how the soul of a human being (it being only a thinking substance) can determine the bodily spirits and so bring about voluntary actions". [source: WP-Elisabeth_of_the_Palatinate (citing Shapiro's 1999 article)] A search summary gives the 2007 book wording as "…can determine the bodily spirits, in order to bring about voluntary actions" (AT III 661; Shapiro 2007 p. 62). Use "trans. Lisa Shapiro" and either wording consistently with *Descartes*, which uses the first. [source: WS-1]
- **The same letter in Anscombe and Geach's translation (1954)**, as SEP-MC quotes it: she presses him "to tell her how the human soul can determine the movement of the animal spirits in the body so as to perform voluntary acts—being as it is merely a conscious substance. For the determination of movement seems always to come about from the moving body's being propelled—to depend on the kind of impulse it gets from what sets it in motion, or again, on the nature and shape of this latter thing's surface. Now the first two conditions involve contact, and the third involves that the impelling thing has extension; but you utterly exclude extension from your notion of soul, and contact seems to me incompatible with a thing's being immaterial" (Anscombe and Geach 1954, pp. 274–5). [source: SEP-MC §2.1] Useful because it gives the reasoning in her own (translated) words.
- **Date**: WP and *Descartes* give 6 May 1643; AT and some editions date it 16 May 1643 (probably Old vs New Style; not verified). [source: WP-Elisabeth; WS-1]
- **Her argument step by step** (SEP-MC, Shapiro, Skirry): (1) A voluntary action requires the soul to determine (set in motion / direct) the bodily spirits. (2) Every determination of motion comes about by impulsion, which requires contact, or depends on the shape of the mover's surface, which requires extension. (3) Descartes's soul is wholly unextended, and contact is incompatible with immateriality. (4) So the soul cannot determine the motion of the spirits: Descartes owes either a new account of causation proper to mind–body action or a different account of the soul's nature. [source: SEP-MC §2.1; SEP-Eli §4.2; IEP-DescMB §4]
- Shapiro: Elisabeth asks specifically about mind-to-body (not body-to-mind) causation; she is committed to a mechanist (efficient-causal) account of causation but open about which. [source: SEP-Eli §4.2]
- SEP-MC generalises: her objection is one instance of (CN) "Any causal relation requires a nexus, some interface by means of which cause and effect are connected"; a soul with no location has no nexus. Whether causation needs a nexus is disputed by Humeans (cause as constant conjunction). [source: SEP-MC §2.1] SEP-Dual: the naive form rests on a "billiard ball" picture of causation; if causation is only constant conjunction there is "no problem in principle". [source: SEP-Dual §3.3]
- **Descartes's replies**:
  - 21 May 1643: primitive notions. "Then, as regards body in particular, we have only the notion of extension, which entails the notions of shape and motion; and as regards the soul on its own, we have only the notion of thought, which includes the perceptions of the intellect and the inclinations of the will" and "Lastly, as regards the soul and the body together, we have only the notion of their union, on which depends our notion of the soul's power to move the body, and the body's power to act on the soul and cause its sensations and passions" (AT III 665; CSMK 218). [source: IEP-DescMB §5] He compared the union with the scholastic notion of heaviness; Elisabeth rejected the model as one Descartes himself had shown unintelligible (20 June). [source: SEP-Eli §4.2]
  - To Gassendi (Fifth Replies, AT VII 213; CSM II 275): "the whole problem contained in such questions arises simply from a supposition that is false and cannot in any way be proved, namely that, if the soul and the body are two substances whose nature is different, this prevents them from being able to act on each other." [source: IEP-DescMB §5]
  - He "admitted to Elisabeth that he didn't have a good answer (3:694)" (Hatfield). [source: SEP-D §3.4] Scholars: replies "evasive" (Shapiro); raise whether the union is a third substance (Garber 1983); imply a contradiction in being both two and one (Mattern 1978); jump between mind–body and body–mind (Rozemond 1999). [source: SEP-Eli §4.2]
  - Skirry's reading: union as a relation of act and potency (soul as substantial form), so voluntary motion is a mode of "the soul and the body together"; but this meets a scholastic problem of formal causation that the scholastics never answered either. [source: IEP-DescMB §5]
  - Pineal gland: in 1640 letters Descartes called it "the principal seat of the soul, and the place in which all our thoughts are formed", reasoning that it is the only single, not double, part of the brain (29 January 1640, AT III:19; 30 July 1640). The fullest account is *The Passions of the Soul* (1649). Willis (1664) and Steno (1669) rejected the neurophysiology; it was "almost universally rejected after his death". Lokhorst lists several interpretations besides textbook interactionism. [source: SEP-Pin]
- Elisabeth's own view: she suggests the mind might be "material and extended" (the vapours affect thought); doubts a thinking thing is a substance properly speaking. Shapiro (1999, 2007): willing to revisit dualism, advocates no alternative; Janssen-Lauret (2018): a "naturalistic dualism", soul both conscious and extended. [source: SEP-Eli §4.2] (Already in *Descartes* and *Women Philosophers*.)
- Jaegwon Kim cites Elisabeth's 20 June letter as the first causal argument for physicalism. [source: WP-Elisabeth] (Already in *Descartes*.)

## 4. Occasionalism and parallelism (brief; cross-ref *Leibniz*, *Spinoza*, *Descartes*)
- SEP-Dual's "progression": Descartes believes in a "more or less natural form of interaction"; Malebranche thought this impossible naturally, so God intervenes on each occasion; Leibniz decided "God might as well set things up so that they always behaved as if they were interacting" (pre-established harmony). Parallelism outside a theistic framework "may seem incredible". [source: SEP-Dual §3.5]
- Occasionalism: God is the only true cause. Malebranche (1638–1715): "there is only one true cause because there is only one true God; … the nature or power of each thing is nothing but the will of God; … all natural causes are not true causes but only occasional causes" (*Search after Truth*). [source: SEP-Occ intro; WP-Nicolas_Malebranche]
- Cartesian occasionalists before Malebranche: Louis de la Forge (1632–66), Géraud de Cordemoy (1614–83), Johannes Clauberg (1622–55), Arnold Geulincx (1624–69). Islamic occasionalism begins with al-Ashʿarī (873–935). [source: SEP-Occ §§1.2–1.3]
- Lee: treating occasionalism as an ad hoc fix for Cartesian interaction is "a caricature at best". [source: SEP-Occ §1.3] (Already in *Descartes*.)
- Interactionism / epiphenomenalism / parallelism defined: mind and body act on each other / body acts on mind but not conversely / neither acts on the other. [source: SEP-Dual §3]
- Spinoza: one thing under two attributes ("usually called parallelism" — *Spinoza* chapter). Leibniz: the clocks, "perpetual miracle" objection to occasionalism (*Leibniz* chapter). Cross-reference only.

## 5. Property dualism and epiphenomenalism
- SEP-Dual distinguishes **predicate dualism** (mental predicates are essential and not reducible to physical ones in Nagel's 1961 bridge-law sense — compatible with materialism; classic sources Fodor 1974 for the special sciences, Davidson for mind) from **property dualism** (two essentially different kinds of property in the world). Example: "hurricane" or "coup d'état" are multiply realizable and not replaceable by a physics description, yet nothing non-physical exists. [source: SEP-Dual §3.2]
- Three classic defences of property dualism: Jackson (1982), Chalmers (1996), Kim (2005). Jackson (2003) later abandoned it for materialism; Chalmers (2016) has expressed a preference for substance dualism over property dualism. [source: SEP-Dual §3.2]
- From c. 2000 a "widely accepted opinion" held that substance dualism is not respectable but property dualism must be taken seriously; that preference is itself challenged (Searle 2002, Zimmerman 2010, Lycan 2013, Weir 2023 …). [source: SEP-Dual §2.4]
- **Epiphenomenalism**: "mental events are caused by physical events in the brain, but have no effects upon any physical events". "Huxley (1874), who held the view, compared mental events to a steam whistle that contributes nothing to the work of a locomotive." William James (1879), rejecting it, said such mental events affect brain activity no more "than a shadow reacts upon the steps of the traveller whom it accompanies". [source: SEP-Epi intro]
- Huxley's essay: "On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata, and its History" (1874), *Fortnightly Review* 22: 555–580, reprinted in *Collected Essays* vol. I (Macmillan 1893) — hence SEP-Dual's "Huxley 1893". [source: IEP-Epi references; SEP-Epi §4] Delivered as an address at the British Association meeting in Belfast in 1874 [source: WS-2]. IEP-Epi calls it "the Presidential Address"; but Huxley was BA president in 1870, not 1874 (see NOT VERIFIED). [source: WP-Thomas_Henry_Huxley]
- The steam-whistle sentence: consciousness is "completely without any power… as the steam-whistle which accompanies the work of a locomotive engine is without influence upon its machinery" (as quoted via Flanagan 1992). [source: WP-Epiphenomenalism] Fuller wording per search summary: the consciousness of brutes "would appear to be related to the mechanism of their body simply as a collateral product of its working, and to be as completely without any power of modifying that working as the steam-whistle which accompanies the work of a locomotive engine is without influence upon its machinery". [source: WS-2 — not opened]
- The essay also compares the soul to "the bell of a clock": "The soul stands related to the body as the bell of a clock to the works, and consciousness answers to the sound which the bell gives out when it is struck" (Huxley 1874, 242). [source: IEP-Epi §1] Huxley held "the argumentation which applies to brutes holds equally good of men". [source: SEP-Epi §4]
- Huxley's evidence: frogs with parts of the brain removed still swim; Dr Mesnet's case of a French soldier, wounded in 1870, who in trance-like states performed complex actions while apparently insensible (pins, shocks, asafoetida). [source: IEP-Epi §2; SEP-Epi §4]
- The word "epiphenomenalism" does not occur in Huxley's 1874 essay (his term was "automatism"); IEP says the term seems to have been introduced in 1890 in William James's *Principles of Psychology*. Charles Bonnet (1755) gave an early formulation ("the soul is a mere spectator of the movements of its body"). [source: SEP-Epi §4; IEP-Epi §§1–2]
- Ancient precursor: Yoga–Sāṃkhya's *puruṣa* as pure awareness with no effect on *prakṛti* (Schweizer 2019). [source: SEP-Dual §3.4]
- **Objections to epiphenomenalism** (SEP-Dual): (1) profoundly counterintuitive — the pain makes you cry; it "tends to be adopted because other options are held to be unacceptable"; (2) William James (1890): if mental states do nothing, why did they evolve? Jackson (1982) replies that the brain state evolves and the sensation is a by-product, like the heaviness of a polar bear's warm coat; rejoinder: the coat's heaviness follows from the laws that make it warm, whereas mind–brain laws are Feigl's "nomological danglers", unexplained add-ons; (3) other minds: if my mental states do not explain my behaviour, inferring them in others is explanatorily idle. [source: SEP-Dual §3.4]

## 6. Causal closure and the exclusion argument (step by step)
- **Completeness of the physical**: "Every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause." Also called (physical) closure (Crane, Papineau, Kim); a stronger "closure" forbids non-physical causes of physical events (Kim 1998, p. 40). It seems a substantive empirical claim, defended by the "no-gap argument" (Papineau 1993; Melnyk 2003) and by conservation laws. [source: SEP-MC §2.4]
- **Papineau (2001, "The Rise of Physicalism", in Gillett and Loewer eds., *Physicalism and its Discontents*)** argues that the emergence of clear-cut evidence for closure under physics, "even in the bodies of living creatures", accounts for the rise of materialism in the 1950s and 1960s. [source: SEP-Dual §2.4; IEP-Excl references]
- History of conservation (IEP-Excl): Descartes's conservation of quantity of motion (mind could alter direction, not speed); Leibniz's conservation law, which he argued closed the physical world to mental causes; Helmholtz's conservation of energy. [source: IEP-Excl §2b]
- **Argument against interactionist dualism (SEP-MC)**: (1) Completeness. (2) No Overdetermination: "There is no systematic overdetermination of physical effects." (3) Suppose for reductio souls routinely cause behaviour. (4) By (1) that behaviour also has a sufficient physical cause, so it is systematically overdetermined, contradicting (2). (5) So souls do not cause behaviour: the dualist is pushed to epiphenomenalism or parallelism. Kim calls systematic overdetermination "absurd" (1993) and one of the "nonstarters" (1998, p. 65); Melnyk: an "intolerable coincidence". [source: SEP-MC §2.4]
- **Replies**: deny Completeness (Baker 1993: entrenched explanatory practice trumps abstract principle; Hendry 2006 downward causation in chemistry; Stapp 2005 quantum gaps; emergentists); defend overdetermination (Mills 1996 counterfactual test; Lowe 2003); conservation reply ("conditionality": conservation holds only for causally isolated systems, and the interactionist denies the body is one — Larmer 1986; Pitts 2019); Robin Collins (2011): energy not conserved in general relativity; Broad (1925): a soul might redistribute energy without changing its quantity; quantum indeterminacy (Eccles, Popper and Eccles 1977; Stapp). [source: SEP-MC §§2.3–2.4; SEP-Dual §3.3]
- **Other interaction problems**: the **pairing problem** (Kim 1973, 2005; Foster 1991): two exactly similar non-spatial souls M1, M2 and bodies B1, B2 — what makes M1 pair with B1? Reply: "individualistic" powers (Unger 2006; Foster). [source: SEP-MC §2.2]
- **Kim's exclusion argument** (against *non-reductive* physicalism; Kim 1989, 1993, *Mind in a Physical World* 1998 (MIT Press), *Physicalism, or Something Near Enough* 2005 (Princeton); contemporary form introduced by Norman Malcolm 1968). IEP-Excl's four jointly inconsistent principles:
  1. Mental causation: some events have mental causes.
  2. Physical causal completeness: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause.
  3. Irreducibility: mental causes of behaviour are distinct from physical causes of behaviour.
  4. Causal exclusion: "No single event can have more than one sufficient cause occurring at any given time—unless it is a genuine case of causal overdetermination" (Kim 2005, 42).
  Non-reductive physicalists accept 1–3; 4 is the objection. Support for 4: the massive-coincidence argument (Kim 1998, 53: barns rarely burn from simultaneous lightning and a dropped match, yet mental causation is ubiquitous), parsimony (Kim 1989), necessity. [source: IEP-Excl §§1–2]
  - SEP-MC's informal version: whenever a mental property M is instantiated it is realized by a physical property P; P is "unproblematically relevant" to the behaviour; "then what causal work is left for M to do? It seems to be causally idle, 'excluded' by the work of P." [source: SEP-MC §6.2]
  - Worked example usable in prose: IEP-Excl's Jennie, whose wanting a peach causes her to eat one. [source: IEP-Excl §1]
- **Replies to exclusion**: (a) *generalization/"causal drainage"*: if it works it excludes every special-science property (biology, geology), and if there is no bottom level all causation drains away (Block 2003; Walter 2008; Fodor 1989; Baker 1993); Kim replies it does not generalize because mental/physical is a relation of higher- to lower-order properties of one thing, not whole to part. [source: IEP-Excl §5; SEP-MC §6.2] (b) *autonomy solutions*: higher-level causes coexist with lower (Yablo 1992's dual-explanandum, Woodward 2008 …). (c) *inheritance solutions*: mental properties inherit their realizers' powers (determinable–determinate, Yablo 1992; constitution, Pereboom 2002; metaphysical necessitation, Bennett 2003, 2008); Jackson and Pettit's distinction between causal efficacy and causal relevance. (d) Accept reduction (Kim's own route for intentional states) or accept epiphenomenalism. [source: SEP-MC §§6.3–6.4; IEP-Excl §3]
- Kim (12 September 1934, Daegu – 27 November 2019); late view: intentional states functionally reducible, qualitative states (qualia) irreducible and epiphenomenal — "physicalism, or something near enough". [source: WP-Jaegwon_Kim]
- (*Causation* says causation "returns in *The Mind–Body Problem*, on whether minds can cause anything" — this section fulfils that.)

## 7. Behaviourism
- **Three kinds** (Graham): *methodological* (psychology should study behaviour, not mental states; Watson); *psychological* (explain behaviour by stimuli, responses, learning histories and reinforcement; Pavlov, Thorndike, Watson, fullest in Skinner's schedules of reinforcement); *analytical/logical/philosophical* (a semantic thesis: the very idea of a mental state is the idea of a behavioural disposition; Ryle, later Wittgenstein "if perhaps not without controversy", Hempel, Carnap; Place for intentional states). Skinner's "radical behaviorism" combines all three. [source: SEP-Beh §§1–2]
- Logical behaviourism's roots are in logical positivism's verificationism; it avoids substance dualism; on it a belief is "a family of tendencies of my body" and so cannot be the cause of the behaviour it consists in. [source: SEP-Beh §3]
- **John B. Watson** (9 January 1878 – 25 September 1958), "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It", *Psychological Review* 20 (1913): 158–77, the "behaviorist manifesto": psychology "as the behaviorist views it is a purely objective experimental branch of natural science. Its theoretical goal is … prediction and control" (1913, p. 158). [source: SEP-Beh §3; WP-John_B._Watson] (SEP's bibliography misprints the title as "…as a Behaviorist Views It".)
- **B. F. Skinner** (20 March 1904 – 18 August 1990); *Verbal Behavior* (1957); in *About Behaviorism* (1974) mental terms that cannot be eliminated can be "translated into behavior". [source: SEP-Beh §3; WP-B._F._Skinner]
- **Carl Hempel** (8 January 1905 – 9 November 1997), "The Logical Analysis of Psychology" (in Feigl and Sellars eds., *Readings in Philosophical Analysis*, 1949; first published in French in 1935 — not verified here): "all psychological statements that are meaningful … are translatable into statements that do not involve psychological concepts" (p. 18 as SEP cites). By 1966 he defected: to characterize behavioural patterns "we need not only a suitable behavioristic vocabulary, but psychological terms as well". [source: SEP-Beh §§4, 8; WP-Carl_Gustav_Hempel]
- **Gilbert Ryle** (19 August 1900 – 6 October 1976), *The Concept of Mind* (1949). He described Cartesian dualism as the "official doctrine" to which "most philosophers, psychologists and religious teachers subscribe, with minor reservation" (Ryle 1949, 1). [source: SEP-Dual §2.4; WP-Gilbert_Ryle]
- Ryle's verdict on the "dogma of the Ghost in the Machine": "I hope to prove that it is entirely false, and false not in detail but in principle. It is not merely an assemblage of particular mistakes. It is one big mistake and a mistake of a special kind. It is, namely, a category-mistake." [source: WP-The_Concept_of_Mind (chapter "Descartes' Myth")] Ryle "consistently hyphenates 'category-mistake'"; Magidor (2013) thinks this is the first use of the label. [source: WP-Category_mistake]
- **The university example** (Concept of Mind, p. 16 in the Chicago edition): a visitor to Oxford [or Cambridge] shown the colleges, libraries [and other buildings] asks "But where is the University?" — he wrongly places the University in the same category as the buildings, whereas it is the way all that he has seen is organised. Second example: a child watching a division march past who, having been shown battalions, batteries and squadrons, asks when the division will appear. [source: WP-Category_mistake; WS-4] Only the quoted question "But where is the University?" is verified verbatim; give the rest as paraphrase (as *Analytic Philosophy since 1950* did). Ryle himself wrote that the "general trend of this book will undoubtedly, and harmlessly, be stigmatised as 'behaviourist'". [source: WP-Gilbert_Ryle]
- **Debate (is Ryle a behaviourist?)**: Tanney: "Ryle is not a philosophical behaviourist—at least he does not subscribe to any of the main tenets associated with that doctrine as it is known today"; his target "was not merely the ghostliness of the mental processes hypothesized by the Cartesian; it was their essential hidden-ness" — whether in an "occult" stream of consciousness or in occult computations and neural events. Graham's SEP-Beh, by contrast, lists Ryle as the analytical behaviourist. [source: SEP-Ryle; SEP-Beh §2] (Already flagged in *Analytic Philosophy since 1950*.)
- Wittgenstein: *Wittgenstein* chapter already says he denied being a behaviourist (beetle in the box; "the object drops out of consideration"). Cross-reference.
- **Objection 1 — circularity (the "intentional circle", Chisholm 1957; Geach 1957, *Mental Acts*)**: a desire to stay dry disposes you to carry an umbrella only if you believe it might rain, and the belief that it might rain disposes you to carry one only if you want to stay dry; so "which behavioral dispositions a desire issues in depends on other states of the desirer" (Block 1981). The paraphrases "almost always use mental terms themselves" — SEP-Beh's dentist example: believing I have a 2pm appointment shows up as arriving at 2pm only given the desire to arrive. Possible reply: paraphrase a whole "swarm" of mental terms at once (Rey 1997) — the move that leads to functionalism. [source: IEP-Beh §(Intentional Circle); SEP-Beh §4]
- **Objection 2 — Putnam's "super-spartans"** ("Brains and Behavior", first appeared 1963; IEP; SEP-Fun dates it 1965): imagine a community ("X-worlders"/"super-super-spartans") who "suppress all … pain behavior" by "a great effort of will" for "what they regard as important ideological reasons" (Putnam 1963: 332–334, Philosophical Papers vol. 2 pagination). They have pain without the behavioural dispositions; and "perfect pain-pretenders" (actors) show no behavioural disposition is sufficient for pain either (Block 1981). Unlike paralysis (a physically specifiable cause, which the behaviourist can build in), will and ideology are mental, so building them in is circular. [source: IEP-Beh §(Paralytics to Perfect Actors); SEP-Fun §2.3]
- **Objection 3 (to psychological behaviourism)**: Chomsky's 1959 review of *Verbal Behavior*: children's rapid language acquisition is radically underdetermined by the evidence and not taught by detailed reinforcement. Cognitive science "began to mature roughly from 1960 until 1985". [source: SEP-Beh §§4–5]
- Functionalism grew directly out of the Chisholm–Geach point: mental states defined by relations to inputs, outputs *and other mental states* (Block 1980). [source: IEP-Beh; SEP-Fun §2.3]

## 8. The identity theory
- Origin papers: U. T. Place, "Is Consciousness a Brain Process?", *British Journal of Psychology* 47 (1956): 44–50; Herbert Feigl, "The 'Mental' and the 'Physical'" (1958, Minnesota Studies in the Philosophy of Science vol. 2; reissued with a Postscript 1967); J. J. C. Smart, "Sensations and Brain Processes", *Philosophical Review* 68 (1959): 141–156. [source: SEP-Id §1 and bibliography]
- Dates: Ullin Thomas Place 24 October 1924 – 2 January 2000 (studied under Ryle at Oxford; bequeathed his brain to the University of Adelaide); J. J. C. Smart 16 September 1920 – 6 October 2012 (Chair of Philosophy at Adelaide 1950–1972); Herbert Feigl 14 December 1902 – 1 June 1988 (Vienna Circle). [source: WP-Ullin_Place; WP-J._J._C._Smart; WP-Herbert_Feigl]
- Place's paper came "after discussions at the University of Adelaide with J.J.C. Smart and C.B. Martin"; Smart, then a Rylean behaviourist, "Being unable to refute Place … soon became converted". Feigl introduced "nomological danglers". The psychologist E. G. Boring (1933) may have first used "identity theory". [source: SEP-Id §1]
- Place's model is constitution, not definition: "This table is an old packing case"; "lightning is an electric discharge"; in 1954 he wrote that objections to "consciousness is a process in the brain" are no greater than to "lightning is a motion of electric charges". "Sensation" and "brain process" differ in meaning but may share a reference (Feigl, Smart; Venus as morning and evening star). [source: SEP-Id §2]
- The identities are **contingent** and discovered empirically ("Aristotle, after all, thought that the brain was for cooling the blood"). [source: SEP-Id §2]
- **"Pain = C-fibre firing"**: early identity theorists (e.g. Smart 1959) argued it makes sense to identify pain with C-fiber stimulation; the stock example in the literature. [source: SEP-Fun §3.4] (It is a philosophers' placeholder: C-fibres are a class of peripheral nerve fibre; do not present it as real neuroscience — not further verified.)
- **Smart's simplicity argument**: Smart (1959) uses explicit appeal to Ockham's razor (dualist correlations would be "nomological danglers"), whereas Place (1956) did not. [source: SEP-Id §4]
- **Max Black's objection** (reported in Smart 1959): if "pain" and "C-fiber stimulation" differ in meaning yet co-refer, they must express different properties ("modes of presentation"), which threatens to reintroduce irreducible mental properties. Smart's reply: the properties are "topic-neutral" (a term he adapted from Ryle): e.g. "something is going on in me that is like what goes on when I see an orange", neutral between physicalism and dualism. [source: SEP-Fun §3.4; SEP-Id §3]
- **Causal-role identity theory (Lewis 1966; Armstrong 1968)**: Lewis: "The definitive characteristic of any (sort of) experience as such is its causal role, its syndrome of most typical causes and effects. But we materialists believe that these causal roles which belong by analytic necessity to experiences belong in fact to certain physical states. Since these physical states possess the definitive character of experiences, they must be experiences." Armstrong, *A Materialist Theory of the Mind* (1968), "Central State Materialism". [source: SEP-Id §4]
- David Malet Armstrong 1926–2014. [source: WP-David_Malet_Armstrong]
- **Type vs token**: the telegram "love and love and love" has two type-words and five token-words; token identity says each particular pain is a particular brain process; type identity says pain (the kind) is a brain-process kind. Functionalists accept token identities, typically deny type identities. [source: SEP-Id §6]

## 9. The multiple-realizability argument (step by step)
- Hilary Putnam, "Psychological Predicates" (1967, in Capitan and Merrill eds., *Art, Mind, and Religion*, Pittsburgh, pp. 37–48), reprinted as "The Nature of Mental States" (Putnam 1975). [source: WP-Multiple_realizability; WP-Functionalism]
- Putnam notes that humans, other primates, mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians "and even mollusks (e.g., octopi)" seem capable of pain; for the brain-state theory to be true there must be a single "physical-chemical kind" common to them all, which is biologically implausible (different neuroanatomy; convergent evolution); the theory is also meant to cover any physically possible pain-bearer (silicon androids, Martians). The early identity theory was "certainly ambitious", and almost certainly false. [source: SEP-MR §1.2]
- Canonical form (Bickle): (1) At least some mental kinds are multiply realizable by distinct physical kinds. (2) If a mental kind is multiply realizable by distinct physical kinds, it cannot be identical to any one of those physical kinds. (3) So at least some mental kinds are not identical to any specific physical kind. "In this simple form, this argument is deductively valid." [source: SEP-MR §1.2]
- **Reply 1 — domain-specific identities (Lewis 1969)**: "There is only one winning lottery number"; "the winning number is 03"; "the winning number is 61" — consistent once you add "per week", "this week", "last week". Likewise "pain is C-fibre firing *in humans*", something else *in molluscs*. Temperature is mean molecular kinetic energy in a gas, something else in a solid or plasma, yet temperature is a textbook reduction. [source: SEP-MR §2.2]
- **Kim (1989, 1992) "local reductions"**: structure-specific bridge laws ("if X is of structure type S, then X is in M iff X is in P"); Kim admits the structure types are probably narrower than species. [source: SEP-MR §2.2]
- **Reply 2 — neuroscience (Kim 1992; Bickle 1998)**: neuroscience assumes continuity of mechanisms across species (macaque visual system for human vision; fMRI localisation). [source: SEP-MR §2.3]
- **Reply 3 — Bechtel and Mundale, "Multiple Realizability Revisited: Linking Cognitive and Neural States", *Philosophy of Science* 66(2) (1999): 175–207**: MR arguments individuate mental kinds coarsely (loose input–output similarity across species) but brain states finely; "when a common grain is chosen for both, mental-neural type-identities holding across species are found"; brain mapping across species shows common realization. Counter: Aizawa contests their reading of the studies; Gillett (2003) on "dimensioned" realization. [source: SEP-MR §§2.3–2.4, 3.1; WP-Multiple_realizability]
- Shapiro (2000) dilemma: either the realizers differ in causally relevant ways (then they are not the same kind) or not (then not genuinely multiple realization). [source: SEP-MR §2.4]
- Other identity-theorist replies: disjunctive physical kinds; time-restricted identities (Braddon-Mitchell and Jackson). [source: IEP-Id]
- Upshot: multiple realizability became the main reason for functionalism and non-reductive physicalism; "Functionalism came to be seen as an improvement on the identity theory". [source: SEP-Id §6; SEP-MC §6.1]

## 10. Anomalous monism (Davidson, "Mental Events", 1970) — brief
- Three principles: Interaction (some mental events causally interact with physical events); Cause-Law (events related as cause and effect are covered by strict laws); Anomalism of the Mental (there are no strict laws on which mental events can be predicted or explained). Resolution: the covering laws must be physical, so every causally interacting mental event is token-identical to some physical event — monism without type reduction. [source: SEP-AM §1]
- Novelty: token identity derived from the *absence* of psychophysical laws, needing no empirical evidence. [source: SEP-AM intro]
- Davidson introduced "supervenience" into philosophy of mind in that paper: "[M]ental characteristics are in some sense dependent, or supervenient, on physical characteristics. Such supervenience might be taken to mean that there cannot be two events alike in all physical respects but differing in some mental respect, or that an object cannot alter in some mental respect without altering in some physical respect" (1970, 214). [source: SEP-Sup §2]
- Main objection: epiphenomenalism — events cause in virtue of physical properties, so mental properties are idle (the "freeloader" problem; Kim). IEP-Epi's soprano: a high C singing of "freedom" shatters a glass; the meaning is causally irrelevant. [source: SEP-AM §6; SEP-MC §5; IEP-Epi §3]

## 11. Functionalism
- Definition: "what makes something a thought, desire, pain (or any other type of mental state) depends not on its internal constitution, but solely on its function, or the role it plays, in the cognitive system of which it is a part"; identity fixed by causal relations "to sensory stimulations, other mental states, and behavior". Simple example: pain as the state caused by bodily injury that produces the belief something is wrong, the desire to be out of it, anxiety and (absent stronger desires) wincing or moaning. [source: SEP-Fun §1]
- Functionalism is officially neutral between materialism and dualism (a non-physical state could play the role), but attracts materialists. Being less "(species-) chauvinistic" (Block 1980) than identity theory is its selling point. [source: SEP-Fun §1]
- Antecedents: Aristotle's soul as the form/function of a body (whether this is functionalism is debated: Shields 1990, Nelson 1990); Hobbes's reasoning as "nothing but reckoning" (*Leviathan* ch. 5). [source: SEP-Fun §2.1]
- **Three strains**: *machine-state functionalism* (Putnam 1960 "Minds and Machines", 1967; mind as a probabilistic automaton/Turing machine table; Putnam later doubted it, *Representation and Reality* 1988); *analytic functionalism* (Lewis 1972 Ramsey-sentence of folk-psychological "platitudes"; Armstrong 1968; Smart 1959; Shoemaker); *psychofunctionalism* (Fodor 1968 *Psychological Explanation*; cognitive psychology's roles, e.g. "memory trace decay"). [source: SEP-Fun §3; IEP-Fun references]
- Machine-state functionalism avoids behaviourism's problem: believing it will rain is "a disposition to take one's umbrella if one looks at the weather report and is in the state of wanting to stay dry". [source: SEP-Fun §3.1]
- Lewis "Mad Pain and Martian Pain" (1980). [source: IEP-Fun references] Lewis and Armstrong are "at bottom functionalist", but identify the role-occupant with the neural state (role vs realizer functionalism). [source: SEP-Id §5; SEP-Fun §3.5]
- **Objection — Block's China brain** ("Troubles with Functionalism", Minnesota Studies in the Philosophy of Science 9 (1978): 261–325; reprinted in Block ed. 1980): the population of China is recruited to duplicate a person's functional organization for a time, receiving input from an artificial body and passing messages by radio and satellites; Block argues such a "homunculi-headed" system would have no qualitative states, so functional equivalence does not suffice for qualia ("absent qualia"). Antecedents: Anatoly Dneprov's story "The Game" (1961); Lawrence Davis (1974). [source: SEP-Fun §5.5.1; WP-China_brain]
  - Step by step: (1) If functionalism is true, any system with the right functional organization has the same mental states. (2) The China brain has the right organization. (3) The China brain has no experiences (no "what it's like" beyond its members'). (4) So functionalism is false (at least for qualitative states).
  - Replies: the intuition reflects prejudice against unfamiliar shapes, slow reaction times or spatially scattered parts (Dennett 1978; Lycan 1981; Schwitzgebel 2015); crude functional theories only (Levin 1985; Van Gulick 1989); Shoemaker: absent qualia in functional duplicates are impossible, as they would make one's own qualia unknowable. Dennett argued the China brain would have a mind. [source: SEP-Fun §5.5.1; WP-China_brain]
  - Block himself suggested qualitative states may be composites of a quale and a functional state, the quale "might be identified with a physico-chemical state" — the objection targets functionalism, not physicalism. [source: SEP-Fun §5.5.1]
  - Number caveat: SEP says China was chosen "because its size approximates the number of neurons"; today's population (~1.4 billion) is far below the ~86 billion neurons of a human brain. Avoid the "approximates" claim. [source: SEP-Fun §5.5.1; WP-China_brain caption]
- **Inverted spectrum** (cross-ref *Consciousness*): someone functionally like us whose experience of red is like our experience of green. Locke's version (*Essay* II.xxxii.15): the idea "that a Violet produced in one Man's Mind by his Eyes, were the same that a Marigold produces in another Man's, and vice versâ" could never be known; Malebranche an immediate precursor. Hardin (1988): asymmetries in colour quality space may rule out undetectable inversion in humans. [source: SEP-QI §2.2; SEP-Fun §5.5.1]
- Other objections listed by SEP: holism, mental causation (exclusion), introspection, norms of reason, qualia/explanatory gap (zombies — Kripke 1972, Chalmers 1996). [source: SEP-Fun §5]
- Searle's Chinese room: pointer to *Minds and Machines* (already sketched in *Analytic Philosophy since 1950*).

## 12. Eliminative materialism
- Thesis: "the radical claim that our ordinary, common-sense understanding of the mind is deeply wrong and that some or all of the mental states posited by common-sense do not actually exist and have no role to play in a mature science of the mind"; e.g. beliefs and desires. [source: SEP-Elim intro, §2]
- Paul Churchland, "Eliminative Materialism and the Propositional Attitudes", *Journal of Philosophy* 78 (1981): 67–90. [source: SEP-Elim bibliography] Opening sentence: "Eliminative materialism is the thesis that our common-sense conception of psychological phenomena constitutes a radically false theory, a theory so fundamentally defective that both the principles and the ontology of that theory will eventually be displaced, rather than smoothly reduced, by completed neuroscience." [source: WS-3 — first clause confirmed against a scan per search summary; the remainder not seen — use only "a radically false theory" unless checked]
- Paul Churchland born 21 October 1942, Vancouver; *Matter and Consciousness* (MIT Press, 1984). Patricia Churchland, *Neurophilosophy* (1986). Stephen Stich, *From Folk Psychology to Cognitive Science: The Case Against Belief* (1983). The term "eliminative materialism" was introduced by James Cornman (1968); precursors Feyerabend, Quine (*Word and Object* 1960), Rorty (1965 "Mind-Body Identity, Privacy, and Categories"). [source: SEP-Elim §§1–2; WP-Paul_Churchland]
- **Folk psychology as a theory ("theory-theory", Sellarsian)**: generalizations such as "If someone has the desire for X and the belief that the best way to get X is by doing Y, then (barring certain conditions) that person will tend to do Y" work like scientific laws; beliefs and desires are its theoretical posits — and theories can be false. [source: SEP-Elim §2.1]
- **Arguments**: (1) stagnation and explanatory failure: folk psychology says nothing about sleep/dreams, mental illness, memory, learning (P. M. Churchland 1981; P. S. Churchland 1986); (2) pessimistic induction: folk physics, folk biology, folk epidemiology were "radically mistaken", so "it seems wildly implausible that this one time we actually got things right" (Churchland 1981); (3) introspection is theory-laden: "introspecting" beliefs may be like "seeing" demonic spirits or celestial spheres (Churchland 1988); (4) Stich: cognitive science should use syntactic, not content-based, taxonomies; Ramsey, Stich and Garon: connectionist models may lack discrete belief-like states. [source: SEP-Elim §§3.1–3.2]
- Historical analogues used by eliminativists: phlogiston, vital force, witches. [source: WP-Eliminative_materialism; WP-Paul_Churchland]
- **Self-refutation objection** (Baker 1987, *Saving Belief*; Boghossian 1990, 1991; Reppert 1992): to assert eliminativism one must believe it, so if it is asserted there are beliefs. Step by step: (1) Asserting P requires believing P. (2) Eliminativists assert "there are no beliefs". (3) So they believe it. (4) So there are beliefs. **Reply** (P. S. Churchland 1986; Cling 1989; Devitt 1990; Ramsey 1991): the thesis "there are no beliefs" is not self-contradictory; premise (1) is itself a folk-psychological assumption, so the objection begs the question. Boghossian's subtler version: content irrealism needs truth conditions it denies; Devitt and Rey reply that this shows eliminativism implausible, not incoherent. [source: SEP-Elim §4.1]
- Other replies: folk psychology is not stagnant and has stimulated research (Greenwood 1991; Horgan and Woodward 1985); incompleteness ≠ radical falsity; folk psychology is not a theory at all (simulation theory). [source: SEP-Elim §§3.1, 4.2] Analytic functionalists add: commonsense psychology "can, over time, absorb information" (against Churchland 1981). [source: SEP-Fun §3.3]
- Dennett's "Quining Qualia" (1988): already in *Analytic Philosophy since 1950*; pointer to *Consciousness*.

## 13. Knowledge argument and zombies — pointers only
- SEP-Dual: the two most important contemporary arguments for dualism are the knowledge argument (Jackson 1982) and the conceivability argument (zombies, Chalmers 1996); the zombie argument establishes only property dualism, unlike Descartes's disembodiment argument. Kripke (1972) argued it is conceivable that a particular pain exist without the brain state it is said to be. [source: SEP-Dual §§4–4.2]
- *Analytic Philosophy since 1950* sets out Mary in five steps with Dennett, the ability hypothesis and "new knowledge, old fact"; *Possibility and Necessity* sets out the zombie argument with objection and reply. Point to *Consciousness*; do not repeat.

## 14. Physicalism, supervenience, non-reductive physicalism
- Physicalism, "in slogan form, the thesis that everything is physical". "Physicalism" was introduced in the 1930s by Otto Neurath (1931) and Rudolf Carnap (1932), then often a linguistic thesis; "materialism" appears in English in the late 17th century; the terms are now often interchangeable. [source: SEP-Phys intro, §1]
- Supervenience: "A set of properties A supervenes upon another set B just in case no two things can differ with respect to A-properties without also differing with respect to their B-properties" — "there cannot be an A-difference without a B-difference" (illustration: a perfect forger's duplicate painting). [source: SEP-Sup intro]
- History: R. M. Hare (1952) used "supervene" for moral/natural properties; G. E. Moore (1922) stated a supervenience thesis; Lloyd Morgan (1923) used "supervene" in the emergentists' different sense; Davidson (1970) brought it to centre stage in philosophy of mind. [source: SEP-Sup §2]
- Non-reductive physicalism: mental properties are not physical but are "realized" or "constituted" by physical properties; it grew out of functionalism and multiple realizability; it is the main target of Kim's exclusion argument. [source: SEP-MC §§6–6.1; IEP-Excl §1]
- No physicalist "since before Smart (1959)" has held that mental predicates are analytically equivalent to physical ones; Nagel-style bridge-law reduction is blocked by multiple realization (Fodor 1974; contra Kim 1993). [source: SEP-Phys §3.1]
- **Hempel's dilemma** (Hempel 1969): define "physical" by current physics and physicalism is false (physics is incomplete); define it by a future ideal physics and it is trivial or empty (who knows what it will contain — perhaps mental items). [source: SEP-Phys §4.3]

## 15. Emergentism (brief)
- British Emergentists: G. H. Lewes (1875) first used "emergence"; J. S. Mill, *A System of Logic* (1843), "On the Composition of Causes": "heteropathic" laws of organised phenomena supplement basic laws; Samuel Alexander (1920): life a "new quality" to be accepted with "natural piety"; C. D. Broad, *The Mind and Its Place in Nature* (1925), the most developed form. [source: SEP-Emer §1]
- Broad: "the characteristic properties of the whole R(A, B, C) … cannot, even in theory, be deduced from the most complete knowledge of the properties of A, B, and C in isolation or in other wholes which are not of the form R(A, B, C)" (1925: 61); emergent features are "completely determined" by lower-level ones (1925: 64); "trans-ordinal" laws are "unique and ultimate". [source: SEP-Emer §1]
- Broad's example of salt: as far as was known, the properties of common salt cannot be deduced from those of sodium and chlorine in isolation (Broad 1937, cited by Smart). [source: SEP-Id intro]
- Decline: quantum chemistry and molecular biology eroded the levels (McLaughlin 1992: "there is not a scintilla of evidence" for fundamental emergent forces); revival since the 1970s via non-linear complex systems; emergentists deny causal closure. [source: SEP-Emer §§1, 4; SEP-MC §2.4]
- Weak vs strong emergence (O'Connor). Physicalists deny "strong emergence" (Smart). [source: SEP-Emer §3; SEP-Id intro]
- Indian link: Adamson and Ganeri (*Classical Indian Philosophy*, 2020) compare the Cārvākas to Mill's emergentism, saying Mill "sounds like a follower of Bṛhaspati". [source: WP-Charvaka] (*Jains, Ājīvikas and Materialists* already says "see *The Mind–Body Problem*" here, so this chapter must pick it up.)

## 16. Hylomorphism: Aristotle's third way (cross-ref *Aristotle*)
- Analogy: soul : body :: form : matter :: Hermes-shape : bronze. *De Anima* II.1, 412b6–9: "It is not necessary to ask whether soul and body are one, just as it is not necessary to ask whether the wax and its shape are one, nor generally whether the matter of each thing and that of which it is the matter are one. For even if one and being are spoken of in several ways, what is properly so spoken of is the actuality" (Shields's translation). Soul is "a first actuality of a natural organic body" (412b5–6). [source: SEP-Ari §§2–3]
- Aristotle does not identify soul and body (he denies it, 412a17; 414a1–20); the soul is generally not separable (413a3–5), but some parts may be, being the actuality of no body part (413a6–7) — hence his special treatment of intellect (*nous*). [source: SEP-Ari §3]
- Shields: hylomorphism "embraces neither reductive materialism nor Platonic dualism", steering a middle course and showing the two are "not exhaustive options". [source: SEP-Ari §3]
- Aristotle argued the intellect is immaterial because a bodily organ is sensitive only to certain things, whereas intellect can receive all forms (*De Anima* 429a10–b9); whether it can exist without the body is debated. [source: SEP-Dual §2.2]
- Modern "hylomorphic dualists" (Oderberg 2005; Aquinas's soul as an incomplete, immaterial substance; Feser 2006): they say interaction is no more mysterious than form–matter relations throughout nature; but this does not answer the causal-closure version of the problem. [source: SEP-Dual §3.6]
- Functionalism claims Aristotle as an ancestor (form of an axe is what enables it to cut). [source: SEP-Fun §2.1] (*Aristotle* chapter already says hylomorphism "cuts across the debate in *The Mind–Body Problem*".)

## 17. Indian views
- Overview (SEP-Dual §2.1): Sāṃkhya and Vaiśeṣika articulate detailed dualist theories, developed by Yoga and Nyāya; Mīmāṃsā presupposes dualism; Vedānta has dualist and idealist sub-schools; Jainism a distinctive dualism (the soul fills only its body, like a lamp's light filling a room); Buddhism various theories; Cārvāka materialism. Sāṃkhya/Yoga's *puruṣa* is pure awareness, whereas Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika's self (*ātman*) has consciousness, volition and cognition; all four make the individual self all-pervading. [source: SEP-Dual §2.1]
- **Sāṃkhya**: Īśvarakṛṣṇa's *Sāṃkhya-kārikā* (c. 350 CE, 72 verses); "a strong Indian example of metaphysical dualism, but unlike many Western counterparts it is atheistic"; two kinds of eternal entity, one *prakṛti* (Nature, unconscious but purposeful) and many *puruṣas* (persons, pure consciousness, inactive). [source: IEP-Sam intro, §§1–3]
- **Mind is on the material side**: from root nature evolve intellect (*buddhi*), then ego (*ahaṃkāra*), then the eleven powers (*indriya*, including *manas*, mind) and five subtle elements, then the gross elements. *Buddhi* is "the locus of cognition, emotion, moral judgment and volition" on the material plane; *manas* coordinates the senses and translates the intellect's commands into actions. [source: IEP-Sam §4]
- Ruzsa: drop the *puruṣa* and "we would still have a fairly complete picture of the world"; Sāṃkhya "looks like a full materialist account of the world, with the passive, unchanging principle of consciousness added almost as an afterthought". [source: IEP-Sam §3] (Already quoted in *Classical Indian Schools*.)
- **Contrast with Descartes (writer's synthesis, flag as such)**: Descartes puts thought, judgement and will on the immaterial side; Sāṃkhya puts all of these in matter and reserves for spirit only witnessing consciousness — so the line between the two substances falls in a different place. Supported by: Descartes's mind includes understanding, willing, imagining and sensing (AT VII 28) [source: IEP-DescMB §4]; Sāṃkhya's *buddhi* etc. are material [source: IEP-Sam §4].
- Sāṃkhya's own interaction problem: "How can the inactive soul influence matter, and how could an unintelligent substance, nature, serve anybody's purpose?" Similes: as unconscious milk flows to nourish the calf, *prakṛti* flows for *puruṣa*; later, illumination/reflection (puruṣa illuminates, prakṛti reflects). [source: IEP-Sam §4] SEP-NatI: *buddhi* reflects pure consciousness "just as the moon lightens up the world by borrowing the reflected light of the sun". [source: SEP-NatI §2.4]
- **Nyāya self (ātman)**: *Nyāya-sūtra* 1.1.10 lists desire, aversion, volition, pleasure, pain and cognition as marks of the self. Argument step by step (Dasti): (1) Desire, cognition, pleasure, pain, volition are qualities. (2) Every quality inheres in a substance. (3) So there is a substance to which they belong. (4) None of the material elements (earth, water …) bears them. (5) So there is a special non-material substance, the self. [source: IEP-Nya §(substance/self)] Diachronic argument: recollection ("that is the very man I saw a week ago") requires one locus across time; Uddyotakara after Vātsyāyana: if I were a different self from yesterday's "me" I could not recollect its experiences, "since one self is unable to recollect the content of another's experience". Synchronic: cross-modal recognition ("that thing I see is the same thing I am touching") needs one experiencer (NS 3.1.1–3). Udayana's *Determining the Truth of the Self* is the apex. [source: IEP-Nya] Buddhist reply: causal continuity within a stream suffices. [source: IEP-Nya] (*Classical Indian Schools* already gives this with Chadha; cross-reference and go to *Self and No-Self*.)
- **Nyāya *manas***: NS 1.1.16 — the absence of simultaneous cognition through all the senses shows a faculty of selective attention; *manas* is "an insentient psychological apparatus which processes the information of the senses", also governing memory retrieval and apperception. Perception is a chain: self–manas, manas–sense organ, sense organ–object (Vaiśeṣika-sūtra 3.1.18). [source: IEP-Nya §(perception)] Nyāya–Vaiśeṣika: mind (*manas*) "is also said to be of corporeal nature and atomic in magnitude though lacking in sensible quality"; consciousness is an adventitious quality of the self, present only when embodied. [source: SEP-NatI §§2.1, 2.5]
- **Cārvāka materialism** (cross-ref *Jains, Ājīvikas and Materialists*): consciousness emerges from the four elements combined in the right proportion though none has it alone, "like emergence of red colour from the chewing of beetle-nut and certain other ingredients, none of which contains red colour"; the self "is nothing but a conscious body". Most interpreters call this reductionist; Ganeri (2012, 30–31) reads it as non-reductive supervenience. [source: SEP-NatI §2.5] (SEP spells "beetle-nut"; correct spelling is betel.)
- The **intoxication analogy**: the *Bṛhaspati-sūtra* fragments (Bhattacharya 2002) — *pṛthivy āpas tejo vāyur iti tattvāni* / *tebhyaś caitanyam* / *kiṇvādibhyo madaśaktivat*, glossed "earth, water, fire, air are the principles; from them consciousness; like the power of intoxication from ferment (*kiṇva*) etc." [source: earlier session's WS, recorded in `research/ancient--indian-dissenters.md` — second-hand, not re-verified] Mādhava's *Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha* (14th c.), Cowell and Gough translation (in *The Pandit* 1874–78; Trübner's Oriental Series 1882): "In this school the four elements, earth, &c., are the original principles; from these alone, when transformed into the body, intelligence is produced", followed by a comparison with the intoxicating power that arises from mixing certain ingredients. [source: WS-6 — first sentence verbatim per summary; the analogy clause not seen verbatim]
- Cārvāka arguments that body is the substratum of consciousness (IEP via K. K. Mittal 1974): "Our observation does not bring forth any instance of a disincarnate consciousness"; sensation and perception arise only as conditioned by the body; medicine shows certain foods and drinks (e.g. Brāhmighṛta) improve intellect. Two sub-views: no self apart from body; or a material (air/breath) soul that perishes with the body. Critics: "the soul cannot be known" does not entail "the soul does not exist". [source: IEP-Mat §3]
- SEP-NatI: "Only Cārvāka hints at the causal closure of the physical world" among Indian schools, whose models of causation otherwise allow matter–consciousness interaction. [source: SEP-NatI §2]
- **Buddhist views** (cross-ref *The Buddha and Early Buddhist Philosophy*, *Mahāyāna Philosophy*): Buddhist theories "do not associate mental phenomena with the activity of a substantive, independent, and enduring self"; human beings are "reducible to the physical and psychological constituents and processes which comprise them" (not-self). The Buddhist "middle way" rejects both eternalism (an abiding self) and annihilationism ("physicalism"). The Abhidharma analysis of materiality "does not imply physicalism … in the sense that everything is or supervenes on the physical"; mental factors (*caitasika*) are not reducible to their physical substrate. Key notions: mind-stream (*citta-santāna*), life-continuum, store consciousness. [source: SEP-BudM intro, §§2–5]
- SEP-Dual classes Abhidharma as a dualism of a distinctive kind: fleeting atom-events (*dharmas*) of fundamentally mental and fundamentally physical kinds, with no enduring soul. [source: SEP-Dual §2.1] (Debate: dualism of events (Robinson) vs. reductionism about persons (Coseru).)
- Guifeng Zongmi (780–841), Tang Buddhist, argued for the fundamentality of awareness: "the qi of Heaven and Earth is fundamentally lacking in awareness. If humans are endowed with qi that lacks awareness, how can awareness suddenly arise? Grass and trees are endowed with the same qi, so why do they not have awareness." (*On Humanity*, as quoted in SEP). [source: SEP-Dual §2.1] — useful bridge to the Chinese section; translator not named by SEP.
- Yogācāra ("mind only"; eight consciousnesses; store consciousness) — already in *Mahāyāna Philosophy*; a pointer suffices (it is idealism/anti-realism about external objects, not a mind–body dualism).

## 18. Chinese: *xin* (heart-mind) — brief
- *Xin* 心 originated as a picture of the heart; because it is credited both with thinking (*si*) and with affective states it is often translated "heart-mind". [source: SEP-Chin intro]
- "The dominant view in scholarship is that Chinese thought is not dualistic in any fashion"; a minority holds other forms of dualism may fairly be attributed. Holists: Roger Ames ("bodyheartminding", with *qi* as the medium), François Jullien (soul-like phenomena are refined *qi*, bone denser *qi*). Mencius: the will "directs" the body's *qi* (Mengzi 2A8); the heart-mind is "the part of a person's qi that has the function of guiding the rest of its qi". [source: SEP-Chin §1]
- **Debate**: Paul Goldin (2003, 2015) and Edward Slingerland (2013, "Body and Mind in Early China", *Journal of the American Academy of Religion* 81(1): 6–55) point to *hun* and *po* souls, *shen* "spirit", Zhuangzi passages (the piglets who leave their dead mother because they loved "what moved" her body, not its shape) as evidence of folk dualism. [source: SEP-Chin §1.2]
- SEP-Dual: early Chinese texts "regularly presuppose dualism (Slingerland 2013)" but are "less preoccupied with the mind-body problem" than India and Greece. [source: SEP-Dual §2]

## 19. Islamic views (cross-ref *Falsafa*, *Al-Ghazālī, Averroes*)
- *Falsafa* already sets out Avicenna's flying (floating) man in five steps with Marmura's objection and Kaukua/Adamson–Benevich replies, and the comparison to the cogito. Here: present it only as a further dualist argument from self-awareness without bodily awareness, structurally like Descartes's (and open to the same objection: unawareness of one's brain does not show one is not one's brain — the "masked man"). Avicenna c. 970/980–1037 (SEP gives ca. 970–1037 and 980–1037 in different entries). [source: SEP-IbnS; SEP-IbnSN; published *Falsafa*]
- SEP-Dual calls the floating man "sometimes regarded as a precursor to similar thought experiments in Descartes". [source: SEP-Dual §2.3]
- Al-Ghazālī (c. 1056–1111): in the *Incoherence* he holds some philosophical teachings (e.g. on the human soul) may be "sound and true" but "not demonstrated"; he condemns as unbelief the teaching that "after death the souls of humans will never again return into bodies" (denial of bodily resurrection). [source: SEP-Ghaz §§2–3] (Not verified this session: the specific 18th discussion on the soul's immateriality — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Ashʿarite kalām: all bodies are atoms bearing momentary accidents; "A person's thoughts, for instance, are considered accidents that inhere in the atoms of the person's brain, while his or her faith is an accident inhering in the atoms of the heart." A strikingly non-dualist (atomist) psychology within Islamic theology. [source: SEP-Ghaz §(occasionalism)]
- Early Abrahamic not straightforwardly dualist: Tertullian and Ibn Gabirol (soul in some sense material); Hobbes perhaps the first to combine an Abrahamic worldview with an unambiguous materialism (Weir 2022). [source: SEP-Dual §2.3]

## 20. Other history useful for the narrative
- Ancient Greek dualism: Plato (Meno, Phaedo, Phaedrus; affinity argument Phaedo 78b–84b); Epicurus after Democritus: the soul is atoms that disperse at death (Letter to Herodotus); Stoics: soul as *pneuma*. [source: SEP-Dual §2.2]
- Upaniṣads (c. 700–500 BCE): the soul (*ātman*) unchanging and imperishable; *Bhagavad Gītā* 2.12–18 ("The body is mortal, but that which dwells in the body is immortal and immeasurable", translation as quoted by SEP, translator not named). [source: SEP-Dual §2.1]
- Lovejoy (1930) called the preceding quarter-century "the Age of the Great Revolt against Dualism". Materialism came to predominate only after Place (1956) and Smart (1959). Dualist neuroscientists: Sherrington (1951), Penfield (1975), Eccles (1994); philosophers Plantinga, Swinburne, Kripke, Popper. [source: SEP-Dual §2.4]
- Women: Elisabeth; Anne Conway's monist argument against Cartesian dualism; Cavendish's thinking matter — already set out in *Women Philosophers of the Early Modern Era*; Poulain de la Barre and Astell used Cartesian dualism for women's equality ("the mind has no sex") — in *Descartes*. Cross-reference; in the modern period cite Patricia Churchland, Martine Nida-Rümelin ("subject-body dualism"), Janet Levin (SEP functionalism), Lisa Shapiro.
- Martine Nida-Rümelin (2012) defends a unitary immaterial subject that is not independent: she prefers "subject-body dualism". [source: SEP-Dual §3.1]

## Debates
- **Is Ryle a logical behaviourist?** Graham (SEP-Beh) lists him as the analytical behaviourist; Tanney (SEP-Ryle) says he subscribes to none of its tenets and attacked the hiddenness of inner processes, physical or ghostly. [source: SEP-Beh §2; SEP-Ryle]
- **Was Descartes an interactionist?** Textbook reading yes; Lokhorst lists alternative interpretations, and Descartes declined to inquire into the mind's power to move bodies in the *Principles*. [source: SEP-Pin] Skirry reads the union scholastically (act–potency). [source: IEP-DescMB §5]
- **Elisabeth's own position**: open but noncommittal (Shapiro) vs naturalistic dualism (Janssen-Lauret). [source: SEP-Eli §4.2]
- **Substance vs property dualism**: early-21st-century orthodoxy favoured property dualism; Chalmers now leans to substance dualism; Weir, Lycan, Zimmerman and others challenge the preference. [source: SEP-Dual §§2.4, 3.2]
- **Is the exclusion argument sound, and does it generalize?** Kim vs Block, Fodor, Baker (generalization/causal drainage), Yablo, Bennett, Pereboom (inheritance/autonomy). [source: SEP-MC §6; IEP-Excl §§3, 5]
- **How real is multiple realization?** Putnam, Fodor vs Lewis, Kim, Bickle, Bechtel and Mundale, Shapiro; Aizawa and Gillett defend MR. [source: SEP-MR]
- **Is the China brain a counterexample?** Block vs Dennett, Lycan, Schwitzgebel; Shoemaker on absent vs inverted qualia. [source: SEP-Fun §5.5.1; WP-China_brain]
- **Is eliminativism self-refuting?** Baker, Boghossian vs P. S. Churchland, Devitt, Rey, Ramsey. [source: SEP-Elim §4.1]
- **Is Cārvāka emergentism reductive?** Most interpreters: reductive; Ganeri: non-reductive supervenience; Adamson and Ganeri compare Mill. [source: SEP-NatI §2.5; WP-Charvaka]
- **Was early Chinese thought dualist?** Holists (Ames, Jullien; "dominant view") vs Slingerland and Goldin. [source: SEP-Chin §1]
- **Is Aristotle a functionalist?** Shields 1990 vs Nelson 1990. [source: SEP-Fun §2.1]
- **Is Abhidharma Buddhism a dualism?** SEP-Dual says a distinctive dualism of mental and physical *dharmas*; Coseru stresses reduction of persons to psycho-physical processes and denies Abhidharma materiality implies physicalism. [source: SEP-Dual §2.1; SEP-BudM]
