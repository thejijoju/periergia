## 1. Berkeley: life

- Born 1685 near Kilkenny, Ireland (SEP-B). WP gives 12 March 1685, at the family home, Dysart Castle, near Thomastown, County Kilkenny; eldest son of William Berkeley. [source: SEP-B §1; WP-George_Berkeley]
- Schooled at Kilkenny College; entered Trinity College Dublin at 15; made a fellow in 1707 (three years after graduating) and ordained in the Anglican Church shortly after. WP: Scholar 1702, BA 1704, MA and junior fellowship 1707. [source: SEP-B §1; WP-George_Berkeley]
- Trinity's curriculum was "notably modern": there he met the new science and philosophy (Descartes, Locke, Malebranche, Newton, Hobbes). His philosophical notebooks (*Philosophical Commentaries*), begun 1707, show immaterialism emerging. [source: SEP-B §1]
- Works: *An Essay Towards a New Theory of Vision* (1709); *Treatise concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge* (1710); *Passive Obedience* (1712); *Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous* (1713); *De Motu* (composed 1720, WP: published 1721); *Alciphron* (1732, Christian apologetics against "free-thinkers", written in America); *Theory of Vision … Vindicated and Explained* (1733); *The Analyst* (1734, critique of the foundations of Newton's calculus); *The Querist* (from 1735; numbered questions, proposing an Irish national bank); *Siris* (1744). He published the *Principles* and *Dialogues* "in his mid-twenties". [source: SEP-B §1; IEP-B; WP-George_Berkeley]
- 1714–20: travels in Europe; 1720, completing a four-year tour as tutor, he wrote *De Motu*. Returned to Trinity until 1724, when appointed Dean of Derry. [source: SEP-B §1]
- Bermuda project: a college in Bermuda (WP: for training ministers and missionaries; SEP-B-act: one aim was to educate and convert Native Americans). He secured a charter and promises of parliamentary funding, married Anne Forster (WP: 1 August 1728, St Mary le Strand, London), and sailed in 1728. They spent three years in Newport, Rhode Island (WP: farm "Whitehall" in Middletown), waiting for money that never came; political support collapsed and they returned in 1731 (SEP-B). WP says he left America in 1732 — **sources differ (1731/1732)**; WRITER: "returned to Britain in 1731" (SEP) or "after three years". [source: SEP-B §1; WP-George_Berkeley]
- **Slaveholding (verified):** "while in Newport, [Berkeley] purchased at least three enslaved people to work in his household and on his farm"; he "did not see a fundamental moral problem in enslaving Africans and kidnapping Native American children", and he "explicitly raises the possibility of kidnapping Native American children for his school". He also "explicitly disclaims some forms of racism". [source: SEP-B-act, intro to Supplement B] WP: "purchased several enslaved Africans" (citing Yale, Slavery & Abolition). [source: WP-George_Berkeley]
- In *The Querist* (Queries 576–580) he proposed that "sturdy Beggars" be "seized and made Slaves to the Public, for a certain Term of Years" (Q 579) — "temporary Servitude" as "the best Cure for Idleness and Beggary" (Q 577). SEP-B-act links his attitudes to enslaved Africans and Native Americans to his disdain for the impoverished Catholic Irish majority. [source: SEP-B-act §B.3]
- Trinity College Dublin: in April 2023 the board decided to remove Berkeley's name from its main library, citing his ownership of enslaved people and his promotion of pro-slavery ideas, after a Legacies Review consultation; his work continues to be taught; in October 2024 the library was renamed after the poet Eavan Boland. [source: WS-10 (Irish Times 26 Apr 2023 and 9 Oct 2024; Inside Higher Ed); WP-George_Berkeley]
- Berkeley gave his Rhode Island farm and many books to Yale (WP, citing Hoeveler). [source: WP-George_Berkeley] (Single source; optional.)
- Bishop of Cloyne (Church of Ireland) from 1734 (nominated 18 Jan, consecrated 19 May 1734) until death. [source: SEP-B §1; WP-George_Berkeley]
- *Siris* (1744): "his last, strangest, and best-selling (in his own lifetime) philosophical work", aiming to establish tar-water ("a liquid prepared by letting pine tar stand in water") as a medical panacea, give it scientific backing, and lead the mind to contemplation of God. [source: SEP-B §1]
- Died 1753 (WP: 14 January 1753) in Oxford, shortly after moving there to supervise his son George's education; buried in Christ Church Cathedral, Oxford. SEP: three of his seven children survived childhood; WP: four survived infancy and at least two others did not — **sources differ**; WRITER: avoid the count. [source: SEP-B §1; WP-George_Berkeley]
- Pronounced "BARK-lee"; the city and University of California, Berkeley (pronounced "BURK-lee") were named after him (naming suggested 1866), inspired by his line "Westward the course of empire takes its way" (WP; line wording not separately verified). [source: WP-George_Berkeley]
- Reception: "many of Berkeley's first readers greeted him with incomprehension"; contemporaries "for the most part regarded him as a purveyor of skeptical paradoxes"; "he influenced both Hume and Kant, and is much read (if little followed) in our own day". [source: SEP-B intro, §2.2.2]

## 2. Berkeley: idealism and the case against matter

- Idealism = "the view that reality consists exclusively of minds and their ideas". Berkeley's target, "materialism", means "the doctrine that material things exist" (not that only matter exists): he attacks Cartesian and Lockean dualism, not just Hobbes. What he denies is mind-independent things. [source: SEP-B intro, §2]
- **esse est percipi**: SEP: the famous phrase is "esse est percipi (aut percipere)—to be is to be perceived (or to perceive), though Berkeley himself used the anglicized Latin *esse is percipi*". The text, *Principles* §3, of unthinking things: "Their esse is percipi, nor is it possible they should have any existence, out of the minds or thinking things which perceive them." For ideas, and so for the physical world, esse est percipi; spirits (minds) are perceivers, not perceived. [source: SEP-B §2, §3.1.1; WS-1 (celt.ucc.ie text of PHK §3); IEP-B]
- Motivation: materialism breeds scepticism (our senses would mislead us about bodies, which need not exist) and atheism (a material world could run without God). [source: SEP-B §2]
- **Argument 1, the core argument (PHK 4; reconstruction after Winkler 1989, 138):** (1) We perceive ordinary objects (houses, mountains, etc.). (2) We perceive only ideas. Therefore (3) ordinary objects are ideas. Valid; (2) is taken from the "idea-theoretic" tradition of Descartes and Locke. Objection (representationalist): distinguish mediate from immediate perception — we immediately perceive only ideas, but mediately perceive material things that the ideas represent; then (3) does not follow. [source: SEP-B §2.1.1]
- **Reply 1, the Likeness Principle (PHK 8):** representation would have to be by resemblance, but "an idea can be like nothing but an idea; a colour or figure can be like nothing but another colour or figure." Support (PC 377): "Two things cannot be said to be alike or unlike till they have been compar'd" — and we can compare only ideas. Consequence: matter is "if not an incoherent notion, at best a completely empty one". [source: SEP-B §2.1.2]
- **Reply 2, anti-abstractionism:** (Introduction to the *Principles*) we cannot form abstract general ideas by stripping particular qualities from an idea (Locke's apparent account); notions like "being" or "mere extension" invoked to characterise matter are objectionably abstract. [source: SEP-B §2.1.3; IEP-B]
- **Reply 3, matter explains nothing (PHK 18–19):** even granting external bodies, materialists "own themselves unable to comprehend in what manner body can act upon spirit"; so the production of ideas "can be no reason why we should suppose matter". Locke himself admitted (Essay 4.3.6) that motion can produce only motion. [source: SEP-B §2.1.4]
- **Against the primary/secondary distinction (First Dialogue):** Philonous argues (a) heat is not distinct from pain, which is in the mind; (b) relativity arguments — what is hot to one hand is cold to the other, sweet to one is bitter to another, microscopes change visual qualities — so sensible qualities cannot belong to mind-independent objects; picking the "true" ones would be arbitrary anthropocentrism. Against Hylas's retreat to Locke's primary qualities: (1) the same relativity arguments apply to primary qualities; (2) we cannot abstract shape from colour, so we cannot conceive bodies extended but not coloured. [source: SEP-B §2.2.2]
- **The "Master Argument" (PHK 22–23; 3D 200):** "I am content to put the whole upon this issue; if you can but conceive it possible for one extended moveable substance, or in general, for any one idea or any thing like an idea, to exist otherwise than in a mind perceiving it, I shall readily give up the cause…" Imagining trees in a park with no one by is just "framing in your mind certain ideas which you call books and trees, and at the same time omitting to frame the idea of any one that may perceive them" — "to make out this, it is necessary that you conceive them existing unconceived or unthought of, which is a manifest repugnancy." Objection (Pitcher 1977): it conflates what we conceive *with* (a representation, which is thought of) and what we conceive *of*; "(as many commentators have observed), this argument fails". Charitable reading (Winkler 1989; Lennon 1988): we cannot represent unconceivedness — but this presupposes representationalism. The name "Master Argument" is a later coinage. [source: SEP-B §2.2.1]
- Positive ontology: ordinary objects are "bundles or collections of ideas" (an apple = colour, taste, smell, figure, consistence "observed to go together", PHK 1); there is "no material world" but "a physical world". Spirits are active, ideas passive. God, an infinite spirit, causes our sensory ideas: argument by elimination (PHK 25–29): not ideas (passive), not me (sensory ideas are involuntary), so another spirit, whose wisdom and benevolence the order of nature shows. [source: SEP-B §3.1]
- Real vs imaginary: real things are involuntary, steadier, more vivid and, above all, ordered by "the Laws of Nature" (PHK 30). Science discovers regularities ("signs, rather than causes", PHK 108). "We ought to think with the learned, and speak with the vulgar" (PHK 51). [source: SEP-B §3.2.1, §3.2.3]
- **The "unperceived tree" objection and replies:** *Principles* 3 gives a counterfactual reading ("if I was in my study I might perceive it, or that some other spirit actually does perceive it"); objection (McCracken 1979): with esse est percipi the desk would "pop in and out of existence". *Three Dialogues* (212, 214–5) instead says unperceived things exist in God's mind — problems: God has no sensations (3D 206), God's ideas are eternal, God has ideas of all possible objects; McCracken's alternative: continued existence as God's decrees (cf. creation, 3D 253). [source: SEP-B §3.2.4]
- Objection that spirits are as unperceivable as matter (Hylas, 3D 232: "you must either admit matter or reject spirit"). Berkeley's reply: we have a "notion" of spirit through reflection on ourselves (3D 231–3; PHK 139–40). In the notebooks he had considered a Hume-like view: "Mind is a congeries of Perceptions. Take away Perceptions & you take away the Mind put the Perceptions & you put the mind" (PC 580). [source: SEP-B §3.2 (spirits)]
- *New Theory of Vision*: the "heterogeneity thesis" (ideas of sight and touch are distinct) and a response to Molyneux's question. [source: SEP-B §4.1 (headings and grep only)]
- Shepherd's critique of Berkeley: she faults him "for holding that sensible objects are only ideas, not for holding that they are ideas" (EPEU 1827). [source: SEP-MS §2.1]

### Objections from outside
- **Johnson's stone (verified, Boswell):** Boswell's *Life of Samuel Johnson* (1791), under 1763 (WS: 6 August 1763): "After we came out of the church, we stood talking for some time together of Bishop Berkeley's ingenious sophistry to prove the non-existence of matter, and that every thing in the universe is merely ideal. I observed, that though we are satisfied his doctrine is not true, it is impossible to refute it. I never shall forget the alacrity with which Johnson answered, striking his foot with mighty force against a large stone, till he rebounded from it, 'I refute it *thus*.'" (Wording per WP-Appeal_to_the_stone, citing Boswell, Great Books ed. 1952 p. 134; WP-George_Berkeley has "untrue" and "I shall never forget" — minor variants; quote the shorter core "I refute it thus".) Commentators (Hallett 1947; Patey 1986) debate whether Johnson missed Berkeley's point (Berkeley never denied stones are solid, only that they are mind-independent). [source: WP-Appeal_to_the_stone; WP-George_Berkeley; WS-2]
- **The limerick:** "There was a young man who said God, / must think it exceedingly odd / if he finds that the tree / continues to be / when no one's about in the Quad." Reply: "Dear Sir, your astonishment's odd / I am always about in the Quad / And that's why the tree / continues to be / since observed by, Yours faithfully, God." (SEP text; SEP's note 18 sources both to Fleming 1985.) Attribution of the first to Ronald Knox (1888–1957) is long-standing but secondary sources only; the reply is anonymous (sometimes "probably" Knox). It is a twentieth-century joke, not an eighteenth-century objection. [source: SEP-B §3.2.4; SEP-B-notes n.18; WS-3]

## 3. Hume: life

- Born David Home, 7 May 1711 (26 April Old Style), in a tenement on Edinburgh's Lawnmarket; second son; changed the spelling to "Hume" in 1734. Died 25 August 1776 in Edinburgh's New Town (St Andrew Square area); never married. [source: WP-David_Hume; SEP-H §1 ("born in Edinburgh")]
- Studied at the University of Edinburgh from about age 11 (SEP: 11 to 15; WP: "either 12 or possibly as young as 10"); did not graduate; family wanted law. Breakdown in 1729 ("disease of the learned"), took years to recover. [source: SEP-H §1; WP-David_Hume]
- Brief merchant's clerkship via Bristol, then 1734 to La Flèche (Anjou, France), where "at the tender age of 23" he began the *Treatise*; he talked with Jesuits of the Royal College there. [source: SEP-H §1; WP-David_Hume]
- *A Treatise of Human Nature*, subtitle "Being an Attempt to introduce the experimental Method of Reasoning into Moral Subjects": Books 1–2 published anonymously 1739; Book 3 and the anonymous *Abstract* 1740. [source: SEP-H §1, §2.1; WP-David_Hume]
- "Dead-born": "My Own Life" (written April 1776): "Never literary attempt was more unfortunate than my Treatise of Human Nature. It fell dead-born from the press, without reaching such distinction as even to excite a murmur among the zealots." SEP calls this "an exaggeration": it sold poorly but did provoke hostile reaction branding him atheist and sceptic. [source: WS-12 (Econlib text; NLS 1829 scan); WP-David_Hume; SEP-H §1]
- Failed chairs: Edinburgh Chair of Ethics and Pneumatical Philosophy, 1745 (public opposition; Hume's anonymous *Letter from a Gentleman to his Friend in Edinburgh*; chair went to William Cleghorn); Chair of Logic at Glasgow, 1751 (even Adam Smith, per WP, opposed his appointment for fear of public opinion). "He would never find academic employment." [source: SEP-H §1; WP-David_Hume]
- *Essays, Moral and Political* 1741–42 (better received); *Philosophical Essays concerning Human Understanding* 1748, retitled *An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding* in 1758; *Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals* 1751, "incomparably the best" of his works (MOL); *Political Discourses* 1752; *Four Dissertations* 1757 (incl. *Natural History of Religion*, "Of the Standard of Taste"). [source: SEP-H §1]
- Librarian of the Advocates Library, Edinburgh, from 1752; *History of England*, 6 vols, 1754–62, a best-seller that made him wealthy; in his lifetime he was known chiefly as historian and essayist. [source: SEP-H §1; WP-David_Hume]
- Secretary to the British ambassador (Lord Hertford) in Paris, 1763–65, chargé d'affaires 1765; adulation of the Parisian literati. [source: SEP-H §1; WP-David_Hume]
- Rousseau: in January 1766 Hume left Paris to bring Jean-Jacques Rousseau to England as a refugee; they fell out ("Rousseau's paranoia would not spare his gracious host"); Hume published an account of the dispute. (SEP's "Returning to Edinburgh in 1766" is loose: WP has Hume in England with Rousseau in 1766, Under-Secretary of State, Northern Department, 1767, back in Edinburgh 1769.) [source: SEP-H §1; WP-David_Hume]
- Disowned the *Treatise*: a 1775 "Advertisement" asked that the *Essays and Treatises* "alone be regarded as containing his philosophical sentiments and principles" — aimed at Reid and Beattie. [source: SEP-H §1]
- Diagnosed with terminal abdominal cancer 1775; arranged posthumous publication of the *Dialogues concerning Natural Religion* (worked on from the early 1750s; WP: begun 1750, completed 1776), which appeared in 1779 (WP: published by his nephew David Hume the Younger, with neither author's nor publisher's name). "My Own Life" was first published by Adam Smith. [source: SEP-H §1; WP-Dialogues_Concerning_Natural_Religion; WP-David_Hume]
- All his works were placed on the Catholic Index of Prohibited Books in 1761 (WP). [source: WP-David_Hume] (single source)
- Kant, *Prolegomena* (1783): the "recollection" (Beck) / "remembrance" (Hatfield) of Hume "first interrupted my dogmatic slumber". [source: WP-David_Hume; WS-9] (Exact English wording varies by translation; paraphrase.)
- Slavery entanglement (debate): a 1766 letter (published by Felix Waldmann 2014) passed a plantation-investment proposal (Dominica) to Lord Hertford, and Hume lent £400 to John Stewart; Waldmann reads this as involvement; Hutton & Ashton (2023) and Bailey (2024) dispute that Hume encouraged or profited; Fieser (2022) thinks the letter reads as encouragement. In September 2020 Edinburgh University renamed the David Hume Tower "40 George Square". [source: WP-David_Hume] (WP only; use cautiously.)

## 4. Hume: theory of ideas

- All mental contents are "perceptions", divided into impressions and ideas, the difference being that between feeling and thinking (T 1.1.1.1); impressions differ by greater "force or vivacity" (T 1.1.1.3). Impressions of sensation vs of reflection (passions, desires). Simple vs complex. [source: SEP-H §2.1]
- **Copy Principle:** all simple ideas are exactly resembling (less vivacious) copies of simple impressions (T 1.1.2.1; EHU 2.5). [source: SEP-H §2.1; SEP-IP §1]
- **Missing shade of blue** (EHU 2.8): a person acquainted with all colours "except one particular shade of blue … will perceive a blank, where that shade is wanting". Hume concedes the counterexample but carries on; commentators (Fogelin 1984; Garrett 1985) say it is unusual in involving gradation and does not affect his uses of the principle. [source: SEP-H §2.1]
- Association of ideas by resemblance, contiguity in time or place, and causation (T 1.1.4.1). [source: SEP-H §2.1]
- **"Hume's fork"** (EHU 4.1): "relations of ideas" (a priori, denial a contradiction, e.g. mathematics) vs "matters of fact" (known by experience; contrary conceivable). "All reasonings may be divided into two kinds, namely, demonstrative reasoning, or that concerning relations of ideas, and moral reasoning, or that concerning matter of fact and existence" (EHU 4.18). Later problematised by Kant's synthetic a priori and Kripke. The name "Hume's fork" is modern. [source: SEP-H §2.2; SEP-IP §1]
- "Commit it then to the flames" (end of EHU 12): "If we take in our hand any volume; of divinity or school metaphysics, for instance; let us ask, Does it contain any abstract reasoning concerning quantity or number? No. Does it contain any experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence? No. Commit it then to the flames: For it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion." [source: IEP-H; WP-An_Enquiry_Concerning_Human_Understanding (citing 1777 ed., p. 166)]

## 5. Hume: the problem of induction (ARGUMENT)

- Text: T 1.3.6 and EHU 4 (with the "sceptical solution" in EHU 5). Causation is the only relation that takes us "beyond the evidence of our memory and senses" (E 4.4; T 1.3.2.3). Hume's example: bread nourishing; gunpowder exploding. [source: SEP-IP §1; SEP-H §2.3]
- **Uniformity Principle:** "all our experimental conclusions proceed upon the supposition, that the future will be conformable to the past" (EHU 4.19); Treatise: "that instances, of which we have had no experience, must resemble those, of which we have had experience, and that the course of nature continues always uniformly the same" (T 1.3.6.4). [source: SEP-IP §1; SEP-H §2.3]
- **Reconstruction (Henderson, SEP-IP §2):** P1 There are only two kinds of arguments, demonstrative and probable (the fork). P2 Inductive inference presupposes the UP. P3 A demonstrative argument establishes a conclusion whose negation is a contradiction. P4 The negation of the UP is not a contradiction ("it implies no contradiction that the course of nature may change", EHU 4.18). C1 No demonstrative argument for the UP. P5 Any probable argument for the UP presupposes the UP. P6 An argument may not presuppose what it is meant to prove (non-circularity: probable reasoning would be "taking that for granted, which is the very point in question", EHU 4.19). C2 No probable argument for the UP. C3 No argument for the UP. … C5 Inductive inference is not justified (by reason). [source: SEP-IP §§1–2]
- SEP-H alternative layout: four candidate foundations (intuition, demonstration, sensation, probable argument); all fail; conclusion: the UP is not founded on reason; our inductive propensities come from "custom or habit", "a propensity to renew the same act or operation without the intervention of any reasoning" (EHU 5.5). [source: SEP-H §2.3]
- Hume's "sceptical solution": custom; "a species of natural instincts, which no reasoning or process of the thought and understanding is able, either to produce, or to prevent" (EHU 5.8); a "pre-established harmony" between nature's course and our ideas (EHU 5.21). Treatise: the mind "is not determin'd by reason, but by certain principles, which associate together the ideas of these objects, and unite them in the imagination" (T 1.3.6.12). [source: SEP-IP §1]
- **Hume and the word "induction":** the argument is not framed in those terms; David Stove noted that the word "inductive" is "one which Hume himself never used in print", and Peter Millican notes Hume's few uses of "induction" treat it as roughly a synonym for "inference" in general, not as the name of his topic. WRITER: say "Hume did not call it 'the problem of induction'; the name is later." Do NOT say he never used the word "induction". [source: WS-4 (Stove, *Probability and Hume's Inductive Scepticism*, online text; Millican 1997 paper on davidhume.org)]
- Interpretive debate: is the conclusion psychological ("descriptivists": reason does not *cause* the inference — Garrett 1997, Owen 1999) or sceptical ("normativists": reason cannot *justify* it — deductivist readings Flew 1961, Stove 1973, now judged refuted; strong sceptical readings Winkler 1999, Millican 2002)? [source: SEP-H §2.3]
- **Responses:**
  - Popper: the problem is insurmountable but science does not use induction — bold conjectures and attempted falsification by modus tollens (*Logik der Forschung* 1935/1959). Objection: many unrefuted hypotheses remain, and "corroboration" smuggles back non-deductive support (Salmon 1981). [source: SEP-IP §6]
  - Strawson (1952), "ordinary language dissolution": asking whether induction as a whole is justified is like asking whether "the law of the land, the legal system as a whole, is or is not legal"; it is analytic that it is reasonable to proportion belief to inductive evidence. Objection (BonJour 1998): conformity to inductive standards does not show the conclusion is likely to be true. [source: SEP-IP §5.2]
  - Reichenbach (1938), "pragmatic vindication": induction cannot be shown to work, but if any method can find a limiting frequency, the straight rule will — like a physician: "if there is any remedy, it is an operation". Objection: many other rules also converge (Salmon 1966). [source: SEP-IP §5.3]
  - Goodman, "new riddle of induction" (*Fact, Fiction and Forecast*, 1955): "grue" (green if observed before t, blue otherwise) — the same evidence supports incompatible predictions; Hume did not explain why we project "green" and not "grue". Cross-ref *Induction, Probability and Inference to the Best Explanation*. [source: SEP-IP §4.2]
  - Hume himself: the problem arises "only at the level of philosophical reflection"; his whole science of man relies on induction. [source: SEP-IP §6]

## 6. Hume: causation

- Idea of cause = contiguity, priority in time, and (most important) necessary connection. Search for the impression of necessary connection: no single case, external or internal, yields it; repetition adds nothing to the objects but produces in the mind a felt transition — an "impression of reflection", a determination/expectation — which we project: the mind's "great propensity … to spread itself on external objects" (T 1.3.14.25). [source: SEP-H §2.2; IEP-HC §2]
- **Two definitions.** Enquiry: (1) "an object, followed by another, and where all the objects, similar to the first, are followed by objects similar to the second"; (2) "an object followed by another, and whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other" (EHU 7.29). Treatise (T 1.3.14.31; SBN 170): (D1) "An object precedent and contiguous to another, and where all the objects resembling the former are placed in like relations of precedency and contiguity to those objects that resemble the latter"; (D2) "… so united with it, that the idea of the one determines the mind to form the idea of the other, and the impression of the one to form a more lively idea of the other" (IEP text has "determined"). The definitions are neither co-intensive nor co-extensive (J.A. Robinson); Old Humeans prioritise the first, New Humeans deny either is a strict definition, Garrett (1997) argues they are co-extensive when construed correctly. [source: SEP-H §2.2; IEP-HC §2]
- **"New Hume" debate:** "Old Humeans" (Winkler 1991, "The New Hume", *Philosophical Review*; Millican 2009) read Hume as denying or making inconceivable objective necessary connection; "New Humeans" — John P. Wright, *The Sceptical Realism of David Hume* (1983); Galen Strawson, *The Secret Connexion* (1989); Kail 2007 — read him as a sceptical realist who allows that real powers exist though unknowable. Strawson: "Hume's Regularity theory of causation is only a theory about (E) [what we can know], not about (O) [what there is]." Kemp Smith (1941) seen as starting the realist line. Edited volume: Read & Richman, *The New Hume Debate* (2000; rev. 2007). [source: SEP-H §2.2; IEP-HC §6]
- Al-Ghazālī comparison (consistent with published chapter): both say observation shows only conjunction, not necessity; differently motivated. Nothing found here contradicts the published claim "no documented evidence that Hume knew al-Ghazālī's work" (not independently checked). [source: published chapter; SEP-causation-medieval fetched but not used]
- Free will: compatibilist; "By liberty, then, we can only mean a power of acting or not acting, according to the determinations of the will" (EHU 8.23). Cross-ref *Free Will and Determinism* (Hobbes chapter calls Hume, with Hobbes, "the classic case"). [source: SEP-H §2.2]
- **Mary Shepherd (1777–1847)**, *An Essay upon the Relation of Cause and Effect, controverting the Doctrine of Mr. Hume…* (1824) and *Essay on the Perception of an External Universe* (1827): born Mary Primrose (31 Dec 1777, Barnbougle Castle, Dalmeny; daughter of the 3rd Earl of Rosebery), died 7 Jan 1847 London. As a student she wrote essays "faulting the logic of Hume's atheistic writings"; prompted by the 1802 Edinburgh controversy in which Thomas Brown defended Hume. Against Hume: (i) causal belief is caused and justified by perception and reason, not habit; (ii) abstraction is possible; (iii) argues (against Hume's separability principle) that "a thing that begins to exist" must have a cause (CP) — something must act to begin it; (iv) "similar causes must have similar effects" (CSP); cause and effect are simultaneous and necessary. Critics (Bolton 2017; Fantl 2016) find the CP argument circular; Rickless and Jessica Wilson (2022) defend it. Admired by Whewell and Lyell as an "unanswerable logician" (daughter's memoir). Her philosophy "dropped from view" after her death and has recently been recovered. [source: SEP-MS §§1–2; WP-Lady_Mary_Shepherd]

## 7. Hume: the self (bundle theory)

- T 1.4.6.3: "For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception." [source: IEP-H; WP-Bundle_theory]
- T 1.4.6.4 (SBN 252): the rest of mankind "are nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement." [source: WS-7 (Siakel 2018, *Hume Studies*, quoting T 1.4.6.4; SBN 252); WP-Bundle_theory (omits "an")]
- Argument (via the Copy Principle): no impression of an enduring, simple self; so no such idea; the self (as regards "thought or imagination", T 1.4.6.5) is a bundle (synchronic) or succession (diachronic) of associated perceptions. [source: SEP-H §2.1]
- **Appendix second thoughts (1740):** "upon a more strict review of the section concerning personal identity, I find myself involv'd in such a labyrinth, that, I must confess, I neither know how to correct my former opinions, nor how to render them consistent." The two principles he "cannot render consistent" — that distinct perceptions are distinct existences, and that the mind never perceives real connexion among distinct existences — are not inconsistent in themselves; "consensus has not been established" on what troubled him (Garrett 1981; Swain; Ainslie 2008; etc.). [source: IEP-H (labyrinth quotation); SEP-H §2.1 (paraphrase of the two principles); WP-David_Hume (Swain)] (The exact wording of the "two principles" sentence was not found in a source read; paraphrase it.)
- Buddhist comparison: the published *Early Buddhism* chapter treats non-self through the five aggregates, Vajirā's chariot verse and Parfit; it does not mention Hume, so no contradiction. James Giles, "The No-Self Theory: Hume, Buddhism, and Personal Identity", *Philosophy East and West* 43 (1993), is a standard comparison (bibliographic listing only). [source: published chapter grep; WP-David_Hume bibliography]
- **Gopnik hypothesis (debate):** Alison Gopnik, "Could David Hume Have Known about Buddhism? Charles François Dolu, the Royal College of La Flèche, and the Global Jesuit Intellectual Network", *Hume Studies* 35 (2009): 5–28. Dolu, a Jesuit at La Flèche when Hume was living nearby, had been on the French embassy to Siam (1687–88) and met Ippolito Desideri, who had been in Tibet 1716–21; so Hume *could* have heard of Buddhist no-self ideas. It is an argument for possibility, not proof; no document shows Hume discussed Buddhism. WRITER: present as a speculative hypothesis. [source: WP-Alison_Gopnik; WS-5]

## 8. Hume: reason and the passions; is and ought

- Theses (T 2.3.3): (1) reason alone cannot produce motives; (2) reason cannot oppose passion. Reason's two functions — demonstration and causal reasoning — neither moves us by itself: "It can never in the least concern us to know, that such objects are causes, and such others effects, if both the causes and effects be indifferent to us" (T 2.3.3.3). Passions are "original existences" with no representative content (T 2.3.3.5: "When I am angry … more than five foot high"). [source: SEP-H §3.3]
- **"Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them" (T 2.3.3.4).** [source: IEP-H §5; SEP-HM §3]
- "'Tis not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger. 'Tis not contrary to reason for me to chuse my total ruin, to prevent the least uneasiness of an Indian or person wholly unknown to me…" (T 2.3.3.6). A passion is "unreasonable" only when founded on a false belief about existence or means. [source: SEP-HM §3]
- Debate: instrumentalist vs sceptic about practical reason; the "Humean theory of motivation" (belief + desire) as traditional reading (Smith; Blackburn; Sinhababu), challenged by Kail, Cohon, Pigden and others. [source: SEP-H §3.3; SEP-HM §3]
- **Motivation Argument (T 3.1.1.6):** (1) Morals influence passions and actions. (2) Reason alone has no such influence. So morals are not derived from reason. "Morals excite passions, and produce or prevent actions. Reason of itself is utterly impotent in this particular." Plus: wilful murder — "The vice entirely escapes you, as long as you consider the object… you find a sentiment of disapprobation… Here is a matter of fact, but 'tis an object of feeling, not of reason" (T 3.1.1.26); the oak and sapling (patricide relation) case (T 3.1.1.24). Critics (Nagel 1970; Korsgaard 1996): Hume's notion of reason is too narrow. [source: SEP-H §4.1.1–4.1.2]
- Moral sense via sympathy and the "steady and general points of view" (T 3.3.1.15). [source: SEP-H §4.1.3]
- **Is–ought (T 3.1.1.27; SBN 469–70):** "In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remark'd, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning … when of a sudden I am surpriz'd to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, *is*, and *is not*, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an *ought*, or an *ought not*. This change is imperceptible; but is, however, of the last consequence…" — "what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it." (WP gives modernised spelling "remarked/surprised"; SEP-HM quotes "altogether inconceivable" and the closing "subvert all the vulgar systems of morality".) [source: WP-Is–ought_problem (full passage); SEP-HM §5]
- **Readings (debate):** dominant 20th-century reading: a logical thesis that no ought follows from purely factual premises — R.M. Hare's "Hume's Law"; some take it as noncognitivism or anti-realism. Cognitivist readings: it only says moral properties are not discoverable by demonstrative reason, or that the original discovery of virtue and vice needs sentiment; Hume himself often reasons from facts to moral conclusions. [source: SEP-HM §5]

## 9. Hume: scepticism

- Only "carelessness and inattention" save us from scepticism about the external world (T 1.4.2.57). [source: SEP-H §2.3]
- Conclusion of Book 1 (T 1.4.7): the "dangerous dilemma"; melancholy and delirium; the "Title Principle": "where reason is lively, and mixes itself with some propensity, it ought to be assented to" (T 1.4.7.11). Enquiry 12: rejects "antecedent" and "Pyrrhonian" scepticism (harmful and psychologically impossible) and endorses "mitigated" or "Academic" scepticism. [source: SEP-H §2.3]
- "Kemp Smith problem" (debate): how to reconcile Hume's scepticism with his naturalistic "science of man" — sceptical readings (Reid, Beattie; Winkler 1999), naturalist readings (Garrett 1997), perspectival readings (Fogelin), etc. [source: SEP-H §2.3]

## 10. Hume: religion

### Miracles (EHU 10) — ARGUMENT
- "A wise man … proportions his belief to the evidence" (EHU 10.4). Proofs vs probabilities. [source: IEP-HR §(Miracles)]
- Definition: "A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature; and as a firm and unalterable experience has established these laws, the proof against a miracle, from the very nature of the fact, is as entire as any argument from experience can possibly be imagined" (EHU 10.12). Footnote's "accurate" definition: "a transgression of a law of nature by a particular volition of the Deity, or by the interposition of some invisible agent". [source: IEP-HR]
- Maxim: "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish" (EHU 10.13; SBN 115–16). "I … always reject the greater miracle." [source: IEP-HR; SEP-Mir]
- **Part I reconstruction (IEP-HR, the "Categorical Argument"):** (1) Beliefs about matters of fact rest on proofs or probabilities of varying strength. (2) When such beliefs conflict, assent goes to the best-supported. (3) Belief in a miracle conflicts with belief in a law of nature. (4) Laws of nature are supported by the strongest proofs ("in all countries and all ages"). (5) Testimony for a miracle is weaker than those proofs. ∴ We should never believe testimony that a miracle has occurred. The conclusion is epistemic (a miracle cannot be *proved*), not that miracles are impossible. [source: IEP-HR]
- **Objection (begging the question, the "Caricature Argument"):** if a miracle is by definition a violation of an exceptionless law, Hume assumes his conclusion; Paley first attributed this to Hume. Reply (Garrett 1997): a "law" here is what plays that role for the judger; the miracle-witness also accepts the law. Price (1777): a miracle is "more properly an event different from experience than contrary to it". [source: IEP-HR; SEP-Mir]
- **Part II, four considerations:** (1) no miracle in history attested by enough witnesses of good sense and learning; (2) we love surprise and wonder; (3) miracle reports "abound among ignorant and barbarous nations" (EHU 10.20); (4) the miracles of rival religions cancel one another (EHU 10.24). Campbell (*Dissertation on Miracles*, 1762) replies that religious passion cuts both ways. [source: IEP-HR; SEP-Mir]
- Modern critics and defenders: John Earman, *Hume's Abject Failure: The Argument Against Miracles* (2000; Bayesian critique); David Johnson, *Hume, Holism, and Miracles* (1999); Robert Fogelin, *A Defense of Hume on Miracles* (2003). Hume's contemporaries all read Part I as an argument (Leland 1755, Adams 1767, Price 1777, Campbell 1762). [source: IEP-HR bibliography; SEP-Mir]

### *Dialogues concerning Natural Religion* (1779)
- Characters: Cleanthes (design argument, "experimental theist"), Demea (a priori/cosmological argument and fideism), Philo (sceptic; "generally identified as representing Hume's views"); narrated by Pamphilus to Hermippus, who judges Cleanthes best. Partly modelled on Cicero's *De Natura Deorum*. [source: SEP-HR §4; WP-Dialogues_Concerning_Natural_Religion]
- Cleanthes's design argument (D 2.5): "Look round the world… nothing but one great machine… Since, therefore, the effects resemble each other, we are led to infer, by all the rules of analogy, that the causes also resemble; and that the Author of Nature is somewhat similar to the mind of man." Structure: analogy of world and machine; similar effects, similar causes. [source: SEP-HR §4; IEP-H]
- Philo's objections: the analogy is weak ("wherever you depart, in the least, from the similarity of the cases, you diminish proportionably the evidence", D 2.7); the universe is a unique effect, seen only in a "narrow corner"; no experience of world-making; inference is limited to a finite, possibly imperfect designer; other principles of order (generation, vegetation) are as good. Demea's a priori argument (Part IX) is rebutted by Cleanthes (existence claims are matters of fact). Problem of evil (Parts X–XI): four circumstances causing natural evil. Part XII: Philo's apparent "reversal", then "double-reversal" (Russell). [source: SEP-HR §§3–5; IEP-HR]
- Debate — was Hume an atheist? Readings: "soft sceptic" (dominant in the last century); hard sceptic/non-dogmatic atheist about "robust" theism (Russell); "attenuated deism" (Gaskin 1988; Kraal 2023); Russell's umbrella term "irreligion" (*The Riddle of Hume's Treatise*, 2008). Nelson Pike vs Kemp Smith on the Part XII reversal. [source: SEP-HR §§10–11; IEP-HR]
- *Natural History of Religion* (1757, in *Four Dissertations*): Hume's inquiry into religion's origin in human nature, as against its foundation in reason (the *Dialogues*' topic). [source: SEP-H §1; IEP-HR]

## 11. Hume on race, women; contemporaries

- **"Of National Characters" footnote (debate, sourced):** "Of National Characters" first appeared 1748; the footnote was added in the 1753(–54) edition of the *Essays*: "I am apt to suspect the negroes and in general all other species of men (for there are four or five different kinds) to be naturally inferior to the whites. There never was a civilized nation of any other complexion than white, nor even any individual eminent either in action or speculation…", ending with a dismissal of a learned Black man in Jamaica as admired "like a parrot, who speaks a few words plainly". In the posthumous 1777 edition the first lines were rewritten to restrict the claim to "the negroes": "There scarcely ever was a civilized nation of that complexion." [source: WP-David_Hume (quoting Immerwahr 1992 and Garrett & Sebastiani 2017); WS-8]
- James Beattie attacked the footnote in *An Essay on the Nature and Immutability of Truth* (1770). John Immerwahr, "Hume's Revised Racism", *Journal of the History of Ideas* 53 (1992), argued Hume revised it in response to Beattie; Aaron Garrett, "Hume's Revised Racism Revisited", *Hume Studies* 26.1 (April 2000): 171–77, disputes that this is established. Emmanuel Chukwudi Eze, "Hume, Race, and Human Nature", *Journal of the History of Ideas* (2000), treats the footnote as integral rather than an afterthought. Garrett & Sebastiani, "David Hume on Race", *Oxford Handbook of Philosophy and Race* (2017). The Jamaican man is usually identified as Francis Williams (NOT verified here — omit the name). [source: WS-8; WS-14; WP-David_Hume]
- Consistency: the published *Enlightenment* history chapter says the footnote was added "in 1753" — consistent (Eze: 1753–54). 
- **Hume and women:** "Of Essay Writing" (1742, *Essays*, vol. 2; later withdrawn) casts Hume as "a Kind of Resident or Ambassador from the Dominions of Learning to those of Conversation" and calls women "the Sovereigns of the Empire of Conversation"; "Of the Study of History" addresses women readers; both are in the "Essays Withdrawn and Unpublished" group of modern editions. The *Treatise* counts chastity and modesty among the artificial virtues, "primarily for women and girls". Debate: Annette Baier read Hume as "remarkably free of discriminatory sexism" (1979 "Good Men's Women"); others (e.g. Sapp; a *Philosophy* article "An Enquiry concerning the Humean Woman") find his remarks close to the sexist conceptions of his time. Anthology: Anne Jaap Jacobson (ed.), *Feminist Interpretations of David Hume* (2000). [source: WS-6; WS-16; SEP-HM §9]
- **Anton Wilhelm Amo** (c. 1703 – c. 1759), Nzema philosopher from Axim (Gold Coast, now Ghana), brought to Germany as a child by the Dutch West India Company; studied at Halle (lost 1729 dissertation *De iure Maurorum in Europa*, "On the Rights of Moors in Europe"), doctorate at Wittenberg 1734 (*De humanae mentis apatheia*, a broadly Cartesian dualist argument that the mind does not sense), taught at Halle and Jena; *Treatise on the Art of Philosophising Soberly and Accurately* (1738); returned to the Gold Coast about 1747. An exact contemporary of Hume; no known contact. English translation of his works, OUP 2020. Cross-ref *Beyond Europe* or *Race, Colonialism and Philosophy*. [source: WP-Anton_Wilhelm_Amo]
- Catharine Trotter Cockburn (1679–1749) defended Locke and wrote on moral obligation; she died in May 1749, and SEP-Cock never mentions Hume — she is NOT a critic of Hume (see NOT VERIFIED). [source: SEP-Cock]

## Debates
- How strong is the Master Argument? Pitcher: it conflates the act of conceiving with what is conceived, so it fails; Winkler/Lennon: charitable reading (we cannot represent unconceivedness), which presupposes representationalism. [source: SEP-B §2.2.1]
- Continued existence of unperceived things: counterfactual reading (PHK 3) vs God's perception (3D) vs God's decrees (McCracken). [source: SEP-B §3.2.4]
- Is Hume's induction argument descriptive (psychology of inference) or normative (no justification)? Garrett, Owen vs Winkler, Millican; deductivist readings (Flew, Stove) rejected. [source: SEP-H §2.3]
- Old Hume vs New Hume on causation: Winkler, Millican vs Wright, G. Strawson, Kail (and Kemp Smith as forerunner). [source: SEP-H §2.2; IEP-HC §6]
- Treatise vs Enquiry: is the later work a mere popularisation (Selby-Bigge) or a real improvement (Millican 2002; Qu 2020)? [source: SEP-H §1]
