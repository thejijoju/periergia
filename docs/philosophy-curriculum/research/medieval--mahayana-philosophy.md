## 1. The rise of Mahāyāna and the Perfection of Wisdom sūtras
- "Mahāyāna" = "Great Vehicle". Jan Nattier: the Indic term was originally an honorific synonym for *Bodhisattvayāna*, the vehicle of a bodhisattva seeking buddhahood for the benefit of all beings. "Hīnayāna" ("lesser vehicle") is a polemical term, comparatively rare in the earliest sources. [source: WP-Mahayana] — **Writer: do not use "Hīnayāna" as a neutral label.**
- Earliest Mahāyāna sūtras: "probably around the 1st century BCE or 1st century CE" (Macmillan Encyclopedia of Buddhism; Hirakawa). [source: WP-Mahayana]
- IEP-Madh: Mahāyāna sūtras "known to have begun circulating only at the beginning of the first millennium"; proponents defended them as authentic word of the Buddha (*buddhavacana*) by "skill in means" (*upāyakauśalya*): an advanced teaching withheld from the first hearers, who might have drawn nihilistic conclusions. [source: IEP-Madh §1]
- Perfection of Wisdom (*Prajñāpāramitā*): Edward Conze: "a collection of about forty texts … composed somewhere on the Indian subcontinent between approximately 100 BC and AD 600". Western scholars traditionally take the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā* ("Perfection of Wisdom in 8,000 Lines") as the earliest, "probably put in writing in the 1st century BCE" (Conze's chronology, based largely on translation dates). Japanese scholars have traditionally put the *Diamond Sūtra* early. [source: WP-Prajnaparamita]
- Hard evidence: Lokakṣema's Chinese translation of the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā* (T224), c. 179 CE, the first extant translation of the genre into a non-Indic language; a partial Gāndhārī Kharoṣṭhī manuscript (the "Split" manuscript) published by Harry Falk and Seishi Karashima in 2012, radiocarbon-dated to the 1st or early 2nd century CE. [source: WP-Prajnaparamita; WP-Aṣṭasāhasrikā]
- The Macmillan Encyclopedia (via WP): "the only evidence" for placing the emergence of Mahāyāna around the beginning of the common era "was not Indian evidence at all, but came from China", i.e. Lokakṣema's translations (last quarter of the 2nd century). [source: WP-Mahayana]
- Earliest Indian inscription with a recognisably Mahāyāna formulation: a Mathurā pedestal naming the Buddha Amitābha, in a regnal year of the Kuṣāṇa king Huviṣka (WP gives "year 28 … around 180 CE" in the text and "year 26 … (153 CE)" in an image caption — **internal inconsistency; say "second half of the 2nd century CE"**). No Indian inscription refers to a named Mahāyāna before the 5th century. [source: WP-Mahayana]
- Schopen: Indian Mahāyāna remained "an extremely limited minority movement … that attracted absolutely no documented public or popular support for at least two more centuries"; Walser: "virtual invisibility in the archaeological record until the fifth century". [source: WP-Mahayana]
- Self-description: the *Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra* calls Yogācāra teaching the "third turning of the wheel of the doctrine", after the first (four noble truths) and second (emptiness). [source: SEP-Yog §1.2; WP-Mahayana]

## 2. Nāgārjuna: dates, legend, works
- Dates: "ca 150–250 CE"; "a Buddhist monk who lived some time between 150 and 250 CE, probably mainly in southern India is rarely disputed". "Very little can be said precisely about Nāgārjuna's life." [source: SEP-Nag intro, §1]. IEP-Nag: "c. 150–c. 250"; IEP-Madh: "flourished around 150 C.E."; WP: most scholars place him in South India between the 1st and 3rd c. CE, conventionally 150–250. [sources as named] **Use "c. 150–250 CE".**
- Discrepancy: SEP-Tsong (Sparham) gives Nāgārjuna as "third-fourth century" — out of line with all other sources; do not follow.
- Sātavāhana connection: the *Ratnāvalī* and *Suhṛllekha* are addressed to a king. IEP-Nag names Gautamīputra Śātakarṇi "(ruled c. 166–196 CE)"; WP (Walser) says archaeological evidence at Amarāvatī points to Yajña Śrī Śātakarṇi (second half of the 2nd c.). **Sources disagree on the king; write "a king of the Sātavāhana dynasty in the Deccan".** Walser: the *Ratnāvalī* plausibly written in the Andhra region near Amarāvatī at the end of the 2nd century. [source: IEP-Nag §1; WP-Nagarjuna]
- Legend (label as legend): biographies in Chinese and Tibetan "written many centuries after his life" with "historically unreliable material". Chinese legend: a Brahmin youth who used magic to enter the royal harem with friends, escaped when they were executed, and renounced the world; later invited to the nāgas' (serpents') kingdom under the ocean where he retrieved the Perfection of Wisdom sūtras, hence "Nāgārjuna". Tibetan death legend: the king's successor asked him to commit suicide; he was beheaded with a blade of kuśa grass. Also alchemy legends (turning rocks into gold: Buton, Tāranātha, Xuanzang). [source: IEP-Nag §1; IEP-Madh §1; WP-Nagarjuna]
- The Nālandā story: IEP-Nag thinks Tibetan sources "may well be basically accurate" in having him study at Nālandā; Walser (via WP) notes Nālandā was not a strong monastic centre until about 425, and Xuanzang and Yijing report no local tales. **Treat Nālandā as legend.** [source: IEP-Nag §1; WP-Nagarjuna]
- Several Nāgārjunas: later tantric and alchemical authors bore the name ("Nāgārjuna II"); attribution of texts is disputed (Ruegg 1981, Lindtner 1982). [source: SEP-Nag §1; WP-Nagarjuna]
- Authorship criterion: by convention "Nāgārjuna" is the author of the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (MMK) and *Vigrahavyāvartanī* (VV). [source: IEP-Madh §1]
- Works (ascription "largely uncontested"): MMK, "the most important", 450 stanzas; *Yuktiṣaṣṭikā* (Sixty Stanzas on Reasoning); *Śūnyatāsaptati* (Seventy Stanzas on Emptiness); *Vigrahavyāvartanī* ("Dispeller of Disputes"/"Turning Back Objections"), 70 verses with a prose autocommentary, answering Buddhist and non-Buddhist objections, especially on epistemology and language; *Vaidalyaprakaraṇa* (refuting Nyāya's categories); *Ratnāvalī* (Precious Garland, ethics, to a king); hymns (*Catuḥstava*); *Suhṛllekha* (Friendly Letter). [source: SEP-Nag §1]
- MMK: 450 verses in 27 chapters (authenticity of the last two disputed); a kārikā text meant to be memorised and filled out by oral commentary. [source: WP-Mūlamadhyamakakārikā] Only Sanskrit commentary to survive: Candrakīrti's *Prasannapadā*, which preserves the Sanskrit verses. [source: IEP-Madh §4]
- Mahāyāna or not? IEP-Nag: very little in the philosophical works shows awareness of Mahāyāna doctrines; the letters (no Sanskrit originals) are the main evidence that he was a Mahāyānist — "an age-old debate". Walser (via WP): he probably lived in a mixed monastery where Mahāyānists were a minority. [source: IEP-Nag §5; WP-Nagarjuna]
- Status: "unanimous agreement" he is "the most important Buddhist philosopher after the historical Buddha himself"; called "the second Buddha" in Tibetan and East Asian traditions. [source: SEP-Nag intro; IEP-Nag intro]

## 3. Emptiness (śūnyatā), svabhāva and dependent origination
- Emptiness is always emptiness *of* something: *svabhāva*, translated "inherent existence", "intrinsic nature", "substance" or "essence". [source: SEP-Nag §2]
- Westerhoff distinguishes svabhāva as (a) essence (fire's heat) — **not** the main target; (b) substance/primary existent (*dravyasat*, the Abhidharma's irreducible dharmas, "independent of anything else") — **the main target**; (c) absolute reality; plus (d) a cognitive sense: an automatic "superimposition" we project, e.g. on our changing aggregates as a permanent self. [source: SEP-Nag §2]
- Abhidharma background: primary existents (*dravyasat*) vs secondary, conceptually constructed existents (*prajñaptisat*): "shoes and ships and sealing-wax" are aggregates of dharmas. Nāgārjuna denies there is any ontological foundation, any "end-point of a chain of ontological dependence relations". [source: SEP-Nag §2] IEP-Madh: "The foundational idea of Madhyamaka is that the set of ultimately existent things is an empty set." [source: IEP-Madh §2a]
- Emptiness ≠ non-existence: *śūnyatā* "does not mean 'non-existence' or 'nihility' (abhāva), but rather the lack of autonomous existence (nihsvabhava)". [source: IEP-Nag intro]
- Link to dependent origination: "insofar as there is nothing that is not dependently originated, there is therefore nothing that is not 'empty'". MMK 24.19 (Arnold's translation): "Since there is no dharma whatsoever that is not dependently originated, therefore there is no dharma whatsoever that is not empty." [source: IEP-Madh §2b] Thakchoe's rendering: "there is no thing that is not dependently arisen; therefore, there is no such thing that is not empty" ([MMK] 24.19). [source: SEP-2T-India]
- Arnold: Mādhyamikas exploit the etymology *sva-bhāva* "existent by itself"; a recent exegetical debate asks whether key arguments equivocate between "identity" and "causally independent existence". [source: IEP-Madh §2b]
- Understanding vs realisation: being convinced by the arguments does not stop things *appearing* to have svabhāva, as the Müller-Lyer lines still look unequal after measuring. [source: SEP-Nag §2]
- "No master argument": Nāgārjuna has no single argument for universal emptiness (Siderits); since nothing has its nature independently of context, the refutation must proceed case by case, opponent by opponent. [source: SEP-Nag §3]
- Three kinds of dependence: mereological (parts), causal, conceptual; only showing that conceptual dependence has no foundation establishes emptiness in the full sense. [source: SEP-Nag §3]
- Emptiness of emptiness: "all things including ultimate truth are ultimately unreal, empty (śūnya) of any intrinsic nature (svabhāva) including the emptiness (śūnyatā) itself". [source: SEP-2T-India §3] Westerhoff: emptiness "is not some kind of primordial reality ante rem but a corrective to a mistaken view". [source: SEP-Nag §2]
- Saṃsāra and nirvāṇa: "there is, on the part of saṃsāra, no difference at all from nirvāṇa" (MMK 25.19, Arnold's translation). [source: IEP-Madh §1]

## 4. Argument reconstruction A: MMK 1 on causation (the tetralemma)
- MMK 1.1 (Arnold's translation): "There do not exist, anywhere at all, any existents whatsoever, arisen either from themselves or from something else, either from both or altogether without cause." [source: IEP-Madh §1, §5] WP's (unattributed) rendering: "Not from itself, not from another, not from both, nor without cause: Never in any way is there any existing thing that has arisen." [source: WP-MMK — translator not named; prefer Arnold's]
- The tetralemma (*catuṣkoṭi*): "a four-fold statement that is meant to identify all possible relations" (same, different, both, neither), the denial of all four horns "meant as an exhaustive refutation"; one modern debate asks whether this shows a non-standard logic. [source: IEP-Madh §5]
- Early Buddhist root: Berger calls it the "four error" denial and traces it to the Buddha's refusal of metaphysical questions (e.g. whether the world has a beginning): denying all four answers was "more a principled refusal to answer than a counter-thesis". [source: IEP-Nag §2] (Berger's reading; flag as one interpretation.)
- Step by step (Westerhoff's and Buddhapālita's reconstruction):
  1. Suppose cause and effect exist with svabhāva: qualitatively distinct, independent, mind-independent objects. [SEP-Nag §3.1]
  2. If so, the effect arises either (a) from itself, (b) from something other, (c) from both, or (d) from neither (no cause). These are exhaustive. [SEP-Nag §3.1; SEP-Madh §3]
  3. (a) Self-causation: if the effect were identical to its cause "it would already exist as the cause and would have no need of coming into being a second time" (Buddhapālita: "there would be no point in the arising of already existent things"); if the effect is "contained" in the cause, it is not found there (no explosion among petrol, oxygen and spark). [SEP-Madh §3; IEP-Madh §5; SEP-Nag §3.1.1]
  4. (b) Other-causation: things distinct by svabhāva can have no dependence relation; then "there would be no constraints on what could arise out of what" — Candrakīrti: a sprout could as well come from the coals of a fire as from a seed; "pitch darkness arises out of light". And the cause depends at least notionally on the effect (nothing is called "cause" without an effect; MMK 4.3 says a cause could not exist without an effect). [SEP-Madh §3, §5; IEP-Madh §5; SEP-Nag §3.1.2]
  5. (c) Both: just the conjunction of two untenable hypotheses; or (best version, potentiality: marble and statue) it concedes the relata lack independent natures. [SEP-Madh §3; SEP-Nag §3.1.3]
  6. (d) Neither: no causation at all; contradicts the causal order of experience and makes knowledge impossible; Buddhapālita: it "would render all practice ineffectual". [SEP-Nag §3.1.4; SEP-Madh §3]
  7. Conclusion: Nāgārjuna "does not conclude that causation is impossible", but that the shared premise — that cause and effect exist with svabhāva — is false; they are mutually dependent and empty. [SEP-Nag §3.1]
- Objection (Ābhidharmika): being caused is compatible with having svabhāva (the Ābhidharmikas "saw no conflict"). Reply (Siderits's reconstruction): causation itself is conceptually constructed; an object essentially involving a constructed property is constructed (chess-game example); so caused things are empty in the deepest sense. "Continues to be debated (see Siderits 2013, Westerhoff 2023, Siderits 2025)." [source: SEP-Nag §3.1]
- Bhāviveka's qualification: denials are of what is so "ultimately"/"essentially" (*svabhāvataḥ*); without it denying conventional origination from other things would be absurd. Bhāviveka also read the negations as sentential (*prasajya*) negations, so denying all four horns is not contradictory. [source: IEP-Madh §5; SEP-Madh §4.2]

## 5. Argument reconstruction B: the self-refutation objection and "I have no thesis"
- Objection (IEP-Nag attributes it to the Nyāya "Logicians"): no claim compels assent unless known to be true; if all things lack a fixed nature, so does the statement "all things are empty"; a universal denial must be "essentially true of all things", so the thesis refutes itself. [source: IEP-Nag §4] SEP-Nag: the VV's objections come "both from Buddhist and non-Buddhist opponents". [source: SEP-Nag §1] **Writer: say "opponents, among them Nyāya logicians"; do not claim every VV objection is Nyāya's.**
- Arnold's statement of the problem: if Madhyamaka's claim is that "the ultimate truth is that there is no ultimate truth", what is the status of that claim? Either it is an exception (performative self-contradiction) or it too is empty — "the emptiness of emptiness" — and then does it lose its purchase? [source: IEP-Madh §3]
- Reply 1 (tu quoque, VV): the Nyāya theory of proof fails by its own standards: a means of knowledge is established either by another (infinite regress: the scale checked by a second scale…), by itself (then proof is superfluous), or by mutual corroboration (circular: "father" because of a son and "son" because of a father). [source: IEP-Nag §4; SEP-Nag §3.4] Westerhoff's reading: instruments and objects establish each other, so neither has svabhāva; a "reflective equilibrium". [source: SEP-Nag §3.4.2]
- Reply 2, the no-thesis statement, VV 29: Berger's paraphrase: "If my statement (about emptiness) were a philosophical thesis, then it would indeed be flawed; but I assert no thesis, and so the flaw is not mine." [source: IEP-Nag §4] Westerhoff's translation (2009 paper "The No-Thesis View", as reported by WS-2): "If I had some thesis the defect [just mentioned] would as a consequence attach to me. But I have no thesis, so this defect is not applicable to me." Verse number 29 confirmed by the title of Kania (2024), "Nāgārjuna's No-Thesis Statement (Vigraha-vyāvartanī 29) as an Absurd Consequence Revisited". [source: WS-2] — **second-hand; see Quotations.**
- How to read it (a debate): (i) sceptical/vaitaṇḍika: Nāgārjuna only refutes, like the Nyāya "destructive debater" (*vitaṇḍā*), which Nyāya thought a lesser form of debate [IEP-Nag §2, §4]; (ii) "therapeutic" (Wittgensteinian or post-structuralist) readings [IEP-Madh §3]; (iii) metaphysical/transcendental and deflationist readings: emptiness = dependent origination is a real claim that rules out e.g. a necessarily existent God [IEP-Madh §3]; (iv) Kania (2024) reads the verse as an absurd consequence within a debate, not Nāgārjuna's own position [WS-2]. Westerhoff: the theory of emptiness is not "an ultimately true theory" but a corrective. [SEP-Nag §3.5]
- Reception: Berger: apart from his two major commentators "this stance … satisfied no one in the Indian philosophical tradition"; a no-thesis debater was suspected of being "more likely a charlatan than a sage". [source: IEP-Nag §4]
- VV's close (Berger's paraphrase): "nothing will prevail for those in whom emptiness will not prevail, while everything will prevail for whom emptiness prevails". Thakchoe cites VV 71 for: where emptiness applies, causal efficacy applies. [source: IEP-Nag §4; SEP-2T-India §3]

## 6. The nihilism charge and replies
- Nihilism (*ucchedavāda*) for Buddhists = actions have no consequences because agents do not endure. "The concern to avoid charges of nihilism represents one of the most significant preoccupations of Mādhyamika philosophers", raised by Buddhists and Brahmanical schools alike. [source: IEP-Madh §2a, §2c]
- MMK 24.1 (objector, Arnold's translation): "If all this is empty, then there's neither production nor destruction; it follows, for you, that the Four Noble Truths don't exist." Reply MMK 24.20: only because everything is empty (dependently originated) can suffering arise and be made to cease; if suffering had svabhāva it could not be interrupted. [source: IEP-Madh §2c]
- Counter-charge: on Madhyamaka's view it is the Abhidharma that is "nihilist", since it supersedes the world of suffering persons by a privileged level of dharmas; Madhyamaka leaves the conventional world "irreducibly conventional". [source: IEP-Madh §2c]
- Westerhoff: ontological nihilism may not even be consistent ("if there is nothing, is there not at least the fact that there is nothing?"); Mādhyamikas reject both svabhāva and its nihilistic opposite. The nihilist reading "has a relatively long history, beginning in ancient India and continuing to find supporters nowadays" (Spackman 2014). [source: SEP-Nag §2]
- Agency: Nāgārjuna compares the agent to an illusion created by a magician that itself creates another illusion (MMK 17.31–32); within the illusion one must still act by its laws (the dream-staircase example). [source: SEP-Nag §3.3]

## 7. The two truths
- MMK 24.8 (Thakchoe): "the Dharma taught by the buddhas is precisely based on the two truths: a truth of mundane conventions and a truth of the ultimate". MMK 24.9: those who do not understand the distinction do not understand the teaching. MMK 24.10 (Arnold): "without relying on convention, the ultimate is not taught; without having understood the ultimate, nirvāṇa is not apprehended". [source: SEP-2T-India §3; IEP-Madh §5]
- Terms: conventional truth *saṃvṛti-satya* (also *vyavahāra-satya*), ultimate truth *paramārtha-satya*. [source: SEP-Madh §4.2]
- Abhidharma version: two sets of things — conventionally existent (*saṃvṛtisat*) macro-objects and ultimately existent dharmas; Arnold compares the folk-psychological vs scientific levels in cognitive science. Madhyamaka rejects this version. [source: IEP-Madh §2a]
- Candrakīrti glosses *saṃvṛti* as a "screen" that conceals the ultimate; the Buddha spoke in the conventions of his hearers (using "I" without a self). [source: SEP-Madh §5.2]
- Common Madhyamaka view: language reaches only the conventional; the ultimate "is beyond the reach of language". Debate: are ultimate and conventional ultimately the same (Thakchoe: "co-constitutive")? [source: SEP-Madh §1; SEP-2T-India §3]
- Svātantrika Jñānagarbha (and Śāntarakṣita): "true convention" (*tathya-saṃvṛti*) vs "false convention" (*mithyā-saṃvṛti*). [source: IEP-Madh §5]

## 8. Āryadeva and the commentators
- **Āryadeva**: fl. ca. 225–250 CE; traditionally born a prince in what is now Sri Lanka, Nāgārjuna's direct disciple. Main work: *Catuḥśataka* (Four Hundred Verses). In 8.10 he says attachment to one's own views is an obstacle to nirvana. Example argument (chapter 14): a pot is not one (it has many characteristics), not many (no characteristic is a pot), not a whole of both (material and immaterial parts cannot cohere) — yet denying both seems to deny it exists. Also: a permanent self could not be harmed, so believers in it cannot coherently forbid killing. [source: SEP-Madh §2; IEP-Madh §4]
- **Buddhapālita** (ca. 470–540): earliest extant MMK commentary apart from the *Akutobhayā* (ascribed to Nāgārjuna, questioned); survives only in Tibetan; shows unwelcome consequences (*prasaṅga*) only. [source: SEP-Madh §3]
- **Bhāviveka** (also Bhāvaviveka, Bhavya): "probably active in the middle part of the sixth century"; c. 500–570 (IEP-Madh); fl. ca. 550 (SEP-Tsong). *Prajñāpradīpa* (Lamp of Wisdom; Tibetan and Chinese only); *Madhyamakahṛdayakārikā* with *Tarkajvālā* (Flame of Reasoning), surveying Śrāvaka, Yogācāra, Sāṃkhya, Vaiśeṣika, Vedānta and Mīmāṃsā. Criticised Buddhapālita because "no reason and no example are given"; Mādhyamikas must state and defend a thesis (all phenomena lack inherent nature) using Dignāga's form of argument, or be guilty of mere *vitaṇḍā*. Ladder metaphor for verbal teaching. [source: SEP-Madh §4; IEP-Madh §4–5]
- **Candrakīrti**: "beginning of the seventh century" (SEP-Madh); c. 600–650 (IEP-Madh); c. 600–c. 670 (WP). *Prasannapadā* (Sanskrit survives) and *Madhyamakāvatāra* (Tibetan only; ten chapters = ten bodhisattva stages; sixth on wisdom, with critiques of Yogācāra). Defended Buddhapālita against Bhāviveka. "Candrakīrti … would exercise little influence in India, though he re-emerges with the Tibetan tradition's interest in him." [source: SEP-Madh §5; IEP-Madh §4–5]
- **Prāsaṅgika/Svātantrika = later Tibetan labels (FLAG)**: "Although those terms were not used by Indian Mādhyamikas themselves, it has become standard practice in modern scholarship" to speak of two subschools (Hayes). Tibetan scholastics named them "many centuries after the fact". Arnold: the distinction is "not without basis" in Indian texts but tells us about "characteristically Tibetan preoccupations"; whether it is a difference of method only or of ontology is debated. [source: SEP-Madh §4, §5.2; IEP-Madh §4; SEP-2T-India §4]
- **Śāntideva** (fl. early 8th c.), *Bodhicaryāvatāra*, chapter 9 on wisdom. [source: IEP-Madh §4; SEP-Madh §6]

## 9. Yogācāra: Asaṅga, Vasubandhu and the texts
- Yogācāra is, with Madhyamaka, one of the two major philosophical traditions of Indian Mahāyāna. Other names: *vijñānavāda* ("doctrine of consciousness"), *cittamātra* ("mind only"), *vijñaptimātra* ("cognition only"). Name probably from the *Yogācārabhūmi*; *yoga* here = meditative cultivation. [source: SEP-Yog intro, §1.1]
- Founders/first systematisers: the (half-)brothers Asaṅga and Vasubandhu, "c. 4th–5th century CE"; followers Sthiramati and Dharmapāla (c. 6th c.). [source: SEP-Yog intro] Asaṅga: fl. 4th c., possibly born in Puruṣapura (Peshawar). [source: WP-Asanga]
- Sources: *Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra* (earliest characteristically Yogācāra sūtra, present form not earlier than the end of the 3rd c. CE, Powers); *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra* (dating uncertain; Schmithausen: after Vasubandhu; Lindtner: early recension known to Nāgārjuna); *Yogācārabhūmi*, ascribed to Asaṅga (Chinese: to Maitreya) but a multi-layered compilation that "lacks … the cittamātra or vijñaptimātra doctrine". [source: SEP-Yog §1.2]
- Asaṅga's works: *Abhidharmasamuccaya*, *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*. "Five Treatises of Maitreya": tradition = the future Buddha; modern scholars (Frauwallner) a historical Maitreya(nātha). Legend: Asaṅga meditated twelve years before meeting Maitreya. [source: SEP-Yog §1.2; SEP-Vasu §1.1]
- Vasubandhu: "4th to 5th century C.E." (SEP-Vasu intro) / "(4th century C.E.)" (SEP-Vasu §1.1): Paramārtha says he taught the crown prince and queen of "Vikramāditya", i.e. Chandragupta II (r. 380–415). From Gandhāra; studied Vaibhāṣika in Kashmir; *Abhidharmakośa* verses summarised Kashmiri Vaibhāṣika doctrine, his auto-commentary (*bhāṣya*) criticised it mainly from a Sautrāntika view; converted to Yogācāra by Asaṅga (traditional story). Yogācāra works: *Viṃśikā* (Twenty Verses, with auto-commentary), *Triṃśikā* (Thirty Verses), *Trisvabhāvanirdeśa* (authorship contested). [source: SEP-Vasu §1; SEP-Yog §1.2]
- **"Two Vasubandhus" debate**: Erich Frauwallner (1951, "On the Date of the Buddhist Master of the Law Vasubandhu") argued there were two Vasubandhus whom the biographies merged; "Versions of this theory hold sway in some areas of the academy"; others see gradual continuity; Robert Kritzer and colleagues argue he "was secretly an advocate of Yogācāra all along". [source: SEP-Vasu §1.1 and bibliography]
- His Yogācāra "became mainstream Buddhist metaphysics in India for half a millennium". [source: SEP-Vasu intro]

## 10. Ālaya-vijñāna and the eight consciousnesses
- Abhidharma: six consciousnesses (five senses + mental). Yogācāra adds a 7th, the defiled mind (*kliṣṭamanas*), and an 8th, store/substratum consciousness (*ālayavijñāna*). [source: SEP-Yog §2]
- Defiled mind: source of our sense of self; four defilements (false view of self, delusion about self, self-conceit, self-love); takes the ālaya as its object and misconceives it as a self. [source: SEP-Yog §2.1]
- Ālaya: stores karmic "seeds" (*bīja*) or impressions (*vāsanā*) until they ripen; solves no-self problems of personal identity and karmic continuity; Schmithausen traces its origin to the meditative state of cessation (*nirodhasamāpatti*): how does consciousness restart after all mental activity ceases? Also takes possession of a new body at conception and leaves at death. Likened in the *Saṃdhinirmocana* to a river; ceases at liberation, so not an ātman — though critics accused Yogācāra of reviving the self. Shared seeds explain intersubjective experience. [source: SEP-Yog §2.2]
- Yogācārabhūmi's eight proofs include: dissimilar successive moments (virtuous after unvirtuous) cannot seed each other; simultaneous multisensory perception (seeing the kettle and hearing it whistle at once) vs Abhidharma's one-sense-at-a-time model. [source: SEP-Yog §2.2]

## 11. Argument reconstruction C: Vasubandhu's Viṃśikā ("only consciousness")
- Thesis: "Everything in the three realms is only appearance (vijñaptimātra)" (Gold's translation); "In the Great Vehicle, the threefold world is only appearance" (Gold). Opening simile: like hairs seen by someone with eye disease. Chadha quotes Feldman's translation: "This [the external world] is consciousness only, because there is appearance of non-existent things, just as a person with cataracts sees non-existent hairs, moons et cetera." [source: SEP-Vasu §4.1, §5; SEP-Perc]
- Realist objection (v. 2): four reasons appearance-only is impossible: things are (1) restricted in place and (2) in time; (3) beings at one place and time experience the same objects; (4) objects perform causal functions that mere appearances do not. [source: SEP-Vasu §4.1; SEP-Yog §3.4 lists three, merging place/time]
- Replies (vv. 3–4), each a single counterexample showing possibility only: (1–2) dream objects appear at definite places and times; (3) hell beings share hallucinated tormentors (if guards were real and could stand hell's fires they would not be there; karma explains them); (4) a wet dream has a physical result from an unreal lover. [source: SEP-Vasu §4.1; SEP-Yog §3.4]
- Parsimony (vv. 6–7, Siderits's "principle of lightness"): if you admit karma (a mental intention) creates physical hell-guards who then cause mental suffering, why not drop the physical middle step? [source: SEP-Yog §3.4; SEP-Vasu §4.1]
- Positive argument (vv. 11–15): every account of physical sense objects fails: a whole over and above parts is not perceived; separate atoms are not perceived; aggregates of partless atoms are impossible (an atom joined on six "sides" has parts; partless atoms cannot cast light on one side and shade on the other); an extended partless whole cannot be partly seen or crossed step by step. Therefore objects are only appearance. Readings: inference to the best explanation (Gold); modus tollens (Kapstein); argument from ignorance — no pramāṇa establishes external objects (Kellner & Taber 2014). [source: SEP-Vasu §4.2; SEP-Yog §3.4]
- v. 16 time-lag argument: by the time we judge "I perceive this", the momentary object and visual cognition are gone (compare modern time-lag arguments). [source: SEP-Yog §3.3]
- Objections: (a) Nyāya (Vātsyāyana) and Śaṅkara: illusion is parasitic on veridical cognition; we cannot imagine an absolutely unreal thing like "a barren woman's son"; (b) Uddyotakara (*Nyāya-Vārttika* 4.2.33): we only know dream objects are unreal because, when awake, we no longer apprehend them; so waking apprehension must indicate existence — the dream argument presupposes the waking/dream contrast it denies. [source: SEP-Perc] (c) Solipsism and moral nihilism: if all is mind, how can we help or harm one another? Vasubandhu: minds act on minds directly (v. 21: buddhas know other minds). [source: SEP-Vasu §4.2; SEP-Yog §3.6] (d) Self-reference (commentary to v. 10): if no dharmas exist, appearance-only doesn't exist either — the same problem as Nāgārjuna's VV. [source: SEP-Vasu §5]
- Gold's "three levels": idealism about appearances; causal idealism (the store-consciousness produces experience); but at the ultimate level only "inconceivable thusness" — "an idealist, but only in the realm of conventions". [source: SEP-Vasu §5]

## 12. The three natures and the idealism debate
- Three natures (*trisvabhāva*): imagined/constructed (*parikalpita*), dependent (*paratantra*), perfected (*pariniṣpanna*); perfected = absence of the imagined in the dependent (Mahāyānasaṃgraha, Triṃśikā); also read as three "naturelessnesses" (Triṃśikā 23–24). [source: SEP-Yog §3.2]
- Magician's elephant (*Trisvabhāvanirdeśa* 27–30, Gold's translation): "The fabricated nature is the elephant; the dependent is its appearance (ākṛti); and the nonexistence of an elephant there is the perfected." "The root-consciousness is like the incantation. Suchness (tathatā) is understood to be like the piece of wood." [source: SEP-Vasu §4.3]
- Yogācāra vs Madhyamaka: on the standard reading, Yogācāra keeps an ultimate foundation (consciousness, the dependent nature) where Abhidharma kept dharmas — "ontological foundationalism", in contrast to Madhyamaka anti-foundationalism; Yogācāra presented itself as a middle way between Abhidharma reification and Madhyamaka's "nihilistic" emptiness (*Madhyāntavibhāga*). [source: SEP-Yog §1.2, §3.2]
- **Idealism vs phenomenology debate**: ontological idealism (Griffiths, Kapstein, Garfield 2002, Schmithausen, Siderits 2007, Arnold, Kellner & Taber, Westerhoff 2018 — "the other, traditionally more dominant route"); epistemological idealism, compared with Kant (Wayman, Kochumuttom, Hayes, Oetke, King); phenomenology that brackets ontology (Lusthaus 2002; Garfield 2015). Even idealists stress it is a "peculiarly Buddhist form of idealism": no Berkeleyan soul or Hegelian spirit, and subject–object duality rejected ultimately. [source: SEP-Yog §3.1]
- Origins debate: idealism grew from meditative experience (Griffiths, Schmithausen) vs from reflection on dreams and illusions (Sharf); Sautrāntika representationalism as catalyst. Mind-only "is conspicuously absent from the earliest strata". [source: SEP-Yog §3.3]

## 13. Dignāga and Dharmakīrti: epistemology and logic
- Dates (give ranges): Dignāga c. 480–c. 540 (SEP-Dhk; IEP-Madh), "fl. ca. 510" (SEP-Madh), "sixth century" (SEP-Epi), "c. 5th–6th century" (SEP-Log), "late 5th to early 6th" (SEP-Yog). Born near Kāñcī per Tāranātha (legend). [sources as named; WP-Dignāga] Dharmakīrti: debated between 6th and 7th c.: Frauwallner 600–660 (argument from silence, "short of conclusive"); Krasser (2012) mid-6th c.; Balcerowicz (2016) 550–610; IEP-Madh c. 600–660; SEP-Yog "c. mid-6th to mid-7th". Yijing (at Nālandā 675–685) calls him a "recent" teacher. "Caution or even agnosticism … still seems to be warranted." [source: SEP-Dhk intro; WP-Dharmakirti]
- School: no Sanskrit name; Tibetan "those who follow reasoning"; modern "pramāṇavāda" ("a convenient Sanskrit misnomer") or "the Epistemological School". "The Buddhist school that provoked the most sophisticated and most important philosophical debates with non-Buddhist rivals." Little influence in China (a few Dignāga works translated; none of Dharmakīrti's); enormous in Tibet. [source: SEP-Dhk intro]
- Works: Dignāga's *Pramāṇasamuccaya* (Compendium of Epistemology; survives in Tibetan; four of six chapters on inference), *Nyāyamukha* (Chinese), *Hetucakra*, *Ālambanaparīkṣā* (8 verses, the object of perception). Dharmakīrti: seven works, in Frauwallner's order *Pramāṇavārttika* (longest; unfinished commentary on Dignāga), *Pramāṇaviniścaya*, *Nyāyabindu*, *Hetubindu*, *Vādanyāya*, plus *Sambandhaparīkṣā* and *Saṃtānāntarasiddhi* (Proof of Other Minds). [source: SEP-Log §4.1; SEP-Dhk intro; SEP-Yog §1.2]
- Dharmakīrti predicted his work would not be understood and "would age in obscurity locked away in itself" — "He was wrong about that". [source: SEP-Dhk intro]
- **Two pramāṇas**: only perception (*pratyakṣa*) and inference (*anumāna*), because there are only two kinds of knowable: the particular (*svalakṣaṇa*) and the general (*sāmānyalakṣaṇa*). Dignāga, PS 1 (translation as given on WP, translator not named): "Sensation and reasoning are the only two means of acquiring knowledge, because two attributes are knowable…". Nyāya by contrast accepted comparison and testimony too. [source: WP-Dignāga; SEP-Dhk §2]
- **Perception vs conceptual thought**: perception is non-conceptual, causally "fixed" by particulars, the only direct access to the real; conceptual thought "superimposes" unreal universals and is "distorted" (*bhrānta*). Consequence: for Dignāga one and the same object can apparently never be both perceived and thought about. [source: SEP-Dhk §2]
- Dharmakīrti's definition of a pramāṇa (PV II.1): a "reliable (avisaṃvādin) cognition", confirmed by causal efficacy (the pot really holds water); pragmatist readings debated (Tillemans: pragmatic theory of justification, weak correspondence theory of truth). [source: SEP-Dhk §2.1]
- Reflexive awareness (*svasaṃvedana*): every cognition is aware of itself, like a lamp. [source: SEP-Dhk §2.1; WP-Dharmakirti]
- Nominalism and momentariness: only particulars are real; real = having causal powers (PV III.3: "Whatever has causal powers … that really exists"); everything real lasts a moment (argument from existence, *sattvānumāna*: a permanent thing could produce effects neither all at once nor in sequence; applied against a permanent creator God). PV I.152 on universals: "Oh my! It's just one disaster after another." [source: SEP-Dhk §1.1–1.3, translations as given in SEP]
- Nominalism and caste: *jāti* means both "universal/kind" and "caste"; attacking real universals undercut the metaphysics of caste (Eltschinger 2000); PV I also attacks Vedic authority. [source: SEP-Dhk §4]
- **Apoha** ("exclusion"): words and concepts do not pick out real universals but "exclusions of what is other": blue things are "non-non-blue". Dignāga (*Sāmānyaparīkṣā*, as quoted in SEP-Dhk): "A word talks about entities only as they are qualified by the negation of other things." Dharmakīrti reinterpreted it causally. Objection (Kumārila, Uddyotakara): circular — to grasp non-non-blue you must already grasp blue. Dharmakīrti's reply (Hugon): tu quoque — one must understand what a term excludes as much as what it applies to. Modern parallel: Saussure's meaning-by-difference (no historical link). [source: SEP-Dhk §2.2]
- **Triple-mark reason (*trairūpya*)** (Dharmakīrti's form): (1) the reason is a property of the subject (*pakṣadharmatva*); (2) positive pervasion: present only in similar instances (*anvaya*); (3) negative pervasion: wholly absent from dissimilar instances (*vyatireka*). Each must be "ascertained". Stock example: fire on the mountain because of smoke. Dignāga's version requires the reason to be known in at least one similar case and no counter-case known. [source: SEP-Dhk §3.1; SEP-Epi]
- Dignāga's canonical syllogism (Katsura's reconstruction): "Sound is non-eternal, because it is immediately connected with an effort; whatever is so connected is observed to be non-eternal, like a pot; whatever is eternal is observed not to be so connected, like space." Distinction between inference for oneself and inference for others (his coinage); the *hetucakra* (wheel of reasons), a 3×3 matrix of nine reason types. [source: SEP-Log §4.1; SEP-Dhk §3.1]
- Dharmakīrti's inference-for-others has two members; stating the conclusion becomes a "point of defeat" in the *Vādanyāya*. Three kinds of good reason: identity of nature ("a tree because a śiṃśapā"), causation ("fire because smoke"), non-perception. [source: SEP-Dhk §3.2; SEP-Epi]
- Influence on Nyāya: Uddyotakara "owes almost everything to his Buddhist adversaries, as opposed to his Nyāya predecessors" in formalising inference (Phillips). [source: SEP-Epi]
- Logic or not? Tillemans: calling it "logic" (after Stcherbatsky's *Buddhist Logic*, 1930/32) can mislead; formal validity is not separately discussed; good reasons combine formal, factual, epistemic and rhetorical conditions. [source: SEP-Dhk §3.1]
- Idealism: "Both Dignāga and Dharmakīrti were, in their final positions, followers of the Yogācāra idealism", but wrote mostly on terms acceptable to realists (Sautrāntika); idealism enters after PV III.194; Dunne's "sliding scales of analysis". *Sahopalambhaniyama*: blue and the awareness of blue are always cognised together. [source: SEP-Dhk §1; SEP-Yog §1.2, §3.5]
- Scripture: on "radically inaccessible" matters only; "it has no claim to certainty"; perception and reasoning trump one's own school's scriptures. Later "lord of reasoning" in Tibet. [source: SEP-Dhk §4]

## 14. Dharmakīrti on other minds and on rebirth
- Other minds (*Saṃtānāntarasiddhi*): my intentional actions are preceded by my volitions; I observe similar-seeming actions not preceded by my volitions; so probably other volitions, other minds cause them. The inference uses only *appearances* of actions, so an idealist can use it as well as a realist; it is inductive, giving probability not proof, and cannot reveal the specific nature of other minds. Ratnakīrti (11th c.) rejected it (the action–volition link is observed only in one's own case) and embraced ultimate solipsism. [source: SEP-Yog §3.6; SEP-Dhk §1]
- Rebirth: Dharmakīrti argued for rebirth, karma, the Four Noble Truths and the Buddha's authority "in extenso in the second chapter of Pramāṇavārttika" (the *Pramāṇasiddhi* chapter). [source: SEP-Dhk §4; WP-Dharmakirti] Structure (second-hand only): an argument against Cārvāka physicalism; Dan Arnold's reading: "sentient phenomena must have among their causes events that are themselves sentient" (a homogeneous cause, *sabhāgahetu*); key studies: Eli Franco, *Dharmakīrti on Compassion and Rebirth* (1997; title and year confirmed in SEP-MindB bibliography); Christian Coseru, "Consciousness and Causality: Dharmakīrti Against Physicalism". [source: WS-4, WS-5] **Writer: keep this to one or two sentences; do not reconstruct premises beyond "consciousness can only arise from prior consciousness, so the first moment of a life's consciousness needs a prior mental cause".** The last clause is the standard gist but was not verified in a source read here; see NOT VERIFIED.

## 15. Spread to Tibet
- Śāntarakṣita (725–788; IEP-Madh, WP): went to Tibet "in about 763" and became first abbot of Samye (Bsam-yas); died 788; succeeded by Kamalaśīla (c. 740–795). *Tattvasaṃgraha* (3,645 verses, 26 chapters, a survey of Indian philosophy) with Kamalaśīla's *Pañjikā*; *Madhyamakālaṃkāra* (97 stanzas) with the "neither one nor many" argument; vv. 92–93: by mind-only one stops taking external objects as real, by Madhyamaka one sees nothing has substance, and one rides "the chariot of both systems". [source: SEP-Madh §8; IEP-Madh §4]
- Samye dates: foundation 763 or 775, consecration 767 or 779 (the *Testament of Ba*'s "hare" and "sheep" years are ambiguous); first seven Tibetan monks ordained c. 779. Invited by king Trisong Detsen (c. 742–797). Tibetan tradition adds Padmasambhava subduing local spirits (legend). [source: WP-Samye; WP-Śāntarakṣita; WP-Trisong_Detsen]
- **Samye debate: legend vs evidence.** Tibetan accounts: Trisong Detsen hosted a debate (usually dated 792–794) between the Chinese Chan monk Moheyan ("sudden" awakening, no-thought) and Kamalaśīla ("gradual" path); Kamalaśīla won, Chan was banned. Evidence and doubts: "many Chinese sources claim Moheyan won" (a Dunhuang Chinese record, Wangxi's *Dunwu dasheng zhengli jue*); historicity questioned by Tucci, Gómez (1983), Ruegg (1992); Arnold: "the occurrence of the debate itself may be apocryphal", though gradualism is reflected in Kamalaśīla's three *Bhāvanākrama* texts. Often misnamed "Council of Lhasa" (after Demiéville's 1952 book); Samye is far from Lhasa. Legend that Kamalaśīla was later murdered "by squeezing his kidneys". [source: IEP-Madh §4; WP-Samye_Debate; WP-Kamalaśīla; WP-Trisong_Detsen]
- Second dissemination: Atiśa Dīpaṃkaraśrījñāna (982–1054), from Bengal, at Vikramaśīla; went to Tibet and wrote the *Bodhipathapradīpa* (Lamp for the Path to Awakening) there; died at Nyêthang. His influence partly explains Tibetan preference for Candrakīrti. [source: IEP-Madh §4, §6; WP-Atiśa]
- Tibetan doxography (*grub mtha'*, Skt. *siddhānta*): ascending hierarchy Vaibhāṣika → Sautrāntika → Yogācāra → Svātantrika → Prāsaṅgika. Prāsaṅgika "became the official philosophical position of Tibetan Buddhism" (Westerhoff). [source: IEP-Madh §6; SEP-Nag intro]
- Dge-lugs curriculum, five topics: Vinaya (Guṇaprabha), Abhidharma (Vasubandhu's *Abhidharmakośa*), epistemology (Dharmakīrti's *Pramāṇavārttika*), Madhyamaka (Candrakīrti's *Madhyamakāvatāra*), stages of the path (*Abhisamayālaṃkāra*). [source: IEP-Madh §6]
- **Tsongkhapa** Losang Drakpa (1357–1419), born October 1357 in Tsongkha (Amdo); over 300 works; *Lam rim chen mo* (1402), *Essence of Eloquence* (1407), *Ocean of Reasoning* on the MMK (1408), *Illuminating the Intent* on the *Madhyamakāvatāra* (1418). Defended Candrakīrti's Prāsaṅgika against Bhāviveka while integrating Dharmakīrti's epistemology; "a robust realism about the conventional world"; emptiness is the ultimate truth "but even this is only true conventionally". Founded Ganden monastery 1409; his followers became the Dge-lugs. [source: SEP-Tsong intro, §1; IEP-Madh §6; WP-Je_Tsongkhapa]
- Critic: Go-rams-pa (1429–1489, Sa-skya) charged that Tsongkhapa confused thinking about the ultimate with the ultimate and so made the ultimate merely conventional. [source: IEP-Madh §6]
- Tibetan debate practice (formal monastic debate, challenger and defender; invocation of Mañjuśrī) is "central in the Gelug sect". [source: IEP-Tib]

## 16. China (cross-reference only)
- Kumārajīva (344–413 per WP; 350–409 per IEP-Madh) translated the MMK (*Zhong lun*), the *Twelve Gate Treatise* and Āryadeva's *Hundred Treatise*: basis of the Sanlun ("Three Treatise") school; also the *Da zhidu lun* (*Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa*) attributed to Nāgārjuna. The Chinese canon lacks Candrakīrti, Dharmakīrti and Śāntarakṣita. [source: IEP-Madh §7]
- Yogācāra in China: Xuanzang brought Dharmapāla's line; *Cheng weishi lun*; Paramārtha (6th c.) and the ninth "taintless consciousness". [source: SEP-Yog §1.2] → Chinese Buddhism and the Rise of Neo-Confucianism.

## 17. Comparisons
- Pyrrhonism: Arnold: Madhyamaka "often … interpreted by modern scholars as having affinities with Hellenistic skepticism". Thomas McEvilley argued Nāgārjuna was influenced by Pyrrhonism, or similar to Sextus Empiricus; Adrian Kuzminski (2008) likewise. [source: IEP-Madh §3; WP-MMK]
- Zeno: parallels with MMK 2 on motion have been drawn (Siderits & O'Brien 1976), though Westerhoff thinks the aims were different. [source: SEP-Nag §3.2]
- Interpretive history: Kantian (Stcherbatsky 1927; Murti 1955), analytic (Robinson 1957; Hayes calls the arguments "fallacies and tricks"), Wittgensteinian/therapeutic, Westerhoff's anti-foundationalism, Garfield's Gelug-influenced reading, Priest's formal model. [source: WP-MMK; SEP-Nag §4; IEP-Madh §3]

## Debates
- **Mahāyāna origins**: lay origin (Przyluski, Lamotte, Hirakawa) vs Mahāsāṃghika origin (Kern and others) vs forest-ascetic hypothesis (Harrison, Nattier, Ray) vs Schopen's "cult of the book"; David Drewes rejects all (no evidence for book shrines; sūtras favour oral/aural practice; few advocate forest-dwelling). Scattered groups rather than one movement (Schopen, Harrison, Nattier). [source: WP-Mahayana]
- **Nāgārjuna: sceptic, nihilist, metaphysician or therapist?** Sceptic/vaitaṇḍika (Berger; Mills, already in Periergia reading lists); nihilist (an Indian charge with modern supporters, Spackman); anti-foundationalist metaphysics (Westerhoff); transcendental/deflationist (Arnold's options); therapeutic/Wittgensteinian. [source: SEP-Nag §2; IEP-Madh §3; IEP-Nag §2]
- **What "no thesis" means** (§5). [IEP-Nag; IEP-Madh; WS-2]
- **Does being caused entail being empty?** Siderits vs Westerhoff, ongoing. [SEP-Nag §3.1]
- **Prāsaṅgika vs Svātantrika**: method only, or ontology (Tibetan view)? Also a later Tibetan classification. [IEP-Madh §4; SEP-2T-India §4; SEP-Madh §5.2]
- **Tsongkhapa vs Go-rams-pa** on the two truths. [IEP-Madh §6]
- **Yogācāra: idealism, epistemic idealism or phenomenology?** (§12). [SEP-Yog §3.1; SEP-Vasu §5]
- **Origin of Yogācāra idealism**: meditation (Schmithausen, Griffiths) vs dream/illusion reflection (Sharf). [SEP-Yog §3.3]
- **One Vasubandhu or two?** Frauwallner vs continuity scholars; Kritzer. [SEP-Vasu §1.1]
- **Dharmakīrti's dates** (6th vs 7th c.) and **was he a pragmatist?** (Powers, Cabezón, Katsura vs Tillemans) [SEP-Dhk intro, §2.1]

## Consistency with published Periergia texts
- *The Buddha and Early Buddhist Philosophy*: says svabhāva, absent from the discourses, "became Nāgārjuna's target" — **consistent** (SEP-Nag: Ābhidharmikas "one of the main targets"); but note Nāgārjuna also targets Nyāya realism (SEP-Nag §3.4, IEP-Nag), so do not say the Abhidharma was his only target. It also says the Sautrāntika "seeds" were "a precursor of the Yogācāra school's thought" — consistent with SEP-Yog §2.2/§3.3. Its "conventionally real" chariot and "later Buddhists formalised this as a distinction between conventional and ultimate truth" — consistent; cross-refer. It gives Vasubandhu no date; this chapter should use "4th–5th century".
- *Jains, Ājīvikas and Materialists*: Jayarāśi's method "borrowed from the Madhyamaka of Nāgārjuna, … refute every version of an opponent's thesis without asserting the opposite" — **consistent** with IEP-Nag's vaitaṇḍika description. Its statement that Buddhists in Dharmakīrti's tradition ground *vyāpti* in identity of nature or causation — **consistent** with SEP-Epi (Dharmakīrti's three kinds of reason; the third, non-perception, could be added here).
- *Hellenistic Philosophy*: "Scholars have also noted parallels with the Buddhist Nāgārjuna" — **consistent** (IEP-Madh; WP-MMK: McEvilley, Kuzminski). This chapter should present influence claims as contested.
- *What Is Philosophy?*: Nāgārjuna "writing at roughly the same time" as the *Nyāya-sūtra* (c. 2nd c.) "attacked its key terms one by one" — **consistent** (SEP-Nag on *Vaidalyaprakaraṇa*). Its Dharmottara (c. 770) mirage case is a later member of the Dharmakīrti tradition; can be cross-referred.
- *History of Philosophy* overview: "Nāgārjuna (c. 150 CE) arguing that all things are 'empty' of intrinsic nature, deploying destructive tetralemmas that read like proto-deconstruction, and Dignāga and Dharmakīrti (5th–7th c.)" — dates **consistent**; "proto-deconstruction" is one interpretation among several (IEP-Madh mentions post-structuralist readings), not a fact — the chapter should not echo it unqualified.
- *Ancient Beginnings* overview: no claims about Mahāyāna; nothing to reconcile.
- Earlier research notes (`ancient--early-buddhism.md`) listed Siderits, *Buddhism as Philosophy*, "first-edition year not verified": SEP bibliographies give **2007** (Ashgate/Hackett).
- **No contradictions found** requiring correction of published texts.
