## The word "philosophy" and its origin story
- "Philosophy" comes from Ancient Greek *philosophía*, "love of wisdom", from *phílos* ("loving, friend of") and *sophía* ("wisdom"). [source: WP-PHIL]
- Wikipedia: "Some sources say that the term was coined by the pre-Socratic philosopher Pythagoras, but this is not certain." [source: WP-PHIL]
- The story that Pythagoras invented the word "philosophy" "goes back to the early Academy, since it is first found in Heraclides of Pontus (Cicero, Tusc. V 3.8; Diogenes Laertius, Proem)". SEP treats it as one manifestation of the later tradition's "attempt to glorify Pythagoras". [source: SEP-PYTH]
- Why the story is doubted (SEP): it appeared not in a historical or biographical text but in a dialogue about Empedocles reviving a woman who had stopped breathing; and it depends on a conception of the philosopher as lacking knowledge but striving for it, between ignorance and knowledge, which is "thoroughly Platonic" (cf. Plato, *Symposium* 204a). Walter Burkert (1960) showed it could not belong to the historical Pythagoras. Riedweg (2005) attempted a partial defence; Huffman (2008) replied; see also Zhmud (2012). So: name it as a later legend whose reliability is doubted, not as fact. [source: SEP-PYTH]
- The word entered English mainly from Old French and Anglo-Norman from around 1175 CE (via Latin *philosophia*). [source: WP-PHIL]
- Before the modern age "philosophy" covered most rational inquiry, including the sciences; "natural philosophy" included physics, chemistry and biology. Example: Newton's 1687 *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, now considered a physics book. The narrower modern sense arrived toward the end of the modern period. [source: WP-PHIL]
- Arabic *falsafa* is a loan word from Greek *philosophía*. [source: SEP-AIG] Two Islamic-world terms are sometimes translated "philosophy": *falsafa* (which also covered logic, mathematics and physics) and *kalām* (literally "speech", rationalist scholastic theology: Ash'arism, Maturidism, Mu'tazilism). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_philosophy]
- Classical Indian traditions are often called *darśana* ("to see", "looking at"); *ānvīkṣikī* ("critical inquiry", "investigation") was the term used by classical Indian authors such as Kauṭilya in the *Arthaśāstra*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_philosophy]
- Japanese *tetsugaku* (哲学), the modern word for philosophy: Nishi Amane (1829–1897) is credited with coining it "sometime in 1867 or earlier" as a translation of Dutch *filosofie*; it first appeared in print in 1874. Earlier he had used *kitetsugaku* (希哲學, "admiring wisdom-study"). The Japanese coinage became the standard academic word for philosophy in Chinese (*zhéxué*), Korean and Vietnamese. Nishi himself did not think philosophy, in the Western sense, existed in the East. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nishi_Amane]
- Kasulis (SEP) on the choice of *tetsugaku*: *-gaku* was the suffix for the new Western-style university disciplines (equivalent to German *Wissenschaft*), so academic philosophers became *tetsugakusha*, "scholars who specialize in the Wissenschaft of wisdom", rather than the more traditional *tetsujin*, "wise people"; Kasulis uses this to contrast detached knowing (the geologist's knowledge of clay) with engaged knowing (the master potter's). [source: SEP-JP]

## What makes a question philosophical (definitions and conceptions)
- Wikipedia's (featured-article) definition: philosophy "is a systematic study of general and fundamental questions concerning topics like existence, knowledge, mind, reason, language, and value. It is a rational and critical inquiry that reflects on its methods and assumptions." [source: WP-PHIL]
- Attempts at a precise definition are controversial and are studied in metaphilosophy; some see essential features, others only "family resemblances", others an empty blanket term. Definitions by method (e.g. pure reasoning) or by topic (the "big questions") tend to be too wide or too narrow. [source: WP-PHIL]
- Kant is reported as uniting philosophy's task under four questions: "What can I know?", "What should I do?", "What may I hope?", and "What is the human being?" (Wikipedia gives no passage; see NOT VERIFIED for the exact source text). [source: WP-PHIL]
- One family of definitions makes philosophy a provisional science whose subdisciplines "cease to be philosophy once they have fully developed" ("the midwife of the sciences"); Quine is cited as a naturalist for whom philosophy is continuous with empirical science. [source: WP-PHIL]
- Wittgenstein: philosophy as a kind of therapy, dispelling misunderstandings caused by the confusing structure of ordinary language. Husserl: philosophy as a "rigorous science" of essences, via suspension (*epoché*) of theoretical assumptions. [source: WP-PHIL]
- Ancient Greek and Roman conception: philosophy as a spiritual practice and way of life (Hadot 1995); e.g. the Stoics treated philosophy as training for *eudaimonia*. [source: WP-PHIL]
- Wilfrid Sellars defined philosophy (as quoted and endorsed by Rorty) as "an attempt to see how things, in the broadest possible sense of the term, hang together, in the broadest possible sense of the term" (IEP's wording; original in Sellars, *Science, Perception and Reality*, 1963). [source: IEP-META]
- Metaphilosophy is "the philosophy of philosophy"; Williamson's *The Philosophy of Philosophy* (2007) is a standard work. [source: IEP-META]
- Latin American metaphilosophy: Augusto Salazar Bondy (1925–1974) called such debates "the philosophy of philosophy" (1968, p. 13). [source: SEP-LAM]

## Arguments: premises, conclusions, validity, soundness
- An argument (in the technical sense) is "a collection of truth-bearers ... some of which are offered as reasons for one of them, the conclusion"; the reasons are "premises". This differs from "argument" as a quarrel. [source: IEP-ARG]
- Premise indicators: "because", "since", "for", "as"; conclusion indicators: "therefore", "thus", "hence", "so" (with the caveat that these words do not always signal an argument). Standard form lists premises, a line, then the conclusion marked "∴". [source: IEP-ARG]
- Arguments differ from explanations: an explanation offers reasons why something is true without trying to persuade that it is (IEP's paresis example). Premises can be "linked" (supporting jointly) or "convergent" (each supporting independently). [source: IEP-ARG]
- Validity (SEP): "Valid deductive arguments are those where the truth of the premises necessitates the truth of the conclusion"; "A valid argument whose premises are also true is a sound argument." Example syllogism given: All humans are living beings; all living beings are mortal; therefore all humans are mortal. [source: SEP-ARG]
- Informal gloss of validity in logic: "an argument is valid if it is not possible for its premises to all be true and its conclusion false". [source: SEP-LC]
- IEP textbook formulation: "A valid deductive argument is one whose logical structure or form is such that if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true. A sound argument is a valid argument with true premises." Inductive arguments are "strong or weak" and "cogent or not cogent": a strong inductive argument's premises render the conclusion likely; a cogent argument is a strong argument with true premises. [source: IEP-DI]
- Deduction is monotonic (adding premises cannot invalidate a valid argument); induction and abduction are "ampliative" (the conclusion goes beyond the premises). SEP's four types: deduction, induction, abduction, analogy; plus fallacies. [source: SEP-ARG]
- Modus ponens: "p; if p then q; therefore q". Induction example: "all ravens are black" from many observed black ravens; abduction example: a doctor's diagnosis from symptoms. [source: WP-PHIL]
- The deductive/inductive distinction is itself debated: Trudy Govier (1987) criticised "the hallowed old distinction"; some authors avoid defining the types and evaluate arguments by deductive vs inductive standards instead. [source: IEP-DI]
- A counterexample "is a specific example that contradicts a claim, hypothesis, or generalization"; a single counterexample disproves a universally stated claim (e.g. 2 is a counterexample to "all primes are odd"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterexample]
- Fallacies: arguments that appear legitimate but are not (Hamblin 1970). SEP says critical reflection on dubious, manipulative arguments (the Sophists) stimulated the development of philosophical method, and that opposition between rational and irrational argument marked "the Indian, Chinese, and Islamicate traditions" too; Aristotle's *Sophistical Refutations* is the classic Greek treatment. [source: SEP-ARG]
- Analogical arguments "are widely present in all known philosophical traditions, including three major ancient traditions: Greek, Chinese, and Indian"; in ancient Chinese philosophy analogy "is perhaps the main form of argumentation"; the Mohists were particularly interested in it. Plato's *Gorgias* compares rhetoric to pastry-baking and philosophy to medicine. [source: SEP-ARG]

## Arguments across traditions (examples for the chapter)
- India — the five-membered argument: the *Nyāya-sūtra* (attributed to Gautama, also called Akṣapāda, c. 2nd century CE; final redaction perhaps 3rd century CE) takes the "syllogism" to have five parts (NS 1.1.32), as does the medical *Caraka-saṃhitā* (CS 3.8.31). The *Caraka-saṃhitā*'s illustration: proposition "The soul is eternal"; reason "because of being un-produced"; example "Space is unproduced and it is eternal"; application "And just as space is unproduced, so is the soul"; conclusion "Therefore, the soul is eternal." Much of the *Nyāya-sūtra* concerns debate (all of Book 5, Book 1 ch. 2). [source: SEP-LI]
- Nāgārjuna's *Vaidalya-prakaraṇa*, roughly contemporaneous, attacks each key term of NS 1.1.1 and judges counter-arguments fallacious as circular or regressive. Buddhist debate manuals include Vasubandhu's *Vāda-vidhi*. [source: SEP-LI]
- Indian theory of knowledge (*pramāṇa-śāstra*) spans "almost twenty centuries"; the principal knowledge sources (*pramāṇa*) are perception, inference and testimony; the Cārvāka materialists accepted only perception and raised the problem of induction, which Buddhists and Naiyāyikas turned into an argument for fallibilism about inference. [source: SEP-EI]
- Indian schools are traditionally classed as *āstika* (orthodox: Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta) and *nāstika* (heterodox: Jain, Buddhist, Ājīvika, Ajñāna, Cārvāka), by acceptance of Vedic authority; this terminology is "a scholarly construct" and sources disagree on the lists. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_philosophy]
- China — the Mohists: Mohism flourished in the Warring States era (479–221 BCE), from Mo Di/"Mozi" (fl. ca. 430 BCE). "Mozi and his followers initiated philosophical argumentation and debate in China", were "the first in the tradition to engage, like Socrates in ancient Greece, in an explicit, reflective search for objective moral standards and to give step-by-step, tightly reasoned arguments", and "advanced the world's earliest form of consequentialism". [source: SEP-MO]
- The Mohist "three *fa*" (standards) for statements: a "root" (precedent of the sage kings), a "source" (what people see and hear) and a "use" (benefit to state, clan and people when adopted in government), from *Mozi* Book 35, "Condemning Fatalism". [source: SEP-MO]
- Wong (SEP) on Chinese wariness of rule-deduction: *Zhuangzi* ch. 17 notes that Yao's abdication to Shun was glorious but Kuai's imitation was disastrous, so it is impossible to establish "any constant rule". [source: SEP-CW]

## Worked argument 1 (counterexample): the Gettier problem
- The "tripartite" (JTB) analysis: S knows that P iff (1) P is true, (2) S believes P, (3) S is justified in believing P. SEP warns that calling this "the traditional analysis" is "a bit of a convenient fiction": it "was primarily articulated in the 20th century by its attackers" (citing Kaplan 1985, Dutant 2015, Antognazza 2015, Le Morvan 2017). [source: SEP-KA]
- Plato is often cited as the origin of the justification condition: in the *Theaetetus* Socrates notes that true opinion produced by a lawyer's sophistry is not knowledge. Wikipedia cites *Meno* 97a–98b and the *Theaetetus*. [source: SEP-KA; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gettier_problem]
- Edmund Gettier, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?", *Analysis* 23 (1963): 121–3. He presented two cases "in which a true belief is inferred from a justified false belief". [source: IEP-GET; SEP-KA]
- Gettier's case II (quoted in SEP from Gettier 1963: 123): Smith has strong evidence that (f) Jones owns a Ford; he infers (g) "Either Jones owns a Ford, or Brown is in Boston", (h) "... or Brown is in Barcelona", (i) "... or Brown is in Brest-Litovsk". Jones in fact does not own a Ford (he is driving a rented car), but "by the sheerest coincidence" Brown is in Barcelona. Smith has a justified true belief in (h) but intuitively does not know it. [source: SEP-KA]
- Step by step: P1 If JTB is correct, every justified true belief is knowledge. P2 Smith's belief in (h) is true, believed and justified. P3 Smith does not know (h). C JTB is false (JTB is not sufficient for knowledge). [reconstruction from SEP-KA; IEP-GET: "JTB is false if there is even one actual or possible Gettier situation"]
- Main objection/repair: add a fourth condition, "no false lemmas" (S's belief is not inferred from any falsehood); Smith's belief was inferred from the false "Jones owns a Ford". [source: SEP-KA]
- Reply: Gettier-style cases without inference. SEP's robot-dog case (James sees a perfect robot dog; a real dog is hidden nearby) and the Barn County ("fake barns") case, "first given by Carl Ginet, and published by Alvin Goldman (1976)": Henry, driving through a county full of barn facades, looks at the one real barn; his justified true belief is not inferred from a falsehood, yet seems lucky and not knowledge. So "no false lemmas" fails. [source: SEP-KA]
- Precursors (SEP): the Indian Buddhist Dharmottara, c. 770 CE (mirage case: we think we see water, it is a mirage, but water is found under a rock at the spot; quoted from Dreyfus 1997: 292); Śrīharṣa gave similar cases in the 12th century; Peter of Mantua (14th century; Plato mistaken for Socrates running while Socrates happens to be running in Rome); Bertrand Russell's stopped clock. Wikipedia adds that Dharmottara's examples are in his commentary on Dharmakīrti's *Ascertainment of Knowledge* (*Pramāṇa-viniścaya*), and that Meinong also acknowledged the problem. [source: SEP-KA; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gettier_problem]
- Russell's stopped-clock wording (as quoted in SEP): "If you look at a clock which you believe to be going, but which in fact has stopped, and you happen to look at it at a moment when it is right, you will acquire a true belief as to the time of day, but you cannot be correctly said to have knowledge." Source book: *Human Knowledge: Its Scope and Limits*, which SEP-RUS dates 1948 (SEP-KA's bibliography misprints it as 1923; use 1948). [source: SEP-KA; SEP-RUS]
- It is "nearly universally accepted amongst contemporary epistemologists that there are counterexamples to the analysis of knowledge as justified true belief", though most agree each element is necessary. [source: SEP-KA]
- Experimental philosophy: Weinberg, Nichols and Stich (2001) reported that Gettier intuitions might vary across cultures (often treated as the start of x-phi); Wikipedia reports that subsequent studies "consistently failed to replicate these results", finding people across cultures share the Gettier intuition. [source: SEP-XPHI; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gettier_problem]

## Thought experiments
- SEP: "Thought experiments are basically devices of the imagination", employed for entertainment, education, conceptual analysis, exploration, hypothesizing, theory selection and more; their typical shape: "we visualize some situation that we have set up in the imagination; we let it run or we carry out an operation; we see what happens; finally, we draw a conclusion." [source: SEP-TE]
- Lucretius' spear (*De Rerum Natura* 1.951–987): toss a spear at the supposed edge of the universe; if it flies through, there was no edge; if it bounces back, something beyond the edge stopped it. Either way there is no edge, so space is infinite. SEP uses it to show both the method and its fallibility (we now know space can be finite but unbounded). [source: SEP-TE]
- Galileo's falling bodies: the first part draws a contradiction from Aristotle's theory, since a cannon ball and musket ball tied together would have to fall both faster and slower than the cannon ball alone (a "destructive" thought experiment). [source: SEP-TE]
- Other standard examples listed by SEP: Newton's bucket, Heisenberg's gamma-ray microscope, Einstein's elevator, Leibniz's mill, Parfit's people who split like amoebas, Thomson's violinist, Searle's Chinese room, Putnam's twin earth, Jackson's Mary the colour scientist, Schrödinger's cat. "Philosophy, even more than the sciences, would be severely impoverished without thought experiments." [source: SEP-TE]
- Thomson's violinist (Thomson 1971), step by step as SEP gives it: the anti-abortion argument runs: a fetus is an innocent person; all innocent persons have a right to life; abortion results in the death of a fetus; therefore abortion is wrong. Thomson's parallel: you wake hooked up to a famous unconscious violinist who needs your kidneys for nine months; the same-structured argument ("unhooking him is wrong") seems unconvincing. Lesson: it separates "right to life" from "right to what is needed to sustain life". Thomson allows a fetal right to life only "for the sake of the argument". Objections: dismiss the method (Dancy 1985), or "rethink" the scenario. [source: SEP-TE]
- SEP-ARG classifies the violinist and Chinese Room as analogical arguments; their "Achilles' heel" is whether source and target are relevantly similar. [source: SEP-ARG]
- History of the term: Ernst Mach is "commonly credited" with introducing "thought experiment" (*Gedankenexperiment*), but SEP says this "is incorrect": the term was used by 1811, in Danish (*Tankeexperiment*) by Hans Christian Ørsted; Georg Lichtenberg (1742–1799) had a tacit theory of "experiments with thoughts". [source: SEP-TE]
- Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna): the case has been made (Kukkonen 2014) that he is "the first philosopher in the Aristotelian tradition, and thus perhaps the first in Western philosophy overall", to try to identify the psychological processes behind hypothetical reasoning; thought experiments are also found in Islamic and Jewish contexts (McGinnis 2018; Fisch 2019). Aristotle himself used thought experiments for persuasion (Corcilius 2018). [source: SEP-TE]
- Debate on how thought experiments give knowledge: James Robert Brown's Platonism (some thought experiments give a priori, fallible knowledge of laws of nature) vs John D. Norton's "argument view" (every thought experiment is a possibly disguised argument from empirical premises; the "picturesque features" are "strictly redundant"). These "represent the extremes of platonic rationalism and classic empiricism". Objections to Norton: where do the premises come from; an argument-form version loses force. Objection to Brown: no criteria for good vs bad thought experiments. [source: SEP-TE]
- The trolley problem: the general question was raised in 1967 by Philippa Foot ("The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect", *Oxford Review* 5, 1967) in discussing abortion and double effect; Judith Jarvis Thomson named it "the trolley problem" in a 1976 article (*The Monist*); Thomson also published "The Trolley Problem" (1985). Earlier versions: Frank Chapman Sharp's 1905 questionnaire; Karl Engisch 1930; Hans Welzel 1951; Avrohom Yeshaya Karelitz's Talmud commentary 1953. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trolley_problem] (SEP-DA cites the reprints: Foot 1978, Thomson 1986.)
- Foot's pair (SEP-DA): a judge who can stop a riot only by framing and executing one innocent, versus a tram driver who can steer from five workmen onto one. Foot said killing was wrong in the first, not the second, explaining this by doing vs allowing (the driver chooses between killing one and killing five). Thomson moved the choice to a bystander, who chooses between killing one and letting five die, which undermined Foot's explanation; in 2008 Thomson changed her mind and argued the bystander may not turn the trolley. [source: SEP-DA]
- Ship of Theseus: Plutarch, *Life of Theseus* 23.1, reports that the Athenians kept Theseus' thirty-oared ship until the time of Demetrius of Phalerum, replacing rotten planks, so that it "became a standing example among the philosophers" for "things that grow". Hobbes, *De Corpore* (1655; Wikipedia's quoted English translation 1656, "Of Identity and Difference") added a custodian who rebuilds a second ship from the old planks. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_of_Theseus]
- SEP's version of the puzzle: one argument says the museum ship is Theseus' (boats survive gradual replacement), another says the custodian's is (things survive disassembly and reassembly); together they imply the ship "survives twice over", and run backwards, two ships occupied the same place at the start. [source: SEP-MC]

## Intuitions, method and the "progress" question
- Methods listed by Wikipedia: conceptual analysis, common sense and intuitions, thought experiments, ordinary-language analysis, phenomenological description, critical questioning; reflective equilibrium; the pragmatic maxim (Peirce); methodological naturalism. [source: WP-PHIL]
- Debate over whether philosophers really use intuitions as evidence (e.g. in reading Gettier's paper): one side sees a scenario plus an intuitive verdict; the other a "(brief) informal argument for his case verdict" (Horvath 2023: 89). PhilPapers' "experimental philosophy" listings grew from under 100 in 2007 to more than 3,400 by January 2025. [source: SEP-XPHI]
- Russell's argument about philosophy's lack of settled results (see Quotations): disciplines that achieve definite knowledge leave philosophy and get their own names (his example: astronomy); what remains are unanswered questions. [source: search excerpts of *Problems of Philosophy* ch. XV; see Quotations for status]

## Why philosophy keeps reading its past
- IEP: "For a long time, most analytic philosophers held that the history of philosophy had little to do with doing philosophy", seeing it as "largely, a series of mistakes"; "in the 1970s a more positive attitude to the history of philosophy began to emerge". Reasons (Sorell and Rogers 2005): history can disclose our assumptions, show the strengths of positions we find uncongenial, and suggest roles for philosophy by showing how it was embedded in wider frameworks. [source: IEP-META]
- Charles Taylor (1984: 17): "Philosophy and the history of philosophy are one"; "we cannot do the first without also doing the second." IEP: many analytic philosophers still treat history as secondary, while many Continental philosophers take this (Hegel-associated) view as axiomatic. [source: IEP-META]
- As a discipline, the history of philosophy asks questions intellectual history does not, such as whether past theories are true and still relevant. [source: WP-PHIL]
- The Socratic problem: Socrates wrote nothing; "All our information about him is second-hand and most of it vigorously disputed"; "Each age, each intellectual turn, produces a Socrates of its own" (Guthrie 1969: "There is, and always will be, a 'Socratic problem'"). A good illustration of why the past is re-read. [source: SEP-SOC]
- SEP notes Socrates' trial and death "is nevertheless the founding myth of the academic discipline of philosophy". [source: SEP-SOC]

## Socrates and Plato on examination and wonder
- Socrates lived 469–399 BCE; born in Athens to Sophroniscus (a stonemason) and Phaenarete (a midwife). [source: IEP-SOC; SEP-SOC]
- The Delphic oracle told Chaerephon "no one is wiser than Socrates" (*Apology* 21a); Socrates tested politicians, poets and craftsmen, and concluded he was better off because he was aware of his ignorance (*Apology* 23a: human wisdom is worth little or nothing). [source: IEP-SOC]
- IEP: "Socrates does not claim here that he knows nothing. He claims that he is aware of his ignorance and that whatever it is that he does know is worthless." (So "I know that I know nothing" is a paraphrase; see NOT VERIFIED.) [source: IEP-SOC]
- The elenchus: cross-examination of a position, usually starting from "what is it?" (e.g. Euthyphro's five attempted definitions of piety, 5d–14a); scholars debate what it refutes and whether it can prove anything. Plato's Socrates also calls himself a midwife (*Theaetetus*). [source: IEP-SOC; SEP: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plato-theaetetus/]
- Socrates calls himself a gadfly and Athens a sluggish horse (*Apology* 30e). [source: IEP-SOC]
- *Apology* 38a: after conviction (IEP: after being "sentenced to death"; Wikipedia: after he chose death rather than exile) Socrates says he could never keep silent, because "the unexamined life is not worth living for human beings". Greek (38a5–6): ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ, literally "but the unexamined life is not livable for a man". [source: IEP-SOC; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_unexamined_life_is_not_worth_living]
- Plato, *Theaetetus* 155d: after Theaetetus says he is dizzy with puzzlement (155c), Socrates says wonder (*thaumazein*) belongs to the philosopher. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theaetetus_(dialogue)] Fowler's 1921 Loeb translation (as hosted on Perseus): "For this feeling of wonder shows that you are a philosopher, since wonder is the only beginning of philosophy." [source: web-search excerpts of Perseus/Goodreads/blog attributing Fowler; see Quotations]
- Aristotle, *Metaphysics* I.2 (982b): wonder is what led people to philosophise; they first puzzled over obvious difficulties, then greater ones (the moon, sun and stars, the origin of the universe); one who is puzzled thinks himself ignorant, so the lover of myth is in a sense a lover of wisdom; this knowledge was sought once necessities were secured. Bekker line given as 982b12 in common citations; a Sachs-translation summary cites 982b4–10. [source: web-search excerpts of Ross's translation on Wikisource/MIT/ellopos.net; exact line not checked against a clean text]

## Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912)
- Published 1912; Russell called it his "shilling shocker"; written for a general audience; it introduces the knowledge by acquaintance / by description distinction; theory of knowledge occupies more space than metaphysics (preface). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Problems_of_Philosophy; SEP-RUS cites it as 1912a]
- Russell (1872–1970); Order of Merit 1949; Nobel Prize for Literature 1950. [source: SEP-RUS]
- Chapter XV, "The Value of Philosophy": the value of philosophy for those who do not study it must be indirect, through its effects on those who do; "The answers suggested by philosophy are none of them demonstrably true"; "The value of philosophy is, in fact, to be sought largely in its very uncertainty"; and philosophy has a value, "perhaps its chief value, through the greatness of the objects which it contemplates, and the freedom from narrow and personal aims resulting from this contemplation." [source: web-search excerpts of ch. XV (paulgraham.com, Gutenberg, americanliterature.com copies); full text not fetchable]
- Wikipedia quotes (citing Russell 1912 p. 91): "the man who has no tincture of philosophy goes through life imprisoned in the prejudices derived from common sense, from the habitual beliefs of his age or his nation, and from convictions which have grown up in his mind without the cooperation or consent of his deliberate reason." [source: WP-PHIL]

## The main branches
- Main branches often listed: epistemology, ethics, logic, metaphysics; other major subfields: aesthetics, philosophy of language, mind, religion, science, political philosophy (also mathematics, history). Divisions "are neither exhaustive nor mutually exclusive"; ethics, political philosophy and aesthetics are sometimes grouped as "value theory". [source: WP-PHIL]
- Epistemology: what knowledge is, how it arises, its limits and value; truth, belief, justification; sources (perception, introspection, memory, inference, testimony); empiricism vs rationalism; the regress problem (foundationalism vs coherentism); scepticism. [source: WP-PHIL]
- Ethics: metaethics, normative ethics (consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics), applied ethics. [source: WP-PHIL]
- Logic: formal and informal logic; deductive, inductive and abductive arguments; formal and informal fallacies. [source: WP-PHIL]
- Metaphysics: most general features of reality (existence, objects and properties, parts and wholes, space and time, causation); ontology; identity through change; particulars vs universals; determinism and free will; "Why is there something rather than nothing?" [source: WP-PHIL]
- Mind: the mind–body problem (materialism, idealism, dualism; functionalism) and the hard problem of consciousness. Language: meaning, reference, truth; prominence in early-20th-century analytic philosophy through Frege and Russell. Science: demarcation from pseudoscience, explanation, laws. Political: legitimacy, justice, liberty, equality. Aesthetics: beauty, aesthetic experience, works of art. Religion: differs from theology in not presupposing one tradition's assumptions. [source: WP-PHIL]

## The main traditions (facts for the overview section)
- Wikipedia names Western, Arabic–Persian, Indian and Chinese as influential traditions, plus Japanese, Latin American and African. [source: WP-PHIL]
- Greek: Western philosophy began in 6th-century BCE Greece with the Presocratics, e.g. Thales (c. 626 – c. 545 BCE); then Socrates (469–399), Plato (427–347), Aristotle (384–322 BCE), per Wikipedia's dates. [source: WP-PHIL]
- Indian: Vedas and Upanishads (2nd–1st millennia BCE); Buddhism and Jainism as non-Vedic movements; classical period from roughly 200 BCE with the six orthodox schools; Śaṅkara (Wikipedia: c. 700–750 CE) systematised Advaita Vedānta; Rāmānuja (Wikipedia: 1017–1137) Viśiṣṭādvaita. [source: WP-PHIL]
- The Buddha's dates are disputed: SEP: "fl. circa 450 BCE"; dates "were thought to be approximately 560–480 BCE, but many scholars now hold that he must have died around 405 BCE". (WP-PHIL gives the older 563–483 BCE; do not use those as settled.) [source: SEP-BUD]
- Chinese: the Hundred Schools of the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods (Confucianism, Daoism, Mohism, Legalism); Confucianism became official ideology under the Han; Buddhism entered China in the 1st century CE; Neo-Confucianism from the 11th century (Song and Ming). Confucius: "trad. 551–479 BCE" (traditional dates); Mencius trad. 372–289 BCE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_philosophy; SEP-CON]
- Islamic: begins with al-Kindī (Wikipedia: 801–873 CE), usually regarded as the first philosopher of the tradition; Avicenna (980–1037); al-Ghazālī (1058–1111) as critic; the classical (peripatetic) period ends with Ibn Rushd/Averroes (late 12th century) in the west, but philosophy continued in the east (Safavid Iran, Ottoman and Mughal empires), e.g. Mullā Ṣadrā (1571–1636). [source: WP-PHIL; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_philosophy]
- Greek-into-Arabic translation: an outline of Aristotelian logic was available in Arabic under al-Manṣūr, who founded Baghdad (762); al-Mahdī (r. 775–785) had the *Topics* translated by Patriarch Timothy I; most philosophical translations date from al-Ma'mūn's reign. Scholars disagree whether *falsafa* arose mainly from the Greek encounter (majority view) or from Qur'anic *ḥikma*. [source: SEP-AIG]
- Jewish: includes all philosophy by Jews or about Judaism; Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BCE – c. 50 CE) harmonised scripture with Greek philosophy; medieval rationalism entered via the Geonim of 10th-century Babylonian academies; Saadia Gaon regarded as the greatest early Jewish philosopher; Maimonides the medieval summit. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_philosophy]
- Japanese: synthesis of Shintō with Confucian and Buddhist thought (both entering Japan in the 6th–7th centuries); Neo-Confucianism influential from the 16th century and in the Edo period; the 20th-century Kyoto School (absolute nothingness, *basho*). Nishida Kitarō's *An Inquiry into the Good* (1911). [source: WP-PHIL; IEP-COMP]
- African: before the 20th century largely oral; systematic academic African philosophy emerged in the 20th century; topics include ethnophilosophy, négritude, pan-Africanism, Ubuntu. Wikipedia notes "ongoing debate about what exactly constitutes 'African philosophy'". Written traditions include ancient Egypt (ma'at; *The Maxims of Ptahhotep*) and 17th-century Ethiopia (Zera Yacob and his disciple Walda Heywat). [source: WP-PHIL; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_philosophy; SEP-AF]
- Placide Tempels, a Belgian missionary priest in the Belgian Congo, published *La Philosophie Bantoue* (1945, English *Bantu Philosophy*), which "triggered much of the debate" over whether traditional sub-Saharan Africa had philosophy; Tempels claimed Bantu peoples had a philosophy but lacked conscious knowledge of it, and that he extracted it. Critics called such work "ethno-philosophy". Hountondji's *Sur la "philosophie africaine": critique de l'ethnophilosophie* (1976; English *African Philosophy: Myth and Reality*, 1983, 2nd ed. 1996). [source: SEP-AF]
- SEP places the debate in the context of colonial racial hierarchy, citing Hume's footnote in "Of National Characters" (1748) on the "natural inferiority" of Black people, and Kant (1764; 1798). [source: SEP-AF]
- H. Odera Oruka (1944–1995), Kenyan, interviewed indigenous sages (sage philosophy); his four "currents": ethnophilosophy, philosophic sagacity, nationalist-ideological philosophy, professional philosophy (Oruka 1990a). [source: SEP-AF; SEP-SAGE]
- Latin American: pre-colonial indigenous thought; colonial scholasticism from around 1550; post-colonial positivism, philosophy of liberation, identity. [source: WP-PHIL] The question whether a distinctive Latin American philosophy exists became central in the second half of the 20th century: Salazar Bondy, *¿Existe una filosofía de nuestra América?* (Mexico City: Siglo XXI, 1968) answered sceptically, blaming underdevelopment and domination; Leopoldo Zea (1912–2004) defended "circumstantialism" (concern with authenticity; Zea 1942, "En torno a una filosofía americana"); José Carlos Mariátegui (1925) lamented that Hispanic-American thought was "a rhapsody composed from the motifs and elements of European thought". Nuccetelli's counterexample: Las Casas's ethics. [source: SEP-LAM]
- Comparative philosophy (IEP): "a subfield of philosophy in which philosophers work on problems by intentionally setting into dialogue sources from across cultural, linguistic, and philosophical streams"; distinct from area-studies philosophy and "world philosophy". First East-West Philosophers' Conference: University of Hawaii, 1939. Van Norden's "Open Letter to the APA" (November 1996) complained comparative work was segregated from mainstream journals. [source: IEP-COMP]
- Hegel's early treatment of China "really cannot properly be called comparative philosophy" because it lacks "serious engagement from the Chinese side" (Jonathan Spence 1998, via IEP). [source: IEP-COMP]
- Jesuits produced the first major European translation of the *Analects*, *Confucius Sinarum Philosophus* ("Confucius the Chinese Philosopher", 1687); European philosophers in the 17th century "immediately recognized it as philosophy" (Van Norden 2017). Leibniz saw a resemblance between the *Book of Changes*' broken/unbroken lines and his binary arithmetic. [source: SEP-CW]
- Peter K. J. Park (2013) argued that the more common 18th-century view was that philosophy began in India and/or Africa and passed to Greece; Cantor (2022): Greek thinkers of antiquity mostly held that philosophy had non-Greek origins, including Egypt and Babylonia. [source: SEP-CW]

## Debates
- Is "philosophy" a Western category? One portrait holds Chinese thought is "wisdom" literature (stories and sayings meant to move people to a way of life) while Western philosophy is "systematic argumentation and theory"; "Those who identify philosophy with the latter will commonly assert that Chinese thought has no philosophy" (see Carine Defoort, "Is There Such a Thing as Chinese Philosophy? Arguments of an Implicit Debate", *Philosophy East and West* 51(3), 2001: 393–413; Van Norden 2017). Wong counters that Chinese wariness of rule-deduction (Zhuangzi ch. 17) is a philosophical position, and that 17th-century Europeans "immediately recognized" Chinese thought as philosophy. [source: SEP-CW]
- Derrida's remark: The Los Angeles Review of Books reports that in 2001, over lunch at the French consulate in Shanghai, Derrida said China "does not have any philosophy, only thought". Yijing Zhang (*Radical Philosophy* 2.04) gives the Chinese report as 中国没有哲学, 但/只有思想 and argues the choice between "but" and "only" changes the meaning (the other version: "China doesn't have philosophy, but it does however have thought"). No primary transcript was found; contemporary reports are Zhang Ning (*21st Century*, 2001) and Zhang Ning's summary of the visit in *Dao* (2002). Write it as "reportedly said" and give both versions. (How Derrida meant it, whether as a dismissal or as a remark that "philosophy" names a Greek-European lineage, is not settled by any source read here; do not characterise his intent.) [source: web search (lareviewofbooks.org; radicalphilosophy.com)]
- Nishi Amane, who coined *tetsugaku*, did not think philosophy existed in the East; his coinage nevertheless gave China, Japan and Korea the word for it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nishi_Amane]
- Does a distinctive Latin American philosophy exist? Salazar Bondy (1968) sceptical (dependence and domination); Zea's circumstantialism defends authenticity; Nuccetelli criticises the "critical theory" as reductionist and self-undermining (it is itself produced by an upper class and borrowed from Europe). [source: SEP-LAM]
- African philosophy: whether ethnophilosophy (Tempels and successors) is philosophy, versus Hountondji's critique and Oruka's sage philosophy. [source: SEP-AF; SEP-SAGE]
- Islamic philosophy's origin: Greek heritage (majority) vs Qur'anic *ḥikma* as main source. [source: SEP-AIG]
- The JTB "tradition": SEP says the "traditional" analysis was largely a 20th-century construction by its critics. [source: SEP-KA]
- Thought experiments: Brown (Platonist) vs Norton (argument view); critics of the method in ethics (Dancy 1985). Whether Gettier-style intuitions are culturally variable (Weinberg, Nichols, Stich 2001) or shared (later replications). [source: SEP-TE; SEP-XPHI; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gettier_problem]
- Value of the history of philosophy: older analytic view (history as a record of mistakes) vs the post-1970s revival and Taylor's "Philosophy and the history of philosophy are one". [source: IEP-META]
- Deductive vs inductive distinction itself contested (Govier 1987). [source: IEP-DI]
- Socratic problem: which Socrates (Plato's, Xenophon's, Aristophanes', Aristotle's) is historical. [source: SEP-SOC]
- Authorship of Zera Yacob's *Hatata* (17th century): Carlo Conti Rossini (1920) proposed it was forged by the 19th-century Capuchin missionary Giusto da Urbino; Eugen Mittwoch (1934) added linguistic arguments; Claude Sumner (1976) rejected the forgery claim; Wikipedia says most scholars now accept Zera Yacob's authorship. Mention as a live question if used. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zera_Yacob_(philosopher)]
