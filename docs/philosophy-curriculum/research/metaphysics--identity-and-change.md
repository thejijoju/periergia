## 1. Two kinds of "same": numerical and qualitative identity
- Qualitative identity = sharing properties, so it comes in degrees ("Poodles and Great Danes are qualitatively identical because they share the property of being a dog... but two poodles will (very likely) have greater qualitative identity"). Numerical identity "can only hold between a thing and itself"; it is the relation by which we count things as one. [source: SEP-Id §1]
- Gallois's framing: change "seems to be the case when a is numerically identical with b without being qualitatively identical with it, that is, when a and b have different properties at different times". Example: "The same pan is at one time cold, another time hot." [source: SEP-IdT §1]
- David Lewis's deflationary line, often quoted: "Identity is utterly simple and unproblematic. Everything is identical to itself; nothing is ever identical to anything except itself." (Lewis 1986, 192–193). [source: SEP-IdT §1]
- Identity is an equivalence relation: reflexive, symmetric and **transitive**; transitivity is what the Ship of Theseus, fission and Reid's officer all strain. [source: SEP-Id §2; SEP-IdT §4]
- Synchronic vs diachronic identity questions; Lowe: "it is an error to suppose that diachronic identity and synchronic identity are different kinds of identity". [source: SEP-Id §5]

## 2. Leibniz's Law and the identity of indiscernibles
- **Leibniz's Law = the indiscernibility of identicals**: "if x is identical with y then everything true of x is true of y". We use its contrapositive whenever we infer from "Fa" and "not-Fb" that a ≠ b. [source: SEP-Id §2]
- **Identity of indiscernibles** = the converse: if everything true of x is true of y, x = y. Trivial if "being identical with x" counts as a property; controversial if restricted to qualitative, non-relational properties. [source: SEP-Id §2]
- Leibniz's Law must not be confused with substitutivity of co-referring names ("Hesperus" has eight letters, "Phosphorus" ten). [source: SEP-Id §2]
- Leibniz considered two leaves or two drops of water as alleged counterexamples (fourth letter to Clarke); his reply was that some imperceptible difference would always exist. The Stoics and Nicholas of Cusa held the principle before him; Aquinas asserted it of separated intelligences. [source: SEP-II §1, §3]
- **Max Black, "The Identity of Indiscernibles", *Mind* 61 (1952): 153–164**: a universe containing only two iron spheres, same diameter, colour, temperature etc., at a distance from each other. If possible, at least some versions of the principle are false. SEP: Black's thought experiment was "provocative, if not completely original" and "set much of the agenda" since the 1950s. [source: SEP-II §1; bibliography]
- Replies to Black: we may be imagining one sphere at a distance from itself (Hacking 1975; O'Leary-Hawthorne 1995); a single scattered object (cf. Hawley 2009); "weak discernibility" (each is at a distance from the other but not from itself: Saunders 2006, Muller 2015), which SEP says does not touch the version Black targeted. Adams (1979) argued from almost-indiscernible to indiscernible worlds. [source: SEP-II]
- Why it matters here: the **change argument** ("if a thing changes something is true of it at the later time that is not true of it at the earlier, so it is not the same") is a misuse of Leibniz's Law; Noonan's answer: what is true of it later is "being muddy at the later time", which was always true of it. Noonan names Heraclitus (river) and Hume (identity over time "a fiction") as holders of such views. [source: SEP-Id §5]

## 3. The river (cross-ref *The Presocratics*; keep short)
- Three alleged "river fragments" (Graham's translations): B12 "On those stepping into rivers staying the same other and other waters flow" (Cleanthes via Arius Didymus via Eusebius); B49a "Into the same rivers we step and do not step, we are and are not" (Heraclitus Homericus); B91[a] "It is not possible to step twice into the same river according to Heraclitus, or to come into contact twice with a mortal being in the same state" (Plutarch). [source: SEP-Her §3.1]
- Only B12 has Heraclitus's "linguistic density"; B49a is in Attic not Ionic; B91 is "patently a paraphrase by an author famous for quoting from memory". Kirk (1954) and Marcovich (1967), following Reinhardt (1916), take B12 as the one genuine river fragment. [source: SEP-Her §3.1]
- Plato, *Cratylus* 402a (= DK A6): "Heraclitus, I believe, says that all things pass and nothing stays, and comparing existing things to the flow of a river, he says you could not step twice into the same river." [source: SEP-Her §3.1]
- Graham's reading of B12: a river "remains what it is by changing what it contains"; if the waters stopped it would be a lake or dry bed; "some things stay the same only by changing"; the body likewise lives by metabolism. The Platonic reading still has advocates (Tarán 1999). The ambiguity of "the same" (rivers or people) allows a second reading (Kahn 1979). [source: SEP-Her §3.1; IEP-Her]
- Cratylus, a later Heraclitean, added the rejoinder "that one cannot step into the same river even once"; Marcovich suggests a misreading of B12 lies behind A6/B91, possibly via Cratylus; Plato may have taken his reading from Cratylus. [source: SEP-Her §3.1]
- Aristotle is the source for Cratylus's "not even once": *Metaphysics* Γ (4).5, 1010a10–15. Cratylus was an Athenian, mid–late fifth century BCE. [source: WP-Cratylus]
- Teaching point (writer's own framing, consistent with sources): the river shows that "same river" and "same water" are different questions — the root of the sortal idea (Locke, Geach, Wiggins below).

## 4. The growing argument and the Stoic Dion/Theon puzzle
- **Debtor's Paradox / Epicharmus**: the comic playwright Epicharmus (Wikipedia: lived c. 550–c. 460 BCE; Sicilian/Doric comedy) told of a debtor who argues: add a pebble to a collection and it is not the same number; add a length to a cubit and it is not the same measure; a man's matter is constantly changing, so he is not the man who took out the loan. The creditor hits him, then says he is no longer the man who struck him. "If constitution is identity, then the debtor's reasoning is sound." [source: SEP-MC §1; WP-Epicharmus_of_Cos]
- The **Growing Argument** (*auxanomenos logos*) was "traced to the fifth-century comic poet named Epicharmus" and used by the Academic sceptics against the Stoics (Long & Sedley 28A). It concludes that what we call growth is really generation and destruction. [source: IEP-Chr §c; WS (Sedley 1982; Bowin 2003 summary)]
- Chrysippus (c. 280–207 BCE, born at Soli) replied, on Sedley's reading, that one thing can fall under different descriptions: as a lump of matter (substrate) it does not persist; as a "peculiarly qualified" individual (*idiōs poion*) it does. IEP compares this with Locke's "same mass / same man" distinction. [source: IEP-Chr]
- **Dion and Theon** (Chrysippus, in a work *On the Growing Argument*, reported by Philo of Alexandria): "let one individual be thought of as whole-limbed; the other as minus one foot. Let the whole-limbed one be called Dion, the defective one Theon. Then let one of Dion's feet be amputated…" (translation as given by IEP; translator not named — likely Long & Sedley). Philo passage = *De aeternitate mundi* 48 = SVF 2.397 [WS only]. [source: IEP-Chr]
- **Sources disagree on Chrysippus's verdict — present carefully.** SEP-MC presents the puzzle's reasoning as leading to "Dion does not survive" (Theon's parts are unchanged, so he survives; two people cannot be in one place). IEP-Chr reports Chrysippus's own answer as the opposite: **Theon perishes and Dion survives** ("Whose foot has been severed?" — Dion's; Theon could not lose a foot he never had); Sedley argues Chrysippus thereby turned the Growing Argument against the Academics: diminution is part of Dion's enduring, while unchanged Theon perishes (Sedley 1982, 270). [source: SEP-MC §1; IEP-Chr]
- Sedley, D., "The Stoic Criterion of Identity", *Phronesis* 27 (1982): 255–275. [source: SEP-Mer bibliography cites Sedley 1982; WS for volume/pages]

## 5. The Ship of Theseus (worked example, step by step)
- **Plutarch, *Life of Theseus* 23.1**: the thirty-oared ship in which Theseus returned from Crete was preserved by the Athenians "down even to the time of Demetrius Phalereus", replacing old planks with new, so that it "became a standing example among the philosophers, for the logical question of things that grow; one side holding that the ship remained the same, and the other contending that it was not the same." [source: WP-Ship_of_Theseus (translation unattributed on the page; wording is the Dryden translation as revised by Clough — WS)]
- Loeb translation (Bernadotte Perrin, 1914) of the same passage: "the thirty-oared galley... so that the vessel became a standing illustration for the philosophers in the mooted question of growth, some declaring that it remained the same, others that it was not the same vessel." [source: WS only (lexundria/Perseus copies seen in search snippets; not fetched)]
- **Key observation for the writer**: Plutarch's Greek phrase is *eis ton auxanomenon logon*, i.e. Plutarch files the ship under **the Growing Argument** (§4) — the ship was an ancient instance of the debate about growth, not a free-standing riddle. [source: WS (Greek quoted in search result); Perrin's "mooted question of growth"; Dryden's "things that grow"]
- Demetrius of Phalerum governed Athens 317–307 BCE [NOT VERIFIED this session — omit dates or check].
- **Hobbes, *De Corpore* (1655; English *Elements of Philosophy, the First Section, concerning Body*, 1656), Part II, ch. 11 "Of Identity and Difference", §7** (chapter/section from WS; chapter title and 1656 pp. 100–101 from WP's citation). Hobbes's English: "if some Man had kept the Old Planks as they were taken out, and by putting them afterward together in the same order, had again made a Ship of them, this would, without doubt, had also been the same Numerical Ship with that which was at the beginning; and so there would have been two Ships Numerically the same, which is absurd." [source: WP-Ship_of_Theseus (block quote from the 1656 English); WS]
- Hobbes's own resolution: "we must consider by what name anything is called when we inquire concerning the Identity of it"; "a Ship, which signifies Matter so figured, will be the same, as long as the Matter remains the same; but if no part of the Matter is the same, then it is Numerically another Ship; and if part of the Matter remains, and part is changed, then the Ship will be partly the same, and partly not the same." (The WP quotation contains ellipses: quote these sentences only as given.) One commentator reads Hobbes as a precursor of relative identity. [source: WP-Ship_of_Theseus; WS]
- **The puzzle stated (SEP-MC's version)**: Some say the original ship is in the museum (boats survive gradual replacement of parts); others that it is with the custodian (things survive disassembly and reassembly). "Both arguments seem compelling, but together they imply that the ship survives twice over." Run backwards: two ships at the end means two ships "occupying the same place at the same time" at the beginning. [source: SEP-MC §1]
- **Step-by-step reconstruction** (from SEP-IdT §4 and SEP-MC §1):
  1. Replacement (the plank-by-plank repaired ship) = the original (continuity of form and use through gradual change).
  2. Reassembly (the custodian's ship) = the original (same planks, same arrangement).
  3. Identity is transitive and symmetric: so Replacement = Reassembly.
  4. But Replacement and Reassembly are two ships, side by side.
  5. Contradiction: at least one premise is false.
  SEP-IdT classifies the ship as "the best known example of an asymmetrical fission case": each later ship has a claim on the original by a *different* relation. [source: SEP-IdT §4]
- **Responses (each with its cost):**
  - *Constitution view*: the ship is never identical with its planks; Replacement is the ship; Reassembly is constituted from the same planks but is a new ship. SEP notes constitution delivers a candidate solution only in such cases. [source: SEP-IdT §4.1]
  - *Relative identity*: Replacement is the same **ship** as the original; Reassembly is the same **collection of planks**; no single "same" links all three, so transitivity is not violated. Cost: useless in symmetrical fission (amoeba). [source: SEP-IdT §4.2]
  - *Chisholm, strict vs loose identity* (after Bishop Butler): in the "strict and philosophical" sense a thing has its parts essentially, so neither later ship is strictly the original; Replacement is the same only in the "loose and popular" sense (an *ens successivum*). [source: SEP-IdT §4.3]
  - *Mereological essentialism* (Chisholm 1973): denies the "apparent truism" that a ship survives replacement of any part. [source: SEP-MC §4]
  - *Temporal parts*: the ships are four-dimensional; they may share early stages (like two roads sharing a stretch). [source: SEP-IdT §4.5; WP-Ship_of_Theseus]
  - *Deflationism* (Carnap 1950, Putnam 1987, Hirsch 2002): the facts are undisputed; the dispute is verbal, over "ship". [source: SEP-MC §7; WP-Ship_of_Theseus]
- Cross-tradition parallel: the Chinese translation of a Mahāyāna treatise, the *Da zhidu lun* (*Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa*), tells of a traveller in the night whose body is torn off part by part by one demon while a second demon replaces each part with a corpse's; the traveller no longer knows who he is. Studied in Jing Huang and Jonardon Ganeri, "Is this me? A story about personal identity from the *Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa / Dà zhìdù lùn*", *British Journal for the History of Philosophy* 29.5 (2021): 739–762. [source: WP-Ship_of_Theseus (citation details given there; article not read)]
- Real-world illustrations: USS *Constitution* (1797; museum: perhaps as little as 8% original); Ise Grand Shrine rebuilt every twenty years with new wood. [source: WP-Ship_of_Theseus]

## 6. The statue and the clay (worked example, step by step)
- Ancient roots: "Various ancient philosophers, including Aristotle, pointed out that statues seem to differ in important respects from the portions of matter from which they are made." (Aristotle's bronze/statue as material cause is in *Aristotle* chapter.) [source: SEP-MC §1]
- **SEP-MC's argument** (Lump bought Monday; sculpted into statue "David" Tuesday):
  1. David did not exist on Monday (but does exist on Tuesday).
  2. Lump did exist on Monday (and also exists on Tuesday).
  3. If (1) and (2), then David is not identical to Lump (Leibniz's Law).
  4. So David is not identical to Lump.
  Also a modal difference: Lump could survive being squashed, David could not. Conclusion implies two objects in one place — "something which we ordinarily take to be impossible" (Wiggins 1968: 90). [source: SEP-MC §1]
- **Five replies** (SEP-MC): (i) accept (4): constitution view (Wiggins 1968) or temporal parts (Lewis 1976); (ii) deny (1): eliminativism (Unger 1979; van Inwagen 1990; Chisholm); (iii) deny (2): dominant kinds (Michael Burke 1992/1994); (iv) deny (3): relative identity (Geach 1967); (v) deflationism (Carnap 1950). [source: SEP-MC §1]
- **Constitution view**: "constitution is not identity"; the statue is constituted by, not identical with, the clay; Wiggins: the constituted object "consists in" and is "nothing over and above" what it is made of. Defended by Wiggins (1968, 2001), Simons, Yablo, **E. J. Lowe** (1995, 2003), **Lynne Rudder Baker** (1997, 2000), **Judith Jarvis Thomson** (1998), Shoemaker, Fine, Koslicki and others; called "the standard account" (Burke 1992). Constitution is not an equivalence relation (Baker 2002). [source: SEP-MC §2; SEP-IdT §4.1]
- Baker, *Persons and Bodies: A Constitution View* (Cambridge UP, 2000): persons are constituted by, not identical with, human organisms; the statue is a statue because "essentially related to an artworld". [source: SEP-PI bibliography; SEP-MC §2]
- **Objections to constitution, step by step:**
  - *Grounding problem*: statue and clay are "perfect duplicates, right down to their subatomic structure", so what grounds their different persistence conditions and kinds? (Simons 1987, Burke 1992, Olson 2001; Bennett 2004 frames it as grounding.) [source: SEP-MC §2]
  - Reply: a difference in kind (Wiggins 2001) — but that only "pushes back the explanatory challenge"; relational differences (Baker's artworld) seem to get things "backwards"; historical/intentional differences (Burke 1992; Catherine Sutton 2012). [source: SEP-MC §2; SEP-IdT §4.1]
  - **Gibbard's Lumpl and Goliath** (Allan Gibbard, "Contingent Identity", *Journal of Philosophical Logic* 4 (1975): 187–221): an artist makes the statue in two pieces and joins them, creating statue (Goliath) and lump (Lumpl) at the same moment; later smashes both at once. They share all historical and relational properties yet seem to differ modally — so relational replies fail, and temporal parts cannot separate them either. [source: SEP-MC §2–3]
  - *Overdetermination*: a statue thrown at a window — did statue or clay break it? "one cause too many". [source: SEP-IdT §4.1]
  - *Anthropic/explosion objection*: why only statue and lump, not "instatues", "litstatues"? Sosa (1987): "the explosion of reality". [source: SEP-MC §2]
  - *Too many thinkers* (Olson 1997; Shoemaker 1999): if a person is constituted by an organism, two thinkers share every thought. [source: SEP-IdT §4.1]
- Temporal-parts reply (§8 below) handles David/Lump (statue a temporal part of lump) but not Lumpl/Goliath; Lewis's counterpart theory (1971, 1986) then says "could survive squashing" expresses different properties with different names; Kit Fine (2003) objects with non-modal differences (well-made, Romanesque). [source: SEP-MC §3]
- **Burke's dominant kinds**: one object is both statue and lump but has only the persistence conditions of its "dominant kind" (the one entailing "the widest range of properties"); sculpting destroys the original lump and replaces it with a statue that is also a lump. [source: SEP-MC §5]
- **Eliminativism**: Unger's mereological nihilism (only simples exist); van Inwagen (1990): the only composites are living things; reply: an "incredulous stare"; nihilists paraphrase ("simples arranged statuewise"). [source: SEP-MC §4]

## 7. Tibbles, the problem of the many, vagueness (brief)
- **Tibbles and Tib**: Tib = Tibbles minus tail. Amputate the tail: Tibbles and Tib now coincide, yet have different histories. One cat on the mat or two? SEP-Id: "Wiggins 1968 contains the first appearance of this version in present-day philosophical literature; an equivalent puzzle is that of Dion and Theon". SEP-MC calls it "A more recent version of the puzzle, due to Peter Geach" — **sources differ on credit**: say "discussed by Wiggins (1968) and Geach". [source: SEP-Id §3; SEP-MC §1; SEP-Mer]
- Responses: deny the tail-complement exists (van Inwagen 1981, Olson 1995); it does not survive (Burke 1995); Tib is not a cat (Wiggins 1980); count by "almost identity" (Lewis 1993); "Tib" and "Tibbles" name the same cat-stage (Hawley 2001, Sider 2001). Noonan: no solution "stands out as clearly superior". [source: SEP-Id §3]
- **Geach's 1001 cats** (Geach 1980, 215): Tibbles has at least 1,000 hairs; for each hair hn there is a part cn = all of the cat except hn; each cn "would clearly be a cat were the hair hn to be plucked out, and we cannot reasonably suppose that plucking out a hair generates a cat, so cn must already have been a cat". So c plus the 1,000 cn's = 1,001 cats, though there is one cat on the mat. [source: SEP-Id §3 (quoting Geach 1980, *Reference and Generality*, 3rd edn)]
- **Problem of the Many**: introduced by Peter Unger, "The Problem of the Many", *Midwest Studies in Philosophy* 5 (1980): 411–67; "A similar problem appeared simultaneously in P. T. Geach (1980)". Paradigm: a cloud whose edge droplets are neither determinately in nor out; every candidate aggregate "has as good a claim to be a cloud as any other", yet there is one cloud. Applies to humans too. Solutions: nihilism, overpopulation, brutalism, relative identity, partial identity (Lewis 1993 "almost one"), vagueness. [source: SEP-PoM]
- **Vague identity — Gareth Evans, "Can there be Vague Objects?", *Analysis* 38 (1978): 208** (a single page). Argument (Evans/Salmon): suppose it is indeterminate whether a = b. But it is determinate that a = a, so a has the property of being determinately identical to a. If a = b, then b has that property too (Leibniz's Law), so it would be determinate that a = b — contradiction. So a ≠ b: indeterminate identity is impossible; vagueness must lie in our terms. Defenders reply that the conclusion shows only "not determinately identical", not "determinately distinct" (Parsons 2000; Barnes 2009). Example of the pull the other way: "Everest" and a precisely defined hunk "Rock". [source: SEP-Id §9; SEP-IdT §3; SEP-Vag bibliography]
- Shoemaker's two halls (Alpha Hall, Beta Hall, flimsy walkway): "the building" is vague, so the identity statement lacks a truth value — vagueness in language. [source: SEP-Id §9]

## 8. Endurance versus perdurance (step by step)
- Definitions: **perdurantists**: ordinary things "have temporal parts (things persist by 'perduring')"; **endurantists**: things "do not have temporal parts; instead, things are wholly present whenever they exist (things persist by 'enduring')". [source: SEP-TP §2]
- **Origin of the terms**: "Lewis (1986a, p. 202) introduces the terms 'endurance' and 'perdurance', attributing them to Johnston" (Mark Johnston). So: Lewis's *usage*, Johnston's coinage. Book: David Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds* (Oxford: Blackwell, 1986), ch. 4 / pp. 202–205. NB SEP-IdT's bibliography misprints the title as "The Plurality of Worlds". [source: SEP-TP §2 reading notes; SEP-Id §5; WP-Perdurantism; WP-Endurantism]
- Lewis's definitions paraphrased (exact wording seen only in WS): something *persists* iff it exists at various times; it *perdures* iff it persists by having different temporal parts at different times, no one part wholly present at more than one time; it *endures* iff it persists by being wholly present at more than one time. Quote only as paraphrase. [source: WS]
- Roads analogy: a temporal part is like one of the road segments of a long road; State Route 9A "turns into" the West Side Highway. [source: SEP-MC §3]
- Perdurantists include Quine (1950, 1960), Lewis (1971, 1976, 1986), Armstrong, Heller, **Sider**; endurantists include **Thomson** (1965, 1983), Geach, Chisholm, Mellor, **Lowe**, Simons. [source: SEP-TP §2]
- Quine: physical objects "conceived thus four-dimensionally in space-time, are not to be distinguished from events, or, in the concrete sense of the term, processes" (1960: 171). [source: SEP-Id §5]
- **The problem of temporary intrinsics** (Lewis 1986, 202–205), step by step:
  1. Persisting things change their intrinsic properties (Plate is round at t1, square at t2; a banana unripe then overripe).
  2. Nothing can be both round and square.
  3. Saying "round *at* t1" makes shape a relation to a time ("relational view") — but shape is intrinsic, not a relation (Lewis's objection).
  4. Presentism avoids it but is a "drastic" price for Lewis (he considers it rejecting other times).
  5. Lewis's solution: the round thing and the square thing are different **temporal parts** of Plate; "there is no problem with the same object having a round part and a square part" — like being hot at the head and cold at the feet.
  [source: SEP-IdT §2.3–2.4; SEP-TP §3]
- Objections and replies: (a) perdurance change "is not really change at all": the whole worm is neither ripe nor unripe and each part never changes; reply: distinguish atemporal from temporal ways of talking. [source: SEP-TP §3] (b) Haslanger (2003): perdurance does not secure that fatness is a property of the *cat*, only of a cat-part. [source: SEP-Id §5] (c) Endurantist alternatives: three-place instantiation (object, property, time); adverbialism "round at-t-ly" (Johnston 1987; **Sally Haslanger** 1989); tensed propositions (Haslanger 1989; Lowe 1987); Lewis (2002) objects to the last; Caplan (2005) replies. [source: SEP-IdT §2.4] (d) The modal argument (Wiggins 1980, Thomson 1983, van Inwagen 1990): "I might have died when I was five", but a fifty-year sum of stages could not have lasted five years — so I am not such a sum; whether valid depends on counterpart theory. [source: SEP-Id §5] (e) "Personites" worry: shorter overlapping sums share my experiences — how do I know I am the person, not a personite? (Tullio and Soriani 2024 defend perdurantism.) [source: SEP-Id §5]
- Perdurance on the puzzles: statue = temporal part of the lump; the two "coinciding" things merely overlap like roads; also handles fission (Lewis 1976: two persons share pre-fission stages). Fails for Lumpl/Goliath. [source: SEP-TP §4.1; SEP-MC §3; SEP-PI §5]
- **Stage theory / exdurance (Sider 1996, 2001; Hawley 2001)**: ordinary names refer to momentary stages; "the banana is overripe" is made true by a present stage; past and future talk is made true by **temporal counterparts**. Advantage: fatness is a property of a cat (every temporal part of a cat is a cat). Price: counting cats over time ("I have only ever owned three cats") is not counting by identity. [source: SEP-TP §2–3; SEP-Id §5; WP-Perdurantism]
- Sider's definition of instantaneous temporal part: x is an instantaneous temporal part of y at t iff (i) x is part of y; (ii) x exists at, but only at, t; (iii) x overlaps every part of y that exists at t. [source: WP-Temporal_parts (citing Sider 2001)]
- Other options: temporary identity (Gallois 1998, *Occasions of Identity*): distinct objects identical at one time and not at another. [source: SEP-Id §5; SEP-IdT §4.6]
- Some think the debate empty or merely verbal; Hawley lists four such doubts. [source: SEP-TP §2]
- Key books: Katherine Hawley, *How Things Persist* (OUP, 2001); Theodore Sider, *Four-Dimensionalism: An Ontology of Persistence and Time* (OUP, 2001). [source: SEP-Id and SEP-IdT bibliographies]

## 9. Relative identity (brief)
- Geach's thesis: "x is the same F as y" does not split into "x is an F, y is an F, and x = y"; things can be the same F but not the same G. Geach also argued no language can express absolute identity. Wiggins (1967, 1980) is the chief opponent on sortal relativity. [source: SEP-Id §3; SEP-RI]
- Deutsch's example: "the same person might be two different passengers, since one person may be counted twice as a passenger." [source: SEP-RI intro]
- Objection: relative identity conflicts with Leibniz's Law (worked out with the statue/clay instance). [source: SEP-IdT §4.2]

## 10. Persons as the hardest case (compact; cross-ref *Locke* and *Self and No-Self*)
- Locke's chapter "Of Identity and Diversity" (*Essay* II.27) was added only in the **second edition, 1694**, encouraged by William Molyneux. Person: "a thinking intelligent Being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider it self as itself, the same thinking thing in different times and places" (L-N 2.27.9); "a Forensick Term appropriating Actions and their Merit"; prince and cobbler (2.27.15). [source: SEP-LPI]
- Butler (*The Analogy of Religion*, 1736, first dissertation "Of Personal Identity"): "consciousness of personal identity presupposes, and therefore cannot constitute, personal identity; any more than knowledge, in any other case, can constitute truth, which it presupposes." [source: SEP-LPI; IEP-PI bibliography]
- Reid (*Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man*, 1785): the brave officer; "Therefore, the general is, and at the same time is not, the same person with him who was flogged at school." Identity is transitive; memory is not. Similar objections earlier from John Sergeant and Berkeley. [source: SEP-LPI; SEP-PI §4]
- Repairs: overlapping chains of memory; quasi-memory (Penelhum 1970; Shoemaker 1970); psychological **connectedness** vs **continuity** defined causally (Shoemaker 1984). [source: SEP-PI §4]
- Three families of answer to the persistence question: psychological-continuity views (most philosophers since the early 20th century: Parfit, Shoemaker, Lewis 1976, Johnston, Nagel...); brute-physical views (Ayers, Olson 1997, Snowdon 2014, van Inwagen 1990, Williams 1956–7, 1970); anticriterialism / "simple view" (Merricks 1998; Swinburne; Lowe). [source: SEP-PI §3; IEP-PI §2a]
- IEP distinguishes Bodily Criterion (Williams; Thomson 1997) and Somatic Criterion (Olson; Snowdon; Mackie); Brain Criterion counts as at bottom psychological. Body-swap (brain transplant) case traced to Locke II.xxvii.15 and Shoemaker 1963 (*Self-Knowledge and Self-Identity*). [source: IEP-PI]
- **Parfit's teletransporter** (*Reasons and Persons*, Oxford: Clarendon, 1984): a machine records your exact state, destroys you, and a replica is built on Mars from local matter; is it travel or death plus copying? Then the "branch-line" upgrade does not destroy the original. Parfit: there is "no further fact"; what matters is **Relation R**, psychological connectedness and/or continuity. [source: WP-Reasons_and_Persons; WP-Teletransportation_paradox; IEP-PI]
- Precedent: Thomas Reid to Lord Kames, 1775: if "two or three such beings should be formed out of my brain; whether they will all be me". Similar machines in Stanisław Lem's *Dialogs* (1957) and Dennett's *The Mind's I* (1981). [source: WP-Teletransportation_paradox]
- **Fission, step by step** (SEP-PI §5): your two cerebral hemispheres are transplanted into two bodies (Lefty, Righty). (1) Each is psychologically continuous with you. (2) Psychological continuity suffices for identity. (3) So you = Lefty and you = Righty. (4) Lefty ≠ Righty. (5) Identity is transitive: contradiction. Replies: (a) **multiple occupancy** (Lewis 1976, Perry 1972, Noonan): there were two people all along, sharing temporal parts; (b) **non-branching** (Shoemaker 1984; Parfit 1984: 207): continuity counts only if unique — so "Fission is death". Objection: you would survive if one hemisphere were destroyed but die if both survive — "the opposite of what we should expect"; you could be "killed" without noticing (IEP). Closest-continuer (Nozick 1981) violates the "only x and y" rule. [source: SEP-PI §5; IEP-PI §3a]
- **"Identity is not what matters"**: Parfit concludes that what you rationally care about is R (with any reliable cause), not identity; double survival through fission is "as good (or even better)" than ordinary survival; "facts about numerical identity... have no practical importance". Lewis (1976) and Parfit (1976) debate whether multiple occupancy saves the importance of identity. [source: IEP-PI §4; SEP-PI §5]
- Parfit: born 1942 in Chengdu, China; died 2 January 2017 in London, aged 74; first paper "Personal Identity" (*Philosophical Review* 80, 1971). [source: WP-Derek_Parfit; SEP-PI bibliography]
- Parfit's "glass tunnel" passage (see Quotations): he found the reductionist view liberating. [source: WP-Derek_Parfit]
- **Animalism** (Olson, *The Human Animal: Personal Identity Without Psychology*, OUP 1997; Snowdon 1990, 2014; Ayers 1990; Carter 1989): we are human organisms; personhood may be a temporary property "like being a student". **Too-many-thinkers argument**, step by step: (1) the human organism sitting in your chair thinks; (2) psychological-continuity views say you are not an organism (the person goes with a transplanted brain, the organism does not); (3) so there are two thinkers where you are, and you cannot know which you are. Reply: organisms do not think (Shoemaker; Lowe; Baker), or "I" refers only to persons (Noonan). Main objection to animalism: after a brain transplant you would stay behind "with an empty head"; Snowdon's reply: that is not obviously false. [source: SEP-PI §6–7]
- **Bernard Williams, "The Self and the Future", *Philosophical Review* 79 (1970): 161–80** (reprinted in *Problems of the Self*, 1973). Two presentations of one case: (i) A and B's psychologies are swapped between bodies; asked beforehand which body should get a reward and which torture, the choice and the outcomes suggest the person goes with the psychology; (ii) told "you will be tortured tomorrow, after your memories are erased and replaced", you still fear the torture — suggesting the person goes with the body. Williams finds the second intuition stronger and leans to bodily continuity. [source: WP-Personal_identity; IEP-PI bibliography]

## 11. Buddhist arguments (cross-ref *Early Buddhism*, *Mahāyāna*; add what they lack)
- The *Milindapañha* dates "sometime between 100 BCE and 200 CE"; composite; dialogue of Nāgasena and Milinda (= Indo-Greek Menander I, c. 150 BCE). English translations: T. W. Rhys Davids (*The Questions of King Milinda*, Sacred Books of the East 35–36, Oxford: Clarendon, 1890–94); I. B. Horner (*Milinda's Questions*, Pali Text Society, 1963–64; the same WP article elsewhere says "1969" — use 1963–64, as also in SEP-MIB's bibliography); Maria Heim (Murty Classical Library, Harvard UP, 2025). [source: WP-Milinda_Panha; SEP-MIB]
- SEP-MIB quotes Nāgasena: "Nāgasena and so on—is only a generally understood term, a designation in common use. For there is no permanent individuality (or self) involved in the matter" (*Questions of King Milinda* II.1.25; translator not named). Words like "chariot" are used only when parts are arranged together; "an individual (satto) is nothing but a conventional designation that applies to the five aggregates". Abhidharma does not deny the physical reality of parts, only of partite wholes such as "chairs, pots, and tables" — distinction *dravyasat* (substantially existent) vs *prajñaptisat* (nominally existent). [source: SEP-MIB]
- For this chapter, the chariot's point is **mereological**: the same reasoning applies to any composite and to "the same chariot" over time; Nyāya's reply that wholes are real (below) shows the debate is about composition as much as selves. (Full step-by-step chariot argument: already in *Early Buddhism*.)
- **Vasubandhu** (4th–5th century CE per SEP-Vas intro; elsewhere in the same entry "4th century C.E."): the commentary (*Bhāṣya*) on his *Treasury of the Abhidharma* (*Abhidharmakośa*) ends with "an extended defense of the Buddhist doctrine of no-self, entitled 'The Refutation of the Person'", aimed chiefly at the Vātsīputrīyas (Pudgalavādins, "Personalists"), with arguments also against a Vaiśeṣika self. James Duerlinger's translation: *Indian Buddhist Theories of Persons: Vasubandhu's 'Refutation of the Theory of a Self'* (RoutledgeCurzon, 2003). [source: SEP-Vas §1 and bibliography] ("Chapter 9": SEP says only "final chapter"; it is conventionally called the ninth chapter, but that numbering was not confirmed this session.)
- **Vasubandhu against the Personalists, step by step** (SEP-Vas): (1) What is real is known by perception or inference; nothing perceived or inferred is a self over and above the aggregates. (2) Everything real is causally efficient; what is not is a conceptual construct (*prajñapti*). (3) Dilemma: if the person is uncaused, that is a non-Buddhist eternal self; if caused by the aggregates, it changes as they change and gives no continuity. (4) So the person is a construct — like "milk", built from separate impressions of sight, smell and taste. Personalist reply: the person is real but "neither the same as nor distinct from" the aggregates, "dependent on" them, and "ineffable". Vasubandhu: "depending on" either concedes it is a concept or makes it caused; ineffability about a perceived thing undermines perception as a means of knowledge. On memory: "The reason I remember my past is because my aggregates now are the causal result of the past aggregates". [source: SEP-Vas §1]
- **Vasubandhu's momentariness argument** (the Buddhist answer to persistence), step by step: (1) change is impossible: "it is not acceptable that it differs from itself" (AKBh 193.9–10); (2) all conditioned things are impermanent; (3) destruction cannot be caused, since a cause must produce something real and destruction is a non-existent (von Rospatt 1995 identifies this as Vasubandhu's new twist); (4) so things destroy themselves by nature, and if it is their nature, they must do so "immediately upon coming into being"; (5) so everything lasts a moment; apparent continuity is a stream of replacements, often compared by modern interpreters to frames of a film. [source: SEP-Vas §2.2]
- Pudgalavāda: "those who affirm the existence of the person"; the Pāli commentarial line "There is nothing else, whether a being, or an entity, or a man or a person" (Dhs-a 155) is aimed against it. [source: SEP-Abh]

## 12. Nyāya: wholes and the recollecting self (cross-ref *Classical Indian Schools*)
- Buddhist position as Dasti summarises it: (i) diachronically, "no substance (including selves) endures for longer than a moment"; (ii) synchronically, wholes "are nothing more than heaps of micro-properties (illustrated by the famous chariot metaphor in The Questions of King Milinda)". [source: IEP-Ny §5a]
- Nyāya on wholes: composite substances have capacities beyond the collection of their parts (NS 2.1.35); if wholes were heaps of imperceptible atoms we could not perceive them at all, yet we do (NS 2.1.36). [source: IEP-Ny §5a]
- Recollection argument: "that is the very man I saw a week ago" requires a locus of memory spanning the interval (NS 1.1.10 and commentaries); Uddyotakara, following Vātsyāyana: if I were a different self from yesterday's "me", I could not recollect what that "me" experienced, "since one self is unable to recollect the content of another's experience". Cross-modal recognition ("that thing I see is the same thing I am touching") needs one subject (NS 3.1.1–3). Apex: Udayana's *Determining the Truth of the Self*. [source: IEP-Ny §5a]
- Terminology caution: IEP does not use *pratyabhijñā* for this argument; and *Pratyabhijñā* is also the name of a Kashmir Śaiva school. Use "recognition" or "recollection" in English. [source: IEP-Ny (absence)]

## 13. Chinese and Islamic perspectives (brief)
- Zhuangzi's butterfly ends: "This is called the Transformation of Things" (Burton Watson, as in the published *Hundred Schools* chapter); Chad Hansen renders the same phrase "This is called 'things change'" (*Zhuangzi* 2:14 in his numbering). Note the passage insists "there must be a difference" between Zhuang Zhou and the butterfly. [source: SEP-Zh; *Hundred Schools* chapter]
- Coutinho (IEP) on Inner Chapter 6: the cosmic process is "like a potter's wheel... on which things are formed, and arise, sharpened, and are ground back down only to be made into new forms"; the sage changes "self-identification", identifying with "the greater flow" (*datong*) rather than "this particular body". Use as the Daoist contrast: persistence is not a puzzle to solve but a transformation to accept. [source: IEP-Zh §f]
- **Ash'arite recreation**: on Perler and Rudolph's account, al-Ash'arī (873–935) held that God "rearranges the atoms that make up this world anew at every instant", continuously creating the accidents; accidents "possess only a momentary existence". [source: SEP-Occ §3] McGinnis: early theologians argued God "creates bodies all at once and that at each moment He recreates them", so "no body survives between two moments" causally; taken to its conclusion, "at each moment God recreates the world, its atoms and accidents", which later theologians embraced as occasionalism. [source: SEP-AIN §2]
- Writer's comparison (supportable from the above): Buddhist momentariness and Ash'arite recreation both make persistence a series of momentary existences; the Buddhists explain the series by causal streams, the Ash'arites by God's continual re-creation. Descartes's *Third Meditation* ("some cause which as it were creates me afresh at this moment") is the European echo. [source: SEP-Occ §3; SEP-Vas §2.2]
- Hume: identity over time is a "fiction" we substitute for related objects (Noonan's summary); for the bundle quotations cross-ref *Berkeley and Hume*. Graham compares B12's river to Hume, *Treatise* 1.4.6 (SBN 258). [source: SEP-Id §5; SEP-Her §3.1]

## Debates
- **Heraclitus: flux or constancy through change?** Plato/Barnes (and Tarán 1999) vs Graham, following Reinhardt, Kirk, Marcovich. [source: SEP-Her §3.1]
- **What did Chrysippus conclude about Dion and Theon?** SEP-MC states the puzzle as implying Dion does not survive; IEP-Chr (following Sedley 1982) reports Chrysippus held Theon perishes and Dion survives; Bowin (2003) and Acevedo offer further readings (WS). [source: SEP-MC; IEP-Chr; WS]
- **Hobbes: who gets the ship?** Hobbes answers relative to the name (matter vs form); some read him as a relative-identity theorist (WS). [source: WP-Ship_of_Theseus; WS]
- **Constitution vs identity** for statue and clay: pluralists (Wiggins, Lowe, Baker, Thomson, Fine, Koslicki) vs monists using temporal parts/counterparts (Lewis), vs eliminativists (Unger, van Inwagen), dominant kinds (Burke), relative identity (Geach), deflationists (Carnap, Hirsch). No consensus. [source: SEP-MC]
- **Endurance vs perdurance vs stage theory**, and whether the debate is substantive at all (SEP-TP §2 lists sceptics). [source: SEP-TP; SEP-Id §5]
- **Can identity be vague?** Evans/Salmon no; Parsons, Barnes, van Inwagen yes or unconvinced. [source: SEP-Id §9; SEP-IdT §3]
- **Psychological continuity vs animalism vs simple view**; whether identity is what matters (Parfit vs Lewis 1976; Williams's bodily intuition). [source: SEP-PI; IEP-PI]
- **Is Parfit's view a constitution view?** IEP's Korfmacher describes Parfit's reductionism with Cellini's bronze Venus: persons "constituted by, but not identical with" body and psychology; Parfit's own slogan (quoted in *Early Buddhism*) is that persons exist "only in the way in which nations exist".
