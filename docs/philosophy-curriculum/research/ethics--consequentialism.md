## 1. What consequentialism is: the basic definitions
- Consequentialism is "the view that normative properties depend only on consequences"; the most prominent case is consequentialism about the moral rightness of acts: whether an act is right "depends only on the consequences of that act or of something related to that act, such as the motive behind the act or a general rule requiring acts of the same kind." [source: SEP-C intro]
- The paradigm is utilitarianism; SEP-C names Bentham (1789), Mill (1861) and Sidgwick (1907) as the classic proponents, who held "hedonistic act consequentialism". [source: SEP-C §1]
- Act consequentialism (SEP-C's formulation): an act is morally right iff it maximizes the good, i.e. iff the total good for all minus the total bad for all is greater than this net amount for any incompatible act available to the agent on that occasion. Hedonism adds: pleasure is the only intrinsic good, pain the only intrinsic bad. [source: SEP-C §1]
- The slogan "the greatest happiness for the greatest number" is "misleading": an act can raise the happiness of most people yet not maximize net good if the smaller number lose more than the greater number gain. [source: SEP-C §1]
- Classic utilitarianism is consequentialist "because of what it denies": that rightness depends directly on anything other than consequences, e.g. on whether the agent promised in the past. On classic utilitarianism what makes promise-breaking wrong is its future effects, not the past promise. [source: SEP-C §1]
- SEP-C breaks classic utilitarianism into separable claims: Consequentialism; Actual Consequentialism; Direct Consequentialism; Evaluative Consequentialism; Hedonism; Maximizing Consequentialism; Aggregative Consequentialism; Total Consequentialism; Universal Consequentialism; Equal Consideration; Agent-neutrality. "Most pairs of these claims are logically independent"; classic utilitarians accepted them all. Dropping one or more yields a "wide variety" of descendant theories, often called "consequentialism" rather than "utilitarianism". [source: SEP-C §1]
- Agent-neutrality (SEP-C's definition): "whether some consequences are better than others does not depend on whether the consequences are evaluated from the perspective of the agent (as opposed to an observer)." [source: SEP-C §1]
- Whether consequentialism *must* be agent-neutral is disputed: McNaughton & Rawling (1991), Howard-Snyder (1994), Pettit (1997) say yes; Bennett (1989), Broome (1991), Skorupski (1995) prefer a broader definition; Sen (1982), Broome (1991) and Portmore (2001, 2003, 2011) allow agent-relativity into the theory of value. [source: SEP-C §2]
- Some philosophers argue any (plausible) moral theory can be represented as a version of consequentialism (Sosa 1993, Portmore 2009, Dreier 1993 and 2011; but see Brown 2011) — the "consequentializing" worry. [source: SEP-C §2]
- Hooker's general definition: a theory is consequentialist "if and only if it assesses acts and/or character traits, practices, and institutions solely in terms of the goodness of the consequences." [source: SEP-RC §1]
- The word "consequentialism" was coined by G. E. M. Anscombe ("a term she invented"), in "Modern Moral Philosophy" (*Philosophy* 33, 1958). For her it was originally a doctrine about *responsibility* (equal responsibility for intended and merely foreseen consequences), not directly about what makes acts right; that meaning "came about only later". [source: SEP-AN; WP-CONS]
- The terms "act" and "rule" utilitarianism were introduced by Richard Brandt in the late 1950s (*Ethical Theory*, 1959, ch. 15). Older labels: "direct"/"extreme" vs "indirect"/"restricted". [source: IEP-AR §3 and annotated bibliography]

## 2. Theory of the good vs theory of the right
- Consequentialism needs (a) a theory of value (what is good) and (b) a theory of the right (how rightness depends on that value). "Consequentialism and the other elements of classical utilitarianism are compatible with many different theories about which things are good or valuable." [source: SEP-C §3]
- Theories of the good: hedonism (Bentham: push-pin as good as poetry if equally pleasant; Mill: higher and lower *qualities* of pleasure, judged by those who have experienced both, *Utilitarianism* 1861; Moore 1903 charged qualitative hedonism with incoherence); preference (desire-satisfaction) utilitarianism; pluralism (Moore's "ideal utilitarianism" adds beauty and knowledge); welfarism (Sen 1979); perfectionism (Hurka 1993); capabilities (Sen 1985, Nussbaum 2000); a "utilitarianism of rights". [source: SEP-C §3]
- The experience machine: Nozick 1974 (pp. 42–45); if pleasure were all that mattered it would seem irrational to refuse to plug in; since refusing does not seem irrational, hedonism "seems inadequate". Hedonist reply: distinguish sensory from propositional pleasure (Feldman). [source: SEP-C §3]
- Distribution: total vs average utilitarianism; prioritarianism (more weight to the worse off); Sidgwick (1907, 417) let distribution break ties. Negative utilitarianism (R. N. Smart 1958 is cited as the classic critique) seems to imply painlessly killing everyone. [source: SEP-C §3]
- Criterion of rightness vs decision procedure: "most classic and contemporary utilitarians and consequentialists do not propose their principles as decision procedures." Bentham: "It is not to be expected that this process [his hedonic calculus] should be strictly pursued previously to every moral judgment" (IPML ch. IV sec. VI). Sidgwick: "It is not necessary that the end which gives the criterion of rightness should always be the end at which we consciously aim" (1907, 413). Golf analogy: the laws of physics govern the ball, but golfers do not calculate forces. A criterion can be "self-effacing without being self-refuting" (Parfit 1984). [source: SEP-C §4]
- Hooker's four reasons the act-by-act calculating procedure would be counterproductive: lack of information; cost of getting it; calculation errors (especially with bias, complexity, haste); "expectation effects" (if everyone calculated, no one could trust anyone; Hodgson 1967, Hospers 1972, Harsanyi 1982 say trust would break down; Singer 1972 and Lewis 1972 say it would not). [source: SEP-RC §4]
- Actual vs expected consequences: Alice buys a runaway teenager a bus ticket home; the bus crashes and he dies. Actual consequentialists say her act was wrong but blameless; others make rightness depend on foreseen, foreseeable, intended or likely consequences ("objective" vs "subjective" consequentialism; Railton 1984). [source: SEP-C §4]
- IEP example: J. J. C. Smart imagines someone who in 1938 saves a drowning man who turns out to be Adolf Hitler; actual-consequence utilitarianism says the rescue was wrong, foreseeable-consequence utilitarianism that it was right. [source: IEP-AR §2]

## 3. Maximising, satisficing, scalar
- Maximizing consequentialism: rightness depends "only on which consequences are best (as opposed to merely satisfactory or an improvement over the status quo)". [source: SEP-C §1]
- Satisficing consequentialism (Michael Slote 1984): we ought to do what creates *enough* utility (criticised by Bradley 2006). Progressive consequentialism (Elliot and Jamieson 2009): we ought to make the world better than it would be if we did nothing, but not as much as we can. [source: SEP-C §6]
- Scalar consequentialism (Norcross 2006, 2020; Sinhababu 2018 on degrees): ranks acts as better or worse without a simple right/wrong verdict — giving $1000 is "better and more right" than $100 and "worse and more wrong" than $10,000. Contrastivist variant (Snedegar 2017). [source: SEP-C §6]
- IEP's "Plain Scalar Consequentialism": "Of any two things a person might do at any given moment, one is better than another to the extent that its overall consequences are better than the other's overall consequences." It "says nothing about right and wrong" (Singer 1977; Norcross 1997). [source: IEP-C §1c]
- Mill on wrongness: "We do not call anything wrong, unless we mean to imply that a person ought to be punished in some way or other for doing it; if not by law, by the opinion of his fellow creatures; if not by opinion, by the reproaches of his own conscience." (*Utilitarianism* 1861, ch. 5, para. 14). SEP-C uses this to show Mill could say we *ought* to maximise without failure always being *wrong*. [source: SEP-RC §6.1; SEP-C §6]

## 4. Act vs rule; motive and global consequentialism; Hooker
- Direct consequentialism: the moral qualities of X depend on X's own consequences (acts, motives, virtues); someone direct about everything is a "global direct consequentialist" (Pettit and Smith 2000; Driver 2012). Indirect: the qualities of an act depend on the consequences of something else, e.g. motive consequentialism (Adams 1976; Sverdlik 2011), virtue consequentialism, rule consequentialism (Marcus Singer 1961). [source: SEP-C §5]
- "What if everybody did that?" fails ("Have some children": if no one did, the species would die out, yet it is not wrong to be childless). Better: "What would happen if everybody were permitted to do that?" → acceptance rule consequentialism: an act is wrong iff it violates a rule whose acceptance has better consequences than acceptance of any incompatible rule (Brandt 1992: rules in individual consciences; Gert 2005: publicly known; Rawls 1955: built into institutions). [source: SEP-C §5]
- Act-consequentialist criterion (Hooker): "An act is wrong if and only if it results in less good than would have resulted from some available alternative act." Rule-consequentialist decision procedure: normally decide by rules whose acceptance produces the best consequences ("Don't harm innocent others", "Don't break your promises", "Pay special attention to the needs of your family and friends", "Do good for others generally"). Most act consequentialists accept that procedure, so are "partial rule-consequentialists"; Moore, Sidgwick, Hare (1981) and Pettit combined an act criterion with a rule-based procedure. [source: SEP-RC §4]
- Full rule-consequentialism: rule-consequentialist answers to three questions — what makes acts wrong, how to decide, when sanctions (blame, guilt, praise) are appropriate. [source: SEP-RC §4]
- Global consequentialism (Pettit and Smith 2000; Kagan 2000 "multi-dimensional direct consequentialism"): assess everything — acts, rules, motives, sanctions — by its own consequences. Objection (Crisp 1992; Streumer 2003; Lang 2004): it can tell you to use the best decision procedure but not do the act it selects, and to blame you for doing what the theory required. Replies: Driver 2014; de Lazari-Radek and Singer 2014. [source: SEP-RC §5]
- Hooker's own formulation (book: *Ideal Code, Real World: A Rule-consequentialist Theory of Morality*, Oxford UP, 2000): rules are assessed by the expected good of their being taught to and accepted by new generations, counting the "teaching"/internalisation costs; acceptance by an "overwhelming majority", made precise as roughly 90% (a compromise: close enough to 100% to keep the ideal of universal acceptance, far enough below to leave room for rules about punishment); ties broken by closeness to conventional morality. Expectablist version: "An act is morally wrong if and only if it is forbidden by the rules that would result in the greatest expected good" (with the tie-breaker). [source: SEP-RC §§6.1–6.3]
- Hooker's three-way argument for rule consequentialism: (1) it supplies an impartial underlying principle for intuitively plausible rules (against "Rossian pluralism", after W. D. Ross 1930) and fits reflective equilibrium (Urmson 1953; Brandt 1967; Hospers 1972; Hooker 2000); (2) assessing acts indirectly produces better consequences (Austin 1832; Brandt; Harsanyi); (3) contractualist routes (Parfit 2011 and others). [source: SEP-RC §7]
- **Objection: collapse.** If "don't steal" is better with exceptions whenever stealing maximises good, the ideal rule becomes "don't steal except when…", i.e. rule consequentialism collapses into act consequentialism (David Lyons 1965: "extensionally equivalent"). **Reply (Hooker):** codes are ranked by the expected good of *teaching and accepting* them; a rule cluttered with exceptions is costly to learn and invites self-serving rationalisation, so the simpler code wins even where following it does not maximise. [source: SEP-RC §8; IEP-AR §5]
- **Objection: incoherence / "rule worship"** (J. J. C. Smart's phrase, Smart 1956): why follow a rule when breaking it would do more good? **Replies:** (a) rule consequentialism includes a "prevent disaster" rule (Brandt 1992), which triggers only when much more is at stake, so it does not collapse; (b) the theory contains no overriding commitment to maximise: its agents' fundamental motive is "to do what is impartially defensible", and they believe acting on impartially justified rules is impartially defensible. [source: SEP-RC §8; IEP-AR §5]
- Hooker: "From the mid 1960s until the mid 1990s, most philosophers thought rule-consequentialism couldn't survive" the collapse/incoherence/rule-worship objections. [source: SEP-RC §9]
- Brad Hooker: born 13 September 1957, American, professor at the University of Reading; Parfit called *Ideal Code, Real World* "the best statement and defence, so far, of one of the most important moral theories." [source: WP Brad_Hooker]
- Act vs rule readings of Mill are a scholarly debate (Urmson 1953 reads Mill as a rule utilitarian; Mill says direct appeal to utility is made when "secondary principles" conflict). [source: IEP-AR §3]

## 5. Mohist consequentialism (the non-Western consequentialism)
- Fraser: the Mohists "advanced the world's earliest form of consequentialism, a remarkably sophisticated version based on a plurality of intrinsic goods taken as constitutive of collective human welfare." [source: SEP-MO intro]
- Leading principle: "inclusive care" (*jian ai* 兼愛) toward others and mutual benefit (*lì* 利), promoting "the benefit of all under heaven". [source: SEP-MO §7; characters from WP-MOH]
- *Lì* ("benefit") is "the general standard of moral permissibility" and refers to "an objective list of three sorts of goods: material wealth, a large population or family, and social order". Unlike Bentham and Mill, it promotes "not individual happiness, but a range of goods, many of them public or collective." [source: SEP-MO §7]
- Key text (Fraser's translation, Book 32, "Condemning Music"): "The task of the benevolent is surely to diligently seek to promote the benefit of the world and eliminate harm to the world and to take this as a model (fa) throughout the world. Does it benefit people? Then do it. Does it not benefit people? Then stop." Chinese as printed by WP-SC (simplified, citing Watson): 利人乎, 即为; 不利人乎, 即止. [source: SEP-MO §7; WP-SC]
- Degrees of wrong: "The more one injures another, the greater his unbenevolence, and the more severe the crime" (Book 17, "Condemning Aggression"). [source: SEP-MO §7]
- Unit of evaluation: "similar to rule utilitarianism, but is probably better characterized as 'practice consequentialism' or, even more appropriately, '*dao* (way) consequentialism'"; the focus is ways/practices (funerals, warfare), not single acts; because a *dao* includes dispositions, it "incorporates some of the characteristics of motive consequentialism." The early texts are "vague" on applying benefit to specific cases. [source: SEP-MO §7]
- Consequentialist arguments justify nine of the ten core doctrines; the tenth, "Heaven's intent", justifies the consequentialist theory itself. [source: SEP-MO §7]
- Burial argument as a template (Book 25, "Moderation in Burial", Fraser): if lavish burials and prolonged mourning "can really enrich the poor, increase the few, secure the endangered, and bring order to the disordered", promote them; if not, eliminate them. [source: SEP-MO §7]
- The three *fa* (standards) for statements (Books 35–37, "Condemning Fatalism"): a **root** (the deeds of the ancient sage-kings), a **source** (what people see and hear), a **use** (when adopted in punishment and government it must produce benefit for state, clan and people). [source: SEP-MO "The Three Fa"]
- Justification: the Mohists ground the consequentialist standard in Heaven's (*Tian*) intention, Heaven being "the highest, wisest moral agent" whose conduct is a reliable model. [source: SEP-MO "Justification and the Role of Heaven"]
- Demandingness in Mohism: if Heaven's impartiality obliged each to act for others "exactly as we act in our own", Mohism would face the objections "commonly leveled at crude forms of utilitarianism" ("I cannot possibly make breakfast for everyone each morning"); the later Mohists allow benefiting some more than others by relationship (see hundred-schools notes, §5). [source: SEP-MO §7]
- Fraser's suggestion: by counting virtues (benevolence, filial piety, loyalty) among its goods, Mohist ethics "may have the resources to avoid an untenable split between justification and motivation" — the problem that a friend's birthday present given out of a desire to maximise happiness may "disappoint and offend". [source: SEP-MO §7]
- SEP-UH (Driver): Mozi (c. 470–c. 391 BCE) "articulated a theory which is considered proto-utilitarian (Van Norden 2007)", with commitments to "impartiality, production of the good, and maximization"; it also notes proto-utilitarian ideas in the medieval Indian Buddhist Śāntideva, "who viewed our moral aims to be properly focused on production of well-being for sentient creatures" (Charles Goodman, *Consequences of Compassion*, 2009). [source: SEP-UH §1]
- "State consequentialism": Ivanhoe and Van Norden (*Readings in Classical Chinese Philosophy*, 2005, p. 60): Mozi "advocated a form of state consequentialism, which sought to maximize three basic goods: the wealth, order, and population of the state". WP-SC calls this "a modern minority theoretical interpretation"; Fraser (*The Philosophy of the Mozi*, 2016, pp. 17, 249) counters that the Mohist concern is to benefit "all the people of the world", not a particular state. [source: WP-SC]

## 6. The trolley problems
- Origin: Philippa Foot, "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect", *Oxford Review* 5 (1967): 5–15 (reprinted in *Virtues and Vices*, 1978). [source: SEP-DE bibliography; SEP-DA §3]
- Foot's pair: a judge who can stop rioters killing five hostages only by framing and executing one innocent man; and the driver of a runaway tram who can steer from a track with five workmen onto one with one. Foot: wrong to kill in the first, not wrong in the second; she explained the difference by doing vs allowing (the judge chooses between killing one and *letting* five die; the driver between killing one and killing five), not by double effect. [source: SEP-DA §3; Foot's wording quoted in WP-TP]
- Foot's wording (as quoted in WP-TP): "he is the driver of a runaway tram, which he can only steer from one narrow track on to another; five men are working on one track and one man on the other; anyone on the track he enters is bound to be killed." [source: WP-TP "Original dilemma"]
- Judith Jarvis Thomson named it "the trolley problem" in "Killing, Letting Die, and the Trolley Problem", *The Monist* 59(2) (1976): 204–17; then "The Trolley Problem", *Yale Law Journal* 94 (1985): 1395–1415. Her 1976 article "initiated the literature on the trolley problem as a subject in its own right". [source: SEP-DE bibliography; WP-TP; WP Judith_Jarvis_Thomson]
- Thomson's **bystander** variant: the choice is made by a bystander at a switch, who clearly chooses between killing one and letting five die, yet turning still seems permissible; this "undermines Foot's claim" that doing/allowing explains the cases. [source: SEP-DA §3] (Dating caveat: SEP-DA cites the bystander case as "Thomson (1986)" — the 1986 reprint of the 1976 paper; SEP-DE cites "Thomson, 1985" for the switch and push examples.)
- **Footbridge / "fat man"**: pushing a large man off a bridge to stop the trolley; most approve of switching but disapprove of pushing, though both save a net four lives. Double effect offers an explanation: the one's death is a foreseen side effect in the switch case but a means in the push case. [source: SEP-DE §§2, 4.5; WP-TP "Related problems"] The 1985 Yale paper's case list (per a Yale course reading guide): Transplant [p. 1396], Bystander at the Switch [1397], Loop Variant [1402], Fat Man [1409]. [source: WS-3]
- **Transplant** (Foot 1966/67; Thomson 1976): a surgeon could cut up a healthy patient in for tests to save five transplant patients. Classical utilitarianism, with the right unrealistic details filled in, seems to imply it is permissible or even obligatory; "Most people find this result abominable." [source: SEP-C §5]
- **Loop** (Thomson 1985): the side track loops back to the main track, so the trolley would hit the five from behind but for the one person's body stopping it; here the one's death is a means, yet many find turning permissible — a problem for double effect. [source: WS-3 (secondary summaries, not the article itself)]
- Frances Kamm's "Doctrine of Triple Effect" (in *Intricate Ethics*, 2007): distinguishes acting *in order that* an evil occur from acting *because* it will occur; in the loop case one redirects because the one will be hit, not in order that he be hit. Critics include S. Matthew Liao and Michael Otsuka (*Utilitas* 2008, "Double Effect, Triple Effect and the Trolley Problem: Squaring the Circle in Looping Cases"). [source: WS-4]
- Kamm's five-and-five case: if not harming had no priority over not aiding, a bystander would be indifferent between turning a trolley from five onto five; but clearly they must not turn it. So if turning is permissible in the standard case, it is "permissible despite these differences" between doing and allowing. [source: SEP-DA §3 (Kamm 2020a)] Kamm: Rutgers and Harvard (emerita); books include *Intricate Ethics* (2007) and *The Trolley Problem Mysteries* (2015). [source: WP Frances_Kamm]
- **Thomson's change of mind** ("Turning the Trolley", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 36(4), 2008): she argued the consensus that the bystander may turn the trolley was mistaken. Offered a third option — turn it onto oneself — few would take it; if you would not pay the cost yourself, you may not impose it on a stranger, which would be like stealing someone's wallet to give to charity. Fitzpatrick (2009) replies that morality cannot demand such self-sacrifice while the reasons to minimise deaths remain. [source: SEP-DA §3; SEP-DE §4.5] (Page discrepancy: SEP-DA gives pp. 359–374, SEP-DE gives 201–12.)
- Three camps on double effect and trolleys (SEP-DE): (1) the paired intuitions prove the intended/foreseen distinction is built into moral judgment (John Mikhail 2011); (2) switching is wrong after all (Thomson 2008; Anscombe 1982 on absolutist double effect); (3) double effect cannot explain switching (McIntyre 2001). [source: SEP-DE §4.5]
- Double effect itself: credited to Aquinas on self-defence (*Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 64 a. 7); standard four conditions (act not bad in itself; bad effect not intended; good effect not produced by means of the bad; proportionate reason). [source: SEP-DE §1]
- Pre-Foot versions: Frank Chapman Sharp's 1905 questionnaire at the University of Wisconsin (switchman's own child on the track); Karl Engisch (1930); Hans Welzel (1951); Rabbi Avrohom Yeshaya Karelitz (1953) on deflecting a projectile from a larger crowd to a smaller one. [source: WP-TP intro]
- Surveys: 2009 PhilPapers survey (Bourget and Chalmers): 68% of professional philosophers would switch, 8% would not, 24% other. [source: WP-TP "Survey data"]
- **Greene's dual-process work.** Greene, Sommerville, Nystrom, Darley and Cohen, "An fMRI Investigation of Emotional Engagement in Moral Judgment", *Science* 293 (14 Sept 2001): 2105–2108 — "personal" dilemmas (footbridge) engaged emotion-related regions more than "impersonal" ones (switch); later work (Greene et al. 2004, 2008) proposed a dual-process model: "Characteristically deontological judgments are preferentially supported by automatic emotional responses, while characteristically consequentialist judgments are preferentially supported by conscious reasoning and allied processes of cognitive control." "The Secret Joke of Kant's Soul" (2008) argues deontology is largely post hoc rationalisation; *Moral Tribes* (2013) proposes utilitarianism ("deep pragmatism") as a "metamorality". [source: WP-JG; SEP-NE "neuroscience of ethics"] (WP-JG prints "preferentially supposed by"; read "supported".)
- Most controversially, Greene "suggested that there are reasons to be suspicious of our deontological judgments, and interpreted his work as lending credence to utilitarian theories"; this "sparked a heated debate" (Berker 2009; Kahane et al. 2011; Christensen 2014) highlighting the danger of drawing "normative conclusions from purely descriptive data". [source: SEP-NE]
- Critics: Selim Berker, "The Normative Insignificance of Neuroscience", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 37(4) (2009): 293–329 [source: SEP-NE bibliography]; Guy Kahane, "On the Wrong Track: Process and Content in Moral Psychology" (*Mind & Language* 2012) and "Sidetracked by trolleys" (*Social Neuroscience* 2015); Kahane, Everett, Earp, Caviola, Faber, Crockett and Savulescu, "Beyond sacrificial harm: A two-dimensional model of utilitarian psychology" (*Psychological Review* 125(2), 2018) — sacrificial dilemmas measure only "permissive attitudes toward instrumental harm", not "impartial concern for the greater good". Thomas Nagel: Greene moves too quickly from impartiality to utilitarianism specifically (Kant and Rawls offer other impartial approaches). [source: WP-JG; WP-TP]
- Later evidence (as WP-JG reports it): lesion studies (ventromedial prefrontal damage → more utilitarian judgments); a 45-country replication found the personal-force effect culturally universal; a 2025 meta-analysis found a small overall effect favouring intuitive deontology (OR 1.18), larger (1.30) for dilemmas with both personal force and instrumentality. [source: WP-JG] (Recent; use only hedged.)
- Joshua Greene: born 1974; Harvard BA philosophy 1997; Princeton PhD 2002 (supervisors David Lewis and Gilbert Harman; Peter Singer on the committee). [source: WP-JG]
- Autonomous vehicles: Germany's 2016 ethics commission adopted 20 rules; rule 8 says genuine dilemmatic decisions between lives "can thus not be clearly standardized, nor can they be programmed such that they are ethically unquestionable." [source: WP-TP]

## 7. Objections and replies
### Justice and the transplant surgeon
- Transplant (above). Utilitarian responses: (a) bite the bullet — the case is too unrealistic for intuitions evolved for normal situations (Sprigge 1965; cf. Singer 1974, Unger 1996, Norcross 1997); (b) count a killing as worse than a death — but if the five are themselves murder victims, transplanting still minimises killings; (c) agent-relative value: the world with the transplant is worse *from the doctor's perspective* (Sen 1982; Broome 1991; Portmore 2001, 2003, 2011); fails if the doctor herself wounded the five; (d) go indirect: a publicly known rule permitting such transplants would not maximise utility (doctors err; patients would fear doctors; trust in medicine collapses), so rule utilitarianism forbids it. Direct consequentialists find it "convoluted" to judge this act by other doctors' mistakes (Smart 1956). [source: SEP-C §5]
- Framed-innocent / judge case (Foot's judge) generalises: a "framed innocent" may complain he was not treated impartially in the relevant sense, since he would never have consented to a system allowing it. [source: SEP-IMP §4.1]
### The separateness of persons
- Rawls: consequentialism (utilitarianism in particular) "does not take seriously the distinction between persons" (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971; SEP-IMP cites p. 187). [source: SEP-IMP §4.1] (Rawls says this more than once; the utilitarianism-chapter notes give p. 27 (1971) / p. 24 (1999) for the §5 occurrence — page is edition- and passage-dependent; do not give a page.)
- Williams: "if Kantianism abstracts in moral thought from the identity of persons, utilitarianism strikingly abstracts from their separateness" (*Moral Luck*, 1981, p. 3). [source: SEP-W §5]
### Integrity: Williams's George and Jim
- Bernard Williams, "A Critique of Utilitarianism", in J. J. C. Smart and Bernard Williams, *Utilitarianism: For and Against* (Cambridge UP, 1973). SEP-W calls Williams's part "a tour de force of philosophical demolition". [source: SEP-W §1]
- **George**: a chemist (WS-5) who is told by utilitarianism to take a job in weapons of mass destruction (chemical and biological warfare research, WS-5), because if he refuses he and his family will suffer poverty and someone else, who would do more harm, will take the job anyway. **Jim**: told by utilitarianism to kill one villager to prevent twenty being killed (UFA 97–99). Secondary sources (WS-5) set Jim in a small South American town where an army captain offers him, as an honoured visitor, the "privilege" of shooting one of twenty captive Indians; if he refuses, all twenty will be killed. [source: SEP-W §5; WS-5]
- Williams did NOT argue that utilitarianism gets these cases wrong: he "even suggests, albeit rather grudgingly, that utilitarianism tells Jim (at least) to do the right thing" ("…if (as I suppose) the utilitarian is right in this case…", UFA 117). The point is "what sort of considerations come into finding the answer" (UFA 99): "If the stories of George and Jim have a resonance, it is not the sound of a principle being dented by an intuition." [source: SEP-W §5]
- Integrity objection: utilitarianism's doctrine of "negative responsibility" makes it indifferent whether a state of affairs "consists in what I do, or is produced by what I do"; agency is always someone's agency; we are not "agents of the universal satisfaction system" (UFA 118). Key passage (UFA 116–117): "It is absurd to demand of such a man, when the sums come in from the utility network which the projects of others have in part determined, that he should just step aside from his own project and decision and acknowledge the decision which utilitarian calculation requires. It is to alienate him in a real sense from his actions and the source of his action in his own convictions. … It is thus, in the most literal sense, an attack on his integrity." "Integrity" here means wholeness (Latin *in-teger*), the ability to act from one's own "ground projects", not the virtue of honesty. [source: SEP-W §5]
- Williams rejects the utilitarian reply that loss of a central project is just a big disutility: "on the contrary, once he is prepared to look at it like that, the argument in any serious case is over anyway" (UFA 116); "the reason why utilitarianism cannot understand integrity is that it cannot coherently describe the relations between a man's projects and his actions" (UFA 100). [source: SEP-W §5]
### "One thought too many" and nearest-and-dearest
- Williams, *Moral Luck* (1981), p. 18 (the essay title "Persons, Character and Morality" is from memory, not checked): a man who saves his wife rather than a stranger: "it might have been hoped by some people (for instance, by his wife) that his motivating thought, fully spelled out, would be the thought that it was his wife, not that it was his wife and that in situations of this kind it is permissible to save one's wife." [source: SEP-W §3]
- Godwin's Fénelon case (*An Enquiry Concerning Political Justice*, 1793, pp. 41–42): if the Archbishop of Cambrai's palace is on fire and only he or his chambermaid can be saved, we ought to save Fénelon (author of *Telemachus*) because "that life ought to be preferred which will be most conducive to the general good." Godwin's "what magic is there in the pronoun 'my'?" is cited as consequentialist rhetoric. [source: SEP-IMP §§2–3; SEP-UH §2.1]
- Michael Stocker, "The Schizophrenia of Modern Ethical Theories", *Journal of Philosophy* 73 (1976): 453–66 — modern theories split justification from motivation (the alienation charge; applies to Kantianism too). [source: SEP-IMP §4.1 and bibliography]
### Alienation and Railton's "sophisticated consequentialism"
- Peter Railton, "Alienation, Consequentialism, and the Demands of Morality", *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 13(2) (1984): 134–171. A "sophisticated" consequentialist "will develop patterns of decision-making that do not, except on rare occasions, refer explicitly to consequentialist aims", so that both psychology and behaviour resemble a typical non-consequentialist's; consequentialism is a criterion of rightness, not a decision procedure. Railton also coined the objective/subjective consequentialism distinction. [source: SEP-IMP §3.2; SEP-C §§4, 6; WP Peter_Railton] Railton: born 23 May 1950; University of Michigan from 1979. [source: WP Peter_Railton]
- Related: Frank Jackson (1991): the efficient strategy for a consequentialist is to concentrate on small groups of particular people, which involves close relationships. Others: Bales 1971, Hare 1981, Brink 1989, Pettit 1997. [source: SEP-IMP §3.2]
- SEP-C's verdict: "It remains controversial, however, whether any form of consequentialism can adequately incorporate common moral intuitions about friendship." [source: SEP-C §6]
### Demandingness
- The objection: classic utilitarianism requires acts that should be options. Example: buying a $100 pair of shoes rather than giving the $100 to a charity that would save a life; watching television; teaching philosophy rather than working for CARE. (Scheffler 1982 cited for the charge.) [source: SEP-C §6]
- Responses: (1) hard line — we really are required to do much more (Kagan 1989; Singer 1993; Unger 1996); the counter-intuitions may be unreliable (Murphy 2000; Mulgan 2001; Singer 2005; Greene 2013). (2) Mill: wrong only if punishable, so "ought" without "wrong". (3) Rule consequentialism: internalising very demanding rules costs too much (Hooker 2000 ch. 8). (4) Agent-relative value. (5) Satisficing (Slote) or progressive consequentialism. (6) Scalar consequentialism. [source: SEP-C §6]
- Shelly Kagan, *The Limits of Morality* (Oxford UP, 1989): attacks both "ordinary morality" assumptions — that morality forbids some acts (harming innocents) even when they would produce more good, and that we are "not required to make our greatest possible contribution to the overall good". Kagan (b. 1954) is Clark Professor at Yale. [source: WP Shelly_Kagan]
- **Scheffler's agent-centred prerogative** (*The Rejection of Consequentialism*, Oxford: Clarendon, 1982; revised ed. 1994): a "hybrid" view — agents are always *permitted* to produce the best outcome, but each also has a prerogative to give greater weight to their own interests and projects than impartial value would, justified as respecting "the independence of the personal point of view"; Scheffler found no comparable rationale for agent-centred *restrictions* (constraints forbidding e.g. killing one to prevent more killings). The revised edition adds "Prerogatives without Restrictions", modifying the prerogative against an objection by Kagan. [source: WS-7; SEP-DEON §2 ("non-consequentialist permissions (Scheffler 1982)")] Scheffler: born 1951; Princeton PhD 1977 under Thomas Nagel; Berkeley 31 years, NYU from 2008; WP lists "agent-centred prerogatives" among his notable ideas. [source: WP Samuel_Scheffler]
- **Murphy** (Liam Murphy, *Moral Demands in Nonideal Theory*, Oxford UP, 2000): the real trouble with the optimizing principle of beneficence is *partial compliance* — those who comply must take up the slack of those who do not; he defends a "collective principle of beneficence" under a "compliance condition" (required sacrifice should not exceed what would be required under full compliance). [source: WS-8; SEP-C §6 bibliography] (WS-8 did not confirm the phrase "cooperative principle"; use "collective principle of beneficence".)
- Brian Barry: consequentialist impartiality would "extend to the whole of conduct the requirements of impartiality that on the common-sense view are restricted to judges and bureaucrats acting in their official capacities" (Barry 1995, 23). [source: SEP-IMP §3.1]
- James Griffin: "Since ought implies can, what lies outside natural human motivation does not even enter contention for being a moral requirement. Norms are not fashioned in a vacuum; they are cut to fit agents like us" (Griffin 1990, 129). [source: SEP-IMP §3.2]
- Rule worship / "prevent disaster" (see §4).

## 8. Singer's drowning-child argument (step-by-step argument for the chapter)
- Publication: Peter Singer, "Famine, Affluence, and Morality", written 1971, published *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 1(3) (Spring 1972): 229–243; prompted by starvation among refugees of the Bangladesh Liberation War (East Bengal, 1971). Singer said (2016 book preface) it had been reprinted in at least fifty anthologies; republished as a book by Oxford UP in 2016 with a foreword by Bill and Melinda Gates. [source: WP-FAM; WS-1]
- Premises (Singer's words as quoted by WP-FAM with page refs):
  1. "Suffering and death from lack of food, shelter and medical care are bad." (p. 231)
  2. "If it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it." (p. 231) — the "strong" version. (WP-FAM prints "…then we ought…"; GWWC's text (WS-1) prints "…we ought, morally, to do it". Use the latter.)
  3. Illustration: "if I am walking past a shallow pond and see a child drowning in it, I ought to wade in and pull the child out. This will mean getting my clothes muddy, but this is insignificant, while the death of the child would presumably be a very bad thing." [source: WS-1 (JSTOR copy and GWWC text as summarised); WP-FAM]
  4. Distance is irrelevant: "It makes no moral difference whether the person I can help is a neighbor's child ten yards from me or a Bengali whose name I shall never know, ten thousand miles away." (p. 231)
  5. Numbers are irrelevant: "The principle makes no distinction between cases in which I am the only person who could possibly do anything and cases in which I am just one among millions in the same position." (p. 232)
  6. Conclusion: the affluent ought to give far more to famine relief than is normal; much of what is regarded as "charity" is in fact duty — Singer argues the traditional distinction between duty and charity cannot be drawn where it usually is. [source: WP-FAM; WS-2]
- Singer also offers a "moderate" version that replaces "anything of comparable moral importance" with "anything morally significant" — still, on his view, requiring large changes in how the affluent live. [source: WS-2] (Exact wording not seen in full text; see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Singer's further claim: "The moral point of view requires us to look beyond the interests of our own society… From the moral point of view, the prevention of the starvation of millions of people outside our society must be considered at least as pressing as the upholding of property norms within our society." [source: WP-FAM]
- Main objection: demandingness (John Arthur 1982; John Kekes 2002; Kwame Anthony Appiah 2006); Singer's claimed straight path from commonsense morality to great giving is "disputed". Appiah (2006): would the most effective act of a man in an expensive suit be to sell the suit and donate rather than save the child? Appiah: he "should save the drowning child and ruin my suit". Other objections from the critical literature in this file: Murphy's partial-compliance (fair-share) point; Williams's integrity/ground projects; Scheffler's prerogative. [source: WP-FAM; WP-EA]
- Replies available (from sources above): the demandingness intuitions may be unreliable (Kagan 1989; Singer 1993; Murphy 2000 chs. 1–4 as cited by SEP-C); Murphy himself argues it is not *unfair* in the standard sense to ask for more than one's fair share (WS-8); the moderate principle still yields large obligations. [source: SEP-C §6; WS-8]
- Influence: Peter Unger, *Living High and Letting Die* (1996); William MacAskill encountered the essay in an undergraduate seminar (WP-FAM) — WP MacAskill says he read it at 18; Larissa MacFarquhar's *Strangers Drowning* (2015). Gilbert Harman reportedly called it "one of the most famous articles written in moral philosophy". [source: WP-FAM; WP William_MacAskill]
- Singer, *The Life You Can Save: Acting Now to End World Poverty* (2009) — ends by proposing a minimum ethical standard of giving; tenth-anniversary edition announced December 2019 as a free e-book. [source: WP The_Life_You_Can_Save]

## 9. Parfit's repugnant conclusion (brief)
- Parfit's formulation (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984): "For any possible population of at least ten billion people, all with a very high quality of life, there must be some much larger imaginable population whose existence, if other things are equal, would be better even though its members have lives that are barely worth living." [source: SEP-RCON intro]
- Total utilitarianism implies it (population Z beats A because of a greater quantity of welfare). Sidgwick had nearly acknowledged it: the point "up to which, on utilitarian principles, population ought to be encouraged to increase, is not that at which the average happiness is the greatest possible… but that at which the happiness reaches its maximum" (1907, p. 418). [source: SEP-RCON §1]
- Mere Addition Paradox: A (everyone very well off) → A+ (A plus extra people with lives worth living, which seems not worse) → B (same number as A+, equal and with higher average and total than A+, so better than A+) → … → Z. Each step looks innocent; the end is repugnant. [source: SEP-RCON §1]
- Average utilitarianism blocks it but has "implications generally regarded as highly counterintuitive" (e.g. that adding very good lives below the average makes things worse) and "has not obtained much acceptance". [source: SEP-RCON §2.1.1; SEP-C §3]
- Some accept the conclusion: Tännsjö (1992, 1998, 2002), Huemer (2008), Mackie (1985), Ryberg; our intuitions may fail with very large numbers, and a life "barely worth living" may not be so bad. [source: SEP-RCON §2.8]
- Arrhenius: avoiding it without "other equally counterintuitive conclusions" has proved "surprisingly difficult"; it is "one of the cardinal challenges of modern ethics". [source: SEP-RCON intro]

## 10. Effective altruism
- Definition (proponents): "using evidence and reason to figure out how to benefit others as much as possible, and taking action on that basis." A "defining feature" is impartiality: global equal consideration of interests. [source: WP-EA]
- Origins: several communities converged from the latter half of the 2000s — the GiveWell evidence-based-charity community, the Giving What We Can / 80,000 Hours pledge-and-careers community, and the rationalist / existential-risk community (Singularity Institute, Future of Humanity Institute, *LessWrong*). The name "effective altruism" was coined in 2011. [source: WP-EA]
- GiveWell: founded 2007 by Holden Karnofsky and Elie Hassenfeld, who had worked at the hedge fund Bridgewater Associates. GiveWell says it has guided 150,000 donors and moved $2.6 billion; "under their cost-effectiveness estimates" this averted 340,000 deaths. [source: WP-GW] Top recommendations have included the Against Malaria Foundation, Malaria Consortium, deworming charities and GiveDirectly. [source: WP-EA]
- Giving What We Can: founded at Oxford in 2009 by Toby Ord (with his wife Bernadette Young) and William MacAskill, launched with 23 members; members pledge at least 10% of income to effective charities. Ord cited Peter Singer and Thomas Pogge. [source: WP-GWWC; WP Toby_Ord]
- Toby Ord: born July 1979, Melbourne; Oxford DPhil; Future of Humanity Institute research fellow 2014–19, senior research fellow until the institute's closure in 2024; *The Precipice: Existential Risk and the Future of Humanity* (March 2020), estimating a 1 in 6 total risk of existential catastrophe in the next century. [source: WP Toby_Ord; WP The_Precipice]
- William MacAskill: born 24 March 1987 (born William Crouch), Glasgow; co-founded GWWC (2009), 80,000 Hours (with Benjamin Todd) and the Centre for Effective Altruism; books *Doing Good Better* (2015), *What We Owe the Future* (2022), co-author *Moral Uncertainty* (2020). [source: WP William_MacAskill]
- Centre for Effective Altruism: name adopted in late 2011 when GWWC and 80,000 Hours members met to name an umbrella body — "the first use of the term in its current sense"; registered as a charity the following year (2012). [source: WP-CEA] (WP-EA says the 2011 vote chose the name; WP-GWWC says the two organisations incorporated CEA in 2012. Say: named 2011, incorporated 2012.)
- Singer used the term in a 2013 TED talk; Singer's *The Most Good You Can Do* (2015) and MacAskill's *Doing Good Better* (2015) are described by B. V. E. Hyde as "the bibles of effective altruism". Singer (2015, p. vii): "Effective altruism is based on a very simple idea: we should do the most good we can." [source: WP-EA]
- EA vs utilitarianism: MacAskill argues EA, unlike utilitarianism, does not claim one should always maximise the good regardless of means or that well-being is the only intrinsic value; Ord: utilitarians are "number-crunching", most effective altruists "guided by conventional wisdom tempered by an eye to the numbers". Critics reply that EA keeps utilitarianism's impartiality, welfarism and good-maximisation (Adams, Crary and Gruen 2023, p. xxvi). MacAskill: "naive calculations that justify some harmful action because it has good consequences are, in practice, almost never correct." [source: WP-EA]
- Cause prioritisation: importance/scale, tractability and neglectedness framework; QALYs per dollar; counterfactual reasoning (career choice, "earning to give"). [source: WP-EA]
- **Longtermism**: MacAskill: "the view that positively influencing the longterm future is a key moral priority of our time"; "strong longtermism" makes it *the* key priority. His three premises (*What We Owe the Future*, 2022): future people count; there could be very many of them; we can make their lives better. Criticisms: unpredictability over long horizons; deprioritising present problems such as climate change (Hupfer's "Far-Future Priority Objection"); reliance on tiny probabilities of huge payoffs (cf. "Pascal's mugging"); overstating human control over history (B. V. E. Hyde). [source: WP-LT; WP What_We_Owe_the_Future]
- Criticisms of EA (beyond FTX): neglect of systemic/political change (Amia Srinivasan: "a rather homogeneous movement of middle-class white men fighting poverty through largely conventional means, but it is at least in theory a broad church"; Judith Lichtenberg; Timnit Gebru); concentrating influence in wealthy donors (Susan Dwyer, Joshua Stein, Olúfẹ́mi O. Táíwò); measurability bias (justice, fairness are hard to quantify); impartiality vs community-based giving (William Schambra). Edited critical volume: Carol J. Adams, Alice Crary and Lori Gruen (eds.), *The Good It Promises, the Harm It Does: Critical Essays on Effective Altruism* (Oxford UP, 2023); Alice Crary, "Against effective altruism", *Radical Philosophy* 210 (Summer 2021). [source: WP-EA]
- **FTX and Sam Bankman-Fried** (hedge carefully; legal facts as reported by Wikipedia): Bankman-Fried (born 5 March 1992) met MacAskill over lunch in 2012 while an MIT undergraduate; MacAskill encouraged him to earn money and donate it. He briefly worked at CEA (director of development, late 2017) before founding Alameda Research; founded FTX in 2019; the FTX Future Fund had MacAskill as an adviser and made a $13.9 million grant to CEA. [source: WP-EA; WP-CEA; WP-SBF]
- FTX, Alameda Research and over 130 affiliated entities filed for bankruptcy on 11 November 2022; Bankman-Fried resigned as CEO that day. Arrested in the Bahamas 12 December 2022 and extradited. Trial began 3 October 2023; on 2 November 2023 a jury found him guilty on all seven counts (fraud, conspiracy, money laundering). On 28 March 2024 he was sentenced to 25 years in prison and ordered to forfeit about $11 billion ($11.02 billion). [source: WP-SBF]
- Post-sentence (very recent, Wikipedia only — hedge or omit): appeal filed April 2024; on 12 June 2026 the Second Circuit is reported to have affirmed the conviction, sentence and forfeiture; in September 2026 he petitioned the U.S. Supreme Court. [source: WP-SBF]
- Effect on EA: the collapse brought "mainstream attention and criticism"; journalists asked whether leaders overlooked warnings; MacAskill condemned Bankman-Fried's actions, saying EA emphasises integrity; Leif Wenar argued the FTX case is not separable from EA's expected-value reasoning. [source: WP-EA]

## 11. Arguments for consequentialism (for balance)
- Default/burden argument: most begin with the presumption that we ought to make the world better when we can; the question is whether constraints or options need adding (Kagan 1989, 1998). [source: SEP-C §7]
- Inference to the best explanation: moderate deontologists say killing one to save five is wrong but killing one to save a million is not, yet cannot non-arbitrarily locate the threshold; consequentialists put the line where benefits most outweigh costs; consequences explain which of two conflicting promises to keep. [source: SEP-C §7]
- Sidgwick's axioms: universalizability, rationality (aim at the good generally), and equality ("the good of any one individual is of no more importance, from the point of view ... of the Universe, than the good of any other"). Harsanyi (1977, 1978): rational people behind a veil of ignorance would favour a kind of consequentialism. [source: SEP-C §7]
- IEP reconstructs two arguments step by step, with objections: the Ideal Spectator argument (an all-knowing, impartially sympathetic spectator would wish for "the greatest possible balance of satisfaction of the desires of all people"; objection: why should absence of bias mean equal sympathy rather than none?), and a Mill-style argument from what is desirable (objection: premise 1, that desiring something is thinking it will increase one's happiness, seems false — people procrastinate, and give their lives for causes). [source: IEP-C §§3c–3d]

## Debates
- **Is Mohism consequentialist?** Fraser (SEP-MO): yes — the world's earliest consequentialism, best called "practice" or "*dao* consequentialism", with a plural objective list of collective goods; utilitarian readings also from Fung Yu-lan, A. C. Graham and Benjamin Schwartz. Divine-command readings: Dennis Ahern (1976), David Soles (1999), Augustine Tseu, criticised by Kristopher Duda (2001). Loy (IEP-MZ §6): the controversy "is not well framed" in the "modern and somewhat alien categories" of command theory and utilitarianism, but the genuine question remains how Heaven's will and good consequences relate. Ivanhoe and Van Norden: "state consequentialism"; Fraser: no, the concern is all people of the world. SEP-UH: "proto-utilitarian" (Van Norden 2007). [source: SEP-MO; IEP-MZ; WP-SC; SEP-UH]
- **What counts as consequentialism**: agent-neutral only (McNaughton & Rawling, Pettit) vs broad (Bennett, Broome, Portmore); and whether any theory can be "consequentialized". [source: SEP-C §2]
- **Act vs rule consequentialism**: collapse/incoherence/rule-worship (Smart, Lyons, Arneson, Card, Wall) vs Hooker's replies. [source: SEP-RC §8; IEP-AR]
- **Trolley cases**: what explains switch vs push — doing/allowing (Foot), double effect (Mikhail), triple effect (Kamm), or nothing because switching is wrong too (Thomson 2008; Anscombe). Whether thought-experiment intuitions are trustworthy at all (Greene's debunking vs Berker, Kahane; Scruton: such dilemmas eliminate "just about every morally relevant relationship"). [source: SEP-DA; SEP-DE; SEP-NE; WP-TP]
- **Integrity**: is Williams offering counterexamples (common misreading) or a claim that utilitarianism cannot describe agency? SEP-W: the latter. Indirect utilitarians (Railton, Sen) try to build integrity into the balance sheet; Williams rejected that move in advance. [source: SEP-W]
- **Demandingness**: bite the bullet (Kagan, Singer, Unger) vs prerogatives (Scheffler) vs fair shares (Murphy) vs rule consequentialism (Hooker) vs satisficing/scalar. [source: SEP-C §6; SEP-IMP]
- **Repugnant conclusion**: reject total view, revise aggregation, or accept (Tännsjö, Huemer). [source: SEP-RCON]
- **EA**: individual giving vs systemic change; maximising vs side-constraints after FTX; longtermism's reliance on expected value with tiny probabilities. [source: WP-EA; WP-LT]
