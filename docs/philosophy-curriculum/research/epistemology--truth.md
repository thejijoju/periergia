## 1. The problem and the truth-bearers

- SEP-T: "The problem of truth is in a way easy to state: what truths are, and what (if anything) makes them true." Whether there is a metaphysical problem of truth at all is itself a standing issue. [source: SEP-T intro]
- The "neo-classical" theories (correspondence, coherence, pragmatist) answer the "nature question": what is the nature of truth? Each makes truth "part of a more thoroughgoing metaphysics or epistemology". Glanzberg says the received forms "depart from the views that were actually defended in the early 20th century", hence "neo-classical". [source: SEP-T §1]
- Candidate truth-bearers: beliefs, propositions, sentences, utterances (also statements, judgments). Moore and Russell's move from the identity theory to correspondence c. 1910 was driven by their doubts about propositions; Tarski took sentences as bearers "for convenience" and "explicitly distances himself" from the contentious issues (Tarski 1944); sentences must be fully interpreted, and Quine's "eternal sentences" have no context-dependence. [source: SEP-T §§2.1, 6.1]
- Glanzberg: the candidates form "a tight circle: interpreted sentences, the propositions they express, the belief speakers might hold towards them, and the acts of assertion they might perform with them", all meaningful; so contemporary debates are "much less concerned with the issue of truth-bearers than were the classical ones". Quineans deny propositions. [source: SEP-T §6.1]
- Aquinas also applied "true" to things and persons (a "true friend" conforms to our or God's conception of a friend); medieval theologians grounded truth in God ("I am the way and the truth and the life", John 14:6). This thing-truth is "disregarded by modern and contemporary analytic philosophers but survives to some extent in existentialist and continental philosophy". [source: SEP-Cor §1]
- T-schema / Tarski biconditionals: an adequate theory of truth for a language L must imply, for each sentence φ of L, "⌜φ⌝ is true if and only if φ" (Convention T; SEP-T simplifies Tarski). "This is an adequacy condition for theories, not a theory itself"; it guarantees the truth predicate is "extensionally correct". The instances are "usually called the Tarski biconditionals". Standard instance: "'Snow is white' is true if and only if snow is white." [source: SEP-T §2.2–2.3]
- Ramsey's stronger "equivalence thesis": ⌜⌜φ⌝ is true⌝ has the SAME MEANING as ⌜φ⌝; the Tarski biconditionals are merely material biconditionals. [source: SEP-T §§5.1–5.2]
- Wright's "platitudes" about truth (useful as a checklist for readers): "To assert is to present as true"; any truth-apt content has a truth-apt negation; "For a sentence to be true is for it to correspond with reality"; "To be true is to 'tell it like it is'"; "Some truths may never be known"; "A sentence may be true without being justified and vice-versa"; "Sentences cannot be more or less true". [source: SEP-Plu §3.1.1, citing Wright 1992: 34–5 and 2001]
- Truth and assertion: "Truth is the aim of assertion" is read as a constitutive rule of a practice (like the rules of chess), not as a claim about every speaker's intention (liars do not aim at truth). Williamson (1996) argues knowledge, not truth, is the norm ("assert only what one knows"). [source: SEP-T §6.6]
- How popular are the theories? PhilPapers Survey 2009 (faculty at 99 leading departments), "Truth: correspondence, deflationary, or epistemic?" — accept or lean toward: correspondence 50.8%; deflationary 24.8%; other 17.5%; epistemic 6.9%. David: "correspondence-type theories may enjoy a weak majority among professional philosophers and … the opposition is divided." [source: SEP-Cor §4] (A 2020 PhilPapers survey exists but was NOT checked here; cite 2009 only.)

## 2. Aristotle's dictum and ancient background

- Aristotle, *Metaphysics* Γ 7, 1011b25 (SEP-Cor's wording; SEP-Cor does not name the translator): "To say of what is that it is not, or of what is not that it is, is false, while to say of what is that it is, and of what is not that it is not, is true". "Virtually identical formulations can be found in Plato (*Cratylus* 385b2, *Sophist* 263b)." [source: SEP-Cor §1] SEP-T quotes the second half as "to say of what is that it is, or of what is not that it is not, is true" and attributes it to "Ross 1928", citing "Metaphysics Γ 7.27" (note "or" vs SEP-Cor's "and"; see NOT VERIFIED). [source: SEP-T §3]
- David: the definition "does not highlight the basic correspondence intuition"; it is "a muted, relatively minimal version of a correspondence theory", and "for this reason it has also been claimed as a precursor of deflationary theories of truth". It "actually begins with the definition of falsehood". [source: SEP-Cor §1]
- Aristotle sounds more like a correspondence theorist in *Categories* (12b11, 14b14): things (*pragmata*) — "his sitting and his not sitting" — underlie "He is sitting" and "He is not sitting"; *De Interpretatione* 16a3: thoughts are "likenesses" (*homoiomata*) of things. [source: SEP-Cor §1]
- Tarski "always stressed that his definition follows the intuitions of Aristotle", influenced by his Polish teachers, especially Tadeusz Kotarbiński; Polish philosophers called it the "classical truth definition". [source: IEP-ST]; SEP-T: "The basic idea of correspondence, as Tarski (1944) and others have suggested, is captured in the slogan from Aristotle's *Metaphysics*". [source: SEP-T §3]
- ARGUMENT (the Sophists' puzzle about falsehood, and Plato's answer — a good opening case): Puzzle: (1) To judge falsely is to judge what is not. (2) One cannot judge what is not, for it is not there to be judged. (3) To judge what is not is to judge nothing, i.e. not to judge at all. (4) So false judgment is impossible. (Cf. *Euthydemus* 283e–288a; *Cratylus* 429c–e; *Republic* 478a–c; *Theaetetus* 188d–190e.) Plato's answer in the *Sophist* (236d–264b; key 261c–263d): a sentence such as "Theaetetus sits" is a complex of name (*onoma*) and verb (*rhēma*); it is true when Theaetetus is in the state ascribed, false when he is in another state. "Only things that are show up in this account": in falsehood the ascribed state still *is*, but differs from his actual state. Thought is "speech that occurs without voice, inside the soul in conversation with itself" (263e). Residual problem: sentences with empty names ("Pegasus flies"). David: Aristotelian subject–predicate analysis "had its origin in Plato's response to a sophistical argument against the possibility of falsehood". [source: SEP-Cor §1.2]
- Carneades (Academic sceptic, 2nd c. BCE), reported by Sextus (*Adv. Math.* vii 168): a presentation "is true when it is in accord (*symphonos*) with the object presented, and false when it is in discord with it". Philoponus (*In Cat.* 81, 25–34): truth lies in neither the thing nor the statement but in the agreement between them — "the simile of the fitting shoe". [source: SEP-Cor §1]
- Aquinas: "*Veritas est adaequatio rei et intellectus*" ("Truth is the equation of thing and intellect"; *De Veritate* Q.1 A.1–3; *ST* I Q.16). "Aquinas credits the Neoplatonist Isaac Israeli with this definition, but there is no such definition in Isaac." [source: SEP-Cor §1.1]
- Medieval "semantic" version: a sentence is true iff "as it signifies, so it is" (*sicut significat, ita est*); Ockham (*Summa Logicae* II) and Buridan (*Sophismata* II) gave lists of truth conditions by sentence type. [source: SEP-Cor §1.1]
- Moderns took correspondence as obvious: Descartes (1639, AT II 597: "the word 'truth', in the strict sense, denotes the conformity of thought with its object"); Kant (B82: "The nominal definition of truth, that it is the agreement of [a cognition] with its object, is assumed as granted"); Spinoza, Locke, Leibniz, Hume; Berkeley "a potentially significant exception". Even William James (1907, p. 96): "Truth, as any dictionary will tell you, is a property of certain of our ideas. It means their 'agreement', as falsity means their disagreement, with 'reality'". [source: SEP-Cor §§1.1, 4]

## 3. Correspondence theory

- Slogan: "A belief is true if and only if it corresponds to a fact." Held by Moore (1953, lectures of 1910–11) and Russell (1910b; 1912). Russell 1912 (*The Problems of Philosophy*, p. 129): "Thus a belief is true when there is a corresponding fact, and is false when there is no corresponding fact". [source: SEP-T §1.1.1; SEP-Cor §1.2]
- Backstory: from 1898 to c. 1910 Moore and Russell rejected idealism but held an IDENTITY theory (Moore 1899, 1902; Russell 1904): a true proposition is identical to a fact; Moore 1902: no "difference between truth and the reality to which it is supposed to correspond". They turned to correspondence c. 1910 because they came to doubt there could be false propositions (they would be fact-like things), and so rejected propositions; Russell later called propositions "curious shadowy things". Moore (1899) had earlier seen correspondence as "a source of idealism". [source: SEP-T §1.1.1]
- Irony: Russell and Moore's formulations are "indebted to their idealist opponents, F. H. Bradley (1883 …) and H. H. Joachim (1906)", who set up correspondence-to-fact as the target. [source: SEP-Cor §1.2]
- Neo-classical version (worked example, Glanzberg's): the belief that (opera singer) Samuel Ramey sings is true because the world contains the fact ⟨Ramey, Singing⟩, built from a particular and a property; it contains no fact ⟨Ramey, Dancing⟩. Correspondence holds when proposition and fact "have the same structure, and the same constituents at each structural position" — they "mirror each-other". The theory is "at its core an ontological thesis". [source: SEP-T §1.1.2]
- Object-based vs fact-based versions: object-based (Plato to the moderns) assumed subject–predicate truth-bearers, so cannot handle conditionals or disjunctions; fact-based versions "became prominent only in the 20th century" (foreshadowed by Hume and Mill). [source: SEP-Cor §1.2]
- Russell's "first truism": in later Russell (1956, p. 182) the existence of facts is "the first truism". [source: SEP-T §1.1.2]
- **Logical atomism / Tractatus**: Wittgenstein (1921) and Russell (1918) restrict correspondence to elementary (atomic) truth-bearers; complex sentences get their truth recursively by truth-tables ("not-p" is true iff "p" is false, etc.). The world is "the totality of atomic facts" (cf. TLP 2.04); no entities for logical constants: TLP 4.0312: "My fundamental idea is that the 'logical constants' are not representatives; that there can be no representatives of the logic of facts"; Russell 1918: "You must not look about the real world for an object which you can call 'or', and say 'Now look at this. This is "or"'". [source: SEP-Cor §7.1]
- Picture theory in one line (cross-ref *Wittgenstein*, do not repeat): "the picture is a model of reality" (TLP 2.12); "That is how a picture is attached to reality; it reaches right out to it" (TLP 2.1511); "The general form of a proposition is: This is how things stand" (TLP 4.5). [source: SEP-W §2.1] SEP-Id adds that whether the *Tractatus* holds a correspondence rather than an identity theory "is questionable", since it calls sentences themselves facts. [source: SEP-Id §2]
- **Austin, "Truth" (1950)**, *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* Supp. Vol. 24: 111–129: each statement is correlated by "demonstrative conventions" with a historic situation and by "descriptive conventions" with a type of situation; it is true if the former is of the latter type (SEP-T: "It is true if the former is of the latter type"). Correspondence is "entirely conventional", not mirroring; "It takes two to make a truth" (Austin 1950, p. 118); traditional theories commit "the error of reading back into the world the features of language" (p. 155). Debated with Strawson 1950. Objection (David): neglects systematicity — language cannot have a separate convention for each statement. Austin: 26 Mar 1911 – 8 Feb 1960. [source: SEP-T §3.3; SEP-Cor §§3, 6; WP-J._L._Austin] (The terms "demonstrative"/"descriptive conventions" are standard but were not seen in the sources read; safer to paraphrase as SEP does.)
- **Correspondence without facts (Tarski, Field)**: Davidson (1969) and Field (1972) saw Tarski's theory as the core of a correspondence theory without facts: truth is determined by reference and satisfaction, "word-to-world" relations. Field 1972 ("Tarski's theory of truth", *J. Phil.* 69: 347–375): Tarski gives only disquotation clauses ("'Snow' refers to snow") and needs supplementing by a (physicalist, causal) theory of reference. Putnam (1985–86): "As a philosophical account of truth, Tarski's theory fails as badly as it is possible for an account to fail." [source: SEP-T §3.1]
- Realism link: Putnam (1978, p. 18): "Whatever else realists say, they typically say that they believe in a 'correspondence theory of truth'." Dummett makes bivalence (every truth-bearer is true or false) the mark of realism. [source: SEP-T §4.1]
- **Truthmaker theory (Armstrong)**: Armstrong, *A World of States of Affairs* (Cambridge UP, 1997): states of affairs (facts) are fundamental and are truthmakers for propositions; many-many relation. Truthmaker principle: for any truth there must be "something in the world which makes it the case, that serves as an ontological ground, for this truth" (1997, p. 115). Schema (Fox 1987): "If φ, then there is an x such that necessarily, if x exists, then φ." Weaker slogan: "truth supervenes on being" (Merricks 2007). Tropes, or only concrete particulars, are alternative truthmakers. [source: SEP-T §§3.3–3.4]
- Origin story: C. B. Martin and Armstrong came to truthmakers by criticising phenomenalism (brute counterfactuals about possible experience), Ryle's behaviourism and Prior's presentism — theories that failed to supply truthmakers ("catching cheaters"); Lewis (1992) called this an overreaction. [source: SEP-TM §4]
- Over-generation problem: if truthmaking = entailment, anything makes every necessary truth true (your fridge's leftovers make 2+2=4 true); Restall (1996) derived "truth-maker monism" (every truthmaker makes every truth true) with the disjunction thesis. [source: SEP-TM §1.1]
- **Negative facts**: what makes "There is no hippopotamus in this room" true? Russell (1918) "finds himself driven to admit negative facts"; Russell (1919, p. 287): "There is implanted in the human breast an almost unquenchable desire to find some way of avoiding the admission that negative facts are as ultimate as those that are positive." Russell also noted there is "no formal test" for being a negative fact ("so-and-so is dead" vs "isn't blind"). Wittgenstein: the non-existence of atomic facts as a "negative fact" (TLP 2.06). Armstrong rejects negative facts and posits a second-order "totality fact" (that these are all the first-order facts); Beall 2000, Cameron 2008, Jago 2018 defend negative facts. Lewis/Milne puzzles: "absolutely nothing exists" could be true yet would need a truthmaker; Milne's sentence "(M) This sentence has no truthmaker". [source: SEP-Cor §7.1; SEP-T §3.4; SEP-TM §2.1–2.2] (The hippopotamus example is a writer's illustration, not from the sources.)
- **What are facts?** David's Objection 3.F2: fact-talk is "parasitic on truth-talk"; facts are "projected from true sentences for the sake of correspondence" (Quine 1987, p. 213; cf. Strawson 1950). 3.F1: "funny facts" — negative, disjunctive, conditional, universal, counterfactual. 3.C1: correspondence must be resemblance, but "an idea can be like nothing but an idea" (Berkeley). [source: SEP-Cor §5] IEP-T's examples: is the fact that corresponds to "Brutus stabbed Caesar" the same as for "Caesar was stabbed by Brutus"? is there a fact that there are fewer than 1,006,455 balls on the table? [source: IEP-T §3]
- Other objections: Objection 1 (too narrow: no moral or logical facts; responses: non-cognitivism, error theory, reductionism, standing firm; Hume's two-pronged definition: "Truth or falsehood consists in an agreement or disagreement either to the real relations of ideas, or to real existence and matter of fact", *Treatise* 3.1.1); Objection 2 (too trivial: "corresponds to the facts" is an idiom). [source: SEP-Cor §5]
- ARGUMENT (the "comparison" or no-independent-access objection — recommended step-by-step reconstruction): (1) If truth is correspondence, then to know a belief is true we must know it corresponds to reality. (2) To know that, we would have to compare the belief with reality as it is in itself, independently of our cognition. (3) We cannot step outside our minds; all our access to the world is mediated by cognition. (4) So, on the correspondence theory, knowledge is impossible (scepticism) (cf. Kant 1800, intro vii). (5) Scepticism is unacceptable, so the correspondence theory must be replaced by an epistemic account (e.g. Blanshard 1941). "Ever since Berkeley's attack on the representational theory of the mind, objections of this sort have enjoyed considerable popularity." REPLY (David): two hidden assumptions fail. (i) It assumes S knows x only if S knows that x is true — not required by standard definitions; it confuses knowing with knowing that one knows. (ii) It assumes that if truth = F, one knows x is true only if one knows x has F: by that standard no one who did not know water is H2O could know the Nile contains water. One can taste the water or ask a reliable informant; likewise the correspondence theory does not require "actually comparing a belief with a fact". [source: SEP-Cor §9.2] A Tarski-friendly version of the objection is Brentano's (comparison impossible), discussed by Woleński. [source: IEP-ST]
- **Slingshot (brief)**: "Inspired by an allegedly similar argument of Frege's, Davidson (1969) argues that the correspondence theory is bankrupt because it cannot avoid the consequence that all true sentences correspond to the same fact: the Big Fact." Premises: (i) logically equivalent sentences are substitutable in "the fact that…"; (ii) co-referring singular terms are substitutable there. Using the descriptions "the x such that x = Diogenes & p" and "…& q", one moves from "s corresponds to the fact that p" to "s corresponds to the fact that q" for any q with the same truth-value. Davidson (IEP-T quotes "True to the Facts"): "if true statements correspond to anything, they all correspond to the same thing". First versions: Church (1943) and Gödel (1944); adapted by Quine (1953, 1960) against modal logic. Name: "because a giant opponent is brought down by a single small weapon, allegedly"; dubbed "slingshot" by Jon Barwise and John Perry (1981). Replies: deny (i) (logical equivalence is not sufficient for fact identity) or (ii) (definite descriptions are not genuine singular terms; Neale 2001); Gödel (1944) observed it fails on Russell's theory of descriptions. [source: SEP-Cor §9.1; IEP-T §3; WP-Slingshot_argument]

## 4. Coherence theory

- Slogan: "A belief is true if and only if it is part of a coherent system of beliefs"; coherence stronger than consistency; offered "as an analysis of the nature of truth, and not simply a test or criterion". Truth is "a content-to-content, or belief-to-belief, relation". [source: SEP-T §1.2]
- Harold H. Joachim, *The Nature of Truth* (Oxford: Clarendon, 1906): "Truth in its essential nature is that systematic coherence which is the character of a significant whole" (p. 76); only the "whole complete truth" (p. 90) is fully true; individual judgments are true only to a degree. This is the theory Russell (1910a) attacks. [source: SEP-T §1.2]
- Brand Blanshard, *The Nature of Thought* (London: Allen & Unwin, 1939), ch. XXVI; Blanshard (27 Aug 1892 – 19 Nov 1987), American defender of rationalism and idealism, held a coherence theory "closely related to Joachim's". [source: SEP-T §1.2; SEP-Coh §2.2; WP-Brand_Blanshard]
- F. H. Bradley (30 Jan 1846 – 18 Sep 1924; *Appearance and Reality* 1893): Bradley (1914) held coherence as "mutual explanatory support"; but "many scholars read Bradley as holding a version of the identity theory" (Candlish; Baldwin 1991). Debate; present both. [source: SEP-Coh §1, §2.1; SEP-T §1.2; SEP-Id §2; WP-F._H._Bradley]
- Idealist background: coherence "is typically associated with idealism"; Walker (1989) argues every coherence theorist must be an idealist, though not vice versa; Walker attributes coherentism to Spinoza, Kant, Fichte and Hegel. IEP-T's list of advocates: Leibniz, Spinoza, Hegel, Bradley, Blanshard, Neurath, Hempel (late), Dummett, Putnam. Coherence from idealism "usually leads to the view that truth comes in degrees". [source: SEP-T §1.2; SEP-Coh §2.1; IEP-T §5]
- Versions differ on (a) the coherence relation (consistency — too weak, since two propositions may each be consistent with the set but not with each other; entailment; mutual explanatory support) and (b) the "specified set" (largest consistent set actually believed — Young 1995; what people like us would believe at the limit of inquiry — Putnam 1981; what an omniscient being would believe). Non-omniscient versions reject bivalence and "transcendence". [source: SEP-Coh §1]
- Logical positivist coherentism: Otto Neurath (10 Dec 1882 – 22 Dec 1945) and Hempel (1935) argued we cannot "get outside" our beliefs to compare them with facts; Neurath "adopted a militant syntacticism, avoiding semantic entities and properties such as truth-values, reference and correspondence"; Schlick held a correspondence theory, and "His realism opposed Neurath's coherentism". Neurath's boat (1932b/1983, 92, SEP translation): "There is no way to establish fully secured, neat protocol statements as starting points of the sciences. There is no *tabula rasa*. We are like sailors who have to rebuild their ship on the open sea, without ever being able to dismantle it in dry-dock and reconstruct it from its best components." Earlier version in "Anti-Spengler" (1921; trans. in *Empiricism and Sociology*, 1973, p. 199): "We are like sailors who on the open sea must reconstruct their ship but are never able to start afresh from the bottom." [source: SEP-Coh §2.2; SEP-Neu §3; WP-Neurath's_boat; WP-Otto_Neurath] (Cross-ref *Logical Positivism*, which mentions the boat via Quine.)
- ARGUMENT (Blanshard's route from justification to truth): (1) Coherence with our beliefs is the test of truth. (2) If truth were correspondence to objective facts, coherence would be no test of truth, since "there is no guarantee that a perfectly coherent set of beliefs matches objective reality". (3) So truth cannot consist in correspondence; it consists in coherence. OBJECTION (Rescher 1973): the argument needs coherence to be an INFALLIBLE test; if it is a good but fallible test, as Rescher suggests, the argument fails, and the "falling apart" of truth and justification is to be expected. [source: SEP-Coh §2.2]
- Second epistemological argument (Neurath, Hempel): we can only know that a proposition coheres, never that it corresponds. OBJECTION: it does not follow that propositions do not correspond — only that we cannot know it; correspondence theorists may accept unknowable truths, or argue with Davidson (1986) that coherence is good evidence of correspondence. [source: SEP-Coh §2.2]
- ARGUMENT (the specification objection; originates in Russell 1907, "On the Nature of Truth", *Proc. Aristotelian Society* 7: 228–49): (1) A coherence theory says p is true iff p coheres with a set of propositions. (2) "Jane Austen was hanged for murder" coheres with some set of propositions; "Jane Austen died in her bed" coheres with another. (3) The theory offers no grounds for calling the first false and the second true. (4) Appeals to "most comprehensive" systems, simplicity or empirical adequacy fail, since "there is no reason… why two or more systems cannot be equally large" or equally simple. So coherence cannot be what truth is. REPLY (Young): coherentists never say truth is coherence with ANY set, but with the set of propositions actually believed (or held true); "No one actually believes the set of propositions with which (1) coheres", so (1) is false without contradiction. COUNTER (Walker 1989): the reply needs "S is believed" to be true; if its truth is again coherence, there is a regress, and arbitrary sets can include "'Jane Austen was hanged for murder' is believed"; stopping the regress seems to require an objective fact that S is believed. Young replies the regress is benign and correspondence faces a parallel one. [source: SEP-Coh §3.1] NOTE ON THE EXAMPLE: SEP uses "Jane Austen"; Russell's own example was the Victorian historian and bishop William Stubbs — "Bishop Stubbs was hanged for murder" (WS: Russell 1907 / *Philosophical Essays* 1910; one secondary source contrasts "Bishop Stubbs wore ecclesiastical gaiters"). See NOT VERIFIED for exact wording.
- Russell's second formulation (*Problems of Philosophy*, 1912, ch. XII "Truth and Falsehood"): the first of "two great difficulties" is that "there is no reason to suppose that only one coherent body of beliefs is possible"; a novelist might invent a past for the world that perfectly fits what we know yet is quite different from the real past. [source: WS-5 (search summary; text not read directly — use paraphrase)]
- The transcendence objection: "Jane Austen wrote ten sentences on November 17th, 1807" is either true or false, though no proposition about the exact number coheres with any set of beliefs. Reply: question-begging; coherence with an omniscient being's beliefs is immune. [source: SEP-Coh §3.2]
- The logic objection (Russell 1912): talk of coherence presupposes the law of non-contradiction, whose truth then cannot be explained by coherence. Reply: the law coheres with the belief that communication and reasoning are possible; any coherent set must contain it. [source: SEP-Coh §3.3]
- Newer objections: Thagard (2007, pp. 29–30): if there is a world independent of representations, "the aim of representation should be to describe the world"; McGinn (2002, p. 195): coherence + "p is true iff p" yields "snow falls from the sky iff the belief that snow falls from the sky coheres with other beliefs", i.e. idealism. [source: SEP-Coh §4]
- IEP-T objections: "true for me / false for you" (bismuth melting point) violates non-contradiction; everyone's beliefs are inconsistent ("Absence makes the heart grow fonder" / "Out of sight, out of mind"); "coheres with" may presuppose joint truth (circularity). IEP-T also treats radical postmodernism ("Consensus is truth") as "the most recent coherence theory" (Dowden's classification, not standard). [source: IEP-T §5]
- Davidson: argued via radical interpretation that most of our beliefs are true; in 1986 called his view a coherence theory, but in the "Afterthoughts" withdrew the label. [source: SEP-T §6.5]

## 5. Pragmatist theories (cross-ref *American Pragmatism*; keep short)

- Peirce, "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878), final section: the real is "that whose characters are independent of what anybody may think them to be"; then: "The opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate, is what we mean by the truth, and the object represented in this opinion is the real. That is the way I would explain reality." (EP1: 139). "End" = goal, telos, not a finishing date. [source: SEP-P §3.1; IEP-Prag dates the essay 1878]
- Russell on Peirce's convergence: "Is this an empirical generalization from the history of research? Or is it an optimistic belief in the perfectibility of man?… Whatever interpretation we adopt, we seem committed to some very rash assertion." (1939: 146). Peirce in 1908: "I do not say that it is infallibly true that there is any belief to which a person would come if he were to carry his inquiries far enough. I only say that that alone is what I call Truth" (cited Haack 1976: 246). "Lost facts" objection: the number of cakes on a tray at a long-ago party. [source: SEP-P §3.1]
- Peirce did not reject correspondence outright: he called it merely a "nominal" or "transcendental" definition (CP 5.553, 5.572). [source: SEP-T §1.3]
- James, *Pragmatism* (1907; Harvard 1975 pages): "The true is the name of whatever proves itself to be good in the way of belief, and good, too, for definite assignable reasons" (42); "'The true', to put it very briefly, is only the expedient in the way of our thinking, just as 'the right' is only the expedient in the way of our behaving. Expedient in almost any fashion; and expedient in the long run and on the whole, of course" (106). NB the brief's paraphrase "the expedient in our way of thinking" is wrong: the text says "in the way of our thinking". [source: SEP-P §3.2]
- Russell's criticisms of James: "Pragmatism" (*Edinburgh Review*, April 1909) and "William James's Conception of Truth", both in *Philosophical Essays* (1910). Three classic objections: (1) "when we say that a belief is true, the thought we wish to convey is not the same thought as when we say that the belief furthers our purposes; thus 'true' does not mean 'furthering our purposes'" (Russell 1910 [1994: 98]); useful falsehoods and useless truths; (2) as a mere criterion it presupposes an account of what truth is; (3) "it is so often harder to determine whether a belief is useful than whether it is true" (1910 [1994: 121]). Also Moore (1908) and Lovejoy (1908). Russell's *History of Western Philosophy* (1946: 772): James is committed to the truth of "Santa Claus exists"; SEP-P calls this "unfair". Reply (Misak's "pragmatic elucidation"): pragmatists need not offer a definition but explore truth's connections with practice. [source: SEP-Prag §5.1; SEP-P §3.2]
- Dewey: *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry* (1938) speaks of "warranted assertibility" as the goal of inquiry in place of "truth" and "knowledge"; the 527-page book's index has one entry for "truth" plus "See also assertibility". 1941 reply to Russell, "Propositions, Warranted Assertibility, and Truth": correspondence "in the operational sense… of *answering*, as a key answers to conditions imposed by a lock" (1941: 178). Quine: Dewey's strategy was "simply to avoid the truth predicate and limp along with warranted belief". [source: SEP-Prag §1.3]
- Later heirs: Putnam's "internal realism" (1981) glosses truth as justification under ideal epistemic conditions; Apel and Habermas (ideal speech situation); Wright's superassertibility; Dummett's anti-realism (truth as verifiability/warranted assertibility; denies bivalence; e.g. uranium in a region of the universe too distant ever to inspect). [source: SEP-T §§4.2–4.3; IEP-Prag; SEP-Prag]
- Rorty (4 Oct 1931 – 8 Jun 2007; *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* 1979): IEP-Prag: "According to Rorty, truth has no nature or essence; hence the less said about it, the better"; calling a belief true is to perform a speech act (recommend, caution). Rorty: "there is nothing to be said about either truth or rationality apart from descriptions of the familiar procedures of justification which a given society—ours—uses in one or another area of inquiry" (1991: 23); knowledge and truth are compliments "paid to beliefs which we think so well justified that, for the moment, further justification is not needed" (1991: 24). He rejected the label "relativist" for "ethnocentrism"; Putnam (1999) and Boghossian (2006a) see no real difference. [source: IEP-Prag §2e; SEP-Rel §4.4.1; WP-Richard_Rorty; WP-Philosophy_and_the_Mirror_of_Nature]

## 6. Tarski and the Liar as a constraint (cross-ref *Paradoxes*; do not repeat)

- Tarski: born Alfred Teitelbaum, 14 Jan 1901 – 26 Oct 1983 (died in Berkeley); at UC Berkeley 1942–1983. [source: WP-Alfred_Tarski]
- 1933 Polish monograph; German "Der Wahrheitsbegriff in den formalisierten Sprachen" (1935); English trans. Woodger in *Logic, Semantics, Metamathematics* (1956). Popular paper: "The Semantic Conception of Truth and the Foundations of Semantics", *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 4(3) (1944): 341–376 (IEP-ST's bibliography gives "341–395", an error). [source: IEP-ST §1; SEP-Tar; WS-7]
- Conditions of adequacy: (a) formally correct (no paradox, no circularity); (b) materially adequate (Convention T). Object language vs metalanguage: German as object language, English as metalanguage: "'Schnee ist weiss' in German is true if and only if snow is white." [source: IEP-ST]
- Tarski's theory "lays the ground-work for the modern subject of model theory — a branch of mathematical logic, not the metaphysics of truth"; recursive definition (base clauses plus clauses for "or" and "not" give truth for infinitely many sentences with four clauses). [source: SEP-T §§2, 2.3–2.4]
- Liar as constraint: in a language that can talk about its own semantics, Convention T leads to the Liar; a truth definition must be given "in a metalanguage which is essentially stronger"; Tarski "seems to have thought the Liar shows the ordinary notion of truth to be incoherent". (Already in *Paradoxes*.) [source: SEP-Tar §1.3; Paradoxes notes]
- Tarski's neutrality: "one can accept STT without being committed to strong ontological or epistemological views such as idealism or realism"; Convention T "does not discriminate between realist and anti-realist notions of truth". Is Tarski a correspondence theorist? Woleński: the "prevailing philosophical interpretation" says yes; Tarski "later… expressed greater reservations" about "agreement"/"correspondence". Tarski is not himself a deflationist (Field 1994a, Ketland 1999, Patterson 2012), though deflationists drew on him. Critics: Neurath and Putnam; defenders: Carnap and Popper. [source: IEP-ST; SEP-T §§3.1, 4.2; SEP-Def §2]
- Liar as constraint on deflationism: deflationists must restrict the equivalence schema or change logic. Horwich's earlier response restricts the equivalence schema ("semantic epistemicism"); Field (2008) uses strong Kleene logic with gaps; Armour-Garb and Beall argue deflationists can be dialetheists; Grover (1977) three-valued; gaps sit awkwardly with deflationism (Dummett 1959; Holton 2000). [source: SEP-Def §4.6]
- Davidson's programme: a Tarskian theory of truth as a theory of meaning (Davidson 1967); truth-conditional semantics. [source: SEP-T §6.4; SEP-Def §4.2]

## 7. Deflationism

- General idea: truth "really does not carry metaphysical significance at all"; the equivalence schema is exhaustive; there is no substantive truth property; the concept is useful for generalising. Horwich (1982, p. 182) coined the term "deflationism". [source: SEP-T §5; SEP-Def §§1, 3.1]
- Frege, "Der Gedanke" (1918–19; "The Thought"/"Thoughts", trans. Geach and Stoothoff): "It is … worthy of notice that the sentence 'I smell the scent of violets' has just the same content as the sentence 'It is true that I smell the scent of violets'. So it seems, then, that nothing is added to the thought by my ascribing to it the property of truth." (Frege 1918, 6, as quoted in SEP-Def; IEP-T gives a slightly different wording without "just"). Dummett (1959) traced deflationism to this passage; Soames notes Frege distinguishes opaque ascriptions ("My conjecture is true"). Frege also argued that correspondence must collapse into identity (Frege 1918–19, p. 60) and has a statement of the identity theory in "The Thought" (1918–19: 74). [source: SEP-Def §2; IEP-T §7a; SEP-Cor §6; SEP-Id §2; SEP-T bibliography]
- Ramsey (Frank P. Ramsey, 1903–1930), "Facts and Propositions", *Proc. Aristotelian Society* Supp. Vol. 7 (1927): 153–170, pp. 38–9 of reprint: "'It is true that Caesar was murdered' means no more than that Caesar was murdered, and 'It is false that Caesar was murdered' means no more than Caesar was not murdered"; for described propositions ("He is always right") he proposes "For all p, if he asserts p, p is true", where "p is true" is simply the same as p. Ramsey (1929) made "probably the earliest use of the term 'pro-sentence'". "Redundancy theory" is the usual label. Glanzberg (2003b) doubts Ramsey is really a deflationist. [source: SEP-Def §2; SEP-T §5.1; WP-Frank_Ramsey disambiguation]
- Ayer (1935, 28): "the words 'true' and 'false' are not used to stand for anything, but function in the sentence merely as assertion and negation signs… Consequently, there can be no logical problem concerning the nature of truth." Later Wittgenstein (PI §136): "p is true = p". Strawson (1949, 1950): performative view — "That is true" confirms or endorses; objection (Geach; Huw Price on "'Please shut the door' is true"). [source: SEP-Def §2; IEP-T §7b]
- Quine's disquotationalism (*Philosophy of Logic*, 1970 [1986, 12]): "By calling the sentence true, we call snow white. The truth predicate is a device of disquotation." Generalising "by semantic ascent". Leeds (1978): truth expresses infinite conjunctions/disjunctions. Later Field (1994), Beall (2005). [source: SEP-Def §2; SEP-T §5.3]
- Horwich's minimalism: *Truth* (Oxford: Blackwell, 1990; 2nd edn 1998). Axioms = the instances of "It is true that p if and only if p" (for propositions); truth is a merely "logical property"; the concept "exists solely for the sake of a certain logical need". Horwich born 1947, British, at NYU. [source: SEP-Def §3.1; SEP-T §5.2; WP-Paul_Horwich]
- Prosententialism: Dorothy Grover, Joseph Camp, Nuel Belnap, "A Prosentential Theory of Truth", *Philosophical Studies* 27(2) (1975): 73–125. "That is true" works like a pronoun at sentence level ("lazy" anaphora when it simply repeats a previous assertion; also quantificational, "Everything Smith says is true" = "Everything is such that, if Smith says that it is true, then it is true"); objection: "paraphrastic gymnastics". Grover: 9 Sep 1936 – 10 Nov 2017, New Zealand philosopher of logic at University of Illinois at Chicago. [source: SEP-Def §2; WP-Dorothy_Grover]
- ARGUMENT (the generalisation function — the deflationist's positive case; recommended reconstruction): (1) Suppose Jones thinks Smith infallible. We can say "If Smith says that birds are dinosaurs, then birds are dinosaurs", and likewise for each claim. (2) To state Jones's belief in general we need "For all p, if Smith says that p, then p" — but in English we cannot quantify into sentence position ("p" in the consequent is used, not mentioned). (3) The truth predicate lets us nominalise: "For all x, if Smith said x, then x is true" = "Everything Smith says is true". (4) So "true" is a device for expressing otherwise inexpressible (potentially infinite) generalisations — its "raison d'être" (Field 1994a; Horwich 1998a). Classic examples: "Everything the Pope says is true" (Papal infallibility; Beall, Field), "The next thing that Bill says will be true" (blind ascription; Strawson 1950), Ramsey's "He is always right". OBJECTION ("Generalization Problem": Gupta 1993; Field; Horwich; Halbach; Soames): the instances of the schema, even taken as theorems, cannot prove "All sentences of the form 'If p, then p' are true" (Tarski noted the set is ω-incomplete), so deflationism cannot account for general truths about truth. [source: SEP-Def §§1.3, 4.7; SEP-T §5.3; IEP-T §7a]
- Objection from truth and meaning: deflationists cannot hold a truth-conditional theory of meaning (else "p is true iff a ∈ p" gives a substantive truth notion); thoroughgoing deflationists need a non-truth-conditional account of content (Horwich's use theory; Field's conceptual role; Brandom's inferentialism). [source: SEP-T §6.3; SEP-Def §4.2]
- ARGUMENT (normativity: Dummett 1959 and Wright 1992 — main objection, with reply): Dummett: the T-biconditionals leave out "the point of the concept of truth, or what the concept is used for" — that truth is the aim of assertion. Wright (1992: 15–23): (1) Truth and warranted assertibility are both norms of assertion. (2) Lacking warrant to assert p does not give warrant to assert not-p. (3) But by the equivalence schema, "p is not true" yields "not-p", and "not-p" yields "'not-p' is true". (4) So "not true" behaves differently from "not warranted"; truth is a distinct norm, potentially extensionally divergent from warranted assertibility. (5) So by its own lights deflationism makes truth a substantial norm — deflationism is "inherently unstable". REPLIES: Field (1994a) and Horwich (1996): the truth predicate in stating the norm "Truth is a norm of assertion" (= "for all x, if someone asserts x and x is not true, the assertion is open to criticism") is just playing its generalising role; Rumfitt (1995): add denial and "anti-warrant" and "is not true" becomes a device of rejection. [source: SEP-Def §4.4; SEP-T §6.6]
- Disagreement among sympathisers about whether Tarski biconditionals are logical truths: Priest (2007), Zalta (2014) yes; Cook (2012) no; Beall (2021) "emergent logical property". (Specialist — optional.) [source: SEP-T §5.4]

## 8. Truth pluralism and the identity theory

- Crispin Wright (b. 21 Dec 1942), *Truth and Objectivity* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard UP, 1992): minimalism about the concept (any predicate meeting the "platitudes" is a "lightweight" truth predicate) compatible with truth consisting in different things in different domains: correspondence-like in some, superassertibility (assertibility that would survive arbitrarily close scrutiny) in others. [source: SEP-T §4.4; SEP-Plu §3.1.1; WP-Crispin_Wright; SEP-Prag]
- Michael P. Lynch (b. 1966), *Truth as One and Many* (Oxford: OUP, 2009): truth as a functional role concept, multiply realised — by correspondence for ordinary claims about material objects, by an assertibility property for moral claims. [source: SEP-T §4.4; SEP-Plu bibliography; WP-Michael_P._Lynch]
- ARGUMENT (the scope problem — motivation): (1) Monist inflationary theories say truth is one property F in every domain. (2) Each candidate F is plausible in some domains but not others: correspondence fits "ladders, ladles, and other ordinary objects" but not "comedy, fashion, ethical mores, numbers"; coherence fits legal, comic or mathematical truths but not empirical ones. (3) So no single F explains truth everywhere. (4) So truth consists in several properties F1…Fn (analogy: water includes H2O, D2O, HDO). Name: "the scope problem" (Lynch 2004b, 2009; cf. Sher 1998). [source: SEP-Plu §2]
- OBJECTION (mixed inferences; Tappolet 1997, 2000; Pedersen 2006): "Satiated dogs are lazy. Our dog is satiated. So our dog is lazy" is valid, i.e. truth-preserving — but which truth property is preserved if the premises are true in different ways? Also mixed compounds ("Killing innocent people is wrong and 7 + 5 = 12") and mixed atomic sentences ("Causing pain is bad"). REPLY (Beall 2000): validity as preservation of designated value, the Fi being the designated values. COUNTER (Tappolet 2000): "being designated" is then a universal truth property — the "instability challenge" (Pedersen 2006, 2010). [source: SEP-Plu §§4.4–4.5]
- Identity theory: true propositions (or "thinkables") are identical with facts, not made true by them. Term first used by Stewart Candlish (1989, article on Bradley). Moore 1899/1902, Russell c. 1903–04, Frege 1918–19; recent defenders McDowell (true "thinkables" are facts, 1996: 27–8) and Hornsby (1997). Problems listed by Gaskin: modal problem, "right fact" problem, slingshot, congruence, individuation. Point to make: Wittgenstein's "My fundamental idea…" aside, McDowell presents the identity as "truistic": what one thinks, when one thinks truly, is what is the case. [source: SEP-Id §§1–2, §5 headings; SEP-T §1.1.1]

## 9. Relativism about truth

- Protagoras' fragment (80B1 DK) as given in SEP-Prot: "Of all things the measure is man: of those that are, that they are; and of those that are not, that they are not" — first sentence of the treatise *Truth* (*Alētheia*). (*Socrates and the Sophists* quotes a different but compatible rendering.) [source: SEP-Prot §2.1]
- Peritropē ("turning oneself upside down"; SEP-Th: "table-turning"), apparently first formulated by Democritus (80A15 DK), developed by Plato (*Theaetetus* 171a–c; *Euthydemus*) and Aristotle (*Metaphysics* IV). SEP-Rel's step-by-step version: (1) Most people believe Protagoras' doctrine is false. (2) Protagoras believes it true. (3) By his own doctrine he must grant that his opponents' view is true. (4) So he must believe his own doctrine false. Validity "much disputed": Burnyeat, Denyer and Sedley make it valid; McDowell and Bostock: it proves self-defeat rather than self-contradiction. Reply: restore "for me"/"for you" (*Theaetetus* 161d2–3); Burnyeat (1976a: 44) says Plato's version damages Protagoras only if he is committed to the absolute truth of relativism. Price of the reply: solipsism, no possible contradiction ("it is not possible to contradict", 80A19 DK). [source: SEP-Prot §2.2; SEP-Th §6.3; SEP-Rel §4.3.1] — ALREADY reconstructed in *Socrates and the Sophists*: cite, do not repeat.
- NEW ARGUMENT for this chapter (Siegel's dilemma for "true for", recommended): (1) The relativist says "p is true for S". (2) Either this means just "S believes p" — then relativism is a trivial claim about beliefs, not about truth; or it means "p is true" simpliciter — then it is absolutism. (3) A notion "stronger than 'S believes that p'… but weaker than 'p is true (simpliciter)'" "has proved to be quite difficult" to construct, "arguably beyond the conceptual resources available to the relativist" (Siegel 2011: 203). Further: Mackie (1964: 200): "There are no absolute truths" is absolutely self-refuting; relativist argument is "pragmatically self-refuting" (why argue if persuasion presupposes shared truth?). REPLIES: Hales (1997): drop "everything is relative" for "everything that is true is relatively true", with perspective operators, giving a consistent relativist logic (criticised by Shogenji 1997); a "framework" version: p is true for X iff X would believe p if reasoning cogently by her own standards with full information. Evans (1985: 349) on assertion needing a shared aim; Davidson against conceptual schemes. [source: SEP-Rel §4.3.1]
- Alethic relativism: "is true" is really two-place, "P is true for X" (X a culture, scheme, framework); e.g. "misfortune is caused by witchcraft" true in the Azande framework and false in the Western scientific one. [source: SEP-Rel §4.3]
- New Relativism (MacFarlane): John MacFarlane, *Assessment Sensitivity: Relative Truth and its Applications* (Oxford: OUP, 2014). Truth of an utterance depends in part on a "context of assessment", not only the context of use. Worked example: A: "Pretzels are tasty"; B: "Pretzels are not tasty". Contextualist: A means tasty-by-my-standards, so no real disagreement. New relativist: one proposition is affirmed and denied (genuine disagreement), but it is true relative to A's standards when A assesses and false relative to B's — "faultless disagreement" (Kölbel). Other proponents: Kölbel (2003, 2004), Lasersohn (2005), Wright (2006). Applications: taste, epistemic modals, future contingents, knowledge ascriptions. Relativism is LOCAL (a domain), not global. [source: SEP-Rel §5, §5.1, bibliography]
- Rorty's ethnocentrism (above §5) as a relativist-adjacent position. [source: SEP-Rel §4.4.1]
- IEP-T (Dowden): "Truth is not relative in the sense that something can be true for you but not for me"; "That is true for me but not for you" sensibly means "I believe it, but you do not". (Present as one author's view.) [source: IEP-T §5]

## 10. Truth in other traditions

### India: Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā (cross-ref *Classical Indian Schools*; do not repeat Kumārila's regress argument)
- Knowledge is cognition produced in the right way; pramā ("knowledge") and pramāṇa ("knowledge source") are factive, "like 'knowledge' in English": "no knowledge source ever generates a false belief"; a non-veridical perception is a "pseudo-perception" (*pratyakṣa-ābhāsa*). SEP-IndEp: "There are different theories of truth, but everyone sees knowledge as not only revealing the truth but arising from it." Realists (Mīmāṃsā, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika) stress right origins; Yogācāra idealists and pragmatists stress that knowledge "guides successful action". [source: SEP-IndEp §1.1]
- Nyāya on what truth is (correspondence-like): cognition targets objects via "objecthood" (*viṣayatā*) with a threefold structure (object, property, relation); "In cases of veridical cognition (*pramā*), the portion of cognition which targets a substantive and the portion which targets its property match up." Gaṅgeśa (*Tattvacintāmaṇi*, *pramā-lakṣaṇa-vāda*) "famously defines veridical cognition as 'a cognitive state with predication content x about something in fact qualified by x'". Error: the property targeted "does not actually qualify the substantive". Dasti: Nyāya's theory is "not exactly representational, but relational". [source: IEP-Nya §f.iii]
- Test vs definition: "In his opening comments on the *Nyāya-sūtra*, Vātsyāyana famously provides a pragmatic test (but not definition) of truth: cognitions which guide us to successful action are likely veridical." Doubt triggers examination ("Where there is doubt, there must be ongoing examination", NS 2.1.7). [source: IEP-Nya §f.ii] (Good parallel with the correspondence/criterion distinction and with the pragmatists.)
- Certification (*prāmāṇya*): Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta: intrinsic (*svataḥ prāmāṇya*) — Prābhākara: no cognition purporting to be veridical is wholly wrong, at worst a confusion; Bhāṭṭa (Kumārila): veridicality known via "cognizedness". Nyāya: extrinsic (*parataḥ prāmāṇya*), denies that Kp entails KKp; certification by "apperception" (*anuvyavasāya*); Vātsyāyana (4th c.) rebuts Nāgārjuna's regress charge. [source: SEP-IndEp §1.4 area]
- Debate: Potter (1984) argues classical Indian philosophy does not concern JTB, partly because "in classical Indian philosophy truth is more often understood on a pragmatist or 'workability' model" rather than correspondence (contrast Dasti's relational/matching account and Gaṅgeśa's definition). [source: SEP-IndEp §9.1 area]

### Buddhist two truths (cross-ref *Mahāyāna Philosophy*, which quotes MMK 24.8 and 24.10)
- MMK 24.8 (trans. Sonam Thakchoe): "the Dharma taught by the buddhas is precisely based on the two truths: a truth of mundane conventions and a truth of the ultimate"; 24.9: those who do not understand the distinction do not understand the teaching; 24.10 (trans. Dan Arnold): "without relying on convention, the ultimate is not taught; without having understood the ultimate, nirvāṇa is not apprehended". Terms: conventional *saṃvṛti-satya* (also *vyavahāra-satya*, "transactional"), ultimate *paramārtha-satya*. [source: SEP-2T §§1, 3; IEP-Madh; SEP-Madh §4]
- Thakchoe: Nāgārjuna's theory is "fundamentally different from all theories of truth in other Indian philosophies": the Hindu schools are foundationalist about an ultimate "substantive reality"; on Madhyamaka "all things including ultimate truth are ultimately unreal, empty (*śūnya*)… including the emptiness (*śūnyatā*) itself"; conventional truth tied to dependent arising, ultimate to emptiness, "co-constitutive". [source: SEP-2T §3]
- DEBATE on what "ultimate truth" is: (a) Hayes: *paramārtha* literally "highest goal" (nirvana); the ultimate is a non-conceptual silence, "naturally inexpressible in language"; only transactional truth can be articulated (Bhāvaviveka; Candrakīrti: conventional truth as a "screen"). (b) Westerhoff's semantic reading: "there can be no such thing as ultimate truth, a theory describing how things really are, independent of our interests and conceptual resources"; "All one is left with is conventional truth, truth which consists in agreement with commonly accepted practices and conventions"; even emptiness "is not to be regarded as an ultimately true theory". Westerhoff compares this with anti-realism/warranted assertibility and discusses the objection from scientific progress (reply: treating truths as if non-conventional may be pragmatically useful — "convenient fictions"). [source: SEP-Madh §§4–5; SEP-Nag §6 area] (A natural bridge from Dummett/pragmatism to Madhyamaka; present as Westerhoff's interpretation.)
- Svātantrika: Jñānagarbha's *Commentary on the Distinction Between the Two Truths* (8th c.); "true" vs "false" convention. [source: SEP-Madh; Mahāyāna notes]

### Jain anekāntavāda and syādvāda (cross-ref *Jains, Ājīvikas and Materialists*, which already gives the sevenfold scheme and Webb's self-defeat objection)
- IEP-Jain: reality is "multifaceted (*anekanta*, or 'non-one-sided'), such that no one view can capture it in its entirety; that is, no single statement or set of statements captures the complete truth about the objects they describe"; blind men and elephant; *naya* = partial predication / perspectives: "any judgment is true only from the viewpoint or perspective of the judge, and ought to be so expressed"; the seven predications each prefixed by *syāt* ("perhaps", "from a perspective", "somehow"); they "are not to be understood as seven truth-values, since they are all seven thought to be true". Śaṅkara's inconsistency charge; self-defeat: "What begins as a laudable fallibilism ends as an untenable relativism." [source: IEP-Jain §2]
- Gorisse: Jains "are realist philosophers who consider that human beings know a world that exists in its particularity independently of their knowledge of it"; perspectives arise "from the fact that the known object itself has many aspects". Samantabhadra, *Āptamīmāṃsā* 14: an entity is "somehow" being, non-being, both, indescribable — "in accordance with the speaker's intention and not in an absolute fashion". So: perspectivism grounded in a many-sided reality, not relativism about truth (contrast with Protagoras and MacFarlane). [source: SEP-Jain §§3.4.2–3.4.4]

### China: did classical Chinese thought have a concept of truth?
- Chad Hansen, "Chinese Language, Chinese Philosophy, and 'Truth'", *Journal of Asian Studies* 44(3) (1985): 491–519; *A Daoist Theory of Chinese Thought* (Oxford, 1992). Hansen proposes "we should not treat the Mohists (or other early Chinese theorists) as addressing the semantic issue of whether a claim is true, but the pragmatic or normative one of whether a certain way of using words is appropriate"; we should avoid reading debates on distinguishing *shì* (是 "this", right) from *fēi* (非 "not", wrong) as about truth. Hansen 1983 (*Language and Logic in Ancient China*): early Chinese philosophers were not concerned with theories of truth but with the "pragmatic, behavior-guiding functions of language". [source: SEP-Mo §4; SEP-ChLL §3]
- Critics: A. C. Graham (1989) and Christoph Harbsmeier (1998). Fraser: "Construed as the claim that the Mohists are wholly unconcerned with or lack a concept corresponding to semantic truth, Hansen's thesis is unconvincing, for classical Chinese notions such as 'this' (*shi*), 'fitting' (*dang*), and especially 'so' (*ran*) play conceptual roles that overlap enough with that of semantic truth"; the Mohists recognise a claim satisfying their standards "may yet be mistaken in the sense of falling short of the 'facts' or 'reality' (*qing*)". Fraser 2020a, "Truth in Pre-Han Thought", in *Dao Companion to Chinese Philosophy of Logic*, ed. Yiu-ming Fung (Springer), 113–127. Bo Mou (2016) on Mohist criteria (dual-reference account). [source: SEP-Mo §4 and bibliography; SEP-ChLL §3]
- Worked example (Mohist ghosts): the Mohists' "three standards" (*fa*): a statement must have a "root" (the deeds of the ancient sage-kings), a "source" (checked against "what people see and hear") and a "use" (adopted in government, it must produce benefit, *lì*, for state, clan and people); in Book 31 ("Elucidating Ghosts") Mozi argues that even if there are no ghosts we should behave as if there are, for the social benefit. Fraser: the Mohists apply "a very basic, primitive conception of correctness, of which truth, obligation, permissibility, and other notions are species"; their focus is the proper *dao*, so they "run together the empirical question of whether ghosts exist with the normative question of whether we should act on and promulgate the teaching that they do" — partly supporting Hansen. [source: SEP-Mo §4]
- Later Mohist *kě* (可) is often translated "admissible" rather than "true" because correct use was tied to proper social conduct. [source: SEP-ChLL §3.2]

### Islamic thought (brief)
- *Ḥaqq* (حقّ) is the Arabic word for "truth", also "right" and "reality"; *al-Ḥaqq*, "the Truth", is one of the names of God in the Qur'an. [source: WP-Al-Haqq (stub, "more citations needed" — use only for the gloss)]
- Avicenna: "Further early correspondence formulations can be found in Avicenna (*Metaphysica*, 1.8–9) and Averroes (*Tahafut*, 103, 302)", introduced to the Latin scholastics by William of Auxerre. [source: SEP-Cor §1] In his metaphysics "that which exists in itself is real or true (*al-ḥaqq*), it necessarily exists"; *ḥaqīqa* = reality/true nature of a thing; Lizzini 2023 is "a Commentary on *Ilāhiyyāt* I,8" on Avicenna's concept of truth. Avicenna's characteristic contrast between "the widespread view" (*al-mašhūr*) and "the truth" (*al-ḥaqq*). [source: SEP-ISMeta §§3.4, 4; SEP-ISLog §1] So: Avicenna treats truth both as a property of statements that correspond (Ilāhiyyāt I.8) and as a property of being (God as al-Ḥaqq, the Necessary Existent) — parallel to Aquinas's statement-truth and thing-truth. ## Debates
- **Is Aristotle's dictum a correspondence theory or a precursor of deflationism?** David: "muted, relatively minimal"; "claimed as a precursor of deflationary theories"; Tarski and Woleński read it as the classical (correspondence) conception. [SEP-Cor §1; IEP-ST]
- **Is Tarski's theory a correspondence theory?** Davidson (1969) and Field (1972): core of a correspondence theory without facts; Field: needs a theory of reference; Putnam: fails utterly as philosophy; Woleński: "prevailing" reading says correspondence, but Tarski himself was reserved; it is compatible with anti-realist notions. [SEP-T §§3.1, 4.2; IEP-ST]
- **Bradley: coherence or identity theorist?** Young/Walker vs Candlish/Baldwin. [SEP-Coh §2.1; SEP-T §1.2; SEP-Id §2]
- **Tractatus: correspondence or identity?** Usually correspondence; Gaskin (SEP-Id) questions this. [SEP-Id §2; SEP-Cor §7.1]
- **Was James's theory as crude as Russell said?** Russell/Moore/Lovejoy vs SEP-P ("unfair"), Misak, Ayer, Chisholm. [SEP-Prag §5.1; SEP-P §3.2]
- **Is deflationism stable?** Wright's normativity argument vs Field, Horwich, Rumfitt; generalisation problem (Gupta). [SEP-Def §§4.4, 4.7]
- **Pluralism**: scope problem vs mixed inferences/instability (Tappolet, Pedersen; Beall's designated-values reply). [SEP-Plu]
- **Are there negative facts?** Russell (yes, reluctantly), Armstrong (totality facts instead), Beall/Cameron/Jago (yes). [SEP-T §3.4; SEP-Cor §7.1]
- **Does the peritropē work?** Burnyeat/Denyer/Sedley vs McDowell/Bostock; Burnyeat: it begs the question unless Protagoras claims absolute truth. [SEP-Th; SEP-Rel]
- **Global vs local relativism**: Siegel/Mackie/Boghossian against; Hales's relativist logic; MacFarlane's local assessment-sensitivity. [SEP-Rel]
- **Did early China have a concept of truth?** Hansen (1983, 1985, 1992) vs Graham (1989), Harbsmeier (1998), Fraser (2020a), Mou (2016). [SEP-Mo §4; SEP-ChLL]
- **What is ultimate truth in Madhyamaka?** Ineffable highest goal (Hayes; Bhāvaviveka; Candrakīrti) vs "no ultimate truth", only conventional (Westerhoff's semantic reading); Thakchoe: co-constitutive. [SEP-Madh; SEP-Nag; SEP-2T]
- **Is anekāntavāda relativism?** Webb: ends in "untenable relativism"; Gorisse: realist perspectivism. [IEP-Jain; SEP-Jain]
- **Indian theories of truth: correspondence or "workability"?** Potter (1984) vs the relational/matching account (Gaṅgeśa, Dasti); Vātsyāyana's success-in-action is a test, not a definition. [SEP-IndEp; IEP-Nya]
- **Is "post-truth" a philosophical thesis?** WP-Post-truth: mostly a sociological concern only "remotely related" to theories of truth; some invoke Nietzsche, Foucault, Latour. [WP-Post-truth]
