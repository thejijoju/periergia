## 1. What "consciousness" means: kinds of consciousness
- "Conscious" is applied both to whole organisms (**creature consciousness**) and to particular mental states (**state consciousness**) (Rosenthal 1986; Gennaro 1995; Carruthers 2000). [source: SEP-C §2]
- Senses of **creature consciousness** listed by SEP-C: sentience (capable of sensing and responding; may admit of degrees — "Are fish conscious…? And what of shrimp or bees?"); wakefulness (awake, normally alert — not in deep sleep or coma; dreaming, hypnosis, fugue are borderline); self-consciousness (aware that one is aware); Nagel's "what it is like" criterion; being the subject of conscious states; plus **transitive** consciousness (being conscious *of* something) vs intransitive (Rosenthal 1986). [source: SEP-C §2.1]
- Senses of **state consciousness** (SEP-C lists six): states one is aware of being in (meta-mental; Rosenthal); qualitative states (having qualia, "raw sensory feels"); phenomenal states (the overall structure of experience — spatial, temporal, conceptual — "far more than sensory qualia"); what-it-is-like states; **access consciousness** (Block 1995); narrative consciousness (the "stream of consciousness"; Dennett 1991). [source: SEP-C §2.2]
- Worked examples usable in prose (SEP-C's own): a conscious desire for a cup of coffee = having the desire and being directly aware one has it; perceiving the Merlot one is drinking or the fabric one is examining involves taste or colour qualia. [source: SEP-C §2.2]
- **Block's distinction.** Ned Block, "On a Confusion about a Function of Consciousness", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 18 (1995) (pp. 227–287 incl. commentaries; one reprint cites 227–247 — say only "1995"). "Phenomenal consciousness" = what it is like for a subject to have the experience. Block's access consciousness, as SEP-Neuro summarises it: "A representation is access-conscious if it is poised for free use in reasoning and for direct 'rational' control of action and speech." Block's point: a function of access consciousness is wrongly transferred to phenomenal consciousness. [sources: SEP-Neuro §1.3; SEP-C §2.2; WS-8]
- Ned Block: American philosopher, born 1942. [source: WP-Ned_Block]
- SEP-C's three questions about consciousness: the **Descriptive** (What is it?), the **Explanatory** (How does it come to exist?), the **Functional** (Why does it exist?). Useful chapter frame. [source: SEP-C §3]
- SEP-C notes "conscious" has "no privileged or canonical meaning", but plural concepts may pick out "varying aspects of a single unified underlying mental phenomenon" — an open question. [source: SEP-C §2.3]

## 2. Qualia
- Broad sense (Tye): "the introspectively accessible, phenomenal aspects of our mental lives"; "In this broad sense of the term, it is difficult to deny that there are qualia." Disagreement is about which states have them, whether they are intrinsic, and how they relate to the physical. Tye's examples: seeing bright purple, becoming extremely angry; staring at a bright turquoise colour patch in a paint store vs a dull brown one. [source: SEP-Q intro, §1]
- Singular "quale" (IEP: pronounced "kwol-ay"). C. S. Peirce introduced "quale" into philosophy in 1866 (Tye); C. I. Lewis introduced "qualia" in its contemporary sense in 1929 (*Mind and the World Order*), as properties of sense-data. [sources: SEP-Q §1; IEP-Q §1]
- Narrower senses (Tye): qualia as properties of sense-data; as intrinsic, non-representational, consciously accessible properties of experience (most common recent usage; held e.g. by Nagel 1974, Peacocke 1983, Block 1990); as intrinsic, non-physical, ineffable properties. [source: SEP-Q §1]
- Dennett's target conception: no properties meet "the standard conception of qualia (that is, properties of experience that are intrinsic, ineffable, directly and/or immediately introspectible, and private)". His coffee tasters Chase and Sanborn (Maxwell House coffee): Chase says the taste is the same but he no longer likes it; Sanborn says the taste has changed — "how do they know this?" [source: IEP-Q, eliminativism section] (WP-Illusionism adds the "inexperienced beer drinker" and the neurosurgery inversion cases.)
- "Quining Qualia" date: IEP-Q bibliography gives **1988**, in A. Marcel and E. Bisiach (eds.), *Consciousness in Contemporary Science* (OUP), 43–77. SEP-Q cites a 1990 reprint (Lycan ed.); WP-Illusionism says "1993 paper" (another reprint). **Use 1988** (also what the published *Analytic Philosophy since 1950* chapter says). [sources: IEP-Q bibliography; SEP-Q bibliography; WP-Illusionism]

## 3. Inverted spectrum (brief — cross-ref Locke)
- Locke appears to be the first to formulate an inverted-spectrum case explicitly (an "immediate precursor is Malebranche"), in *Essay* II.xxxii.15, chapter "Of True and False Ideas": "…if the Idea, that a Violet produced in one Man's Mind by his Eyes, were the same that a Marigold produces in another Man's, and vice versâ. For since this could never be known: because one Man's Mind could not pass into another Man's Body, to perceive, what Appearances were produced by those Organs; neither the Ideas hereby, nor the Names, would be at all confounded, or any Falshood be in either." (SEP cites the Nidditch edition, 1689/1975.) [source: SEP-IQ §2.2]
- Locke's own point was that such inversion would make no difference to naming or to "Falshood" — he did not use it against physicalism; the modern popularity is "largely due to the influence of Shoemaker 1982 and Block 1990"; Dennett called it "one of philosophy's most virulent memes" (1991, 389). [source: SEP-IQ intro, §2.2]
- Lycan: Lockean inversion "is regarded as possible by everyone except behaviorists and Wittgensteinians"; to refute representationalism one needs qualia inverted with respect to *all* representational contents — "much more ambitious". [source: SEP-Rep §4.5.3]
- The published *Locke* chapter does NOT mention the inverted spectrum (it covers personal identity/consciousness) — so give the Locke passage here, briefly, and cross-ref *Locke* for his theory of ideas.

## 4. Early versions: Leibniz's mill, Huxley's Djin (cross-ref, don't repeat)

Roger Ariew and Daniel Garber (Hackett, 1989)**: "One is obliged to admit that perception and what depends upon it is inexplicable on mechanical principles, that is, by figures and motions. ## 5. The hard problem and the explanatory gap
- **Name.** Chalmers coined "hard problem" and "easy problems" in a **1994** talk at the Tucson conference "Toward a Science of Consciousness" (WP: "The Science of Consciousness" conference, Tucson, Arizona); published as "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness", *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 2(3) (1995): 200–219; expanded in *The Conscious Mind: In Search of a Fundamental Theory* (OUP, 1996). IEP: "so-named by David Chalmers in 1995". [sources: WP-Hard_problem; IEP-HP intro, §1a]
- **Exact wording (1995, p. 202, emphasis in original), as quoted by IEP-HP:** "What makes the hard problem hard and almost unique is that it goes beyond problems about the performance of functions. To see this, note that even when we have explained the performance of all the cognitive and behavioral functions in the vicinity of experience—perceptual discrimination, categorization, internal access, verbal report—there may still remain a further unanswered question: *Why is the performance of these functions accompanied by experience?*" [source: IEP-HP §1a; same in WP-Hard_problem]
- **Easy problems** (WP's paraphrase of Chalmers): explaining how physical systems give rise to the ability to discriminate, to integrate information, to report, etc.; amenable to explanation in terms of "structures and functions". IEP: the easy problems are "explaining the function, dynamics, and structure of consciousness". Chalmers on GWT (1995): it may explain global accessibility, but "why should global accessibility give rise to conscious experience? As always, this bridging question is unanswered." [sources: WP-Hard_problem; IEP-HP intro]
- **Worked example (WP):** stubbing a toe and yelping — the easy problems concern nerve signals, processing, the yelp; the hard problem is why this is accompanied by the *feeling of pain*, and why it feels this way rather than another (hunger rather than thirst). [source: WP-Hard_problem]
- **The reductive-explanation schema (IEP, following Chalmers and Lewis 1972)**: The gene = the unit of hereditary transmission (by analysis); regions of DNA = the unit of hereditary transmission (by empirical investigation); therefore the gene = regions of DNA (transitivity). Chalmers: works for everything else but not consciousness, because consciousness "cannot be functionally analyzed", as shown by the continued conceivability of zombies, spectrum inversion, the other-minds problem, and the knowledge argument. Result: "either eliminate consciousness … or add consciousness to our ontology as an unreduced feature of reality, on par with gravity and electromagnetism." **Good basis for a step-by-step argument (see Arguments, A2).** [source: IEP-HP §1a]
- Chalmers is a naturalist though not a physicalist: "naturalistic dualism" — consciousness a fundamental feature of the natural world. [sources: IEP-Q §6; WP-Hard_problem citing Chalmers 1995 pp. 209–10]
- **Explanatory gap.** Joseph Levine, "Materialism and Qualia: The Explanatory Gap", *Pacific Philosophical Quarterly* 64 (1983): 354–361; example "Pain is the firing of C fibers". IEP: a good explanation should deductively entail what it explains (lightning example: given a large electrical discharge at t, we can deduce lightning at t); for consciousness, "No matter how detailed our specification … there is an open question about whether consciousness is present." Levine himself "has been reluctant to draw any anti-physicalist ontological conclusions" (SEP-C). Levine (b. 17 Jan 1952; UMass Amherst); book *Purple Haze: The Puzzle of Consciousness* (OUP 2001). [sources: IEP-HP §1c; SEP-C §5.2; WS-8; WP-Joseph_Levine]
- SEP-C distinguishes strengths of the gap claim: practical (present limits), in-principle for humans (McGinn), in-principle for any cognizer; "The stronger one's epistemological premise, the better the hope of deriving a metaphysical conclusion" — but the stronger, the harder to assume without begging the question. [source: SEP-C §5.2]
- Chalmers: Australian philosopher, born 20 April 1966 (per the *Possibility and Necessity* notes); UC Santa Cruz professor 1995–98; University of Arizona 1999–2004. [source: WP-David_Chalmers]
- **Chalmers–Koch bet**: in 1998 Christof Koch bet Chalmers a case of wine that the neural underpinnings of consciousness would be understood by 2023; in 2023 Koch conceded and gave Chalmers the wine. [sources: WP-Christof_Koch; WP-David_Chalmers]
- **Meta-problem** (Chalmers 2018, *JCS* 25(9–10): 6–61): "the problem of explaining why we think that there is a [hard] problem of consciousness" — strictly an easy problem but "deeply connected to the hard problem". [source: WP-Hard_problem]
- **Surveys** (2020 PhilPapers survey, per WP): 62.42% of philosophers agreed the hard problem is real, 29.76% disagreed; 51.93% accept or lean towards physicalism; 4.51% eliminativism. Use only with "according to the 2020 PhilPapers survey". [source: WP-Hard_problem]
- **Chalmers's taxonomy** (from "Consciousness and its Place in Nature", via WP): Type-A materialism (deny the hard problem; Dennett, the Churchlands, Frankish); **Type-B** (a posteriori physicalism: accept the epistemic gap, deny an ontological one — the gap lies in how we conceptualise); Type-C (the gap will close with future science); Type-D (interactionist dualism) and Type-E (epiphenomenalism); **Type-F** (panpsychism / Russellian monism). [source: WP-Hard_problem §§ on types]
- Critics who reject the problem: Dennett ("will be solved in the process of solving" the easy problems; stage magic; change blindness); Dehaene (*Consciousness and the Brain*, 2014: the "easy problems" are the hard ones; the hard problem rests on "ill-defined intuitions"); Peter Hacker ("The hard problem isn't a hard problem at all"). [source: WP-Hard_problem]

## 6. Nagel's bat
- Thomas Nagel (b. 4 July 1937), "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?", *The Philosophical Review* 83(4) (October 1974): 435–450; reprinted in *Mortal Questions* (1979). [sources: WP-What_Is_It_Like; WP-Thomas_Nagel]
- **Exact sentence:** "an organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something that it is like to *be* that organism—something it is like *for* the organism." (italics as in WP's quotation) [sources: WP-What_Is_It_Like; WP-Animal_consciousness] — **already quoted (first half) in *Analytic Philosophy since 1950*; repeat only once, as the definition of phenomenal consciousness.**
- Opening line: "Consciousness is what makes the mind-body problem really intractable" (IEP: "the mind-body problem really intractable" (Nagel 1974, 435)). Also (p. 436): "Without consciousness the mind-body problem would be much less interesting. With consciousness it seems hopeless." [sources: IEP-HP §1b (435); WS-2 for the "hopeless" lines — secondary only, but several copies agree]
- Nagel: physical facts are "objective facts par excellence – the kind that can be observed and understood from many points of view and by individuals with differing perceptual systems" (1974, 442); even knowing all objective facts about bat sonar we would not know what it is like to perceive by it. "Nagel does not argue against physicalism, but rather claims that we presently have no understanding of how it can be true." [source: SEP-KA §1]
- Nagel calls for a future "objective phenomenology" that would "describe, at least in part, the subjective character of experiences in a form comprehensible to beings incapable of having those experiences" (1974, 449). [source: IEP-HP §3d]
- Nagel assumes "conscious experience is a widespread phenomenon", present in many animals; bats chosen as mammals with echolocation, a sense very unlike ours. [source: WP-What_Is_It_Like]
- Criticisms: Dennett (1991) — the "interesting or theoretically important" features of bat experience are open to third-person study; Dennett also notes B. A. Farrell's 1950 *Mind* article "Experience" raised the question earlier; Kathleen Akins (1993) — much bat neuroscience bears on Nagel's question; Peter Hacker (2002, *Philosophy* 77) — the formula is "malconstructed"; Schwitzgebel & Gordon (2000) — sighted humans echolocate unawares. [source: WP-What_Is_It_Like]

## 7. Jackson's Mary (knowledge argument) — the deeper replies only
- Frank Cameron Jackson (b. 31 Aug 1943), Australian philosopher, ANU. "Epiphenomenal Qualia", *Philosophical Quarterly* 32 (1982): 127–136; "What Mary Didn't Know", *Journal of Philosophy* 83 (1986): 291–295. [sources: WP-Frank_Cameron_Jackson; SEP-KA bibliography]
- **Exact setup (1982), as quoted by SEP-KA:** "Mary is a brilliant scientist who is, for whatever reason, forced to investigate the world from a black and white room via a black and white television monitor. She specializes in the neurophysiology of vision and acquires, let us suppose, all the physical information there is to obtain about what goes on when we see ripe tomatoes, or the sky, and use terms like 'red', 'blue', and so on. She discovers, for example, just which wavelength combinations from the sky stimulate the retina, and exactly how this produces via the central nervous system the contraction of the vocal chords and expulsion of air from the lungs that results in the uttering of the sentence 'The sky is blue'.… What will happen when Mary is released from her black and white room or is given a color television monitor? Will she learn anything or not? It seems just obvious that she will learn something about the world and our visual experience of it. But then is it inescapable that her previous knowledge was incomplete. But she had all the physical information. Ergo there is more to have than that, and Physicalism is false." [source: SEP-KA §2] (Spelling: SEP uses US "specializes/color"; WP uses "specialises/colour".)
- Jackson's other case, **Fred**, who sees a colour unknown to normal perceivers. [source: SEP-KA §2]
- SEP-KA's two versions: weaker (V1: knowledge — conclusion merely epistemic) vs stronger (V2: facts — "There are non-physical facts concerning human color vision"); Jackson meant the stronger. [source: SEP-KA §3.1] **The five-step SEP reconstruction, Dennett's blue-banana objection, the Lewis–Nemirow ability hypothesis with Lewis's "It isn't knowing-that. It's knowing-how", and "new knowledge, old fact" are ALREADY SET OUT in *Analytic Philosophy since 1950*. Do not repeat; recap in a sentence and go deeper with the following.**
- Real-life near-Mary: the psychologist Knut Nordby, a colour-vision specialist who was a complete achromat. Survey: 82% of non-philosopher respondents said Mary in the room would not know what it is like to see red (Gregory, Hendrickx & Turner 2022). [source: SEP-KA §4.1]
- **Ability hypothesis** (detail not in the published chapter): Nemirow — "knowing what an experience is like is the same as knowing how to imagine having the experience" (1990, 495); Lewis — "knowing what it is like is the possession of abilities: abilities to recognize, abilities to imagine, abilities to predict one's behavior by imaginative experiments" (1983, 131). **Objection (Conee 1994):** "Martha", "highly skilled at visualizing an intermediate shade", has never seen cherry red; told it lies midway between burgundy red and fire red, she has the *ability* to imagine it but, until she uses it, does not know what it is like — so abilities are not sufficient; and a person with no capacity to imagine colours still knows what green is like while staring at it — so not necessary (Conee; Alter 1998). Levin (1986): "it would be perverse to claim that bare experience can provide us only with practical abilities". [source: SEP-KA §§4.3–4.4]
- **Acquaintance hypothesis (Earl Conee 1994, "Phenomenal Knowledge", *Australasian Journal of Philosophy* 72: 136–150):** a third kind of knowledge, neither knowing-that nor knowing-how; acquaintance "requires the person to be familiar with the known entity in the most direct way that it is possible for a person to be aware of that thing" and "experiencing a quality is the most direct way to apprehend a quality" (1994, 144). Mary gains acquaintance with a physical quality Q she already knew all about, but no new propositional knowledge. Michael Tye (2009) later adopted this, abandoning his earlier reply. Objection: acquaintance may itself yield new propositional knowledge (Alter 2023); Gertler (1999): the dualist, not the physicalist, can explain why acquaintance requires having the experience. [sources: SEP-KA §4.5; WP-Knowledge_argument for the journal reference]
- **Phenomenal concepts (Brian Loar, "Phenomenal States", 1990, revised 1997):** phenomenal concepts are **recognitional** concepts — "To have the phenomenal concept of blueness is to be able to recognize experiences of blueness while having them"; such a concept refers "directly" to a physical property, by being "triggered" by it. Common core of New Knowledge/Old Fact views: phenomenal and physical concepts are "cognitively independent" — one cannot see a priori that they pick out the same property — so Mary learns old facts under new concepts; the view supplies an "error theory" of why the argument seems sound. Holders include Horgan 1984, Churchland 1985, Tye 1995, Lycan 1996, Loar, Perry 2001, Papineau 2002, Balog 2012. [source: SEP-KA §§4.6–4.7]
- **Jackson's change of mind:** "Jackson (1995) argues that knowledge about qualia is impossible if qualia are epiphenomenal and he concludes that something must be wrong with the knowledge argument. In Jackson (2003) and Jackson (2007) he argues that the argument goes wrong in presupposing a false view about sensory experience and that it can be answered by endorsing strong representationalism." Works: "Postscript on 'What Mary Didn't Know'" (1995, in Moser & Trout eds., *Contemporary Materialism*); "Postscript on Qualia" (**1998**, in his *Mind, Method and Conditionals*, Routledge); 2003 ("Mind and Illusion"); 2007 ("The Knowledge Argument, Diaphanousness, Representationalism"). IEP: "In the late 1990's, Jackson changed his mind". **Say: "from the mid-1990s" / "by 1995 he held the argument must go wrong, and in 1998 and after embraced physicalism" — consistent with *Analytic Philosophy since 1950*.** [sources: SEP-KA §5 and bibliography; IEP-KA §1, §7]
- Jackson 2003, p. 439 (as quoted by IEP-KA): "We have ended up agreeing with Laurence Nemirow and David Lewis on what happens to Mary on her release. But, for the life of me, I cannot see how we could have known they were right without going via representationalism." [source: IEP-KA §7]
- Jackson's own reason (WP): when Mary first sees red she says "Wow!"; if qualia were epiphenomenal they could not cause that. In 2023 (podcast with Goff and Frankish) Jackson: "I no longer accept the argument", though physicalists should address it "really seriously". [source: WP-Knowledge_argument — secondary; use only attributed to Wikipedia's report]
- **Objectivism reading (Nagel line)**: Howell (2007) argues all physicalist replies depend on acquaintance, which is incompatible with "objectivism" — SEP-KA §4.9. Optional.

## 8. Zombies — hard-problem framing only (the conceivability argument is in *Possibility and Necessity*)
- Definition for this chapter: zombies are "creatures physically (and so functionally) identical to us, but lacking consciousness" (IEP-HP); they "must be like normal human beings in all physical respects". [sources: IEP-HP §1a; SEP-Z §1]
- History (not in P&N): Descartes "did everything short of spelling out the idea"; G. F. Stout (1931) described what is in effect a zombie world implied by epiphenomenalism — bodies "making and using bridges, telephones and telegraphs, … speaking in Parliament, … arguing about materialism" with no experiencing beings, "prima facie incredible to Common Sense"; Keith Campbell's "imitation man" (1970); Robert Kirk put zombies forward against physicalism (1974); Block and Shoemaker on functional duplicates without qualia; "The most systematic use … is by David Chalmers 1996". [source: SEP-Z §1]
- **Phenomenal concept strategy** (term from Stoljar 2005): there is only a conceptual gap; features of phenomenal concepts mislead us into supposing an ontological gap. Loar (1999, 467): "Given that properties are constituted by the world and not by our concepts, it is fair of the physicalist to request a justification of the assumption that conceptually distinct concepts must express metaphysically distinct properties." Phenomenal concepts "express the very properties they pick out, as Kripke observed in the case of 'pain'". **Chalmers's dilemma (2007):** let C be the psychological "key features" we have and zombies lack; if it is conceivable that the physical facts hold without C, C is not physically explicable; if not conceivable, C cannot explain our epistemic situation. [source: SEP-Z §5.2]
- **Russellian monism and zombies:** physics tells us only "structural" properties; the intrinsic properties underlying them might be phenomenal or protophenomenal (Chalmers 2010, 151); Chalmers: "a highly distinctive form of physicalism that has much in common with property dualism and that many physicalists will want to reject" (2010, 152). Goff (2010, 2017): argument from **ghosts** (pure subjects with no physical nature) instead of zombies. [source: SEP-Z §5.3]
- **Consistency with *Possibility and Necessity*:** that chapter already gives the three-step zombie argument, the a posteriori physicalist objection (Hesperus/Phosphorus analogy), Chalmers's two-dimensional reply and Russellian-monism dilemma, and Frankish's anti-zombies. **Do not repeat; cross-refer.**

## 9. Physicalist replies
### Type-B physicalism
- See §5 taxonomy and §8 phenomenal concept strategy. IEP calls it "weak reductionism": the hard problem reflects two ways of knowing (introspection, neuroscience), not two kinds of thing. [sources: WP-Hard_problem; IEP-HP §3c]

### Illusionism
- **Term coined by Keith Frankish (b. 7 Nov 1962, British)** in "Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness", *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 23(11–12), 2016 (special issue), reissued as the edited book *Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness* (Imprint Academic, 2017). Strong illusionism: phenomenal properties do not exist; weak: they exist but are misrepresented. Key notion: **quasi-phenomenal property** — a physical property that introspection misrepresents as phenomenal. Illusionism "replaces the hard problem with the illusion problem—the problem of explaining how the illusion of phenomenality arises and why it is so powerful". Frankish (2020): "Illusionists do not deny the existence of consciousness, but they do offer a different account of what consciousness is". [sources: WP-Illusionism; WP-Keith_Frankish]
- Dennett's reply paper: "Illusionism as the Obvious Default Theory of Consciousness" (2016): explain "how the 'magic' is done". Chalmers (2018): "strong illusionism is widely regarded as very implausible" but illusionists who wish to dissolve the hard problem must be strong ones. Others listed by Frankish as advocates: Georges Rey, Jay Garfield (who links it to Buddhist critiques and Sellars's "Myth of the Given"), Amber Ross; "explorers": Michael Graziano (attention schema theory), François Kammerer, Derk Pereboom. [source: WP-Illusionism]
- **Objection (Searle):** "where consciousness is concerned the existence of the appearance is the reality" (Searle, *The Mystery of Consciousness*, 1997). **Reply:** Frankish — the illusion is understood in "informational/reactive terms"; there need be an inner "representer" but no inner "observer"; Pereboom — the seeming phenomenality of the illusion is itself a further misrepresentation. [source: WP-Illusionism]
- Dennett (28 Mar 1942 – 19 Apr 2024). *Consciousness Explained* (Little, Brown, 1991): **multiple drafts model**, no "Cartesian theater" where it all comes together; consciousness is "various events of content-fixation occurring in various places at various times". [sources: WP-Daniel_Dennett; WP-Consciousness_Explained]

### Higher-order theories (Rosenthal)
- What makes a mental state M conscious is a higher-order representation of M (a higher-order perception — "inner sense" — or a higher-order thought). David Rosenthal (1986, 1993, 2005) is the main proponent of actualist **HOT theory**: a conscious state is one "actually causing an activated thought (generally a non-conscious one) that I have M, and causing it non-inferentially"; the HOT typically has the form "I am in mental state M". [source: SEP-HOT intro, §4]
- **Objections:** (1) **targetless HOTs** — a HOT could occur with no first-order state (Byrne 1997; Neander 1998; Levine 2001); Rosenthal (2005) seems to accept that a suitable HOT suffices even then; (2) **the rock** (Goldman 1993; Stubenberg 1998): thinking about a rock does not make it conscious, so why should thinking about a perception make the perception conscious?; (3) **animals and infants** — do cats and dogs have HOTs with mental-state concepts? (Dretske 1995; Tye 1995). [source: SEP-HOT §§7.1–7.3]
- Reply strands: dispositionalist HOT (Carruthers) and self-representational theories (Kriegel) escape targetless HOTs because the higher-order state cannot exist without its target. [source: SEP-HOT §7.1]

### Representationalism (cross-ref *Perception and the External World*, which already covers intentionalism and quotes Harman's tree)
- Strong representationalism (Dretske, Tye, Lycan): "representation of a certain kind suffices for a sensory quality, where the kind can be specified in functionalist or other familiar materialist terms". Dretske (*Naturalizing the Mind*, 1995) requires the representation to be sensory and "systemic"; Tye (*Ten Problems of Consciousness*, 1995) requires it to be "nonconceptual and 'poised'"; Lycan (1996) appeals to functional role. Pure representationalism is held by no one, since "representation alone is cheap and ubiquitous". [source: SEP-Rep §§2, 2.1]
- Tye on Mary (1995): phenomenal blueness has a physical nature but cannot be fully understood except under a phenomenal concept (indexical: "this particular hue"). [source: SEP-KA §4.7]
- Counterexamples to representationalism: blurry vision (Boghossian & Velleman 1989; Tye's informational reply 2003), Block on attention ("Attended things look bigger, faster, more saturated"), inversion cases. [source: SEP-Rep §4.5]
- Fred Dretske (9 Dec 1932 – 24 Jul 2013); Michael Tye (b. 1950, British, UT Austin). [sources: WP-Fred_Dretske; WP-Michael_Tye]

### Mysterianism
- Colin McGinn (b. 1950), "Can We Solve the Mind-Body Problem?", *Mind* 98 (no. 391), 1989: 349–366; *The Problem of Consciousness* (1991). "Permanent mysterianism": the solution is **"cognitively closed"** to us, given our cognitive capacities; inspired partly by Chomsky's modular view of mind. IEP: "We are like squirrels trying to understand quantum mechanics." SEP-C: McGinn (1995) argued that the spatial character of our perceptual and scientific concepts unsuits us; facts about the psychophysical link "are as cognitively closed to us as are facts about multiplication or square roots to armadillos". [sources: WP-New_mysterianism; IEP-HP §3d; SEP-C §5.2]
- Name: Owen Flanagan, *The Science of the Mind* (1991), called them "the new mysterians" after the rock group Question Mark and the Mysterians. "Temporary mysterianism" (Nagel 1974; Levine 2001) vs permanent (McGinn) — IEP's terms. Chomsky's "problems" vs "mysteries". [sources: WP-New_mysterianism; IEP-HP §3d]
- **Objection (IEP):** "it is hard to establish just what sorts of problems are permanently beyond our ken"; given the history of surprising breakthroughs, what looks unbridgeable may be a temporary roadblock. Dennett attacked McGinn in "The Brain and Its Boundaries", *TLS*, 10 May 1991. [sources: IEP-HP §3d; WP-New_mysterianism]

## 10. Panpsychism and Russellian monism
- **Definition:** literally "everything has a mind"; in contemporary debate, "the view that mentality is fundamental and ubiquitous in the natural world". The panpsychist need not hold that a rock has a mind — only its fundamental parts. Contemporary forms are **panexperientialist** (experience, not thought, is fundamental); "Panpsychism is often caricatured as the view that electrons have hopes and dreams". Variants: constitutive vs emergentist; panpsychism vs **panprotopsychism**; micropsychism vs **cosmopsychism**. [source: SEP-Pan §§2.1–2.4]
- **Russellian monism:** Bertrand Russell, *The Analysis of Matter* (1927); Arthur Eddington independently in his 1927 Gifford lectures (published 1928). Two theses: physics tells us only the extrinsic/relational/dispositional nature of matter; the intrinsic nature it leaves out accounts for consciousness. Panpsychist and panprotopsychist forms. Attraction: avoids dualism's causal-closure problem and physicalism's grounding problem. Russell (1927, 325): otherwise "all the things in the world will merely be each other's washing". [source: SEP-Pan §§2.5, 3.2] SEP has a dedicated entry (Alter & Pereboom, rev. 2023). [SEP-RM]
- **Nagel's 1979 argument ("Panpsychism", in *Mortal Questions*)**, four premises: Material Composition; Realism (mental states are genuine properties of organisms); No Radical Emergence (all properties of a complex organism are intelligibly derived from properties of its parts); Non-Reductionism (mental states are not intelligibly derived from physical properties alone) → there must be non-physical properties of basic matter. Compatible with panprotopsychism. [source: SEP-Pan §3.1]
- **Galen Strawson** (b. 1952), "Realistic Monism: Why Physicalism Entails Panpsychism", *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 13(10–11) (2006): 3–31 (SEP-Pan's bibliography misprints the title as "Realistic Materialism"; WP and other sources give "Realistic Monism"). "Emergence can't be brute … For any feature Y of anything that is correctly considered to be emergent from X, there must be something about X and X alone in virtue of which Y emerges" (2006a: 18). Liquidity emerges intelligibly from molecules; experience cannot intelligibly emerge from the wholly non-experiential. Reply volume: Freeman (ed.) 2006. [sources: SEP-Pan §3.1; WP-Galen_Strawson; WP-Panpsychism]
- **Philip Goff** (b. 1978, British, Durham): *Galileo's Error: Foundations for a New Science of Consciousness* (Pantheon/Rider, 2019); intrinsic nature argument; Einstein-vs-Lorentz simplicity analogy against the "incredulous stare". [sources: SEP-Pan §§3.2, 4.1; WP-Philip_Goff_(philosopher)]
- **The combination problem**: "the hardest problem facing panpsychism"; term from William Seager (1995); traced to William James, *The Principles of Psychology* (1890), vol. 1, ch. 6 ("The Mind-Stuff Theory"), p. 160. [source: SEP-Pan §4.2]
- **James's "twelve men" passage, as quoted in SEP-Unity (James 1890: 160):** "Take a sentence of a dozen words, take twelve men, and to each one word. Then stand the men in a row or jam them in a bunch and let each think of his word as intently as he will; nowhere will there be a consciousness of the whole sentence." ⚠ The SEP rendering appears to drop words (the commonly cited original reads "…and take twelve men and tell to each one word" — WS-3, unconfirmed). **Safest: quote only "let each think of his word as intently as he will; nowhere will there be a consciousness of the whole sentence"**, or paraphrase the first half. [sources: SEP-Unity §2.2.1; WS-3]
- James's companion passage (SEP-Pan, James [1890] 1981: 160): "Take a hundred of them [feelings], shuffle them and pack them as close together as you can … still each remains the same feeling it always was, shut in its own skin, windowless, ignorant of what the other feelings are and mean." Note: James's own argument is that there is no combination of any kind (Shani 2010); and SEP notes James's commitment to panpsychism "remains somewhat controversial" since he attacked the "mind dust" theory. [sources: SEP-Pan §4.2; WP-Hard_problem note quoting SEP]
- **Subject-summing problem**: Coleman (2014) — each subject's viewpoint excludes others', so summing is incoherent; Goff's "Conceivable Isolation of Subjects" and the **micro-experiential zombie** (physically like us, micro-parts conscious, no macro-consciousness) — uses the very conceivability reasoning panpsychists use against physicalism. [source: SEP-Pan §4.3]
- **Reply** (SEP-Pan): consensus that "there is currently no wholly adequate solution", but compare Darwinism, which waited decades for genetics; a research programme takes time. [source: SEP-Pan §4.2]
- **Decombination problem** for cosmopsychism; SEP-Ś says Śaṅkara's theory of reflection is "a novel way of addressing the decombination problem in cosmopsychism—the explanatory gap between universal and individual organism consciousness" (citing Albahari 2019). Good bridge to Advaita. [source: SEP-Ś §3]
- IEP-Pan's historical arguments: continuity (Anaximenes' pneuma etc.), non-emergence ("ex nihilo, nihil fit"), W. K. Clifford 1874 (no line can be drawn in the phylogenetic chain). [source: IEP-Pan §3]

## 11. The science of consciousness
- **NCC**: modern neuroscience focuses on neural correlates of consciousness (Crick & Koch 1990, "Towards a neurobiological theory of consciousness", *Seminars in the Neurosciences* 2: 263–275). Chalmers's definition: "An NCC is a minimal neural system N such that there is a mapping from states of N to states of consciousness, where a given state of N is sufficient under conditions C, for the corresponding state of consciousness" (Chalmers 2000: 31); Koch: "the minimal set of neuronal events and mechanisms jointly sufficient for a specific conscious percept" (Koch 2004: 16). SEP-Neuro: "correlates are not necessarily explanatory". Crick's *The Astonishing Hypothesis* (1994). [sources: SEP-Neuro §1.6; WP-NCC]
- Contrast method: binocular rivalry (Logothetis). Eric Kandel: locating NCCs would solve an easy problem, not the hard one. [sources: SEP-Neuro §5.2 heading; WP-NCC; WP-Hard_problem]
- **Global workspace theory**: Bernard Baars, *A Cognitive Theory of Consciousness* (Cambridge UP, 1988), inspired by AI "blackboard" architectures; neural version (global neuronal workspace, GNW) by Stanislas Dehaene (b. 12 May 1965, French) with Kerszberg & Changeux 1998, Dehaene & Naccache 2001: a state is conscious "when and only when it (or its content) is present in the global neuronal workspace making the state (content) globally accessible to multiple systems"; Dehaene et al. 2006: subliminal / preconscious / conscious. Dehaene, *Consciousness and the Brain* (Viking, 2014). [sources: SEP-Neuro §3.1; WP-Global_workspace_theory; WP-Stanislas_Dehaene]
- **Integrated information theory (IIT)**: proposed by Giulio Tononi (b. 1960, Italian) in 2004 ("An information integration theory of consciousness", *BMC Neuroscience* 5:42). Starts from phenomenological "axioms" (consciousness exists; intrinsic, specific, unitary, definite, structured); consciousness = integrated information, Φ; Φ > 0 implies consciousness. The cerebellum, with four to five times as many neurons as the cortex, is said to have Φ ≈ 0. Consequences: even a 2-D grid of inactive logic gates "may in fact have a large value of PHI" (Tononi 2014); Scott Aaronson's counterexamples. SEP-Neuro: IIT "is probably best understood as a metaphysical theory"; Searle: IIT "implies panpsychism". IIT 4.0 (Albantakis et al., *PLOS Comput. Biol.* 2023). [sources: SEP-Neuro §3.4; WP-IIT; WP-Giulio_Tononi]
- **2023 "pseudoscience" letter** (hedge carefully): a letter posted on 15 September 2023 to the preprint server PsyArXiv, "The Integrated Information Theory of Consciousness as Pseudoscience" (Fleming, Frith, Goodale, Lau, LeDoux et al.), signed by 124 scholars, said that until IIT is empirically testable it should be labelled pseudoscience. Many researchers defended IIT. An anonymous survey of 60 consciousness researchers: 8% fully agreed with the letter, 20% "not at all". The editors of *Nature* (2025) wrote that "such language has no place in a process designed to establish working relationships between competing groups." In March 2025 *Nature Neuroscience* ran a critics' commentary ("What makes a theory of consciousness unscientific?") and replies by Tononi et al. and by Gomez-Marin & Anil Seth. [source: WP-IIT — secondary; present as "a public dispute", not a verdict]
- **Adversarial collaboration (Cogitate)**: Templeton funding (over $6 million, announced 2019) for a preregistered test of IIT vs GNWT, with both theories' proponents agreeing in advance on predictions; initial results announced June 2023; peer-reviewed results: Cogitate Consortium, "Adversarial testing of global neuronal workspace and integrated information theories of consciousness", *Nature* 642: 133–142 (published 30 April 2025). Findings (WS summary of the paper): content information in visual, ventrotemporal and inferior frontal cortex; results matched some predictions of both theories but challenged key tenets of each (e.g. for IIT, no sustained synchronisation within posterior cortex; WP says none of GNWT's preregistered predictions passed the agreed threshold, two of three of IIT's did). **Say: "the results embarrassed both theories in different ways"**; do not declare a winner. [sources: WP-IIT; WS-7]
- **Blindsight**: lesions to primary visual cortex (V1) produce blindness in part of the visual field, yet patients, when made to guess, discriminate stimuli there above chance while denying they see them; Lawrence Weiskrantz (28 Mar 1926 – 27 Jan 2018), Oxford, credited with discovering it (with colleagues, early 1970s); patient "DB"; book *Blindsight: A Case Study and Implications* (OUP 1986). A patient with bilateral V1 damage walked down a hallway around obstacles he said he could not see (de Gelder et al. 2008). [sources: SEP-Neuro §4.2; WP-Blindsight; WP-Lawrence_Weiskrantz]
- Philosophical use: blindsight is often cited as a dissociation of access-like processing from phenomenal consciousness (Block 1995 discusses "superblindsight") — **not verified in sources read; if used, attribute generally**.
- **Split-brain**: corpus callosotomy (severing the ~200 million–fibre corpus callosum) to treat severe epilepsy, mostly in the 1960s; in everyday life patients seem unified, but in the lab show behaviour "suggestive of two independent centers of consciousness". Roger W. Sperry (20 Aug 1913 – 17 Apr 1994), Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 1981; with Michael Gazzaniga, "The Split Brain in Man", *Scientific American* (1967). Gazzaniga's left-hemisphere "interpreter" (chicken claw / snow shovel example). [sources: SEP-Unity §3.1; WP-Split-brain; WP-Roger_Wolcott_Sperry; WP-Michael_Gazzaniga] (SEP-Unity says "approximately 200 million neurons"; strictly these are axons/fibres — say "some 200 million nerve fibres".)

## 12. Animal (and machine) consciousness
- **Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness**: proclaimed 7 July 2012 at the Francis Crick Memorial Conference, Churchill College, Cambridge; written by Philip Low, edited with Panksepp, Reiss, Edelman, Van Swinderen and Koch; signed that evening in the presence of Stephen Hawking (WS-5). SEP-Animal: "signed by 22 neuroscientists". Key text: "the weight of evidence indicates that humans are not unique in possessing the neurological substrates that generate consciousness. Non-human animals, including all mammals and birds, and many other creatures, including octopuses, also possess these neurological substrates." [sources: SEP-Animal §4.6; WP-Animal_consciousness; WS-5]
- **New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness**: unveiled 19 April 2024 at the NYU conference "The Emerging Science of Animal Consciousness"; initiated by Kristin Andrews, Jeff Sebo and Jonathan Birch; "strong scientific support for attributions of conscious experience to other mammals and to birds" and "at least a realistic possibility of conscious experience in all vertebrates (including reptiles, amphibians, and fishes) and many invertebrates (including, at minimum, cephalopod mollusks, decapod crustaceans, and insects)"; "when there is a realistic possibility of conscious experience in an animal, it is irresponsible to ignore that possibility in decisions affecting that animal." Signed by about 40 at launch, over 500 within weeks (SEP). **Debate:** Hakwan Lau (2024) argues it exaggerates the support even for mammals and birds. [sources: SEP-Animal §4.6; WP-Animal_consciousness; WS-6]
- HOT theories face the animals objection (§9). [source: SEP-HOT §7.3]
- Machine consciousness: point to *Minds and Machines: Could AI Think?*. Hooks verified here: IIT implies a behaviourally perfect system might experience nothing, and opposes computational functionalism (WP-IIT); SEP-Q has a §13 "Qualia and AI"; Birch's *The Edge of Sentience* covers AI (subtitle). [sources: WP-IIT; SEP-Q contents; SEP-Animal bibliography]

## 13. Indian traditions (cross-ref the history chapters; add only the consciousness angle)
- **Sāṃkhya** (Īśvarakṛṣṇa's *Sāṃkhya-kārikā*, c. 350 CE, 72 verses — IEP; check consistency with *Classical Indian Schools*): two kinds of entity, Prakṛti (nature, unconscious though purposeful) and many puruṣas. Puruṣa "is pure consciousness: it enjoys and witnesses Prakṛti's activities, but does not cause them"; "absolutely inactive", "uncaused, eternal, all-pervasive, partless". Cognition, emotion, volition belong to the *buddhi* (intellect), a material product of Prakṛti — so on Sāṃkhya, **thinking is material but consciousness is not**: a striking inversion of the modern view that cognition is the easy part. [source: IEP-S §§ on puruṣa, buddhi]
- Five Sāṃkhya arguments for puruṣa (IEP): complex structures serve something else (bed for a sleeper); consciousness and privacy are opposite to the guṇas, so need a non-material cause; coordination needs a supervisor; immediate awareness of ourselves as experiencers; liberation requires something to be liberated. Plurality of puruṣas: different births and deaths, different actions, different private experiences. [source: IEP-S]
- **Debate**: many modern scholars read puruṣa as strictly unchanging (A. B. Keith thought this made Sāṃkhya "a hopeless bundle of contradictions"; Gerald Larson translates "contentless consciousness"); Ruzsa argues the Kārikā's "inactive" (*a-kriya*) means only no spatial motion or mechanical effect. Ruzsa: Sāṃkhya looks like "a full materialist account of the world, with the passive, unchanging principle of consciousness added almost as an afterthought" (already quoted in *Classical Indian Schools* — don't repeat). [source: IEP-S]
- **Advaita Vedānta**: consciousness (*cit*, *caitanya*) as the fundamental reality, identical with brahman; from the empirical standpoint it is the witness (*sākṣin*) "by which all mental cognition is revealed as known"; from the absolute standpoint it is stripped of all relational properties, "including intentionality and its status as a witness". Contemporary comparisons with top-down **cosmopsychism** (Gasparri 2019; Vaidya 2020; Albahari 2019) and witness-consciousness literature (Albahari 2009; Fasching 2011). [source: SEP-Ś §§2.4, 3] **The published *Vedānta* chapter already sets out Śaṅkara's witness/self-luminosity argument step by step with the Nyāya/Rāmānuja objection and the deep-sleep reply — cross-refer, don't repeat.**
- **Nyāya**: Nyāya-sūtra 1.1.10 — "Desire, aversion, volition (prayatna), pleasure, pain, and cognition are characteristics of the self". Argument: cognition, desire etc. are qualities; qualities inhere in substances; not in earth, water etc.; so in a non-material self. These qualities "are transitory and come into existence only when the self is in contact with the mind-sense organs-body complex"; they "are not its essential qualities"; the self lacks them in deep sleep and permanently in liberation — "an insentient stone-like condition" (Dravid 1995). [sources: SEP-Pers (Chadha) Nyāya section; IEP-N §on self] — i.e. **consciousness is a non-essential (adventitious) quality of the self**; the Sanskrit term *āgantuka* was NOT found in sources read, so use "non-essential/transitory quality", not the Sanskrit. (The *Classical Indian Schools* chapter already gives the qualities→substance argument and Chadha's causal-idleness worry.)
- Nyāya on self-awareness: cognitions "rarely present themselves directly"; they are grasped by later **apperceptive** cognitions (*anuvyavasāya*-type view; the Sanskrit term not in IEP text read), via the inner organ (*manas*); Gaṅgeśa defends this against the Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā view that each cognition is reflexively self-aware. Note the parallel with **higher-order** theories. [source: IEP-N §on perception]
- **Yogācāra**: "consciousness-only" (*vijñaptimātra*/*cittamātra*), eight consciousnesses including the storehouse consciousness (*ālaya-vijñāna*) holding karmic seeds. [source: WP-Yogachara; SEP-MIB §5.5 heading] **Already covered in *Mahāyāna Philosophy* (incl. Vasubandhu's *Twenty Verses*, Gold's translation, the caution about "mind-only") — cross-refer only.** Chinese: Xuanzang (602–664) founded the Chinese "Consciousness-only" school (already in *Chinese Buddhism and the Rise of Neo-Confucianism*); Xuanzang held the seeds real, Sthiramati nominal (per the *Chengweishilun*). [sources: WP-Yogachara; published Chinese Buddhism chapter]
- **Reflexive awareness (svasaṃvedana / svasaṃvitti) — NOT yet covered step by step in any published chapter (the *Vedānta* chapter mentions only that Vasubandhu and Dharmakīrti held that a cognition "reveals both itself and its object").** SEP-MIB: the Dignāga–Dharmakīrti school accepts reflexive awareness "in the classical Yogācāra sense of self-luminosity (svaprakāśa)", with "the example of a lamp that illuminates itself while at the same time revealing other objects"; "the main argument for reflexive awareness is offered by Dignāga" (c. 480–540): each cognition has a double aspect, appearing as apprehending subject (*grāhakākāra*) and apprehended object (*grāhyākāra*); Dignāga, *Pramāṇasamuccaya* I.10 (Hattori 1968 trans., p. 97): "that which appears in cognition is the known object (prameya), the reliable cognition (pramāṇa) and its result, are [respectively] the subjective aspects [of the cognition] and the cognition [itself]". Dharmakīrti (*Pramāṇaviniścaya* I.55): "If ascertaining were not itself [directly] perceived, the perception of objects would not be established." [source: SEP-MIB §7.3]
- **Memory argument** (Dignāga): "Memory proves a self-reflexive nature of cognition because one is able to remember both the object and one's former cognition of it." Dharmakīrti: cognition cannot be cognised by a further cognition on pain of infinite regress; cognition and object "are perceived together". Earlier: the Mahāsāṃghika view preserved in the *Mahāvibhāṣā* ("like a lamp that can illuminate itself and others"). [source: WP-Svasaṃvedana — secondary; good for a step-by-step]
- **Madhyamaka critique**: Candrakīrti (c. 600–650) and Śāntideva (fl. 8th c.) reject inherent reflexivity "even on a conventional level, since that would imply that consciousness is self-validating" (Candrakīrti, *Madhyamakāvatāra* VI.72–78); Candrakīrti treats all seeing as "seeing as" (Tillemans). Śāntarakṣita defends reflexivity: "This reflexive awareness of that cognition is none other than its non-insentience" (*Tattvasaṃgraha* 2000). Jay Garfield (2006) defends the Madhyamaka critique; Dan Arnold compares svasaṃvedana to Kant's transcendental unity of apperception. [sources: SEP-MIB §7.3; WP-Svasaṃvedana] (Śāntideva's "a sword cannot cut itself" image is widely cited but was NOT found in sources read — and note the *Vedānta* chapter gives Śaṅkara's parallel "Just as a knife cannot cut itself" against the Buddhists. Do not attribute the sword line to Śāntideva without checking.)
- Dharmakīrti against the materialist (Cārvāka) claim that consciousness arises from the four elements: then consciousness "ought to arise whenever the elements occur, which is to say everywhere"; consciousness cannot arise from what is not sentient. A classical anti-emergence argument — compare Strawson. [source: SEP-MIB §7.3]
- Cross-cultural parallels SEP-MIB lists: Williams relates Śāntarakṣita's reflexivity to Aristotle's *De Anima*, Brentano and Sartre; Dreyfus compares Dharmakīrti and Husserl. [source: SEP-MIB §7.3]
- **Phenomenology pointer**: Husserl (time-consciousness), Merleau-Ponty, Zahavi on pre-reflective self-consciousness, Varela/Thompson/Rosch *The Embodied Mind* (1991) — all already in *Phenomenology*; SEP-C §1 lists Husserl (1913, 1929), Heidegger (1927), Merleau-Ponty (1945) as extending consciousness study to the bodily and interpersonal. [source: SEP-C §1; published Phenomenology chapter]

## 14. Arguments to set out step by step (recommended; all premises sourced above)
**A1. Nagel's subjectivity argument** [IEP-HP §1b; SEP-KA §1]
1. Facts about conscious experience are subjective: fully graspable only from a particular type of point of view (what it is like *for* the bat).
2. Physical facts are objective "par excellence": understandable from many points of view.
3. Scientific (physical) explanation proceeds by moving away from any particular point of view.
4. So a complete physical account would leave out what it is like to be the subject.
5. Conclusion (Nagel's own, modest): we do not at present understand how physicalism *could* be true — not that it is false.
- Objection: Dennett/Akins — the theoretically interesting features of bat experience are third-person accessible (range limits of sonar etc.). Reply: what is left over, on Nagel's view, is exactly the "for the bat" character, which no range-limit fact conveys; Nagel calls for an "objective phenomenology".

**A2. The hard problem as failure of reductive explanation** [IEP-HP §1a; SEP-Z §5.2]
1. Reductive explanation = functional analysis of the target + empirical discovery of what plays the role (gene → DNA).
2. Every "easy" phenomenon (learning, discrimination, report) can be functionally analysed.
3. Consciousness cannot be: zombies and inverted spectra remain conceivable after any proposed functional analysis.
4. So consciousness cannot be reductively explained.
5. So either eliminate it or take it as fundamental (Chalmers's "naturalistic dualism", or Russellian monism).
- Objection (type-B / phenomenal concept strategy, Loar): step 3 shows only a conceptual gap — phenomenal concepts are recognitional and pick out physical properties directly; conceptually distinct concepts need not express distinct properties. Reply (Chalmers 2007 dilemma): either the "key features" of phenomenal concepts are themselves not physically explicable, or they cannot explain why we, unlike zombies, are in the epistemic situation we are.
- Alternative objection (illusionism): deny the datum; reply (Searle): for consciousness the appearance *is* the reality.

**A3. Panpsychism from anti-emergence, and the combination problem** [SEP-Pan §§3.1, 4.2–4.3]
1. Human experience is real (Realism).
2. We are made of ordinary matter, nothing immaterial (Material Composition).
3. No radical emergence: properties of a complex thing must be intelligibly derivable from properties of its parts.
4. Experience is not intelligibly derivable from wholly non-experiential properties (Non-Reductionism; Strawson: no conceptually homogeneous vocabulary spans the two).
5. So the fundamental constituents of matter have experiential (or protoexperiential) properties.
- Objection 1 (incredulous stare): electrons with experience is absurd. Reply: relativity and common ancestry with apes are counterintuitive too; judge by simplicity (Goff).
- Objection 2 (combination problem — James's twelve men; Coleman's exclusion; Goff's micro-experiential zombie): micro-experiences do not add up to a unified subject, so step 5 fails to explain *our* consciousness; and the same conceivability reasoning panpsychists use against physicalism applies here. Reply: no adequate solution yet (SEP consensus), but compare Darwinism awaiting genetics; or move to cosmopsychism (and face the *de*combination problem — where Śaṅkara's reflection theory has been invoked).

**A4. Dignāga's memory argument for reflexive awareness, and the Madhyamaka reply** [WP-Svasaṃvedana; SEP-MIB §7.3]
1. I can later remember not only the object (blue) but my seeing of it.
2. One can remember only what one was earlier aware of.
3. So the earlier cognition was itself experienced when it occurred.
4. It was not experienced by a second, separate cognition (regress: that one would need a third — Dharmakīrti).
5. So each cognition is aware of itself as it is aware of its object (the lamp).
- Objection (Candrakīrti, Śāntideva): inherent self-awareness would make consciousness self-validating and possessed of intrinsic nature (*svabhāva*), contrary to emptiness; (cf. Nyāya: cognitions are known by later apperceptive cognitions — a "higher-order" view). Reply (Śāntarakṣita): reflexivity is simply what it is for cognition not to be insentient — "its non-insentience"; no extra entity is posited. Modern echo: HOT vs self-representational theories (SEP-HOT §6).

## Debates
- Is there a hard problem at all? Chalmers, Levine, Nagel (yes) vs Dennett, Churchlands, Frankish, Dehaene, Hacker (no / misconceived); 2020 PhilPapers: ~62% yes. [IEP-HP; WP-Hard_problem]
- Does an explanatory gap imply an ontological gap? Levine (not necessarily), Chalmers (yes, given strong conceivability), type-B physicalists (no: conceptual gap only). [SEP-C §5.2; SEP-Z §5.2]
- Knowledge argument: where it fails — learning claim (Dennett; Jackson after 1995), propositional knowledge (Lewis, Nemirow; Conee), new facts (Loar, Tye, Papineau), or not at all (Alter, Nida-Rümelin). [SEP-KA; IEP-KA]
- Higher-order vs first-order (representationalist) theories, e.g. over animals and targetless HOTs. [SEP-HOT; SEP-Rep]
- Panpsychism: taken seriously by Chalmers, Goff, Strawson, Seager; combination problem regarded even by proponents as unsolved. [SEP-Pan]
- Is IIT science? 2023 letter and 2025 *Nature Neuroscience* exchange; *Nature* editors deprecated the "pseudoscience" language. [WP-IIT]
- Cogitate results: support/challenge both theories; interpretation disputed. [WS-7; WP-IIT]
- New York Declaration: endorsed by 500+; Lau (2024) says it overstates the evidence. [SEP-Animal §4.6]
- Sāṃkhya puruṣa: strictly unchanging (Larson, Keith) vs not (Ruzsa). [IEP-S]
- Svasaṃvedana: Dignāga, Dharmakīrti, Śāntarakṣita (for) vs Candrakīrti, Śāntideva (against); Nyāya's apperception vs Prābhākara self-revelation; Garfield defends the Madhyamaka side. [SEP-MIB §7.3; IEP-N]
