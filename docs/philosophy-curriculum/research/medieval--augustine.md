## 1. Christianity meets Greek philosophy (2nd–3rd centuries)
- Justin Martyr (c. 90–100 – c. 165), "Justin the Philosopher", born to a Greek family at Flavia Neapolis (modern Nablus) in Samaria. [source: WP-Justin_Martyr]
- Justin's own account of his search: a Stoic teacher could not explain God to him; a Peripatetic was too eager for his fee; a Pythagorean demanded he first learn music, astronomy and geometry; he then adopted Platonism, and finally was converted after meeting an old man (possibly a Syrian Christian). [source: WP-Justin_Martyr] WRITER: this is Justin's self-portrait in the *Dialogue with Trypho*, a literary conversion story; present it as his account.
- Works: the *First Apology* (addressed to Antoninus Pius and his adopted sons, so 147–161) and the *Dialogue with Trypho*. He held that manifestations of the Logos preceded Christ's incarnation and that Greek philosophers such as Socrates and Plato (he names Socrates and Heraclitus) were in effect Christians. [source: WP-Justin_Martyr]
- Justin was tried with six companions before the urban prefect Junius Rusticus and beheaded, around 165, under Marcus Aurelius. [source: WP-Justin_Martyr]
- Clement of Alexandria (Titus Flavius Clemens, c. 150 [WP also gives c. 147] – c. 215): influenced by Hellenistic philosophy, especially Plato and the Stoics, more than any Christian thinker of his time; main works *Protrepticus*, *Paedagogus* (c. 198), *Stromata*; also *Who Is the Rich Man That Shall Be Saved?* (c. 203). He treated philosophy as a preparatory discipline for the Greeks before Christianity and argued Greek philosophy had non-Greek origins (Plato and Pythagoras taught by Egyptians). He met Pantaenus of the Alexandrian catechetical school; whether the school was a formal institution with a succession of heads before Origen is disputed. [source: WP-Clement_of_Alexandria]
- Origen (c. 185 – c. 253), native of Alexandria; taught by a certain Ammonius, whom most scholars identify with Ammonius Saccas, Plotinus' teacher (identification disputed). Used philosophy "as an ancillary to the exposition and harmonization of scripture"; first Christian to speak of three "hypostases" in the Trinity. [source: SEP-Ori]
- Origen: left Alexandria in 216; settled in Caesarea (Palestine), where he wrote *Against Celsus* (reply to Celsus' anti-Christian *True Logos*, written c. 170); his school's preparatory course was a survey of all Greek schools that admitted providence (Gregory Thaumaturgus' testimony); tortured under Decius in 251; anathematized "in or around the year 553". His major work *On First Principles* survives entire only in Rufinus' Latin. [source: SEP-Ori]
- Origen's "synergistic" reading of election (salvation from cooperation of grace and human initiative) was standard in early Christianity until Augustine rejected it. [source: SEP-Aug §7.6]
- Tertullian (Quintus Septimius Florens Tertullianus, c. 155 – c. 220), from Carthage in Roman Africa; prolific Latin author; drawn about 207 to the "New Prophecy" (Montanism), though most scholars now reject the claim that he left the mainstream church or was excommunicated. Some sources call him Berber; René Braun suggested Punic origin. [source: WP-Tertullian]
- "What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?": in *On the Prescription of Heretics* (De praescriptione haereticorum) ch. 7. Latin: "Quid ergo Athenis et Hierosolymis? quid Academiae et Ecclesiae? quid haereticis et Christianis?" Peter Holmes's translation (Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 3, 1885): "What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem? What concord is there between the Academy and the Church?" The chapter (Holmes's heading: "Pagan Philosophy the Parent of Heresies") argues that heresies come from philosophy, e.g. Valentinus from Plato's school and Marcion's god from the Stoics, and draws on Colossians 2:8. [source: WS-2, WS-3 (search results quoting the ANF text and the Latin); WP-Tertullian]
- WRITER: context matters. The question is from a treatise against heretics, about method (philosophical inquiry versus the authority of scripture and the apostolic churches); Tertullian himself used Stoic ideas (e.g. God's "corporeity", Stoically understood). [source: WP-Tertullian; WS-2; SEP-Ori mentions "the Stoicizing theology of Tertullian"]
- Original sin had a tradition in African Christianity, especially in Tertullian, before Augustine. [source: SEP-Aug §7.6] Tertullian was perhaps the first to hold hereditary transmission of sin, via traducianism. [source: WP-Original_sin]

## 2. Augustine's life
- Aurelius Augustinus: 13 November 354 – 28 August 430; born at Thagaste in Roman Africa (modern Souk Ahras, Algeria), province of Numidia. [source: SEP-Aug §1; WP-Augustine_of_Hippo]
- Mother Monnica (also "Monica"), a devout Christian (her name appears only once in the Confessions, at 9.37); father Patricius, baptized on his deathbed (SEP; WP: a pagan who converted on his deathbed); Patricius died 372. A brother Navigius and an unnamed sister. [source: SEP-Aug §1, §9; WP-Augustine_of_Hippo; IEP-Aug]
- Monnica's death: SEP gives 388 (at Ostia); WP-Saint_Monica gives c. 332 – 387. Sources differ; safe wording "died at Ostia in 387 or 388, as the family waited to sail for Africa".
- Family: "middle-class parents" whose resources were strained by his studies (SEP); WP says they were *honestiores* (local upper class) and that the name Aurelius suggests ancestors enfranchised by Caracalla's edict of 212. A patron, Romanianus, helped fund his studies at Carthage. [source: SEP-Aug §1; WP-Augustine_of_Hippo]
- Education: grammar and rhetoric at Madauros and Carthage. He never mastered Greek. [source: SEP-Aug §1; WP-Augustine_of_Hippo]
- At about 18 in Carthage he took a partner and lived in a monogamous union with her for about 14 years (SEP; WP says "over fifteen years"); their son Adeodatus was baptized with his father in Milan and died young (SEP: c. 390 aged 18; WP: 372–388). The sources do not name her. [source: SEP-Aug §1, §9; WP-Augustine_of_Hippo]
- At 18 Cicero's (now lost) protreptic dialogue *Hortensius* "enflamed him for philosophy" (Confessions 3.7 in SEP's numbering). [source: SEP-Aug §4; IEP-Aug]
- Manichaeism: a dualistic religion of Persian origin that in North Africa had become a variety of Christianity, persecuted as heresy. From c. 373 Augustine was a "hearer" (auditor) for nine years (SEP; WP says ten), never one of the ascetic "elect". [source: SEP-Aug §1; WP-Augustine_of_Hippo]
- Career: taught at Thagaste and Carthage; went to Rome in 383; through the prefect Symmachus won the post of professor of rhetoric at Milan (then the western capital) and imperial panegyrist, arriving in late 384 (WP). SEP compresses this as "In 383 he moved to Milan". [source: SEP-Aug §1; WP-Augustine_of_Hippo]
- In Milan he dismissed his partner to make way for an advantageous marriage, arranged by Monnica (the fiancée was under age; the marriage never happened). SEP: what is unusual is not the behaviour but that he mentions it and reflects on the pain. [source: SEP-Aug §1, §9; WP-Augustine_of_Hippo; IEP-Aug]
- Influences in Milan: Bishop Ambrose (339–397), who taught him allegorical exegesis, and Neoplatonically inclined Christians (Simplicianus). [source: SEP-Aug §1; WP-Augustine_of_Hippo]
- "Books of the Platonists" (Confessions 7.13), read in 386 in the Latin translation of Marius Victorinus. Which texts is debated: most scholars now accept some treatises of Plotinus (e.g. Enneads I.6, I.2, V.1, VI.4–5) plus a selection of Porphyry. [source: SEP-Aug §4] IEP-Aug says "most scholars agree" they were translations of Plotinus' Enneads. WRITER: say "works of Plotinus and probably Porphyry".
- Conversion in a garden in Milan, summer 386 (SEP); late August 386 (WP citing Brown 2000; IEP "around August"), aged 31. Under a fig tree he heard a child's voice chanting "tolle lege, tolle lege" ("take and read"), opened Paul's letters at random and read Romans 13:13–14 ("not in rioting and drunkenness, not in chambering and wantonness…"); his friend Alypius was converted with him. The model was Antony of Egypt, converted by a single verse. [source: IEP-Aug; WP-Augustine_of_Hippo; SEP-Aug §7.4] (Confessions 8.12.29.)
- He resigned his chair; winter 386/7 at Cassiciacum near Milan (estate of Verecundus) with Monnica, Adeodatus, Alypius and friends, where he wrote the first surviving works, the Cassiciacum dialogues. Baptized by Ambrose at Easter 387 (WP: Easter Vigil, 24–25 April 387) with Adeodatus and Alypius. [source: SEP-Aug §1; IEP-Aug; WP-Augustine_of_Hippo]
- "Vision of Ostia": shortly before Monnica's death, mother and son after a long conversation "reach a sudden insight into what contemplation of the transcendent God in eternal bliss must be like" (Confessions 9.24–25). [source: SEP-Aug §9]
- Ordained priest at Hippo Regius (modern Annaba, Algeria) in 391, "apparently against his will"; bishop c. 396 (SEP), or coadjutor bishop 395 and full bishop shortly after (WP). [source: SEP-Aug §1; WP-Augustine_of_Hippo]
- As bishop: controversies with Manichees, Donatists, Pelagians and pagans. [source: SEP-Aug §1]
- Donatists: claimed to be the pure African church of those steadfast in the last great persecution; from 405 subject to imperial anti-heresy laws, intensified after the conference of Carthage (411). Augustine developed a theory of religious coercion based on an intentionalist account of Christian love. [source: SEP-Aug §1] IEP-AugPol quotes him: those who are loved "ought to be both prevented from evil and compelled to good" (translator not named). [source: IEP-AugPol]
- Death: the Vandals (Arian Christians) under Gaiseric besieged Hippo from May/June 430 (siege lasted to August 431); Augustine fell ill about three months into the siege and died 28 August 430, having had the penitential Psalms hung on his walls; Possidius of Calama, his biographer, was present. He is said to have died with a word of Plotinus on his lips (Possidius, Vita 28.11, after Enneads I.4.7). [source: SEP-Aug §1; IEP-Aug; WP-Augustine_of_Hippo; WP-Siege_of_Hippo_Regius]
- Output: about 100 books, 300 letters and 600 sermons survive; the *Retractationes* (to 428) reviews his books chronologically. [source: SEP-Aug §2] (WP gives different sermon figures; use SEP.)
- Recognized as a Doctor of the Church in 1298 (Boniface VIII). [source: WP-Augustine_of_Hippo]

## 3. Berber/African identity (a debate: present as such)
- WP: "Scholars generally agree that Augustine and his family were Berbers … but were heavily Romanized, speaking only Latin at home"; Monnica is assumed Berber from her name (Brett and Fentress: "Monnica is the only Berber name commonly used in English"); his first language was likely Latin. He calls himself African in passing (Ponticianus "a country man of ours, insofar as being African"; Apuleius "as an African is better known to us Africans"). Thagaste was a Roman-Berber municipium mainly inhabited by romanized Berbers. [source: WP-Augustine_of_Hippo; WP-Thagaste; WP-Saint_Monica]
- SEP (Tornau) is more cautious: Monnica's name "may suggest a Berber or Punic origin", but "there is little in Augustine's work to indicate an African rather than a Roman identity" (but see van Oort 2023); there was a distinct African Christian tradition, of which he was part, and whose Donatist variety he fought (Wilhite 2017). [source: SEP-Aug §1]
- Reception: postcolonial approaches and Black and African theology either highlight his African identity or read him as a representative of Roman imperialism (Wilhite 2013; on the Maghreb, Gronemann 2021). [source: SEP-Aug §11] Justo González reads him as a "mestizo", formed by both Roman and African heritage. [source: WS-11]
- WRITER: say "a Roman African from Numidia, probably of Berber descent on his mother's side; how much that mattered to him is debated".

## 4. Works and the Confessions as a new genre
- Cassiciacum dialogues (386/7): *Contra Academicos*, *De beata vita*, *De ordine*, *Soliloquia*; then *De libero arbitrio* (begun 388, finished perhaps 395), *De magistro* (388–391), *De vera religione* (389–391). He then abandoned the dialogue form. [source: SEP-Aug §2]
- Anti-Manichean *De natura boni* (399): evil a privation of goodness, not a substance. [source: SEP-Aug §2]
- *Confessions* (Confessiones), 13 books, c. 396–400 (SEP; WP 397–400). SEP: "probably his most original work … 'philosophy in autobiography' (Mann 2014) rather than an autobiography in a modern sense"; "unique in the ancient literary tradition but greatly influenced the modern tradition of autobiography; … philosophy from a first-person perspective". Books 1–9 narrate his life to Monnica's death; books 10–13 treat memory, time and creation (exegesis of Genesis). [source: SEP-Aug intro, §2; IEP-Aug; WP-Confessions_(Augustine)] "Many have described the Confessions as the first autobiography." [source: WP-Confessions_(Augustine)] WRITER: hedge "first autobiography".
- The pear theft (Book 2): he and friends stole pears they did not want and mostly threw away; he analyses it as sin for its own sake, linked to Adam and Eve; sin as disordered love of lesser goods. [source: IEP-Aug; WP-Augustine_of_Hippo]
- *De doctrina christiana* (396/7–426/7), *De trinitate* (399–c. 419/426), *De Genesi ad litteram* (401/2–416), *De civitate dei* (begun 412, completed 426). [source: SEP-Aug §2]
- Augustine had no medieval/modern distinction between philosophy and theology; Christianity is "the true philosophy". [source: SEP-Aug §3]
- "Faith seeking understanding" (a label used since Anselm; cf. De trinitate 15.2 "fides quaerit, intellectus invenit"): faith prior in time, understanding higher in value. Favourite proof-text: Old Latin Isaiah 7:9, "nisi credideritis, non intellegetis" ("unless you believe, you will not understand"). [source: SEP-Aug §5.3; IEP-Aug]

## 5. Answers to scepticism, and "si fallor, sum"
- *Contra Academicos* (386), his earliest surviving work, written to escape the "despair" of thinking truth unattainable; his knowledge of scepticism came from Cicero's *Academica* and *Hortensius*, not from a living school. [source: SEP-Aug §5.1]
- Strategy: he need only show some knowledge immune to doubt: (1) self-referential knowledge; (2) knowledge of how things appear to me ("something appears white to me" even if I do not know it is white, C. Acad. 3.26); (3) logical and mathematical truths, valid whether awake or dreaming. Kirwan (1989) was unimpressed; Nawar (2019) defends. He also told a story that the sceptical Academics were secret Platonists. [source: SEP-Aug §5.1]
- The "cogito-like" argument: City of God 11.26, "si enim fallor, sum" ("for if I am mistaken, I exist"). Used from his earliest works (De beata vita 7) to his latest (De trinitate 15.21); also De libero arbitrio 2.7, Confessions 7.5, Enchiridion 7.20. [source: SEP-Aug §5.1; WP-Cogito,_ergo_sum]
- ARGUMENT, step by step (reconstruction from SEP-Aug §5.1 and City of God 11.26 as quoted in WP):
  1. The sceptic objects: "What if you are mistaken?"
  2. Suppose I am mistaken in believing that I exist.
  3. Only something that exists can be mistaken.
  4. So if I am mistaken, I exist ("si fallor, sum").
  5. So whether or not I am mistaken, I exist; my belief "I exist" cannot be false.
  6. By the same reasoning I know that I live and that I know this; and (Augustine adds) I love this existing and knowing, and I know that I will. Since I am as certain of my willing as of my existing, my volitions are mine and I am responsible for them (against Manichean dualism, which blamed an alien evil substance in the soul).
  - Objection (ancient sceptic, per SEP): subjective or formal certainties cannot justify claims to objective knowledge of the external world. Reply (SEP): that was not Augustine's aim; it is enough that, even with maximal concessions about the senses, an inner area of certainty remains, from which the ascent to God and Truth can begin.
- Uses: in De libero arbitrio 2 the certainty of existence, life and understanding becomes the first step of an ascent to God as immutable truth; in De trinitate it opens the analysis of the mind as image of the Trinity. [source: SEP-Aug §5.1]
- Comparison with Descartes: SEP says it is "similar to (and probably inspired) the Cogito of Descartes (Matthews 1992; Menn 1998; Fuchs 2010)", but lacks the Cartesian systematic role: "there is no attempt to found a coherent and comprehensive philosophy on it". [source: SEP-Aug §5.1] In the seventeenth century the cogito "was soon recognized as an Augustinian idea". [source: SEP-Aug §11]
- Descartes' own verdict, letter to Andreas Colvius (1640), Cottingham et al. translation: he went to the town library to read the passage and found Augustine "does use it to prove the certainty of our existence. He goes on to show that there is a certain likeness of the Trinity in us… I, on the other hand, use the argument to show that this I which is thinking is an immaterial substance with no bodily element. These are two very different things. In itself it is such a simple and natural thing to infer that one exists from the fact that one is doubting that it could have occurred to any writer." [source: WP-Cogito,_ergo_sum, citing CSMK vol. III p. 159]
- Postmodernist thinkers (Marion, Milbank) have set Augustine's love-centred self against the "isolation of the Cartesian subject". [source: SEP-Aug §11] Cross-reference: the Avicenna "flying man" is another precursor (WP-Cogito; Periergia overview).

## 6. Illumination
- A non-empiricist theory of knowledge, probably a Neoplatonic reading of Plato's recollection: true knowledge needs first-hand insight; intelligible truths (mathematics, logic, basic moral truths) cannot be abstracted from the senses or passed on by a teacher's words; we see them "for ourselves" by a standard found within. God is the condition and criterion of this insight. [source: SEP-Aug §5.2]
- Two images: the "inner teacher" (Christ present in the soul; De magistro, c. 390) and illumination (Soliloquia 1.12–15; De trinitate 12.24), from Plato's Analogy of the Sun (Republic 508a–509b): as the sun makes objects visible to the eye, God makes intelligibles visible to reason. [source: SEP-Aug §5.2]
- De magistro's puzzle: we learn only through signs, but we cannot understand a sign unless we already know what it signifies; so nothing is learned from signs alone; Christ, the inner teacher, teaches. [source: IEP-Aug]
- Later (De trinitate 12.15.24) he explicitly rejects Platonic recollection in favour of illumination within a theory of creation; whether the early Augustine believed in the soul's pre-existence is unclear. [source: SEP-Aug §5.2; IEP-Aug]
- Interpretation debate: illumination should not be read as making humans passive recipients; we "consult" the inner truth (Gilson 1943; Schumacher 2011 on medieval and modern debates). [source: SEP-Aug §5.2] Illumination "is generally conceived of as distinctively Christian, distinctively medieval, and distinctively Augustinian", though it has ancient origins. [source: SEP-Ill]

## 7. Time and memory (Confessions XI)
- Context: an exegesis of Genesis ("In the beginning"); God creates time with changeable things and is himself timelessly eternal, so there was no time "before" creation (Conf. 11.13.15 in IEP numbering). The distinction of eternity and time is Platonic (Timaeus 37c–38b; Plotinus, Enneads III.7). [source: SEP-Aug §10; IEP-Aug]
- Augustine's purpose is ethical and first-personal rather than a definition of physical time. [source: SEP-Aug §10]
- ARGUMENT, step by step (from SEP-Aug §10 and IEP-Aug §4d):
  1. Time has three parts: past, present, future.
  2. The past no longer exists; the future does not yet exist.
  3. The present has no extension: any "present" year, day or hour divides into parts past and future, so the present shrinks to a durationless point.
  4. So none of the three parts seems to exist; yet we do speak of long and short times and we measure them.
  5. What we measure cannot be the non-existent past or future, nor the extensionless present.
  6. Therefore past and future exist in the mind, as present memory of the past, present attention to the present and present expectation of the future (Conf. 11.20.26).
  7. Evidence: reciting a psalm or poem known by heart, the words pass from expectation through attention into memory (11.28.38).
  8. So time is "a distention of the soul" (distentio animi, 11.26.33), the nearest the book comes to a definition (perhaps echoing Plotinus, Enneads III.7.11).
  9. Ethical close: against the soul's distension in time Augustine sets Paul's "straining forward" (Philippians 3:12–14), turning from temporal dispersion to God's eternity.
  - Objection the writer can use: if time is in the mind, what was measured before there were minds, and how do different minds share one time? (WRITER: present this as an obvious objection, not as one attributed to a named critic; none was verified.) SEP notes that outside the Confessions Augustine discusses cosmic or physical time, so the "subjectivist" reading may overstate his view: Husserl, Heidegger and Ricœur paid it "enormous" attention, "some of whom credited Augustine with their own subjectivist understanding of time". [source: SEP-Aug §10, §11]
- Ricœur's *Time and Narrative* uses Confessions XI extensively. [source: WP-Confessions_(Augustine)] Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* opens with Augustine on language learning. [source: SEP-Aug §11]
- Memory: Confessions 10 is an ascent through memory (Conf. 10.8–38). [source: SEP-Aug §4]

## 8. Evil as privation (against the Manichees)
- The question "unde malum?" (where does evil come from?) haunted him from youth (Conf. 7.7). The Manichean answer (two principles, good and evil) freed God of responsibility but compromised his omnipotence. After the Platonist books he adopted the Neoplatonic view (Plotinus, Enneads I.8) that evil is not a substance but a privation or corruption of good, developed mainly against the Manichees. [source: SEP-Aug §7.5; IEP-Aug]
- ARGUMENT, step by step (from SEP-Aug §7.5 and IEP-Aug §3b):
  1. God, who is wholly good, created everything that exists.
  2. So everything that exists is good "in its degree" (degrees of being and goodness).
  3. Created things, made from nothing, are changeable and so can lose goodness.
  4. Evil is this loss: a corruption or lack of the good a thing should have; only corruption is evil, the nature itself remains good.
  5. Evil is parasitic: illness is an absence of health, possible only while some health remains; remove all health and the illness goes too (De natura boni 6).
  6. So evil is not a thing God made, and no evil substance rivals God (against the Manichees).
  7. Moral evil is evil will; an evil will has no "efficient" cause but only a "deficient" one, the will's own turning away from God (City of God 12.6–9). Thought experiment: two people of equal disposition face the same temptation; one yields, one resists; the difference can only be the will's free choice (City of God 12.6).
  - Objection: some evils seem positive, not mere lacks, e.g. pain (Todd Calder: pain is bad in itself, not merely an absence of pleasure), and cruelty or malice. [source: WP-Absence_of_good] Ancient objection: the Manichee Fortunatus argued God must still be implicated in evil (public debate with Augustine). [source: WP-Augustinian_theodicy]
  - Reply (privation theorists): the badness of pain lies in disorder or malfunction of the organism, a lack of proper order. [source: WP-Absence_of_good]
- Augustine rejected Plotinus' equation of matter with evil: formless matter is a created capacity to receive form. [source: SEP-Aug §7.5]
- Augustine's wider response to evil (IEP lists): free will; humility as cure for pride; aesthetic order (God uses evil like an ugly square in a mosaic, De ordine 1.1.2); suffering as just punishment; suffering as purification (anticipating "soul-making"); plenitude of lesser goods. [source: IEP-Aug §3b]
- The label "Augustinian theodicy" is John Hick's, in *Evil and the God of Love* (1966), contrasted with an "Irenaean" theodicy. [source: WP-Augustinian_theodicy]
- Julian of Aeclanum later accused Augustine of crypto-Manicheism; SEP: Julian had to ignore privatio boni to make the charge. [source: SEP-Aug §4]

## 9. Free will, grace, the Pelagian controversy, original sin
- Augustine's notion of will (voluntas) is broader than Aristotle's boulēsis or Stoic prohairesis; it has been claimed he "discovered" the will (Dihle 1982; Kahn 1988), contested by Frede 2011, who stresses his Stoic debts. [source: SEP-Aug §7.4] (Debate.)
- *De libero arbitrio* (On Free Choice of the Will): free will is a good but only an "intermediate" good, which can be misused, and so is the source of moral evil; book 1 says "nothing is as completely in our will as will itself". [source: SEP-Aug §7.4]
- Divided will (Confessions 8): before his conversion he was torn between two wills, both his own; habit had become a kind of necessity; grace healed the will in the garden. [source: SEP-Aug §7.4]
- Turning point c. 395–397, *Ad Simplicianum* 1.2 (on Romans 9, Jacob and Esau): even the beginning of faith is a gift of grace ("prevenient", not "cooperative" grace); humanity after the Fall is a "lump of sin" (massa peccati), from which God justly saves some. Augustine himself later wrote (Retractationes 2.1): "I tried hard to maintain the free decision of the human will, but the grace of God was victorious" (SEP-FWF's translation; Latin "vicit dei gratia"). [source: SEP-Aug §7.6; SEP-FWF; IEP-Aug]
- Original sin as personally imputable guilt justifying damnation is new with Ad Simplicianum (Flasch 1995); WP: Augustine was "the first to add the concept of inherited guilt (reatus)" and the first author to use the phrase "original sin", against the Pelagians. [source: SEP-Aug §7.6; WP-Original_sin]
- WP: Augustine's reading of Romans 5:12 relied on a Latin rendering (introduced by Ambrosiaster) of "because all sinned" as "in whom [Adam] all sinned"; Eastern Orthodoxy rejects inherited guilt. [source: WP-Original_sin] WRITER: attribute to "many scholars"/present briefly.
- Pelagius, a British ascetic; Augustine became aware of the movement c. 412; Pelagius held humans by nature free and able not to sin. Caelestius was condemned at Carthage in 411; Pelagius was acquitted at the Synod of Diospolis (Lod) after disowning Caelestius' teachings; Augustine and African bishops secured condemnation as heresy in 418 (Council of Carthage). [source: SEP-Aug §1; WP-Pelagianism]
- Last decade: bitter debate with Julian of Aeclanum, who accused him of Manicheism and denying free will; Augustine's unfinished last work, *Contra Iulianum opus imperfectum*. [source: SEP-Aug §1, §2]
- Augustine's formula: Adam had the ability not to sin; original sin turned it into an inability not to sin; grace restores the ability not to sin in this life and will give the inability to sin in the next (City of God 22.30; De correptione et gratia 33). Augustine accepts the principle of alternative possibilities only for the first humans. [source: SEP-Aug §7.4]
- Predestination ("double predestination"; the term was coined by Gottschalk in the ninth century); no one can know in this life whether they are among the saved; North African monks used a version of the Stoic "Lazy Argument" against rebuke, answered in De correptione et gratia (426/7). [source: SEP-Aug §7.6, §11]
- Foreknowledge (City of God 5.9–10): God foreknows our volitions exactly as what they are, our own volitions, not compulsions; this argument is independent of grace. [source: SEP-Aug §7.6]
- Debate on development: shifts emphasized by Flasch 1995 and Lettieri 2001; continuity by Carol Harrison 2006. Whether grace is "irresistible" is partly an un-Augustinian question (Wetzel 1992), though some late texts present it coercively. [source: SEP-Aug §7.6]
- Legacy: Luther, Calvin (double predestination), the Jansenists. [source: SEP-Aug §11]

## 10. The City of God, peace, just war, slavery
- Prompted by Alaric's sack of Rome in 410, which pagans blamed on the abandonment of the old gods, but "by no means just a response"; begun 412, completed 426; 22 books. Books 1–10 refute Roman religion and Greek (esp. Platonic) philosophy as paths to happiness; books 11–22 tell the origin, course and ends of the two cities. [source: SEP-Aug §1, §2, §8; WP-The_City_of_God]
- Two cities defined by two loves (14.28): the city of God loves God even to contempt of self; the earthly city loves self even to contempt of God. Neither is identical with the Church or the state; both are mixed in history and separated only at the end. The city of God is a "resident alien" (peregrinus) in this world. [source: SEP-Aug §8; IEP-Aug]
- Everyone seeks peace; peace as ordered subordination (City of God 19.13); earthly peace is used by citizens of the heavenly city and enjoyed by the earthly; Christians can cooperate for it (Jeremiah's advice to the exiles in Babylon, 19.26). [source: SEP-Aug §8; IEP-Aug]
- Against Cicero's definition of a commonwealth as bound by justice (De re publica 1.39), Augustine defines a people as "a coming together of many rational beings joined in like-minded communion around what it loves" (19.24); there was never true justice in Rome on Cicero's terms. [source: SEP-Aug §8; IEP-Aug]
- No empire is sacred; the two-cities doctrine "deliberately precludes any promotion of the emperor or the empire to a providential and quasi-sacred rank". Medieval "political Augustinianism" later projected the two cities onto Church and State. [source: SEP-Aug §8, §11]
- Just war: war results from sin and lust for power; Letter 138 rebuts the claim that Christianity demands impracticable pacifism; a fully just war would be waged out of love for the enemy's good, "utopian" in a fallen world; wars are relatively just if defensive and properly declared (cf. Cicero, De officiis 1.36–37) or commanded by legitimate authority (Contra Faustum 22.74–78 per SEP; WP gives 22.69–76). Intention is the main test ("not a bodily action, but an inward disposition"). Ambrose, influenced by Cicero, preceded him; Augustine never wrote a systematic doctrine. [source: SEP-Aug §8; WP-Just_war_theory; IEP-AugPol]
- Slavery: "unnatural domination of humans over humans", a stain of postlapsarian life, yet an evil put to good use when it secures order (19.15); "Like the vast majority of ancient Christian theologians, Augustine has little or no interest in social reform" (Rist 1994). [source: SEP-Aug §8] City of God 19.15 (Dods): God "did not intend that His rational creature, who was made in His image, should have dominion over anything but the irrational creation"; slavery came by sin, and the apostle "admonishes slaves to be subject to their masters". [source: WS-10, quoting Dods translation pages]
- Private property and the state as institutions of the fallen condition. [source: IEP-AugPol]

## 11. Women, sexuality (with the debate)
- SEP: "Outright misogyny is rare in Augustine", but he took women's natural and social subordination for granted; Eve was subordinate even in paradise; marriage under the Fall is, for wives, a kind of slavery to be accepted with humility (as Monnica did). [source: SEP-Aug §9]
- Against much patristic consensus he held that woman too is made in God's image, because the image lies in the intellectual soul, not the gendered body (De Genesi ad litteram 3.22.34), and that sexual difference will persist in resurrected bodies (City of God 22.17); but in De trinitate 12 he maps man/woman onto higher/lower reason, so woman is God's image "qua human being, but not qua woman", giving social inequality metaphysical significance (Stark 2007). [source: SEP-Aug §9]
- Women in the early dialogues: Monnica takes part in the Cassiciacum dialogues and is credited with a philosophy of her own (De ordine 1.31–32; 2.45); the dialogues' inclusion of women, teenagers and the illiterate appeals to the "Philosophy for All" movement (Kenyon 2021). [source: SEP-Aug §3, §9, §11]
- Sexuality: concupiscence is a general term for disordered volition, not specifically sexual; Adam and Eve's sin was disobedience rooted in pride; its punishment is the disobedience of our own selves, shown most obviously in uncontrollable sexual arousal (City of God 14.15–16). Unlike most earlier Christian writers, he thought there would have been sex and procreation in paradise, without concupiscence. After the Fall, concupiscence accompanies every procreation and transmits original sin; it can be put to good use in marriage aimed at children. [source: SEP-Aug §9]
- Victims of rape who did not consent remain without sin (City of God 1.16–28), part of his theory that only the will's consent is culpable. [source: SEP-Aug §7.4]
- Debate: "energetic criticism" by Elaine Pagels (*Adam, Eve and the Serpent*, 1988/89) partly for the impact on Christian sexual morality; "moderate defense" by Lamberigts (2000); also E. Clark 1996, Brown 1988 (*The Body and Society*). Opposed in the 20th–21st centuries from "humanist, liberal, feminist" standpoints. Hannah Arendt (dissertation on Augustine's concept of love) blamed him for making love of neighbour an instrument of personal happiness. [source: SEP-Aug intro, §9, §11]

## 12. Boethius
- Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius. Dates differ by source: SEP-Boe (rev. 2026) born c. 475–7, died "526?" ("probably in 526"); SEP-Med (same author) 476–c. 525; WP and IEP c. 480–524. WRITER: write "born about 475–480, executed in 524 or soon after (some scholars now argue 526)". [source: SEP-Boe §1; SEP-Med §1.3; WP-Boethius; IEP-Boe]
- Born into the Roman aristocracy (the Anicii) around the time the last western emperor, Romulus Augustulus, was deposed (476); orphaned and raised by Quintus Aurelius Memmius Symmachus, whose daughter Rusticiana he married; consul 510; his two sons were both consuls in 522. [source: SEP-Boe §1; WP-Boethius]
- Lived under Theoderic (Theodoric the Great), an Ostrogoth educated at Constantinople, king in Italy 493–526 (ruling from Ravenna), an Arian Christian who let the Roman families keep their traditions. [source: SEP-Boe §1; WP-Theodoric_the_Great; IEP-Boe]
- Became Theoderic's Master of Offices (magister officiorum); fell out with courtiers, probably by attacking corruption; after defending the ex-consul Albinus against a charge of treasonous correspondence with the emperor Justin I (accuser Cyprianus), he was accused of treason and magic, imprisoned (in or near Pavia; WP names the Ager Calventianus) and executed; Symmachus was executed soon after. Methods of execution vary in the sources. [source: SEP-Boe §1; WP-Boethius]
- Translation project: announced (c. 516, second commentary on On Interpretation) a plan to translate and comment on all of Plato and Aristotle, cut short by execution; in practice he translated Aristotle's logic and Porphyry's *Isagoge*, wrote commentaries (two each on On Interpretation and the Isagoge, one on the Categories) and logic textbooks (categorical and hypothetical syllogisms, topical differentiae, division), plus arithmetic and music handbooks. No Plato translation is known. [source: SEP-Boe §1–2; WP-Boethius]
- His translations remained standard throughout the Middle Ages (except the lost Posterior Analytics); his logic was practically the only Aristotle in Latin until the twelfth century. [source: SEP-Boe §8; WP-Boethius]
- Porphyry's three questions about universals (do they exist or are they mere concepts? if they exist, bodily or not? separate or in sensibles?) reached the Middle Ages through Boethius; Boethius answers through abstraction. [source: SEP-Boe §2]
- Theological treatises (opuscula sacra): apply Aristotelian logic to the Trinity and Christology, a method "Augustine had anticipated but not developed". [source: SEP-Boe §4]
- Whether he studied in Alexandria (Courcelle) is doubted (Moorhead; IEP). [source: WP-Boethius; IEP-Boe; SEP-Boe §1]

### The Consolation of Philosophy
- A prosimetrum (prose with verse interludes): dialogue between the imprisoned Boethius and Lady Philosophy; it contains no explicit reference to Christianity, which is an interpretive problem. [source: SEP-Boe §1, §5]
- Book I: his fall; Philosophy's task, to show he has no good reason to complain and that it is false that the wicked prosper. Book II: fortune's fickleness, the "wheel of Fortune". [source: SEP-Boe §5; WP-On_the_Consolation_of_Philosophy; IEP-Boe]
- ARGUMENT on fortune (Books II–III, from SEP-Boe §5):
  1. The goods of fortune (riches, status, power, sensual pleasure) are of limited value; some (friends, family) have real value.
  2. True goods are virtue and sufficiency, which is what seekers of riches and power really want.
  3. Boethius has lost no true good, and keeps his family.
  4. Stronger (III.10, after the hymn III m.9 modelled on Plato's Timaeus): perfect good and perfect happiness are God.
  5. So true happiness cannot be touched by changes of fortune.
  - Book IV (after Plato's Gorgias): the wicked are powerless, since everyone wants happiness = the good, which the wicked fail to get; evil is a privation of being, so the wicked punish themselves, becoming lower than human. Objection (Boethius the character, IV.5): what wise man would prefer exile and disgrace to wealth and power at home? Reply: providence (God's unified view) unfolds in time as fate; apparent injustices serve hidden good purposes.
- ARGUMENT on foreknowledge and free will (V.3–6, from SEP-Boe §6):
  1. God foresees everything infallibly.
  2. If God foresees that I will do X, I will necessarily do X (otherwise God's foreknowledge would be mere opinion, which it is "wicked" to believe of God).
  3. So nothing we do or will is free; then rewards, punishments and prayer are pointless.
  - Analysis: on one reading the argument commits a modal scope fallacy ("necessarily, if God knows X, X" does not give "X is necessary"); on Marenbon's reading (2013) it is not fallacious: knowing a future event means judging it fixed, so if it could be otherwise God would hold a false belief.
  - Philosophy's reply: (a) the Modes of Cognition principle: "everything that is known is grasped, not according to its own power, but rather according to the capacity of those who know it"; (b) God is eternal, and eternity is not endless duration but "the whole, simultaneous and perfect possession of unbounded life" (V.6, Marenbon's wording in SEP); (c) so God knows our future acts as we know present ones, as present, without causing them; (d) two necessities: "simple" (the sun rises; humans die) and "conditional" (if I am seen walking, I am necessarily walking), the latter not constraining freedom; in themselves future events remain free.
  - Most interpreters read this as making God atemporal; Marenbon reads it as Aristotelian necessity of the present; Noble (2024) and Rogers (2024) argue he was not an incompatibilist. A twist at the end: God's knowledge "sets the measure for all things", which may seem to make God the determiner of events. [source: SEP-Boe §6]
- The eternity definition "became the starting-point for almost every later medieval discussion of God and time" and the standard definition in the universities; Chase (2014) compares it to block-time theories. Aquinas adopted the Boethian solution. [source: SEP-Boe §6, §8; SEP-FWF]
- Interpretation debate: traditional reading (Philosophy authoritative); Relihan's ironic reading (philosophy insufficient without faith; genre of Menippean satire) judged "too extreme" by Marenbon; Marenbon's own modest revisionism (Philosophy right but incomplete); Magee (2024). [source: SEP-Boe §7]
- Influence: translated into nearly every medieval vernacular and into Greek and Hebrew; translators include King Alfred (Old English), Jean de Meun (Old French), Chaucer (Middle English, *Boece*), and Elizabeth I; echoes in Dante, Chaucer's *Troilus and Criseyde*, Christine de Pizan. "Along with Augustine and Aristotle, he is the fundamental philosophical and theological author in the Latin tradition." [source: SEP-Boe §8; WP-On_the_Consolation_of_Philosophy]
- Venerated as a martyr (Saint Severinus) at Pavia (cult confirmed 1883), buried in San Pietro in Ciel d'Oro, where Augustine's remains also lie; his martyr status is "dubious". [source: WP-Boethius]

## 13. Legacy (Augustine)
- "The greatest influence on medieval Latin philosophy, as well as, by some chronologies, the founder of the tradition". [source: SEP-Med] Often listed as the first medieval philosopher, but SEP prefers "a Christian philosopher of late antiquity" who did not live in a "medieval" world. [source: SEP-Aug intro]
- Anselm's proof develops De libero arbitrio 2; Aquinas had little interest in his Platonism; Descartes; Jansenists; 20th century: Husserl, Heidegger, Ricœur, Arendt, Wittgenstein. [source: SEP-Aug §11]

## Debates
- Did Augustine "discover" the will? Dihle 1982 and Kahn 1988 yes; Frede 2011 stresses Stoic precedents. [source: SEP-Aug §7.4]
- Augustine's cogito and Descartes: similar and probably an inspiration (Matthews 1992; Menn 1998) versus different in purpose and systematic role (SEP; Descartes himself in 1640). [source: SEP-Aug §5.1; WP-Cogito,_ergo_sum]
- Plotinus or Porphyry as main source of the "Platonist books": a 20th-century dispute, now mostly settled by compromise (both). [source: SEP-Aug §4]
- Continuity or rupture in his doctrine of grace (Flasch, Lettieri versus C. Harrison). [source: SEP-Aug §7.6]
- Augustine's African/Berber identity (WP "generally agree… Berbers"; SEP cautious; postcolonial readings both ways; González's "mestizo"). [source: WP-Augustine_of_Hippo; SEP-Aug §1, §11; WS-11]
- Sexuality and women: Pagels' critique versus Lamberigts' moderate defence; SEP: misogyny rare but subordination assumed and given metaphysical weight. [source: SEP-Aug §9]
- Slavery: SEP (after Rist) says he had "little or no interest in social reform"; City of God 19.15 says slavery is not natural but a result of sin, yet tells slaves to serve; scholars debate whether this is acceptance or a theological reframing (no named scholarly position verified beyond Rist/SEP). [source: SEP-Aug §8; WS-10]
- Religious coercion of Donatists: SEP describes his theory as grounded in an intentionalist account of love; IEP-AugPol raises the question whether compelling people to do good can produce the change of heart required. [source: SEP-Aug §1; IEP-AugPol]
- Time: subjectivist reading (Husserl, Heidegger, Ricœur) versus SEP's warning that Confessions XI is ethical/first-personal and Augustine elsewhere treats cosmic time. [source: SEP-Aug §10–11]
- Boethius on foreknowledge: atemporal-God reading (majority) versus Marenbon's necessity-of-the-present reading; incompatibilist (majority) versus Noble 2024 and Rogers 2024. Consolation as straightforward, ironic (Relihan) or modestly revisionist (Marenbon). Whether the argument of V.3 is a scope fallacy. [source: SEP-Boe §6–7]
- "Medieval" or "late antique"? Augustine is "often listed as the first medieval philosopher" but best read as a late-antique thinker (SEP). [source: SEP-Aug intro; SEP-Med]
- Tertullian: "What has Athens…" is often taken as anti-philosophy; it is a polemic against heretics' use of philosophy, and *credo quia absurdum* is an Enlightenment invention (Peter Harrison 2017). [source: WP-Tertullian; WP-Credo_quia_absurdum; WS-2]
