## Background: the word and the idea before Husserl
- In its root sense "phenomenology" is the study of phenomena, appearances [source: SEP-PH §3].
- The Latin term "Phenomenologia" was introduced by Christoph Friedrich Oetinger in 1736; the German term was then used by Johann Heinrich Lambert, a follower of Christian Wolff; Kant used it occasionally, as did Fichte; in 1807 Hegel wrote *Phänomenologie des Geistes* (*Phenomenology of Spirit*); by 1889 Brentano used the term for "descriptive psychology"; "from there Edmund Husserl took up the term" [source: SEP-PH §3].
- Lambert's main philosophical work is *Neues Organon* (1764), which studied rules for distinguishing subjective from objective appearances and "contains one of the first appearances of the term phenomenology" [source: WP-JHL]. (Lambert, Swiss polymath 1728–1777; Kant meant to dedicate the *Critique of Pure Reason* to him [source: WP-JHL].)
- So the 18th-century meaning was "the theory of appearances fundamental to empirical knowledge, especially sensory appearances" [source: SEP-PH §3]. Hegel's book has the word in its title but is a different project (cross-ref *German Idealism*).
- SEP-PH adds that phenomenology "has been practiced, with or without the name, for many centuries": "When Hindu and Buddhist philosophers reflected on states of consciousness achieved in a variety of meditative states, they were practicing phenomenology" [source: SEP-PH §4]. (Useful for the global framing; it is Smith's broad usage, not a historical claim of influence.)
- Franz Brentano: born 16 January 1838, Marienberg am Rhein; ordained a Catholic priest 1864; professor at Würzburg (full professor 1873); struggled with the dogma of papal infallibility (1870) and withdrew from the priesthood and his chair; *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* vol. 1 published 1874; then professor at Vienna; died in Zurich 17 March 1917 [source: SEP-BR §1]. His 1862 dissertation was *On the Several Senses of Being in Aristotle* [source: SEP-BR §1].
- Brentano's students included Husserl, Meinong, Ehrenfels, Marty, Stumpf, Twardowski and Sigmund Freud [source: SEP-BR §1].
- Brentano's thesis: every mental phenomenon is directed toward some object, and only mental phenomena are so directed — "the hallmark of Brentano's descriptive psychology" [source: SEP-PH §3]. In context the famous passage offers one of six criteria for distinguishing mental from physical phenomena, to define the subject matter of scientific psychology, not a systematic theory of intentionality [source: SEP-BR §3].
- Interpretive caveat: the passage "clearly suggests" that the intentional object is part of the mental act ("immanentism"); some Brentano scholars now argue this immanent reading is too strong [source: SEP-BR]. His students criticised the unclear status of the intentional object (it seems to duplicate the object, so two people could never think of the same thing); Twardowski distinguished content from object, followed by Meinong and Husserl [source: SEP-BR].
- Husserl's own verdict (letter to Marvin Farber, 18 June 1937): Brentano's psychology "is anything but a science of intentionality, the proper problems of intentionality never dawned upon him" [source: SEP-HU §3]. Also Brentano held that psychology is the science on which logic should be based — the very psychologism Husserl attacked [source: SEP-HU §3].
- Heidegger first read Brentano's *On the Manifold Meaning of Being according to Aristotle* as a 17-year-old (IEP) / around 1909 (WP), and by his own account it inspired his lifelong question of being [source: IEP-H §1; WP-MH].

## Husserl: life
- Edmund Husserl, born 8 April 1859 in Proßnitz (Prostějov), Moravia, then Austrian Empire, now Czech Republic, "in a Jewish family" [source: SEP-HU §1].
- Studied astronomy, mathematics, physics and philosophy at Leipzig from 1876 (Wundt among his teachers); mathematics in Berlin from 1878 under Kronecker and Weierstrass; friend there of Tomáš Masaryk; PhD in mathematics at Vienna, January 1883, on the calculus of variations; briefly Weierstrass's assistant [source: SEP-HU §1]. So: trained as a mathematician.
- Attended Brentano's lectures in Vienna 1884–86 and switched to philosophy; habilitation at Halle under Carl Stumpf, *On the Concept of Number* (1887); Privatdozent at Halle for 14 years [source: SEP-HU §1].
- Converted to Protestantism in 1886 [source: SEP-HU §1] (IEP-HU: baptised in 1886 at Halle; married Malvine Steinschneider, also from Prossnitz's Jewish community, who was baptised before the wedding).
- 1891: *Philosophy of Arithmetic*, which "sought to provide a psychological foundation for mathematics"; reviewed critically by Frege (1894), "who accused Husserl of psychologism" [source: SEP-HU §1]. On whether Frege's review changed Husserl's mind, see Debates.
- *Logical Investigations* 1900–1901 (over 700 pages), Husserl's "breakthrough" to phenomenology; secured him a post at Göttingen, 1901–1916 (Extraordinarius, then Ordinarius from 1906) [source: SEP-HU §1, §2].
- Göttingen attracted Adolf Reinach, Alexandre Koyré, Edith Stein, Helmuth Plessner, Moritz Geiger and Roman Ingarden [source: SEP-HU §1].
- 1916: moved to Freiburg, taking Heinrich Rickert's chair [source: SEP-HU §1]. His son Wolfgang was killed at Verdun on 8 March 1916 [source: WP-EH].
- Assistants: Edith Stein 1916–18; Heidegger 1919–23 (WP-EH says 1920–23); Eugen Fink was his last assistant [source: SEP-HU §1, §3; WP-EH].
- Retired 1928, succeeded by Heidegger [source: SEP-HU §1].
- Paris lectures at the Sorbonne, 23 and 25 February 1929; expanded with Fink; published in French as *Méditations cartésiennes* (1931), translated by Gabrielle Peiffer and Emmanuel Levinas (with advice from Koyré); German text only in 1950 [source: WP-CM; SEP-HU §1].
- Under National Socialism Husserl, "due to his Jewish ancestry", was barred from official academic activity, lost his right to teach and publish, "and eventually also his German citizenship" [source: SEP-HU §1].
- 1935 lectures in Vienna and Prague became *The Crisis of European Sciences and Transcendental Phenomenology* (1936); only the first two parts appeared, in a Belgrade journal (*Philosophia*), because as a Jew he had no public platform in Germany [source: SEP-HU §1; WP-EH]. (WP-EH gives "Prague in 1935 and Vienna in 1936"; SEP-HU says both were 1935; the Vienna lecture "Philosophy and the Crisis of European Humanity" is dated 1935 in WP-EH's own link — use 1935.)
- Died in Freiburg 27 April 1938, of pleurisy after a fall in autumn 1937; Fink gave the eulogy [source: SEP-HU §1; WP-EH].
- Letter to Dietrich Mahnke, 4 May 1933: "the erection of a spiritual ghetto, into which I and my children … are to be driven … We are no longer to have the right to call ourselves German" [source: SEP-HU §1, quoting Husserl 1994: iii 491–2]. In the same letter he accused Heidegger of antisemitic sentiments [source: SEP-HU §1]; WP-HN quotes the letter (Kisiel & Sheehan, *Becoming Heidegger*, p. 413): Heidegger's "very public, very theatrical entrance into the Nazi Party on May 1 … his anti-Semitism, which he came to express with increasing vigor".
- Nachlass: at his death the research manuscripts, lecture manuscripts and unpublished books came to "some 45,000 pages" (SEP-HU) / "approximately 40,000 pages" of Gabelsberger shorthand (WP-EH, WP-VB) — give "40,000–45,000". Husserl thought the manuscripts the most important part of his work [source: SEP-HU §1].
- Herman Leo Van Breda (1911–1974), a young Belgian Franciscan writing a doctorate on Husserl, went to Freiburg in 1938 and smuggled the manuscripts and Husserl's library out of Germany; with the support of Belgian prime minister Paul-Henri Spaak they went via the Belgian embassy in Berlin and diplomatic couriers to Leuven [source: WP-VB; SEP-HU §1 "succeeded in smuggling Husserl's research manuscripts out of Germany and to safety in a monastery in Belgium"]. The Husserl Archives were set up at the Institute of Philosophy, Leuven, before WWII began; they moved the papers out of the university library a week before it burned on 17 May 1940; in 1943 they were dispersed for safety (incl. Postel Abbey) [source: SEP-HU §1; WP-VB]. (WP-EH dates the transfer "in 1939"; the trip was 1938 — say "1938–39".)
- Almost the entire first edition of *Experience and Judgment* (Prague, 1939) was destroyed by the Germans after they annexed Czechoslovakia [source: SEP-HU §1].
- Merleau-Ponty was the first outside visitor to the Leuven archives, April (spring) 1939, reading *Ideas II* and unpublished parts of *Crisis* [source: SEP-HU §1; SEP-MP §1].
- The critical edition, *Husserliana*, has so far 44 volumes and has forced revision of older readings (showing interests in embodiment, sociality, passivity, historicity, ethics) [source: SEP-HU §1].

## Husserl: Logical Investigations and the critique of psychologism
- Psychologism (the target): since perceiving, judging and knowing are psychical processes, logic would be part of psychology and logical laws psychological regularities [source: SEP-HU §2].
- Husserl's arguments: (1) it confuses "ideal and real laws … normative and causal regulation … logical and real necessity" (Hua 18/80 [Findlay 2001/I: 50]); (2) logic is exact and certain, psychology empirical and merely probable; (3) it confuses the act of knowing (a temporal process) with its object (an atemporal ideality): you and I can think the same Pythagorean theorem with different thought processes; if content reduced to thinking, no content could be repeated or shared, which would undermine science — so psychologism undermines itself as a scientific theory [source: SEP-HU §2].
- The second volume then turns to how ideal objectivity is known in subjective acts, offering "phenomenology" as a method [source: SEP-HU §2].
- In the first edition Husserl called phenomenology "descriptive psychology", which he later regretted as a serious mischaracterisation [source: SEP-HU §3].
- Intentionality analysed: matter vs quality of an act (what it is about and as what, versus believing/doubting/hoping); empty (signitive) vs fulfilled (intuitive) givenness — thinking the passport is in the drawer vs seeing it there; truth and evidence as the "fulfilment" of the meant by the given [source: SEP-HU §2].
- First edition of LI denied a pure ego ("quite unable to find this ego"); by the second edition (1913) he had changed his mind [source: SEP-HU §4].
- LI combined a Brentanian line in psychology (and William James, whose *Principles of Psychology* "greatly impressed Husserl") with a logical line from Bolzano and contemporaries including Frege [source: SEP-PH §3]. (SEP-PH dates James's *Principles* 1891; the book is normally dated 1890 — do not print a year from this source.)

## Husserl: Ideas I (1913), epoché, reduction, noesis/noema, transcendental turn
- *Ideas Pertaining to a Pure Phenomenology and to a Phenomenological Philosophy I* (1913) appeared in the first volume of Husserl's *Jahrbuch für Philosophie und phänomenologische Forschung* [source: IEP-HU; SEP-HU §1]. The *Jahrbuch* was co-edited by Husserl, Geiger, Pfänder, Reinach and Scheler [source: SEP-RE §1; SEP-SC §1].
- Ideas I endorsed transcendental idealism; "for many of the early phenomenologists, such a move constituted a betrayal of the core ideas of phenomenology", while Husserl complained they had not understood him [source: SEP-HU §1].
- Ingarden: "the idealistic tendencies apparent in volume I of the Ideen had been opposed by his disciples when the work was being studied during the seminars at Göttingen" (Ingarden 1962: 159); Stein (1932) said the disciples were surprised by the idealistic passages and called Fink a latecomer [source: IEP-HU].
- The epoché and the transcendental reduction are absent from LI and central from Ideas I on [source: SEP-HU §3].
- Natural attitude: in ordinary life (and positive science) we take the world's reality for granted [source: SEP-HU §3; IEP-PH §2b citing Husserl 1982 (Kersten) §30].
- Epoché, Kersten wording (Ideas I §32): "We put out of action the general positing which belongs to the essence of the natural attitude; we parenthesize everything which that positing encompasses with respect to being" [source: IEP-PH §2b].
- Earlier (*The Idea of Phenomenology*, 1907 lectures) the "epistemological reduction" gives the positing of a transcendent world "an index of indifference" [source: IEP-PH §2b].
- The epoché is not doubt or denial of the world: its aim is "to suspend or neutralize a certain dogmatic attitude towards reality"; "'The' world is not lost as a result of the epoché" (Hua 15/366) [source: SEP-HU §3]. In *Crisis* Husserl compares it to moving from two-dimensional to three-dimensional life (Hua 6/121 [Carr 1970: 119]) [source: SEP-HU §3].
- The transcendental reduction ("re-ducere", leading back) is the systematic analysis of the correlation between constituting subjectivity and constituted objectivity; the epoché is its first step [source: SEP-HU §3].
- "Principle of all principles" (Ideas I §24, Kersten): "every originary presentive intuition is a legitimizing source of cognition, that everything originally … offered to us in 'intuition' is to be accepted simply as what it is presented as being, but also only within the limits in which it is presented there" [source: IEP-PH §2b].
- Eidetic reduction: "free variation in imagination" to find invariant essential features [source: IEP-PH §2c].
- Noesis and noema: in Ideas I Husserl introduced two Greek words (from *noéō*, to perceive/think): noesis = the act, noema = its ideal content, "the object as intended" [source: SEP-PH §3; IEP-PH §3].
- Husserl explicitly links "transcendental" to Kant's legacy and "Copernican turn" [source: SEP-HU §3]; 1924: "In the phenomenological reduction, rightly understood, is predelineated in essence the marching route towards transcendental idealism" (Hua 8/181) [source: SEP-HU §3]. In the Fourth Cartesian Meditation transcendental phenomenology is said to be "nothing other than transcendental idealism" [source: WP-CM].
- But he rejected subjective idealism and phenomenalism: a material thing "is intrinsically not an experience but instead a totally different kind of being" (Hua 3/71); yet "it cannot have the sense that the world is independent of an actually existing consciousness" (Hua 36/78) [source: SEP-HU §3]. 1931 (in IEP-HU's quotation): "I still consider … every form of the usual philosophical realism nonsensical in principle, no less so than that idealism which it sets itself up against" [source: IEP-HU].
- Husserl rejected Kant's thing-in-itself as unintelligible [source: SEP-HU §3].
- Descartes: credited with a "radical first-personal turn" but Husserl "reject[ed] nearly all the well-known doctrinal content of the Cartesian philosophy" (Hua 1/43) [source: SEP-HU §3]. (Cross-ref *Descartes*.)

## Husserl: time-consciousness, embodiment, intersubjectivity, lifeworld
- *Lectures on the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time* (1905 lectures; edited by Heidegger, published 1928 "with only the slightest acknowledgment of Stein's work") [source: SEP-HU §1, §4; SEP-ST §1].
- Structure: primal impression + retention (of the just-elapsed phase) + protention (of the phase about to occur) [source: SEP-HU §4]. Example: hearing C–D–E, at E one still hears (does not remember or imagine) D and C as just past [source: SEP-HU §4].
- Retention/protention differ from memory and expectation (acts in their own right); protention explains surprise (wrong note in a melody; a stone wall behind a door) [source: SEP-HU §4].
- Static vs genetic phenomenology (from late WWI), "sedimentation"; later "generative" phenomenology (Steinbock's term) [source: SEP-HU §4].
- Leib (lived body) vs Körper (body as object); the body is the "zero-point" / "absolute here"; double-touch (one hand touching the other), later taken up by Merleau-Ponty [source: SEP-HU §5].
- Empathy (Einfühlung): from c.1905 (Hua 13); against Lipps's imitation/projection account; empathy is "like and unlike perception": we can perceive others' minds in expression, but never with the originality of self-experience — and this asymmetry is what makes the other *other* (Hua 1/139) [source: SEP-HU §5].
- Objectivity = what is in principle available to others; "Transcendental intersubjectivity is the absolute and only self-sufficient ontological foundation" (Hua 9/344) [source: SEP-HU §5]. *Cartesian Meditations* §61 criticises Scheler [source: SEP-HU §5]; the Fifth Meditation treats intersubjectivity [source: WP-EH].
- Lifeworld (Lebenswelt) in *Crisis*: the taken-for-granted world of experience; Galileo's "mathematizing reinterpretation of nature" led people to "take for true being what is actually a method" (Hua 6/52 [Carr 1970: 51]) [source: SEP-HU §6]. *Crisis* is in three parts; Part II discusses Galileo and introduces the lifeworld [source: WP-CR].
- Homeworld/alienworld (Heimwelt/Fremdwelt) in the 1930s; Husserl linked the Greek birth of theory to maritime contact with other cultures [source: SEP-HU §6].
- Husserl's dictum read as method: "The true method follows the nature of the things to be investigated and not our prejudices and preconceptions" (Hua 25/26) [source: SEP-HU §6].

## The reduction step by step (argument reconstruction)
Sources: SEP-HU §3; IEP-PH §2b–d; SEP-MP §3; IEP-HU.
- P1. Every science and everyday belief presupposes that objects can appear to us meaningfully; that presupposition cannot be examined by those same sciences [SEP-HU §3].
- P2. In the natural attitude we simply posit the world as existing independently, and this positing can distort description ("describing things as 'we know they must be' rather than how they are actually given") [IEP-PH §2b].
- Step 1 (epoché): suspend ("bracket", "put out of action") the general positing, and make no use of the sciences' claims — without denying or doubting the world [IEP-PH; SEP-HU].
- Step 2 (reduction proper): attend to the world purely as it is given, and to the acts that give it — the correlation of noesis and noema [SEP-HU §3].
- Step 3 (eidetic reduction): vary examples in imagination to grasp essential structures [IEP-PH §2c].
- C. Phenomenology becomes a presuppositionless, a priori science of how any object comes to be given — and, on Husserl's own reading, a transcendental idealism [SEP-HU §3].
- Motivations given by IEP-PH: epistemic modesty (immune to scepticism about the external world), the chance to analyse the natural attitude itself, purity of description.
- Objection 1 (idealism / "betrayal"): Göttingen realists (Ingarden, Stein, Conrad-Martius, Reinach's circle) held that the turn made the world dependent on consciousness; Ingarden and Philipse read Husserl as a reductive idealist for whom beings are created by consciousness [source: SEP-HU §3; IEP-HU; SEP-IN §1].
  - Reply: the reduction concerns meaning/givenness, not the creation of being (Carr, Crowell, Thomasson); Husserl rejected both realism and traditional idealism; "the world is not lost" [source: SEP-HU §3; IEP-HU]. Zahavi: Husserl "is an idealist" — "incontestable" — but the character of that idealism is contested; it is not a monism and claims only that mind and world cannot be divorced [source: SEP-HU §3].
- Objection 2 (Heidegger): our basic relation to the world is practical, not a belief that could be bracketed; any bracketing of the factual world is a mistake (Frede 2006, quoted by IEP-PH). Heidegger himself redefined the reduction as leading "back from the apprehension of a being … to the understanding of the being of this being" (Basic Problems, 1982 tr., p. 21) [source: IEP-PH §2d].
- Objection 3 (Merleau-Ponty): reflection is always situated in the world it reflects on, so "the most important lesson of the reduction is the impossibility of a complete reduction" (PP 14/lxxvii, Landes) [source: SEP-MP §3]. Merleau-Ponty did not reject the reduction but reinterpreted it as existential rather than idealist or naturalistic, describing "a transcendental field" rather than a transcendental ego [source: SEP-MP §3].
  - Reply/debate: Taminiaux reads the reduction as an open-ended set of approaches; Dermot Moran insists on its tie to transcendental consciousness; Joel Smith (2005) argues Merleau-Ponty keeps the epoché as method while rejecting the idealist framing [source: WS snippet summarising these — use only as "scholars disagree"].

## Edith Stein (1891–1942)
- Born 12 October 1891 in Breslau (now Wrocław, Poland) into a Jewish family; father died when she was two; mother ran the family lumberyard, seven children [source: SEP-ST §1].
- Studied psychology at Breslau (William Stern, Hönigswald); moved to Göttingen in 1913 after reading LI; member of the Göttingen Philosophical Society with Reinach, Ingarden and Conrad-Martius; attended Scheler's lectures [source: SEP-ST §1].
- Worked as a volunteer nurse in an Austro-Hungarian military hospital (typhus ward) from April 1915 [source: WP-ES].
- Doctorate summa cum laude, 1916 (Freiburg); dissertation partly published as *Zum Problem der Einfühlung* (*On the Problem of Empathy*, 1917); a historical part is lost [source: SEP-ST §1; WP-ES]. English: Waltraut Stein (1989) [source: SEP-ST bibliography].
- Husserl's "first paid assistant" October 1916 – February 1918; resigned in frustration; transcribed and edited "with major interventions" *Ideas II* (published 1952) and the time-consciousness lectures (published by Heidegger 1928) [source: SEP-ST §1; IEP-HU on Ideas II].
- Habilitation was "hitherto denied to women"; Göttingen rejected her in 1919; Husserl's recommendation of 6 February 1919 "was not particularly supportive" [source: SEP-ST §1]. WP-ES: her application "was officially rejected because she was a woman"; after she protested to Berlin citing Emmy Noether's habilitation, the minister of education issued a supportive note, but her candidacy still failed (faculty wariness, Husserl's lack of support — "he doubted the appropriateness of academic careers for women"; Husserl refused it "a limine" at Freiburg) [source: WP-ES].
- Empathy, per SEP-ST: a sui generis intentional act, experienced first-personally but presenting another's experience as non-originary; she distinguishes empathy from sympathy, fellow-feeling, emotional contagion and "feeling-one-with"; an early "direct perception" account of social cognition (I can "see" embarrassment in a blush) [source: SEP-ST §2.3]. Her dissertation reviews Lipps, Scheler and Dilthey and appeared when Husserl's own writings on empathy were unpublished [source: SEP-ST §2.3].
- Summer 1921, at the home of Theodor Conrad and Hedwig Conrad-Martius, she read Teresa of Ávila's autobiography; baptised 1 January 1922 at Bergzabern (baptismal name Theresia Hedwig), with Conrad-Martius (a Protestant) as godmother by special dispensation [source: SEP-ST §1; WP-ES; WP-HCM].
- Translated Aquinas's *De Veritate* and Newman; 1931 *Potency and Act*; post at Münster; dismissed 19 April 1933 because of her Jewish descent [source: SEP-ST §1].
- Entered the Carmel at Cologne October 1933; religious name Teresa Benedicta of the Cross (April 1934); moved to the Carmel at Echt, Netherlands, in 1938 (after Kristallnacht, WP-ES); wrote *Finite and Eternal Being* (posthumous 1950) and *The Science of the Cross* [source: SEP-ST §1; WP-ES].
- After the Dutch bishops condemned Nazi antisemitism (26 July 1942), she and her sister Rosa were arrested by the Gestapo on 2 August 1942, reached Auschwitz on 7 August and died there on 9 August 1942 [source: SEP-ST §1].
- Beatified by John Paul II in Cologne, 1 May 1987; canonised 11 October 1998 [source: SEP-ST §1; WP-ES]. Patronage listed includes Europe [source: WP-ES].
- Her case is the chapter's clearest example of a woman phenomenologist blocked by both sexism and antisemitism, and of Husserl's limited support.

## Other early phenomenologists (brief)
- Munich circle: grew around Theodor Lipps (Pfänder, Geiger, Conrad, Daubert, later von Hildebrand, Hedwig Martius) [source: SEP-SC §1; SEP-RE §1].
- Max Scheler: born 22 August 1874, Munich, into an orthodox Jewish family; met Husserl in 1901; lost his teaching right c.1910 after scandals; works *Phenomenology and Theory of the Feeling of Sympathy and of Love and Hate* (1913), *Formalism in Ethics and Non-Formal Ethics of Value* (1913/1916); Cologne from 1919; died Frankfurt 19 May 1928 [source: SEP-SC §1].
- Adolf Reinach: born 1883, Mainz, Jewish family; habilitation under Husserl at Göttingen 1909; *The Apriori Foundations of the Civil Law* (1913, first *Jahrbuch*), an early theory of social acts such as promising (speech acts); baptised, killed in Flanders 1917 [source: SEP-RE §1]. Stein edited his writings (published 1921) [source: SEP-ST §1; WP-ES].
- Hedwig Conrad-Martius: 27 February 1888 – 15 February 1966; won a 1912 Göttingen prize for "The Intuitional-Theoretical Principles of Positivism"; ontologist; married Theodor Conrad; had one Jewish grandparent and her work was stalled under Nazism [source: WP-HCM].
- Roman Ingarden: born Kraków 5 February 1893; studied with Twardowski in Lwów and with Husserl at Göttingen 1912–15 and Freiburg; PhD 1918 on Bergson; *The Literary Work of Art* (1931); *The Controversy over the Existence of the World* (Polish, 1947–48); banned from teaching 1949–57 under Stalinism for alleged "idealism" — "ironically, a philosophical position against which Ingarden fought for most of his life"; died 14 June 1970 [source: SEP-IN §1]. A "realist phenomenologist who ardently resisted Husserl's apparent turn to transcendental idealism" [source: SEP-IN §1].

## Heidegger: life to 1927
- Martin Heidegger, born 26 September 1889, Messkirch, south-west Germany; father a master cooper and Catholic sexton [source: SEP-H §1].
- Prepared for the priesthood; Jesuit novice 1909, discharged within a month (heart trouble); studied theology, then philosophy, mathematics and natural sciences at Freiburg [source: IEP-H §1; WP-MH; SEP-H §1].
- Doctorate 1913, "The Doctrine of Judgment in Psychologism"; habilitation 1915 on Duns Scotus (SEP-H: written under Rickert; IEP-H puts the doctorate under Rickert) [source: SEP-H §1; IEP-H §1].
- Husserl's assistant from 1919 [source: SEP-H §1]. Marburg 1923–28 (students: Gadamer, Löwith, Jonas, Arendt) [source: SEP-H §1].
- Private contempt for Husserl, letter to Löwith 1923: "I publicly burned and destroyed the Ideas … I am now convinced that Husserl was never a philosopher, not even for one second in his life" (quoted in Sheehan 1997: 17) [source: SEP-HU §1].
- *Being and Time* (*Sein und Zeit*), February 1927, published "partly because of administrative pressure"; full professor at Marburg 1927; Husserl's Freiburg chair 1928 [source: IEP-H §1; SEP-H §1].
- Dedication: "to Edmund Husserl in friendship and admiration" (SZ vi) [source: SEP-H §1; SEP-HU §1].
- 1941 edition: under pressure from the publisher Max Niemeyer, Heidegger agreed to remove the dedication; the acknowledgment of Husserl remained in a footnote on page 38; the dedication was restored in post-war editions [source: WP-HN citing Safranski 1998 pp. 253–8; WP-MH; WP-EH]. - English translations: Macquarrie & Robinson (1962); Joan Stambaugh (1996), revised by Dennis J. Schmidt (2010) [source: WP-BT; SEP-H].

## Heidegger: Being and Time — concepts
- The question of being: "the concrete working out of the question of the 'meaning' (Sinn) of 'being'" (SZ 1); time is "the possible horizon for any understanding whatsoever of being" (SZ 1) [source: SEP-H §2].
- Incomplete: only about one-third of the planned book was published; the two published divisions analyse human existence in terms of time [source: SEP-H §2].
- Ontological difference: "Being is essentially different from a being … We call it the ontological difference" (Basic Problems, 1982 tr., p. 17) [source: IEP-PH §2d]. Ontological vs ontic inquiry [source: SEP-H §2].
- Dasein: ordinary German for "existence"; Heidegger's word for us insofar as we understand being (SZ 12) [source: SEP-H §2.1.3].
- Three main kinds of being: readiness-to-hand (Zuhandenheit; MR "readiness-to-hand", SR "handiness", Wrathall "availableness"); presence-at-hand (Vorhandenheit; MR "presence-at-hand", SR "objective presence", Wrathall "occurrentness"); existence (Dasein's) [source: SEP-H §2.1].
- The hammer: equipment shows itself in use; M&R §15: "The kind of Being which equipment possesses – in which it manifests itself in its own right – we call 'readiness-to-hand'"; use has its own "sight", "circumspection" [source: IEP-PH §3, quoting Heidegger 1962 §15]. "A ready-to-hand hammer has various properties, including Being-the-perfect-size-for-the-job-at-hand" (IEP-PH's gloss). Presence-at-hand shows up when "a tool turns out to be damaged" (SZ 73) — "pure occurrentness announces itself" [source: SEP-H §2.1.2].
- Being-in-the-world: Dasein is constituted by practical and social contexts, against "post-Cartesian accounts of human beings as subjects in an objective world" [source: SEP-H intro].
- Das Man ("the they" in MR and SR; "the anyone" in Wrathall): socially shared norms; the everyday self is the "they-self"; "everyone is the other, and no one is himself" (MR, H. 128) [source: SEP-H §2.2.2; SEP-EX §5.4].
- Fallenness draws on Christian theology "and Kierkegaard in particular" [source: SEP-H §2.3]. "Like Kierkegaard, Heidegger interprets anxiety as an individualizing mood" [source: SEP-EX §5.4]. (Consistent with Periergia's *Schopenhauer and Kierkegaard*, which says Kierkegaard's critique of "the public" passed to Heidegger.)
- Anxiety: in it the world's mattering "collapses" and things are "of no consequence" (SZ 186); it shows we still care about our being [source: SEP-H §2.2.3.1].
- Care (Sorge): Being and Time analyses "the structure of 'care' as such" [source: WP-BT]; in anxiety we learn that all our concerns are rooted in fundamental care [source: SEP-H §2.2.3.1]. (No single-sentence verified definition of care was found; the writer should keep it general: care = Dasein's being, the way its own being and its world matter to it.)
- Authenticity (Eigentlichkeit, from eigen, "own"): owning one's existence; many interpretations (Dreyfus: practical wisdom; Crowell: responsiveness to reasons; Blattner/McManus: self-ownership; Guignon: narrative) [source: SEP-H §2.4; SEP-EX §5.4].
- Resoluteness (Entschlossenheit): authentic Dasein holds itself free "for the possibility of taking it back" (MR, H. 308) [source: SEP-EX §5.4].
- Temporality is what "makes ontology possible" (GA24: 323) [source: SEP-H §2]. Planned Part 2 would have treated Aristotle, Descartes and Kant [source: SEP-H §2].

## Being-towards-death (argument reconstruction)
Sources: SEP-H §2.4.1; SEP-EX §5.4; SEP-LE; WS on Sartre.
- Distinctions: perishing (Verenden) of mere living things; demise (Ableben), the end of a person's life; death (Tod) proper, "the possibility of the absolute impossibility of Dasein" (SZ 250) [source: SEP-H §2.4.1]. ("Dasein never perishes", SZ 247.)
- P1. Dasein is the being whose being is an issue for it, always projecting into possibilities.
- P2. Death is Dasein's ownmost possibility: no one can die it for me; it is not to be outstripped; it is certain but indefinite as to when [source: SEP-H; WS snippets of M&R §§52–53 — see Quotations caveat].
- P3. The "they" conceals this, treating death as something that happens to "one", someday [source: SEP-H "Inauthentic Dasein flees … seeks reassurance that it is living as one is supposed to (SZ 267–8)"].
- P4. Anticipation (running forward toward death) is not "brooding over" death (SZ 261) but taking up one's existence as finite [source: SEP-H].
- C. Anticipation "individualizes Dasein down to itself" (SZ 263) and frees it from possibilities that "may accidentally thrust themselves upon one" (SZ 264), making authentic choice possible: "an impassioned freedom towards death—a freedom which has been released from the illusions of the 'They'" (MR, H. 266) [source: SEP-H; SEP-EX].
- Interpretations: "existential death" reading (Blattner, Thomson: the collapse of one's identity-defining projects, which one can live through); modal/dual-aspect reading (Mulhall, Wrathall: death is demise viewed as possibility; facing finitude shows each choice forgoes others, SZ 285) [source: SEP-H §2.4.1].
- Objection (Philipse): the death/demise distinction is "a mesmerizing play with words" [source: SEP-H §2.4.1].
- Objection (Sartre, *Being and Nothingness*, 1943): death is not "my peculiar possibility" but "a contingent fact which as such on principle escapes me and originally belongs to my facticity"; it cannot give life meaning; Sartre links death with the absurd; we are free not "for" death but "against" it [source: WS snippets of Barnes translation notes and articles — NOT read in full; use cautiously].
- Objection (Levinas): authenticity does not lie in "securing our freedom for our most personal possibility, death"; "we can approach death as possibility only through that of others"; death is an alterity as radical as the other person; *Time and the Other* (1947): "In death the existing of the existent is alienated … It is not unknown but unknowable" (TO 75); Heidegger's frame "underestimates the significance of the encounter with the other person" [source: SEP-LE §§2.1–2.2, 3].
- Watsuji's objection (see Reception): emphasising being in time "is to discover human existence on the level only of individual consciousness" [source: SEP-WA].
- Replies: the modal reading answers Sartre: death is not an event I experience but the structure of finitude in my possibilities [source: SEP-H §2.4.1, Wrathall/Mulhall]; Heidegger insists authenticity is not a detached "free floating 'I'" (MR, H. 298) and choices are guided by "historicity" [source: SEP-EX §5.4]; the EDI reading makes death something lived through, answering the "it can't be experienced" worry [source: SEP-H]. Some scholars argue Sartre misreads Heidegger's perishing/demise/death distinctions [source: WS snippet].

## Heidegger: the turn and later work
- Kehre ("turn"): either a reversal of perspective from Dasein's being to being itself, or a change in being's relation to us; scholars "disagree about the precise timing, nature, extent, and even existence of this turn" (Braver 2021) [source: SEP-H §4]. After the turn, "being is not something we accomplish ourselves" (Richardson) [source: SEP-H §4]. WP-MH: as early as 1930, clearly established by the 1940s.
- 1930s–40s: lectures on Nietzsche and Hölderlin [source: SEP-H §1]; Nietzsche lectures 1936–1940 at Freiburg, published 1961 [source: WP-MH] (consistent with Periergia's *Nietzsche* chapter). *Introduction to Metaphysics* lecture course 1935 [source: IEP-H].
- "The Question Concerning Technology" ("Die Frage nach der Technik"): lecture 1953, published 1954 in *Vorträge und Aufsätze*; English tr. William Lovitt (1977) [source: SEP-H bibliography; IEP-H bibliography].
- Gestell: the gathering of all modes of "setting in place", a "challenging-forth" that forces nature to yield; default translation "enframing" (other proposals: Kisiel's "syn-thetic com-posit(ion)ing", Wrathall's "inventory"); things appear as Bestand ("standing reserve", "stock", "resource"); technology is "the supreme danger" (GA7: 31 / QCT 26) [source: SEP-H §5.2; SEP-HA].
- "nature becomes one gigantic petrol station, a source of energy for modern technology and industry" (GA16: 523 / *Discourse on Thinking* 50) [source: SEP-H §5.2.2].
- Bremen lecture, 1949: "Agriculture is now a motorized food industry, the same thing in its essence as the production of corpses in the gas chambers and the extermination camps, the same thing as blockades and the reduction of countries to famine, the same thing as the manufacture of hydrogen bombs" [source: WP-MH; WP-HN gives a slightly different translation — name it as a translation and cite Sheehan/Gordon].
- Died 26 May 1976 in Freiburg, buried in Messkirch 28 May [source: SEP-H §1].

## Heidegger and Nazism
- Elected rector of Freiburg on 21 April 1933 (took office the next day) [source: WP-HN; IEP-H "April 21, 1933"]. SEP-H: "elected Rector … in April 1933".
- Joined the NSDAP: 1 May 1933 [source: SEP-H; WP-HN; Husserl's letter "May 1"]. (IEP-H says 3 May 1933 — record as variant; use 1 May.)
- Rectoral address, 27 May 1933: "Die Selbstbehauptung der deutschen Universität" ("The Self-Assertion of the German University") [source: IEP-H; WP-HN].
- As rector he was responsible for Gleichschaltung of the university [source: SEP-H §1]; co-signed a telegram of Nazi rectors to Hitler, 20 May 1933 [source: WP-HN]; gave a "Declaration of Support for Adolf Hitler and the National Socialist State", November 1933 [source: IEP-H].
- Resigned April 1934 (IEP-H: 23 April 1934); remained a party member until 1945 [source: SEP-H; WP-HN; IEP-H].
- Husserl's exclusion: Baden's Reichskommissar Robert Wagner suspended Jewish officials on 6 April 1933; Husserl was notified of his leave on 14 April by Heidegger's predecessor, before Heidegger took office; WP-HN concludes the termination "did not involve any specific action on Heidegger's part" [source: WP-HN]. WP-EH says Husserl was banned from the library on 6 April and reinstated the following week after public outcry; WP-HN: "There is no truth to the oft-repeated story that during Heidegger's time as Rector, the University denied Husserl access to the university library" [source: WP-EH; WP-HN].
- Heidegger did not attend Husserl's cremation in 1938; he later called this "a human failure" and apologised in a letter to Husserl's widow [source: WP-MH; WP-HN].
- Post-war: Denazification Committee report September 1945 (four counts incl. introducing the Führerprinzip); barred by the French military government from teaching [source: SEP-H §1; WP-HN]. Classified as a "Mitläufer" (follower) in March 1949 [source: WP-HN]. Teaching ban lifted 1949 (SEP-H) or 1951 (WP-HN, which adds emeritus status 1953 in its lead but "reintegrated in 1951, given emeritus status" in its body) — sources disagree: say "lifted in 1949–51".
- Karl Jaspers's 1945 report: "In the twenties, Heidegger was no anti-Semite …" [source: WP-HN]; Jaspers spoke against him at the hearings; Arendt spoke on his behalf [source: WP-HN, with citation-needed flag — use cautiously].
- Der Spiegel interview 23 September 1966 (WP-HN; WP-MH says 23 Sept), published 31 May 1976, five days after his death, as "Only a God Can Save Us" [source: WP-MH; WP-HN].
- SEP-H judgement (Wrathall 2025): "It is no longer possible to doubt" that he was "a real Nazi: his involvement was a matter of conviction rather than compromise" (Young 1997: 4); "an unabashed anti-Semite" (Sheehan 2015a); after the war he "repeatedly lied or distorted the record"; "never publicly and explicitly renounced his support for National Socialism"; on the rare occasions he addressed the Holocaust he deflected, equating the Shoah with the Soviet occupation of East Germany [source: SEP-H §3].
- Sheehan: "We have his statements about the six millions unemployed at the beginning of the Nazi regime, but not a word about the six million who were dead at the end of it" [source: WP-HN].
- He held "a racist and anti-Semitic conception of the essence of the German people, although he rejected the Nazi's biologistic conception of race" [source: SEP-H §3].
- 1935 lecture: "the inner truth and greatness of this movement" (Introduction to Metaphysics) [source: WP-MH; WP-HN; SEP-H via Habermas].
- Black Notebooks (Schwarze Hefte): 34 notebooks, October 1931 – 1970; edited by Peter Trawny for the Gesamtausgabe (Klostermann); first volumes (GA 94–96, *Überlegungen II–XV*) published 2014; nine volumes, GA 94–102, about 3,384 pages; English *Ponderings* tr. Richard Rojcewicz (2016–17) [source: WP-BN; SEP-H bibliography].
- They contain antisemitic statements that reignited the debate in 2014 [source: WP-BN; WP-HN].
- Peter Trawny (born 1964, University of Wuppertal), *Heidegger und der Mythos der jüdischen Weltverschwörung* (2014); English *Heidegger and the Myth of a Jewish World Conspiracy*, tr. Andrew J. Mitchell (University of Chicago Press, 2015); coined "being-historical" (seinsgeschichtlich) antisemitism — antisemitism built into Heidegger's narrative of the history of being [source: WP Peter_Trawny; WS (press.uchicago.edu, NDPR)].
- Emmanuel Faye, *Heidegger, l'introduction du nazisme dans la philosophie* (2005; English 2009, Yale): Heidegger's work is "not at all a 'philosophy'"; he introduced "the very content of Nazism and Hitlerism" into philosophy [source: SEP-H §3; WP-HN]. Earlier: Victor Farías (1987) and Hugo Ott (1988) [source: SEP-H §3].
- Paul Celan: read at Freiburg 24 July 1967 with Heidegger in the audience; visited Heidegger's hut at Todtnauberg on 25 July 1967 and signed the guestbook; their conversation is unrecorded; Celan's poem "Todtnauberg" is dated 1 August 1967; a common reading is that it expresses Celan's wish for an apology [source: WP-HN, citing Felstiner 1995 p. 246]. Celan was a Jewish survivor of camps run by Nazi Germany's Romanian allies [source: WP-HN].
- Hannah Arendt: Heidegger's student at Marburg; a "complicated love affair" from 1925; SEP-H quotes Thomson for a relationship "stretching across half a century, from 1925 to 1975 (and ending only when Arendt died, 6 months before Heidegger)"; WP-MH calls the affair itself "four-year" (begun 1925, when he was 35 and she 19) [source: SEP-H §1; WP-MH]. Say: an affair begun in 1925, a relationship lasting until her death in 1975.

## Merleau-Ponty (1908–1961)
- Born 14 March 1908, Rochefort-sur-Mer; father died 1913 [source: SEP-MP §1]. (IEP-MP says his father was killed in WWI; SEP-MP says only that he died in 1913, a colonial artillery captain — do not say "killed in the war".)
- École Normale; attended Kojève's Hegel lectures and Gurwitsch's lectures on Gestalt psychology; *The Structure of Behavior* (1942) [source: SEP-MP §1].
- First outside visitor to the Husserl Archives, April 1939 [source: SEP-MP §1].
- Lieutenant 1939–40, wounded June 1940, Croix de guerre; resistance group Socialisme et Liberté with Sartre [source: SEP-MP §1].
- *Phénoménologie de la perception* completed 1944, published 1945; defended July 1945 [source: SEP-MP §1, §3]. English: Colin Smith (1962), Donald Landes (2012) [source: WP-PoP; SEP-MP bibliography] (WP-PoP gives Landes 2013; SEP 2012 — use 2012).
- Co-founded *Les Temps modernes* with Sartre and Beauvoir (October 1945); political editor until December 1952, leaving over political disagreements (Korean War) [source: SEP-MP §1].
- Lyon (from 1945), Sorbonne chair of child psychology (1949), Collège de France chair 1952 — at 44 the youngest ever elected [source: SEP-MP §1].
- Died of a heart attack in Paris on 3 May 1961, aged 53, "with Descartes' *Optics* open on his desk" [source: SEP-MP §1]. *The Visible and the Invisible* (manuscript and working notes) published posthumously by Claude Lefort in 1964 [source: SEP-MP §1, bibliography].
- Method: against both empiricism and "intellectualism"; the reduction as existential; "Husserl's transcendental is not Kant's" (PP preface, 2012: lxxvii) [source: SEP-MP §3; SEP-HU §5].
- Perception: the basic level is the gestalt, figure against ground; "return to the lived world beneath the objective world" (PP 83/57) [source: SEP-MP §3].
- The lived body ("le corps propre", one's own body; "body-subject"): not merely an object but a permanent condition of experience [source: WP-MMP; SEP-MP §3]. It is always in the perceptual field yet never fully seen; it has "double sensations"; it is "an 'I can' rather than an 'I think'" [source: SEP-MP §3].
- Body schema; habit body (sedimentations of past activities) vs present body [source: SEP-MP §3].
- Motor intentionality: bodily activity is itself intentional; "it is the body which 'catches' and 'comprehends movement'. The acquisition of a habit is indeed the grasping of a significance, but it is the motor grasping or a motor significance" (Smith tr., 142–3); "it is the body which 'understands'" (144) [source: IEP-PH §3].
- Schneider: a brain-injured patient studied by Adhémar Gelb and Kurt Goldstein; an extended case study; he "lacks the ability to 'project' into virtual space"; his injury disrupted the "intentional arc" that "projects around us our past, our future, our human milieu …" (PP 170/137) [source: SEP-MP §3]. (Details of his symptoms not verified — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Phantom limb: used as one of the pathological contrasts revealing the body's "being-toward-the-world" [source: SEP-MP §3]. (No further detail verified.)
- Habit examples: "For the player in action the football field is not an 'object'" (Structure of Behavior) [source: IEP-MP §3]; learning a new dance without analysing it [source: IEP-MP].
- "One's own body is in the world just as the heart is in the organism" (PP 245/209) [source: SEP-MP §3].
- Language as bodily gesture, "singing the world" (PP 228/193) [source: SEP-MP §3].
- Late ontology: "flesh" (la chair) and "chiasm"/intertwining; flesh is "closest to the notion of an 'element' in the classical sense" (V&I 184/139); "The handshake too is reversible" (V&I 187/142) [source: SEP-MP §6].
- Objections: Irigaray (1993) — the ontology of flesh relies on feminine/maternal metaphors while rendering sexual difference invisible; Derrida (*On Touching*, 2000) — privileges immediacy and coincidence over distance [source: SEP-MP §7]. IEP-MP (Reynolds): he "never quite gets away from a subject/object dichotomy" (in the reading of some critics) and later admitted the "tacit cogito is impossible" (V&I 171) [source: IEP-MP].
- Replies/influence: feminist and "critical phenomenology" (Young, Alcoff, Ahmed, Weiss, Ngo, Salamon) draw on him "with important qualifications"; cognitive science (Gallagher, Thompson) [source: SEP-MP §7].

## Reception beyond Europe; feminist and analytic engagement
- Kyoto School: Tanabe Hajime (1885–1962) studied with Husserl at Freiburg 1922–23 and befriended Heidegger; on returning to Japan in 1924 he was "the first scholar in the world to write an article on Heidegger's thought" ("A New Turn in Phenomenology: Heidegger's Phenomenology of Life", *Shisō*, October 1924) [source: SEP-KY §3; WP-HT]. He contributed "Todesdialektik" (dialectic of death) to Heidegger's 70th-birthday Festschrift (Neske, 1959) [source: SEP-KY bibliography]. He criticised Heidegger's Nazi-era stance, yet himself wrote in support of the Japanese war effort [source: WP-HT].
- Nishitani Keiji studied with Heidegger 1937–39 and was often invited to his house to explain Zen [source: SEP-KY].
- Watsuji Tetsurō (1889–1960): in Germany 1927–28 (14 months), "among the very first to read" *Sein und Zeit*; his *Fūdo* (1935; English *Climate and Culture*) is "both an appreciation of, and a critique of Heidegger": Heidegger "under-emphasizes spatiality, and over-emphasizes temporality"; to emphasise our being in time "is to discover human existence on the level only of individual consciousness" (Watsuji 1961: 9); humans are "betweenness" (ningen), social and spatial [source: SEP-WA].
- Heidegger and Daoism: in summer 1946 Heidegger worked with the Chinese scholar Paul Shih-yi Hsiao (Xiao Shiyi, 1911–1986) on a German translation of the *Laozi* (*Daodejing*); they did about eight chapters; it was not completed and the manuscript has not been found; Hsiao's account is "Heidegger and Our Translation of the Tao Te Ching", in Graham Parkes (ed.), *Heidegger and Asian Thought* (University of Hawai'i Press, 1987), pp. 93–103 [source: WS snippets (De Gruyter/JCIEA 2023 article; Parkes essay); SEP-KY bibliography confirms Parkes 1987]. Partially verified — say "about eight chapters" and "summer 1946".
- Reinhard May, *Ex oriente lux* (1989), English *Heidegger's Hidden Sources: East Asian Influences on His Work*, tr. with an essay by Graham Parkes (Routledge, 1996): argues Heidegger borrowed ideas, sometimes nearly verbatim, from German translations of Daoist and Zen texts [source: WS (Routledge, CiNii)] — a contested claim; present as May's thesis.
- Fanon: studied at Lyon (MD 1951), "sometimes attending Merleau-Ponty's lectures" [source: WP-FF]. *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952): its method is primarily "existential-phenomenological"; chapter 5 is "L'expérience vécue" (Philcox: "lived-experience"); the influence of Sartre and Merleau-Ponty supports this, but the method is debated (Négritude, psychoanalysis, Hegel, Marx) [source: SEP-FA].
- Feminist phenomenology: Beauvoir (cross-ref *Existentialism*); Iris Marion Young, "Throwing Like a Girl: A Phenomenology of Feminine Body Comportment, Motility and Spatiality" (1980, *Human Studies*; reprinted 1990 and in *On Female Body Experience*, 2005) uses Beauvoir and Merleau-Ponty [source: WP-TLG; SEP-FB §8.1]. Social norms yield an "inhibited intentionality", an interruption of the pre-reflective engagement Merleau-Ponty described; women often experience their bodies as things "looked at and acted upon"; "She often lives her body as a burden, which must be dragged and prodded along, and at the same time protected" (1980 [2005: 36]); such experiences derive from women's situation, not anatomy [source: SEP-FB §8.1]. Feminist phenomenology after Beauvoir began with Bartky and Young in the late 1970s and became widespread in the 1990s [source: SEP-FB §8].
- Hubert Dreyfus (15 October 1929 – 22 April 2017), Berkeley: used Heidegger (and Merleau-Ponty) against classical AI — "Alchemy and Artificial Intelligence" (RAND, 1965), *What Computers Can't Do* (1972); *Being-in-the-World: A Commentary on Heidegger's Being and Time, Division I* (1991); he never predicted computers could not beat humans at chess [source: WP-HD]. Dreyfus's "absorbed coping" reading is quoted in SEP-H §2.1.1.
- Dan Zahavi (born 1967, Copenhagen; PhD Leuven 1994): pre-reflective self-consciousness, empathy and social cognition; co-editor of *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences* 2007–2025 [source: WP-DZ]. He wrote the current SEP Husserl entry [source: SEP-HU].

## Debates
- Did Frege's 1894 review make Husserl abandon psychologism? Føllesdal (1958) said so; Mohanty (1977) and Bernet, Kern & Marbach (1993) point instead to Husserl's own study of Lotze and Bolzano [source: SEP-HU note 1].
- Husserl's idealism: reductive/metaphysical idealist (Ingarden, Philipse) vs meaning-not-being reading (Carr, Crowell, Thomasson) vs "irreducibility of ideality" (Willard, D. W. Smith); Zahavi: an idealist, but neither monism nor phenomenalism [source: SEP-HU §3]. Realist vs idealist periods (Ingarden: realist LI, later abandoned; Fink and Husserl himself: continuous) [source: IEP-HU].
- The noema: "Fregean"/representationalist reading (Føllesdal 1969; Smith & McIntyre; Dreyfus) — the noema is a sense by which consciousness is directed — vs presentationalist reading (Sokolowski, Drummond) — the noema is the object itself as intended [source: SEP-HU §3].
- Heidegger's "being": intelligibility interpretation (Dreyfus, Sheehan) vs Blattner (criterial standards) and McDaniel/Olafson (equivocal) [source: SEP-H §2].
- Death vs demise: Philipse ("play with words") vs existential-death interpretation (Blattner, Thomson) vs modal/dual-aspect (Mulhall, Wrathall) [source: SEP-H §2.4.1].
- Authenticity: Aristotelian/phronetic (Dreyfus), transcendental (Crowell), self-ownership (Blattner, McManus), narrative (Guignon) [source: SEP-H §2.4].
- The Kehre: timing, extent and even existence disputed; continuity (Withy) vs discontinuity (Keiling) [source: SEP-H §4].
- Heidegger's Nazism and his philosophy: Young (philosophy can be fully "de-Nazified") vs Faye (not philosophy at all); SEP-H (Wrathall) takes a middle line: the philosophy is fruitful but Nazi-era work "bears unmistakable resonances" with Nazi ideology (völkisch nationalism, anti-modernism) [source: SEP-H §3]. On the Black Notebooks: Trawny's "being-historical antisemitism"; Escudero ("for every piece of evidence for anti-Semitism there is another piece of evidence against it" — but he concedes non-racial, "spiritual" antisemitism); Knowles (2019) ties the antisemitism to his philosophy of language [source: WP-HN; WP-BN]. IEP-H (older entry) presents a more sympathetic account (covert criticism in lectures, party membership "a mere formality", Gestapo surveillance) — note the contrast with SEP-H 2025 [source: IEP-H §1].
- Heidegger's Asian sources: May (1989/1996) argues for direct borrowing from Daoist/Zen translations; others treat the links as dialogue/affinity [source: WS]. 
- Fanon's method: existential-phenomenological (Lewis Gordon) vs other frames (Négritude, psychoanalysis, Marxism; Rabaka, Bhabha) [source: SEP-FA].
- Merleau-Ponty's "flesh": Irigaray and Derrida critiques vs his continuing influence in feminist and critical phenomenology [source: SEP-MP §7].
- Husserl's lifeworld and science: whether Husserl was an instrumentalist about unobservables (Soffer, Wiltsche, Hardy, Trizio) [source: SEP-HU §6].
