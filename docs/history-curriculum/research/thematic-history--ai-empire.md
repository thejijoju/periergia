## 1. Computing's imperial and military genealogy

### Hollerith and the census
- Herman Hollerith's electromechanical tabulators, supplied under contract to the US Census Office, were used for the 1890 US census. With the other changes (larger population, more data items, more staff), processing time fell from eight years for the 1880 census to six years for the 1890 census; the 1895 Commissioner's report said the Eleventh Census would be completed "at least two years earlier" than the Tenth. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Hollerith]
- Hollerith's machines were used for censuses in England and Wales, Italy, Germany, Russia, Austria, Canada, France, Norway, **Puerto Rico, Cuba and the Philippines** (the last three under US control after 1898), and again in the 1900 US census. "Many major census bureaus around the world leased his equipment and purchased his cards." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Hollerith]
  - Note for the writer: Wikipedia lists the places but does not date each census. Saying "including the censuses of the United States' new colonies of Puerto Rico, Cuba and the Philippines" is fine; do not attach specific years.
- Hollerith's company was merged in 1911 into the Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company, which was renamed International Business Machines (IBM) in 1924. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Hollerith]

### Bletchley Park and the imperial network of code-breaking
- In January 1945, 8,995 people worked at Bletchley Park and its outstations, about three-quarters of them women. Colossus was designed and built by Tommy Flowers at the Post Office Research Station, Dollis Hill. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bletchley_Park] (Already in the Digital Age chapter.)
- Bletchley was part of an imperial network. The Government Code and Cypher School set up the Far East Combined Bureau (FECB) in Hong Kong in 1935. Its naval staff moved in 1940 to Singapore, then to Colombo (Ceylon), then to Kilindini, Mombasa (Kenya). Its Army and Air Force staff went from Singapore to the Wireless Experimental Centre outside Delhi, India. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bletchley_Park]
- In a historical echo, the British government held the first international **AI Safety Summit at Bletchley Park** on 1–2 November 2023. Its "Bletchley Declaration" was signed by 28 countries, including the United States and China. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_Safety_Summit_2023]

### Cold War funding: ARPA/DARPA and SAGE
- The Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA) was created on 7 February 1958 by President Eisenhower in response to the Soviet launch of Sputnik 1 in 1957. It was renamed DARPA in March 1972, renamed ARPA again in February 1993, and renamed DARPA again in March 1996. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DARPA]
- The Dartmouth workshop itself (summer 1956, about six to eight weeks) was funded by the **Rockefeller Foundation**, not the military. McCarthy approached the Foundation in early 1955; the proposal is dated 31 August 1955 (some accounts say it was formally proposed on 2 September 1955). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_workshop]
- In June 1963 MIT received a $2.2 million grant from the newly formed ARPA, which funded Project MAC, absorbing the AI Group founded by Minsky and McCarthy five years earlier. DARPA continued to provide $3 million a year until the 1970s and made similar grants to Newell and Simon at Carnegie Mellon and to Stanford. J. C. R. Licklider believed ARPA should "fund people, not projects!" [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_artificial_intelligence]
- After the **Mansfield Amendment of 1969**, DARPA came under pressure to fund "mission-oriented direct research, rather than basic undirected research", and it moved towards projects such as autonomous tanks and battle-management systems. Its DART logistics tool was credited (by DARPA's supporters) with saving billions in the 1991 Gulf War. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_artificial_intelligence]
- The US **Strategic Computing Initiative** (1983–1993), inspired by Japan's Fifth Generation project (MITI set aside $850 million in 1981), cost the Department of Defense about $1 billion in total. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_Computing_Initiative; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_artificial_intelligence]
- **SAGE** (Semi-Automatic Ground Environment, an air-defence network) became operational in the late 1950s and early 1960s (the first direction centre, DC-01, opened on 26 June 1958). It coordinated radar data to produce a single picture of North American airspace. Its estimated total cost was **$8–12 billion**, "four times the cost of the Manhattan Project" (Wikipedia's wording; an estimate). Its AN/FSQ-7 computer, built by IBM, was "the largest discrete component-based computer ever built". It grew out of MIT's Whirlwind computer (completed 1951) and remained the backbone of NORAD air defence into the 1980s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-Automatic_Ground_Environment]
- IBM "benefited immensely" from SAGE, notably in developing the IBM 704 (Redmond and Smith, *From Whirlwind to MITRE*, 2000). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-Automatic_Ground_Environment]
- Historiography: Paul N. Edwards, *The Closed World: Computers and the Politics of Discourse in Cold War America* (MIT Press, 1996/1997), is the standard account of SAGE and Cold War computing; it is cited in the SAGE article. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-Automatic_Ground_Environment] (The SAGE article's citation gives 1997; the first edition is usually dated 1996. Use "1996" only if checked; "Edwards's *The Closed World*" without a year is safe.)
- Through Project Maven (from April 2017), the Pentagon applied AI image recognition to drone footage. In June 2018, after employee protests, Google said it would not renew its Maven contract when it expired in March 2019. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_arms_race] Crawford's *Atlas of AI* recounts the episode and notes that the work moved to Palantir. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_of_AI]

## 2. The precedent: Headrick's tools of empire
- Daniel R. Headrick, *The Tools of Empire: Technology and European Imperialism in the Nineteenth Century* (Oxford University Press, 1981), examined steam transport, firearms, communications and medicine, treating technology "as one element in imperial expansion rather than as a sufficient explanation". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_R._Headrick] (Already in the Infrastructure and Imperial Expansion chapters; Imperial Expansion quotes his later "necessary, if not sufficient".)
- Headrick's sequels are *The Tentacles of Progress: Technology Transfer in the Age of Imperialism, 1850–1940* (1988); *The Invisible Weapon: Telecommunications and International Politics, 1851–1945* (1991), on submarine telegraph cables and international politics; and *Power over Peoples: Technology, Environments, and Western Imperialism, 1400 to the Present* (2010). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_R._Headrick]
- From the 1850s until 1911, British submarine cable systems dominated the North Atlantic. Britain sought a worldwide network within the empire, the **All Red Line**. In 1892 British companies owned and operated **two-thirds of the world's cables**, and by 1923 their share was still **42.7 per cent** (figures cited to Headrick). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_communications_cable]
- In 1870, at the British government's behest, Bombay was linked to London by submarine cable. British officials thought lines crossing non-British territory were a wartime risk. In the First World War, Britain's telegraph communications were "almost completely uninterrupted", while it quickly cut Germany's cables worldwide. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_communications_cable]
- The earlier "electronic colonialism" thesis: Herbert Schiller's *Mass Communication and American Empire* (1969) argued that poorer nations were subordinated through imported communication equipment and foreign-produced software. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_colonialism]

## 3. The material empire of AI

### Chips and the Taiwan chokepoint
- TSMC "accounts for approximately 70% of the global market share" of the semiconductor foundry market (undated in the article; treat as mid-2020s). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TSMC] (TSMC's founding by Morris Chang in 1987 is in the Digital Age chapter.)
- In the early 2020s, Taiwanese manufacturers made about 60% of all semiconductors (by multiple estimates), and TSMC made over 90% of the most advanced generation of chips (*Foreign Policy*, February 2023). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_shield]
- The "silicon shield" is the idea that Taiwan's chip industry is so important that it would deter a Chinese attempt to seize the island. The phrase was in use by 2000 (*New York Times*/IHT opinion piece, 29 September 2000, "A 'Silicon Shield' Protects Taiwan From China"). In 2022 Matthew Pottinger challenged the idea, arguing that China does not behave in ways that seem rational to democratic audiences. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_shield]
- Chris Miller, *Chip War: The Fight for the World's Most Critical Technology* (Scribner, October 2022). Miller is an economic historian at Tufts University's Fletcher School and a nonresident senior fellow at the American Enterprise Institute. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chip_War]
- Nvidia: by 2025 it controlled "more than 80% of the market for GPUs used in training and deploying AI models". It closed above $4 trillion in market value for the first time on 10 July 2025, and in 2025 became the first company to pass both $4 trillion and $5 trillion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nvidia]

### Export controls and the US–China contest
- Effective **7 October 2022**, the US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security imposed export controls aimed at China's ability to obtain and develop advanced computing and chip-making items. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_New_Export_Controls_on_Advanced_Computing_and_Semiconductors_to_China] (Already in the Digital Age chapter.)
- In October 2023 the rules were tightened: more entities were covered and the technical thresholds were revised. Nvidia's H20 was designed to fit under them. The Biden administration's "AI Diffusion" interim final rule was published on 13 January 2025, with compliance due from 15 May 2025. The Trump administration's Commerce Department announced on 12–13 May 2025 that it would rescind the rule. H20 sales to China were blocked in April 2025, and licences were then promised in July 2025. [source: WebSearch summary of CRS report R48642 (22 Aug 2025) and Covington, DLA Piper, TechRepublic and Computer Weekly coverage — https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-08-22_R48642_d391ba178c53d974aae1bec10d900622fca3f984.html; https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-us-ai-chips-diffusion-rule-changes/] (Secondary sources. Keep to "in 2025 the rules were loosened and tightened by turns" unless more detail is needed.)
- China's State Council issued "A Next Generation Artificial Intelligence Development Plan" (State Council Document [2017] No. 35) on **20 July 2017**. It set the goal of China becoming the preeminent country for AI research and application by 2030. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_industry_in_China] The *Financial Times* (9 July 2017) reported Beijing's aim of a $150 billion AI industry by 2030. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_arms_race] Some analysts link the shift to AlphaGo's 2016 defeat of Lee Sedol. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_industry_in_China]
- In September 2017 Vladimir Putin said: "Whoever becomes leader in this sphere will become the ruler of the world" (as reported by *Fortune*, 4 September 2017). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_arms_race]
- "AI nationalism": the term comes from a 2018 essay of that title by the British technology investor Ian Hogarth, who predicted that AI policy would become central to government agendas. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_nationalism]
- The Stanford AI Index 2025 (published 7 April 2025) counted 40 "notable" AI models from US institutions in 2024, 15 from China and 3 from Europe (all French, per IEEE Spectrum). It put US private AI investment in 2024 at about $109.1 billion, roughly 12 times China's $9.3 billion. Nearly 90% of notable models came from industry. [source: WebSearch summary of https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report and https://spectrum.ieee.org/ai-index-2025]
- Biden's Executive Order 14110 on "Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy" AI was signed on 30 October 2023. Trump revoked it within hours of taking office on 20 January 2025. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_14110] Trump's Executive Order 14179, "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence", was signed on 23 January 2025. It aims to maintain "global AI dominance" and to promote AI "free from ideological bias or social agendas". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_14179]
- Stargate: an AI joint venture of OpenAI, SoftBank, Oracle and MGX, announced at the White House on 21 January 2025 as a $500 billion investment. It started with $100 billion, with plans to reach $500 billion by 2029. Bloomberg reported on 7 August 2025 that the project had not raised funds to meet the initial budget. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stargate_LLC]
- DeepSeek is based in Hangzhou and owned and funded by the hedge fund High-Flyer. It released its R1 model and chatbot in January 2025. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeepSeek] (Already in the Digital Age chapter.)

### Rare earths and minerals
- The IEA estimated that in 2024 China accounted for about 91% of global rare-earth separation and refining and 94% of sintered permanent-magnet production (IEA, 23 October 2025). China has controlled exports of some rare earths and processing technology since 2016. In 2025 it introduced two waves of export controls, and the second was later suspended for one year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rare-earth_industry_in_China]
- China's share of rare-earth production rose from 21% to 60% between 1985 and 1995. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rare-earth_industry_in_China]
- Cobalt: the Cobalt article says the DRC "currently produces 63%" of the world's cobalt (undated). The Digital Age chapter gives about three-quarters in 2024 (USGS). The share has risen; if used, give "about two-thirds to three-quarters, depending on year". Siddharth Kara's *Cobalt Red* (2023) describes artisanal mining and child labour. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobalt]
- Crawford's *Atlas of AI* opens with resource extraction, including the Thacker Pass lithium mine (Nevada). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_of_AI] The "Lithium Triangle" of Argentina, Bolivia and Chile is defined by the Salar de Uyuni (Bolivia), Salar de Atacama (Chile) and Salar del Hombre Muerto (Argentina). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium_Triangle]

### Energy and water for data centres
- The IEA (*Energy and AI*, April 2025) estimated that data centres used about **415 TWh** of electricity in 2024, about **1.5%** of global electricity. Its Base Case projects about **945 TWh by 2030**, just under 3% of global consumption, with AI the main driver. The United States accounts for the largest share of the increase, followed by China. Data centres account for only about a tenth of global electricity demand growth to 2030, less than industrial motors, air conditioning or electric vehicles. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_AI; WebSearch summary of https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary]
- The IEA put US data-centre consumption at about 180 TWh in 2024 (secondary summaries give about 45% of the global total). [source: WebSearch summary of IEA *Energy and AI* coverage] (Secondary; use with "about".)
- In April 2026 the IEA reported that data-centre electricity use "surged in 2025" despite bottlenecks, with project growth straining grid connections and planning systems. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_AI (citing IEA, 16 April 2026)] (The 2025 total was not retrieved; do not give a figure.)
- Google's 2024 environmental report said its 2023 greenhouse-gas emissions rose 13% year on year, mainly because of data-centre energy use and supply-chain emissions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_AI]
- Water: estimates vary greatly with location, cooling design and electricity source, and researchers distinguish water *withdrawal* from *consumption*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_AI] See NOT VERIFIED for the IEA water figure, and the next item for a cautionary case.
- **Cautionary case:** In November 2025, after fact-checking by Andy Masley and others, Karen Hao said that the published *Empire of AI* overstated the water use of a data centre in Chile **by a factor of 1,000** because of a unit-conversion error (reported by *Wired*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_AI]
- A Global South example of energy limits: in May 2024 Microsoft and G42 announced a $1 billion geothermal-powered data centre at KenGen's Green Energy Park near Olkaria, Kenya (100 MW initially, possibly rising to 1 GW). By 2025–26 it was reported stalled or suspended. President William Ruto was quoted: "one data centre requires 1,000 MW and yet we have 2,300 MW. For us to do the data centre, we have to shut down half the country." [source: WebSearch summary of https://www.thinkgeoenergy.com/kenya-suspends-plans-for-microsofts-geothermal-powered-data-centre/ and https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/microsofts-1-billion-kenya-data-center-stalls-over-disagreements-on-power-capacity] (Secondary press; describe as "reported", and give the quotation only with this attribution.)

### Undersea cables
- 2Africa, led by Meta (Facebook) with a consortium of eight members including China Mobile and MTN, is the world's largest subsea cable: 45,000 km, with 46 landing stations in 33 countries. It was announced in May 2020, and Meta said in November 2025 that the core system was complete. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2Africa]
- China's **Digital Silk Road**, the digital part of the Belt and Road Initiative, was announced by Xi Jinping in **2015**. It covers 5G networks, fibre-optic cables, satellites and data centres. China frames it as building a "community of common destiny" in cyberspace. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Silk_Road] In October 2018 Huawei and Hengtong announced production for the PEACE cable (Pakistan and East Africa Connecting Europe). Huawei has operated in Kenya since 1998. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huawei]
- "About 99% of the world's data traffic" crosses submarine cables: this is already in the Infrastructure chapter; cross-refer.

## 4. Data colonialism and extraction
- "Data colonialism" as a concept is associated with Nick Couldry and Ulises A. Mejias. In *The Costs of Connection: How Data Is Colonizing Human Life and Appropriating It for Capitalism* (Stanford University Press, 2019), they argue that the data economy reorganises human life into a continuous source of raw material for capitalist accumulation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_colonialism]
- Their sequel is *Data Grab: The New Colonialism of Big Tech and How to Fight Back* (2024; University of Chicago Press in the US, W. H. Allen in the UK). A *Lawfare* review says the authors do not use colonialism as a metaphor. A *Surveillance & Society* review (Mavrlja, 22(4), 2024) summarises their view that the phenomenon is "not new, but rather a continuation of colonial ways", likened to colonial land grabs. [source: WebSearch summary of https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/data-colonialism--and-the-political-economy-of-big-tech and https://www.erudit.org/fr/revues/survsoc/2024-v22-n4-survsoc09803/1115681ar.pdf]
- Kate Crawford (Australian, born 1974; PhD, University of Sydney; principal researcher at **Microsoft Research**; co-founded the AI Now Institute with Meredith Whittaker in 2017). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kate_Crawford]
- *Atlas of AI: Power, Politics, and the Planetary Costs of Artificial Intelligence* (Yale University Press, 25 May 2021). It covers mining (Thacker Pass), labour (Amazon warehouses, Mechanical Turk), datasets built without consent (ImageNet), "emotion recognition" based on Paul Ekman's coding system, state surveillance (Snowden, Project Maven), and AlphaGo's environmental cost. Crawford also compares Google's "TrueTime" with colonial efforts to control time. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_of_AI]
- Karen Hao, *Empire of AI: Dreams and Nightmares in Sam Altman's OpenAI* (Penguin Press, 20 May 2025; 496 pp.). It draws on interviews with about 260 people, and its title refers to the colonial empires of the 1800s. Hao reports from Colombia, the Philippines, Venezuela and Kenya, where she interviewed low-wage contract workers who sorted graphic content to train ChatGPT. Its chapter titles echo imperial history ("Divine Right", "A Civilizing Mission", "Plundered Earth", "A Formula for Empire", epilogue "How the Empire Falls"). It won the 2025 National Book Critics Circle Award for Nonfiction. OpenAI declined to cooperate, and Sam Altman publicly criticised the book. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_AI]
- Hao covered AI for *MIT Technology Review* (senior AI editor, 2018–2022), the *Wall Street Journal* (2022–23) and *The Atlantic* (2023–24). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karen_Hao]
- **East India Company comparison:** reviewers (not Hao herself, per Wikipedia) compared *Empire of AI* to William Dalrymple's *The Anarchy: The Relentless Rise of the East India Company* (2019), as the story of another corporate empire. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_AI] Attribute it to "some reviewers".
- A parallel controversy from 2016: India's regulator TRAI banned Facebook's zero-rated "Free Basics" on 8 February 2016 under the "Prohibition of Discriminatory Tariffs for Data Services Regulations, 2016". In January 2016 TRAI had accused Facebook of misleading advertising and astroturfing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet.org] On 10 February 2016 the Facebook board member Marc Andreessen, replying to a critic who called Free Basics "internet colonialism", tweeted: "Anti-colonialism has been economically catastrophic for the Indian people for decades. Why stop now?" He deleted it and apologised, and Facebook said it "strongly reject[s]" the sentiments. [source: WebSearch summary of https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2016/2/11/facebook-board-member-apologises-for-india-tweets and https://qz.com/613815/facebook-board-member-marc-andreessen-indians-shouldve-embraced-free-basics-and-colonialism]

## 5. The labour behind AI
- TIME, Billy Perrigo, 18 January 2023, "OpenAI Used Kenyan Workers on Less Than $2 Per Hour to Make ChatGPT Less Toxic". Workers employed by the contractor Sama labelled violent and sexual-abuse text. Agents took home about $1.32–$1.44 an hour after tax, and quality analysts up to $2. OpenAI's contracts paid Sama $12.50 an hour. Sama ended its OpenAI work in February 2022, eight months early. Sama disputed the figures, saying workers earned $1.46–$3.74 an hour after tax. [source: WebSearch summary of https://time.com/6247678/openai-chatgpt-kenya-workers/; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sama_(company)] One worker described the experience as "torture". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sama_(company)]
- Sama (Samasource) was founded in 2008 by Leila Janah, who coined the term "microwork" and presented it as a way to help poor workers worldwide. It became for-profit in 2019. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sama_(company)]
- Daniel Motaung, a South African moderator for Sama, said he was fired in 2019 for organising a strike. In May 2022 he sued Sama and Meta in Kenya, alleging trauma, pay "as low as $1.50 per hour" and union-busting. A TIME report said moderators were recruited believing they would work in call centres. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sama_(company)]
- 2026 developments: Meta ended its Sama contract in April 2026 after reports that Sama workers were viewing private footage from Ray-Ban Meta AI glasses, and Sama laid off over 1,000 workers in Kenya. Wikipedia says Sama ended its Kenya and Uganda operations on 15 September 2026, but marks this "citation needed". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sama_(company)] (Use only the April 2026 contract end and layoffs, hedged as recent.)
- Scale AI set up the Remotasks platform in 2017 and in 2019 created "Smart Ecosystem Philippines" to run it there. Many Filipino workers were freelance contractors outside labour law. Pay for some tasks fell below one cent after Remotasks expanded to India and Venezuela. Late payments were "commonplace". A 2022 Oxford Internet Institute (Fairwork) study found it met only one of ten fair-work criteria (*Washington Post*, Rebecca Tan, 28 August 2023, "digital sweatshops"). In early 2024 Remotasks dropped several countries, including Kenya, Nigeria and Pakistan (*Rest of World*, 28 March 2024). Meta owns 49% of Scale AI. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_AI]
- Venezuela: Karen Hao and Andrea Paola Hernández, "How the AI industry profits from catastrophe", *MIT Technology Review*, 20 April 2022, argued that Venezuela's economic collapse made it "ground zero" for a new model of labour exploitation in data labelling, with platforms such as Appen. [source: WebSearch summary of https://www.technologyreview.com/2022/04/20/1050392/ai-industry-appen-scale-data-labels/ and https://just-tech.ssrc.org/citation/how-the-ai-industry-profits-from-catastrophe/]
- "Ghost work" was coined by the anthropologist Mary L. Gray and the computer scientist Siddharth Suri in *Ghost Work: How to Stop Silicon Valley from Building a New Global Underclass* (2019). Lilly Irani (UC San Diego) argues that platforms such as Amazon's Mechanical Turk hide workers to sustain an image of technological "magic" for investors. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_work]
- Sama employees helped form the **African Content Moderators Union** in 2023 (the Digital Age chapter dates it to 1 May 2023, Nairobi). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sama_(company)]
- In 2024 Gebru's DAIR institute, with the Weizenbaum Institute and TU Berlin, released a report on the exploitation of "data workers". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_Artificial_Intelligence_Research_Institute]

## 6. Platform power and surveillance capitalism
- Shoshana Zuboff, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019; Profile Books in the UK). Zuboff says Google and Facebook invented surveillance capitalism as "a new logic of accumulation", calls Google its pioneer, and argues that, unlike industrial capitalism, it does not depend on its populations as employees or customers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveillance_capitalism; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Age_of_Surveillance_Capitalism]
- The term was used earlier: Vincent Mosco used "surveillance capitalism" in 2014, and Zuboff set out "prediction products" and "behavioral futures markets" in a lecture published in 2016. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveillance_capitalism]

## 7. Algorithmic governance, facial recognition and the export of surveillance
- Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru, "Gender Shades" (2018), found that commercial gender-classification systems from IBM, Microsoft and Face++ performed worse on darker-skinned faces. Error rates for darker-skinned women were as high as 34.7%, against under 1% for lighter-skinned men. IBM later discontinued its facial-recognition software (2020). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joy_Buolamwini] Buolamwini founded the Algorithmic Justice League in 2016. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_Justice_League]
- In 2019, Gebru and other researchers signed a letter calling on Amazon to stop selling facial recognition to police. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timnit_Gebru]
- China: Skynet was created in 2005, and by 2013 its existence was revealed with over 20 million cameras. In 2019 the "Skynet" system had an estimated 200 million cameras, and over 700 million cameras were reported in China as of August 2023. IHS Markit estimated 770 million cameras worldwide at the end of 2019, about 415.8 million of them in China. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_surveillance_in_China] (These are estimates; attribute them.)
- Xinjiang: the **Integrated Joint Operations Platform (IJOP)**, reported by Human Rights Watch and the *Washington Post* in 2018, gathers biometrics, including DNA, to track individuals, particularly Uyghurs. Since at least 2017 police have forced Uyghurs to install the Jingwang Weishi monitoring app. Xinjiang's security spending rose 90% in 2017 to $8.52 billion. Re-education camps were set up from 2017. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_surveillance_in_China]

Chinese firms (especially Huawei, Hikvision, Dahua and ZTE) supplied the technology to 63 countries, Huawei alone to at least 50, and **US firms (IBM, Palantir, Cisco) to 32**. The index does not separate lawful from abusive use and relies on open-source reporting. [source: WebSearch summary of https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2019/09/the-global-expansion-of-ai-surveillance] (Note for balance: surveillance exports are not only Chinese.)
- The African Digital Rights Network found forms of digital authoritarianism used by governments in ten African countries: South Africa, Cameroon, Zimbabwe, Uganda, Nigeria, Zambia, Sudan, Kenya, Ethiopia and Egypt. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_authoritarianism]
- Clearview AI (US) matches faces against a database it says holds more than 70 billion images scraped from the internet. It is not offered in the EU, UK, New Zealand, Canada or Australia after regulators' actions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clearview_AI]

## 8. Digital sovereignty, the Global South and decolonising AI
- The African Union Executive Council endorsed the **Continental Artificial Intelligence Strategy** ("Harnessing AI for Africa's Development and Prosperity") at its 45th Ordinary Session in **Accra, Ghana, on 18–19 July 2024**, together with the African Digital Compact. It is development-oriented and calls for unified national approaches. (One outlet dates it 9 August 2024, apparently the date the document was posted.) [source: WebSearch summary of https://au.int/en/pressreleases/20240828/african-union-committed-developing-ai-capabilities-africa and https://au.int/sites/default/files/documents/44004-doc-EN-_Continental_AI_Strategy_July_2024.pdf]
- India: the **IndiaAI Mission** was approved on 7 March 2024 with ₹10,371.92 crore. Nearly 45% of the budget goes to a compute portal. In January 2025 the minister Ashwini Vaishnaw said it would build native AI for Indian languages on shared compute of about 18,000+ GPUs (about 10,000 initially, plus 8,693 to be added), mostly Nvidia H100s, at under ₹100 an hour after a 40% subsidy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_industry_in_India]
- India's Bhashini language platform was launched in Gandhinagar in July 2022, aiming to cover all 22 Eighth Schedule languages. BharatGen, a government-funded open-source multilingual foundation-model project, was launched on 30 September 2024. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhashini; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_industry_in_India]
- The 2026 **India AI Impact Summit** in New Delhi was the fourth global AI summit, after Bletchley (2023), Seoul (2024) and Paris (2025), and the first hosted by a Global South country. Modi inaugurated it on 19 February 2026. Wikipedia gives its dates as both 16–20 and 16–21 February 2026, so say "February 2026". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India_AI_Impact_Summit_2026]
- At the Paris AI Action Summit (10–11 February 2025, co-chaired by Macron and Modi), US Vice-President JD Vance warned against "excessive regulation" and against cooperating with "authoritarian regimes". The US and UK refused to sign the declaration on inclusive and sustainable AI. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_Action_Summit_2025]
- Language: "AI is primarily trained on English". As of 2024, most AI systems are trained on only about 100 of the world's roughly 7,000 languages, and models trained mostly on English data tend to present Western views as the norm. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics_of_artificial_intelligence]
- BLOOM (2022), from the volunteer BigScience workshop, is a 176-billion-parameter open-access model for 46 natural languages and 13 programming languages. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLOOM_(language_model)]
- **Masakhane** ("we build together" in isiZulu) is a grassroots African NLP community started in 2019 by Jade Abbott and Laura Martinus (South Africa), around the Deep Learning Indaba. Its aim is "to strengthen and spur NLP research in African languages, for Africans, by Africans". [source: WebSearch summary of https://venturebeat.com/ai/the-masakhane-project-wants-machine-translation-and-ai-to-transform-africa and https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.11529] (Secondary; founding details vary: some credit Abbott alone.)
- **Decolonial AI:** Shakir Mohamed, Marie-Therese Png and William Isaac, "Decolonial AI: Decolonial Theory as Sociotechnical Foresight in Artificial Intelligence", *Philosophy & Technology* 33(4), 2020, pp. 659–684 (preprint July 2020). They propose three tactics: a critical technical practice of AI, reverse tutelage and reverse pedagogies, and renewed affective and political communities. [source: WebSearch summary of https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s13347-020-00405-8.pdf and https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.04068] (The authors were then at DeepMind; this was not verified, so do not state it.)
- **Gebru and the AI-ethics dissidents:** Timnit Gebru was born in 1983 in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. She is of Eritrean origin and fled at 15 during the Eritrean–Ethiopian War. She joined Google in 2018 to co-lead its Ethical AI team with Margaret Mitchell. Her departure in December 2020, over a paper Google asked her to retract ("On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots", with Emily Bender, Angelina McMillan-Major and Mitchell, the last under the pseudonym "Shmargaret Shmitchell"), is disputed. Google said it accepted her resignation, and Gebru says she had not resigned. About 2,700 Google employees and over 4,300 academics and supporters signed a letter condemning her alleged firing. Mitchell was also fired, in February 2021. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timnit_Gebru; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_parrot]
- Gebru co-founded the Black in AI network (with Rediet Abebe; the article confirms a Black in AI link but this co-founder detail was not checked; say only "Black in AI"). She founded the **Distributed AI Research Institute (DAIR)** in December 2021, "an independent, community-rooted institute set to counter Big Tech's pervasive influence on the research, development and deployment of AI". Alex Hanna and Dylan Baker left Google to join it in February 2023. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_Artificial_Intelligence_Research_Institute; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timnit_Gebru]

## 9. Development arguments and unequal exposure
- IMF (report of 14 January 2024; remarks by Kristalina Georgieva): AI would expose almost 40% of global employment. About 60% of jobs are exposed in advanced economies, 40% in emerging markets and 26% in low-income countries, and about half of exposed jobs in advanced economies could be hurt while others gain productivity. The IMF warned that poorer countries are less ready to capture the benefits, which "could exacerbate the digital divide and cross-country income disparity". [source: WebSearch summary of press coverage, e.g. https://www.scmp.com/news/world/europe/article/3248417/ai-affect-60-cent-advanced-economy-jobs-imfs-kristalina-georgieva] (Press coverage, not the IMF text.)
- The AU strategy, the IndiaAI Mission and BharatGen frame AI as a development tool under national or continental control, not only as a threat (sources above).

## Debates
- **Is "colonialism" the right word?** Couldry and Mejias insist that data colonialism is not a metaphor but a new phase of colonial appropriation (per the *Lawfare* review). Critics argue for analytical precision: Orit Halpern cautions against "historical flattening" through broad analogies between past empires and platforms. Others say the concept treats decoloniality as a motif, is essentialist about "colonialism", or flattens how data power works in different places. Supporters call it "compelling and useful". [source: WebSearch summary of critiques, incl. https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/handle/1887/4257815 and https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/uneven-development-on-data-colonialism-history-and-the-archive/] (No source found arguing the analogy "trivialises" colonial violence; do not attribute that charge to anyone.)
- **Empire of firms or of states?** Hao frames OpenAI as an empire and some reviewers compare it to the East India Company. Others stress state rivalry: the 2017 Chinese plan, Putin's 2017 remark, Hogarth's "AI nationalism" (2018), and the US export controls of 2022–25. The two views meet in Stargate (a White House-announced private venture) and in China's "national AI teams" of firms such as Baidu, Tencent, Alibaba, SenseTime and iFlytek. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_AI; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_nationalism; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_industry_in_China]
- **Precedents and their limits:** Headrick's work shows cables, steamships and medicine enabling empire, and Britain owned two-thirds of the world's cables in 1892. Headrick himself treats technology as an element, not a sufficient cause. Whether AI repeats that pattern is an argument, not a finding. Note the irony that the military genealogy is real (ARPA, SAGE, Maven), while the Dartmouth workshop itself was philanthropically funded. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_R._Headrick; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_communications_cable; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_workshop]
- **Is the "silicon shield" real?** It is the deterrence argument for Taiwan, challenged by Matthew Pottinger in 2022. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_shield]
- **Export of surveillance, Chinese or global?** Feldstein's index found Chinese firms in 63 countries but US firms in 32. Digital-authoritarianism studies stress China, while the ADRN study shows African governments' own agency. [sources above]
- **Labour, exploitation or opportunity?** Samasource was founded (2008) to bring "microwork" to poor workers. TIME (2023), the *Washington Post* (2023) and MIT Technology Review (2022) documented low pay, trauma and precarity, and Sama disputed TIME's pay figures. Present both. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sama_(company); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_AI]
- **Environmental costs, how big?** The IEA projects data centres doubling to about 945 TWh by 2030 but at only about a tenth of demand growth. Water claims are highly sensitive to method (note Hao's 1,000-fold Chile error). [sources above]
- **Can AI be governed differently?** Compare the EU's risk-based AI Act (in the Digital Age chapter); the US swing from EO 14110 (2023) to EO 14179 (2025); the US and UK refusal to sign the Paris declaration (2025); the AU's development-oriented strategy (2024); India's state-funded compute and language models (2024–26); grassroots efforts such as Masakhane and DAIR; and decolonial proposals (Mohamed, Png and Isaac 2020).
- **Inside critics:** Crawford works at Microsoft Research, and Gebru and Mitchell were at Google. The AI-ethics critique came partly from inside Big Tech, and the 2020–21 departures became emblematic of its limits. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kate_Crawford; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timnit_Gebru]
