## The question and the field: definitions
- The history of emotions is a field of historical research on human emotion, "especially variations among cultures and historical periods in the experience and expression of emotions"; it began in the 20th century with writers such as Lucien Febvre and Peter Gay. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_emotions]
- Like the sociology and anthropology of emotions, it assumes that not only the expression of feelings but the feelings themselves are learned; many historians therefore treat emotion as a category of analysis as fundamental as class, race or gender. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_emotions]
- Main analytic concepts in current use (Katie Barclay's 2021 "State of the Field" in *History*): emotional communities, emotional regimes, emotional styles and emotional practices. Main debates: universalist versus constructivist accounts (are basic emotions a human universal?), and how far historians should study named emotion words versus unnamed affect visible, for instance, in body language. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_emotions]
- Recent work (for example Rob Boddice, *The History of Emotions*, Manchester UP, 2018) engages the neurosciences and frames the field as part of a "biocultural" historicism; since the 2010s some historians also use computer-assisted "sentiment mining" of digitised texts (e.g. Dutch theatre texts 1600–1800; Dutch parliamentary debates 1945–1989). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_emotions]

## Febvre's call (1941) and Elias's civilizing process (1939)
- Lucien Febvre (22 July 1878 – 11 September 1956), French historian, co-founder of the *Annales* school. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucien_Febvre]
- His essay "La sensibilité et l'histoire. Comment reconstituer la vie affective d'autrefois?" appeared in *Annales d'histoire sociale* 3 (1941), pp. 5–20; it is cited as the main precursor of the field ("histoire des sensibilités"). One French encyclopedia dates it to 1942; JSTOR-style records give Jan.–June 1941. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_emotions; WebSearch (universalis.fr; JSTOR record)]
- Febvre argued that historians had left emotional life almost entirely out of their reconstructions of past societies. Barbara Rosenwein, reviewing the field in 2002, called his 1941 call "public policy masquerading as history" (her p. 823), i.e. driven by present concerns. [source: WebSearch summarising Mandrou and Rosenwein, "Worrying about Emotions in History", AHR 107:3 (2002), 821–45 — https://alioshabielenberg.com/?p=862]
- Norbert Elias (22 June 1897, Breslau – 1 August 1990, Amsterdam), German-Jewish sociologist, later British citizen. Fled to Paris in 1933, Britain in 1935; his mother was deported to Theresienstadt on 30 August 1942 and murdered in Treblinka. Interned as an "enemy alien" in 1940 (Liverpool, Isle of Man). First secure academic post at Leicester in 1954, aged 57; professor of sociology at the University of Ghana, Legon, 1962–64. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norbert_Elias]
- *Über den Prozeß der Zivilisation* (*The Civilizing Process*) was published in two volumes in Basel in 1939; virtually ignored because of the war, it became famous after republication in 1969. In 1998 the International Sociological Association ranked it the seventh most important sociological book of the 20th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Civilizing_Process]
- Argument: from roughly the post-medieval period, European standards on violence, sexual behaviour, bodily functions, table manners and speech were transformed by rising "thresholds of shame and repugnance", spreading outward from court etiquette; internalised self-restraint was linked to the rise of the centralised state and ever denser webs of social interdependence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norbert_Elias; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Civilizing_Process]
- Critique: Rosenwein (2002) identified a "grand narrative" in which Western civilisation = increasing emotional restraint, with Elias at its centre, resting on a "hydraulic" model of emotions as "great liquids within each person, heaving and frothing, eager to be let out"; she proposed instead plural "emotional communities" existing in every period. [source: WebSearch summarising Rosenwein 2002 — https://alioshabielenberg.com/?p=862]

## The field's tools
### Emotionology (Stearns and Stearns, 1985)
- Peter N. Stearns (born 3 March 1936, London, to American parents), historian at George Mason University (provost 2000–2014). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Stearns]
- Peter N. Stearns and Carol Z. Stearns, "Emotionology: Clarifying the History of Emotions and Emotional Standards", *American Historical Review* 90:4 (1985), 813–836 (WP gives pp. 813–830). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_emotions; WebSearch]
- Emotionology = the standards by which a society evaluates emotions and the institutions that reflect and promote them, distinguished from emotional experience itself; the Stearnses argued emotion is partly cognitive and therefore social, so its forms can vary between and within societies. They drew heavily on prescriptive sources such as advice manuals. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Stearns; WebSearch (historycooperative.org)]
- Books: Carol Z. Stearns and Peter N. Stearns, *Anger: The Struggle for Emotional Control in America's History* (University of Chicago Press; WP lists 1989 — see Not verified); Peter N. Stearns, *American Cool: Constructing a Twentieth-Century Emotional Style* (NYU Press, 1994). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Stearns]
- *American Cool* argues that from the 1920s (or a little before) the Victorian middle-class emotional culture eroded and a new aversion to emotional intensity — "cool" — solidified by the 1960s; in the 1890s American men were still advised by leading authorities to use their anger. [source: WebSearch (NYU Press open-access text, library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/88279)]

### Emotives and emotional regimes (Reddy, 1997/2001)
- William M. Reddy (born 30 June 1947), historian and cultural anthropologist, Duke University (William T. Laprade Distinguished Professor Emeritus since 2017). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Reddy]
- Reddy introduced "emotives" in "Against Constructionism: The Historical Ethnography of Emotions", *Current Anthropology* 38 (1997), 327–351, and developed them in *The Navigation of Feeling: A Framework for the History of Emotions* (Cambridge UP, 2001). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotive_(sociology); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Reddy]
- An emotive, modelled on J. L. Austin's performative speech acts, is an emotional utterance that both describes and alters the speaker's feeling: saying "I am angry" or "I love you" helps make, intensify or hide the feeling. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotive_(sociology)]
- An "emotional regime" is the set of normative emotions plus the official rituals, practices and emotives that express and inculcate them (Rosenwein's summary of Reddy). Strict regimes produce "emotional suffering"; people seek "emotional refuges"; Reddy proposes "emotional liberty" as a criterion for judging regimes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion_regime; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_emotions]
- Reddy's case study is France c. 1700–1850: court honour codes formed a strict regime; salons and literary circles were emotional refuges; a "sentimentalist" regime prizing sincerity and transparent feeling fed the Revolution, whose demands for visible proofs of love of the patrie and hatred of enemies escalated into spirals of suspicion and the Terror; after Robespierre a more sceptical post-sentimental order followed ("liberal reason, romantic passions"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion_regime]
- Reddy also wrote *The Making of Romantic Love: Longing and Sexuality in Europe, South Asia, and Japan, 900–1200 CE* (University of Chicago Press, 2012), a comparative global study. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Reddy]

### Emotional communities (Rosenwein, 2002/2006)
- Barbara H. Rosenwein (born 1 March 1945, Chicago), medievalist at Loyola University Chicago (1971–2014). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_H._Rosenwein]
- She introduced "emotional communities" in "Worrying about Emotions in History", AHR 107:3 (2002), 821–845, and *Emotional Communities in the Early Middle Ages* (Cornell UP, 2006). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_emotions; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_H._Rosenwein]
- Emotional communities are social groups (monasteries, courts, families, political circles) that share norms about which emotions are valued, how they are expressed and interpreted; several exist side by side in any society, overlapping or competing. Contrasted with Reddy's top-down regimes, her emphasis is horizontal plurality. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_H._Rosenwein; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion_regime]
- Later books: *Generations of Feeling: A History of Emotions, 600–1700* (Cambridge UP; WP gives both 2015 and 2016); with Riccardo Cristiani, *What Is the History of Emotions?* (Polity; WP gives 2017 and 2018); *Anger: The Conflicted History of an Emotion* (Yale, 2020); *Love: A History in Five Fantasies* (Polity, 2022). She edited *Anger's Past* (1998). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_H._Rosenwein]

### Emotional practices (Scheer, 2012) and other frameworks
- Monique Scheer (born 1967, Tulsa), historical and cultural anthropologist, University of Tübingen; research scholar at the Berlin Center for the History of Emotions from 2008. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monique_Scheer]
- Monique Scheer, "Are Emotions a Kind of Practice (and Is That What Makes Them Have a History)? A Bourdieuan Approach to Understanding Emotion", *History and Theory* 51:2 (May 2012), 193–220: emotions as embodied, habitual practices (drawing on Bourdieu's habitus). Her book *Enthusiasm: Emotional Practices of Conviction in Modern Germany* (OUP, 2020). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monique_Scheer; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_emotions]
- "Emotional style" was discussed by Benno Gammerl, *Rethinking History* 16:2 (2012). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_emotions]
- Jan Plamper (23 March 1970 – 30 November 2023), German historian (Goldsmiths 2012–2021, then Limerick); Dilthey Fellow at Frevert's Berlin centre 2008–2012. Wrote *Geschichte und Gefühl. Grundlagen der Emotionsgeschichte* (Siedler, 2012), in English *The History of Emotions: An Introduction* (OUP, 2015); and the 2010 *History and Theory* interview with Reddy, Rosenwein and Stearns (vol. 49:2, 237–265). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Plamper; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_emotions]
- Thomas Dixon, *From Passions to Emotions: The Creation of a Secular Psychological Category* (Cambridge UP, 2003): "emotions" as a single psychological category is modern; by about 1850 it had absorbed older terms (passions, affections, sentiments) in most English-language psychological writing; Scottish philosopher Thomas Brown (1778–1820) is central; the book runs from Augustine and Aquinas to Bain, Spencer and Darwin. A critic (Rosenkrantz, *Journal of the History of Philosophy*) argued Dixon overstates Brown's originality and that the real shift came in the seventeenth century. [source: WebSearch (Cambridge UP sample pages, assets.cambridge.org/97805218/27294; Metapsychology review; JHP review)]

## The "rational self": passions, reason and sympathy
- René Descartes, *Les Passions de l'âme* (*The Passions of the Soul*), completed 1649, his final treatise, dedicated to Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia, with whom he had corresponded since 1643; it synthesised that exchange. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passions_of_the_Soul]
- Descartes treated the passions physiologically, via "animal spirits" produced in the blood (a role like the nervous system): "My design is not to explain the passions as an Orator ... nor even as a Philosopher, but only as a Physicist" (letter, 14 August 1649). He named six primitive passions: wonder, love, hatred, desire, joy and sadness (art. 69). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passions_of_the_Soul]
- Note for the writer: Descartes did not simply reject emotion; his "rational self" is the mind-body dualism that Damasio later called an "error" (see Neuroscience).
- David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40), Book 2 "Of the Passions": reason alone cannot motivate action and cannot oppose a passion; "'Tis not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Treatise_of_Human_Nature]
- Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759): "sympathy" was Smith's term for the feeling of understanding others' passions, working by imaginative mirroring in a spectator; it built on the moral sense tradition of Shaftesbury, Hutcheson and Hume; moral judgement is formed by an imagined "impartial spectator". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Theory_of_Moral_Sentiments]
- Cross-reference: the published Enlightenment chapter already introduces Hume (on religion and causation) and Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776) and Anton Wilhelm Amo's 1734 dissertation criticising Descartes's mind-body account; it does not discuss the passions.

## Sensibility, sentiment and public weeping (eighteenth century)
- "Sensibility" — acute responsiveness, including to others' emotions — emerged in eighteenth-century Britain from sense-based philosophy (Locke's *Essay*, 1690) and nerve medicine: George Cheyne's "English Malady" blamed over-taxed nerves, while sensitive nerves were thought to give keener perception of beauty and moral truth. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensibility]
- Sentimental novels featured characters prone to weeping, fainting and fits as signs of fine feeling; key English titles: Richardson's *Pamela* (1740), *Clarissa* (1748); Sterne's *A Sentimental Journey* (1768); Mackenzie's *The Man of Feeling* (1771); continental: Rousseau's *Julie, or the New Heloise* (1761), Goethe's *The Sorrows of Young Werther* (1774). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentimental_novel; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julie;_or,_The_New_Heloise]
- *Julie* (Amsterdam: Marc-Michel Rey, 1761) was perhaps the best-selling novel of the eighteenth century; readers wrote to Rousseau "in droves" describing their "tears", "sighs", "torments" and "ecstasies" — one said violent sobbing cured his cold (Robert Darnton, "Readers Respond to Rousseau", in *The Great Cat Massacre*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julie;_or,_The_New_Heloise]
- *Werther* (1774) caused "Werther fever"; the novel and Werther-style dress were banned in Leipzig in 1775, and the book also in Denmark and Italy, over fears of imitative suicide (the "Werther effect"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sorrows_of_Young_Werther]
- Backlash: Samuel Johnson's *Idler* No. 100 mocked performative pity; by the 1790s anti-sensibility writers associated emotional volatility with the French Revolution and branded "sensible" figures as unpatriotic and effeminate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensibility]
- Sentimental literature encouraged identification with the weak (orphans, condemned criminals) and fed campaigns against the death penalty and slavery; Harriet Beecher Stowe's *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (1852) is a landmark American sentimental novel. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentimental_novel]

## Victorian restraint and the stiff upper lip
- "Stiff upper lip" = fortitude and self-restraint in expressing emotion, traditionally attributed to the British; it became symbolic of Victorian English public-school training (character-building sport, as in Newbolt's "Vitaï Lampada", corporal punishment, cold showers). Commonly cited examples: Uxbridge at Waterloo, HMS *Birkenhead* (1852), Captain Oates (1912). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stiff_upper_lip]
- Thomas Dixon, *Weeping Britannia: Portrait of a Nation in Tears* (OUP, 2015), traces six centuries of British weeping from Margery Kempe onward and argues the stiff upper lip was a relatively brief product of a militaristic, imperial era at the end of the Victorian period (reviewers summarise it as roughly 1870–1945), later projected back as a timeless national trait. [source: WebSearch (reviews: reviews.history.ac.uk/?p=56700; Spectator Australia; Prospect)]
- Stearns's *American Cool* similarly dates American emotional "cool" to the twentieth century, not the Victorian era, which in his account still prized intense, if channelled, feeling (see Tools).

## Emotions as practices in time: fear, love, anger, grief, nostalgia, melancholy, trauma
- Nostalgia: coined in 1688 by Johannes Hofer (1669–1752) in a Basel medical dissertation, from Greek *nostos* (return home) and *algos* (pain), for the homesickness of Swiss mercenaries ("mal du Suisse"); Swiss songs were reportedly forbidden to them (Rousseau, *Dictionnaire de musique*, 1767); still diagnosed among soldiers in the American Civil War; by the 1870s medical interest had almost vanished. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nostalgia]
- Melancholy: Greek *melaina cholē*, "black bile"; Hippocrates treated melancholia as a disease of excess black bile; the 10th-century Persian physician Al-Akhawayni Bukhari described it as a chronic illness caused by black bile acting on the brain; Robert Burton's *The Anatomy of Melancholy* (1621) treated it as both literary and medical. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melancholia]
- Shell shock: the term was first published by Charles Myers in *The Lancet*, 13 February 1915; during 1917 it was banned as a diagnosis in the British Army; a War Office Committee of Enquiry into "Shell-Shock" reported in 1922. Of about 240,000 British courts martial and 3,080 death sentences in the war, 346 were carried out; 268 for desertion and 18 for cowardice; on 7 November 2006 the UK gave the executed a posthumous conditional pardon. Sassoon and Owen were at Craiglockhart War Hospital. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shell_shock]
- Love across cultures: the Arabic story of Layla and Majnun concerns the 7th-century poet Qays ibn al-Mulawwah, called Majnun ("mad", lit. "possessed by jinn") for his love; Nizami Ganjavi's Persian narrative poem (1188) carried it into Persian, Turkish and Indic literatures (Amir Khusrow 1299, Jami 1484, Fuzuli d. 1556). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Layla_and_Majnun]
- Ibn Hazm's *Ṭawq al-Ḥamāmah* (*The Ring of the Dove*), Córdoba/al-Andalus, 1022, a treatise on love emphasising restraint and chastity; one theory holds that troubadour courtly love was influenced by Arabic poetry of al-Andalus (María Rosa Menocal, 1987), and von Grunebaum traced "love for love's sake" to Arabic literature of the 9th–10th centuries. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ring_of_the_Dove; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtly_love]
- Anger in America: see Stearns and Stearns, *Anger*; Rosenwein, *Anger* (2020). Grief and anger among the Ilongot: see Anthropology section.

## Honour, shame and humiliation
- Ruth Benedict's *The Chrysanthemum and the Sword* (1946) popularised the distinction between "guilt cultures" (American) and "shame cultures" (Japanese); in a shame or "honour–shame" culture, control works through shame and the threat of ostracism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guilt%E2%80%93shame%E2%80%93fear_spectrum_of_cultures]
- J. G. Peristiany (ed.), *Honour and Shame: The Values of Mediterranean Society* (London: Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 1965), grew from a 1959 Wenner-Gren conference initiated by Julian Pitt-Rivers; it includes Pitt-Rivers, "Honour and Social Status". Michael Herzfeld, "Honour and Shame: Problems in the Comparative Analysis of Moral Systems", *Man* 15 (1980), 339–351, led the critique of treating the Mediterranean as a single honour-and-shame culture area ("Mediterraneanism"). [source: WebSearch (econbiz.de record; journal articles citing Herzfeld 1980)]
- Reddy's France: honour codes of the absolutist court formed a strict emotional regime; see also his *The Invisible Code: Honor and Sentiment in Postrevolutionary France, 1815–1848* (1997). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion_regime; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Reddy]
- Ute Frevert (born 10 June 1954, Schötmar): *Men of Honour: A Social and Cultural History of the Duel* (English 1995); *Emotions in History – Lost and Found* (CEU Press, 2011); *Learning How to Feel* (OUP, 2014, with others); *The Politics of Humiliation: A Modern History* (OUP, 2020). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ute_Frevert]
- *The Politics of Humiliation* distinguishes public shaming (peaking in early modern Europe with state-decreed pillories, beatings, branding) from modern humiliation; liberal societies largely abolished state shaming but not humiliation, which persists in, e.g., international politics (chapter 3, "Honour and the Language of Humiliation in International Politics"). [source: WebSearch (reviews.history.ac.uk/review/2464)]

## Beyond Europe: non-Western concepts and the anthropology of emotion
- Rasa (India): the *Natyashastra*, attributed to Bharata Muni and probably completed between 200 BCE and 200 CE, devotes chapter 6 to rasa — the aesthetic "relish" of emotion evoked in an audience from underlying *bhava* (states). It names eight rasas: śṛṅgāra (love), hāsya (mirth), raudra (fury), karuṇa (compassion), bībhatsa (disgust), bhayānaka (terror), vīra (heroism), adbhuta (wonder). A ninth, śānta (peace), was added later after debate between the 6th and 10th centuries; Abhinavagupta (c. 1000 CE) gave the fullest exposition. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rasa_(aesthetics); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natya_Shastra]
- China: *qing* (情) is translated "emotion", "feeling", "sentiment" or "passion"; the *Book of Rites* names seven emotions (qiqing): happiness, anger, grief, fear, love, hate, desire. Daoist teaching (Zhuangzi) aimed at freedom from qing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qing_(philosophy)]
- Mencius (c. 371 – c. 289 BCE): the "four beginnings" — commiseration (beginning of humanity), shame and dislike (righteousness), deference (propriety), sense of right and wrong (wisdom) — illustrated by everyone's alarm at a child about to fall into a well (*Mencius* 2A:6); against Xunzi's view that human nature is bad. Here moral feelings are the root of virtue, not reason's opposite. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mencius]
- Japan: psychoanalyst Takeo Doi (Doi Takeo, 1920–2009) made *amae* — dependence on another's indulgent benevolence — central to Japanese relationships in *Amae no Kōzō* (1971; English *The Anatomy of Dependence*, Kodansha, 1973). Critics say he overstated its uniqueness (nihonjinron). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amae; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takeo_Doi]
- Ifaluk (Micronesia): Catherine Lutz (born 1952), *Unnatural Emotions: Everyday Sentiments on a Micronesian Atoll and Their Challenge to Western Theory* (University of Chicago Press, 1988). Ifaluk *fago* combines compassion, love and sadness; *song* is "justifiable anger", a pro-social moral response to wrongdoing, associated with clan chiefs and flowing down the hierarchy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_Lutz; WebSearch (peacefulsocieties.uncg.edu summary of Lutz; press.uchicago.edu)]
- Ilongot (Bugkalot), northern Luzon, Philippines: Michelle Zimbalist Rosaldo (1944–1981) and Renato Rosaldo did fieldwork there 1967–69 and in 1974; Michelle published *Knowledge and Passion: Ilongot Notions of Self and Social Life* (Cambridge UP, 1980) and died in an accidental fall during fieldwork in 1981 (11 October 1981). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelle_Rosaldo; WebSearch (Duke UP page for *The Day of Shelly's Death*)]
- Renato Rosaldo's essay "Grief and a Headhunter's Rage" reports an older Ilongot man's explanation that rage born of grief impelled headhunting; Rosaldo said he could not grasp it until his own bereavement. First appeared 1984; reprinted in *Culture and Truth* (1989). [source: WebSearch (Kyoto SEAS article, kyoto-seas.org/pdf/49/2/490203.pdf; Duke UP)]
- Colonial India: Margrit Pernau, *Emotions and Modernity in Colonial India: From Balance to Fervor* (OUP India, 2019), using Urdu sources 1857–1914, traces a shift from an ideal of balance to a valuing of intense, untameable passion as a sign of a community's vitality. [source: WebSearch (india.oup.com product page; library records)]

## Universalism: Darwin, Ekman and critics
- Charles Darwin, *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals*, published by John Murray on 26 November 1872, quickly sold almost 7,000 copies; it argued expressions are evolved and shared with animals, with culture only auxiliary: "The young and the old of widely different races, both with man and animals, express the same state of mind by the same movements." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Expression_of_the_Emotions_in_Man_and_Animals]
- Darwin circulated a questionnaire from 1867 through missionaries, merchants and travellers asking about expression among different peoples, and used photographs (including Duchenne de Boulogne's). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Expression_of_the_Emotions_in_Man_and_Animals]
- Paul Ekman (15 February 1934 – 17 November 2025), US psychologist: against the 1950s anthropological view (e.g. Margaret Mead) that expressions are learned, he and Wallace Friesen reported recognition among the preliterate Fore of Papua New Guinea ("Constants across cultures in the face and emotion", 1971); with colleagues he named six basic emotions — anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise — later expanded; Facial Action Coding System finalised 1978. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Ekman; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion_classification]
- Critiques: methods called circular (posed photographs matched to the same preset labels); studies failed to confirm discrete expressions; TSA's SPOT airport screening based partly on his work was criticised as untested; Mead criticised him in 1975. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Ekman]
- Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez and Pollak, "Emotional Expressions Reconsidered", *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 20:1 (2019), reviewing more than 1,000 studies, concluded that facial movements are not universally diagnostic of emotions; Barrett's press summary: people scowl when angry about 25% of the time. [source: WebSearch (psychologicalscience.org report page; PMC6640856)]

## The neuroscience turn
- Joseph E. LeDoux (born 7 December 1949, Eunice, Louisiana), NYU neuroscientist: *The Emotional Brain* (Simon & Schuster, 1996); his rat fear-conditioning work proposed two routes to the amygdala — a quick subcortical "low road" and a slower cortical "high road". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_E._LeDoux]
- LeDoux later (2012, 2015) rejected calling these "fear circuits": they are "survival circuits" for detecting and responding to threat; conscious fear is constructed by cortical circuits; "I'm not studying emotion. I'm studying the function of survival circuits." Books: *Anxious* (2015), *The Deep History of Ourselves* (2019). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_E._LeDoux]
- "Amygdala hijack" was coined by Daniel Goleman in *Emotional Intelligence* (1995), popularising LeDoux's findings. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amygdala_hijack]
- Antonio Damasio (born 25 February 1944, Lisbon), USC: *Descartes' Error: Emotion, Reason, and the Human Brain* (Putnam, 1994; the title has no "s" after the apostrophe). Using the 1848 Phineas Gage case and modern patients with frontal damage, he argued reasoning needs emotional signals from the body; Descartes's mind-body separation was the "error". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes%27_Error; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Damasio]
- Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis (emotional body states, processed in ventromedial prefrontal cortex and amygdala, bias decisions) was tested with the Iowa gambling task (Bechara et al., 1994). Later book: *The Feeling of What Happens* (1999/2000 per WP; NYT best books 2001 — see Not verified). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somatic_marker_hypothesis; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Damasio]
- Lisa Feldman Barrett (born 1963, Toronto), Northeastern University: theory of constructed emotion (earlier "conceptual act model", 2006), popularised in *How Emotions Are Made: The Secret Life of the Brain* (Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, 2017). Emotions are not hard-wired "circuits" but constructed in the moment by the whole brain from interoception, concepts (culturally learned) and social reality; analogy: continuous wavelengths sorted into culturally variable colour categories. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_constructed_emotion; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisa_Feldman_Barrett]
- Limits of brain imaging: Lindquist, Wager, Kober, Bliss-Moreau and Barrett, "The brain basis of emotion: a meta-analytic review", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 35:3 (2012), 121–143, found "little evidence that discrete emotion categories can be consistently and specifically localized to distinct brain regions"; the amygdala is active across anger, disgust, fear, happiness and sadness, not fear alone. [source: WebSearch (PubMed/Cambridge record, doi 10.1017/S0140525X11000446; Lindquist & Barrett, *TiCS* 2012)]
- Historians' dialogue: constructionist neuroscience (Barrett) converges with historians' claim that emotions are learned; Boddice frames a "biocultural" history; Reddy's emotives were offered explicitly "against constructionism" as a middle path. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_emotions]

## Emotional labour and capitalism
- Arlie Russell Hochschild (born 15 January 1940, Boston), UC Berkeley sociologist. "Emotion Work, Feeling Rules, and Social Structure", *American Journal of Sociology* 85:3 (1979), 551–75, introduced "feeling rules" (shared standards for how one ought to feel). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Managed_Heart; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion_regime]
- *The Managed Heart: Commercialization of Human Feeling* (University of California Press, 1983) studied flight attendants (trained to stay calm and cheerful, and to manage passengers' fears while suppressing their own) and bill collectors (trained to feel suspicion of debtors). She coined "emotional labor"; distinguished "surface acting" (displaying feelings one lacks) from "deep acting" (changing one's feelings to fit); emotional labour falls disproportionately on women. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Managed_Heart; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_labor; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arlie_Russell_Hochschild]
- Her fieldwork included Delta Air Lines' stewardess training centre, where (she recalls) a pilot told trainees to smile "like you really mean it"; post-1978 US airline deregulation pushed competition toward service. [source: WebSearch (UC Press edition text, supersummary.com summary) — wording secondary, use paraphrase]
- Later work extends the theme globally: Filipina nannies who leave their own children to care for American children (*Global Woman*, the "global care chain"); *The Second Shift* (1989); *Strangers in Their Own Land: Anger and Mourning on the American Right* (2016); *Stolen Pride: Loss, Shame, and the Rise of the Right* (2024). Hochschild has said popular use of "emotional labor" has suffered "concept creep" (2018). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arlie_Russell_Hochschild; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_labor]

## Debates
- Universalism vs construction: Darwin and Ekman (evolved, universal basic emotions and expressions) versus anthropologists (Mead, Lutz, Rosaldo) and Barrett's constructed emotion; Barrett et al. (2019) and Lindquist et al. (2012) challenge universal facial signals and dedicated brain regions; Ekman's defenders point to cross-cultural recognition data. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Ekman; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_constructed_emotion]
- Reddy's middle path: emotives were proposed "against constructionism", allowing a universal capacity for feeling while historicising its expression and political management (and a normative criterion, emotional liberty). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotive_(sociology); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion_regime]
- Regimes vs communities: Reddy's top-down, state-linked regimes versus Rosenwein's plural, overlapping communities. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_H._Rosenwein]
- Progress narrative: Elias's long rise of self-restraint (the "civilizing process") versus Rosenwein's rejection of the "grand narrative" and "hydraulic" model; Dixon's and Stearns's dating of restraint to a recent period (c. late 19th–mid 20th century) also undercuts a linear story. [source: WebSearch (Rosenwein 2002 summary); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norbert_Elias]
- Words vs experience: Dixon shows "emotion" itself is a modern category (c. 1800–1850 in English); critics say the shift came in the seventeenth century. Barclay notes debate over named emotions versus unnamed affect. [source: WebSearch (Dixon reviews); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_emotions]
- Honour-shame and Mediterraneanism: Peristiany/Pitt-Rivers model versus Herzfeld's critique of a homogeneous culture area; Benedict's guilt/shame dichotomy is now widely seen as reductive (the WP article flags unsourced shame-culture characterisations as possible "Orientalism"). [source: WebSearch (Herzfeld 1980 citations); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guilt%E2%80%93shame%E2%80%93fear_spectrum_of_cultures]
- Uniqueness claims: Doi's amae criticised as nihonjinron overstatement; cross-cultural psychologists liken it to attachment theory. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amae]
- Emotional labour: Hochschild warns of "concept creep" as the term spread to unpaid domestic work and "mental load". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_labor]
