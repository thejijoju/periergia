## Domestication and staple crops (cross-refer to The Neolithic Revolution; do not repeat its detail)
- Asian rice (*Oryza sativa*) was domesticated in China; WP's lead gives "approximately 13,500 to 8,200 years ago", the body cites Fornasiero et al. (2022) for "9,000 years ago", in the Yangtze region. African rice (*Oryza glaberrima*) was domesticated independently in West Africa about 3,000 years ago. Rice is the staple of over half the world's population. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice]
- *Indica* rice appears to have arisen when *japonica* reached India about 4,500 years ago and hybridised with local wild/proto-indica rice (Choi et al. 2017). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice]
- Wheat: the archaeological record suggests it was first cultivated in the Fertile Crescent around 9600 BCE; hunter-gatherers in West Asia harvested wild wheats for thousands of years before domestication. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat]
- Maize was domesticated by Indigenous peoples in southern Mexico about 9,000 years ago from wild teosinte. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize]
- Potato: single origin in southern Peru / extreme north-west Bolivia (around Lake Titicaca), domesticated about 7,000–10,000 years ago from the *Solanum brevicaule* complex (Spooner et al. 2005). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato]
- Cassava (manioc): progenitor populations centred in west-central Brazil, where it was likely first domesticated no more than 10,000 years ago. Today it is the third-largest source of carbohydrates in the tropics after rice and maize; more than 500 million people depend on it; Nigeria is the largest producer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassava]
- Sorghum was domesticated more than 5,000 years ago in eastern Sudan (Atbara and Gash rivers); impressions near Kassala date to 3500–3000 BCE (Butana Group); sorghum bread survives in a Predynastic Egyptian grave of c. 3100 BCE (Museo Egizio, Turin). It is the world's fifth-most important cereal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum]
- Pearl millet: domesticated in the West African Sahel; domesticated grains from the Tilemsi Valley, northern Mali, date to 2500–2000 BCE (Manning et al. 2011). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_millet]
- Sugarcane (*Saccharum officinarum*) was first domesticated in the New Guinea area; Austronesian sailors carried it to Polynesia, Island Melanesia and Madagascar, and to India and then southern China by 500 BCE. Strabo's sources reported "reeds in India" that "yield honey without bees". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugarcane]

## Food and empire
- Rome's *cura annonae*: Gaius Gracchus's grain law was approved in 123 BC; initially about 40,000 adult male citizens were eligible. In 58 BC Clodius gave free grain to an estimated 320,000; Caesar reduced this to 150,000; Augustus set it at about 200,000, and numbers stayed roughly stable until near the end of the Western Empire. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cura_annonae]
- Under the Severans (Septimius Severus, 193–211) grain was replaced by bread and olive oil added; Aurelian (270–275) added wine and pork. Egypt, North Africa (Carthage region) and Sicily were the main sources; the Vandal takeover of Africa (c. 439) cost the West most of its grain supply. Rickman estimates Rome needed 40 million *modii* (about 272,000 tonnes) a year; Erdkamp a minimum of 150,000 tonnes. Rome's population is estimated at about 1 million by the 2nd century AD. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cura_annonae]
- Tiberius in AD 22 called the grain supply an imperial duty whose neglect would bring "the utter ruin of the state" (as paraphrased/quoted in WP from Tacitus via Rickman; treat as attributed, not exact). Juvenal's "bread and circuses" (*panem et circenses*) is the satirical label. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cura_annonae]
- China's "ever-normal granary" (*changping cang*): WP says the first was built in 54 BC (Han); the idea was promoted by Wang Anshi under the Northern Song. Under the Qing a nationwide state granary system held about 2.2–3.3 million tonnes of grain; it decayed during the Taiping era (1850s). The English term "ever-normal granary" was taken up by US Agriculture Secretary Henry A. Wallace (read c. 1926, promoted after the 1934 drought). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buffer_stock_scheme]
- The standard study is Pierre-Étienne Will and R. Bin Wong, with James Lee, *Nourish the People: The State Civilian Granary System in China, 1650–1850* (University of Michigan Center for Chinese Studies, 1991; open-access edition 2020). [source: https://press.umich.edu/Books/N/Nourish-the-People]
- Grand Canal: expanded and completed by Emperor Yang of Sui in 609, linking the rice-rich Jiangnan to the capital at Luoyang and the northern armies; its conscripted labour helped bring down the Sui. The current Jing–Hang main stem runs about 1,776 km from Beijing to Hangzhou; the longest artificial waterway in the world; UNESCO World Heritage Site 2014. (The Tang and Song chapter already covers the Sui canal — cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canal_(China)]
- Inca *qullqa* (storehouses): built in groups on dry hillsides, with under-floor ventilation; round ones typically held maize, rectangular ones *chuño* (freeze-dried potato). Maize and quinoa kept 1–2 years, chuño and dried meat 2–4 years; chroniclers claimed up to 10. Above Lake Cotapachi near Cochabamba stood 2,400 qullqas; about 1,000 have been found at Paria. (Inca Empire chapter covers storehouses briefly — cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qullqa]

## Spices and the Indian Ocean
- Black pepper is native to the Malabar Coast of south-western India, the ancient world's sole source; it was exported to the Middle East and Rome. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_pepper]
- In the late 2nd century BC Ptolemaic Greeks learned (from Indian sailors, per WP) to sail directly from Aden to western India with the monsoon winds (the "Hippalus" tradition). Many recipes in the Roman cookbook *Apicius* use pepper. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spice_trade]
- Cloves and nutmeg were endemic to the Maluku (Moluccas) and Banda Islands; Austronesian/Indonesian sailors carried cinnamon and cassia to East Africa; Arab sailors from Yemen and Oman dominated later Indian Ocean routes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spice_trade]
- Vasco da Gama reached Calicut via the Cape in 1498; Albuquerque took Malacca in 1511; António de Abreu's expedition reached Banda in early 1512. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spice_trade]
- Banda 1621: the pre-conquest population is generally estimated at 13,000–15,000; about 1,000 Bandanese likely remained as forced labourers; survivors fled to Kei and Seram Laut. Coen divided the nutmeg land into 68 *perken* worked by imported enslaved labourers. Run was traded under the Treaty of Breda (1667). (Trading Companies chapter covers this — cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banda_Islands]

## Sugar and the plantation
- Sidney W. Mintz (16 November 1922 – 27 December 2015), anthropologist; doctoral fieldwork among sugar-cane workers in Santa Isabel, Puerto Rico (PhD Columbia 1951); taught at Yale, then helped found anthropology at Johns Hopkins. *Sweetness and Power* is "considered one of the most influential publications" in cultural anthropology and food studies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Mintz]
- Mintz's British per-capita sugar figures as reported by reviewers: about 4 lb a year in 1700, 18 lb in 1800 (NYRB review, 1985). A Mintz essay elsewhere gives 13 lb at the end of the 18th century — use "roughly fourfold or more across the eighteenth century" or attribute the 4→18 lb series to Mintz via the review. [source: https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1985/10/24/conspicuous-consumption/]
- In the 19th–20th centuries the need for sugar labour drove migration, including Indian indentured labourers after slavery. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugarcane]

## The Columbian exchange of foods (cross-refer to The Columbian Exchange)
- "Columbian exchange" was coined by Alfred W. Crosby in *The Columbian Exchange* (1972). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbian_exchange]
- Nunn and Qian (2011, *QJE*) estimate the potato contributed roughly 12–25% of Afro-Eurasian population growth 1700–1900 (WP's paraphrase) and 47% of the increase in urbanisation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbian_exchange]
- Chillies originated in Central/South America and were first cultivated in Mexico (6,000–9,000 years ago). The Portuguese carried them to Africa, Arabia, and to Goa, Sri Lanka and Malacca in the 16th century; the Spanish spread them via the Manila galleons; arrival in East Asia (late 16th century) is less documented, probably through Canton and Nagasaki. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chili_pepper]
- Tomato: wild ancestor native to western South America; first European mention in Pietro Andrea Mattioli's 1544 herbal; Mattioli named them *pomi d'oro* ten years later; a basket of tomatoes reached Cosimo de' Medici's household in 1548. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomato]
- Potato blight (*Phytophthora infestans*) has been traced to the Toluca Valley, Mexico; it probably reached Europe in 1844. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_(Ireland)]

## Tea, coffee and chocolate
- Tea drinking was mainly southern Chinese before the mid-8th century and spread widely under the Tang, also to Korea, Japan and Vietnam; Lu Yu wrote *The Classic of Tea* (*Cha Jing*) under the Tang; Tang tea was boiled with ingredients; steeping became popular under the Song; loose-leaf tea had replaced cake and powdered tea by the end of the 16th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_tea]
- The Tea Horse Road traded Chinese tea for Tibetan horses from Tang–Song times to the Republic. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tea_Horse_Road]
- A Dutch East India Company ship brought the first tea to Amsterdam in the early 17th century; Catherine of Braganza's marriage to Charles II (1662) brought tea-drinking to the English court; by 1750 tea was Britain's national drink; tea was paid for in silver, and Britain turned to Indian opium to obtain it (Opium Wars — cross-refer to History and Drugs). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_tea]
- Tea plants were found in Assam in 1824; the British introduced tea culture into India in 1836 and Ceylon in 1867; a fungus cut Ceylon coffee output by 95% in the 19th century. Tea became widely popular among Indians only from the 1950s, after an India Tea Board advertising campaign. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_tea]
- Coffee: wild *Coffea arabica* is Ethiopian, but the drink began in Yemen, where 15th-century Sufis used it for night prayers; by the early 16th century it had reached Mecca, Cairo, Damascus and Istanbul. Mocha was the world's sole gateway until the late 17th century; the Dutch planted it in Java; Gabriel de Clieu took seedlings to Martinique in 1720; by 1788 Saint-Domingue supplied half the world's coffee; Brazil became the largest producer by 1852 and has remained so; Vietnam became second-largest in 1999. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_coffee]
- Cacao was first domesticated in present-day Ecuador; the Mokaya of coastal Chiapas drank cacao by 1900 BC; cacao beans served as currency by at least 400 BC; the Maya introduced chocolate to the Aztecs. The earliest documented arrival in Spain is 1544, brought by Q'eqchi' Maya nobles with Dominican friars; first official cargo 1585. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_chocolate]
- Van Houten patented cocoa-butter pressing in 1828; Daniel Peter invented milk chocolate in 1875. The Portuguese took cacao from Brazil to São Tomé in 1824, recreating slave plantations; after abolition in 1869 a captive workforce was kept by "legal trickery"; São Tomé and Príncipe was the largest producer in 1905. Child labour remains an issue; global chocolate trade over US$100 billion (2018). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_chocolate]

## Famine and its politics
- Amartya Sen (born 3 November 1933, Santiniketan, Bengal); Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences 1998. *Poverty and Famines: An Essay on Entitlement and Deprivation* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1981) argued famine arises not only from lack of food but from inequalities in the mechanisms distributing it; on Bengal, that an urban war boom raised food prices so rural workers' wages could not buy food. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amartya_Sen]
- The debate: "food availability decline" (FAD) versus "failure of exchange entitlements" (FEE). Peter Bowbrick argued for a real supply drop in Bengal; S. Y. Padmanabhan and Mark Tauger argue brown-spot rice disease was badly underestimated; WP says academic consensus generally follows Sen's FEE account (flagged "citation needed"). More recent work stresses politics: an inept, panicky wartime government versus deliberate colonial priorities. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal_famine_of_1943]
- Irish Great Famine (*An Gorta Mór*), 1845–1852: excess deaths 775,000 to 1.5 million; 1 million or more emigrated; at least 2.1 million left 1845–55; population fell 20–25% between 1841 and 1871; worst year 1847 ("Black '47"); pre-famine population about 8.5–8.75 million. Large food exports continued and London refused to bar them. Blight elsewhere caused some 100,000 deaths in Europe (Ó Gráda, Vanhaute, Paping). Most historians reject the genocide charge, but the question persists publicly. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_(Ireland)]
- Bengal famine of 1770: WP infobox gives 1–10 million deaths; traditional figures of 7–10 million (a quarter to a third of the population) come from Company-era estimates. KNOWN_ISSUES records Rajat Datta's estimate of about 1.2 million and Dyson's view that 10 million is "barely credible". Present as a contested range. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Bengal_famine_of_1770]
- Bengal famine of 1943: estimates 0.8–3.8 million; Ó Gráda (2007): "today the scholarly consensus is about 2.1 million"; Sen estimated 2.7–3 million for 1943–46; the Famine Inquiry Commission (Woodhead, 1945) about 1.5 million; Greenough 3.5–3.8 million. Causes included the loss of Burmese rice after Japan's occupation, "denial policies" against rice and boats (March 1942), wartime inflation, inter-provincial trade barriers, the October 1942 cyclone and brown-spot disease, and limited relief from Churchill's war cabinet; over half of deaths came in 1944 from disease. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal_famine_of_1943]
- Mike Davis, *Late Victorian Holocausts: El Niño Famines and the Making of the Third World* (Verso; WP calls it a 2000 book, the Library of Congress/ISBN edition is dated 2001): the El Niño famines of 1876–78, 1896–97 and 1899–1902 in India, China, Brazil, Ethiopia, Korea, Vietnam, the Philippines and New Caledonia killed 30–60 million, which he blames on laissez-faire, Malthusian colonial ideology. Davis also compared the Great Leap famine with the Indian famines of the late 19th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Victorian_Holocausts ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Famine]
- Great Leap Forward famine (1959–61, or 1958–62): WP gives 15–46 million, with newer estimates 30–46 million. Use the Maoism chapter's attributed range (Cao Shuji 32.5 m; Yang Jisheng 36 m; Dikötter at least 45 m). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Chinese_Famine]
- Holodomor (1932–33): WP's main range is 3.5–5 million; a 2003 joint UN statement of 25 countries said 7–10 million; 70–80% of deaths in eight oblasts fell in the first seven months of 1933 (Wolowyna 2021). See KNOWN_ISSUES for lower estimates (about 3–5 million). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holodomor]
- Alex de Waal, *Mass Starvation: The History and Future of Famine* (Polity, 2018): the publisher says mass starvation killed about ten million people every decade until the 1980s but had all but disappeared by the early 2000s; he credits politics (fewer wars, less totalitarian rule) and warns the decline may be reversing. [source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/D7392901096BCBAACDC5057ACEB56CE8/S0001972018000633a.pdf ; https://now.tufts.edu/2018/01/18/mass-starvation-political-weapon]

## Industrial food
- Nicolas Appert began experimenting in 1795 with food sealed in glass jars and boiled; in 1810 the French Interior Ministry paid him 12,000 francs on condition he publish the process (which he did, 1810). WP also says the army offered a prize of 12,000 francs in 1795. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Appert]
- Peter Durand received English patent no. 3372 on 25 August 1810 for preserving food in glass, pottery, tin or other metal vessels (possibly as agent for Philippe de Girard); he sold it in 1811 to Bryan Donkin and John Hall, whose early market was the Army and Navy; by 1817 Donkin had sold £3,000 of canned meat in six months. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Durand ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canning]
- Refrigeration: James Harrison built an ice machine at Geelong, Australia, in 1851 (patent 1856); Carl von Linde's work was driven by brewers. The *Strathleven* landed frozen meat in London on 2 February 1880; the *Dunedin* sailed from Port Chalmers on 15 February 1882 with 4,331 mutton, 598 lamb and 22 pig carcasses, arrived in London 98 days later with only one carcass condemned; within five years 172 frozen-meat shipments had gone from New Zealand to Britain. (Second Industrial Revolution chapter covers this — cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refrigeration ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunedin_(1874_ship)]
- Clarence Birdseye introduced flash-frozen food to the American public from 1929, inspired by Inuit practice seen in Labrador (1912, 1916). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frozen_food]
- Haber–Bosch: Haber demonstrated ammonia synthesis in 1909; Bosch scaled it up for BASF; first industrial plant at Oppau in 1913 (20 tonnes/day by 1914). Haber won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1918, Bosch 1931. Ammonia output about 230 million tonnes (2018); the process uses 1–2% of world energy and causes about 3% of CO2 emissions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process]
- Vaclav Smil: without yield increases since 1900, the 2000 harvest "would have required nearly four times more land" and cultivation "would have claimed nearly half of all ice-free continents, rather than under 15%". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process]

## The Green Revolution
- The term was coined by USAID administrator William S. Gaud in a speech on 8 March 1968 (quotation below). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Revolution]
- Origins in Mexico from the 1940s: Henry A. Wallace persuaded the Rockefeller Foundation to fund an agricultural station; with Mexican government support under Ávila Camacho (the Mexican Agricultural Program). Mexico has been called the Green Revolution's "birthplace" and "burial ground". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Revolution]
- Norman Borlaug (25 March 1914 – 12 September 2009) developed semi-dwarf, high-yield, disease-resistant wheats in Mexico; Mexico became a net wheat exporter by 1963; wheat yields nearly doubled in India and Pakistan between 1965 and 1970; Nobel Peace Prize 1970. The oft-quoted "billion lives saved" traces to Gregg Easterbrook's 1997 *Atlantic* article ("may have prevented a billion deaths") — attribute, do not state as fact. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Borlaug]
- M. S. Swaminathan invited Borlaug to India; Punjab was the chosen state. IRRI (Philippines, Ford and Rockefeller foundations) released IR8 rice in 1966; Philippine rice output rose from 3.7 to 7.7 million tons in two decades. Yuan Longping's hybrid rice in China is credited with comparable impact. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Revolution]
- Critics: Vandana Shiva (born 5 November 1952), *The Violence of the Green Revolution: Ecological Degradation and Political Conflict in Punjab* (Dehra Dun, 1989; Zed Books edition 1991); she founded Navdanya in 1991 (seed banks). Other criticisms in WP: heavy fertiliser, pesticide and irrigation dependence, loss of crop diversity, soil degradation. A counterpoint: Shiva advised Sri Lanka's 2021 ban on chemical fertilisers, which was followed by sharp falls in farm output (attributed to news sources in WP). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandana_Shiva ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Revolution]

## Fast food, globalisation and cuisine
- McDonald's was founded on 15 May 1940 in San Bernardino by Richard and Maurice McDonald; the "Speedee Service System" came in 1948; Ray Kroc opened his Des Plaines franchise in 1955; at end-2025 the system had 45,356 restaurants in more than 100 countries, about 95% franchised. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonald%27s]
- George Ritzer proposed "McDonaldization" in a 1983 article and developed it in *The McDonaldization of Society* (1993): efficiency, calculability, predictability and control, a reworking of Weber's rationalisation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonaldization]
- James L. Watson (ed.), *Golden Arches East: McDonald's in East Asia* (Stanford University Press, 1997): case studies of Beijing (Yunxiang Yan), Hong Kong (Watson), Taipei (David Wu), Seoul (Sangmee Bak) and Japan (Emiko Ohnuki-Tierney), afterword by Mintz; argues McDonald's was localised by consumers and changing family life, challenging the cultural-imperialism thesis. [source: https://www.asianstudies.org/publications/eaa/archives/eaa-interview-with-james-l-watson-on-golden-arches-east-mcdonalds-in-east-asia/]
- Jack Goody (27 July 1919 – 16 July 2015), *Cooking, Cuisine and Class: A Study in Comparative Sociology* (Cambridge University Press, 1982): asks why a differentiated haute cuisine did not develop in (West) Africa as in Eurasia, beginning from the LoDagaa and Gonja of northern Ghana. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Goody ; https://wellcomecollection.org/works/b379bkf8]
- Arjun Appadurai (born 1949, Mumbai), "How to Make a National Cuisine: Cookbooks in Contemporary India", *Comparative Studies in Society and History* 30:1 (1988), 3–24: English-language cookbooks for a new middle class built a national cuisine from regional ones without hiding regional/ethnic roots. [source: https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/comparative-studies-in-society-and-history/article/how-to-make-a-national-cuisine-cookbooks-in-contemporary-india/A61B2DF54090EC3B3D83616C3CDABFA8]
- Rachel Laudan (born 1944), *Cuisine and Empire: Cooking in World History* (University of California Press, 2013): empires were the main political units that shaped and spread cuisines; cooking is a technology; the rise of "middling cuisines" for new middle classes; a challenge to romantic, agrarian, nationalist food-movement myths. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Laudan ; https://www.splendidtable.org/story/the-empire-the-political-unit-that-shaped-and-transmitted-cuisines]
- UNESCO intangible heritage: the gastronomic meal of the French (November 2010); traditional Mexican cuisine (2010, "the Michoacán paradigm"); the Mediterranean diet (2010; later extended to seven countries); washoku, Japanese cuisine (2013, after a 2012 nomination); gimjang/kimjang, Korean kimchi-making (December 2013). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_cuisine ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_cuisine ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_diet ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_cuisine ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gimjang]

## Food sovereignty and hunger today
- La Vía Campesina ("the peasant way") was founded in 1993 at Mons, Belgium, in reaction to the GATT Uruguay Round; now 182 organisations in 81 countries; its members coined "food sovereignty" in 1996. The 2007 Declaration of Nyéléni (Sélingué, Mali, about 500 delegates from more than 80 countries) defined it; by 2020 at least seven countries had written it into constitutions or laws. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Campesina ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_sovereignty]
- FAO/IFAD/UNICEF/WFP/WHO, *The State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World 2025* (released 28 July 2025): about 673 million people (range 638–720 million; about 8.2% of humanity) faced hunger in 2024, 15 million fewer than in 2023; Asia 323 million (6.7%); Africa over 20%, 307 million, and rising; about 2.3 billion faced moderate or severe food insecurity; about 2.6 billion could not afford a healthy diet; 512 million projected chronically undernourished in 2030, almost 60% in Africa. (Check whether a SOFI 2026 has appeared before publication.) [source: https://data.unicef.org/resources/sofi-2025/ ; https://ethiopia.un.org/en/node/299138]

## Debates
- Famine causation: food availability decline (Bowbrick; Tauger and Padmanabhan on brown-spot disease) versus Sen's entitlement failure; then politics (Sen's democracy argument; de Waal's "mass starvation" as a political crime; Davis's colonial-ideology indictment). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal_famine_of_1943 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Famine]
- Culpability: Churchill and the war cabinet in 1943 (critics versus "inept wartime government" view); whether the Irish famine was genocide (most historians say no; a public debate continues); the Holodomor genocide debate (covered in Soviet Union chapter). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_(Ireland) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal_famine_of_1943]
- Green Revolution: yields and averted famine (Borlaug, Swaminathan, the "billion lives" claim) versus ecological and social costs, inequality and Punjab conflict (Shiva); Shiva's own critics after Sri Lanka 2021. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandana_Shiva]
- Globalisation of food: homogenising McDonaldization (Ritzer) versus localisation and consumer agency (Watson, *Golden Arches East*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonaldization ; https://www.asianstudies.org/publications/eaa/archives/eaa-interview-with-james-l-watson-on-golden-arches-east-mcdonalds-in-east-asia/]
- Cuisine and identity: Goody's comparative class/hierarchy approach; Appadurai on cookbooks making national cuisine; Laudan against romantic-nationalist "food movement" myths and in favour of "middling cuisines"; UNESCO heritage listing as nation-branding. [sources above]
- Commodity history: Mintz linked Caribbean slavery to British working-class consumption; Mintz himself criticised world-systems determinism (Global History chapter). Carney and Rosomoff restore African crops and enslaved people's agency. [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/461627]
- Roman grain dole: older literature treats the annona as a "briberous and corrupting" imperial device; recent work (Erdkamp, Rickman) treats it as supply policy, and notes most *annona* inscriptions concern local city supply, not the capital's dole. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cura_annonae]

## Conflicts with published chapters
- **Great Leap famine**: the 20th Century overview says "30–45 million … the largest in recorded history"; Maoism, Modern China and Chinese History use about 15–45 million or more (Dikötter at least 45 m). WP: 15–46 m, newer estimates 30–46 m. Use the attributed range and cross-refer to Maoism.
- **Holodomor**: Soviet Union and 20th Century overview say 3.5–5 million; East European Identities says about 3–5 million; KNOWN_ISSUES lists lower recent estimates. Use "about 3–5 million, by recent estimates", attributed, and cross-refer.
- **Bengal 1770**: British Empire gives "1 to 10 million" (matches WP infobox); KNOWN_ISSUES flags Datta's ~1.2 m and Dyson. Present as contested.
- **Bengal 1943**: Second World War and British Empire give 0.8–3.8 m, consensus about 2.1 m — consistent with WP/Ó Gráda.
- **Irish famine**: British Empire (775,000–1.5 m) and Nationalism ("roughly a million") are consistent with WP.
- **Indian famines 1876–78**: British Empire gives 5.6–9.6 m; WP Famine article cites Davis for 6.1–10.3 m. Attribute Davis's figure if used.
- **Sen democracy quotation**: Maoism quotes "no substantial famine has ever occurred in a democratic country". This exact wording was not verified; the verified form in an interview is "No famine has ever taken place in the history of the world in a functioning democracy" (attributed to *Development as Freedom*, 1999). Don't use either as an exact quotation without checking the book.
- **Rice dates**: Neolithic gives Yangzi rice domestication "roughly from 8000 to 4000 BCE" as a long process; WP's lead gives 13,500–8,200 years ago, body 9,000 years ago. Compatible if framed as a protracted process; keep the Neolithic chapter's framing.
- **Sorghum**: Neolithic says eastern Sahel by about 3000 BCE; WP: more than 5,000 years ago in eastern Sudan, Kassala 3500–3000 BCE. Consistent.
- **Columbian Exchange**: China population "150 million around 1600 to over 300 million by 1800" — KNOWN_ISSUES gives late-Ming estimates of 160–231 m; and sweet potato had already reached Polynesia c. 1000–1200 CE. Avoid repeating the 150 m figure.
- **Coffee**: History and Drugs says coffee was "first cultivated in Ethiopia and Yemen"; WP says wild arabica is Ethiopian but the drink and cultivation began in Yemen. Minor nuance.
- **Haber–Bosch**: Second Industrial Revolution says "by one estimate about half the nitrogen in human bodies now comes from it" — not re-verified here; dates (1909, 1913) agree.
- **Overlaps to cross-refer, not repeat**: Neolithic (domestication centres); Columbian Exchange (crops, sugar complex, Brazil/Barbados, Mintz, Crosby); Global History (Mintz's argument); Trading Companies (Banda 1621, tea and opium); History and Drugs (coffeehouses, tea and opium, cacao); Tang and Song (Sui Grand Canal); Inca Empire (qollqa); Rome (annona, if covered); Second Industrial Revolution (Dunedin 1882, Swift, Haber–Bosch); Maoism/Rural China (Great Leap famine, Xiaogang); Soviet Union/East European Identities (Holodomor); British Empire (Indian famines, Irish exports); Victorian Era (Corn Laws repeal 1846).
