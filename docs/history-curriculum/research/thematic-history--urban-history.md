## The field: urban history and its founders
- Urban history studies how populations concentrate in cities over time and the social, political, cultural and economic contexts of cities; most scholarship has focused on the "metropolis", with much less on small towns and (until recently) suburbs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_history]
- Only a handful of studies attempt a global history of cities; the article names Lewis Mumford's *The City in History* (1961) as the notable example (Paul Bairoch's *Cities and Economic Development* (1988) is also cited). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_history]
- H. J. (Harold James) Dyos (1921–1978), of the University of Leicester, was the leading promoter of urban history in Britain, especially of the Victorian city. He became Professor of Urban History at Leicester in 1973 (a personal title; there was no department). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_James_Dyos]
- Dyos founded the *Urban History Newsletter* (1963, per his biography; the Urban History article says he formed the Urban History Study Group in 1962). The newsletter became the *Urban History Yearbook* (1974–1991) and then the journal *Urban History* (1992–present). Say "in the early 1960s" to avoid the 1962/1963 discrepancy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_James_Dyos ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_history]
- Dyos's edited volume *The Study of Urban History* (1968) opened up the field's method; with Michael Wolff he edited *The Victorian City: Images and Realities* (2 vols, 1973). His own books include *Victorian Suburb* (1961). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_history ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_James_Dyos]
- Dyos rejected the quantitative methods of the American "new urban history", being interested in larger social structures (the slum, the whole city) rather than individuals. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_history]
- The "new urban history" emerged in the 1960s as a branch of social history, using quantitative methods (census records) to study ordinary city people; a landmark was Stephan Thernstrom's *Poverty and Progress: Social Mobility in a Nineteenth Century City* (1964), on Newburyport, Massachusetts, 1850–1880. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_history]
- The US Urban History Association was founded in 1988; the quarterly *Journal of Urban History* has been published since 1974. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_history]
- In China, Maoist ideology's emphasis on peasant uprisings led to neglect of urban history until the 1980s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_history]
- The Chicago school of sociology (Robert E. Park, Ernest Burgess, Roderick McKenzie, Louis Wirth and others) took Chicago as its laboratory; Burgess proposed that cities grow by radial expansion in concentric zones (Park, Burgess and McKenzie, *The City*, 1925). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_school_(sociology)]
- Lewis Mumford (born Flushing, Queens, New York; died 26 January 1990, aged 94, at Amenia, New York) was architectural critic for *The New Yorker* for over 30 years; he was influenced by the Scottish planner Patrick Geddes. His earlier urban book was *The Culture of Cities* (1938). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Mumford]
- *The City in History: Its Origins, Its Transformations, and Its Prospects* (Harcourt, Brace & World, New York, 1961; 657 pages) won the 1962 US National Book Award for Nonfiction. Mumford was harshly critical of urban sprawl and argued planning should stress an "organic" relationship between people and their living spaces. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_City_in_History ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Mumford]
- Jacobs's *Death and Life* was a finalist for the same 1962 National Book Award for Nonfiction that Mumford's book won (useful juxtaposition). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Death_and_Life_of_Great_American_Cities]
- Mumford reviewed Jacobs in *The New Yorker*, 1 December 1962, under the title "Mother Jacobs' Home Remedies" (reprinted as "Home Remedies for Urban Cancer"); Paul Goldberger calls it "stunningly vicious", while noting Mumford gave Jacobs some credit. [source: https://paulgoldberger.com/lectures/jane-jacobs-centennial-lecture/ (via WebSearch; secondary)]
- Henri Lefebvre (1901–1991), French philosopher and sociologist, taught at Strasbourg (from 1961) and Nanterre (from 1965); he introduced the "right to the city" in *Le Droit à la ville* (1968), published before the May 1968 revolts, and later wrote *The Production of Space* (1974), one of the most cited works of urban theory. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Lefebvre]

## The urban revolution and the first cities
- V. Gordon Childe (1892–1957) coined the term "urban revolution" in the 1930s; he first discussed it in *Man Makes Himself* (1936) and set out his ten criteria in the article "The Urban Revolution", *Town Planning Review* 21 (1950), pp. 3–17. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_revolution]
- Childe's ten criteria (paraphrase): large, dense settlements; full-time specialists (craftsmen, transport workers, merchants, officials, priests); surplus paid as tithe or tax to a deity or divine king; monumental public buildings; a ruling class absorbing the surplus; writing; exact and predictive sciences (arithmetic, geometry, astronomy); sophisticated art styles; regular long-distance "foreign" trade; a state organised by residence rather than kinship. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_revolution]
- Childe located the urban revolution in Mesopotamia in the 4th millennium BCE; his concept describes the transition from villages to state-level urban societies, which occurred independently in several parts of the world. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_revolution]
- Lefebvre later borrowed the phrase for a different idea in *La Révolution urbaine* (1970) (the article's cited chapter title "From the Urban Revolution to La Revolution Urbaine"); mention only lightly. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_revolution]
- Uruk: type site of the Uruk period (c. 4000–3200 BCE in the article's dating). At its peak around 3100 BCE it may have had up to 50,000 residents (Hans Nissen), with 80,000–90,000 in its environs (Guillermo Algaze), making it the largest urban area in the world then. Other settlements of the time were generally about 10 hectares. The site covers about 5.5–6 km². [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruk]
- Historical-city tables (Morris 2010; Modelski 2003; Chandler 1987) give Uruk about 45,000 (Morris) or 40,000 (Modelski) in 3000 BCE, and Modelski 80,000 in 2800 BCE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_cities_throughout_history]
- Çatalhöyük (central Anatolia, near Konya): a very large Neolithic and Chalcolithic "proto-city" occupied c. 7500–5600 BCE, flourishing c. 7000 BCE; found by James Mellaart in 1958, excavated 1961–65, and by Ian Hodder's team from 1993; UNESCO World Heritage Site 2012. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%87atalh%C3%B6y%C3%BCk]
- Çatalhöyük population: initial estimates were 5,000–7,000, but recent work suggests only 600–800 people lived at Çatalhöyük East in an average year of the Middle phase (6700–6500 BCE). It shows strong evidence of an egalitarian society, no houses with distinctive elite features, and little gender-based social distinction. This is the core of the "town or city?" debate: large and dense but without specialists, monuments or hierarchy, so it fails most of Childe's criteria. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%87atalh%C3%B6y%C3%BCk]
- Mohenjo-daro (Sindh, Pakistan) was built c. 2500 BCE and abandoned by c. 1700 BCE; covered area about 300 hectares; estimated population "at least 40,000" (the *Oxford Handbook of Cities in World History* offers a "weak" estimate of about 40,000 at peak). Planned rectilinear layout on a grid, mostly fired brick; over 700 wells, plus drainage and bathing systems; the Great Bath measures about 12 × 7 × 2.4 m. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohenjo-daro]
- Teotihuacan (about 40 km northeast of Mexico City): population estimated at least 25,000 but likely about 100,000 at its height, possibly the sixth-largest city in the world of its time; in its classic period (350–650 CE in the article's phasing) about 100,000 people and 2,000 buildings within 18 km²; 80–90 per cent of the valley's population lived in the city; multi-family apartment compounds; probably multi-ethnic (Maya, Zapotec, Oto-Pamean elements). Elite compounds along the Avenue of the Dead bear burn marks; decline in 650–750 CE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teotihuacan]
- Tenochtitlan: founding date traditionally 1325 (the date 13 March 1325 was chosen in 1925); joined to the mainland by causeways with removable bridges; two terracotta aqueducts over 4 km long from Chapultepec; a levee of 12–16 km credited to Nezahualcoyotl completed c. 1453. When Cortés arrived (8 November 1519), population estimates range from 200,000 to 400,000; the most common estimates are over 200,000, and one academic survey gives 212,500 on 13.5 km². Only Paris, Venice and Constantinople in Europe might have rivalled it. Cortés estimated 60,000 people trading daily at the Tlatelolco market; Sahagún gave 20,000 on ordinary days and 40,000 on feast days. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenochtitlan]
- The city was divided into four zones and each into 20 districts (*calpulli*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenochtitlan]
- Cahokia (across the Mississippi from St Louis), c. 1050–1350 CE: largest Mississippian urban settlement and largest urban centre north of Mesoamerica. About 1,000 people before c. 1050; a 2007 *Quaternary Science Reviews* study estimated growth "from between 1,400 and 2,800 people to between 10,200 and 15,300 people" between 1050 and 1100 (central 1.8 km² only). Overall peak estimates range from 6,000 to 40,000; if the highest is right, no US city was larger until Philadelphia passed 40,000 in the 1780s. Large-scale immigration fed the 1050s "Big Bang"; a large fire c. 1160–1170 began depopulation; abandoned by 1400 apart from a brief Oneota reoccupation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahokia]
- Great Zimbabwe (near Masvingo): settled from c. 1000 CE; stone construction 11th–15th centuries; capital of the Kingdom of Great Zimbabwe from the 13th century; replaced Mapungubwe as trade centre c. 1300; Great Enclosure walls up to 11 m high, about 250 m long. Traditional estimates of up to 18,000 inhabitants; a more recent survey concluded the population probably never exceeded 10,000. Covered an area similar to medieval London, densely built inside the enclosures but not outside. Rhodesia's white government pressured archaeologists to deny African construction; African origin became scholarly consensus only in the 1950s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Zimbabwe]
- Angkor: the Angkorian period began in 802 (Jayavarman II); "Angkor" derives via Khmer *nokor* from Sanskrit *nagara*, "city". In 2007 an international team using satellite and other techniques concluded Angkor was the largest pre-industrial city in the world by surface area, with an urban sprawl of at least 1,000 km² linked by roads and canals; it was the largest urban agglomeration before the Industrial Revolution by area, though not by population. Its low-density, dispersed pattern means it has no formal boundary. Agricultural systems may have supported 750,000–1,000,000 people. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angkor]
- A 2021 *Science Advances* study (Sarah Klassen et al.), using lidar, archaeology and modelling, estimated the Greater Angkor Region's peak population in the 13th century at about 700,000–900,000, over a roughly 3,000 km² urban–rural landscape. [source: https://angkordatabase.asia/publications/diachronic-modeling-of-the-population-within-the-medieval-greater-angkor-region-settlement-complex ; https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/560558 (via WebSearch)]
- Roland Fletcher (University of Sydney) argues that pre-industrial cities were not all compact: "low-density" dispersed agrarian urbanism existed at Greater Angkor, Anuradhapura and Polonnaruwa (Sri Lanka) and the Classic Maya lowland cities. With Robin Coningham (*Antiquity*, 2015) he gives the largest Maya centres about 100–200 km² and Anuradhapura and Angkor about 500–1,000 km², and links their breakdown between the 9th and 16th centuries to climate instability; he compares them to today's sprawling low-density megacities. [source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-humanities/articles/10.3389/fdigh.2019.00014/pdf ; https://www.uib.no/en/ahkr/140040/low-density-urbanism-context-and-significance (via WebSearch)]
- Monica L. Smith argues that cities were not inevitable but were crucial to the rise of *Homo sapiens*, and that ancient and modern cities share problems such as trash and pollution. [source: https://anthro.ucla.edu/publication/cities-the-first-6000-years (via WebSearch)]

## Great premodern cities (attributed population ranges)
- Rome, Jiankang (Nanjing) and Chang'an may have been the first cities with 1,000,000 people, as early as the 1st century CE or as late as the 8th; later cities that may have reached 1 million include Luoyang, Kaifeng, Hangzhou, Beijing and Edo. There is wide agreement that London was the first to reach 2 million and New York the first to reach 10 million. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_cities_throughout_history]
- Rome around 1 CE: Morris 1,000,000; Modelski 800,000. Around 100 CE: Modelski 1,000,000; Chandler 450,000. So "perhaps half a million to a million". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_cities_throughout_history]
- Chang'an (Han): population of the walled city 246,200 in 80,000 households by the 2 CE census, with 682,000 in the metropolitan region (Nylan). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27an]
- Sui–Tang Chang'an: Emperor Wen of Sui founded a new capital, Daxing, in 582 southeast of the Han city; renamed Chang'an in 618 by the Tang. Around 750 Chinese records called it a "million-man city"; most modern estimates put the population within the walls at about 800,000–1,000,000 (Chandler; Modelski). Grid of about 108–110 walled wards in the outer city (160 in another count) with East and West Markets opened and closed by drum and gong; nightly curfew lifted only for the Lantern Festival. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27an]
- Victor Cunrui Xiong calls Sui–Tang Chang'an "the most important city in early imperial China". Its plan was copied on a smaller scale in Japan's Heijō-kyō (Nara) and Heian-kyō (Kyoto), Silla's Gyeongju and Balhae's Sanggyeong. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27an]
- Heian-kyō (Kyoto): Emperor Kanmu entered the new capital on 18 November 794 (Julian); the planned city measured about 4.5 km east–west by 5.2 km north–south, less than a third of Tang Chang'an's area. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heian-ky%C5%8D]
- Baghdad: the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur built the Round City in 762–766 (construction began 2 August 762, per al-Ya'qubi), officially Madinat al-Salam, "City of Peace"; a circle about 1 km in radius (about 3 km²), with chroniclers reporting 100,000 workers. Baghdad was likely the world's largest city from shortly after its foundation until the 930s; estimates put its 10th-century population between 1.2 and 2 million, though the tables give Chandler 700,000 (800 CE) and 900,000 (900 CE). Use "perhaps several hundred thousand to over a million". Sacked by the Mongols in 1258. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round_City_of_Baghdad ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_cities_throughout_history]
- Constantinople: Roman/Byzantine capital from 330 to 1453, then Ottoman capital to 1922. Under Justinian I (6th century) about 500,000 people (Charanis; some suggest more); about 400,000 in the late 12th century, including 60,000–80,000 foreigners; only about 35,000 when Michael VIII recaptured it in 1261, about 70,000 by the end of his reign. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople]
- Edo grew from a fishing village into perhaps the world's largest city, with an estimated population of about 1 million by the early 18th century (1721 is commonly cited). Samurai and daimyō residences occupied up to 70 per cent of Edo's area (because of the *sankin-kōtai* alternate-attendance system); commoners made up about half the population. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo]
- Around 1800, all three tables put Beijing at about 1.1 million, the world's largest city; London led from 1825 to 1918 and passed 5 million (6.5–6.6 million by 1900). In 1950 New York was the only urban area over 10 million. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_cities_throughout_history ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megacity]
- Timbuktu: a centre of trade and Islamic learning under Mali and Songhay (Djinguereber, Sidi Yahya and Sankore mosques/madrasas); one source gives a population of about 10,000 by 1300, rising to about 50,000 in the 1500s (weakly sourced, Maynes and Waltner, a general history; use "tens of thousands" if at all). Moroccans occupied it in 1591 after the Battle of Tondibi; in 1593 many scholars were executed or exiled. Private libraries held up to 700,000 manuscripts in 2003. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbuktu]
- In January 2013 Islamist groups retreating from Timbuktu set fire to the Ahmed Baba Institute; the BBC reported that about 28,000 of its roughly 30,000 manuscripts had been removed to safety. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbuktu]

## The "Islamic city" and other urban forms
- Janet Abu-Lughod, "The Islamic City—Historic Myth, Islamic Essence, and Contemporary Relevance", *International Journal of Middle East Studies* 19:2 (1987), 155–176, criticised the Orientalist model of a single "Islamic city" (mosque, bazaar/suq, baths, walled quarters), associated with French scholars such as Georges Marçais. She argued it was built on too few cases (mainly North African and Syrian) and described outcomes rather than processes; her alternative stressed processes such as gender segregation, the legal distinction between Muslims and non-Muslims, and Sharia-based property and neighbour law shaping space. [source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0020743800031822 ; https://www.relbib.de/Record/1637320507 (via WebSearch; abstract-level only)]
- Tang Chang'an's walled wards and curfew show the planned, state-controlled Chinese capital form (see above); Edo's samurai-dominated land use shows a castle-town form. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27an ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo]

## The industrial city: Manchester, Engels, cholera and sanitary reform
- Manchester's rapid, unplanned growth made it "the world's first industrialised city"; it was dubbed "Cottonopolis" and "Warehouse City". The number of cotton mills peaked at 108 in 1853; it gained city status in 1853; the Manchester Ship Canal opened in 1894. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester]
- Friedrich Engels's *Die Lage der arbeitenden Klasse in England* (*The Condition of the Working Class in England*), published in German in 1845 (English translation 1887), was written from his 1842–44 stay in Salford and Manchester. It argued industrialisation had made workers worse off; in industrial cities mortality from smallpox, measles, scarlet fever and whooping cough was four times that of the surrounding countryside, and from convulsions ten times; death rates in Manchester and Liverpool were 1 in 32.72, 1 in 31.90 and even 1 in 29.90, against 1 in 45 or 46 nationally. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Condition_of_the_Working_Class_in_England]
- Edwin Chadwick's *Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population of Great Britain*, begun 1839, published 1842; the first Public Health Act followed in 1848. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Chadwick]
- Cholera killed 14,137 Londoners in 1849. After the Great Stink of 1858, Joseph Bazalgette's London main drainage (begun 1859) built about 82 miles of intercepting main sewers and 1,100 miles of street sewers, relieving the city of cholera epidemics. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Bazalgette]
- Paris and cholera: Haussmann's first north–south boulevards (Strasbourg, Sébastopol, from 1855) cut through central districts where the cholera epidemic had been worst. Eugène Belgrand, appointed Director of Water and Sewers in March 1855, built new aqueducts and sewers; Paris's sewer system expanded fourfold between 1852 and 1869. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haussmann%27s_renovation_of_Paris]
- Mumbai's Dharavi grew around tanneries (the first moved there from peninsular Bombay in 1887) and other polluting trades pushed out of the colonial city, which got no colonial investment in sanitation or roads; a plague in 1896 killed over half of Mumbai's population (as the article states; treat with caution). Today Dharavi covers about 2.39 km² with about 1 million people and an annual turnover estimated at over US$1 billion (2009). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharavi]

## Planning and its hubris
### Haussmann's Paris
- Napoleon III made Georges-Eugène Haussmann prefect of the Seine on 22 June 1853 and asked him to *aérer, unifier, et embellir* Paris (give it air, unify it, make it beautiful). Work ran from 1853; Haussmann was dismissed in 1870; projects continued until 1927. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haussmann%27s_renovation_of_Paris]
- Between 1830 and 1848 Paris saw six or seven armed uprisings in narrow central streets (the article gives both numbers in different sections). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haussmann%27s_renovation_of_Paris]
- On 1 January 1860 Paris annexed eleven suburban communes (including Belleville, Montmartre, Passy, Vaugirard), growing from 12 to 20 arrondissements and from 3,300 to 7,100 hectares, its population instantly rising by 400,000 to 1,600,000. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haussmann%27s_renovation_of_Paris]
- Finance: Haussmann paid contractors in vouchers bought by the Crédit Foncier, raising 463 million francs by 1867; in 1867 Jules Ferry mocked "Les Comptes fantastiques d'Haussmann" (a pun on *Les Contes d'Hoffmann*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haussmann%27s_renovation_of_Paris]
- Social effects: thousands of families and businesses were displaced; rents rose by about 300 per cent during the Second Empire while most wages stagnated; low-income Parisians moved from the centre to cheaper outer districts (the 1st arrondissement fell from 89,519 people in 1861 to 74,286 in 1872). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haussmann%27s_renovation_of_Paris]
- Before Haussmann Paris had only four public parks; he and Alphand created or remade the Bois de Boulogne and the Bois de Vincennes (1860–65), the latter for the working-class east. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haussmann%27s_renovation_of_Paris]
- The Third Republic continued and completed his projects despite having attacked them. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haussmann%27s_renovation_of_Paris]

### The garden city
- Ebenezer Howard published *To-morrow: A Peaceful Path to Real Reform* (1898), revised in 1902 as *Garden Cities of To-morrow*. First Garden City Ltd was created in 1899 (per the movement article; the company is also dated 1903 in the Howard article) to build Letchworth, about 34–37 miles north of London, planned by Raymond Unwin and Barry Parker for 30,000–35,000 people with a surrounding agricultural green belt. Howard bought land for Welwyn Garden City in 1919. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebenezer_Howard ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_city_movement]
- Robert Fishman called the financing model "philanthropic land speculation". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_city_movement]
- Global spread: Den-en-chōfu in Tokyo; Patrick Geddes's plan for Tel Aviv (1925–27); garden suburbs in Jerusalem and Haifa; Canberra's planners (the Griffins); British and French colonial towns in Africa; Radburn, New Jersey and Greenbelt, Maryland in the US. Britain's post-1945 New Towns (over 30, from Stevenage to Milton Keynes) followed Howard. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_city_movement ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebenezer_Howard]

### Le Corbusier and modernism
- Le Corbusier presented the Ville Contemporaine (three million inhabitants in skyscrapers) in 1922; *Vers une architecture* (*Towards an Architecture*) in 1923; the Plan Voisin of 1925, sponsored by the car and aircraft maker Gabriel Voisin, would have replaced a large area of central Paris on the Right Bank with 18 identical skyscrapers for 78,000 residents on 260 hectares, only 12 per cent built up. Never built. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plan_Voisin ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Corbusier]
- Ville Radieuse (Radiant City), an unrealised design of 1930, published as a book in 1933; Jacobs described it as 24 towering skyscrapers in a park at 1,200 inhabitants per acre. His Obus plan for Algiers adapted it to a colonial city. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ville_Radieuse ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Death_and_Life_of_Great_American_Cities]
- He was a founder of CIAM (first meeting June 1928); the Athens Charter, drafted on board ship in 1933, was published in 1943 and influenced planners of the 1950s–60s. He worked under the Vichy regime and was briefly adviser to Alexis Carrel's eugenics foundation (resigned 20 April 1944); his politics remain contested. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Corbusier]
- Chandigarh: the American planner Albert Mayer was chosen in December 1949; his garden-city/Radburn-influenced plan passed to Le Corbusier after Matthew Nowicki died in a plane crash (August 1950); Le Corbusier arrived in India in February 1951 joining Maxwell Fry and Pierre Jeanneret and working with a team of young Indian architects and planners. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandigarh]
- Brasília, founded 21 April 1960 by President Juscelino Kubitschek to replace Rio de Janeiro, built to Lúcio Costa's Plano Piloto (a Le Corbusier student) with Oscar Niemeyer's buildings; workers and migrants settled in satellite cities such as Taguatinga. James Holston's *The Modernist City: An Anthropological Critique of Brasília* (1989). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bras%C3%ADlia]
- Pruitt–Igoe, St Louis: 33 eleven-storey buildings by Minoru Yamasaki, 2,870 apartments, first occupied 1954; legally desegregated in 1954 with up to 40 per cent white tenants initially, but exclusively African American by the mid-1960s; over two-thirds vacant by 1970; demolition began with a televised implosion in 1972. Charles Jencks called it "the day Modern architecture died"; more recent work stresses St Louis's population loss and the housing authority's fiscal problems; the federal government imposed the uniform 11-storey height and high density. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pruitt%E2%80%93Igoe]

### Jane Jacobs versus Robert Moses
- Jane Jacobs (née Butzner; born 4 May 1916, Scranton, Pennsylvania; died 25 April 2006, Toronto) worked for *Architectural Forum*; her first public criticism of Moses was "Downtown Is for People" in *Fortune* (1958). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Jacobs]
- *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (Random House, New York, 1961) opens: "This book is an attack on current city planning and rebuilding." It coined "eyes on the street" and "mixed primary uses" and proposed four "generators of diversity": mixed primary uses, short blocks, buildings of various ages, and density. It criticised Howard's garden city, the "Decentrists" (including Mumford) and Le Corbusier's Radiant City. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Death_and_Life_of_Great_American_Cities ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Jacobs]
- Critics dismissed her as a "housewife" and "militant dame"; later left critics faulted the book for leaving out race and endorsing what she called "unslumming" (gentrification). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Jacobs]
- Washington Square: a coalition begun by local activist Shirley Hayes, later joined by Jacobs and backed by Eleanor Roosevelt, Margaret Mead, Lewis Mumford and Carmine De Sapio, stopped Moses's road through the park; it closed to traffic on 25 June 1958. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Jacobs]
- Jacobs chaired the Joint Committee to Stop the Lower Manhattan Expressway; she was arrested on 10 April 1968 at a hearing (charges reduced to disorderly conduct), moved to Toronto in 1968 and helped stop the Spadina Expressway there. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Jacobs]
- Robert Moses (born New Haven, 18 December 1888; died 29 July 1981) never held elective office but held up to 12 titles at once: New York City Parks Commissioner (1934–1960), chairman of the Triborough Bridge (and Tunnel) Authority (1934–1968), etc. He built Jones Beach, the Long Island parkways, the Triborough Bridge (1936), the Verrazzano–Narrows Bridge (1964), the Cross Bronx, Brooklyn–Queens and Long Island expressways. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Moses]
- Cross Bronx Expressway, built 1948–1972: required relocating as many as 60,000 residents and is blamed for worsening the South Bronx's decay. An estimated 170,000 people were evicted in New York for public projects in the seven postwar years. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_Bronx_Expressway ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Moses]
- Robert Caro's *The Power Broker* (1974; Pulitzer Prize), about 1,200 pages, cemented Moses's negative reputation, including charges of racism (Jones Beach permits, pool siting, Stuyvesant Town's exclusion of Black residents). Revisionists, notably Hilary Ballon and Kenneth T. Jackson with three 2007 exhibitions, argued for his achievements. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Moses]

## Segregation built into space
- Carl H. Nightingale, *Segregation: A Global History of Divided Cities* (University of Chicago Press, 2012), traces urban racial segregation to European colonialism, beginning with British Madras and the East India Company's division of Calcutta into "White Town" and "Black Town". He describes a global "segregation mania" in the 1890s–1900s, using plague-control and sanitation arguments (Hong Kong, Bombay), spreading through imperial officials, reformers and the real-estate industry to South Africa and the US. [source: https://www.buffalo.edu/ubreporter/archive/2012_09_06/nightingale_segregation.html ; https://newbooksnetwork.com/carl-h-nightingale-segregation-a-global-history-of-divided-cities (via WebSearch)]
- In South Africa, sanitation and plague prevention served as pretexts to move people of colour to city fringes, creating Soweto and other townships. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slum]
- "Redlining" was coined in the 1960s by the sociologist John McKnight for Chicago banks' classification of neighbourhoods as "hazardous" because of their racial makeup. The National Housing Act of 1934 created the Federal Housing Administration; in 1935 the Federal Home Loan Bank Board asked the Home Owners' Loan Corporation to map 239 cities with colour-coded "residential security maps" (A green to D red). Between 1945 and 1959 African Americans received less than 2 per cent of federally insured home loans. The Fair Housing Act of 1968 outlawed such discrimination. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redlining]
- Richard Rothstein, *The Color of Law: A Forgotten History of How Our Government Segregated America* (2017), argues segregation was *de jure*. Economists (Aaronson, Hartley and Mazumder, 2021) found HOLC "D" grading had lasting effects on homeownership, home values and credit scores into 2010. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redlining]
- Levittown, New York (initially "Island Trees", announced 7 May 1947), built 1947–1951 by Levitt & Sons for returning veterans; FHA-backed 30-year mortgages with no down payment; the lease barred occupation by anyone "other than members of the Caucasian race". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levittown,_New_York]
- South Africa's Group Areas Act (1950; re-enacted 1957 and 1966; repealed 30 June 1991) assigned racial groups (whites, coloureds, Indians, "natives") to separate residential and business areas, with forced removals; people of colour often had to commute long distances. On 9 February 1955, 2,000 police began removing Sophiatown residents to Meadowlands/Soweto; the area was rebuilt for whites as Triomf. (Caution: the Wikipedia article wrongly names the first act as the "Population Registration Act" and gives a promulgation date of 7 July 1950; do not use that date without checking.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_Areas_Act]
- Haussmann's reconstruction pushed poorer Parisians to the outskirts and shanty towns, reinforcing class segregation (Plan Voisin article's background). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plan_Voisin ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haussmann%27s_renovation_of_Paris]

## Suburbanization and sprawl
- Suburbanization takes many forms globally: planned satellite towns in Europe, rail-oriented suburbs in East Asia, Canadian high-rises, and informal peri-urban growth in the Global South; in the US it is bound up with redlining, discriminatory lending and white flight. GI Bill benefits helped veterans buy suburban homes after 1945. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suburbanization]
- Moses's parkways opened Long Island to mass suburbanization and set the template for urban highway design (Caro). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Moses]
- Dyos and Reeder's "Slums and Suburbs" (1973) linked the rapid growth of central slums and peripheral suburbs through flows of capital. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_James_Dyos]

## The Global South's urban century: megacities and informality
- Mike Davis (10 March 1946 – 25 October 2022), Marxist historian of Southern California (*City of Quartz*, 1990; *Late Victorian Holocausts*). *Planet of Slums* (Verso, London and New York, 2006) surveys gargantuan slums from Cape Town and Caracas to Casablanca and Khartoum; critics (Andy Merrifield, Tom Angotti) called it "anti-urban" and "overly apocalyptic". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Davis_(scholar) ; https://publishersweekly.com/9781844670222 (via WebSearch)]
- UN definition of a slum household: lacking one or more of improved water, improved sanitation, sufficient living area, durable housing, and security of tenure. UN-Habitat estimated nearly 1 billion slum dwellers in 2006; the absolute number passed 1 billion in 2018 (per the Megacity article). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slum ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megacity]
- Kibera, Nairobi: density estimated at 2,000 people per hectare. Orangi, Karachi, is described as the largest slum. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slum]
- UN DESA defines a megacity as an urban agglomeration of over 10 million (it earlier used 8 million). Counts vary by definition: 32 (EU Global Human Settlement Layer, 2024), 33 (UN DESA, 2025), 39 (OECD, 2020), 42 (Demographia, 2025). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megacity]
- Greater Tokyo was the world's most populous metropolitan area from 1955 to 2025 (37.4 million in 2020); in the UN's 2025 revision Jakarta overtook it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_cities_throughout_history ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megacity]
- UN DESA, *World Urbanization Prospects 2025* (released November 2025), using the new "Degree of Urbanisation" method: cities hold 45 per cent of the world's 8.2 billion people (about 20 per cent in 1950), towns 36 per cent, rural areas 19 per cent. Under aggregated national definitions the urban share is 58 per cent. Largest cities: Jakarta about 41.9 million, Dhaka about 36.6–37 million, Tokyo about 33 million; then New Delhi, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Cairo, Manila, Kolkata, Seoul (nine of ten in Asia). Dhaka is projected to become the largest by 2050. Caveat: Statistics Indonesia counts about 12 million residents in Jakarta's administrative area. [source: https://www.un.org/sustainabledevelopment/blog/2025/11/press-release-wup2025/ ; https://desapublications.un.org/un-desa-voice/feature/december-2025/latest-urbanization-data-reveal-worlds-most-populous-cities (via WebSearch; un.org pages could not be fetched directly)]
- The urban-majority milestone: UNFPA's *State of World Population 2007: Unleashing the Potential of Urban Growth* (July 2007) said that in 2008 the world would reach "an invisible but momentous milestone": for the first time more than half its population, 3.3 billion, would live in urban areas. It was a projection, using national urban definitions; under the 2025 Degree of Urbanisation method the shares look very different (cities 45 per cent, cities plus towns 81 per cent). Present the 2007/2008 crossing as a definitional milestone. [source: https://www.unfpa.org/publications/state-world-population-2007 ; https://www.prb.org/resources/urban-population-to-become-the-new-majority-worldwide (via WebSearch)]
- UNFPA 2007 also noted that most urban growth now came from natural increase rather than rural migration. [source: https://www.prb.org/resources/urban-population-to-become-the-new-majority-worldwide (via WebSearch)]
- Robert Neuwirth estimated over 1 billion squatters worldwide (one in seven). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Informal_housing]

## Questions of the urban age: rights, housing, gentrification, climate
- Lefebvre summarised the right to the city as a "demand ... [for] a transformed and renewed access to urban life". David Harvey revived it in "The Right to the City", *New Left Review* 53 (September–October 2008). Brazil's City Statute of 2001 wrote the right to the city into federal law; the UN's New Urban Agenda (Habitat III, Quito, 2016) adopted the vision of "cities for all". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_the_city]
- Ruth Glass (1912–1990), German-born British sociologist who founded UCL's Centre for Urban Studies in 1958, first used "gentrification" in its current sense in 1964 (*London: Aspects of Change*), with Islington among her examples. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Glass ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentrification]
- There is no agreed definition of gentrification; a Brookings report (Kennedy and Leonard, 2001) calls the term "both imprecise and quite politically charged". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentrification]
- Urban heat island: cities are warmer than their surroundings, especially at night; Luke Howard's study of London (1818) first reported it, finding the centre warmer at night by about 2.1 °C (3.7 °F). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island]
- Sponge cities: a nature-based flood and stormwater model proposed by the landscape architect Kongjian Yu and adopted by China's State Council as national policy in 2014. A 2010 Ministry of Housing survey found 62 per cent of 351 Chinese cities flooded in 2008–2010. Comparable approaches: "green infrastructure" (Europe), "low-impact development" (US), "water-sensitive urban design" (Australia). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponge_city]
- Fletcher links the collapse of low-density cities such as Angkor to climate stress on sprawling infrastructure and draws lessons for today's low-density megacities. [source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-humanities/articles/10.3389/fdigh.2019.00014/pdf (via WebSearch)]

## Debates
- **What counts as a city?** Childe's ten-trait checklist (1950) versus cases that fail it: Çatalhöyük (dense but egalitarian, 600–800 people on recent estimates rather than the old 5,000–7,000: a large village or "proto-city"); Indus cities and Teotihuacan without known palaces or royal portraits (possible collective rule); Andean khipu instead of writing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%87atalh%C3%B6y%C3%BCk ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teotihuacan]
- **Compact versus low-density urbanism.** Fletcher (with Evans, Coningham and others) argues Angkor, Anuradhapura and the Maya cities were genuine low-density agrarian cities, overturning the assumption that premodern cities were small and dense; Angkor was the largest by area before industrialisation. Groslier's "hydraulic city" (1950s) versus scholars who saw the reservoirs as symbolic (see the published Southeast Asia chapter). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angkor ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-humanities/articles/10.3389/fdigh.2019.00014/pdf]
- **The "Islamic city".** The Orientalist model (Marçais and others) of a city shaped by Islam versus Abu-Lughod (1987), who called it a "myth" built on few cases and proposed processes (law, gender segregation, confessional quarters) instead. [source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0020743800031822]
- **Were Haussmann's boulevards built against barricades?** Critics from Zola to René Héron de Villefosse and Lewis Mumford said yes; Haussmann admitted using the argument to win funding but denied it drove the design; Patrice de Moncan calls the strategic reading "very reductive", and in 1871 the army defeated the Commune by avoiding the boulevards. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haussmann%27s_renovation_of_Paris]
- **Engels and the standard of living.** Engels's pessimism shaped Marxist historians of industrialisation; the optimist–pessimist debate is covered in the Industrial Revolution chapter (cross-refer). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Condition_of_the_Working_Class_in_England]
- **Moses: villain or builder?** Caro's *Power Broker* (1974) versus the 2007 revisionism of Hilary Ballon and Kenneth T. Jackson ("Every generation writes its own history"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Moses]
- **Jacobs's blind spots.** Celebrated for defending neighbourhood life, criticised from the left for ignoring race and welcoming "unslumming"; Mumford's 1962 review attacked her as naive. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Jacobs]
- **Did architecture kill Pruitt–Igoe?** Jencks's verdict versus later emphasis on federal cost limits, St Louis's depopulation and fiscal crisis, and racial politics ("doomed from the outset", *Architectural Review*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pruitt%E2%80%93Igoe]
- **Where does segregation come from?** Nightingale's global, colonial-origins account (Madras, Calcutta, 1890s "segregation mania") versus nation-centred US accounts; Rothstein's *de jure* thesis versus economists (Price Fishback and others; see the Modern America chapter) who find the HOLC maps followed rather than caused lending patterns, with the FHA's role stronger. [source: https://www.buffalo.edu/ubreporter/archive/2012_09_06/nightingale_segregation.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redlining]
- **Is the slum a catastrophe or a city in the making?** Davis's apocalyptic *Planet of Slums* versus critics (Merrifield, Angotti) who call it "anti-urban"; the Dharavi economy (over US$1 billion turnover) is often cited on the other side. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Davis_(scholar) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharavi]
- **How urban is the world?** Answers depend on definitions: 58 per cent urban on national definitions versus 45 per cent in cities (81 per cent in cities plus towns) on the UN's 2025 Degree of Urbanisation; Jakarta at 41.9 million versus 12 million administrative residents. [source: https://www.un.org/sustainabledevelopment/blog/2025/11/press-release-wup2025/ (via WebSearch)]

## Conflicts with published chapters
Contradictions (the writer should not repeat the published figure without a hedge; candidates for KNOWN_ISSUES):
- **Çatalhöyük population** — `history--before-writing--neolithic-revolution` says "several thousand people lived in a dense cluster of mud-brick houses" (dated 7100–5950 BCE). - **Population of Lagos/Kinshasa vs UN 2025** — `history--regional-histories--modern-africa` gives Lagos 17–21 million, Kinshasa about 18.5 million (other definitions).
