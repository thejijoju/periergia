## Progressive Era and the 1910s
- The Progressive Era is dated c. 1890s–1920s; reformers targeted political machines and won direct primaries, direct election of senators (Seventeenth Amendment), initiatives and referendums, and women's suffrage. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_Era]
- Muckraking magazines, notably *McClure's*, exposed corporate monopoly and machine politics (e.g. Ida Tarbell on Standard Oil, 1903 cover). Upton Sinclair's *The Jungle* (1906) on meatpacking led, amid public outcry, to the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act (both 1906); Sinclair quipped: "I aimed at the public's heart and by accident, I hit it in the stomach." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_Era]
- Theodore Roosevelt's "Square Deal" promised trust-busting, railroad regulation and pure food and drugs; Woodrow Wilson created the Federal Reserve, passed the FTC and Clayton Antitrust Acts — and accelerated the segregation of federal employees. In the South, progressivism coexisted with Jim Crow and Black disenfranchisement; in the West, with anti-Asian sentiment. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_Era]
- The Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire, Greenwich Village, New York, Saturday 25 March 1911, killed 146 garment workers — 123 women and girls and 23 men. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangle_Shirtwaist_Factory_fire]
- The "Red Summer" of 1919: white supremacist terrorism and race riots in more than three dozen cities and one rural Arkansas county (the Elaine massacre); Chicago and Omaha among them. The term was coined by James Weldon Johnson, then an NAACP field secretary. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Summer]
- Tulsa race massacre, Greenwood District, 31 May – 1 June 1921: death toll unknown; the 2001 Oklahoma Commission gives estimates "ranging from the official count of 36 to approximately 300". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa_race_massacre]
- Lynching: the Tuskegee Institute recorded 3,446 Black and 1,297 white victims between 1882 and 1968 (4,743 total; another passage gives 4,733 as of 1959), peaking in the 1890s. Tuskegee recorded Emmett Till (1955) as the last "classical" lynching. Congress finally passed a federal anti-lynching law, the Emmett Till Antilynching Act, in 2022. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynching_in_the_United_States]

## The Great Migration
- About 6 million Black Americans moved out of the rural South to the Northeast, Midwest and West between 1910 and 1970. Historians often split it: a first wave (1910–1940) of about 1.6 million, and a Second Great Migration (1940–1970) of at least 5 million. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Migration_(African_American)]
- James N. Gregory's figures (*The Southern Diaspora*): 204,000 Black southerners left in 1900–1910; about 1.4 million in the 1940s, 1.1 million in the 1950s, 2.4 million in the 1960s and early 1970s; the migration ended by the late 1970s as deindustrialisation set in. In 1900 only 740,000 African Americans (8%) lived outside the South; by 1970 more than 10.6 million (47%). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Migration_(African_American)]
- Pull factors: First World War labour shortages (European immigration fell from about 1.2 million in 1914 to 300,000 the next year) and northern labour agents; push factors included Jim Crow terror. Chicago's Black population grew from 278,000 to 813,000 in 1940–60. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Migration_(African_American)]
- Isabel Wilkerson describes "six million Black Southerners [moving] out of the terror of Jim Crow to an uncertain existence in the North and Midwest". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Migration_(African_American)]

## Women's suffrage (1920)
- Congress passed the Nineteenth Amendment (House 21 May 1919, Senate 4 June 1919). Tennessee became the 36th state to ratify, and the amendment took effect on 18 August 1920; it was certified on 26 August 1920. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution]
- Leaders: Carrie Chapman Catt (NAWSA); Alice Paul and Lucy Burns (National Woman's Party, militant tactics). Black clubwomen founded the National Association of Colored Women in 1896 (Frances E. W. Harper, Josephine St. Pierre Ruffin and others). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution]
- Limits: about three million women south of the Mason–Dixon line stayed disfranchised; Black women faced poll taxes, fraud, intimidation and violence (e.g. women beaten while trying to register in Birmingham, Alabama, in 1926) until the Voting Rights Act of 1965. Native Americans were citizens from 1924, but some states barred them from voting until 1957 (Miguel Trujillo's 1948 suit won the vote in New Mexico and Arizona). Alice Paul's NWP then began the Equal Rights Amendment campaign. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution]

## The 1920s: nativism, culture wars, the New Negro
- Immigration Act of 1924 (Johnson–Reed Act / National Origins Act), signed by Calvin Coolidge, 26 May 1924: it banned immigration from Asia (including Japan) and capped annual quotas for the rest of the world outside the Western Hemisphere at 165,000 — an 80% cut from the pre-1914 average. Quotas were cut from 3% of each nationality's 1910 population (Emergency Quota Act 1921) to 2% of the foreign-born counted in the 1890 census, chosen to favour northern and western Europeans; from 1927 a 150,000 cap based on 1920 "national origins". It also created the Border Patrol. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_Act_of_1924]
- Its architects, Rep. Albert Johnson (a eugenics advocate) and Sen. David Reed, conceived it as a bulwark against "a stream of alien blood"; the census choice was made at the urging of a eugenics subcommittee chaired by Madison Grant. The Klan supported it. The 1924 system governed policy until 1952, and the national-origins quotas lasted until the 1965 Act. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_Act_of_1924]
- The second Ku Klux Klan was founded in 1915 atop Stone Mountain, Georgia, by William Joseph Simmons, inspired by *The Birth of a Nation*; its membership peaked in 1924–25 at an estimated 3 to 6 million, well beyond the South. White robes and cross-burning came from Thomas Dixon's novel *The Clansman* (1905), not the first Klan. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Ku_Klux_Klan]
- Prohibition: the Eighteenth Amendment was ratified on 16 January 1919 (by 46 of 48 states) and enforced by the Volstead Act; national prohibition ran 1920–1933 and ended with the Twenty-first Amendment on 5 December 1933. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prohibition_in_the_United_States]
- Scopes trial: *Tennessee v. John Thomas Scopes*, 10–21 July 1925, under the Butler Act banning the teaching of human evolution; the ACLU defended (Clarence Darrow), William Jennings Bryan prosecuted; Scopes was fined $100, and the verdict was overturned on a technicality. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scopes_trial]
- Harlem Renaissance / "New Negro Movement", 1920s–30s, named after Alain Locke's anthology *The New Negro* (1925); Hubert Harrison had founded the Liberty League and *The Voice* in 1917; writers included Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Claude McKay, Anne Spencer. Hughes: Harlem gave the courage "to express our individual dark-skinned selves without fear or shame". Caribbean immigrants fed the movement. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlem_Renaissance]
- Marcus Garvey's Universal Negro Improvement Association was founded in Jamaica on 20 July 1914 and grew into a mass movement in the US; Garvey championed Back-to-Africa (though he never visited Africa). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Negro_Improvement_Association ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Garvey]

## The Great Depression
- Black Thursday was 24 October 1929; on Black Tuesday, 29 October, share prices fell by $14 billion in a day and more than $30 billion in the week. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression_in_the_United_States]
- Unemployment: the government did not calculate rates in the 1930s. Stanley Lebergott's widely accepted estimate puts the peak at 24.9% in 1933; Michael Darby (1976) put the peak at 22.5% in 1932 (he counted relief workers as employed). Unemployment fell from about 25% to about 9% in 1933–37, then rose from 14.3% in 1937 to 19.0% in 1938 in the "Roosevelt recession". (The two 1937 figures come from different series in the same article; say "fell sharply, then jumped again in 1937–38".) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression_in_the_United_States]
- Dust Bowl: about 3.5 million people moved out of the Plains states in 1930–40, many to California ("Okies", "Arkies"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_Bowl]
- Mexican Repatriation (1929–39): about 82,000 were formally deported; estimates of all who moved to Mexico range from 300,000 to 2 million, most between 500,000 and 1 million, peaking in 1931. An estimated 40–60% of the "voluntary" repatriates were US citizens, overwhelmingly American-born children. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Repatriation]

## The New Deal and its exclusions
- FDR was inaugurated on Saturday 4 March 1933 (the last March inauguration) and gave a 1,883-word, roughly 20-minute address. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_inauguration_of_Franklin_D._Roosevelt]
- Social Security Act, signed 14 August 1935 (drafted by a committee under Labor Secretary Frances Perkins), created old-age insurance and unemployment insurance. It excluded agricultural labour, domestic service, government employees and many teachers, nurses, hospital employees, librarians and social workers. As a result, 65% of the African American workforce was excluded at first (and 27% of white workers); many were covered only after the 1950 and 1954 expansions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Security_Act]
- National Labor Relations Act (Wagner Act), signed 5 July 1935: it guaranteed collective bargaining but excludes agricultural and domestic workers (and supervisors, government employees, independent contractors). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Labor_Relations_Act_of_1935]
- Black workers served in segregated CCC units. Federal housing policy (the FHA's underwriting manual, which HOLC maps followed) marked neighbourhoods with "inharmonious racial groups" red or yellow and required restrictive covenants for federally backed projects. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal]
- Conservative southern Democrats controlled Congress's major committees in the 1930s. The New Deal article attributes the exclusion of farm and domestic workers from Social Security to Treasury Secretary Morgenthau's insistence that workers outside industry would not be "paying their way" (citing Zelizer). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal]

## Second World War home front
- Executive Order 8802, signed 25 June 1941, banned racial discrimination in defence industries and created the Fair Employment Practice Committee. It was issued under pressure from A. Philip Randolph (Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters), Walter White and the March on Washington Movement, which had planned a march for 1 July 1941. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_8802]
- Double V campaign: launched by the *Pittsburgh Courier* on 7 February 1942 (victory over fascism abroad and racism at home), after a letter from James G. Thompson of Wichita, Kansas, published 31 January 1942. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_V_campaign]
- Wartime full employment: unemployment fell from 7.7 million (spring 1940) to 1.5 million (fall 1942) and an all-time low of 700,000 (fall 1944), out of a labour force of about 54 million. White workers in Detroit's UAW plants sometimes struck against the promotion of Black workers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_home_front_during_World_War_II]
- Women's work: figures vary — the *Encyclopedia of American Economic History* (cited by WP) says the number of working women rose from 12 million to 20 million by 1944; elsewhere the article says nearly 19 million women held jobs, with only about 3 million new entrants. The share of women working fell from 36% to 28% by 1947. Use "roughly 12 million to 19–20 million". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosie_the_Riveter]
- Japanese American incarceration: Executive Order 9066, 19 February 1942; about 120,000 people of Japanese descent on the West Coast held in 10 War Relocation Authority camps (in California, Arizona, Wyoming, Colorado, Utah, Idaho and Arkansas); about two-thirds were US citizens. In Hawaii, where more than 150,000 Japanese Americans were over a third of the population, only 1,200–1,800 were held. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internment_of_Japanese_Americans]
- *Korematsu v. United States* (1944) upheld the removals; *Ex parte Endo*, decided the same day, held that a loyal citizen could not be detained. Exclusion orders were rescinded on 17 December 1944. About 20,000 Japanese Americans served in the war. Camp hospitals recorded 5,981 births and 1,862 deaths. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internment_of_Japanese_Americans]
- The 1983 commission report *Personal Justice Denied* found little evidence of disloyalty. The Civil Liberties Act of 1988 (signed by Reagan) apologised, attributing the policy to "race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership", and paid $20,000 to each surviving detainee — more than $1.6 billion to 82,219 people by 1992. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internment_of_Japanese_Americans]
- Terminology: in 2022 the AP stylebook advised "incarcerated"/"incarceration" over "internment", since about two-thirds were citizens. FDR himself called the camps "concentration camps" (press conference, 20 October 1942); Harold Ickes in 1946: "We gave the fancy name of 'relocation centers' to these dust bowls, but they were concentration camps nonetheless." Recommend "incarceration" in prose. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internment_of_Japanese_Americans]
- Zoot Suit Riots, Los Angeles, 3–8 June 1943: US servicemen attacked Mexican American youths and other zoot-suit wearers (also Filipino and Black youths). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoot_Suit_Riots]
- Bracero Program, 1942–1964: a US–Mexico guest-worker scheme that issued 4.6 million employment contracts (contracts, not distinct individuals) in 24 states; the largest guest-worker programme in US history. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bracero_Program]

## Postwar affluence, suburbia, redlining and the GI Bill
- Home Owners' Loan Corporation created 13 June 1933 to refinance defaulted mortgages. In 1935 the Federal Home Loan Bank Board asked HOLC to make "residential security maps" of 239 cities; red ("D") areas were often marked for their Black residents (e.g. a 1937 Philadelphia zone citing a "concentration of Negros and Italians"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Owners%27_Loan_Corporation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redlining]
- The Supreme Court ruled racial covenants unenforceable in *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948) but did not make them illegal; the Fair Housing Act (signed 11 April 1968) outlawed housing discrimination. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redlining ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_Housing_Act]
- Levittown, New York: clause 25 of the standard lease barred "non-White" occupancy; William Levitt said: "As a Jew, I have no room in my heart for racial prejudice, but the plain fact is that most whites prefer not to live in mixed communities." The FHA insured only non-mixed developments. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levittown,_New_York]
- GI Bill (Servicemen's Readjustment Act), signed 22 June 1944, drafted largely by the American Legion. By 1956, 7.8 million veterans had used its education benefits (2.2 million at college, 5.6 million in training programmes); it offered low-interest, zero-down-payment mortgages. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servicemen%27s_Readjustment_Act_of_1944]
- Unequal effects: Katznelson says "the law was deliberately designed to accommodate Jim Crow" (administration was left to local and state bodies). In the New York and northern New Jersey suburbs, 67,000 mortgages were insured under the bill, fewer than 100 to non-whites. By 1946 only a fifth of the 100,000 Black veterans who had applied for education benefits were in college; HBCUs turned away an estimated 20,000 veterans. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servicemen%27s_Readjustment_Act_of_1944]
- Economists Sarah Turner and John Bound (*Journal of Economic History*, 2003) found large educational gains for white men and for Black men born outside the South (about 0.4 extra years of college), but little effect for Black southerners, which widened the Black–white gap in the South. [source: https://www.nber.org/digest/dec02/gi-bill-world-war-ii-and-education-black-americans]
- J. K. Galbraith's *The Affluent Society* was published in 1958. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Affluent_Society]
- The Bracero era also saw "Operation Wetback" (from 17 June 1954). The INS claimed 1.1–1.3 million expulsions, but DHS statistics show 17,695 "removals" and 232,769 "returns" in fiscal year 1955. Treat the headline figure as an official claim, not a count. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Wetback]

## The Cold War at home: McCarthyism
- Truman's Executive Order 9835 (21 March 1947) began loyalty screening of all federal civil servants. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCarthyism]
- Julius and Ethel Rosenberg were arrested in 1950 for atomic espionage and executed in 1953; the Hollywood Ten were blacklisted. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCarthyism]
- Senator Joseph McCarthy's rise began with his speech at Wheeling, West Virginia, on 9 February 1950. No recording survives; he is usually quoted as claiming "a list of 205" State Department communists. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_McCarthy]
- Army–McCarthy hearings: on 9 June 1954 the Army's counsel Joseph N. Welch rebuked McCarthy over his attack on Fred Fisher. The Senate censured McCarthy on 2 December 1954 by 67–22. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_McCarthy]
- Lavender scare: in 1953 the State Department reported firing 425 employees on allegations of homosexuality; Eisenhower's Executive Order 10450 (1953) barred gay people from federal employment. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lavender_scare]

## The civil rights movement
- *Brown v. Board of Education*, decided 17 May 1954, unanimously (9–0) under Chief Justice Earl Warren; NAACP counsel Thurgood Marshall argued that "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal". *Brown II* (1955) ordered desegregation only "with all deliberate speed". Cold War concerns were explicit: Justice Douglas wrote that US treatment of its "colored minorities" mattered for relations with India, and Warren said "Our American system like all others is on trial". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_v._Board_of_Education]
- Emmett Till (born 25 July 1941), aged 14, was abducted and lynched in Mississippi on 28 August 1955, and his killers were acquitted. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmett_Till]
- Montgomery bus boycott: Rosa Parks (who had attended Highlander Folk School that year) was arrested on 1 December 1955. The boycott ran from 5 December 1955 to 20 December 1956 (381 days), when *Browder v. Gayle* took effect. Organisers included Jo Ann Robinson's Women's Political Council and E. D. Nixon; the Montgomery Improvement Association made Martin Luther King Jr. a national figure. Earlier, 15-year-old Claudette Colvin and 18-year-old Mary Louise Smith had been arrested for the same refusal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montgomery_bus_boycott]
- Little Rock Nine, 1957: Governor Orval Faubus blocked the students; Eisenhower sent the 101st Airborne. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Rock_Nine]
- March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom, 28 August 1963, organised by Bayard Rustin and A. Philip Randolph; estimates were 200,000–300,000, most often 250,000, and about 75–80% of marchers were Black. King spoke 16th of 18 speakers; Mahalia Jackson called out "Tell 'em about the dream, Martin!" before his improvised peroration. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_on_Washington_for_Jobs_and_Freedom ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Have_a_Dream]
- Freedom Summer (June–August 1964), the Mississippi voter-registration campaign, during which James Chaney, Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner were murdered. Fannie Lou Hamer, vice-chair of the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, challenged the 1964 Democratic convention. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Summer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fannie_Lou_Hamer]
- Civil Rights Act of 1964: proposed by Kennedy in June 1963; after a 72-day Senate filibuster it passed 290–130 (House) and 73–27 (Senate) and was signed by Johnson on 2 July 1964. It outlaws discrimination by race, colour, religion, sex or national origin; Title VII covers employment. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1964]
- Selma to Montgomery marches, 7–25 March 1965 ("Bloody Sunday" on the Edmund Pettus Bridge on 7 March; John Lewis among the leaders; prompted by the killing of Jimmie Lee Jackson). Voting Rights Act signed on 6 August 1965: it banned literacy tests and imposed federal preclearance. By the end of 1965 a quarter of a million new Black voters were registered (a third by federal examiners); by the end of 1966 only 4 of 13 southern states had under 50% of African Americans registered. *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013) disabled preclearance. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selma_to_Montgomery_marches ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act_of_1965]
- Watts, Los Angeles, 11–16 August 1965: 34 killed. The Kerner Commission (set up July 1967 after more than 150 riots, notably Detroit) warned in its 1968 report: "Our nation is moving toward two societies, one black, one white—separate and unequal." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watts_riots ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerner_Commission]
- War on Poverty: announced in the State of the Union address on 8 January 1964 against a poverty rate of about 19%. The official poverty rate fell from 19.5% (1963) to 11.1% (1973). Great Society laws included Medicare and Medicaid (Social Security Amendments of 1965), Head Start, the Job Corps and the Elementary and Secondary Education Act. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_on_poverty ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Society]

## Malcolm X and Black Power
- Malcolm X (born Malcolm Little, 19 May 1925; later el-Hajj Malik el-Shabazz) left the Nation of Islam in March 1964, made the Hajj, gave "The Ballot or the Bullet" in April 1964, and urged self-defence "by any means necessary". He was assassinated at the Audubon Ballroom, New York, on 21 February 1965. *The Autobiography of Malcolm X* (with Alex Haley) appeared posthumously in 1965. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_X]
- In 2021 two of the three men convicted of his murder, Muhammad Abdul Aziz and Khalil Islam, were exonerated after a review found the FBI and NYPD had withheld evidence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_X]
- Stokely Carmichael (later Kwame Ture; born in Trinidad, came to the US at 11), newly chair of SNCC, gave his first "Black Power" speech in Greenwood, Mississippi, in June 1966 during James Meredith's March Against Fear, after his 27th arrest. Richard Wright had used the phrase in his 1954 book *Black Power*. Carmichael told King he raised it "to force you to take a stand for Black Power"; King replied, "I have been used before." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stokely_Carmichael]
- Black Panther Party founded in Oakland in late October 1966 by Huey P. Newton and Bobby Seale (about 5,000 members in 1969). The Panthers were targets in 233 of the 295 authorised "Black nationalist" COINTELPRO actions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Power_movement ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Panther_Party]

## Latino and Asian American movements; the 1965 Immigration Act
- Delano grape strike: begun on 8 September 1965 by the mainly Filipino Agricultural Workers Organizing Committee (Larry Itliong); a week later the mainly Mexican National Farm Workers Association (César Chávez, Dolores Huerta) joined; the two merged in August 1966 into what became the UFW. The strike and boycott ended on 29 July 1970 with contracts. (The infobox gives 7 September as the start; the text gives 8 September.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delano_grape_strike]
- "Sí se puede" is credited to Huerta by some and attributed to Chávez by researchers Hammerback and Jensen; Chávez himself credited Huerta. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolores_Huerta]
- Chicano Movement leaders: Chávez (California), Reies López Tijerina (New Mexico land-grant movement), Rodolfo "Corky" Gonzales (Denver; poem *Yo Soy Joaquín*; *El Plan Espiritual de Aztlán*, adopted March 1969), José Ángel Gutiérrez (Texas, La Raza Unida Party), Héctor P. García (American GI Forum). Brown Berets and MAYO drew on the Black Panthers. COINTELPRO infiltration and police violence contributed to the movement's decline by the mid-1970s. Chicanas formed the Comisión Femenil Mexicana Nacional and fought coerced sterilisation (*Madrigal v. Quilligan*, 1975). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicano_Movement]
- East L.A. walkouts: the first on 5 March 1968, involving thousands of students (teacher Sal Castro arrested), described as "the first major mass protest against racism undertaken by Mexican-Americans". The National Chicano Moratorium antiwar march was in Los Angeles in 1970. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_L.A._walkouts ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicano_Movement]
- *Mendez v. Westminster* (Ninth Circuit, 1947) ended the segregation of Mexican American pupils in California; Earl Warren, then governor, supported integration after it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendez_v._Westminster ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_v._Board_of_Education]
- Asian American movement: Yuji Ichioka and Emma Gee founded the Asian American Political Alliance at UC Berkeley in May 1968 and coined the term "Asian American" (replacing "Oriental"). The Third World Liberation Front strikes at San Francisco State (from November 1968) and Berkeley (January 1969), multiracial coalitions of Black, Latino, Asian and Native students, won ethnic studies programmes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_American_movement ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_World_Liberation_Front]
- Vincent Chin, Chinese American draftsman, was beaten to death in Detroit (died 23 June 1982) by Chrysler supervisor Ronald Ebens and his stepson, laid-off autoworker Michael Nitz. They pleaded guilty to manslaughter and got three years' probation and a $3,780 fine; Ebens's federal civil-rights conviction was overturned. The case galvanised Asian American activism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killing_of_Vincent_Chin]
- Hart–Celler Act (Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965), signed 3 October 1965 by LBJ at the foot of the Statue of Liberty, abolished the national-origins quotas from 1 July 1968. It set 170,000 for the Eastern Hemisphere (20,000 per country) and — for the first time — 120,000 for the Western Hemisphere, with preference for family reunification and skills. Johnson: "not a revolutionary bill. It does not affect the lives of millions." In fact, immigration from Asia and Africa rose sharply in following decades. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_and_Nationality_Act_of_1965]

## Second-wave feminism, gay liberation, Roe
- Betty Friedan's *The Feminine Mystique* was published by W. W. Norton on 19 February 1963 and sold over a million copies at first. NOW was founded on 30 June 1966 by Friedan, Pauli Murray and 47 others. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Feminine_Mystique ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Organization_for_Women]
- Title IX was signed on 23 June 1972. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_IX]
- Equal Rights Amendment: it passed the House on 12 October 1971 (354–24) and the Senate on 22 March 1972 (84–8) with a seven-year deadline, later extended to 1982; it fell three states short of the 38 needed. Phyllis Schlafly (whose *A Choice Not an Echo*, 1964, backed Goldwater and sold over three million copies; Eagle Forum founded 1972) led STOP ERA, arguing it would end housewives' Social Security protections and subject women to the draft. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_Rights_Amendment ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phyllis_Schlafly]
- *Roe v. Wade*, decided 22 January 1973, 7–2 (opinion by Justice Blackmun), grounded abortion rights in a Fourteenth Amendment "right to privacy", with a trimester framework. *Planned Parenthood v. Casey* (1992) kept the core holding but replaced the trimesters with an "undue burden" test. *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022) overruled Roe. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roe_v._Wade]
- Stonewall: a police raid on the Mafia-owned Stonewall Inn, Greenwich Village, at 1:20 a.m. on 28 June 1969 set off several nights of riots. The first anniversary was marked by marches that became Pride parades; Stonewall National Monument dates from 2016. Caution: the historian David Carter acknowledged in 2019 that the account of Sylvia Rivera's presence was fabricated, and witnesses including Marsha P. Johnson agreed Rivera was not there that night. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonewall_riots]
- AIDS was first recognised in 1981 from clusters of rare illnesses among gay men in Los Angeles, New York and San Francisco; combination antiretroviral therapy from the mid-1990s more than halved deaths (David Ho, *Time* Man of the Year 1996). The Reagan administration's slow response is widely criticised. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_in_the_United_States ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan]

## The rise of conservatism
- Barry Goldwater's *The Conscience of a Conservative* (1960). He voted for the 1957 Civil Rights Act but against the 1964 Act (objecting to Titles II and VII), and later called that vote one of his greatest regrets. Nomination speech, 1964: "extremism in the defense of liberty is no vice! … moderation in the pursuit of justice is no virtue!" [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Goldwater]
- 1964 result: Johnson won 61.1% of the vote and 486 electoral votes to 52; Goldwater carried only Arizona and the five Deep South states, which had not voted Republican since Reconstruction (Louisiana in 1956 apart). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_United_States_presidential_election]
- Reagan's "A Time for Choosing" speech (1964) launched him; he was elected governor of California in 1966 and cracked down on university protests. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan]
- Nixon's 1968 "law and order" and "states' rights" campaign, aided by Strom Thurmond (who had switched parties in 1964); Kevin Phillips popularised (but did not coin) "Southern strategy". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_strategy]
- Moral Majority founded in 1979 by Jerry Falwell Sr. and Paul Weyrich; the IRS's moves against segregated Christian academies (*Green v. Connally*, 1971; Bob Jones University) were part of the Christian right's origins. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_Majority]
- 1980: Reagan won 489 electoral votes to Carter's 49 and 50.7% of the vote to 41.0% (Anderson 6.6%). In August 1980 he spoke at the Neshoba County Fair near Philadelphia, Mississippi — near where three civil-rights workers were murdered in 1964 — saying "I believe in states' rights". 1984: 525 electoral votes, 49 states, 59%. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_United_States_presidential_election ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan]

## Deindustrialisation and the Rust Belt
- The Rust Belt (formerly Steel Belt or Factory Belt), centred on the Great Lakes and Mid-Atlantic; the term spread in the 1980s in contrast to the booming Sun Belt. Steel faced competition from Germany and Japan; firms moved to the Sun Belt or abroad. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rust_Belt]
- Youngstown's "Black Monday": on 19 September 1977 Youngstown Sheet and Tube closed its Campbell Works and furloughed 5,000 workers. Bethlehem Steel ended most operations in 1982 (bankrupt 2001). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Youngstown_Sheet_and_Tube ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rust_Belt]
- From 1970 to 2006, Cleveland, Detroit, Buffalo and Pittsburgh lost about 45% of their population; Detroit fell from 1,849,568 (1950) to 951,270 (2000). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rust_Belt]
- The classic economic study of the subject is Bluestone and Harrison (1982). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deindustrialization]

## Mass incarceration
- Nixon declared drug abuse "public enemy number one" at a press conference on 17 June 1971; the media popularised "war on drugs". Reagan signed the Anti-Drug Abuse Acts of 1986 and 1988 (cross-refer to *History and Drugs* for the 100:1 ratio). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_on_drugs ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan]
- Incarceration count and rate (jails plus state and federal prisons; World Prison Brief): 1970 — 328,020 (161 per 100,000); 1980 — 503,586 (220); 1990 — 1,148,702 (457); 2000 — 1,937,482 (683); 2008 — 2,307,504 (755). The total peaked around 2008–09 and fell about 25% by the end of 2021. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incarceration_in_the_United_States]
- Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act, signed 13 September 1994: the largest crime bill in US history, with 100,000 police, $9.7 billion for prisons, a federal "three strikes" provision and an end to Pell grants for prisoners. Then-Senator Joe Biden drafted the Senate version; it included the assault-weapons ban and the Violence Against Women Act. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violent_Crime_Control_and_Law_Enforcement_Act]
- Crime fell sharply in many countries from the early 1990s (the "crime drop"), which complicates purely crime-driven explanations of incarceration growth after 1990. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_drop]

## The 1990s
- Los Angeles, 29 April – 3 May 1992, after the acquittal of the officers who beat Rodney King: 55 killed (63 if eight unrelated deaths are counted), 2,383 injured, more than 12,000 arrested, over $1 billion in damage. Koreatown was disproportionately hit; the 1991 killing of Latasha Harlins by a Korean American shopkeeper was among the grievances. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Los_Angeles_riots]
- NAFTA was signed into law by Clinton on 8 December 1993 and took effect on 1 January 1994. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Free_Trade_Agreement]
- The 1994 "Contract with America" (Gingrich and Armey): Republicans won the House for the first time in 40 years. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contract_with_America]
- Oklahoma City bombing, 19 April 1995, by Timothy McVeigh and Terry Nichols: 168 killed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City_bombing]
- Welfare reform (PRWORA), signed 22 August 1996, replaced AFDC with TANF, fulfilling Clinton's pledge to "end welfare as we know it". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_Responsibility_and_Work_Opportunity_Act]
- The 1990s expansion was the longest on record until 2019; the federal deficit of $290 billion (1992) became a $236 billion surplus (2000). Clinton was impeached on 19 December 1998 and acquitted by the Senate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1990s_United_States_boom ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Clinton]

## Debates
- **The "long civil rights movement".** Jacquelyn Dowd Hall, "The Long Civil Rights Movement and the Political Uses of the Past", *Journal of American History* (March 2005; from her 2004 OAH presidential address; she had used the term in 2001). She argues the "classical" 1954–68 narrative (Montgomery to Selma, King, the 1964 and 1965 Acts) is too narrow and triumphalist; the movement began in the 1930s–40s labour radicalism of the New Deal era, extended beyond the 1960s and was national, not merely southern. Critics: Sundiata Keita Cha-Jua and Clarence Lang, "The 'Long Movement' as Vampire: Temporal and Spatial Fallacies in Recent Black Freedom Studies", *Journal of African American History* 92 (2007) — the frame makes the movement virtually eternal, collapses "civil rights" into "Black Power" and erases regional (Mason–Dixon) distinctions; Eric Arnesen, "Reconsidering the 'Long Civil Rights Movement'" (*Historically Speaking*, 2009); Christopher Schmidt on legal history (*Law and Social Inquiry*, 2016); Thomas Holt's *The Movement* reasserts the "classic" phase. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_civil_rights_movement ; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/489819 ; https://edisciplinas.usp.br/mod/resource/view.php?id=348275]
- **The New Deal's achievements and limits.** Ira Katznelson (*When Affirmative Action Was White*, 2005; *Fear Itself*, Bancroft Prize 2014) argues that southern Democrats' power shaped programmes that excluded or under-served Black Americans (farm and domestic exclusions; local administration), and that the GI Bill "was deliberately designed to accommodate Jim Crow". Counterpoints: Larry DeWitt (*Social Security Bulletin*, 2010) rejects the view that racism drove the Social Security exclusions, citing administrative reasons, and the New Deal article attributes the exclusion to Morgenthau's fiscal insistence. Turner and Bound (2003) find the GI Bill helped Black men outside the South but widened the gap in the South — supporting a regional rather than uniform reading. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ira_Katznelson ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Security_Act ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal ; https://www.nber.org/digest/dec02/gi-bill-world-war-ii-and-education-black-americans]
- **Redlining and the state.** Richard Rothstein (*The Color of Law*, 2017) argues segregation is *de jure* — the product of explicit government policy at federal, state and local levels — not *de facto* private prejudice. Kenneth T. Jackson's *Crabgrass Frontier* (1980s) made HOLC central. Economic historian Price Fishback and co-authors (2021) argue the blame on HOLC is misplaced: HOLC refinanced Black homeowners' loans roughly in proportion to their share, and 90% of its refinancing was done before the maps were drawn. The FHA's underwriting rules remain the stronger case. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Color_of_Law ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Owners%27_Loan_Corporation]
- **Origins of the New Right.** Lisa McGirr (*Suburban Warriors*, 2001) gives a "bottom-up" account: Orange County, California, was "a prototype" of a new conservative milieu built by affluent, educated suburbanites — defence-industry workers, evangelicals and homeowners — not status-anxious marginal people, blending fundamentalism, nationalism and western libertarianism. One critique notes she did not press her subjects on their dependence on federal defence spending. On the South: the "top-down" Southern-strategy view (racial backlash drove realignment) is the scholarly consensus, but Matthew Lassiter, Kevin Kruse and Joseph Crespino stress suburban, class-based politics ("demographic change played a more important role than racial demagoguery" — Lassiter); Byron Shafer and Richard Johnston argue economics and class mattered more than race; Dean Kotlowski and others give Nixon's record a mixed reading. [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/669380 ; https://splcenter.org/fighting-hate/intelligence-report/2003/harvard-professor-researches-rise-goldwater-conservatism ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_strategy]
- **The "American century" framing.** Henry Luce coined it in a *Life* editorial of 17 February 1941, urging the US to abandon isolationism and take on a missionary world role. David Harvey notes Luce preferred "century" to "empire"; critics called his "jingoistic missionary zeal". Andrew Bacevich (ed.), *The Short American Century: A Postmortem* (Harvard, 2012): "To the extent that an American Century ever did exist, that moment has now definitively passed"; Walter LaFeber: "The American Century was stillborn"; within the same volume Emily Rosenberg and David M. Kennedy dissent (Kennedy defends the postwar order). A regional history should note that the framing centres foreign power, while this chapter treats the US as a society. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Century ; https://www.cjr.org/critical_eye/revisiting_henry_luces_america.php ; https://kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/andrew-j-bacevich/short-american-century/print]
- **Mass incarceration: Alexander and her critics.** Michelle Alexander (*The New Jim Crow*, 2010) argues mass incarceration, driven by the drug war, is a "racial caste" system — an "undercaste" — and a deliberate rollback of civil-rights gains. Elizabeth Hinton (2016) traces the carceral state back to Johnson's War on Poverty / war on crime and the 1974 juvenile justice act. Critics: James Forman Jr. (*Locking Up Our Own*, 2017) says Alexander obscures African American support for tougher crime laws and downplays violent crime; John Pfaff (*Locked In*) argues drug offenders are a small share of prisoners, most prisoners are held for violent crimes, and state and county systems (not the federal drug system) drive the numbers; John McWhorter says she oversimplifies; Michael Javen Fortner (*Black Silent Majority*, 2015) argues working- and middle-class Black New Yorkers pressed for the 1973 Rockefeller drug laws (reviewers dispute his evidence). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Jim_Crow ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Hinton ; https://reason.com/2015/12/20/harlems-drug-warriors/]
- **Black Power: civil rights' "evil twin"?** Older narratives cast Black Power as the movement's destructive counterpart. Peniel E. Joseph ("The Black Power Movement: A State of the Field", *JAH*, December 2009; *Waiting 'Til the Midnight Hour*, 2006) founded "Black Power Studies", arguing that Black Power activists overlapped with civil rights, helped transform postwar America, and that Malcolm X and King converged as radicals. [source: https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/the-black-power-movement-a-state-of-the-field/70581113 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peniel_E._Joseph ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Power_movement]
- **McCarthyism's name and reality.** Ellen Schrecker suggests "Hooverism" (after J. Edgar Hoover), since McCarthy was less central than the broader apparatus. Venona decryptions and Soviet archives confirm substantial Soviet espionage in the 1940s and Moscow's funding of the CPUSA, which some (e.g. Daniel Patrick Moynihan) cite to temper the liberal account; this does not vindicate McCarthy's own charges. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCarthyism]
