## 1. Name and dates
- CW gives the period as generally 12 March 1947 (Truman Doctrine) to 26 December 1991 (dissolution of the USSR), noting historians do not fully agree on start and end. [source: CW]
- First use of "cold war" for the US–Soviet confrontation: a speech by Bernard Baruch, 16 April 1947, written by journalist Herbert Bayard Swope ("we are today in the midst of a cold war"). Walter Lippmann popularised the term with his 1947 book *The Cold War* (a collection of 14 columns replying to Kennan's "X" article, first column 2 September 1947); he traced it to the 1930s French *la guerre froide*. [source: CW; XA]

## 2. Origins, 1945–1949
- Churchill's "Sinews of Peace" (Iron Curtain) speech: 46 minutes, 5 March 1946, Westminster College, Fulton, Missouri, audience of 1,500. [source: ICS]
- Iran 1946: Soviet troops stayed in northern Iran past the agreed withdrawal deadline, under cover of the Azerbaijan People's Government and the Kurdish Republic of Mahabad; plus the Turkish Straits crisis. [source: CW]
- **Long Telegram:** Kennan dictated it to his secretary Dorothy Hessman on 22 February 1946 in Moscow; "a little over 5,000 words", the longest telegram in the State Department's history to that point (XA). It answered queries about Stalin's February 1946 speech (Kennan's memoir says it replied to the Treasury's bewilderment at the USSR's refusal to join the IMF and World Bank). Washington accepted it as the best explanation of Soviet behaviour; Stalin saw a leaked copy and had ambassador Nikolai Novikov send a counterpart telegram from Washington (the Novikov telegram, September 1946). [source: XA; CW]
- **"X" article:** "The Sources of Soviet Conduct", *Foreign Affairs*, July 1947, signed "X" because Kennan was a serving official; it brought "containment" into wide use. Arthur Krock's column led to Kennan being identified (Forrestal had shown Krock a draft with the name). Kennan later admitted that his undefined "counterforce" confused readers, and he moved from perimeter to "strongpoint" defence. [source: XA]
- Thomas Borstelmann notes Kennan's few experiences outside Europe; Kennan recalled his "entire diplomatic experience took place in rather high northern latitudes", and Borstelmann describes his disdain for the peoples of Africa, Asia, the Middle East and Latin America. Useful for the global framing. [source: XA]
- **Truman Doctrine:** In February 1947 Britain said it could no longer finance the Greek government against the Communist-led Democratic Army. Truman addressed a joint session of Congress on 12 March 1947 (an 18-minute speech); Marshall and Kennan had criticised the draft's "rhetoric"; Vandenberg had advised playing up the threat. In May 1947 Congress approved $400 million of aid to Greece and Turkey. Critics noted the Greek monarchy was itself repressive; Stalin had told the Greek Communists to cooperate with the British-backed government. [source: TD; CW]
- **Marshall Plan:** announced in Marshall's Harvard address, 5 June 1947; offered to all Europe including the USSR, which refused and kept its satellites (and pressured Finland) out. Truman signed it on 3 April 1948 (CW). MP: $13.3 billion to 17 countries; Congress allocated $12.4 billion over four years (MP gives both figures; "about $13 billion" is safe). MP also notes $14 billion+ of US aid/loans before end-1947 not counted in the plan. Stalin's alternative was the Molotov Plan, institutionalised as Comecon in January 1949. [source: MP; CW; COM]
- National Security Act (July 1947) created the Department of Defense, the CIA and the NSC. Cominform founded September 1947. [source: CW]
- February 1948: Communist coup in Czechoslovakia. April 1948: Italian Christian Democrats beat the Communist–Socialist alliance with covert US support. [source: CW]
- **Berlin blockade:** 24 June 1948 – 12 May 1949, triggered by Western currency reform and plans for a West German state. Airlift (Operation Vittles from 25 June 1948) ran until 30 September 1949: 2,334,374 tons (nearly two-thirds coal) on 278,228 flights; US 76.4%, RAF 23.3%; at its peak landings "every thirty seconds"; candy drops ("raisin bombers"). Deaths: BB text says 101 fatalities (40 Britons, 31 Americans), mostly accidents; infobox gives 39 British, 31 American, 1 Australian plus 15 German civilians. Say "about a hundred people died, mostly in crashes". [source: BB]
- **NATO:** North Atlantic Treaty signed 4 April 1949 by the Western Union states plus the US, Canada, Portugal, Italy, Norway, Denmark and Iceland (12 founders); Canadian diplomat Lester Pearson was a key drafter. Article 5: an armed attack on one is an attack on all (invoked only once, after 11 September 2001). West Germany joined in 1955. [source: NATO; CW]
- Federal Republic of Germany established from the three Western zones in 1949 (CW: "April 1949" for the establishment; give only the year); the GDR proclaimed October 1949. [source: CW]
- **Warsaw Pact:** signed in Warsaw in May 1955 by the USSR and seven Eastern Bloc states, a response to West Germany's rearmament inside NATO. The USSR had proposed in 1954 that it join NATO; rejected. Its largest operation was the invasion of a member, Czechoslovakia, August 1968 (Albania and Romania did not take part; Albania withdrew). Dissolved 25 February 1991 (at a meeting in Hungary). [source: WP]
- **NSC-68:** 66-page top-secret paper presented to Truman 7 April 1950; called for a hydrogen bomb, big military build-up and aid to allies. NSC article: it called for tripling defence spending from $13 billion to $40–50 billion; actual US defence spending rose from about 5% to 14% of GDP, 1950–53. (CW says "quadrupling" — conflict; use "tripling".) Ernest May: it "provided the blueprint for the militarization of the Cold War". [source: NSC; CW]
- 1949: Communist victory in China; Westad attributes it to fewer military mistakes than Chiang made and Chiang antagonising too many groups. "Pactomania": ANZUS 1951, SEATO 1954, treaties with Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Philippines, Thailand. [source: CW]

## 3. The nuclear arms race
- **Soviet bomb:** RDS-1 ("First Lightning", US code-name Joe-1), 29 August 1949, Semipalatinsk, Kazakh SSR, 22 kilotons, modelled on Fat Man (atomic spies). US detected fallout; Truman announced on 23 September 1949 that "an atomic explosion occurred in the U.S.S.R." [source: RDS; NAR]
- **H-bomb:** Truman ordered a crash programme January 1950. Ivy Mike, 1 November 1952, Elugelab island, Enewetak Atoll: 10.4 megatons, first full-scale Teller–Ulam test,, a proof of concept not usable as a deliverable weapon. USSR: single-stage RDS-6s, August 1953; true two-stage RDS-37, 1955 (about 3 Mt). UK 1957, China 1967, France 1968 tested multi-megaton devices. [source: IVY]
- **Tsar Bomba**, 30 October 1961, Novaya Zemlya, 50 megatons, the most powerful weapon ever tested; Sakharov oversaw the project. [source: TSAR]
- **Stockpiles** (STK table, citing Norris & Kristensen, *Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists*, 2010). USSR/Russia: 1950: 5; 1955: 200; 1960: 1,605; 1965: 6,129; 1970: 11,643; 1975: 19,055; 1980: 30,062; 1985: 39,197; 1990: 37,000. Soviet stockpile peaked in 1986. US: 1945: 2; 1950: 299; 1955: 2,422; 1960: 18,638; 1965: 31,149. US peak: NWUS gives 31,225 total warheads in 1967 (another sentence "31,000 in 1967"); STK text says the US peak was 1966. Say "about 31,000 in the mid-to-late 1960s". (US figures after 1965 in the STK table look possibly misaligned; do not quote them.) UK peaked just under 500 (1981); France just over 500 (1992). South Africa built six weapons in the 1980s and dismantled them. FAS (2015) estimated 125,000 weapons built 1945–2013. MIRVs: over the following 10 years the two sides added 12,000 warheads. [source: STK; NWUS; NAR]
- Delivery: by 1960 both had ICBMs and SLBMs (the triad). Sputnik was launched on an R-7, the Soviet ICBM. [source: NAR; SPK]
- **Tests:** over 2,000 nuclear tests since 1945 (NWT; CTBT says "over 2,000 between 1945 and 1996"). US 1,054 by official count (STK table: 1,032) including 904 in Nevada and 106 at the Pacific Proving Grounds and elsewhere in the Pacific; USSR 715 (most at Semipalatinsk and Novaya Zemlya); France 210 (4 atmospheric near Reggane and 13 underground at In Ekker in the Algerian Sahara, the rest at Moruroa and Fangataufa, French Polynesia); UK 45 (12 in Australia — Montebello, Maralinga, Emu Field — 9 at Kiritimati/Christmas Island, 24 joint in Nevada); China 45 at Lop Nur, Xinjiang. Testing peaked in the 1960s–70s. [source: NWT; STK]
- **Marshall Islands (Pacific):** Bikini Atoll: 23 (or 24) US tests, 1946–58, total about 77–78.6 Mt. In February 1946 the US moved Bikini's 167 inhabitants "temporarily"; their leader King Juda said "we will go believing that everything is in the hands of God." They were moved to Rongerik (unable to sustain them) and later Kili. Operation Crossroads (July 1946): the Baker shot, which Glenn Seaborg called "the world's first nuclear disaster". LBJ promised return in 1968; residents were removed again in 1978. US has paid more than $300 million into trust funds. [source: BIK]
- **Castle Bravo**, 1 March 1954, Bikini: 15 Mt against a predicted 6 (range 4–8), about 1,000 times Hiroshima; the largest US test ever. Fallout fell on Rongelap and Utirik, evacuated after about 48 hours to three days, with radiation sickness; Rongelap residents returned in 1957 (82 people) and were later removed again; by 1963 thyroid tumours appeared, including in 20 of 29 Rongelap children exposed. The 23 crew of the Japanese fishing boat *Daigo Fukuryū Maru* (*Lucky Dragon No. 5*) got acute radiation syndrome; radioman Kuboyama Aikichi died six months later. Strong international reaction against atmospheric testing. [source: BRAVO; BIK]
- **Semipalatinsk (Kazakhstan):** site chosen by Beria in 1947, who called the 18,000 km² steppe "uninhabited"; built with Gulag labour. SEM lead: 468 tests, 1949–89; SEM body: 456 explosions (340 underground, 116 atmospheric) — say "over 450". A quarter of all tests in history. Kazakh experts estimate 1.5 million people were exposed to fallout; effects concealed until after closure. Olzhas Suleimenov founded the Nevada-Semipalatinsk movement in 1989 (one of the first major anti-nuclear movements in the USSR; mass rally at Karaaul). Nursultan Nazarbayev raised the site in the Supreme Soviet (30 May 1989) and resisted its expansion; closed 29 August 1991. [source: SEM]
- **MAD:** term coined (cynically, spelling "mad") by Donald Brennan of Herman Kahn's Hudson Institute in 1962; declared doctrine under Defence Secretary Robert McNamara in the early 1960s; earlier Dulles's "massive retaliation" (MAD article says "Instant Retaliation"). Reagan called MAD a "suicide pact" when announcing SDI (1983). [source: MAD; SDI]

## 4. Crises, hot war in Korea, and diplomacy
- **Korea:** divided at the 38th parallel (1945); separate states 1948 (Kim Il Sung; Syngman Rhee). North invaded 25 June 1950; UN Security Council (Soviet delegate absent) backed defence; UN command of 21 countries, US ~90% of personnel. Pusan Perimeter; Inchon landing 15 September 1950; Chinese People's Volunteers crossed the Yalu 19 October 1950; armistice 27 July 1953, DMZ; no peace treaty. [source: KW]
- **Korea deaths:** about 1 million military and an estimated 1.5–3 million civilian dead (KW lead); Cumings: "about 3 million", mostly civilians. Chinese dead: official 2010 figure 183,108 (plus 25,621 missing); US DoD estimated about 400,000 Chinese killed; Cumings up to 900,000. US: 36,515 dead or missing (infobox). South Korean military 137,899 dead. US dropped 635,000 tons of bombs (plus 32,557 tons napalm), more than in the whole Pacific War; almost every substantial building in the North destroyed. Massacres: Bodo League killings by South Korean forces, summer 1950 — 4,934 victims verified by the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, historians estimate at least 60,000, up to 200,000; of massacres petitioned to that commission, 82% by South Korean forces, 18% by North Korean; No Gun Ri (US troops killed refugees, July 1950; up to 400 per AP 1999). [source: KW]
- **1956:** Hungary 23 October – 4 November (fighting to 10 November); Nagy declared withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact 1 November; about 2,000 Hungarians (HUN lead; other estimates higher) and 700 Soviet soldiers killed; 200,000 Hungarians fled. Suez: Israel invaded 29 October, Britain and France 31 October; US, USSR and UN pressure forced withdrawal; canal closed to March 1957. Khrushchev, 18 November 1956: "We will bury you". [source: HUN; SUEZ; CW]
- **Berlin crises and Wall:** Khrushchev's 1958 "free city" ultimatum; 3.5 million East Germans (20% of population) left before the Wall; construction began 13 August 1961; at least 140 deaths at the Wall (ZZF); opened 9 November 1989. [source: BW; CW]
- **Cuban Missile Crisis** (16–28 October 1962; "13 days"; Soviet "Caribbean Crisis", Cuban "October Crisis"): background — US Thor missiles in Britain (1959) and Jupiters in Italy and Turkey (1961), the Bay of Pigs (April 1961), and the US sabotage campaign (Cuban Project/Mongoose) from November 1961; Khrushchev–Castro agreement July 1962. U-2 photographs in October; ExComm; 22 October "quarantine". 27 October: Major Rudolf Anderson's U-2 shot down over Cuba (Soviet commanders authorised it; Khrushchev told his son it was Cubans "at the direction of Raúl Castro" — conflicting). Same day, submarine B-59: captain Savitsky wanted to fire a nuclear torpedo; launch needed the consent of three officers and Vasily Arkhipov refused — "accounts differ" on how the argument ended; publicly known only from 2002. Settlement: missiles removed under a public US no-invasion pledge and a secret deal to remove Jupiters from Turkey; quarantine ended 20 November. In 1992 it was confirmed that Soviet forces in Cuba already had tactical nuclear warheads; Castro's 26 October telegram appeared to urge a pre-emptive strike if Cuba were invaded. [source: CMC; ARK]
- **Hotline:** memorandum signed 20 June 1963 in Geneva; operational 30 August 1963; first used 1967 (LBJ); never a red telephone (teletype). [source: HOT]
- Prague Spring: Dubček from 5 January 1968; invasion 20–21 August 1968 by the USSR, Bulgaria, East Germany, Hungary and Poland; "normalisation" under Husák. [source: PRA; CW]

## 5. Arms control
- Antarctic Treaty in force 1961 (first Cold War arms-control agreement). [source: CW]
- **Partial Test Ban Treaty:** signed Moscow 5 August 1963 by USSR, UK, US; in force 10 October 1963; bans tests in atmosphere, outer space and under water. Driven by public anxiety about fallout; atmospheric radioactivity fell afterwards. France and China, non-signatories, kept testing in the atmosphere until 1974 and 1980. [source: PTBT]
- **Outer Space Treaty** signed 27 January 1967. [source: SR]
- **NPT:** negotiated 1965–68 by the Eighteen Nation Committee on Disarmament (Geneva); opened for signature 1 July 1968; in force 1970; extended indefinitely 1995. Recognises five nuclear-weapon states that tested before 1967: US (1945), USSR (1949), UK (1952), France (1960), China (1964). Predictions at the time: 25–30 nuclear states within 20 years. India, Israel, Pakistan never joined; North Korea left (2003). [source: NPT]
- China's first bomb: 16 October 1964 (Project 596), 22 kt; China acknowledged Soviet assistance. [source: SSS]
- **SALT:** talks opened Helsinki, November 1969. SALT I and the ABM Treaty signed 26 May 1972 (SALT I froze strategic ballistic-missile launchers). SALT II signed Vienna 1979, never ratified after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (SALT text says "1980 Soviet invasion" — the invasion began December 1979); both sides respected it until it expired end-1985. ABM Treaty lasted 30 years; US withdrew June 2002. [source: SALT; ABM; CW]
- **INF Treaty:** signed by Reagan and Gorbachev 8 December 1987; eliminated all ground-launched missiles of intermediate and shorter range; 2,692 missiles destroyed by May 1991. START I signed 1991. Summits: Geneva, November 1985; Reykjavík, October 1986 (Gorbachev's proposal of deep cuts foundered on SDI). [source: INF; SALT; CW; NAR]
- **CTBT** adopted by the UN General Assembly 10 September 1996; not yet in force. [source: CTBT]

## 6. The Third World as battleground (Westad's "global Cold War")
- CW: the US used the CIA "to undermine neutral or hostile Third World governments and to support allied ones"; decolonisation, nationalism and communist parties overlapped. [source: CW]
- **Iran 1953:** Mosaddegh's government nationalised the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company (Majlis vote March 1951); Britain organised a boycott; Churchill solicited Eisenhower in early 1953 (Truman had opposed a coup). Operation Boot (MI6) / TP-AJAX (CIA). After a failed attempt on 15–16 August, on 19 August 1953 CIA-backed crowds (including paid mobsters) and army units took Tehran; 200–300 killed. Mosaddegh sentenced to three years (then house arrest). The Shah ruled 26 more years until 1979; SAVAK. In August 2013 the US government formally acknowledged its role; a released CIA history says the coup was carried out "under CIA direction" and "as an act of U.S. foreign policy, conceived and approved at the highest levels of government". Iranian actors mattered too: Ayatollah Kashani broke with Mosaddegh; the August 1953 referendum to dissolve parliament (99.9%) was widely seen as fraudulent. [source: IRN]
- **Guatemala 1954:** Arévalo (elected after the 1944 revolution) then Árbenz (from 1951) — land reform (Decree 900) hit the United Fruit Company, which lobbied Washington; the Dulles brothers had UFC links. Truman's PBFortune (1952); Eisenhower authorised PBSuccess in August 1953; the CIA trained 480 men under Carlos Castillo Armas, who invaded 18 June 1954 with psychological warfare and bombing; the army would not fight; Árbenz resigned 27 June. Executions afterwards: hundreds to 5,000. Civil war 1960–1996, including genocide of Maya peoples (detail belongs to *Latin America in the Cold War*). [source: GUA]
- **Congo:** independence 30 June 1960; army mutiny; Katanga and South Kasai seceded with Belgian support; UN peacekeepers would not fight the secession, so Lumumba turned to the USSR; Mobutu's coup (September 1960); Lumumba captured, sent to Katanga, tortured and shot by Tshombe's Katangan authorities with Belgian mercenaries, 17 January 1961; body dissolved in acid. Eisenhower authorised a CIA plan to poison Lumumba (September 1960), not carried out. Belgium apologised in 2002 for "moral responsibility"; returned Lumumba's tooth to his family in 2022. Hammarskjöld killed in a plane crash 1961. Crisis 1960–65 killed around 100,000; ended with Mobutu in power. Che Guevara led about 100 Cubans in eastern Congo in 1964–65 (Gleijeses). [source: CON; LUM; Gleijeses search below]
- **Indonesia 1965–66:** after the 30 September Movement's failed coup, the army under Suharto blamed the PKI (the world's third-largest communist party: ~300,000 cadres, ~2 million full members). Killings October 1965 – March 1966, worst in Central and East Java, Bali, north Sumatra; victims included Gerwani women, unionists, ethnic Chinese (in the thousands, Cribb and Coppel), abangan Javanese. **Death toll:** "most widely published estimates" 500,000 to 1 million; some estimates 2–3 million; a 1976 Indonesian security command estimate 450,000–500,000; Robert Cribb suggests 500,000 as most accurate; Suharto confidant Jan Walendouw cited 1.2 million; Vincent Bevins "up to a million or perhaps more". Up to 1.5 million imprisoned at some point. The US embassy supplied lists of up to 5,000 suspected communists; US, UK and Australian black propaganda; a 1968 CIA report called it one of the worst mass murders of the century (quotation below). Led to Suharto's 30-year New Order and Sukarno's fall; still largely omitted from Indonesian textbooks. [source: IND]
- **Angola:** civil war 1975–2002 (MPLA vs UNITA, FNLA fading); Cuban troops and Soviet support for the MPLA; US and South Africa backed UNITA; 500,000–800,000 dead, over a million displaced. Gleijeses: Castro decided on 4 November 1975 to send troops in response to the South African invasion, not on Soviet orders. Clark Amendment (barring US aid) lifted 1985. Cuito Cuanavale (13 January – 23 March 1988), called the largest conventional battle in sub-Saharan Africa since WWII; both sides claimed victory; Cuban troops numbered 12,000–15,000 in the south by 1987. New York Accords, 22 December 1988 (Angola, Cuba, South Africa): Namibian independence and withdrawal of foreign troops. [source: ANG; Gleijeses search]
- **Mozambique:** civil war 1977–1992, FRELIMO (Marxist) vs RENAMO, backed by Rhodesia then South Africa; over a million killed or starved, 5 million displaced; Rome General Peace Accords 1992 after Soviet and South African support collapsed; mediated by the Mozambican Christian Council and Italy (Sant'Egidio not named in MOZ lead). [source: MOZ]
- **Horn of Africa:** the Derg overthrew Haile Selassie 12 September 1974; Red Terror (Qey Shibir) from 1976, escalated after Mengistu became chairman 3 February 1977 (estimates range absurdly widely, 10,000–980,000; say "tens of thousands at least"). Somalia invaded the Ogaden (July 1977 – March 1978); the USSR switched sides to Ethiopia: a $1 billion airlift, more than 12,000 Cubans and 1,500 Soviet advisers under General Vasily Petrov; Somalia defeated. Famine 1983–85 killed 400,000–590,000; Ethiopian civil war 1974–91 left at least 1.4 million dead (1 million famine-related); Mengistu fled 28 May 1991. [source: OGA; ETH; RT]
- **Afghanistan:** Soviet invasion December 1979 (Storm-333) after the 1978 Saur Revolution and the Herat uprising (March 1979); mujahideen backed by Pakistan, US (Operation Cyclone), UK, China, Saudi and Gulf states, Iran; 1–3 million Afghans dead (6.5–11.5% of 13.5 million); millions of refugees (most to Pakistan and Iran; Iran alone ~1.8 million in one table). Soviet dead: official 14,453 (other estimates to 26,000). Whether Stingers (from September 1986) were a turning point is disputed. Last Soviet troops left 15 February 1989. NAM split over it. Ended détente (Carter: SALT II withdrawn, grain embargo, Moscow Olympic boycott; Soviets boycotted Los Angeles 1984). [source: AFG; CW; CW79; NAM]
- CW: "Millions died in the superpowers' proxy wars"; Kim (2014): East Asia's regional total 1945–94 was 10.4 million (Chinese civil war 1 million, Korea 3 million, Vietnam 2 million, Cambodia 1–2 million). Nearly 100,000 Americans died in Korea and Vietnam; US Cold War military spending ~$8 trillion. [source: CW]
- Cuba: revolution 1 January 1959 (Castro, Guevara). [source: CW] (Latin America detail belongs to its own chapter.)

## 7. Bandung and Non-Alignment
- Asian–African Conference, Bandung, West Java, 18–24 April 1955; 29 countries representing 1.5 billion people (54% of humanity); convened by Indonesia, Burma, India, Ceylon and Pakistan; coordinated by Ruslan Abdulgani. Aims: Afro-Asian economic and cultural co-operation, opposition to colonialism and neocolonialism. Final communiqué adopted the ten-point *Dasasila Bandung* incorporating the UN Charter and the Five Principles of Peaceful Coexistence. Zhou Enlai, who survived an assassination attempt on the way to the conference, was conciliatory; Nehru argued for non-alignment and against military pacts and was disappointed by his reception (Zhou privately called him arrogant). Sukarno sought support for Indonesia's claim to West Papua. [source: BAN]
- Richard Wright attended with Congress for Cultural Freedom funding and wrote *The Color Curtain* — a neat link between Bandung and the cultural Cold War. [source: BAN]
- **Non-Aligned Movement:** founded at the Belgrade conference, September 1961, initiated by Tito, with Nasser, Nehru, Nkrumah and Sukarno; Castro chaired the 1979 Havana summit (his "Havana Declaration" wording below); the movement split over Afghanistan (1979). Today 120 states. Bandung and NAM ran in parallel and clashed before the 1964 Cairo NAM conference. [source: NAM; CW]
- Khrushchev courted India and other neutrals. [source: CW]

## 8. Sino-Soviet split and Nixon in China
- Strains from 1956 (Khrushchev's secret speech; Mao defended Stalin and scorned "peaceful coexistence"; Khrushchev called Mao a "lunatic on a throne", disturbed by his attitude to nuclear war). In 1960 the USSR withdrew 1,390 technicians and ended 600 contracts (Chinese records). By 1961 China denounced Soviet "revisionist traitors"; later "social imperialism". Border fighting March 1969 (Zhenbao/Damansky Island); declassified sources indicate the USSR considered a large nuclear strike; Chinese leaders evacuated Beijing. [source: SSS; CW]
- Nixon in Beijing 21–28 February 1972 — first US presidential visit; leverage against Moscow; full diplomatic relations 1979. [source: NIX; SSS]

## 9. Space race
- Sputnik 1, 4 October 1957, Tyuratam (Baikonur), Kazakh SSR, on an R-7; radio signal for three weeks; fell 4 January 1958; caused the American "Sputnik crisis". Laika on Sputnik 2, 3 November 1957 — died of stress and overheating on the fourth orbit (revealed 2002; officially said to have died when oxygen ran out). Vanguard failed 6 December 1957; Explorer 1 launched 31 January 1958. NASA created by Act signed 29 July 1958. [source: SPK; SR]
- Gagarin, Vostok 1, 12 April 1961, a single 108-minute orbit. Kennedy's goal (25 May 1961) of a Moon landing "before this decade is out". Tereshkova, Vostok 6, 16 June 1963, first woman. Apollo 1 fire 27 January 1967. Apollo 11 landed 20 July 1969 (Armstrong, Aldrin; Collins in orbit). Apollo–Soyuz docking July 1975 (agreed April 1972). [source: SR]

## 10. Culture and propaganda
- Both superpowers ran official and covert campaigns to persuade foreign audiences their system was the future; Communist parties painted the US as culturally shallow, the US highlighted Soviet censorship. [source: CCW]
- **Congress for Cultural Freedom:** founded 1950 at a conference in West Berlin; active in 35 countries at its height, more than 20 magazines (including *Encounter*, London, 1953–90), exhibitions and conferences; aimed at the non-communist left. CIA funding exposed by *Ramparts* and the *Saturday Evening Post* in 1967 (CCF lead says former CIA agents confirmed it in 1966 — date conflict; say "exposed in 1966–67"); Thomas Braden admitted the CIA subsidised *Encounter*. Frances Stonor Saunders argues it was a covert propaganda network. [source: CCF]
- Jazz ambassadors: State Department tours by Louis Armstrong, Duke Ellington, Dizzy Gillespie — meant to project racial democracy and counter Soviet criticism of US racism; Armstrong himself criticised racial injustice. MoMA and Abstract Expressionism (Pollock) in the scholarly debate on cultural diplomacy. [source: CCW]
- Kitchen Debate: Nixon and Khrushchev, 24 July 1959, American National Exhibition, Sokolniki Park, Moscow — a model American house full of consumer goods; result of the 1958 US–Soviet cultural agreement. [source: KIT]

## 11. Détente
- Détente began 1969 under Nixon (Russian *razryadka*); SALT I/ABM 1972; trade agreements 1972–74; Brandt's *Ostpolitik*; Helsinki Final Act of the CSCE, 1975 (economic, political, human-rights "baskets"); Jackson–Vanik amendment. Unravelled over proxy conflicts in Africa and human rights; ended with Afghanistan. Soviet military spending in the 1970s: 40–60% of the federal budget and 15% of GDP (CW). Historians still debate how successful détente was. [source: DET; CW]
- Proxy conflict continued during détente: Middle East, Chile, Ethiopia, Angola. [source: CW]

## 12. The "second Cold War" (1979–1985)
- Afghanistan; Thatcher (1979), Reagan (1980); Reagan Doctrine of rolling back Soviet influence. SS-20s: 130 deployed by end-1979 with 390+ warheads; NATO's response, Pershing II, first deployed in Europe in 1983. SDI announced 1983. Reagan's "evil empire" (1983). KAL 007 shot down 1 September 1983 (269 aboard). FleetEx '83. [source: CW79; CW]
- 26 September 1983: Soviet early-warning system Oko reported one, then four more US ICBM launches; Stanislav Petrov judged it a false alarm and did not pass it up the chain; the system had malfunctioned. [source: PET]
- Able Archer 83, NATO exercise from 7 November 1983, with new realism (coded communications, heads of government); some Soviet leaders feared a disguised first strike; Soviet air units in East Germany and Poland put on alert (4th Air Army loaded nuclear weapons, per AA83); "some" consider it one of the closest moments, "other scholars have disputed this". Operation RYaN (KGB/GRU from 1981). [source: AA83]
- Diggins: "Reagan went all out to fight the second cold war, by supporting counterinsurgencies in the third world." Cox: "The intensity of this 'second' Cold War was as great as its duration was short." [source: CW]
- Ending (brief; own chapter): Gorbachev from 1985; Geneva 1985, Reykjavík 1986, INF 1987; withdrawal from Afghanistan 1989; Wall opened 9 November 1989; Warsaw Pact dissolved February 1991; USSR dissolved 26 December 1991. [source: CW; WP]

## Debates
- **Orthodox:** first US school; blames Soviet expansion into Eastern Europe (e.g. Herbert Feis, *From Trust to Terror*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historiography_of_the_Cold_War]
- **Revisionist:** William Appleman Williams, *The Tragedy of American Diplomacy* (1959), Wisconsin School: Americans an empire-building people (Open Door economic expansion); US leaders, fearing loss of markets, exaggerated the Soviet threat; Williams drew no moral distinction between Stalin in Eastern Europe and US policy in the Third World. Walter LaFeber (*America, Russia, and the Cold War*, 1972) traced roots to 19th-century rivalry in East Asia; Gar Alperovitz (*Atomic Diplomacy*, 1965) argued the bombs were used to intimidate the USSR; Joyce and Gabriel Kolko called US policy reflexively counter-revolutionary. [source: HIST; WAW]
- **Post-revisionist:** Gaddis (1972) shared responsibility; Melvyn Leffler (*A Preponderance of Power*, 1992) argued fears of socioeconomic dislocation, revolutionary nationalism, British weakness and Eurasian power vacuums, more than Kremlin actions, drove US initiatives to shape a world congenial to US security. After the Soviet archives opened, Gaddis (*We Now Know*, 1997) put more weight on Stalin and ideology and is now seen as more "orthodox"; Bruce Cumings attacked post-revisionism as moralistic. [source: HIST; GAD; LEF]
- **Global / Third World turn:** Westad: ideology drove both superpowers' interventions; Third World leaders sought them and share blame; the Cold War's most important consequences were in the South; his conclusion casts it, seen from the South, as a continuation of colonialism by other means (secondary summary). Critics (AHR review) call the central claim "more complicated and problematic" than it looks. Gleijeses shows Cuban agency in Africa; Lüthi shows the Sino-Soviet split had its own ideological and domestic Chinese logic. [source: WES; WebSearch results: https://issforum.org/roundtables/PDF/GlobalColdWar-Roundtable.pdf; https://dbpia.nl.go.kr/ahr/article/112/3/806/27688; https://origins.osu.edu/review/clashing-shades-red]
- **"Long peace" vs hot wars in the South:** Gaddis, "The Long Peace: Elements of Stability in the Postwar International System", *International Security* 10:4 (Spring 1986): bipolarity and nuclear deterrence (among other factors) produced the longest great-power stability of the century (book version 1987). Against it: CW and Kim (2014) — millions died in proxy wars, 10.4 million in East Asia alone 1945–94; Heonik Kwon (2010), following LaFeber and Cumings, argues the "long peace" was a Northern experience while postcolonial societies lived through civil war and massacre. Kagan (*Commentary*) questioned explaining restraint by "maturity" when one side sought change. [source: LP; CW; WebSearch: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/446174/summary; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/632285/pdf; https://www.commentary.org/articles/donald-kagan/the-long-peace-by-john-lewis-gaddis/]
- **Was nuclear deterrence the cause of peace?** MAD article: "nuclear peace" theorists vs critics who see proliferation raising the risk; near-misses (Cuba, Petrov, Able Archer — contested) cut against complacency. [source: MAD; PET; AA83]
- **Iran 1953:** the CIA's own history and the 2013 release confirm direction by the US; the role of Iranian clergy (Kashani), the army and Mosaddegh's own referendum is debated. [source: IRN]
- **Détente:** "an ongoing debate among historians" over how successful it was; Reagan said the Soviets had used it to further their interests. [source: DET]
