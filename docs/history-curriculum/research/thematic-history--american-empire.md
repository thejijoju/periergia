## The isolationist inheritance: Washington's Farewell Address
- Washington's Farewell Address was first published in *Claypoole's American Daily Advertiser* on 19 September 1796, about ten weeks before the presidential electors voted; it announced that he would not seek a third term. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington%27s_Farewell_Address]
- James Madison prepared a first draft in June 1792; in 1796 Washington revised it with Alexander Hamilton, who "wrote most of it" (Library of Congress, via WP); the ideas were Washington's. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington%27s_Farewell_Address]
- A large part of the address warns against permanent alliances with foreign nations, especially European ones, urging the country to use its "isolated position" to stay neutral. Context: Washington had tried to stay neutral in the war between Britain and revolutionary France, and the address also warned against parties (Hamilton's Federalists and Jefferson's Democratic-Republicans). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington%27s_Farewell_Address]
- The phrase "entangling alliances" is not Washington's: Jefferson's first inaugural (1801) spoke of "peace, commerce, and honest friendship with all nations—entangling alliances with none". [source: WebSearch summary of constitutingamerica.org / mountvernon.org, https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/past-projects/quotes/article/it-is-our-true-policy-to-steer-clear-of-permanent-alliance-with-any-portion-of-the-foreign-world]
- Washington's advice was qualified ("so far, I mean, as we are now at liberty to do it"), and he allowed "temporary alliances for extraordinary emergencies". [source: https://billofrightsinstitute.org/primary-sources/washingtons-farewell-address (via WebSearch)]
- In the early Cold War, "disregarding George Washington's dictum of avoiding entangling alliances", the US established 44 formal alliances and other relationships with nearly 100 countries (WP, citing Andrew J. Pierre, 1972). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_imperialism]

## The Monroe Doctrine (1823) and its corollaries
- President James Monroe set out the doctrine on 2 December 1823 in his seventh annual message to Congress; it was not called the "Monroe Doctrine" until 1850. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monroe_Doctrine]
- It was written chiefly by Secretary of State John Quincy Adams. Its core passage: the American continents "are henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers"; the US would stay neutral about existing European colonies but opposed "interpositions" against the newly independent Spanish American republics. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monroe_Doctrine]
- Britain had proposed a joint declaration; for many years the Royal Navy was in practice the only enforcer of the doctrine, the US Navy being small (US State Department Office of the Historian, via WP). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monroe_Doctrine]
- Latin American reaction was "generally favorable but on some occasions suspicious"; Latin Americans knew their independence depended on the British navy. In 1826 Simón Bolívar called the Congress of Panama, the first Pan-American meeting. When Britain reasserted sovereignty over the Falkland Islands in early 1833, the US did nothing, which George C. Herring says "confirmed Latin American and especially Argentine suspicions of the United States". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monroe_Doctrine]
- In the Venezuela boundary dispute, Secretary of State Richard Olney wrote to Britain on 20 July 1895: "The United States is practically sovereign on this continent, and its fiat is law upon the subjects to which it confines its interposition." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monroe_Doctrine]
- The Roosevelt Corollary grew out of the Venezuelan crisis of 1902–03 (European blockade over unpaid debts; a Hague arbitration of 22 February 1904 gave the blockading powers preferential payment). Elihu Root sketched it on 20 May 1904 and Theodore Roosevelt set it out in his annual message of 6 December 1904. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roosevelt_Corollary]
- Roosevelt Corollary text (verified wording): "Chronic wrongdoing, or an impotence which results in a general loosening of the ties of civilized society, may in America, as elsewhere, ultimately require intervention by some civilized nation, and in the Western Hemisphere the adherence of the United States to the Monroe Doctrine may force the United States, however reluctantly, in flagrant cases of such wrongdoing or impotence, to the exercise of an international police power." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roosevelt_Corollary]
- The Corollary turned a defensive warning to Europe into a claimed right of US intervention (Alan McPherson, 2016, via WP). Its first application was the Dominican Republic (1905, customs receivership). Herbert Hoover in 1930 endorsed the Clark Memorandum, which separated the Corollary from the Monroe Doctrine; the policy is dated 1904–1934. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roosevelt_Corollary]
- In 1940–41 the US invoked the Monroe Doctrine to occupy Greenland after Germany occupied Denmark (agreement of 9 April 1941 signed by Hull and the Danish ambassador Henrik Kauffmann, acting without his government's consent). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proposed_United_States_acquisition_of_Greenland]
- Secretary of State John Kerry told the Organization of American States in November 2013 that the "era of the Monroe Doctrine is over". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monroe_Doctrine]

## Continental expansion, Indigenous dispossession and the Mexican War
- "Manifest destiny": most historians credit the editor John L. O'Sullivan with coining it in 1845, though some argue that the unsigned editorial "Annexation" in which it first appeared was written by the journalist Jane Cazneau. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifest_destiny]
- Verified wording (1845): it was "our manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions" (quoted in Hietala). A later O'Sullivan column (on Oregon) claimed "the right of our manifest destiny to overspread and to possess the whole of the continent which Providence has given us". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifest_destiny]
- Democrats used the idea for the 1845 annexation of Texas as a slave state, the Oregon boundary dispute, and the Mexican War; most Whigs opposed it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifest_destiny]
- The Mexican–American War ran from 25 April 1846 to 2 February 1848. The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (negotiated by Nicholas Trist, who ignored his recall) ceded present-day California, Nevada and Utah and parts of Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico and Wyoming, and recognised the loss of Texas. Mexico lost "more than half of its territory" (Schenoni, 2024, via WP). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican%E2%80%93American_War]
- Deaths: the US lost 13,283 dead (most from disease) of 78,718 who served; Mexican deaths, including civilians killed by violence and deaths from disease, "may have reached 25,000" (Clodfelter, 2017). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican%E2%80%93American_War]
- The Comanche, Apache and Navajo raids had left Mexico's north vulnerable before the war — an Indigenous dimension to the story. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican%E2%80%93American_War]
- Opponents: the freshman congressman Abraham Lincoln challenged Polk's claim that American blood had been shed on American soil, calling it "a bold falsification of history". Foreign-born men were 47 per cent of Zachary Taylor's forces (Tyler V. Johnson). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican%E2%80%93American_War]
- Ulysses S. Grant, who fought in it, wrote in his *Personal Memoirs* (1885) that he regarded the war "as one of the most unjust ever waged by a stronger against a weaker nation. It was an instance of a republic following the bad example of European monarchies". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican%E2%80%93American_War]
- Native dispossession is told in the chapter on Native American and Indigenous Studies (Indian Removal Act, 28 May 1830; more than 60,000 people moved; the Trail of Tears; Wounded Knee 1890; allotment). Cross-refer; do not retell. [source: published chapter supabase/content/history--regional-histories--native-american-studies.sql]
- A. G. Hopkins argues that the nineteenth-century continental expansion, while imperialistic, did not create an empire, partly because the Native population was comparatively small — a position reviewers flag as a limitation. Most historians of settler colonialism disagree; present it as contested. [source: WebSearch summary of Cambridge Core and H-Diplo reviews, https://issforum.org/roundtables/PDF/Roundtable-XX-33.pdf]

## Hawaiʻi: overthrow (1893) and annexation (1898)
- Sugar drove the story: the first permanent plantation was on Kauaʻi in 1835; after the reciprocity treaty of 1875, sugar land grew from about 12,000 acres to 125,000 acres by 1891. The US began leasing Pearl Harbor on 20 January 1887. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overthrow_of_the_Hawaiian_Kingdom]
- In 1887 the "Bayonet Constitution", drafted by Lorrin Thurston and forced on King Kalākaua under threat of the militia, limited the monarch's power and disenfranchised two-thirds of Native Hawaiians through new property and income qualifications. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overthrow_of_the_Hawaiian_Kingdom]
- On 17 January 1893 Queen Liliʻuokalani (reigned from 29 January 1891) was overthrown by the Committee of Safety, a 13-member council made up mostly of foreign-born residents and Hawaiian subjects of American descent (accounts of its composition differ). The American minister John L. Stevens landed Marines and sailors from USS *Boston*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overthrow_of_the_Hawaiian_Kingdom; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lili%CA%BBuokalani]
- Liliʻuokalani's protest (verified wording): "That I yield to the superior force of the United States of America whose Minister Plenipotentiary, His Excellency John L. Stevens, has caused United States troops to be landed at Honolulu and declared that he would support the Provisional Government." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overthrow_of_the_Hawaiian_Kingdom]
- President Grover Cleveland blocked immediate annexation. James Blount's report (17 July 1893) blamed US diplomatic and military representatives; Cleveland called it a "substantial wrong" and wanted to restore the queen. The Senate's Morgan Report (1894) contradicted Blount and exonerated Stevens. The Republic of Hawaii was declared on 4 July 1894. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overthrow_of_the_Hawaiian_Kingdom]
- After a failed royalist rising in January 1895, Liliʻuokalani was arrested, convicted by a military tribunal of misprision of treason, imprisoned in her own palace, and abdicated on 24 January 1895. She wrote *Hawaii's Story by Hawaii's Queen* (1898) and composed "Aloha ʻOe"; she died on 11 November 1917. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overthrow_of_the_Hawaiian_Kingdom; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lili%CA%BBuokalani]
- Kūʻē petitions: between 11 September and 2 October 1897 the Hui Aloha ʻĀina and Hui Kālaiʻāina gathered 21,269 signatures against annexation, "more than half of the 39,000 native Hawaiians" (WP, citing the National Archives). Noenoe K. Silva rediscovered the petitions while researching *Aloha Betrayed*. Senator George Hoar read the petition text in the Senate on 9 December 1897 (one review). Some sources speak of 95 per cent of Native Hawaiians opposing annexation — use "more than half of Native Hawaiians signed" as the safe figure. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newlands_Resolution; WebSearch on Silva, https://www.hawaii.edu/malamalama/2011/10/aloha-betrayed/]
- McKinley's 1897 annexation treaty lacked two-thirds support in the Senate. During the war with Spain, Hawaiʻi was annexed instead by a joint resolution (needing only simple majorities), the Newlands Resolution, which passed the House 209–91 and the Senate 42–21 and was signed on 7 July 1898. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newlands_Resolution]
- At the annexation ceremony at ʻIolani Palace on 12 August 1898 almost no Native Hawaiians attended; those on the streets wore royalist ʻilima blossoms. The Territory of Hawaii was created in 1900. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overthrow_of_the_Hawaiian_Kingdom; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newlands_Resolution]
- Statehood: the Hawaii Admission Act was signed 18 March 1959; a plebiscite of 27 June 1959 showed at least 93 per cent approval on every major island; statehood took effect on 21 August 1959 (50th state). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_Admission_Act]
- The Apology Resolution (Public Law 103-150, signed 23 November 1993) acknowledges that the overthrow "occurred with the active participation of agents and citizens of the United States" and that Native Hawaiians "never directly relinquished" their claims to sovereignty. The Hawaiian sovereignty movement still disputes the legality of annexation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apology_Resolution; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newlands_Resolution]

## 1898: Cuba, Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines
- Cuba's War of Independence began in 1895 under José Martí (killed in 1895); Spain's Valeriano Weyler ("the Butcher") imposed the reconcentration policy. In 1894, 90 per cent of Cuba's exports went to the US (Louis A. Pérez Jr.). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93American_War]
- USS *Maine* exploded in Havana harbour on 15 February 1898 (33 African American seamen were among the dead). The Navy's 1898 inquiry blamed an external explosion but named no culprit; the cause remains disputed. The Hearst and Pulitzer papers blamed Spain ("Remember the Maine, To Hell with Spain!"), though historians no longer think "yellow journalism" was the main force shaping opinion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93American_War]
- The Teller Amendment (20 April 1898) pledged that the US would not take sovereignty over Cuba. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teller_Amendment]
- The war ran from 21 April (blockade; war declared 25 April, backdated) to 13 August 1898. On 1 May Commodore George Dewey destroyed the Spanish fleet at Manila Bay; El Caney and San Juan Hill were fought on 1 July; Santiago de Cuba fell on 17 July; US troops landed in Puerto Rico on 25 July. Cuban insurgent forces under Calixto García had been fighting since 1895 (about 53,000 rebels over 1895–98, Clodfelter). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93American_War]
- US deaths: 385 killed in action and 2,061 dead of disease (Clodfelter) — disease killed far more Americans than Spanish bullets. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93American_War]
- Louis A. Pérez Jr. argues that US accounts long wrote Cubans out of the "Spanish–American War" and prefers "the War of 1898"; one minority view (Pérez) holds that McKinley intervened because he feared the Cubans would win on their own. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93American_War]
- Treaty of Paris, signed 10 December 1898: Spain gave up Cuba and ceded Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines; the US paid $20 million for the Philippines. Felipe Agoncillo, representing the Philippine Republic, was denied any part in the talks. The Senate approved it on 6 February 1899 by 57 to 27, just over two-thirds; Andrew Carnegie, Grover Cleveland, Mark Twain and Samuel Gompers were among the anti-imperialists who opposed it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_(1898)]
- Cuba: the Platt Amendment (signed 2 March 1901) was written word for word into Cuba's 1901 constitution (approved by the Cuban assembly 12 June 1901) and the 1903 Treaty of Relations; it gave the US a right to intervene and naval-base leases. Cuba became formally independent on 20 May 1902; the US occupied it again 1906–09. The 1934 treaty dropped the intervention clauses. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platt_Amendment; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Neighbor_policy]
- Guantánamo Bay naval base dates from 1903 (the oldest US overseas naval base); the lease was $2,000 a year in gold until 1934, then $4,085; since 1959 Cuba's government has protested the base as imposed by force and illegal; the lease ends only by mutual consent or US withdrawal; a detention camp has operated there since 2002. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guantanamo_Bay_Naval_Base]
- Guam: Chamorro people settled the Marianas about 3,500 years ago; Magellan arrived 6 March 1521; Spain colonised Guam from 1668; the US captured it in 1898. Japan seized it on 8 December 1941 and held it for two and a half years until US forces retook it on 21 July 1944 ("Liberation Day"). The Guam Organic Act of 1950 granted US citizenship. The Defense Department owns about 29 per cent of the island (Andersen Air Force Base, Naval Base Guam). Population 170,476 (September 2026); Chamorros are the largest group but a minority. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guam; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territories_of_the_United_States]

## The Philippine–American War (1899–1902, and after)
- Filipinos proclaimed independence on 12 June 1898 and founded a republic under Emilio Aguinaldo (the published Southeast Asia chapter covers the Katipunan revolution of 1896). [source: published chapter history--regional-histories--modern-southeast-asia.sql; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War]
- McKinley's proclamation of 21 December 1898 promised "benevolent assimilation, substituting the mild sway of justice and right for arbitrary rule". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War]
- War began on 4 February 1899; Filipino forces turned to guerrilla war by November 1899; Aguinaldo was captured on 23 March 1901; the US declared the war over on 4 July 1902 (amnesty proclamation), but resistance continued — Macario Sakay's Tagalog Republic until 1906, and the Moro wars until the battle of Bud Bagsak on 15 June 1913. At Bud Dajo (March 1906) up to 900 people, including women and children, were killed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War]
- The Schurman Commission declared that "there being no Philippine nation, but only a collection of different peoples", Filipinos were "wholly unprepared for independence". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War]
- Deaths (attribute every figure): US — 1,020 killed in action and 3,176 dead of disease (Oxford Companion), "over 4,200" per the State Department; Filipino combatants — 16,000–20,000 killed (American estimate), about 10,000 (Aguinaldo's estimate), "over 20,000" (State Department); civilians — modern historians generally put 200,000–250,000, most from famine and disease (WP; Ramsey, Clodfelter); a cholera epidemic at the war's end killed 150,000–200,000 (David Silbey); some estimates reach 1 million; Luzviminda Francisco cites a reported remark by General J. Franklin Bell of "over 600,000" dead in Luzon; Andrea Pitzer says over 11,000 died in concentration camps; Glenn May's study of Batangas stresses crop failure, cholera and malaria. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War]
- After a surprise attack at Balangiga, Samar (September 1901) killed "almost fifty" US soldiers (WP; see Conflicts), General Jacob H. Smith ordered his troops to "kill everyone over the age of ten" and make Samar a "howling wilderness"; Major Littleton Waller countermanded it. Bell's campaign in Batangas and Laguna concentrated civilians in zones. The "water cure" was widely used in interrogation; Roosevelt privately called it "an old Filipino method of mild torture". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War]
- Paul Kramer (*The Blood of Government*, 2006) noted the resemblance between US policies in the Philippines and European colonial practice in Asia and Africa. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_imperialism]
- The Tydings–McDuffie Act (24 March 1934) set a ten-year transition to independence and created the Commonwealth of the Philippines; it also reclassified Filipinos in the US as aliens and set an immigration quota of 50 a year. Independence came on 4 July 1946. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tydings%E2%80%93McDuffie_Act; published Southeast Asia chapter]

## The Insular Cases and the territories today
- The Insular Cases are Supreme Court decisions of 1901 on the territories taken from Spain; strictly, six cases (Judge Juan Torruella). *Downes v. Bidwell* (decided 27 May 1901) created the distinction between "incorporated" territories (on a path to statehood, Constitution fully applies) and "unincorporated" ones (Constitution applies only in part). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insular_Cases]
- *Downes* called the inhabitants "alien races" and said "the administration of government and justice according to Anglo-Saxon principles may for a time be impossible"; *DeLima v. Bidwell* termed them "savage tribes". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insular_Cases]
- In *United States v. Vaello Madero* (21 April 2022) Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote that "The Insular Cases have no foundation in the Constitution and rest instead on racial stereotypes. They deserve no place in our law," calling them "shameful". In October 2022 the Court declined (denied certiorari) to hear *Fitisemanu v. United States*, leaving them in place. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insular_Cases; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitisemanu_v._United_States]
- Puerto Rico: civil government under the Foraker Act (1900); US citizenship by the Jones–Shafroth Act (1917); a constitution and the "Estado Libre Asociado" / "Commonwealth" from 25 July 1952 (the name suggested by Luis Muñoz Marín). The State Department's manual says "Commonwealth" "does not describe or provide for any specific political status". Residents cannot vote for president and have only a non-voting resident commissioner in Congress. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rico; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territories_of_the_United_States]
- Repression of the independence movement: the 1948 "Gag Law" (Ley de la Mordaza); the Ponce massacre, Palm Sunday 21 March 1937, when police under Governor Blanton Winship fired on a Nationalist march, killing 17 civilians plus one policeman and one National Guardsman (friendly fire); the march also protested the imprisonment of Pedro Albizu Campos; the 1950 uprisings and the 1954 shooting in the House of Representatives. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponce_massacre; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rico]
- Status votes: 2012 (61.16% statehood in the second question, a result WP flags as failed verification), 2017 (statehood, low turnout), 2020 (about 52% yes to immediate statehood), November 2024 (statehood again); the Puerto Rico Status Act passed the House in 2022 but not the Senate. A 2016 law (PROMESA) created a federal financial oversight board. Hurricanes Irma and Maria struck in 2017. Population 3,285,874 (2020 census). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rico]
- Vieques: in 1941 the Navy bought or seized almost 80 per cent of the island for bombing and training; after the civilian guard David Sanes was killed by a stray bomb in 1999, a civil-disobedience campaign forced the Navy out in May 2003; the island is now a Superfund site. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vieques,_Puerto_Rico]
- American Samoa: partitioned by the Tripartite Convention (2 December 1899); deeds of cession 1900 and 1904; ratified by Congress 1929. People born there are US nationals, not citizens — the only territory where birth does not confer citizenship. Samoan *matai* (chiefs) blocked an Organic Act in 1949 for fear that incorporation would undermine *faʻa Samoa* and communal land, and many Samoan leaders still prefer the status quo, so this is not simply imposed from outside. It has the highest military enlistment rate of any US state or territory. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Samoa; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territories_of_the_United_States]
- Other citizenship dates: US Virgin Islands bought from Denmark in 1917 (citizenship 1927); Guam 1950. The five inhabited territories had 3,569,284 people in 2020 (down from 4,100,594 in 2010). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territories_of_the_United_States]
- Daniel Immerwahr (*The Guardian*): maps of the contiguous US "give [mainlanders] a truncated view of their own history, one that excludes part of their country." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territories_of_the_United_States]

## Panama and the Canal Zone
- Panama broke from Colombia in November 1903 with US naval protection after the Colombian Senate balked at canal terms. The Frenchman Philippe Bunau-Varilla, acting as Panama's representative before actual Panamanians could arrive, signed the Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty (18 November 1903), granting the US "in perpetuity the use, occupation, and control" of a zone about five miles either side of the canal; he warned that without agreement the US would withdraw its protection. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panama_Canal_Zone]
- The canal opened to commercial traffic in August 1914. Workers were paid on a "gold roll" (almost all white Americans) and a "silver roll" (mostly Black Caribbean workers, often from Barbados and Jamaica); in December 1906 the commission counted 3,700 gold-roll and 13,000 silver-roll employees, and race became the deciding factor. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panama_Canal_Zone]
- Martyrs' Day: protests over flying Panama's flag in the Zone began on 9 January 1964; after three days about 22 Panamanians and four US soldiers were dead. It is seen as a major reason for the handover. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martyrs%27_Day_(Panama)]
- The Torrijos–Carter Treaties, signed 7 September 1977, abolished the Zone on 1 October 1979 and transferred the canal to Panama at noon on 31 December 1999; under the Neutrality Treaty the US kept a permanent right to defend the canal's neutrality. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torrijos%E2%80%93Carter_Treaties]
- Operation Just Cause (from 20 December 1989) deposed Manuel Noriega. The Pentagon estimated 516 Panamanians killed (314 soldiers, 202 civilians); the UN cited 500 civilian dead; a Panamanian human-rights group gave 2,000–3,000 (cited by Chomsky); 23 US soldiers died. The UN General Assembly, the OAS and the European Parliament condemned it as a violation of international law. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_invasion_of_Panama]

## The Banana Wars and occupations (1898–1934)
- "Banana Wars" covers US occupations, police actions and interventions in Central America and the Caribbean from 1898 to 1934 — in Cuba, Panama, Honduras, Nicaragua, Mexico (Veracruz), Haiti and the Dominican Republic. The term was popularised by Lester D. Langley's *The Banana Wars* (1983). WP gives 402 US Marines dead in 1915–34 (Hans Schmidt). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banana_Wars]
- Haiti (28 July 1915 – 1 August 1934; last Marines left 15 August 1934): 330 Marines landed at Port-au-Prince after President Vilbrun Guillaume Sam was lynched; WP says the National City Bank of New York had pressed Wilson to act. On 17 December 1914 eight Marines had already removed about $500,000 of Haiti's gold reserves. The occupation ended Haiti's constitutional ban (dating from independence) on foreign ownership of land, and Haiti had no legislature until 1929. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_occupation_of_Haiti]
- Haitian resistance: the *caco* wars, led by Charlemagne Péralte (killed) and Benoît Batraville; the occupation used a corvée of forced labour that caused "hundreds to thousands" of deaths. Haitian deaths over the occupation: 3,250–15,000 (Schmidt and others). At Les Cayes on 6 December 1929 Marines fired on about 1,500 protesters, killing 12–22 and wounding 51. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_occupation_of_Haiti]
- Dominican Republic (15 May 1916 – 18 September 1924): aimed to force repayment of European debts; peasant guerrillas called *gavilleros* resisted in the eastern sugar provinces (El Seibo, San Pedro de Macorís); Rafael Trujillo rose through the US-created National Guard. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Government_of_Santo_Domingo]
- Nicaragua (4 August 1912 – 2 January 1933, with a break in 1925–26): from 1927 Augusto César Sandino's army fought the Marines; Neill Macaulay counts 136 Marines dead in the second occupation and 1,115 Sandinista insurgents killed. Sandino was executed on 21 February 1934 by National Guard forces on the orders of Anastasio Somoza García, who seized power in 1936 and founded a dynasty that ruled for over 40 years; the Sandinista National Liberation Front took his name and overthrew the Somozas in 1979. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_occupation_of_Nicaragua; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augusto_C%C3%A9sar_Sandino]
- Smedley Butler, the most-decorated Marine of his era, in a 1933 speech (verified wording): "I was a racketeer, a gangster for capitalism. … I helped make Mexico, especially Tampico, safe for American oil interests in 1914. I helped make Haiti and Cuba a decent place for the National City Bank boys to collect revenues in. I helped in the raping of half a dozen Central American republics for the benefits of Wall Street." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_imperialism]
- Good Neighbor policy: Hoover began withdrawing; Franklin Roosevelt said in his inaugural address of 4 March 1933, "I would dedicate this nation to the policy of the good neighbor". It brought the Montevideo Convention (1933), the 1934 treaty with Cuba annulling the Platt Amendment, withdrawal from Haiti in 1934, and negotiated compensation after Mexico nationalised foreign oil in 1938. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Neighbor_policy]

## Wilson, the League and the "Wilsonian moment"
- Wilson set out the Fourteen Points on 8 January 1918; they did not use the word "self-determination", but anti-colonial nationalists read them that way. Erez Manela calls the resulting hopes the "Wilsonian moment"; Wilson had no intention of supporting self-determination that conflicted with the victorious Allies' interests. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourteen_Points]
- At Paris on 11 April 1919 Japan's racial equality proposal won 11 of 17 votes in the League commission with no votes against; Wilson, as chairman, ruled that it needed unanimity. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_Equality_Proposal]
- On 19 November 1919 the Senate rejected the Versailles treaty with Lodge's reservations, 39 for and 55 against (two-thirds needed); the US never joined the League. Historians point to the feud between Wilson and Henry Cabot Lodge, Wilson's failing health, and long-standing ambivalence about foreign commitments. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lodge_Reservations; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_and_the_League_of_Nations]

## The Second World War and the liberal order
- Henry Luce's editorial "The American Century", *Life*, 17 February 1941 (verified wording): Americans were called "to create the first great American Century" and "to exert upon the world the full impact of our influence, for such purposes as we see fit and by such means as we see fit". David Harvey notes Luce preferred "American century" to "empire". (Already in the Modern America chapter — cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Century]
- Bretton Woods conference: 730 delegates from 44 Allied nations, 1–22 July 1944, at the Mount Washington Hotel, New Hampshire; it created the IMF and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development; Keynes (Britain) and Harry Dexter White (US Treasury) had drawn up rival plans. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bretton_Woods_Conference]
- Marshall Plan: proposed in Marshall's Harvard address, June 1947; signed by Truman 3 April 1948; $13.3 billion to 17 European countries over four years (other counts differ); the USSR refused and kept Eastern Europe out. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Plan]
- NATO: North Atlantic Treaty signed 4 April 1949 by 12 states (the five Western Union states plus the US, Canada, Portugal, Italy, Norway, Denmark and Iceland); 32 members today; Article 5 has been invoked only once, after 11 September 2001. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO]
- Geir Lundestad (1986), on Western Europe 1945–52 (verified wording): "If this American expansion created what we could call an American empire, this was to a large extent an empire by invitation." He also showed that the US interfered in Italian and French politics, helping to push the Communists out of Italy's government in May 1947. Lundestad (1945–2023) later directed the Norwegian Nobel Institute. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_imperialism; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geir_Lundestad]
- Okinawa: the Battle of Okinawa (1945) killed 40,000–150,000 civilians, depending on the estimate (Masayasu Oshiro puts total Okinawan dead at about 150,000). The US ruled Okinawa until its reversion to Japan in 1972 (up to 1,200 nuclear weapons may have been stored at Kadena in the 1960s — speculated, never confirmed); the Koza riot of 1970 targeted the US presence. Okinawa is 0.6 per cent of Japan's land but holds about 62 per cent of exclusive-use US bases in Japan (USFJ article; often cited as 70 per cent). US Forces Japan: about 55,000 personnel, 15 major bases. In a 2014 *Ryūkyū Shimpō* poll, 80 per cent wanted Futenma moved out of the prefecture. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Okinawa; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Forces_Japan]
- Marshall Islands: the US held 105 atmospheric and underwater tests at its Pacific Proving Grounds, 1946–62; the Marshall Islands tests were 14 per cent of US tests but nearly 80 per cent of total yield (about 210 megatons); Castle Bravo (1954, 15 Mt) spread fallout over inhabited islands. In February 1946 the 167 people of Bikini were moved ("we will go believing that everything is in the hands of God", Chief Juda) and were starving on Rongerik by 1947. The 23 crew of the Japanese boat *Daigo Fukuryū Maru* were irradiated. At least $759 million has been paid to Marshall Islanders. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Proving_Grounds; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_testing_at_Bikini_Atoll]

## Containment, covert action and the Cold War wars
(These are already told in full in the Global Cold War, Latin America in the Cold War, Vietnam War and Modern Middle East chapters. Summarise and cross-refer.)
- Korea: war from 25 June 1950 to the armistice of 27 July 1953; about one million military deaths and an estimated 1.5–3 million civilian deaths; US 36,515 dead or missing (WP infobox); North Korea became one of the most heavily bombed countries in history. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_War]
- Iran: coup of 15–19 August 1953 (CIA's TP-AJAX, MI6's Operation Boot) overthrew Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh after his 1951 oil nationalisation; 200–300 killed; in August 2013 the US formally acknowledged its role by releasing documents. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1953_Iranian_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat]
- Guatemala: Operation PBSuccess (authorised August 1953) trained a 480-man force under Carlos Castillo Armas; Jacobo Árbenz resigned on 27 June 1954; the CIA's later search (PBHistory) found no evidence of Soviet control; executions after the coup ran from hundreds to 5,000 (Grandin); the civil war of 1960–96 followed, including a genocide of Maya peoples. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1954_Guatemalan_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat]
- Congo: the Congo Crisis (1960–65) killed around 100,000 people; the US and USSR backed opposing factions; it ended with Mobutu's rule (second coup November 1965). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_Crisis]
- Chile: coup of 11 September 1973; an estimated 40,000 Chileans were tortured under Pinochet; declassified material (including 2023 releases) shows Nixon and Kissinger knew of the military's plans; Kissinger told Nixon, "In the Eisenhower period, we would be heroes," and Nixon replied, "our hand doesn't show on this one." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_Chilean_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat]
- Vietnam: 58,281 US dead (Vietnam Veterans Memorial Fund; the National Archives counts 58,220); Vietnam's government in April 1995 gave 1.1 million PAVN/VC dead and 2.0 million Vietnamese civilian dead on both sides for 1955–75; other estimates range from about 1.35 million (Guenter Lewy, 1965–74) to 3.8 million (a 2008 *BMJ* study, 1955–2002). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War_casualties]

## The unipolar moment and the War on Terror
- Joseph Nye coined "soft power" in *Bound to Lead: The Changing Nature of American Power* (1990): "when one country gets other countries to want what it wants might be called co-optive or soft power in contrast with the hard or command power of ordering others to do what it wants". Developed in *Soft Power: The Means to Success in World Politics* (2004). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_power]
- Unipolarity: Krauthammer's "The Unipolar Moment" (1990/91) is in the Post-Cold War chapter; William Wohlforth: "a unipolar system is one in which a counterbalance is impossible. When a counterbalance becomes possible, the system is not unipolar"; WP notes a unipolar state is not the same as an empire. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polarity_(international_relations)]
- Iraq War: 20 March 2003 – 18 December 2011; justified by false claims of weapons of mass destruction and links to al-Qaeda; no stockpiles were found; UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan called the invasion illegal under the UN Charter. US deaths 4,508 (DoD/DMDC list 4,506); estimates of Iraqi deaths range from 151,000 violent deaths to June 2006 (Iraq Family Health Survey) to 1,033,000 (ORB poll, 2007); Iraq Body Count recorded 103,160–113,728 civilian deaths from violence to December 2011. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_War; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casualties_of_the_Iraq_War]
- Donald Rumsfeld, 28 April 2003: "We don't seek empires. We're not imperialistic. We never have been." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_imperialism]
- War on Terror: the Costs of War Project (Watson Institute, Brown) estimates 906,000–937,000 direct deaths and 4.5–4.7 million total deaths (with 3.6–3.8 million indirect, calculated with the Geneva Declaration's ratio of four indirect deaths per direct death) across Afghanistan, Iraq, Libya, Pakistan, Somalia, Syria and Yemen; 38 million displaced; over $8 trillion in costs. Note the method: the indirect figure is a multiplier, not a count. Practices condemned included drone strikes, torture, extraordinary rendition and black sites. The term was officially dropped for "Overseas Contingency Operations" in 2009. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_on_terror]
- Africa: US Africa Command was established 1 October 2007 and activated 1 October 2008, headquartered in Stuttgart, Germany (no African state agreed to host it — not verified, do not use). Camp Lemonnier in Djibouti (leased from Djibouti's government, in use since 2002) is the largest US base in Africa. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Africa_Command; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_Lemonnier]
- Diego Garcia: Britain detached the Chagos Archipelago from Mauritius in 1965 and made it available to the US under a 1966 exchange of notes; Diego Garcia's people were removed in 1971 and the rest of the archipelago emptied by 1973; a joint US–UK base (about 2,000 personnel and contractors) replaced them; Britain admitted two US rendition flights used it in 2002. A UK–Mauritius treaty of 22 May 2025 (not in force as of November 2026) would recognise Mauritian sovereignty with a 99-year arrangement for the base. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diego_Garcia]

## Bases abroad
- David Vine's count (*Base Nation*, 2015) was about 800 bases abroad, including 174 in Germany, 113 in Japan and 83 in South Korea. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_imperialism]
- Vine, Patterson Deppen and Leah Bolger (Quincy Institute brief, September 2021): about 750 bases in 80 foreign countries and colonies (territories) after the Afghan withdrawal — nearly three times the number of US embassies, consulates and missions (276). Critics dispute what counts as a "base"; give it as an estimate by these authors. [source: https://quincyinst.org/report/drawdown-improving-u-s-and-global-security-through-military-base-closures-abroad/ (via WebSearch)]
- Chalmers Johnson argued in 2004 that the military base was America's version of the colony ("America's Empire of Bases"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_imperialism]

## Recent shifts, 2025–26 (date and attribute; this is "too recent for history's verdict", as the Post-Cold War chapter says)
- "Donroe Doctrine": the term appeared on a *New York Post* cover of 8 January 2025 and spread in autumn 2025; the administration's National Security Strategy (November 2025) explicitly asserts a "Trump Corollary" to the Monroe Doctrine. An executive order of 20 January 2025 renamed the Gulf of Mexico the "Gulf of America" in federal usage. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donroe_Doctrine; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_expansionism_under_Donald_Trump]
- Panama: in December 2024 Trump demanded the canal back; on 7 January 2025 he would not rule out military force; on 2 February 2025 he vowed to "take back" the canal, citing Chinese influence (Hong Kong's CK Hutchison ran two of its five ports; a BlackRock-led consortium agreed on 5 March 2025 to buy its port holdings). On 9–10 April 2025 a US–Panama deal allowed US troop deployments with Panama's consent. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_expansionism_under_Donald_Trump]
- Venezuela: on 3 January 2026 (Operation Absolute Resolve) US forces bombed targets in northern Venezuela and captured President Nicolás Maduro and Cilia Flores, taking them to New York for trial on drug charges; about 80 people died. Delcy Rodríguez was sworn in as acting president on 5 January; the State Department under Marco Rubio has since exercised control over core aspects of Venezuela's governance, including finances and oil. Trump spoke of "the Donroe Doctrine" and said "American dominance in the Western Hemisphere will never be questioned again". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_States_intervention_in_Venezuela; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donroe_Doctrine]
- Greenland: (fully told in the Cryo-History chapter — cross-refer) the US considered buying it in 1867 and 1910 and secretly offered to in 1946; a 1951 treaty gave the US a role in its defence (Pituffik, formerly Thule, is the remaining base). Trump's 2019 proposal was called "absurd" by Mette Frederiksen. In January 2026 he threatened 10 per cent tariffs (rising to 25 per cent) on eight European countries; at Davos on 21 January 2026 he ruled out force, then dropped the tariffs after talks with Mark Rutte. An agreement was reached on 18 September and signed on 22 September 2026 at the UN, expanding the US military footprint while affirming Danish sovereignty and Greenlandic self-determination. A poll found 85 per cent of Greenlanders opposed a US takeover. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland_crisis; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proposed_United_States_acquisition_of_Greenland]
- Canada: from January 2025 Trump tied tariffs to calls for Canada to become the "51st state". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_expansionism_under_Donald_Trump]
- Iran war: since 28 February 2026 the US and Israel have fought Iran; the opening strikes killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei; the UN Secretary-General and many states condemned the strikes as illegal; ceasefires followed in April, and a memorandum to end the war was signed on 17 June 2026. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Iran_war]
- Tariffs: the 2025 "Liberation Day" tariffs and the Supreme Court ruling of 20 February 2026 are in the Globalization and Neoliberal Age chapters — cross-refer, do not repeat.

## Debates
- **Is "empire" the right word?** William Appleman Williams, *The Tragedy of American Diplomacy* (1959; expanded 2nd edition 1962), founder of the "Wisconsin School", made the Open Door Notes (to China, c. 1899) central and called the Open Door "America's version of the liberal policy of informal empire or free trade imperialism"; he later wrote *Empire as a Way of Life* (1980). (The Global Cold War chapter introduces him — cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Appleman_Williams]
- **Empire by invitation:** Lundestad (1986) argued that Western Europeans largely invited American power after 1945, while showing that the US also intervened in Italian and French politics; critics note that "invitation" came from some elites and not others. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_imperialism]
- **Hidden empire:** Immerwahr argues that the US has always been an empire, has usually hidden it, and after 1945 swapped colonies for a "pointillist" network of bases, using technology (synthetic rubber and plastics — the wartime government built 51 synthetic-rubber plants to Germany's three) to choose "globalisation rather than colonisation". [source: WebSearch summary of reviews (Routes 2021; Review of International American Studies), https://journals.us.edu.pl/index.php/RIAS/article/download/10281/8852]
- **The "imperial turn":** Paul Kramer, "Power and Connection: Imperial Histories of the United States in the World", *American Historical Review* 116:5 (December 2011), argues that the "imperial" is a useful frame for three lines of inquiry: how power works through long-distance connections, how societies are unevenly transformed by them, and comparison between large systems of power; it confronts "American exceptionalism". Harvey Neptune's 2024 "post-colonial response" revisits it. [source: https://www.paulkrameronline.com/power-and-connection-imperial-histories-of-the-united-states-in-the-world/ (via WebSearch)]
- **Empire, embraced:** Max Boot (*Weekly Standard*, 15 October 2001, "The Case for American Empire") urged the US to accept the role; Niall Ferguson wrote that "the US is an empire and … this might not be wholly bad", likening it more to Rome than Britain. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_imperialism]
- **Hegemony, not empire:** liberal internationalists (G. John Ikenberry and Michael Mandelbaum, "The Case for Goliath", *Foreign Affairs* 2006) see the US acting as a kind of world government that gained influence without seeking it; A. G. Hopkins (2018) argues the US stopped being an empire once its insular territories were decolonised, and that after 1945 even its hegemony was limited. Critics (Chalmers Johnson, Vine) answer that bases, interventions and unincorporated territories make "empire" accurate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_imperialism; WebSearch on Hopkins reviews]
- **The territories themselves:** Puerto Rico's status votes, Gorsuch's call to overrule the Insular Cases, and American Samoan leaders' preference for keeping national status to protect communal land show that "decolonisation" means different things to different peoples. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitisemanu_v._United_States; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Samoa]
- **Who acted?** Pérez (Cuba), Silva (Hawaiʻi), and Latin American historians stress local agency — Cuban insurgents, Kūʻē petitioners, Sandino, the *gavilleros*, Panama's 1964 students — against older accounts centred on Washington. Grandin is criticised by some reviewers (e.g. *A Contracorriente*) for making Latin America a place "acted upon". [source: WebSearch on Grandin reviews, https://acontracorriente.chass.ncsu.edu/index.php/acontracorriente/article/download/282/409/1070]
