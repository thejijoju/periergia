## Before the modern state system: early treaties and "peace" in other traditions
- The Egyptian–Hittite peace treaty (also called the "Eternal Treaty" or "Silver Treaty") was concluded around 1259 BCE between Ramesses II of Egypt and Ḫattušili III of the Hittites. It is the oldest known *surviving* peace treaty (the much older Ebla–Abarsal treaty may be the earliest recorded diplomatic treaty), and the only ancient Near Eastern treaty that survives in versions from both parties. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian–Hittite_peace_treaty]
- The text does not mention the Battle of Kadesh (c. 1274 BCE), though it is sometimes called the "Treaty of Kadesh"; the war continued inconclusively for about fifteen years after the battle. [source: same]
- The Egyptian version is carved in hieroglyphs at Karnak and the Ramesseum; the Hittite version, in Akkadian (the diplomatic lingua franca), was found on clay tablets at Hattusa by Hugo Winckler in 1906. Both copied now-lost silver tablets. A copy hangs at UN Headquarters in New York. [source: same]
- Terms: mutual non-aggression, a mutual defensive alliance, extradition of political refugees, and (Hittite version) Egyptian support for Ḫattušili's successors; the gods of both lands were called as witnesses. Scholars (Bryce) stress that the treaty was more about alliance (against rising Assyria, and to shore up Ḫattušili's legitimacy) than about "peace for its own sake". [source: same]
- Ashoka and Kalinga: the war ended c. 261 BCE, in the eighth year after Ashoka's coronation. In Rock Edict XIII he records 150,000 deported, 100,000 killed and many more dead, and "deep remorse". (Figures are Ashoka's own.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalinga_War]
- Mozi (c. 470 – c. 391 BCE), Chinese philosopher of the Warring States era, preached "impartial caring" or "universal love" (*jian ai*) against the Confucian ethic of graded attachment; Mohism is known for opposing offensive war. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozi] (The "against offensive war" doctrine is well known but the fetched page's grep did not return that phrase; treat as general knowledge to confirm if used in detail.)
- Pax Romana is conventionally dated 27 BCE (Augustus) to 180 CE (death of Marcus Aurelius). Walter Goffart: the *Cambridge Ancient History* volume for 70–192 CE "is called 'The Imperial Peace', but peace is not what one finds in its pages". Arthur Eckstein counters that it must be compared with the much more frequent wars of the Republic. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Romana]
- Tacitus puts in the mouth of the Caledonian chief Calgacus (*Agricola*): "Auferre trucidare rapere falsis nominibus imperium, atque ubi solitudinem faciunt, pacem appellant" — one translation: "To ravage, to slaughter, to usurp under false titles, they call empire; and where they make a desert, they call it peace." (Translations vary; the Latin is the safe form.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace]
- Pax Mongolica (also "Pax Tatarica") is a historians' term, modelled on Pax Romana, for the eased trade and communication across Eurasia under Mongol rule in the 13th–14th centuries, after "nearly a century of conquest and civil war"; it ended with the break-up of the khanates and the Black Death in the mid-14th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Mongolica]
- The Peace of God was first proclaimed at the Council of Charroux in 989, protecting church property, agricultural resources and unarmed clerics (later peasants and other non-combatants). The Truce of God, first proclaimed at the Council of Toulouges in 1027, restricted fighting by days of the week and seasons; a Norman version banned private war from Wednesday evening to Monday morning; Pope Urban II proclaimed the Truce in 1095 (the year of the First Crusade's launch at Clermont). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_and_Truce_of_God]
- The Great Law of Peace (Gayanashagowa; Mohawk Kaianereʼkó꞉wa) is the oral constitution of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy of Mohawk, Onondaga, Oneida, Cayuga and Seneca, credited to the Peacemaker (Dekanawidah) and his spokesman Hiawatha; it is recorded with wampum belts, has 117 articles in its written English form, and is symbolised by the Tree of Peace (eastern white pine). The Tuscarora joined in 1722. Founding date disputed: the Wikipedia article gives "circa 1102" and "c. 1190"; other scholarship 1142 (eclipse) or c. 1450–1600. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Law_of_Peace]
- Influence debate: Johansen and Grinde argued the Haudenosaunee example inspired framers of the US Constitution; Congress passed Concurrent Resolution 331 (21 October 1988) recognising that influence; the thesis is contested (e.g. eagle-and-arrows imagery is common in European heraldry). [source: same]
- East Asian "tribute system": the term was a modern Western construct (John King Fairbank and Teng Ssu-yu, early 1940s; Fairbank's *The Chinese World Order*, 1968). Critics say it "turned a flexible practice with multiple meanings into an overly formalized ritual system". David Kang et al. (2019) argue that Vietnamese rulers, inside the hierarchy, paid little military attention to China (Vietnam–China relations 1365–1841). Chinese state discourse now draws on "harmony" readings of the tributary past. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tributary_system_of_China]

## Westphalia (1648) and its myth
- The Peace of Westphalia was two treaties signed in October 1648 at Osnabrück and Münster (Treaty of Münster with France: 24 October 1648), ending the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648), which killed between 4.5 and 8 million people. A separate Peace of Münster (30 January 1648) between Spain and the Dutch Republic ended the Eighty Years' War. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_of_Westphalia]
- 109 delegations took part (16 European states, 66 Imperial States representing 140, and 27 interest groups) but never met in plenary; they arrived 1643–46 and left 1647–49, with most present January 1646 to July 1647. [source: same]
- Political scientists long treated 1648 as the origin of the modern system of sovereign states (Leo Gross, 1948). Andreas Osiander ("Sovereignty, International Relations, and the Westphalian Myth", *International Organization* 55:2, 2001) writes that "the treaties confirm neither [France's or Sweden's] 'sovereignty' nor anybody else's; least of all do they contain anything about sovereignty as a principle." Others (Hall Gardner, Stéphane Beaulac) call Westphalian sovereignty substantially a myth; the treaties gave France and Sweden rights to intervene in the Empire; post-colonial scholars note its limited relevance to non-Western state systems. Daniel Philpott (2001) defends a stronger reading. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westphalian_system]

## Kant and the peace movements of the nineteenth century
- Immanuel Kant, *Zum ewigen Frieden. Ein philosophischer Entwurf* (*Perpetual Peace: A Philosophical Sketch*), Königsberg, 1795: "Preliminary Articles" for immediate steps and "Definitive Articles"; later associated with democratic-peace, commercial-peace and institutional-peace theories. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpetual_Peace:_A_Philosophical_Sketch]
- First formal peace societies: New York Peace Society (1815, David Low Dodge), Massachusetts Peace Society, later merged into the American Peace Society; the London Peace Society (Society for the Promotion of Permanent and Universal Peace) founded 1816 by the Quaker William Allen. In the 1840s British women formed small "Olive Leaf Circles". Under Elihu Burritt and Henry Richard the London society convened the first International Peace Congress in London in 1843. Randal Cremer and Frédéric Passy founded the Inter-Parliamentary Union in 1889. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_movement]
- Bertha von Suttner (9 June 1843 – 21 June 1914), Austrian novelist; briefly Alfred Nobel's secretary in Paris in 1876; her novel *Die Waffen nieder!* (*Lay Down Your Arms!*, 1889) ran to 37 editions in 15 languages; first woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize (1905). She died a week before Sarajevo. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertha_von_Suttner]
- Nobel's will: the prize goes to whoever has "done the most or the best work for fraternity between nations, for the abolition or reduction of standing armies and for the holding and promotion of peace congresses"; chosen by a five-member committee appointed by the Norwegian parliament; first awarded 1901 (Dunant and Passy). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Peace_Prize]
- Controversial awards include Kissinger and Lê Đức Thọ (1973), when two committee members resigned; *Foreign Policy* listed Gandhi among those who "never won the prize, but should have". The 2026 prize went to Navi Pillay (announced October 2026, per Wikipedia citing nobelpeaceprize.org; confirm before publishing). [source: same]
- Gandhi was nominated in 1937, 1938, 1939, 1947 and 1948 (about 12 nominations in all); after his assassination (30 January 1948) the committee awarded no prize for 1948, reportedly saying there was "no suitable living candidate". (Journalistic sources; the quoted phrase should be attributed as "reportedly".) [source: https://www.businesstoday.in/india/story/why-mahatma-gandhi-never-received-the-nobel-peace-prize-despite-multiple-nominations-559229-2026-10-02 ; https://southasia.ucla.edu/history-politics/gandhi/gandhi-nobel-peace-prize]

## Vienna (1814–15), the balance of power and the Concert of Europe
- The Congress met from September 1814 to June 1815; the Final Act was signed on 9 June 1815, nine days before Waterloo, by Austria, France, Portugal, Prussia, Russia, Sweden-Norway and Britain. Its goal was to resize the powers so they would balance one another. The Final Act condemned the slave trade. The Ottoman Empire was invited but sent no delegation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Vienna]
- Paul Schroeder ("Did the Vienna Settlement Rest on a Balance of Power?", *AHR* 1992; *The Transformation of European Politics, 1763–1848*, 1994) argues the old balance-of-power formulae were destabilising and predatory, and that Vienna instead set rules producing a stable "equilibrium". Kissinger's *A World Restored* (1957) is the classic realist reading. [source: same]
- The Concert of Europe (1814–1914): the five great powers acting together to keep the status quo; the formal "Congress System" collapsed in the 1820s; a second phase from the 1880s to 1914 coincided with peaceful partition of Africa and Asia among the powers. Holy Alliance members (Russia, Austria, Prussia) used it against liberal and national movements. It failed with the Balkan crises of 1914. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concert_of_Europe]

## The Hague Conferences (1899, 1907)
- Proposed by Tsar Nicholas II on 24 August 1898; the conference opened 18 May 1899 (the Tsar's birthday); acts signed 29 July 1899, in force 4 September 1900. Main result: the Permanent Court of Arbitration, "the first global mechanism for the peaceful resolution of international disputes"; 26 signatories of the 1899 Convention I. Compulsory arbitration was favoured by most (including the US, Britain, Russia, France, Qing China and Persia) but vetoed by a few led by Germany. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hague_Conventions_of_1899_and_1907]
- Second Conference (suggested by Theodore Roosevelt in 1904, delayed by the Russo-Japanese War): 15 June – 18 October 1907; thirteen treaties and one declaration; in force 26 January 1910. A third conference planned for 1914/15 never met. Russia's delegation was led by Friedrich Martens (the Martens Clause). [source: same]
- 1907: some 44 states attended, about half of them Latin American; Brazil's Rui (Ruy) Barbosa led opposition to a court weighted towards the great powers in the name of the legal equality of states. China's delegate asked for a clearer legal definition of "war" (Ryan Mitchell, 2021). [source: https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781315717890-11/identity-formation-theorization-decline-latin-american-international-law-liliana-obreg%C3%B3n ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hague_Conventions_of_1899_and_1907] (44 states from a chapter abstract; not checked against conference records.)

## The First World War, women's peace activism and Paris 1919
- International Congress of Women at The Hague opened 28 April 1915: about 1,200 delegates from twelve countries (Germany sent 28; British attendance was cut when the government suspended the ferry and withheld passports). A delegation later presented Wilson with a proposal for neutral mediation. It created the International Women's Committee for Permanent Peace, renamed the Women's International League for Peace and Freedom (WILPF) at Zurich in 1919 (over 200 women from 17 nations, meeting at the same time as the Paris conference). WILPF is headquartered in Geneva with sections in 37 countries. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_International_League_for_Peace_and_Freedom ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Congress_of_Women]
- Jane Addams (6 September 1860 – 21 May 1935): co-founded Hull House, Chicago (1889); founded the Woman's Peace Party (January 1915, with Carrie Chapman Catt; about 3,000 women); WILPF's first president; *Newer Ideals of Peace* (1907); in 1931 the first American woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize (shared with Nicholas Murray Butler). Emily Greene Balch, WILPF co-founder, won in 1946. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Addams ; WILPF page]
- Wilson's Fourteen Points: speech to Congress, 8 January 1918. Point I: "Open covenants of peace, openly arrived at"; the last point called for a general association of nations guaranteeing "political independence and territorial integrity [of] great and small states alike". Lenin's Decree on Peace (November 1917) had already demanded peace "without annexations or indemnities", self-determination and an end to secret diplomacy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourteen_Points]
- Paris Peace Conference: opened 18 January 1919 at the Quai d'Orsay; diplomats from 32 countries and nationalities; ended formally 21 January 1920 with the League's first General Assembly; the peace process continued to the Treaty of Lausanne (July 1923). Japan's Racial Equality Proposal was blocked, chiefly at Australian (Billy Hughes) insistence, with British acquiescence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Peace_Conference_(1919–1920)]
- Wilson received the 1919 Nobel Peace Prize (awarded 10 December 1920) "for his role as founder of the League of Nations". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodrow_Wilson]
- League of Nations: Covenant signed 28 April 1919 as Part I of Versailles; League founded 10 January 1920; 58 members at its height (28 September 1934 – 23 February 1935); ceased operations 18 April 1946. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations]
- Kellogg–Briand Pact (Pact of Paris; "General Treaty for Renunciation of War as an Instrument of National Policy"), signed in Paris 27 August 1928; 57 state parties by 1929. Article I: the parties "condemn recourse to war for the solution of international controversies and renounce it as an instrument of national policy". Devised by the lawyers Salmon Levinson and James T. Shotwell; it did not stop Manchuria (1931) or Abyssinia (1935), and states began waging undeclared wars, but it underpinned the "crime against peace" charge at Nuremberg and Tokyo. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kellogg–Briand_Pact]

## San Francisco, the UN and human-rights law
- The UN Conference on International Organization (San Francisco Conference): 25 April – 26 June 1945; delegates from 50 Allied nations, 850 delegates and some 3,500 attendees in all; the Charter was signed 26 June 1945 at the Herbst Theatre; Poland signed later (15 October) as the 51st original member. Charter in force 24 October 1945. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Conference_on_International_Organization ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter_of_the_United_Nations]
- The Security Council/General Assembly design copied the League structure devised by Jan Smuts and Lord Robert Cecil (MacMillan). Article 2(4) bans "the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state"; Article 2(7) protects domestic jurisdiction; Article 51 preserves self-defence. [source: Charter page]
- Universal Declaration of Human Rights: adopted 10 December 1948 at the Palais de Chaillot, Paris (Resolution 217); 48 in favour, none against, 8 abstentions, 2 not voting, out of 58 members. Drafting committee chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt, including John Humphrey (Canada, first draft), René Cassin (France, second draft and structure), P. C. Chang (China), Charles Malik (Lebanon) and Hansa Mehta (India). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights]
- René Cassin (1887–1976), French Jewish jurist, wounded in the First World War; Nobel Peace Prize 1968 for his work on the UDHR. (Wikipedia's article gives inconsistent day/month dates for birth and death; use years only.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/René_Cassin]
- Raphael Lemkin (24 June 1900 – 28 August 1959), Polish Jewish lawyer, coined "genocide" (*Axis Rule in Occupied Europe*, 1944), moved by the Armenian genocide; lost 49 relatives in the Holocaust. Genocide Convention adopted unanimously 9 December 1948 (the day before the UDHR), in force 12 January 1951; 154 parties as of September 2026. One of the first petitions under it (Civil Rights Congress, 1951) accused the US over its treatment of Black Americans. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael_Lemkin ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genocide_Convention]

## Peacekeeping
- First mission: UNTSO (1948, the Arab–Israeli ceasefire), still operating; UNMOGIP (Kashmir) followed. The first armed force, UNEF, was approved by General Assembly Resolution 1001 (ES-I) on 7 November 1956 after the Suez Crisis, the idea of Lester Pearson (Nobel Peace Prize 1957) and Dag Hammarskjöld; it stayed on the Egypt–Israel line until Egypt asked it to leave in May 1967 (withdrawn by 17 June 1967). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Emergency_Force ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_peacekeeping]
- Since 1948 more than two million people from 125 countries have served in 72 UN operations; the forces won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1988. As of 2026: eleven active operations, budget $5.66 billion, about 53,000 personnel from 117 countries; the largest contributors are Nepal, Rwanda, Bangladesh, India and Pakistan. No new operation has been authorised since 2017 (Haiti), the longest gap since the 1980s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_peacekeeping]
- Failures and harms: UNAMIR in Rwanda (1994) and Srebrenica led to the Brahimi Report (2000). Nepalese peacekeepers' base in Haiti introduced cholera (2010), which killed about 10,000 and infected more than 800,000 over a decade. Reported sexual exploitation and abuse allegations run at roughly 50–100 a year, with substantial under-reporting. [source: same]
- Effectiveness: Virginia Page Fortna, *Does Peacekeeping Work?* (2008), finds peacekeeping substantially extends peace after civil wars; studies link it to fewer civilian deaths, varying with mandate. [source: same]

## Scientists and the bomb
- Russell–Einstein Manifesto: issued in London on 9 July 1955 at a press conference at Caxton Hall chaired by Joseph Rotblat; eleven signatories including Einstein, who signed shortly before his death on 18 April 1955. It called for a scientists' conference. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell–Einstein_Manifesto]
- Pugwash: the industrialist Cyrus Eaton hosted the first conference at Pugwash, Nova Scotia (his birthplace) in July 1957 (a planned meeting in India, invited by Nehru, fell through after Suez); about 22 scientists attended (figure flagged "citation needed"). Rotblat and Pugwash shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 1995. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pugwash_Conferences_on_Science_and_World_Affairs]
- Linus Pauling (1901–1994): Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1954 and Peace 1962 — the only person to win two unshared Nobel Prizes. With Barry Commoner he circulated a petition from May 1957; on 15 January 1958 he and his wife Ava Helen presented it to Dag Hammarskjöld, signed by 11,021 scientists from fifty countries; *No More War!* (1958). The State Department denied him a passport in 1952. The 1962 prize was announced on 10 October 1963, the day the Partial Test Ban Treaty entered into force. A Senate subcommittee (1960) called him "the number one scientific name in virtually every major activity of the Communist peace offensive in this country"; *Life* headlined his prize "A Weird Insult from Norway". He regretted that Ava Helen did not share it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linus_Pauling]
- The Baby Tooth Survey (St Louis CNI, published by Louise Reiss, 1961) showed strontium-90 from fallout in children's teeth and helped build pressure for the test ban. [source: same]

## Mass disarmament campaigns
- Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament (CND): committee formed November 1957 (Canon John Collins chair, Bertrand Russell president, Peggy Duff organising secretary); first public meeting at Methodist Central Hall, Westminster, 17 February 1958; Aldermaston Marches 1958–65; Gerald Holtom designed its symbol in 1958 from the semaphore letters N and D — now the international peace symbol. Membership peaked at 110,000 in 1983. The Greenham Common Women's Peace Camp shaped its second wave. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campaign_for_Nuclear_Disarmament]
- Nuclear Freeze: launched by Randall Forsberg's "Call to Halt the Nuclear Arms Race" (1980); on 12 June 1982 about a million people rallied in New York during the UN's Second Special Session on Disarmament, called the largest peace rally in US history; the House passed a (non-binding, amended) Freeze resolution 278–149 on 4 May 1983. Gorbachev later said his "new thinking" absorbed the demands of antiwar organisations (Wittner). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_Freeze_campaign ; https://voteview.com/rollcall/RH0980083]
- Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons: adopted 7 July 2017, in force 22 January 2021; 96 signatories, 75 parties (per Wikipedia, 2026). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_on_the_Prohibition_of_Nuclear_Weapons]

## Religious witness
- George Fox made the first Quaker peace declaration in 1651. The 1660 Declaration to Charles II: "We utterly deny all outward wars and strife, and fightings with outward weapons, for any end, or under any pretence whatsoever, and this is our testimony to the whole world." (Wikipedia's quote opens "...principles and practices we do utterly deny, with all outward wars, and strife, and fightings with outward weapons, for any end, or under any pretence whatsoever, and this is our testimony to the whole world." Use this wording.) The 1947 Nobel Peace Prize went to the Quakers, accepted by the American Friends Service Committee and the British Friends Service Council. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testimony_of_peace]
- Desmond Tutu (7 October 1931 – 26 December 2021), Anglican Archbishop of Cape Town; Nobel Peace Prize 1984; chaired the TRC. He was criticised as too radical by many white South Africans and too moderate by some activists. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desmond_Tutu]
- The 14th Dalai Lama (born 6 July 1935) fled to India in 1959, set up the government in exile (Mussoorie, then Dharamshala from 1960), advocates the "Middle Way Approach" since the early 1970s, and won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1989 (after Tiananmen). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/14th_Dalai_Lama]

## Nonviolence and liberation
- Satyagraha ("holding firmly to truth", "truth force") was coined by Gandhi (1869–1948); used in South Africa and India (the 1930 Salt March); it influenced King, James Bevel and the anti-apartheid struggle. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyagraha] (Published Apartheid chapter dates its first use to Johannesburg 1906.)
- Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan ("Bacha Khan", 1890–1988), a devout Muslim Pashtun, founded the nonviolent Khudai Khidmatgar ("Servants of God") movement in 1929 on satyagraha principles; India awarded him the Bharat Ratna in 1987. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdul_Ghaffar_Khan]
- Martin Luther King Jr.: Montgomery bus boycott (1955), Nobel Peace Prize 1964; visited India February–March 1959 (funded partly via the American Friends Service Committee). In "Letter from Birmingham Jail" (1963) he wrote of "negative peace which is the absence of tension" and "positive peace which is the presence of justice". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr. ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_and_conflict_studies]
- Nelson Mandela: initially committed to nonviolence, co-founded Umkhonto we Sizwe in 1961 (sabotage; first bombings 16 December 1961, soon after Luthuli's Nobel); sentenced to life in the Rivonia Trial; served 27 years; shared the 1993 Nobel Peace Prize with F. W. de Klerk. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nelson_Mandela] — useful for the "freedom fighter" vs "peacemaker" tension.
- Chenoweth and Stephan, *Why Civil Resistance Works* (2011): 323 campaigns 1900–2006; major nonviolent campaigns succeeded 53 per cent of the time against 26 per cent for violent ones; Chenoweth's "3.5% rule" (every campaign with active participation of 3.5 per cent of the population succeeded). The rule has been criticised as misused. [source: https://www.nonviolent-conflict.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/Why-Civil-Resistance-Works..The-Strategic-Logic-of-Nonviolent-Conflict-Book.pdf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erica_Chenoweth]
- Leymah Gbowee led Women of Liberia Mass Action for Peace, which helped end the Second Liberian Civil War in 2003; Nobel Peace Prize 2011 with Ellen Johnson Sirleaf and Tawakkul Karman "for their non-violent struggle for the safety of women and for women's rights to full participation in peace-building work". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leymah_Gbowee]

## Cold War diplomacy: Ostpolitik
- Egon Bahr proposed "Wandel durch Annäherung" ("change through rapprochement") in a 1963 speech at Tutzing. Willy Brandt (chancellor 1969–74): Treaty of Moscow (12 August 1970), Treaty of Warsaw (December 1970, accepting the Oder–Neisse line), the Warsaw Kniefall (December 1970, at the Ghetto Uprising memorial), Four Power Agreement on Berlin (3 September 1971), Basic Treaty with East Germany (21 December 1972). Nobel Peace Prize 1971. The CDU opposed it; Barzel's 1972 no-confidence vote failed by two votes, later found to have been bought by the GDR. Kohl kept the policy after 1982. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willy_Brandt ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostpolitik]

## Later settlements
- Camp David: Sadat and Begin, 13 days at Camp David (5–17 September 1978); two frameworks signed 17 September 1978; the Egypt–Israel treaty followed in 1979; shared 1978 Nobel Peace Prize. The Palestinian framework was written without Palestinians. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_David_Accords]
- Dayton: initialled at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base, Dayton, Ohio, on 21 November 1995 by Milošević, Izetbegović and Tuđman; signed in Paris 14 December 1995; brokered by Richard Holbrooke. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dayton_Agreement]
- Good Friday (Belfast) Agreement: 10 April 1998; talks chaired by US envoy George Mitchell; referendums 22 May 1998 (Northern Ireland 71% yes on 81% turnout; Republic 94% yes on 56% turnout; almost all Catholics but 57% of Protestants voted yes). The Northern Ireland Women's Coalition took part. More than 3,500 died in the Troubles. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Friday_Agreement]
- Colombia: talks with FARC from 2012 in Havana; a gender sub-commission (2014), which may have made Colombia the first peace negotiation to address LGBTI rights; the agreement was rejected 50.2% to 49.8% in the 2 October 2016 referendum; a revised deal was signed 24 November 2016 and ratified by Congress 29–30 November. (Wikipedia article flagged as partly AI-generated; facts here are standard.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombian_peace_process]
- PA-X (PeaceRep, University of Edinburgh) records 2,257 peace agreements from more than 170 peace processes between 1990 and the end of 2025 (v10). [source: https://www.peaceagreements.org/downloads]

## Truth commissions and transitional justice
- Perhaps the first: Uganda, 1974 (Commission of Inquiry into the Disappearances of People since 25 January 1971). The first effective: Argentina's CONADEP, created by Raúl Alfonsín on 15 December 1983; its report *Nunca Más* was delivered 20 September 1984 and opened the way to the Trial of the Juntas. Chile's commission (April 1990) was the first to use "Truth and Reconciliation" in its name. Others: Nepal 1990, El Salvador 1992, Guatemala 1994 (Historical Clarification Commission), Sierra Leone, DR Congo, Morocco (Equity and Reconciliation), Germany (two on East Germany), Mauritius (slavery and indenture), Canada (residential schools, established 2006; final report 2015). Priscilla Hayner's definition: a temporary, officially authorised body investigating a pattern of abuses and engaging directly with the affected population. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth_commission]

## Peace research: Galtung and positive peace
- Johan Galtung (24 October 1930 – 17 February 2024), Norwegian sociologist, principal founder of peace and conflict studies; founded the Peace Research Institute Oslo (PRIO) in 1959 (director to 1969/70; sources differ), the *Journal of Peace Research* in 1964, and held the world's first chair in peace and conflict studies (Oslo, 1969); jailed six months as a conscientious objector when Norway refused peace-related alternative service; Right Livelihood Award 1987; founded TRANSCEND (1993). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johan_Galtung]
- Negative peace = absence of direct violence; positive peace = absence of structural violence/presence of justice. Galtung introduced the pair in his 1964 *JPR* editorial and expanded positive peace to "structural violence" in "Violence, Peace and Peace Research" (*JPR* 6:3, 1969, 167–191); later "cultural violence" (ideas legitimising violence). The terms predate him: Jane Addams contrasted "newer ideals of peace" with "negative peace" (lectures from 1899; book 1907), and King used both terms in 1963. Boutros-Ghali's *An Agenda for Peace* (1992) popularised peacebuilding. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_and_conflict_studies ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johan_Galtung ; https://transcend.org/tms/?p=270554]

## Recent trends (attributed and dated)
- UCDP (Uppsala Conflict Data Program) counts an armed conflict from 25 battle-related deaths a year with a state as one party; Correlates of War requires 1,000. UCDP/PRIO data run from 1946. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uppsala_Conflict_Data_Program]

NPR reported about 244,600 killed in all organised violence in 2025, the highest since 1994. ## Debates
- **Imperial peace:** is Pax Romana / Mongolica / the tributary order "peace" or pacification? Goffart vs Eckstein on Rome; Calgacus as Roman self-criticism; Fairbank's tribute model vs its critics and Kang's "hierarchy" thesis. [sources above]
- **Westphalian myth:** Gross (1948) and IR textbooks vs Osiander (2001), Beaulac, Gardner; Philpott defends a sovereignty revolution. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westphalian_system]
- **Vienna:** balance of power (Kissinger) vs rule-based equilibrium (Schroeder); did the Concert end in the 1820s, 1850s or 1914? Peace among the powers coincided with conquest in Africa and Asia. [sources: Congress of Vienna, Concert of Europe pages]
- **Was 1919 a failed peace?** Keynes's "Carthaginian peace" vs MacMillan's defence; the "Wilsonian moment" (Manela) for colonised peoples; Japan's racial-equality defeat. [source: Paris Peace Conference page; published First World War chapter]
- **Outlawing war:** naive (Kellogg–Briand as "a scrap of paper") vs Hathaway and Shapiro's argument that it remade international law (conquest became illegal). [source: Kellogg–Briand page]
- **The Long Peace and the decline of violence:** Gaddis ("The Long Peace: Elements of Stability in the Postwar International System", *International Security* 10:4, 1986); Pinker, *The Better Angels of Our Nature* (2011); Taleb and Cirillo ("On the statistical properties and tail risk of violent conflicts", *Physica A*, 2016) find no decline in war's lethality; Clauset (2018, *Science Advances*) finds the post-1945 peace could plausibly be chance; Braumoeller (2019) finds no decline in war initiation since 1815, only in the use of force between states since the Cold War; Freedman (2014) on alliances and deterrence; the long peace was a Northern experience (published Global Cold War chapter). 2025's record conflict count (UCDP) is now part of the debate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Peace ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Better_Angels_of_Our_Nature]
- **Negative vs positive peace:** is peace the absence of war or the presence of justice? Addams, King, Galtung; critics say "structural violence" stretches "violence" too far (general debate; no named critic verified).
- **Truth vs justice:** amnesty-for-truth (South Africa) vs prosecutions (Argentina's Trial of the Juntas); survivors' complaints about reparations (Khulumani). [sources: TRC pages]
- **Does peacekeeping work?** Fortna's positive findings vs Rwanda, Srebrenica, Haiti cholera, sexual abuse. [source: UN peacekeeping page]
- **Nonviolence vs armed struggle:** Gandhi, Ghaffar Khan, King vs Mandela's turn to MK; Chenoweth and Stephan's data. [sources above]
- **Did peace movements matter?** Wittner and Evangelista argue the antinuclear movement shaped Gorbachev's "new thinking"; Reagan opposed the Freeze. [source: Nuclear Freeze page]
- **Women's inclusion:** CFR data and Resolution 1325 vs persistent exclusion. [sources above]
