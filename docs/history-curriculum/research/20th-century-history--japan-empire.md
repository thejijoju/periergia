## 1. Framing: Asia's only modern colonial power
- Japan's colonial ideology preached the unity of Asian races under Japanese leadership against Western imperialism, but in practice installed bureaucrats and engineers devoted to efficiency and modernisation; Japanese was the official language of the bureaucracy and taught in schools as the "national language". [source: GEA]
- Precedent and imitation: in 1872 the US minister Charles DeLong and adviser Charles LeGendre urged Japan to occupy Taiwan and "civilize" its Indigenous people as the US had done with Native Americans; LeGendre urged a Japanese Monroe Doctrine. Japan later compared its Asian expansion to the Monroe Doctrine and the Roosevelt Corollary (e.g., at the Lansing–Ishii talks). [source: GEA] (Part of this passage carries a "citation needed" tag; use as "Japanese leaders drew on Western precedents, including the Monroe Doctrine".)
- Katsura Tarō told the New York Times (c. 1905) that Japan would force "upon Korea and China the same benefits of modern development that have been in the past forced on us". [source: GEA]

## 2. Taiwan after 1915 (brief; Meiji chapter has 1895–1915)
- Historians divide Japanese rule into three phases: military suppression (1895–1915), *dōka* assimilation (1915–37) and *kōminka* Japanization (1937–45), with a separate policy for Indigenous peoples. [source: TWN, citing Shih 2022 and Leo Ching 2001]
- Whether to call 1895–1945 "Japanese rule" (日治) or "Japanese occupation" (日據) is a live political dispute in Taiwan; the KMT-led Executive Yuan ordered "occupation" in 2013; historian Chou Wan-yao prefers "rule". [source: TWN]
- Taishō-era politics: "Taiwan is Taiwan people's Taiwan" became a common position of anti-Japanese groups; in December 1920 Lin Hsien-tang and 178 Taiwanese petitioned Tokyo for self-determination (rejected); the petition movement for a Taiwanese parliament lasted 13 years and, though unsuccessful, prompted local assemblies in 1935. [source: TWN]
### Musha (Wushe) incident, 1930
- Began 27 October 1930, the last major uprising against Japanese rule in Taiwan. Seediq of Musha (Nōkō District, Taichū Prefecture; today Ren'ai Township, Nantou) led by Mona Rudao attacked police sub-stations and then the elementary school, where hundreds of Japanese had gathered for an athletics meet. [source: MUS]
- Causes cited: police misconduct, forced labour (TWN: labour in camphor extraction), disrespect for Indigenous customs; the Japanese classed Indigenous people as *seiban* ("raw barbarians"). The trigger: a police officer, Katsuhiko Yoshimura, struck Mona Rudao's son Daho Mona at a wedding feast. Mona Rudao had been one of 43 Indigenous leaders taken on a tour of Japan. [source: MUS; TWN]
- 134 Japanese, including women and children, and two Han Taiwanese in Japanese dress were killed; most victims were beheaded. [source: MUS] (TWN's sentence "Approximately 350 students, 134 Japanese, and 2 Han Chinese" is ambiguous; use MUS's figure.)
- Governor-General Ishizuka Eizō sent about 2,000 troops (TWN: 2,000–3,000 troops and Indigenous auxiliaries); the army air corps dropped gas bombs — MUS calls it "allegedly" the first chemical warfare in Asia and notes Haruyama Meitetsu's view that tear gas and a cyanide–tear gas mix were used rather than mustard gas. Say "poison gas", with caution. [source: MUS; TWN]
- Mona Rudao killed himself on 28 November 1930; resistance was suppressed by the third week of December. Japanese colonial records: 644 Seediq killed or committed suicide, 564 surrendered. [source: TWN citing Ching 2001; MUS] (MUS's "290 of whom committed suicide" is tagged citation needed — do not use.)
- Ishizuka and his chief civil administrator Hitomi Jirō resigned in January 1931. In the "Second Musha Incident", 25 April 1931, Indigenous groups working with the Japanese attacked the village where about 500 surrendered Seediq were confined and beheaded all remaining males over 15 (tagged citation needed in MUS; phrase cautiously). [source: MUS]
- After Musha, policy shifted: Indigenous people were reclassified as *Takasagozoku* and drawn into *kōminka* as "imperial subjects"; Leo Ching argues *kōminka* became the dominant expression of colonial control. [source: MUS; TWN]
- Remembered in the 2011 Taiwanese film *Seediq Bale* and Wu He's novel *Remains of Life* (2000; English 2017). [source: MUS]
### Kōminka, 1937–45
- From 1937 the *kōminka* (literally "transforming colonial peoples into imperial subjects") movement aimed to make Taiwanese loyal subjects rather than supporters of China. Chinese sections of newspapers and Classical Chinese in schools were removed in April 1937; China's and Taiwan's history were removed from the curriculum. [source: TWN; KOMT]
- A name-change campaign began in 1940; about 7% of Taiwanese had taken Japanese names by the end of the war. Taiwanese opera, puppet plays, temple paper-burning were banned; Shinto shrine worship and domestic altars encouraged. [source: TWN]
- TWN judges *kōminka* "generally unsuccessful", with few Taiwanese becoming "true Japanese"; many who adopted Japanese identity did so pragmatically. [source: TWN]
- By 1939 industrial output exceeded agricultural output in Taiwan. In 1943 primary education became compulsory; by 1944 nearly three in four children were enrolled. [source: TWN]
- From 1937 to 1945 over 207,000 Taiwanese were employed by the Japanese military; roughly 50,000 died or went missing; the 1942 Special Volunteer System recruited Indigenous men as the Takasago Volunteers. [source: TWN]
- Between 1,000 and 2,000 Taiwanese women were in the "comfort women" system (TWN); CW says "more than 2,000"; Yoshimi notes more than half were minors. [source: TWN; CW] Use "one to two thousand or more".
- Administration handed to the Republic of China on 25 October 1945 (Governor-General Andō Rikichi). Almost 350,000 Japanese civilians lived in Taiwan at war's end. [source: TWN]

## 3. Korea, 1910–45
### Military rule, land and settlers (1910–19)
- The Kempeitai (military police) took over policing from June 1910 under Akashi Motojirō; this period is called "military rule" (*budan seiji*). [source: KOR; MAR]
- Governor-General Terauchi's Land Survey Bureau registered ownership by written documents, dispossessing many cultivators with customary rights; the Oriental Development Company and Japanese landlords expanded. Many owners and workers became tenants paying over half their crop in rent. [source: KOR] (KOR's land-share percentages "36.8 to 39.8 to 52.7%" are unclear and partly uncited — do not use.)
- Japanese in Korea: about 561,000 of over 21 million in 1934 (under 3%); 651,000 by 1939. Over 981,000 Koreans lived in Japan by 1939. [source: KOR]
### The March First Movement, 1919
- Background: Wilson's Fourteen Points; Gojong's sudden death on 21 January 1919 and rumours of poisoning; the Tokyo students' February 8 Declaration. [source: MAR]
- Thirty-three representatives (Cheondoists, Christians, Buddhists) signed the Declaration of Independence on 25–27 February; 21,000 copies were printed on 27 February. On 1 March 29 of the 33 read it at the restaurant T'aehwagwan and reported themselves to the police; students read it publicly in Tapgol (Pagoda) Park. [source: MAR]
- Scale: roughly 1,500–1,800 protests, 0.8–2 million participants, out of a population of about 16–17 million; one Japanese police report gave 1,214 protests with 1.1 million participants. [source: MAR]
- Casualties: Park Eun-sik's Korean count (1920) 7,509 killed, 15,961 wounded, 46,948 arrested; Japanese officials reported 553 killed and over 12,000 arrested (1 March–11 April), and 8 police and soldiers killed. Infobox range: "around 798 to 7,509". [source: MAR; KOR]
- Jeamni massacre, 15 April 1919: Japanese forces lured 20–30 villagers into a church, shot them and burned the church. [source: MAR]
- Yu Gwan-sun, 16, a student at Ewha Haktang, spread the movement to Cheonan; on 1 April 3,000 gathered and military police killed 19, including her parents; she died in Seodaemun Prison on 28 September 1920 and is remembered as a martyr. [source: MAR]
- Consequences: Korean Provisional Government in exile (Shanghai); the movement inspired May Fourth organisers such as Fu Ssu-nien. South Korea's constitution names the Provisional Government "born of the March First Independence Movement of 1919" in its first sentence; North Korea teaches it with Kim Il Sung's family at the centre. [source: MAR]
### "Cultural rule" (bunka seiji) from 1919
- Governor-General Saitō Makoto's concessions: Korean newspapers allowed (the *Dong-A Ilbo* and *Chosun Ilbo* founded), the Chōsen Art Exhibition, a Government-General library; previously (1915–early 1920) only the government-run *Maeil Sinbo* was permitted. [source: MAR]
- Debate: contemporaries called the concessions cosmetic ("merely one of those face-saving diplomatic schemes of Japan", *Evening Star*, 22 August 1919). Historian Michael Shin argues colonial authority actually expanded: expenditure doubled 1919–21, police presence grew, management of culture replaced passive punishment. Shin also sees 1919 as the turning point from elite to popular nationalism, and the print boom of cultural rule as feeding nationalism. [source: MAR]
- Saitō told *The Independent* (January 1920) that Japan had "no other desire than to improve the condition of the Korean people". [source: MAR]
### Kantō Massacre, 1923 (Koreans in Japan)
- After the Great Kantō earthquake of 1 September 1923, military, police and vigilantes murdered an estimated 6,000 people (current estimates 6,000–9,000), mainly Koreans, also Chinese, misidentified Japanese, and leftists; suspects were tested with shibboleths such as "十五円五十銭"; official reports in September claimed five Koreans killed. [source: KAN]
### Assimilation and wartime mobilisation (1937–45)
- *Naisen ittai* ("Japan and Korea as one body") from 1938. All Korean newspapers except the official one were closed in 1940; the Korean Language Society was suppressed in 1942; Korean-language courses were phased out by 1943. [source: KOR]
- Ideology: the *Nissen dōsoron* theory of common Japanese–Korean ancestry; Governor-General Koiso (1944) used the *Nihon Shoki* to argue Koreans should "awaken" to their "true nature" as Japanese. Assimilation did not mean equality: Imaizumi Teisuke compared the relationship to man and woman, rider and horse. (Parts of this KOR section are tagged citation needed; use only as "official ideology claimed a common ancestry".) [source: KOR]
### Name changes (sōshi-kaimei)
- A 1911 order (No. 124, 11 November 1911) had *forbidden* Koreans to take Japanese-style names. Governor-General Minami Jirō's Ordinance No. 19 (1939) required the creation of a household surname (*shi*) and No. 20 (1940) permitted changing given names. [source: SOS]
- Registration window 11 February–10 August 1940: about 3.9% of households had registered by April, 80.3% by 10 August, after governors were told to complete registration by 20 July. Only 9.6% changed given names. Refusers faced dismissal, school expulsion and denial of services. [source: SOS]
- Debate: most historians see official compulsion; they disagree whether it came from low-level officials, regional bodies or colonial policy; others argue many Koreans adopted names voluntarily to avoid discrimination. The 1946 US Military Government Name Restoration Order (23 October 1946) let Koreans restore names. [source: SOS]
### Forced labour and military service
- From 1939, labour shortages led to organised recruitment of Koreans for Japan, through agents and later directly, "often involving elements of coercion"; by 1942 the National Mobilization Law was extended to conscript Korean workers for Korea, Manchukuo and Japan. SCAP estimated over 2 million Koreans in Japan by war's end. [source: KOR]
- KOR states that of 5,400,000 Koreans conscripted, about 670,000 were taken to Japan (including Karafuto) and about 60,000 died there. KOR's further "270,000 to 810,000" deaths in Korea and Manchuria comes from R. J. Rummel and should not be used. [source: KOR] (Treat 5.4 million as one widely cited estimate; ABHN also gives "as many as 670,000 Korean conscripts to Japan".)
- Korean labourers at Tarawa: 129 of 1,200 survived the battle. [source: KOR]
- Hiroshima/Nagasaki: about 5,000–8,000 Koreans killed in Hiroshima and 1,500–2,000 in Nagasaki; the South Korea Atomic Bomb Victims Association says about 70,000 Koreans were exposed. Most were drafted workers. [source: ABHN; KOR] (Prince Yi U of the Korean imperial house died at Hiroshima. [source: ABHN])
### Economy and the "colonial modernity" debate (see Debates)
- Output in agriculture, fishery, forestry and industry rose about tenfold 1910–45 (KOR chart); most major firms were Japanese-owned with key posts reserved for Japanese; most coal, iron and crops were shipped to Japan; the 1918 rice shortage drove export of Korean rice while Korean consumption fell. [source: KOR]

## 4. China before 1931: the Twenty-One Demands (brief; FWW chapter covers)
- Presented 18 January 1915 by the Ōkuma government (Prime Minister Ōkuma Shigenobu); Japan would keep Germany's Shandong holdings and gain influence in Manchuria and Inner Mongolia; section 5 would have given Japan a decisive voice in Chinese finance, policing and government — in effect a protectorate. China published the secret demands; Britain and the US forced Japan to drop section 5; agreements ratifying the first four groups were signed 25 May 1915. A spontaneous nationwide boycott of Japanese goods followed. [source: TWD]

## 5. Taishō democracy and its erosion (brief)
- Usually dated c. 1905 (Hibiya riots) to 1932; first true party cabinet under Hara Takashi in 1918 after the rice riots; 1925 General Election Law (universal male suffrage, electorate quadrupled) alongside the Peace Preservation Law. Ended after the Depression, the 1931 Mukden incident and assassinations, notably the May 15 Incident of 1932 (Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi killed); "national unity" cabinets followed. [source: TAI]
- Andrew Gordon's alternative term "imperial democracy": popular movements demanded participation while fervently supporting emperor and empire. [source: TAI] (Already in Meiji notes; one sentence.)
- Taiwanese and Korean petitioners and students drew on Taishō liberalism (TWN; MAR). Connects the domestic and colonial stories.

## 6. Manchuria 1931 and Manchukuo
- Mukden incident, 18 September 1931: Lt Suemori Kawamoto exploded a small charge by the South Manchuria Railway near Mukden (Liutiao Lake); so weak a train passed minutes later; planned by Itagaki Seishirō and Ishiwara Kanji (with Doihara Kenji and others) by 31 May 1931; Kwantung Army commander Honjō Shigeru was at first appalled but approved after the fact. In Chinese memory "9.18"; sirens sound in many Chinese cities on 18 September. [source: MUK]
- Tokyo "was shocked by the news of the Army acting without orders", but felt powerless and sent reinforcements (gekokujō). [source: JIM]
- Manchukuo proclaimed 18 February 1932, formally established 1 March 1932 at Changchun, renamed Xinjing; Japan recognised it on 15 September 1932 (Japan–Manchukuo Protocol). Puyi was Chief Executive, then Emperor (era name Kangde) from 1 March 1934; Puyi "was nothing more than a figurehead"; Japanese vice-ministers made the decisions. Motto "Five Races Under One Union" (Chinese, Mongols, Manchus, Koreans, Japanese). Only party: the Concordia Association. [source: MAN]
- Resistance: Anti-Japanese Volunteer Armies; pacification took several years. The Han majority largely held Manchuria to be part of China. [source: MAN]
- Population early 1934 about 30.9 million: about 96% Chinese, 590,760 Japanese, 680,000 Koreans (figures tagged citation needed; give as approximate). By 1945 over a million Japanese settlers; the Korean population also rose. [source: MAN]
- Industry under vice-minister Kishi Nobusuke (the "key architect" 1935–39) and the Manchurian Industrial Development Company; the army used Manchukuo as a laboratory for a "national defence state". [source: MAN]
- Forced labour: a joint study by Zhifen Ju, Mitsuyoshi Himeta, Toru Kubo and Mark Peattie says more than ten million Chinese civilians were used as slave labour in Manchukuo; the Benxihu Colliery disaster (world's worst mine disaster) happened there. [source: MAN]
- Oroqen and Nanai (Hezhen) peoples suffered steep population collapse (Hezhen down 80–90%, 1931–45) through opium, forced labour and relocation. [source: MAN]
- Drugs: the Tokyo tribunal found Japan used Manchukuo's "alleged but false independence" for drug traffic; "In 1937, it was pointed out in the League of Nations that 90% of all illicit white drugs in the world were of Japanese origin". [source: MAN quoting IMTFE]
- Popularity at home: Louise Young: "Had they wished, it would have been possible in 1931 and 1932 for journalists and editors to express anti-war sentiments"; the *Asahi* swung to support the war; Manchuria was sold as Japan's "lifeline". [source: MAN]
- Soviet invasion 8 August 1945; Puyi abdicated after the surrender announcement of 15 August. [source: MAN]

## 7. The war in China from 1937
- 7 July 1937, Marco Polo Bridge near Wanping: night exercises, a missing private (Shimura Kikujirō), exchange of fire; "who fired first" remains contested. A local ceasefire on 11 July failed as both sides reinforced. [source: SSJW; MPB]
- Battle of Shanghai (August–November 1937); Nanjing fell 13 December 1937; the Nationalist government moved to Chongqing, which was heavily bombed (more than 5,000 civilians died in the first two days of raids in 1939). [source: SSJW]
- Taierzhuang (March–April 1938) was a decisive Chinese victory; 1938 Yellow River flood (cross-ref only). Operation Ichi-Go (1944): about 500,000 Japanese troops, the largest campaign of the war. [source: SSJW]
### Nanjing, as a range
- Mass rape and murder of civilians and prisoners from 13 December 1937; traditionally "six weeks" (a phrase from Miner Searle Bates's testimony), but Kasahara and Wakabayashi define the atrocity zone as the city plus six counties, 4 December 1937 – 28 March 1938. [source: NAN]
- Death toll estimates: IMTFE over 200,000; "newer estimates" 100,000–200,000; the full range runs from 40,000 (city only) to over 340,000 (Shanghai–Nanjing region). Rapes: 4,000 to over 80,000, about 20,000 most common. [source: NAN]
- Positions: Tokushi Kasahara "more than 100,000 and close to 200,000, or maybe more"; Bob Wakabayashi far exceeds 100,000 but under 200,000, and "estimates over 200,000 are not credible"; Ikuhiko Hata (*Nankin jiken*, 1986) up to 40,000 and calls 300,000 a "symbolic figure"; China officially 300,000 (1947 Nanjing tribunal "more than 300,000"; memorial hall). John Rabe estimated 50,000–60,000 civilians killed within the walls; Lewis Smythe's 1938 survey 12,000 in the city and 26,780 in the counties. [source: NAN; HATA]
- Mufushan, 15–17 December: 17,000–20,000 prisoners machine-gunned by the Yangtze (NAN); other research cited in NAN gives 4,000–20,000 versus the Chinese 57,418 — a range. [source: NAN]
- The Nanking Safety Zone committee (Rabe, Bates, Magee, Smythe, Fitch, McCallum) sheltered refugees and later testified. [source: NAN]
- Japanese historiography: David Askew's "Great Massacre School" vs "Illusion School"; Hata as the "centrist" school (tens of thousands). Tomio Hora's 1967 book and Honda Katsuichi's 1970s *Asahi* series opened the debate. [source: NAN; HATA]
- Trials: Matsui Iwane convicted at Tokyo ("He did nothing, or nothing effective, to abate these horrors") and hanged 23 December 1948 with Hirota Kōki (6–5 vote on Hirota's sentence); Prince Asaka, ranking officer at the height of the atrocities, was granted immunity as an imperial family member; Tani Hisao executed by the Nanjing tribunal 26 April 1947. [source: NAN]
- Memory: Nanjing Massacre Memorial Hall (1985); first national memorial day 13 December 2014; UNESCO Memory of the World listing 9 October 2015. The Yasukuni museum panel says Chinese plain-clothes soldiers were "dealt with severely" rather than acknowledging a massacre. [source: NAN]
### Unit 731
- Secret Kwantung Army biological and chemical warfare unit under Ishii Shirō, based at Pingfang, Harbin; founded 1933 (predecessor Tōgō Unit at Zhongma Fortress, Beiyinhe); a 1936 decree by Hirohito expanded it. About 3,000 staff; up to 600 prisoners held at once; victims ("maruta", logs) mainly Chinese, also Russians, Koreans and others. [source: U731]
- In-facility deaths: "at least 3,000" is "the widely accepted figure in the literature"; former member Okawa Fukumatsu claims 10,000+; the article lead's "14,000" conflicts — give "at least 3,000, possibly far more". [source: U731]
- Field attacks: plague-infected fleas dropped on Ningbo and Changde (1940–41), at least 12 large field trials and at least 11 cities attacked; Sheldon Harris estimates over 200,000 deaths from Japanese germ warfare; a 2002 Changde symposium claimed about 580,000. SSJW: "at least 200,000 deaths", the largest biological attacks in history. [source: U731; SSJW]
- Postwar: 12 members tried at Khabarovsk (1949); Ishii and others received US immunity in exchange for data (later judged of little value); the Tokyo District Court on 28 August 2002 acknowledged Japanese biological warfare in China but denied compensation; the Japanese government has never formally acknowledged the experiments. [source: U731]
- Chemical weapons: Nationalist statistics record 1,973 uses of gas (July 1937–September 1945); Yoshimi and Matsuno show use was authorised by Imperial orders; 375 authorised uses during the Battle of Wuhan (August–October 1938). [source: SSJW]
### The "Three Alls" (sankō sakusen)
- "Kill all, burn all, loot all" — a Chinese term (first in the CCP's *Liberation Daily*, July 1941), popularised in Japan in 1957 by veterans released from the Fushun centre in the confession book *Sankō*; the Japanese army called it *jinmetsu sakusen* (annihilation operations). [source: TAP]
- Response to the CCP's Hundred Regiments Offensive (August 1940); anti-guerrilla drives in Hebei from 1938 (an order approved by Hirohito, 2 December 1938) were formalised by Tanaka Ryūkichi in 1940 and escalated under Okamura Yasuji (North China Area Army from July 1941); Imperial General Headquarters Order No. 575 of 3 December 1941; continued to March 1945. "Unpopulated zones" (*mujin chiku*) were created along railways and mountains. [source: TAP; SSJW]
- Deaths: Himeta Mitsuyoshi — TAP gives "more than 2,700,000" civilians (1996 study); SSJW gives "at least 2.47 million". Communist base-area population reportedly fell from 44 million to 25 million. Give "more than two million (Himeta)". [source: TAP; SSJW]
- Genocide debated: Gavan McCormack says it merits consideration as genocide; Wakabayashi says it could qualify but counters that the victims were killed for resisting. [source: TAP]
### Scale of Chinese losses
- Modern estimates 10–20 million Chinese dead, "a consensus of 15 million to 16 million"; Odd Arne Westad about 14 million (12 million civilians, 2 million soldiers); Rana Mitter considers Westad's figures conservative; PRC official figures over 20 million dead and 35 million casualties; Bian Xiuyue (CASS) 20.6 million dead 1931–45. The war created 95 million refugees (SSJW). Henan famine 1942–43: 2–3 million starvation deaths. Property losses US$383 billion (1937 dollars). [source: SSJW]
- SSJW also cites more than 200,000 Chinese "comfort women" (Su Zhiliang's estimate; see §10). [source: SSJW]
- Mitter: the war started in 1937, deserves a central place in WWII history, and China tied down huge Japanese forces; the British edition is *China's War with Japan, 1937–1945: The Struggle for Survival*, US edition *Forgotten Ally* (2013). [source: MIT; WebSearch: https://historynet.com/wwii-book-review-forgotten-ally/; https://www.business-standard.com/article/beyond-business/the-allies-forgotten-ally-113091701096_1.html]

## 8. The Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere
- Konoe and Arita proclaimed a "New Order in East Asia" (Japan, Manchukuo, China) on 3 November 1938; Foreign Minister Arita Hachirō coined "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere" in a radio address on 29 June 1940; Konoe extended it to Southeast Asia on 1 August 1940. [source: GEA]
- Slogans: "Asia for the Asiatics", liberation from Western colonialism; but the secret 1943 *Investigation of Global Policy with the Yamato Race as Nucleus* placed Japan at the top; spokesmen called the Sphere a device for the "development of the Japanese race". [source: GEA]
- After the US oil and steel embargo, Japan attacked British and Dutch colonies 7–19 December 1941 for raw materials. [source: GEA]
- Greater East Asia Conference, Tokyo, 5–6 November 1943 (English the common language): Tōjō, Zhang Jinghui (Manchukuo), Wang Jingwei, Ba Maw (Burma), José P. Laurel (Philippines), Prince Wan Waithayakon (Thailand), and Subhas Chandra Bose (Azad Hind; observer). No Indonesians were invited. Mainly propaganda; no practical plans. [source: GEAC; GEA; DEI]
- Many welcomed the Japanese at first, but the occupiers' brutality led many to regard Japan as "worse than their former colonial rulers". [source: GEA]
- Filipinos joked it should be "prosperity-ko" (*ko* = "mine"). [source: GEA]

## 9. Occupied Southeast Asia
### Indonesia (Dutch East Indies)
- Invasion began 10 January 1942. Sukarno and Hatta had foreseen that a Japanese advance might help independence. The Japanese 16th Army in Java gave Sukarno a Central Advisory Board; formed PETA (Defenders of the Homeland, October 1943) and the Jawa Hōkōkai (1944); Koiso promised independence "in the future" on 7 September 1944; BPUPK (independence preparatory committee) established 29 April 1945 and drafted a constitution; Rear-Admiral Maeda Tadashi funded Sukarno–Hatta tours. Sukarno proclaimed independence on 17 August 1945. [source: DEI; SUK]
- *Rōmusha* ("paid conscripted labourer"): the US Library of Congress estimates 4–10 million in Java were forced to work; about 10% were women; Ricklefs estimates 200,000–500,000 Javanese sent off Java, only 70,000 surviving; Shigeru Satō estimates about 270,000 sent off Java, 135,000 repatriated, with about 15% dead or stranded. Also unpaid *kinrōhōshi* labour (about 200,000 in 1944). Used on the Burma–Thailand and Sumatra railways. [source: ROM]
- Death toll: "a later UN report" states 4 million died in Indonesia from famine and forced labour, including about 30,000 European internees and soldiers; ROM adds about 2.4 million famine deaths in Java 1944–45. [source: DEI; ROM] (Single, loosely sourced figure; present as "a later UN report estimated".)
- Resistance: Singaparna (Tasikmalaya) uprising February 1943 under Kiai Zainal Mustafa (86 Japanese and 153 villagers died); Cirebon rice-quota revolts 1944; Supriyadi's PETA mutiny at Blitar, February 1945; the Aceh *ulama* revolt November 1942. [source: DEI]
- Memory: Suharto discouraged public discussion of Japanese war crimes; the 1973 film *Romusha* and the 1974 Malari riots against Japan. [source: DEI]
- Dutch study: of 200–300 European women in military brothels, "some sixty five were most certainly forced into prostitution". [source: DEI]
### The Philippines
- Invaded 8 December 1941, ten hours after Pearl Harbor; Bataan fell 9–10 April 1942, Corregidor 6 May. Most of the elite served under the Japanese; independence declared October 1943 under José P. Laurel's Second Republic, "ineffective and unpopular". [source: PHL; GEA]
- Bataan Death March from 9/10 April 1942: about 72,000–78,000 POWs (about 66,000 Filipino, 12,000 American); 5,000–18,000 Filipino and 500–650 American deaths before Camp O'Donnell. [source: BDM]
- Resistance: 277 guerrilla units, some 260,715 people; Hukbalahap (People's Anti-Japanese Army) under Luis Taruc armed about 30,000. [source: PHL]
- Manila, February–March 1945: at least 100,000 civilians killed during the battle from all causes, including massacres by Japanese (mainly naval) troops under Iwabuchi Sanji; Yamashita hanged 23 February 1946. [source: MNL]
- About 527,000 Filipinos killed from all causes (131,000–164,000 in 72 war-crime events). [source: PHL]
### Malaya and Singapore
- Singapore fell 15 February 1942. *Sook Ching* ("purge through cleansing"), 18 February – 4 March 1942: Kenpeitai screening of Chinese men aged 18–50, with killings at Changi, Punggol and elsewhere; extended into Malaya (up to 25,000 Chinese estimated killed in Johor; Kota Tinggi 2,000 on 28 February). [source: SOOK; MAL]
- Death toll: Japan claimed no more than about 6,000 (Kawamura Saburō's diary: 5,000 reported by 23 February); Lee Kuan Yew "about 70,000" (including Malaya); infobox consensus 40,000–50,000; historians' retrospective range 25,000–50,000 in Singapore plus about 20,000 in Malaya. Lt-Col. Hishakari Takafumi testified to an order to kill 50,000. [source: SOOK]
- The Chinese community in Malaya was forced to raise M$50 million "as atonement"; only $28 million raised, the rest by loan. [source: MAL]
- In 1947 a British court sentenced Kawamura and Oishi Masayuki to death; Tsuji Masanobu, the planner, escaped trial. In 1966 Japan agreed S$50 million (half grant, half loan) as a "gesture of atonement", avoiding the word reparations; no official apology. The Civilian War Memorial dates from 1963. [source: SOOK]
- Malayan resistance: Malayan Peoples' Anti-Japanese Army (MPAJA), mainly Chinese and communist; Batu Caves raid 1 September 1942 after the double agent Lai Teck's tip. Malay nationalists of Kesatuan Melayu Muda (Ibrahim Yaacob) and the Indian Independence League/INA (40,000 by 1 September 1942) cooperated with Japan via Fujiwara Iwaichi. Japan transferred Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan and Terengganu to Thailand (1943). [source: MAL]
### Burma and the Burma–Thailand Railway
- Aung San and the Thirty Comrades trained by the Japanese (Minami Kikan, Colonel Suzuki Keiji); Burma Independence Army formed December 1941; nominal independence 1 August 1943 under Ba Maw (Aung San war minister). Disillusioned, Aung San and communists and socialists formed the Anti-Fascist Organisation in August 1944; the Burma National Army rose against Japan on 27 March 1945. Civilian deaths under occupation 170,000–250,000. Kalagong massacre, July 1945 (about 600 villagers). [source: BUR]
- Railway: 415 km Ban Pong–Thanbyuzayat, completed October 1943 (lines met 17 October); at least 250,000 Southeast Asian civilians forced to work (another passage: over 180,000), more than 90,000 civilians died, as did about 12,000 Allied POWs; about 90,000 Burmese and 75,000 Malayans worked on it; more than 100,000 Malayan Tamils, about 60,000 died (MAL gives "150,000 Tamils" killed — conflicting; use BRW's). No reparations for Southeast Asian labourers; 800 Koreans among the guards. [source: BRW; MAL]
### Vietnam: the famine of 1944–45
- Northern Vietnam (Tonkin), October 1944 – late 1945, under Japanese occupation and Vichy French administration until the Japanese coup of 9 March 1945 (then Trần Trọng Kim's Empire of Vietnam). Deaths estimated 400,000 to 2 million; twenty provinces reported 380,000 starvation deaths by May 1945; a French officer estimated half a million; Ho Chi Minh's independence proclamation of 2 September 1945 cited 2 million. [source: VNF]
- Causes: compulsory French rice purchases at fixed low prices, Japanese and French requisitioning, forced cultivation of jute and industrial crops, rice and maize burned as fuel, US bombing of transport, and the floods of August–September 1944. The Viet Minh led seizures of granaries and grew into a mass movement. [source: VNF] (VNF carries an "original research" tag and many "citation needed" tags; give the range, the multiple causes, and Gunn's book as the authority.)

## 10. The "comfort women" system
- First comfort stations: Shanghai 1932 (Okamura Yasuji ordered them after mass rapes); after Nanjing the army generalised the system; justifications were preventing rape (and anti-Japanese feeling), venereal disease and leaks of secrets. [source: CW]
- Origins: Korea, China, the Philippines, Taiwan, Burma, Thailand, Indochina, Malaya, Manchukuo, the Dutch East Indies, Timor, New Guinea and others; Japanese women (some already prostitutes) were the first recruited. [source: CW]
- Numbers (range and debate): "most historians" 20,000–200,000; Japanese historians 20,000–200,000; Yoshimi about 200,000 in about 2,000 stations; Su Zhiliang 360,000–410,000, half Chinese; Hata 10,000–20,000 (earlier about 90,000, per Chunghee Sarah Soh). Yoshimi's 1940 venereal-treatment sample: 51.8% Korean, 36% Chinese, 12.2% Japanese. Many records were burned in 1945. [source: CW; YOS]
- Kim Hak-sun came forward in August 1991, the first Korean survivor to testify publicly, after the Japanese government denied state involvement in a June 1991 Diet session. [source: CW]
- January 1992: Yoshimi published Defense Agency documents (e.g. the 4 March 1938 notice "Concerning the Recruitment of Women for Military Comfort Stations") showing military involvement; Chief Cabinet Secretary Katō Kōichi apologised (14 January 1992); Prime Minister Miyazawa apologised in Seoul (17 January 1992). [source: CW; YOS]
- Kono Statement, 4 August 1993 (see Quotations). [source: KONO]
- Asian Women's Fund (1995–2007): privately funded payments with a signed prime-ministerial apology; 61 Korean, 13 Taiwanese, 211 Filipino and 79 Dutch women received it; many Korean survivors rejected it as unofficial; dissolved 31 March 2007. [source: CW]
- Abe stated on 1 March 2007 that there was no evidence the government had kept sex slaves; Abe confirmed in March 2014 that he would not revise the Kono Statement; a 2014 review team (including Hata) reported on its drafting. [source: CW; KONO]
- 28 December 2015 Abe–Park agreement declared the issue resolved "finally and irreversibly"; Japan gave ¥1 billion to a foundation, which the Moon Jae-in government dissolved in 2019. [source: CW; KJA]
- The Asahi Shimbun retracted (August 2014) articles based on Seiji Yoshida's claims of abducting women; it said this did not weaken the evidence for the system. [source: CW]
- The Tokyo Trial did not address sexual enslavement; in 2007 Yoshimi and Hayashi Hirofumi found Tokyo Trial documents showing coercion of women from Indonesia, Indochina and China. [source: CW]
- Testimony of Kim Hak-sun (1991) — see Quotations.
- US House Resolution 121, 30 July 2007. [source: KOR]

## 11. Pearl Harbor, the Pacific war and the end (keep short)
- Attack at 7:48 a.m. Hawaiian time, 7 December 1941 (8 December in Tokyo), 353 aircraft from six carriers under Nagumo Chūichi, planned by Yamamoto Isoroku; all eight US battleships damaged, four sunk; US dead about 2,400 (2,008 sailors, 109 Marines, 218 soldiers plus civilians). Simultaneous attacks on the Philippines, Guam, Wake and British territories. [source: PH]
- Firebombing: 64 Japanese cities; Tokyo, 9–10 March 1945, about 100,000 dead. Hiroshima 6 August, Nagasaki 9 August 1945; 150,000–246,000 killed (Hiroshima 90,000–166,000; Nagasaki 60,000–80,000). Soviet declaration of war 8 August, invasion of Manchuria; surrender signed 2 September 1945. [source: ABHN; SSJW; MAN]

## 12. Postwar reckoning
### Tokyo Trial
- Convened 29 April 1946; judges from eleven countries (including India and the Philippines); 28 defendants (Class A). Judgment read 4–12 November 1948; seven hanged at Sugamo on 23 December 1948 (Doihara, Hirota, Itagaki, Kimura, Mutō, Tōjō, Matsui); sixteen life sentences, all survivors paroled 1952–58; Kishi Nobusuke among 42 suspects released untried. Hirohito was not prosecuted or called; crimes against Koreans and Taiwanese, and Japan's strategic bombing, were not prosecuted. [source: IMTFE]
- Separate opinions: Webb (Australia) on Hirohito's responsibility; Bernard (France): the war "had a principal author who escaped all prosecution"; Röling (Netherlands) wanted neutrals and Japanese on the bench; Pal (India) dissented completely. [source: IMTFE]
- Lesser trials: more than 5,700 charged with conventional war crimes in about fifty locations; 984 sentenced to death; China held 13 tribunals (504 convictions, 149 executions). [source: IMTFE]
### Radhabinod Pal
- Indian jurist (27 January 1886 – 10 January 1967), born in Bengal (now Bangladesh); the only judge who found all defendants not guilty; opposed retroactive application of crimes against peace and the conspiracy charge; his dissent ran to 1,235 pages and was banned until the occupation ended in 1952; it became a basis for Japanese nationalist arguments; monuments to him stand at Yasukuni and the Kyoto Ryozen Gokoku Shrine. Pal also wrote that the evidence of atrocities was "overwhelming". Ashis Nandy links the dissent to Pal's legal training and to "Asian solidarity". Manmohan Singh (2005) and Abe (meeting Pal's son, 2007) invoked it. [source: PAL; IMTFE]
### Apologies and compensation
- Murayama Statement, 15 August 1995 (see Quotations). Koizumi repeated its language (2005) but his Yasukuni visits worsened relations; Yasukuni enshrines 14 convicted Class A war criminals. [source: MURA]
- Japan–ROK treaty 1965: Park Chung-hee's government sought $364 million for forced labour; Japan provided $800 million in grants and loans, which Park spent on development. [source: CW]
- Singapore: 1966 "gesture of atonement" (§9). [source: SOOK]
### Textbook controversies
- Postwar private authorship with Ministry of Education screening (from 1947). Tokushi Kasahara identifies three government challenges to textbooks: 1955 ("Deplorable Textbooks" booklets), early 1980s, and from 1997. [source: TXT]
- Ienaga Saburō (1913–2002) sued the state from 12 June 1965; in the third suit the Supreme Court on 29 August 1997 upheld the screening system as constitutional but found illegal censorship of passages on Unit 731 and rape at Nanjing, awarding ¥400,000. [source: IEN]
- 1982: the *Asahi* reported (26 June) that the Ministry made a textbook change "invaded" (*shinryaku*) to "advanced" (*shinshutsu*) in North China; China protested; Miyazawa's statement of 26 August 1982 led to the "Neighbouring Country Clause" (November 1982). TXT states that September 1982 investigations found that particular change had not actually been made. [source: TXT]
- 2001: the *New History Textbook* (Japanese Society for History Textbook Reform) was approved; it was used by only 0.039% of junior high schools as of August 2001; protests in China and Korea, 2005. By the late 1990s most textbooks mentioned Nanjing, Unit 731 and the comfort women. 2007 Okinawa rally (over 100,000) against removing the military's role in mass suicides. [source: TXT]
- Daniel Sneider's comparative studies: Japanese textbooks mostly factual "dry chronology"; Chinese and Korean texts nationalist; US texts myth-make too. [source: TXT]

## Debates
- **Colonial modernity vs colonial exploitation (Korea, Taiwan).** Korean nationalist historiography frames 1910–45 as repression ("colonial plunder") and resistance. Against it, a "colonial modernisation" view: Atul Kohli argues that Japanese rule built a highly penetrating, production-oriented state whose "Japanese lineage" South Korea's developmental state under Park Chung-hee fell back into; KOR also cites Randall Jones ("laid the foundation for future growth in several respects"). Critics argue that postwar change came partly from reversing Japanese agrarian and education policies, and that continuity arguments carry a "technocratic bias". Shin and Robinson's *Colonial Modernity in Korea* (1999) tries to escape both by asking how modernity was produced and used for colonial domination. Mark Caprio: Japanese policy "systematically excluded the majority of Koreans" from these institutions; Japanese conservative nationalists who stress development "neglect" coercion. In Taiwan the same quarrel runs through the very naming of the period ("rule" vs "occupation"). [source: KOR; https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/statedirected-development/colonial-origins-of-a-modern-political-economy-the-japanese-lineage-of-koreas-cohesivecapitalist-state/050481F07C128F75C77D8093F09EB71A; https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/wdevel/v25y1997i6p867-881.html (1997 *World Development* critique, authors not verified); TWN]
- **Was Japanese imperialism like the West's?** Myers and Peattie's volume set it in global colonialism, neither justifying nor condemning; Duus calls Meiji imperialism "backward imperialism", shaped by a sense of inferiority to the West, and stresses the joining of economic penetration ("abacus") and force ("sword"); Kohli stresses that Japan's colonialism was more intense and "architectonic" than European colonialism because Japan was itself a late developer. Japanese propaganda framed expansion as anti-colonial liberation, and Pankaj Mishra reads the 1943 conference as a moment of Asian assertion against white rule, while Ba Maw himself condemned the militarists' imposition and Willard Elsbree found no "real unity of interests" with the nationalists. [source: WebSearch: https://muse.jhu.edu/book/75892; Duus JAS review URL above; Kohli Cambridge URL above; GEA; GEAC]
- **Collaboration or nationalism?** Sukarno and Hatta, Aung San and Ba Maw, Laurel, Bose, Ibrahim Yaacob worked with Japan; the occupation trained armies (PETA, BIA/BNA) and political structures that fed postwar independence, yet the same nationalists (Aung San in 1945, PETA's Supriyadi) turned on Japan, and Indonesia remembered rōmusha suffering. Present as a tension: occupation as accelerant of decolonisation vs occupation as exploitation "worse than their former colonial rulers". [source: DEI; BUR; GEA; MAL]
- **Nanjing numbers.** Askew's "Great Massacre" vs "Illusion" schools, with Hata as "centrist": Chinese official 300,000; IMTFE 200,000+; Kasahara and Wakabayashi 100,000–200,000; Hata up to 40,000; deniers reject it. Hata's 1997 Princeton clash with Iris Chang; Hata nonetheless praised by Jansen ("the most reasonable of many Japanese studies"). The 300,000 figure is symbolically central in China; some Japanese scholars call it exaggeration. [source: NAN; HATA]
- **"Comfort women": coercion and numbers.** Yoshimi (documents; about 200,000; military responsibility) vs Hata (10,000–20,000; "none of the comfort women were forcibly recruited"; criticised by other Japanese scholars for minimising hardship; Soh says he lowered his estimate for political reasons). Su Zhiliang's 360,000–410,000 adds Chinese victims. Chizuko Ueno warns that "no positive sources" arguments ignore the mass destruction of documents in 1945. Soh complicates the "paradigmatic narrative" by adding Korean patriarchy and recruiters, and criticises the Korean Council's "canonized story" — and is herself criticised. Government level: Kono (1993) vs Abe (2007), the 2014 review and the 2015 "final and irreversible" agreement later hollowed out. [source: CW; KONO; YOS; HATA; WebSearch: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/413038]
- **Victors' justice?** Pal's dissent (no retroactive crimes against peace; colonialism and the atomic bombs ignored) and Röling's reflections vs the majority judgment; Pal adopted by Japanese nationalists (Yasukuni monument) though he called the atrocity evidence "overwhelming"; Nandy's reading; Bernard's objection to the emperor's absence; the US immunity for Unit 731 and Asaka as the other side of "victors' justice". [source: IMTFE; PAL; U731; NAN]
- **Chinese losses and "forgotten" China.** Westad's about 14 million vs Mitter (Westad's figures conservative) vs PRC 20 million+; Mitter's case that China's war began in 1937 and was central to Allied victory, and that the Cold War buried it. [source: SSJW; MIT; Mitter review URLs above]
- **Three Alls as genocide?** McCormack (merits consideration), Paine ("war of annihilation"), Wakabayashi (could qualify, but victims were killed for resisting). [source: TAP]
- **Cultural rule: reform or tighter control?** Cosmetic concessions vs Michael Shin's expansion-of-control argument, and the simultaneous flowering of Korean print culture. [source: MAR]
- **Sōshi-kaimei: forced or chosen?** 80.3% registered in six months under pressure; historians dispute who drove compulsion, and a minority stress voluntary adoption to escape discrimination. [source: SOS]
- **Taishō democracy: shallow or real?** Superficial-borrowing view vs deep-change view; Gordon's "imperial democracy" fusing popular rights with empire. [source: TAI]
