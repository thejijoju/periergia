## 1. The defeat of 1940 and the armistice
- Battle of France: 10 May – 25 June 1940. Germany committed 141 divisions and about 3.3 million troops; the Allies had 135 divisions. [source: BOF infobox]
- The German plan switched the main effort to the Ardennes and a Meuse crossing at Sedan (13 May). Ernest May (*Strange Victory*, 2000) credits Manstein, Guderian, Rundstedt, Halder and Hitler jointly. He calls the outcome a "triumph of intellect" that also needed "outrageous good luck", and he judges Allied intelligence analysis "abysmal". [source: BOF]
- Marc Bloch (1886–1944), historian and veteran of the battle, wrote *Strange Defeat* in 1940 (published 1946). He was later killed as a resister (FR lists him among intellectuals who died for the Resistance). [source: BOF; FR]
- French military losses: the French Defence Historical Service gives 85,310 killed (including 5,400 Maghrebis), 12,000 missing, 120,000 wounded and 1,540,000 prisoners (including 67,400 Maghrebis). Recent French research gives 55,000–85,000 killed. [source: BOF] Other figures in other articles: ARM says 92,000 French dead, and the BOF infobox note says about 60,000. Use "between about 55,000 and 90,000 French soldiers killed".
- Prisoners: BOF says 1,540,000 were taken to Germany in August 1940, of whom roughly 940,000 remained until 1945, 24,600 died in captivity and 71,000 escaped. POW says about 1.8 million were captured (about 10% of France's adult men). VF says "two million", and elsewhere "nearly 1,800,000". ARM says "nearly 1,000,000" spent five years in camps. Use "about 1.6–1.8 million captured; close to a million still held in 1945". [source: BOF; POW; VF; ARM]
- Colonial prisoners were held in camps in France ("Frontstalags"), not in Germany, in poor conditions shaped by Nazi racial ideology. [source: POW; VF]
- Massacres of Black colonial soldiers in 1940: at least 3,000 Senegalese tirailleurs were murdered after capture (BOF). Germans separated white and Black prisoners and shot the Black ones, for example at Erquinvillers (9 June) and at Chasselay near Lyon (19 June 1940, about 50 machine-gunned by tanks, an incident photographed by the perpetrators). [source: BOF; TIR]
- The government left Paris and moved to Bordeaux on 10 June, declaring Paris an open city. Italy entered the war on 10 June. [source: ARM; VF]
- Reynaud wanted to fight on from North Africa; Pétain and Weygand demanded an armistice. Reynaud resigned, and President Albert Lebrun appointed the 84-year-old Pétain prime minister. The date is **16 June** in VF, ARM and PET's lead, but PET's narrative says Lebrun accepted the resignation on 17 June. Use 16 June. [source: VF; ARM; PET]
- On 16 June the cabinet's vote was uncertain: ten ministers wanted to fight on, seven favoured an armistice, and eight were undecided but swung to an armistice. [source: PET]
- Pétain's radio broadcast of 17 June 1940 announced that the fighting must stop, before Germany had even agreed to negotiate (see Quotations). [source: https://lessentieldeleco.fr/1638-17-juin-1940-petain-et-le-discours-de-la-honte/ ; https://www.defense.gouv.fr/chemins-memoire/histoire-memoires/ressources-historiques/seconde-guerre-mondiale/operations-militaires/1939-1940-1 (search summaries)]
- The armistice was signed at 18:36 on 22 June 1940 in the Compiègne forest, in the same railway carriage as the 1918 armistice. Keitel signed for Germany and General Charles Huntziger led the French delegation. Hitler sat in Foch's chair, heard the preamble and left. The ceasefire took effect at 00:35 on 25 June, after a separate Franco-Italian armistice. Hitler had the site demolished and took the carriage to Berlin. [source: ARM]
- Terms: German occupation of about three-fifths of metropolitan France, north and west of a line through Geneva and Tours to the Spanish border, including every Channel and Atlantic port. France paid occupation costs of about 400 million francs a day; MBF gives 20 million Reichsmarks a day at a rate fixed at 1 RM = 20 FF, which comes to the same sum. The fleet was disarmed but not surrendered. France kept its empire. A small "Armistice Army" was allowed. Prisoners would stay prisoners until hostilities ended. [source: ARM; MBF; VF]
- Article 19, the "surrender on demand" clause: France had to hand over any German national the Germans asked for. Keitel said it was aimed at those who had "fomented the war", a euphemism for Jews, especially German Jewish refugees. [source: ARM]
- Armistice Army: 3,768 officers, 15,072 NCOs and 75,360 men, with no tanks. It was disbanded in November 1942. [source: VF]
- Hitler's reasons for an armistice: to stop France fighting on from North Africa, to take the fleet out of the war, to save himself the cost of administering France, and because Germany lacked the navy to seize the colonies. [source: ARM; VF]
- Mers-el-Kébir, 3 July 1940: the British attacked the French fleet near Oran and killed 1,297 French servicemen (VF: "over 1,300"). Vichy broke off relations with Britain on 8 July. [source: MEK; VF]

## 2. Zones and the demarcation line
- The *zone libre* covered 246,618 km² (about 45% of France) and held about 33% of the labour force. The demarcation line took effect on 25 June 1940 and crossed 13 of the 90 departments. [source: ZL; MBF]
- Alsace and Moselle were annexed de facto, and their men were conscripted into the Wehrmacht (the *malgré-nous*; about 40,000 became casualties). Nord and Pas-de-Calais were attached to the military administration in Brussels. A coastal *zone interdite* and a north-eastern "forbidden zone" were also set up; refugees were barred from returning to the latter, which was intended for German settlement. [source: MBF]
- There was a small Italian zone in the Alps. After 11 November 1942 Italy held everything east of the Rhône except Lyon and Marseille, plus Corsica. In the Italian zone Jews were generally spared until September 1943. [source: MBF; HF]
- Crossing the line required an *Ausweis*. Cross-line mail was limited to pre-printed family cards. The occupied zone kept German time, one hour ahead. [source: MBF]
- Census of 1 April 1941: 25.07 million people in the occupied zone and 14.2 million in the unoccupied zone, not counting POWs or Alsace-Lorraine. [source: MBF]
- Case Anton, 11 November 1942: in response to Torch, Germany and Italy occupied the southern zone, which was renamed *zone sud*. [source: ZL; MBF; PET]

## 3. Vichy: the regime and the National Revolution
- Vichy (Allier) was chosen as a spa town with many hotels. Lyon was ruled out because of Mayor Herriot's republican associations; Bordeaux lay in the occupied zone. It was never officially declared the capital. [source: VF]
- 10 July 1940: the National Assembly voted full powers to Pétain, including the power to write a new constitution, by **569 to 80**. Abstentions were 20 according to VF and 18 according to PET. The 80 became known as the "Vichy 80". Twenty-seven deputies and senators were absent because they had sailed on the *Massilia* to North Africa. [source: VF; PET]
- Constitutional Acts of 11–12 July 1940: Pétain became "head of the French State" (*chef de l'État français*) and Laval his designated successor. The motto *Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité* was replaced by *Travail, Famille, Patrie*. [source: VF]
- Laval, 6 July 1940: "parliamentary democracy has lost the war; it must disappear, ceding its place to an authoritarian, hierarchical, national and social regime." [source: VF]
- Legitimacy: Free France and every postwar government called Vichy illegal. The ordinance of 9 August 1944 declared its constitutional acts void *ab initio*. Julian Jackson argues that at the start "Vichy was both legal and legitimate": over 40 countries recognised it, including the US, Canada and China. [source: VF]
- The National Revolution's features (NR): anti-parliamentarism; the Pétain cult (his portrait everywhere, and the song *Maréchal, nous voilà !*); corporatism and a Labour Charter that abolished free trade unions and the right to strike; a "return to the land"; clericalism; state antisemitism; persecution of the "Anti-France" (Maurras's term for "Protestants, Jews, Freemasons and foreigners"). NR: "None of these changes was forced on France by Germany." [source: NR; VF]
- Joan of Arc replaced Marianne as the national symbol. Vichy promoted motherhood (Mother's Day medals), tightened divorce, restricted married women's employment and opposed birth control and abortion. [source: VF]
- Charles Maurras called Pétain's accession a "divine surprise". [source: VF]
- The Riom Trial (1942–43) was Vichy's attempt to blame Third Republic leaders for the defeat. Reynaud was arrested in September 1940. [source: NR; VF]
- Anglophobia was central to Vichy propaganda, which exploited Dunkirk, Mers-el-Kébir and Dakar. [source: VF]
- Laval was dismissed and arrested on 13 December 1940 and replaced by Flandin, then Darlan. He returned as head of government on 18 April 1942 and held the post until August 1944. [source: LAV]
- Pétain stayed popular: Richard Griffiths writes of "tremendous" enthusiasm in 1940, and large crowds cheered him in Paris on 28 April 1944. [source: PET]

## 4. State collaboration
- Montoire, 24 October 1940: Pétain met Hitler and they shook hands. The meeting was arranged by Laval, and the photograph was used in Nazi propaganda. [source: VF; PET]
- 30 October 1940: Pétain on the radio: "J'entre aujourd'hui dans la voie de la collaboration" (see Quotations). [source: VF]
- Laval's radio speech of 22 June 1942 said he "wished for a German victory" because otherwise "Bolshevism [would] establish itself everywhere". [source: LAV; VF]
- Paxton: Vichy was "not a puppet state, but rather an autonomous government". Sarah Fishman sums up the historiography: Vichy was neither a puppet nor a caretaker regime, nor was it monolithic. Norman Davies says it was "formed by French politicians and by French choices." [source: VF]
- Historians distinguish state collaboration from *collaborationnistes*, ideological fascists in Paris such as Doriot (PPF), Déat and Brasillach. Stanley Hoffmann used the term in 1974. The Legion of French Volunteers Against Bolshevism (LVF) later fed into the SS Charlemagne division. [source: VF]
- Paris Protocols, 28 May 1941 (Darlan): Germany got access to bases in Syria. The protocols were never ratified, but German and Italian aircraft did refuel in Syria. [source: SYR]
- France provided an estimated 42% of all "foreign aid" to the German economy. The Germans took about 20% of French food production according to VF (MBF says 80%; see conflicts), plus half the meat. [source: VF; MBF]
- Bousquet's GMR police units became the core of the postwar CRS riot police. Carrel's eugenics foundation became INED. [source: VF]

## 5. The persecution of Jews and the Vél d'Hiv
- Jewish population: VF gives about 350,000 in 1940, fewer than half of them citizens, and about 200,000 in the Paris area. HF gives a peak of 340,000 and "approximately 330,000" in 1939. (The Holocaust notes use 340,000.) [source: VF; HF]
- 15,000 people, mostly Jews, were denaturalised between June 1940 and August 1944 after a review of naturalisations since 1927. [source: VF]
- German ordinance of 27 September 1940 (VDH; VF gives 21 September): Jews in the occupied zone had to register. Nearly 150,000 did so in the Seine department. The French police built the "fichier Tulard", which was later handed to the Gestapo (Dannecker). [source: VDH; VF]
- **First *Statut des Juifs*, 3 October 1940**: it defined the "Jewish race" and barred Jews from the civil service, the army, teaching, the press, entertainment and some professions. Raphaël Alibert drafted it. A document found in 2010 shows Pétain made it harsher in his own hand. Article 9 applied it to Algeria, the colonies, Tunisia, Morocco and the mandates. It was published with the 4 October law in the *Journal officiel* on 18 October 1940. It was Vichy's own initiative. [source: SJ; HF; PET]
- 4 October 1940 law: prefects could intern foreign Jews (camps such as Gurs, Rivesaltes, Les Milles). [source: SJ; HF; VF]
- Operation Bürckel, 22–23 October 1940: about 6,500 Jews from Baden and the Saar-Palatinate were dumped into the unoccupied zone, and Vichy interned them at Gurs and elsewhere. [source: HF]
- Commissariat-General for Jewish Affairs (CGQJ), 29 March 1941: Xavier Vallat ran it, then Darquier de Pellepoix from May 1942. [source: VF]
- **Second *Statut*, 2 June 1941** (published 14 June 1941): it replaced the first, extended the registration of Jews nationwide and in Vichy North Africa, and lengthened the list of banned occupations. [source: HF; SJ]
- Pétain's chief of staff, quoted in FR: "Germany was not at the origin of the anti-Jewish legislation of Vichy. That legislation was spontaneous and autonomous." [source: FR]
- "Green ticket" round-up, 14 May 1941: 3,747 foreign Jewish men were arrested and sent to Pithiviers and Beaune-la-Rolande. 20 August 1941: more than 4,000 Jews were arrested in the 11th arrondissement, which opened Drancy. [source: VDH; DRA]
- First deportation convoy: 27 March 1942, Drancy (Paris) to Auschwitz. Yellow star made compulsory in the occupied zone (age 6 and up) from 29 May 1942; it was never required in the southern zone. [source: HF; VSY]
- **Vél d'Hiv round-up, 16–17 July 1942**: it was planned by Bousquet, Darquier, Dannecker and Knochen and carried out by about 9,000 French police, with a few hundred PPF volunteers. The Germans had planned for 22,000 arrests. Paris police records give **13,152 arrested**. The number of children is given as **4,115** (VDH lead; HF) or **4,051** (VDH body; VF), and women as 5,802 (VDH) or 5,082 (VF). Those arrested were held in the arena without food, water or sanitation, then sent to Drancy, Pithiviers and Beaune-la-Rolande, and from there to Auschwitz. [source: VDH; VF; HF]
- Children: Laval ordered children under 16 deported along with their parents, which the Germans had not asked for. Bousquet cancelled exemptions for children. Of the roughly 3,900 Vél d'Hiv children who were deported, none survived except six adolescents. The roundup was over a quarter of the 42,000 Jews sent from France to Auschwitz in 1942, of whom 811 returned. [source: VDH; LAV; BOU]
- Laval to Pastor Marc Boegner (Protestant leader): Boegner said "I talked to him about murder, he answered me with gardening." [source: VDH]
- August 1942: Bousquet's police arrested about 7,000 foreign Jews in the southern zone. Marseille, 22–24 January 1943: identity checks on 40,000 people and about 2,000 deported; the Old Port quarter (30,000 people) was expelled and then destroyed. [source: VF]
- Oberg–Bousquet accords (presented 8 August 1942) gave the French police formal "autonomy" in return for collaboration. [source: BOU]
- Drancy was run by the French police until 3 July 1943, then by the SS under Alois Brunner. More than 67,000 of the roughly 75,000 deported were sent from Drancy, in 64 convoys. The last convoy left Bobigny on 31 July 1944. The camp was liberated on 17 August 1944. [source: DRA; HF]
- Izieu, 6 April 1944: Klaus Barbie seized 44 Jewish children and 7 adults from a children's home. None of the children survived. [source: DRA]
- **Totals (give with sources):** the Klarsfeld memorial counts about 76,000 Jews deported from France; one summary says more than 75,700, with 2,564 survivors (VF: 2,566). About 3,000 more died in French camps and about 1,000 were shot. That gives a total of slightly under 77,500 dead, about a quarter of the Jews of France. Roughly a third of those deported (about 25,000 of 75,000) were French citizens. HF: "about 75,000" deported and 73,500 murdered; 75% of Jews in France survived. Holocaust notes: about 72,500 killed of more than 75,000. About 11,400 deported children, of whom perhaps 300 survived. [source: VF; HF; https://drancy.sites.northeastern.edu/the-shoah-in-france ; https://encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/klarsfeld-serge (search summaries)]
- Chirac's 1995 speech used the figure 76,000 (VF: "complicit in the deportation of 76,000 Jews"). [source: VF]
- Rescue: Le Chambon-sur-Lignon (Pastors André Trocmé and Édouard Theis; all 13 Protestant pastors there took part). HF says the town sheltered an estimated 800–1,000 Jewish children, and up to 5,000 refugees passed through. OSE saved 7,000–9,000 Jewish children. France has 4,303 Righteous Among the Nations (Holocaust notes). [source: CHA; HF; FR]
- Romani people were interned (Arc-et-Senans). About 5,000 Spanish Republicans deported from France died at Mauthausen. [source: VF]

## 6. Daily life, rationing, STO
- Shortages came from occupation costs, the artificial exchange rate, the blockade, fuel shortages and the absence of POW labour. Substitutes were everywhere: wood-gas cars, wooden shoe soles, barley-and-chicory "coffee", swedes and Jerusalem artichokes. [source: MBF]
- Official rations were starvation level: VF gives 1,013 or fewer calories a day, MBF 1,300 or fewer. A large black market grew up; the "BOF" (*beurre-œufs-fromage*) profiteers were resented. [source: VF; MBF]
- 800,000 POW wives managed alone on a modest allowance. [source: VF]
- About 85,000 children fathered by Germans had been born by October 1943 ("horizontal collaboration"). [source: FR]
- Allied bombing: nearly 75,000 French civilians killed and 550,000 tons of bombs dropped; France was the second most bombed country in the West after Germany. [source: MBF]
- ***Relève***, announced by Laval on 22 June 1942: one POW released for every three volunteer workers. Under it 80,000 POWs were released and 240,000 workers went to Germany. Fritz Sauckel, the "slavemaster of Europe", was the German labour plenipotentiary from 21 March 1942. [source: STO; LAV]
- Law of 4 September 1942: all men aged 18–50 and single women aged 21–35 were liable for any work the government required. **Law of 16 February 1943 creates the STO**: men born 1920–22 were required to work in Germany. Women were not generally called up. [source: STO]
- **600,000–650,000** French workers were sent to Germany between June 1942 and July 1944. France was the third-largest source of forced labour after the USSR and Poland. A further 250,000 POWs were "transformed" into civilian workers. French workers were 15% of Germany's labour force by August 1944 (VF). French conscripts were the only ones required to go by their own state's laws. [source: STO; VF]
- Nearly 200,000 men hid to avoid STO, and about a quarter of them joined the Resistance full-time. This is how the maquis began. [source: STO; FR]
- Speer–Bichelonne agreement, 15 September 1943: firms working for Germany were exempted from STO levies. [source: STO]

## 7. The Milice and the terror of 1943–44
- The Milice française was created on 30 January 1943, succeeding Darnand's SOL. Laval was its formal head and Joseph Darnand its effective leader; Darnand held an SS rank and swore an oath to Hitler. It had 25,000–30,000 members by 1944 (Julian Jackson and others). Its full-time armed wing was the *Franc-Garde*. Members were exempt from STO. [source: MIL; VF]
- It hunted resisters and helped round up Jews. In January 1944 it extended into the former occupied zone. The Battle of Glières (March 1944) was its largest operation, and it had to call in German troops. On 14 July 1944 it killed 34 rioting prisoners at La Santé. [source: MIL; HF]
- After resisters killed Philippe Henriot, Vichy's propaganda minister ("the French Goebbels"), on 28 June 1944, the Milice murdered Victor Basch and Georges Mandel. Paul Touvier had seven Jewish prisoners shot at Rillieux on 30 June 1944. [source: MIL; TOU]
- In August 1944 Pétain rebuked Darnand for "excesses". Darnand replied that he should have "thought of this before". [source: PET; MIL]
- Spring 1944 was a period of *la guerre franco-française* between Milice and maquis (Ian Ousby). [source: FR]
- Hostage policy: after the communist Pierre Georges ("Colonel Fabien") killed a German naval officer at Barbès métro on 21 August 1941, Stülpnagel's "Code of Hostages" followed. After killings in Nantes (20 Oct 1941) and Bordeaux, hostages were shot (FR: 50 + 50, and 98 in all elsewhere). [source: FR]
- Oradour-sur-Glane, 10 June 1944: an SS company of *Das Reich* killed 642 people (190 men, 247 women, 205 children). [source: ORA]

## 8. Resistance
### De Gaulle and Free France
- De Gaulle was a brigadier-general and under-secretary for war under Reynaud. He flew to London on 17 June. The Appeal of 18 June was a four-minute speech broadcast on the BBC at 10 pm BST and repeated four times the next day. The BBC did not keep a recording. [source: A18]
- Few French people heard it. Many recollections are "false memories". The 22 June speech was more widely heard and is often confused with it. Of 10,000 French citizens in Britain only 300 volunteered, and of more than 100,000 French soldiers temporarily in Britain only 7,000 stayed. [source: A18]
- "France has lost a battle, but has not lost the war" is from the poster *À tous les Français* (London, early August 1940), not the 18 June broadcast. A18 gives 3 August in one section and "5 August" in an image caption. [source: A18]
- Free France was renamed *France combattante* (Fighting France) on 13 July 1942. The French National Committee was formed in September 1941. CFLN in Algiers, 3 June 1943, with Giraud and de Gaulle as co-presidents. GPRF, 3 June 1944. The Allies recognised the GPRF on 23 October 1944. [source: FF; VF]
- Muracciole (2009): 73,300 men served in Free French forces from summer 1940 to the summer 1943 merger: 39,300 French, 30,000 colonial soldiers (mostly from sub-Saharan Africa) and 3,800 foreigners. [source: FF]

### Internal resistance and unification
- FR estimates organised resistance at 1–3% of the population. [source: FR]
- Early movements in the south: *Combat* (Henri Frenay), *Libération-Sud* (Emmanuel d'Astier de La Vigerie), *Liberté* (François de Menthon); in the north *Défense de la France* and others. Most began with underground newspapers. [source: FR]
- The early resistance included nationalists of the right: Marie-Madeleine Fourcade (Alliance), Colonel Rémy, and La Rocque's Klan network. Jean-Pierre Azéma coined *vichysto-résistant*. [source: FR]
- **Jean Moulin** (20 June 1899 – 8 July 1943): prefect of Eure-et-Loir in Chartres, dismissed by Vichy on 2 November 1940. He reached London and was parachuted into the Alpilles on 1 January 1942 (code names "Rex" and "Max") to unite the movements. He founded the **CNR**, which first met in Paris on 27 May 1943 with eight movements, two unions and six parties. He was arrested at Caluire (Lyon) on 21 June 1943 and tortured by Klaus Barbie. His death was registered at Metz station; its exact circumstances are uncertain. Who betrayed him (René Hardy and others) remains disputed. His ashes were moved to the Panthéon on 19 December 1964 with a speech by André Malraux. [source: JM; FR]
- CNR programme, 15 March 1944: an action plan plus postwar measures (universal suffrage, press freedom, social security). It fed postwar *dirigisme*. [source: JM; VF]
- **Communists**: the PCF was banned in 1939 after the Nazi–Soviet pact and followed the Comintern's anti-war line for the first year of the occupation. Some acted from May 1941, but organised armed resistance came after 22 June 1941. The FTP was formed early in 1942 from three groups (party members, young communists and foreign workers). By 1944 FTP strength was an estimated 100,000. After the war the PCF called itself *le parti des 75,000 fusillés*; FR: "close to 30,000" French of all tendencies were shot, "only a few thousand" of them communists. [source: FR; FTP]
- **Maquis**: rural bands, at first young men fleeing STO. The Vercors "Free Republic" was declared on 3 July 1944 and crushed by German assault by the end of July. More than 10,000 German troops were committed. [source: FR; VF]
- FFI: about 100,000 in June 1944 and 400,000 by October 1944. Eisenhower valued the Resistance at ten to fifteen divisions at the time of the landings. Losses: credible estimates start at 8,000 killed in action and 25,000 shot; 86,000 people were deported from France without racial motive. [source: FR]

### Foreigners and immigrants
- **FTP-MOI** (*main-d'œuvre immigrée*): the immigrant wing of the FTP, organised in Paris in 1941. Many members were Jewish, and there were Hungarians, Armenians, Poles, Italians and Spaniards. It reported via Jacques Duclos to the Comintern. The Paris group was led by Boris Holban, then by Joseph Epstein and Missak Manouchian (July–November 1943). In Toulouse the 35th Brigade "Marcel Langer". [source: MOI]
- **Missak Manouchian**: born 1909 in Adıyaman, a survivor of the Armenian genocide raised in an orphanage at Jounieh, Lebanon. He came to France in 1925. Registered birth year 1906; his notebooks found in 2023 show 1909. He was arrested on 16 November 1943 by French police. Twenty-three were tried before a German military court (from 17 February 1944). Twenty-two were shot at Mont-Valérien on 21 February 1944; Olga Bancic was beheaded at Stuttgart on 10 May 1944. Manouchian and his wife Mélinée were moved to the Panthéon on 21 February 2024, making him the first communist there. [source: MAN; MOI]
- ***Affiche rouge***: a red poster with ten of the men, their foreign names and their "crimes", headed "Des libérateurs? La libération par l'armée du crime!". About 15,000 copies were printed. People wrote *Morts pour la France* on them. [source: AFF; MOI]
- Spanish Republicans: about half a million fled to France in 1939. 120,000–150,000 remained in 1940. Up to 60,000 escaped from labour companies into the Resistance. The XIV Corps (about 3,400 by June 1944) became the AGE. About 350 Spaniards served in the 2e DB company "La Nueve" (9th company, 3rd battalion, RMT). [source: FR; FF]
- Jews were about 1% of the population but an estimated 15–20% of the Resistance (FR). The *Armée juive* was founded in 1942 and became the OJC in 1944, with about 400 members. [source: FR]

### Women
- Women were 15–20% of resisters and 15% of political deportees. They were couriers, typists, liaison agents and safe-house keepers. Only Marie-Madeleine Fourcade headed a network (Alliance). No woman led one of the eight major movements, a maquis or a Liberation Committee. Lucie Aubrac co-founded Libération-Sud. Germaine Tillion was in the Musée de l'Homme network. Madeleine Riffaud (FTP) shot a German NCO on 23 July 1944. [source: WFR; FR]
- Women's vote: VF says France gave women the vote "in 1944"; FR says de Gaulle granted it "in 1945". (Interwar chapter: "1944".) See conflicts.

## 9. The empire
- **Equatorial Africa first**: Chad rallied on 26 August 1940 under Governor **Félix Éboué**, followed by Cameroun (27 August), French Congo (29 August) and Ubangi-Shari (30 August). Gabon was taken by force, 27 October – 12 November 1940 (Leclerc, Koenig). [source: VF; EBO; FF]
- Éboué (26 Dec 1884 – 17 May 1944): born in Cayenne, French Guiana, grandson of slaves. He was the first Black man appointed to a senior colonial post (acting governor of Guadeloupe, 1936), became governor of Chad in 1939 and governor-general of French Equatorial Africa from 1940 to 1944. His *New Indigenous Policy* fed into the Brazzaville Conference of 1944. He was reburied in the Panthéon in 1949, the first Black man so honoured. [source: EBO]
- Brazzaville Manifesto and Empire Defence Council, 27 October 1940. Brazzaville served as Free France's capital from 1940 to 1942. [source: FF]
- Eric Jennings argues that Free France drew its early strength from French Equatorial Africa rather than London (1940 – mid-1943), and Florence Bernault stresses the forced labour and taxation imposed on Africans. [source: https://www.history.utoronto.ca/node/1149 ; Bernault review, Journal of African History (search summaries)]
- Dakar (Operation Menace), 23–25 September 1940: the Anglo–Free French attack was repelled. [source: VF; FF]
- Kufra, Libya: Leclerc's force from Chad, including the Régiment de Tirailleurs Sénégalais du Tchad, took the Italian fort in early 1941. Oath of Kufra, 2 March 1941 (see Quotations). Strasbourg was liberated on 22 November 1944. [source: LEC; MBF]
- Bir Hakeim, June 1942: Koenig's 1st Free French Brigade held off the Afrika Korps. [source: FF]
- Pacific and India: French India declared for the Allies. In Tahiti, a referendum of 2 September 1940 voted 5,564 to 18 to join Free France. New Hebrides (20 July 1940), New Caledonia (19 September 1940). The Bataillon du Pacifique fought at Bir Hakeim. [source: VF]
- **Colonial troops**: up to 200,000 tirailleurs served, about 9% of French forces (TIR also says 179,000 recruited, 40,000 of them deployed to western Europe). The French Expeditionary Corps in Italy was 60% colonial, mostly Moroccan; the 3rd Algerian and 2nd/4th Moroccan divisions were 60–65% Maghrebi. The Moroccan *goumiers* committed mass rapes in Italy (the *marocchinate*). The 9th Colonial Infantry Division fought from Toulon to the Swiss border, August–November 1944. [source: TIR; FF]
- ***Blanchiment***: for Paris, Allied commanders insisted on a white unit. Leclerc's 2e DB (10,500 French, 3,600 Maghrebis and about 350 Spaniards) had its Black African soldiers transferred out. Later, Black troops were replaced by white recruits on de Gaulle's order. LP says nearly two-thirds of the Free French army was African colonial troops. [source: LP; FF; TIR]
- **Thiaroye**, 1 December 1944: French troops shot repatriated tirailleurs, former POWs protesting unpaid wages. 35–300 were killed. In 2014 Hollande called it a "bloody repression", and in 2024 Macron called it a "massacre". [source: THI; TIR]
- **Indochina**: Admiral Jean Decoux governed for Vichy. Japanese troops entered on 22 September 1940 (Lạng Sơn incident) and controlled the whole colony by July 1941, with the French left administering it. In the Franco-Thai war (from October 1940) Japan imposed mediation and Thailand got territory. Ho Chi Minh returned in February 1941 and founded the Viet Minh in May 1941. The Japanese coup of 9 March 1945 ended French rule. The famine of 1944–45 killed 1–2 million ("up to 2 million"; WW2 notes: 400,000–2 million). Ho Chi Minh's letter of 26 September 1945 said the French had sold the Vietnamese to Japan twice in four years. [source: IND; VF]
- **Syria and Lebanon**: Operation Exporter, 8 June – 14 July 1941 (Convention of Acre), was fought by British, Australian, Indian and Free French forces against Dentz's Vichy army. Vichy figures give 6,352 casualties; 5,668 Vichy men defected to the Free French and 37,563 were repatriated to France. Catroux recognised Syrian and Lebanese independence in the name of Free France on 26 November 1941. Lebanon became independent on 22 November 1943. In 1945 French repression in Damascus led to British intervention (the Levant Crisis) and French withdrawal the next year. [source: SYR]
- **North Africa 1942**: Operation Torch, 8 November 1942. In Algiers a resistance coup of about 400 men neutralised the Vichy 19th Corps command for some hours (VF: "within 15 hours"). Vichy forces fought: TOR infobox gives 1,346+ Vichy dead (VF says Vichy killed 479 Allied troops; TOR gives 556 US and 574 UK dead). Darlan switched sides and was recognised as high commissioner. He was assassinated on 24 December 1942 by Bonnier de La Chapelle, and Giraud succeeded him. The Crémieux decree, restoring citizenship to Algerian Jews, was restored on 20 October 1943. [source: TOR; VF; FF; HF]
- Madagascar: the British took it, 5 May – 6 November 1942. [source: VF]
- Sétif, 8 May 1945: harsh French repression in Algeria on VE Day; some historians call it the "real beginning of the Algerian War". [source: VF]

## 10. Liberation and the épuration
- Normandy, 6 June 1944. Provence (Dragoon), 15 August 1944: de Lattre's 1st French Army, backed by over 25,000 maquisards. Most of France was liberated by September 1944. [source: ZL; FR]
- Liberation of Paris, 19–25 August 1944: an FFI rising (Colonel Henri Rol-Tanguy, a communist) after police and métro strikes, and a truce brokered by the Swedish consul Raoul Nordling. Choltitz surrendered on 25 August. Losses: about 1,600 resisters and 130 men of the French army killed; 3,200 Germans killed and 12,800 captured. De Gaulle spoke at the Hôtel de Ville on 25 August and led the parade on 26 August. [source: LP]
- Vichy leaders were taken to Sigmaringen (from 7 September 1944). The enclave had about 6,000 people, including Céline. [source: VF]
- ***Épuration sauvage*** (summary executions): the most-cited figure is about **10,000**. Jules Moch's inquiry counted 9,673; a 1952 inquiry gave 8,867 plus 1,955 of unknown motive, 10,822 in all. Robert Aron in 1960 said 40,000. Pétainist circles claimed 100,000. De Gaulle and mainstream historians accept about 10,000. VF: about 6,000 before the Liberation and 4,000 after (prefects' estimates, 1948 and 1952). [source: FR; VF]
- At Le Grand-Bornand on 24 August 1944 the FFI executed 76 captured miliciens (FR says "as many as 77"). [source: MIL; FR]
- **The *tondues***: 10,000–30,000 women had their heads shaved (FR). Fabrice Virgili estimates about 20,000 between 1943 and 1946 and argues many were not guilty of sexual relations with Germans. [source: FR; https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/85369 (review, search summary)]
- ***Épuration légale***: about 300,000 cases investigated, more than half closed without indictment. Between 1944 and 1951 the courts passed **6,763 death sentences**, 3,910 of them in absentia. Of the 2,853 passed in person about 73% were commuted, leaving **791 executions**. Nearly 50,000 people received *dégradation nationale* for the new offence of *indignité nationale* (ordinance of 26 August 1944). Amnesty laws followed in 1951 and 1953. [source: EPU] (FR: "over 700" legal executions; VF: about 120,000 condemned and 1,500 death sentences. See conflicts.) Administrative internment held 70,000–80,000 people at its peak in late 1944. [source: EPU]
- FR: amnesties cut imprisoned collaborators from 40,000 to 62. [source: FR]
- Pétain was tried from 23 July to 15 August 1945 and sentenced to death; de Gaulle commuted the sentence to life. He died on the Île d'Yeu on 23 July 1951, aged 95. Laval was tried in October 1945 in a rushed trial (Paxton and Geoffrey Warner criticise it), attempted suicide by poison, and was shot at Fresnes on 15 October 1945. Darnand and Brasillach were executed. [source: PET; LAV; EPU]
- Overall French dead about 580,000 (MBF; BOF). [source: MBF]

## 11. Memory
- **Gaullist and resistancialist myth**: de Gaulle's line from 1944 was that almost the whole nation had resisted apart from a few traitors (HF). Rousso coined *résistancialisme* for the inflated memory of the Resistance; he says it rose among Gaullists and communists after the war. [source: HF; TVS]
- The "sword and shield" thesis (Pétain the shield, de Gaulle the sword) was first developed by Robert Aron, *Histoire de Vichy* (1954). Rousso deconstructed it. Pétain used it at his trial. [source: VSY; HVF]
- *The Sorrow and the Pity* (Marcel Ophuls) is a two-part documentary about Clermont-Ferrand made for French TV in 1969. It was refused broadcast, shown in cinemas from 1971, and first shown on French TV in 1981. [source: SOR]
- **Paxton**: *Vichy France: Old Guard and New Order, 1940–1944* (1972; French translation 1973). It argued that Vichy sought collaboration, did not practise "passive resistance", and that its reforms were its own. It met "intense vitriol" in France: Admiral Auphan called Paxton a liar on TV in 1976. Gérard Noiriel says it "had the effect of a bombshell". The French speak of the "Paxtonian revolution". Marrus and Paxton, *Vichy France and the Jews* (1981), was the first book wholly on the subject. [source: PAX; HF]
- **Rousso**, *Le Syndrome de Vichy* (1987; English 1991). "Un passé qui ne passe pas". Four stages: unfinished mourning (*deuil inachevé*, to 1954); repression (*refoulement*, 1954–71); the "broken mirror" (Ophuls, Paxton; to 1974); then "obsession" (from 1974). He also coined *négationnisme*. [source: TVS; ROU]
- Government positions: Mitterrand said in September 1994: "I will not apologize in the name of France. The Republic had nothing to do with this." On 16 July 1995 Chirac recognised the responsibility of the French state. Hollande on 22 July 2012 said "in France, by France". Macron on 16 July 2017 said "It was indeed France that organised this". [source: VSY; HF; VF]
- Trials:
  - **Barbie**: head of the Lyon Gestapo, the "Butcher of Lyon". US Army intelligence (CIC) used him and helped him reach Bolivia in 1951; the US apologised to France in 1983. He was extradited in 1983, convicted in 1987 of crimes against humanity and sentenced to life (France had abolished the death penalty in 1981), and died in prison in Lyon in 1991. His lawyer Jacques Vergès tried to turn the trial towards Moulin's betrayal. [source: BAR; JM]
  - **Touvier**: a Milice officer sentenced to death in absentia. The death penalty lapsed under the statute of limitations in 1966, and Pompidou pardoned him in 1971, which caused an outcry. Georges Glaeser's complaint followed (1973). He was arrested on 24 May 1989 at an SSPX priory in Nice. In 1994 he became the first Frenchman convicted of crimes against humanity (for Rillieux) and died in 1996. [source: TOU; VF]
  - **Bousquet**: in 1949 he received five years' *dégradation nationale*, immediately lifted. He was later a friend of Mitterrand. Indicted in 1991, he was murdered by Christian Didier on 8 June 1993 before trial. [source: BOU; VF]
  - **Papon**: secretary-general of the Gironde prefecture, responsible for Jewish affairs. About 1,600 Jews were deported from Bordeaux in 12 trains (July 1942 – August 1944), including 130 children under 13. *Le Canard enchaîné* exposed him on 6 May 1981 while he was Giscard's budget minister. He was charged in 1983. His trial ran from 8 October 1997 to 2 April 1998, the longest in French history. He was convicted of complicity in crimes against humanity and sentenced to 10 years. He fled to Switzerland, was returned on 22 October 1999, released in 2002 on health grounds, and died in 2007. As Paris police prefect he ordered the 17 October 1961 massacre of Algerian demonstrators (PAP gives 200–300 killed). Paxton testified at the trial (31 October 1997). [source: PAP; VSY; PAX]

## Debates
- **Puppet or autonomous?** Paxton argues that Vichy was an autonomous government that sought collaboration and acted "in parallel" with Germany; Davies and Fishman broadly agree. Jackson stresses Vichy's concern to preserve sovereignty, which made French police do the arresting, and calls the persecution "an autonomous policy, with its own indigenous roots". [source: VF; VDH]
- **Was Vichy fascist?** Stanley Payne says "distinctly rightist and authoritarian but never fascist", and Olivier Wieviorka also says it was not fascist. Paxton says fascist elements were minor but sees it as fascist overall. Roger Griffin calls it "para-fascist". Zeev Sternhell calls it "no less fascist than Mussolini's Italy". H. R. Kedward sees at least its last two years as "a variant of fascism". Some historians stress the Milice period. [source: VF; NR]
- **Legality**: most French historians and every postwar government say the 10 July 1940 vote was illegal. Jackson argues that at the start Vichy was "both legal and legitimate". [source: VF]
- **"Shield" thesis and the survival rate**: Robert Aron (1954) presented Pétain as a shield, with a "good Vichy" (Pétain, 1940–42) and a "bad Vichy" (Laval, 1942–44). Laval's defenders and later Vichy apologists point to the relatively high survival rate of Jews in France, especially French citizens (LAV repeats such figures). Paxton, Marrus and Rajsfus reply that Vichy handed over foreign Jews without protecting French ones, that the toll was limited by wagon shortages, public reaction and the Italian zone, and that Laval added the children. Susan Zuccotti finds Vichy did prioritise deporting foreigners until at least 1943. [source: VSY; HVF; VF; LAV]
- **How big and how effective was the Resistance?** FR estimates 1–3% of the population. Eisenhower credited ten to fifteen divisions' worth, while historians "still debate" its military value. Paxton dated serious resistance to "well into 1941", which upset resistance veterans. [source: FR; PAX]
- **Who resisted? (Gildea)**: Gildea argues the story should be "resistance in France", restoring foreigners, Jews, Spanish Republicans and women. Robert Tombs reviewed it critically ("Occupation Hazards", *Literary Review*; argument not read). [source: Gildea search summaries above]
- **Free France's African base (Jennings)**: Jennings argues Free France was African before it was metropolitan, from 1940 to mid-1943. Critics such as Bernault stress the forced labour and taxes that supported it. [source: Jennings search summaries]
- **Memory (Rousso and his critics)**: Rousso's four stages and *résistancialisme*. Pierre Nora sees Rousso's generation as having "flipped the script" towards a France that was generally collaborationist, with a few heroes. Bertram Gordon debated how to find the "syndrome" in the historical record. [source: TVS]
- **Moulin's betrayal**: suspicion fell on René Hardy (tried and acquitted after the war). Vergès and Gérard Chauvy accused Raymond and Lucie Aubrac, and Patrick Marnham pointed at communists. Daniel Cordier's biography defended Moulin. None of this is settled. [source: JM]
- **The épuration: too harsh or too lenient?** Pétainist circles spoke of 100,000 killed and a "Red Terror"; Aron said 40,000; the consensus is about 10,000 summary executions. Others say France punished too few, noting its rate of legal executions was lower than in Belgium or Norway. Virgili treats the head-shavings as a gendered ritual of national renewal. [source: VF; FR; Virgili review]
