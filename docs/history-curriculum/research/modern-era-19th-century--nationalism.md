## 1. Romantic nationalism: Herder and Fichte
- Johann Gottfried Herder: born 25 August 1744 at Mohrungen (now Morąg, Poland) in the Kingdom of Prussia; died Weimar 18 December 1803. Studied at Königsberg under Kant; protégé of J. G. Hamann. [source: HER]
- Herder argued that true German culture was to be found among the common people (*das Volk*) and that folk songs, folk poetry and folk dances expressed the spirit of the nation. Wikipedia associates him with the term *Volksgeist*. [source: HER] (Caveat: HER's infobox uses *Volksgeist*, and the term is commonly attached to him. Whether Herder himself used that exact word is something I could not settle. Safer wording: "the spirit of a people".)
- *Treatise on the Origin of Language* (1772): language is "the natural organ of the understanding"; language and cultural tradition, rather than the state, bind a "nation". He inspired the Brothers Grimm's folk-tale collecting. [source: HER]
- 1773 essay on Ossian and the songs of ancient peoples (in a manifesto with Goethe and Möser): "A poet is the creator of the nation around him, he gives them a world to see and has their souls in his hand to lead them to that world." [source: HER]
- *Ideas for a Philosophy of the History of Mankind* (*Ideen zur Philosophie der Geschichte der Menschheit*), four volumes, 1784–1791. [source: HER]
- Herder was a pluralist, not a chauvinist: "every nation bears in itself the standard of its perfection, totally independent of all comparison with that of others". He warned that "national glory is a deceiving seducer". He opposed absolutism and Prussian nationalism and supported the French Revolution. One encyclopedia calls him "an anticolonialist cosmopolitan precisely because he is a nationalist". [source: HER]
- After a 1769 trip he predicted that Slavic nations would play a major role in Europe and hoped a "cultured nation" would awaken in Ukraine. He also speculated that the Hungarian nation might be absorbed into the surrounding Slavic cultures, a prediction still debated in Hungary. [source: HER]
- Folk-song collection: HER says the collection was published in 1773 as *Stimmen der Völker in ihren Liedern*. That is wrong. It appeared as *Volkslieder* in 2 volumes (Leipzig: Weygand, 1778–79). The title *Stimmen der Völker in Liedern* comes from the expanded posthumous edition of 1807 (Tübingen: Cotta), edited by Karoline Herder and Johannes von Müller. [source: https://catalog.folger.edu/record/355848 ; https://opendata.uni-halle.de/handle/1981185920/90353]
- Johann Gottlieb Fichte, *Addresses to the German Nation* (*Reden an die deutsche Nation*): delivered at the Prussian Academy of Sciences in Berlin in winter 1807–08 under French occupation, published 1808. It stressed the distinctiveness of the German language and traditions. [source: FIC; ROM]
- Prussia's defeat at Jena (1806) spurred German romantic nationalism. ROM argues that Fichte, Kleist and Arndt (poetry of the 1813–15 war) pushed German nationalism in an ethnic rather than civic direction. [source: ROM]
- Romantic "national epics" were collected or composed, especially among minorities in the Russian Empire: the Finnish *Kalevala*, the Estonian *Kalevipoeg*, the Latvian *Lāčplēsis*. Smetana's *Má vlast* ("Vltava") is a later example of musical nationalism. [source: ROM]

## 2. The revolutions of 1848 ("springtime of the peoples")
- A wave of revolutions from January 1848 to October 1849, the most widespread revolutionary wave in European history. German liberals called it the *Völkerfrühling*, "springtime of the peoples". [source: R48]
- It began with the Sicilian revolution in January 1848 and spread after the February Revolution in France. Over fifty countries were affected, with no significant coordination among the revolutionaries. Causes included liberalism, nationalism, demands for press freedom, workers' economic demands, and the 1845–47 food crisis (the potato failure) combined with an industrial recession. [source: R48]
- Uprisings followed within weeks in Munich, Vienna, Buda and Pest, Venice, Kraków, Milan and Berlin. Metternich resigned after fighting in Vienna on 13–15 March and went into exile. [source: R48]
- Lasting gains included the abolition of serfdom in Austria and Hungary. In Austria the Reichstag abolished serfdom by a patent of 7 September 1848, and Emperor Ferdinand freed the mostly Ruthenian serfs of Galicia in 1848. Other gains: the end of absolute monarchy in Denmark and representative democracy in the Netherlands. [source: R48]
- Nations clashed with each other: German against Pole in Posen, German against Dane in Schleswig (the First Schleswig War), German against Czech in Bohemia. The Hungarian Diet required voters to speak Hungarian, which disenfranchised non-Hungarians. Serbs of Vojvodina, Romanians of Transylvania and some Slovaks fought for the Habsburgs against the Hungarian revolutionaries. [source: R48]
- Frankfurt National Assembly: first sat on 18 May 1848 and was elected indirectly by near-universal manhood suffrage. On 27 March 1849 it passed a constitution and offered the imperial crown to Frederick William IV of Prussia, who refused it. Privately he likened the crown to "a dog collar, binding [him] to the revolution of '48". The Baden uprising, the last in Germany, capitulated on 23 July 1849. [source: R48; GER]
- Austrian counter-revolution: Windisch-Grätz bombarded Prague in June 1848. Radetzky defeated Piedmont at Custoza on 24 July 1848. Vienna was retaken on 31 October 1848, and the Frankfurt deputy Robert Blum was executed. Ferdinand abdicated in favour of Franz Joseph in December 1848. The Kremsier Reichstag was dissolved on 7 March 1849 and the March Constitution was imposed. [source: R48; ITA]
- Hungary: the revolution began on 15 March 1848 in Pest with the "12 points" and Sándor Petőfi's poem ("We swear by the God of the Hungarians; we swear, we shall be slaves no more"). Kossuth led the government. In 1849 Hungary declared independence and dethroned Franz Joseph. Russia intervened: R48 gives 200,000 Russian plus 175,000 Habsburg troops against about 170,000 Hungarians, while another passage of R48 says "over 300,000" Russian troops, so the figures conflict. Hungary surrendered on 13 August 1849, and Komárom held out until early October. Batthyány and the Thirteen Martyrs of Arad were executed. [source: R48]
- Lajos Kossuth: 19 September 1802 – 20 March 1894; died in exile in Turin. [source: KOS]
- Palacký and Austroslavism: in his "Letter to Frankfurt" Palacký refused to join the German pre-parliament because, as a Czech, he had no interest in German affairs. He presided over the Slavic Congress in Prague in June 1848 and advocated Austroslavism, a federal Austria with autonomy for its Slavic peoples. His *History of the Czech Nation in Bohemia and Moravia* appeared in 5 volumes, 1836–1867. He was called "Father of the Nation"; 14 June 1798 – 26 May 1876. [source: PAL; R48]
- Ukrainian (Ruthenian) movement: the Supreme Ruthenian Council was founded in Lviv/Galicia on 2 May 1848 and was headed by the Greek Catholic bishop Gregory Yakhimovich. [source: R48]
- Beyond Europe: New Granada (Colombia) under José Hilario López abolished slavery and the death penalty (from 1849); the Praieira revolt in Pernambuco, Brazil (1848–52); the Chilean revolution of 1851. [source: R48]
- Aftermath: about 4,000 exiles ("Forty-Eighters") went to the US. Historians long called 1848 a failure. Christopher Clark instead argues that it produced a "revolution in government". [source: R48]

## 3. Italy (the Risorgimento)
- *Risorgimento* literally means "rising again". [source: ITA]
- After 1815 the Congress of Vienna restored the old states. Austria ruled Lombardy–Venetia directly and dominated Parma, Modena and Tuscany. [source: ITA]
- Giuseppe Mazzini (born 22 June 1805, Genoa) founded Young Italy in 1831 in exile in Marseille. Its aim was "One, free, independent, republican nation" and its motto "God and the People". It had about 60,000 adherents in 1833. He founded Young Europe at Bern in spring 1834 with exiles from Italy, Poland and Germany. [source: MAZ]
- 1848–49: the Five Days of Milan (18–22 March 1848) expelled the Austrian garrison. Charles Albert of Piedmont-Sardinia declared war on Austria, was beaten at Custoza (24 July 1848) and Novara (March 1849), and abdicated. The Roman Republic (February 1849) fell to French troops on 29 June 1849 and the pope was restored. Venice surrendered in August 1849. [source: ITA; R48]
- Cavour (1810–1861): a modernizer who favoured railways, banks and free trade. He founded the newspaper *Il Risorgimento* and became prime minister of Piedmont-Sardinia in 1852. Piedmont joined Britain and France in the Crimean War (1855). [source: ITA]
- Lessons drawn after 1849, per ITA: expel Austria with French help, accept that the pope could not lead Italy, and build on Piedmont's monarchy because republicanism was too weak. [source: ITA]
- War of 1859: a secret alliance with Napoleon III (Plombières). Austria fielded 140,000 men against Sardinia's 70,000. Battles of Magenta (4 June) and Solferino. Armistice of Villafranca on 12 July 1859: Lombardy went to Sardinia, and Venetia stayed Austrian. In 1860 the central Italian duchies and Papal Legations were annexed (22 March 1860). Savoy and Nice were ceded to France by the Treaty of Turin (24 March 1860). [source: ITA]
- Garibaldi: born 4 July 1807 in Nice (then Piedmontese), died 2 June 1882 on Caprera. Sentenced to death after a Mazzinian rising in 1834, he spent 14 years in South America. He fought for the Riograndense (Ragamuffin) rebels in Brazil from 1835, where he met Anita (Ana Maria de Jesus Ribeiro), who fought alongside him. He commanded Uruguay's fleet and an Italian Legion (the first "Redshirts") in the Uruguayan Civil War, 1842–48; Montevideo had 4,205 Italians out of 30,000 people in an 1843 census. Known as the "Hero of the Two Worlds". [source: GAR]
- Expedition of the Thousand: sailed from Quarto near Genoa on 6 May 1860 and landed at Marsala, Sicily, on 11 May. The volunteers numbered 1,089 and carried obsolete muskets. Victory at Calatafimi (ITA says 13 May; EXP says 15 May, against about 3,000 royal troops; use 15 May). Garibaldi proclaimed himself dictator of Sicily in Victor Emmanuel's name on 14 May. He entered Naples by train on 7 September 1860. [source: ITA; EXP]
- Cavour sent the Sardinian army through the Papal States (Castelfidardo; Ancona surrendered 29 September) to stop Garibaldi marching on Rome. Garibaldi handed his power to Victor Emmanuel at the meeting near Teano. [source: ITA]
- On 17 March 1861 the parliament in Turin proclaimed Victor Emmanuel II King of Italy. Rome was declared capital on 27 March 1861, though it was not yet in Italian hands. Cavour died in June 1861, aged 50. [source: ITA]
- Garibaldi was wounded at Aspromonte (28 August 1862) under the slogan "Rome or Death" and defeated at Mentana (3 November 1867). [source: ITA]
- Venetia was gained in 1866 through the Prussian alliance, despite Italian defeats at Custoza (24 June) and Lissa (20 July). In the Venetian plebiscite of 21–22 October 1866 only 69 of more than 642,000 ballots were against annexation. Historians suggest the vote was held under military pressure. [source: ITA]
- Rome: the French garrison left in August 1870 because of the Franco-Prussian War. Italian troops breached the Aurelian Walls at Porta Pia on 20 September 1870; 49 Italian soldiers, 4 officers and 19 papal troops died. A plebiscite was held on 2 October. The capital moved from Florence to Rome in July 1871. Pius IX declared himself a "prisoner in the Vatican", and the Church's non-cooperation was fully reversed only by the 1929 Lateran Treaty. [source: ITA]
- Unification as "Piedmontization" (Martin Clark): Piedmont's Statuto Albertino, taxes and officials were imposed, and promises of regional government were broken in 1861. "Brigandage" insurrections in the South followed, described as "a mixture of spontaneous peasant movement and a Bourbon-clerical reaction". Northern correspondents depicted the South as "barbaric". [source: ITA]
- Language: Tullio De Mauro estimated that only about 2.5% of Italy's population (about 630,000 of more than 25 million) could speak standard Italian in 1861. Arrigo Castellani estimated nearly 10%. Literacy rose from 25% in 1861 to 60% in 1911. [source: ITL]
- About 60% of the labour force worked in agriculture after unification. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Italy]

## 4. Germany: "blood and iron"
- German Confederation (1815–1866): 39 states under Austrian presidency. The Prussian Zollverein (customs union) began in 1818 and spread in later decades. Friedrich List called the railways and the Zollverein "Siamese Twins". [source: GER]
- Otto von Bismarck: born 1 April 1815 at Schönhausen to a Junker family; died 30 July 1898. Minister President of Prussia from 1862; Chancellor 1871 – 18 March 1890; nicknamed the "Iron Chancellor". [source: BIS]
- "Blood and iron" speech, 30 September 1862, to the Budget Committee of the Prussian House of Representatives during the army-budget conflict. Translation: "...Not through speeches and majority decisions will the great questions of the day be decided—that was the great mistake of 1848 and 1849—but by iron and blood (Eisen und Blut)." The popular form "Blut und Eisen" reverses his word order. [source: BAI]
- Three wars: (1) the Second Schleswig War against Denmark, 1864, with Austria as Prussia's ally; the needle gun helped. (2) The Austro-Prussian War, 1866, decided at Königgrätz/Sadová; the Peace of Prague dissolved the German Confederation. The North German Confederation was formed: Prussia annexed Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau and Frankfurt, and the constitution was adopted on 1 July 1867. (3) The Franco-Prussian War, 19 July 1870 – 28 January 1871. [source: GER; FPW]
- Ems telegram, 13 July 1870: Bismarck edited it to provoke France. Bismarck later wrote: "This text produced the effect of a red flag on the Gallic bull." [source: FPW]
- Sedan: Napoleon III was captured on 2 September 1870 and the Third Republic was proclaimed. Paris was besieged from 19 September 1870. [source: GER; FPW]
- On 18 January 1871 Wilhelm I was proclaimed in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles. The date was chosen to coincide with the 1701 coronation of the first King of Prussia. Bismarck read the proclamation, and the Grand Duke of Baden cried "His Majesty, Kaiser Wilhelm", avoiding the constitutional title "German Emperor", which Wilhelm disliked. The legally decisive dates were 1 January 1871 (new constitution), 4 May 1871 and 10 May 1871 (Treaty of Frankfurt). [source: PRO; GER]
- Treaty of Frankfurt (10 May 1871): Alsace and part of Lorraine were annexed as the *Reichsland* Elsass-Lothringen. France paid an indemnity of 5 billion francs within five years, and German troops withdrew in September 1873. [source: TOF; FPW]
- Franco-Prussian War casualties per the FPW infobox: German 44,781 dead; French 138,871 dead; an estimated 450,000 French civilian excess deaths 1870–71. [source: FPW]
- The Empire had 25 constituent states plus the Reichsland. This was the *Kleindeutsche Lösung* (Lesser German solution), which excluded Austria. [source: GER]
- Historians debate whether Bismarck had a master plan for unification or simply expanded Prussian power. GER says they conclude that factors beyond his *Realpolitik* mattered: the Zollverein, railways, the 1848 liberals, and Roon and Moltke's army. [source: GER]
- Nation-building after 1871: the *Kulturkampf* (1872–78) against the Catholic Church brought the May Laws of 1873 and the Congregations Law of 1875. Germanisation of Poles: German became the language of primary schooling in Posen in 1873 and of administration in 1876. Polish was abolished in Posen and West Prussian primary schools in 1887. In 1885–86 more than 30,000 non-citizens were expelled from the eastern provinces, mostly Poles and several thousand Jews. The Prussian Settlement Commission (1886, 100 million marks) bought land for German settlers. Danish-speakers in North Schleswig were also targeted. [source: BIS; GER]

## 5. Greek and Balkan independence from the Ottomans
- Serbian Revolution, 1804–1835: First Serbian Uprising 1804–1813, Second 1815–1817. Serbia went from Ottoman province to constitutional monarchy. [source: SER]
- Rigas Feraios imagined a "Balkan Republic" of all Balkan peoples, including Turks. He was strangled by the Ottomans in Belgrade in June 1798. Adamantios Korais, an Enlightenment scholar in Paris, promoted Greek education. [source: GWI]
- The Filiki Eteria (Friendly Society) was founded in 1814 in Odessa by merchants Skoufas, Xanthos and Tsakalov. GWI says its basic objective was a revival of the Byzantine Empire with Constantinople as capital, not a national state. [source: GWI]
- The war ran 1821–1829. Revolt began on 21 February 1821 in the Danubian Principalities (Alexander Ypsilantis); the Maniots declared war on 17 March 1821. 25 March is celebrated as Independence Day. Kolokotronis captured Tripolitsa in September 1821. The slogan was "Eleftheria i thanatos" (Freedom or death). [source: GWI]
- The Ecumenical Patriarch Gregory V was hanged in Constantinople on Easter Sunday 1821, even though he had condemned the revolt. [source: GWI]
- Chios, April 1822: of 100,000–120,000 Greeks, about 25,000 were killed and about 45,000 (mostly women and children) sold into slavery. [source: GWI]
- Massacres on both sides: the revolutionaries massacred Muslims and Jews (Tripolitsa), and Ottoman forces massacred Greeks. St. Clair called the war "a series of opportunist massacres". Estimated deaths: 230,000–600,000 Greek civilians and 25,000 Muslim civilians. [source: GWI]
- Philhellenism: Lord Byron died of fever at Missolonghi in 1824. His poetry and Delacroix's paintings rallied European opinion. Ottoman atrocities received more coverage in Europe than Greek ones. [source: GWI]
- Muhammad Ali of Egypt sent his son Ibrahim Pasha's army to help the Sultan. Battle of Navarino, 20 October 1827: British, French and Russian fleets destroyed the Ottoman–Egyptian fleet; of 89 ships only 14 returned to Alexandria, with over 8,000 dead against 181 Allied deaths. George IV called it "this untoward event". [source: GWI]
- The Treaty of Adrianople (1829) forced the Ottomans to accept Greek autonomy. The London Protocol of 3 February 1830 recognized an independent Greek state. Kapodistrias was assassinated in 1831. In 1832 the powers chose the Bavarian prince Otto as king; the Treaty of Constantinople of 21 July 1832 fixed the Arta–Volos border. [source: GWI]
- The new state had about 800,000 people, less than one-third of the 2.5 million Greeks in the Ottoman Empire. Hence the *Megali Idea* of uniting all Greeks. GWI says the war legitimized small nation-states and encouraged the Polish November Uprising. [source: GWI]
- Congress of Berlin (13 June – 13 July 1878), chaired by Bismarck after the Russo-Turkish War: recognized independent Romania, Serbia and Montenegro. Bulgaria was split into an autonomous principality, Eastern Rumelia, and Macedonia, which was returned to the Ottomans. Bulgarians were excluded from the talks. Austria-Hungary was allowed to occupy Bosnia-Herzegovina. [source: BER]

## 6. The Habsburg multinational empire and the Compromise of 1867
- In 1848 the Austrian Empire contained Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, Croats, Ukrainians/Ruthenians, Romanians, Slovaks, Slovenes, Serbs and Italians. [source: R48]
- After the defeats of 1859 and 1866, and near bankruptcy (in 1858, 40% of state spending already went to debt service), Franz Joseph reconciled with Hungary. [source: AHC]
- The Compromise (*Ausgleich*) of 1867 created a dual monarchy and real union. Austria (Cisleithania) and Hungary (Transleithania) had separate parliaments and prime ministers, with common foreign affairs, defence, and a finance ministry for those two portfolios. Economic terms were renegotiated every ten years. Franz Joseph was crowned King of Hungary on 8 June 1867, with Andrássy placing the crown. Franz Joseph: "There were three of us who made the agreement: Deák, Andrássy and myself." [source: AHC]
- Many ethnic Hungarians saw 1867 as a betrayal of 1848, and the pro-Compromise Liberal Party relied heavily on minority voters. [source: AHC]
- Minority rights: Austria's 1867 constitution and Hungary's Nationalities Law of 1868 are cited as early minority-rights laws. Even so, Magyarization pressure grew. [source: AHC; MAG]
- 1910 census of Hungary (table in MAG): Hungarian 54.5%, Romanian 16.1%, Slovak 10.7%, German 10.4%, Serbian 2.5%, Ruthenian 2.5%. MAG elsewhere says Hungarian-speakers were 54.5% "including c. 5% Jews"; this figure appears to exclude Croatia-Slavonia. [source: MAG]
- By 1910, 96% of civil servants and 97% of judges had Hungarian as their first language. About 87% of minority citizens (8,895,925 people) could not speak Hungarian, per MAG. The Apponyi education law of 1907 made Hungarian compulsory in all schools, and the Černová massacre of 1907 killed 15 Slovaks. [source: MAG] (Caution: the MAG and AHC articles have a noticeably pro-Hungarian slant in places; use the hard numbers, not their framing.)
- Historians' "national indifference" thesis: Tara Zahra, "Imagined Noncommunities: National Indifference as a Category of Analysis," *Slavic Review* 69:1 (Spring 2010), pp. 93–119. It builds on Gary Cohen's work on Prague and studies the many Habsburg subjects who did not identify with any one nation. [source: https://www.doi.org/10.1017/S0037677900016715 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_indifference]

## 7. Irish and Polish nationalism (and Hungary above)
- Poland: the November Uprising began on 29 November 1830 in Warsaw, led by cadets under Piotr Wysocki. The Sejm deposed Nicholas I as King of Poland in February 1831. Warsaw fell in September 1831 and resistance collapsed by October. [source: NOV]
- The January Uprising began on 22 January 1863 and was fought mainly as guerrilla war. The insurgent government decreed that "all sons of Poland are free and equal citizens" and promised land to peasants. It spread to Lithuania and Belarus. Afterwards came executions, deportations to Siberia, the end of the Kingdom of Poland's autonomy, and restrictions on Polish. Jan Matejko painted *Poland – The Year 1863*. [source: JAN]
- Ireland: the Union took effect on 1 January 1801. Daniel O'Connell, "The Liberator" (6 August 1775 – 15 May 1847), mobilized Catholic Ireland and won Catholic emancipation in 1829, then campaigned for Repeal. [source: R48; OCO]
- Young Ireland was founded in 1839; its July 1848 rising failed at Ballingarry. [source: R48]
- Great Famine, 1845–1852: excess deaths 775,000 to 1.5 million (roughly one million). One million or more emigrated, and at least 2.1 million left in 1845–55. Population fell 20–25% between 1841 and 1871, from about 8.5–8.75 million before the famine. [source: FAM]
- The Irish Republican Brotherhood (Fenians) was founded on 17 March 1858 and had a US counterpart, the Fenian Brotherhood. [source: IRB]
- Home Rule: Isaac Butt founded the Home Government Association in 1870. Parnell turned it into the Irish Parliamentary Party in 1882. Gladstone's First Home Rule Bill (1886) was defeated in the Commons by 30 votes, splitting the Liberals and causing riots in Belfast. The Second (1893) passed the Commons but was defeated in the Lords. The Third (1912) became the Home Rule Act 1914. [source: HR]
- Cultural nationalism: the Gaelic Athletic Association was founded on 1 November 1884 at Hayes' Hotel, Thurles (Michael Cusack and others). The Gaelic League (Conradh na Gaeilge) was founded on 31 July 1893 by Douglas Hyde. [source: GAA; CNG]

## 8. Ottoman responses: Tanzimat and Ottomanism
- The Tanzimat ("Reorganization"), 1839–1876, began with the Edict of Gülhane on 3 November 1839 under Abdülmecid. Its principal architect was Mustafa Reşid Pasha; Âli Pasha and Fuad Pasha followed. It brought Nizamiye (secular) courts, modern education, railways, crackdowns on the slave trade and codified law, but also heavy foreign debt and Balkan tensions. TAN says the legacy is contested: either it built a strong national government or it accelerated fragmentation. [source: TAN; GUL]
- It built on Mahmud II's reforms, including the destruction of the Janissaries in the "Auspicious Incident" of 1826. [source: TAN]
- The Reform Edict of 18 February 1856 promised equality in education, government appointments and justice regardless of creed. It is often seen as a product of British and French pressure after the Crimean War. [source: REF56]
- The Ottoman Nationality Law of 19 January 1869 created a common Ottoman citizenship regardless of religion or ethnicity, mixing *jus soli* and *jus sanguinis* on the French model. [source: OTT]
- Ottomanism (*Osmanlılık*) aimed at the "Unity of the Peoples" (*İttihad-ı Anasır*) to keep the millets from tearing the empire apart. Many non-Muslims rejected it as an end to their privileges, and many Muslims as an end to their superior position. It revived after the 1908 Young Turk Revolution and lost most adherents in the Balkan Wars of 1912–13. [source: OTT]
- The Young Ottomans were a secret society founded in 1865 that wanted constitutional government. Namık Kemal (21 December 1840 – 2 December 1888) championed "freedom and fatherland" (*vatan*). His play *Vatan Yahut Silistre* (Homeland, or Silistra) tells of loyalty to the homeland in the Crimean War. Its staging led to the closure of his newspaper *İbret* and his exile to Famagusta, Cyprus. VAT's infobox dates the premiere to 1872; 1873 is commonly given, so the date is unverified. [source: YO; NK; VAT]
- Midhat Pasha "dreamed of an Empire in which 'there would be neither Muslim nor non-Muslim but only Ottomans'". The Constitution (*Kanun-ı Esasi*) is dated 23 December 1876. OC says it was influenced by the 1863 Armenian National Constitution and that Krikor Odian helped draft it. [source: OC]
- Abdul Hamid II prorogued parliament on 14 February 1878, after the Russo-Turkish War, and ruled autocratically for about three decades. [source: OC]
- Recommended framing: Selim Deringil compares Abdülhamid's Ottoman state with other late "legitimist" empires (ceremony, schooling, building an "Ottoman citizenry"). [source: https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BA35820911 ; publisher description via search]

## 9. Japan: state-building nationalism (Meiji)
- On 3 January 1868 imperial rule was declared restored; the Boshin War followed in 1868–69. [source: MEI]
- Background: the Mito school. Aizawa Seishisai popularized *kokutai* ("national body/polity") in *Shinron* (New Theses, 1825), which also introduced *sonnō jōi* ("revere the Emperor, expel the barbarians"). The slogan *fukoku kyōhei* ("rich nation, strong army") also comes from this background. [source: KOK; MEI]
- The Charter Oath was issued on 6 April 1868. Clause 5: "Knowledge shall be sought throughout the world so as to strengthen the foundation of imperial rule." [source: MEI; CO]
- The domains (*han*) were abolished in August 1871 and replaced by prefectures; the daimyō were summoned and informed without consultation. [source: MEI]
- Universal male conscription came in 1873: four years' service from age 21, then three in the reserves. Samurai stipends were made commutable to bonds in 1874 and compulsorily commuted in 1876. The Dampatsurei edict of 1871 ended the topknot. Saigō Takamori's Satsuma Rebellion was crushed by the conscript army. [source: MEI]
- The state created a standard language (*hyōjungo*) based on the Tokyo samurai dialect. Mass public schooling included "moral training" in duty to the Emperor and state. [source: MEI]
- State Shinto: *shinbutsu bunri* (separation of Shinto and Buddhism) led to the destruction of tens of thousands of Buddhist images and temples. [source: MEI]
- The Meiji Constitution was promulgated on 11 February 1889 on a Prusso-German model; the first Diet met when it came into force (1890). [source: MC]
- Imperial Rescript on Education, 30 October 1890: 315 characters, read aloud at school events, memorized by students, and distributed with the Emperor's portrait. It was drafted mainly by Inoue Kowashi with Motoda Nagazane under Yamagata Aritomo. It taught loyalty and filial piety ("should emergency arise, offer yourselves courageously to the State"). [source: IRE]
- Fukuzawa Yukichi: *Gakumon no Susume* (An Encouragement of Learning), 1872–76: "Heaven never created a man above another nor a man below another." His *Seiyō Jijō* (Conditions in the West) sold about 250,000 copies. In the 1885 editorial "Datsu-A Ron" (Leaving Asia) he urged Japan to distance itself from "backward" neighbours. [source: FUK]
- Internal colonization: the Ryukyu Kingdom was made a domain in 1872 and annexed as Okinawa Prefecture in 1879 (the "Ryukyu Disposition"), cutting its tributary tie to Qing China. The Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act of 1899 designated the Ainu a "former" indigenous people subject to assimilation. [source: RYU; AINU]
- Carol Gluck argues that the formulation and communication of the new national values (the "emperor system" ideology) was less consistent and more contested than usually supposed. *Japan's Modern Myths* (Princeton, 1985). [source: https://press.princeton.edu/node/29040]

## 10. Egypt: 'Urabi and early Egyptian nationalism
- Rifa'a al-Tahtawi, a figure of the *nahda*, spread interest in ancient Egypt and ideas of the "good Egyptian" and patriotism. He co-founded with Ali Mubarak the modern Egyptian school system. [source: EGN]
- Background: the Khedive Isma'il's debts led to Anglo-French control of finances through the Caisse de la Dette Publique. Turks, Circassians and Albanians held most elite posts. [source: URV]
- Ya'qub Sanu's Paris-based satirical magazine *Abu Naddara*, written in colloquial Egyptian Arabic, is credited with coining the slogan "Egypt for the Egyptians" (*Miṣr li'l-Miṣriyyīn*). [source: URV; Britannica via https://www.britannica.com/biography/Urabi-Pasha (search result)]
- Ahmed 'Urabi: 31 March 1841 – 21 September 1911, born near Zagazig and educated partly at al-Azhar. He was the first Egyptian political and military leader to rise from the *fellahin* (peasantry). [source: URA]
- In September 1881 'Urabi refused orders to leave Cairo and demanded the dismissal of Turco-Circassian generals and an elected government; the Khedive Tewfik gave in. On 8 January 1882 an Anglo-French Joint Note backed the Khedive, and 'Urabi became Minister of War. The movement was opposed by Europeans, large landowners, the Turco-Circassian elite and high ulama, and supported by most of the population. [source: URV; AEW]
- In July 1882 the British fleet under Admiral Seymour bombarded Alexandria; the French fleet refused to take part. Wolseley defeated 'Urabi at Tel el-Kebir on 13 September 1882, and Cairo surrendered. [source: URV; URA]
- 'Urabi's death sentence was commuted to exile, and he left on 28 December 1882 for Ceylon, where he supported Muslim education (Zahira College, 1892). He was allowed to return in May 1901. [source: URA]
- In 1952 Naguib recited 'Urabi's words to Tewfik that Egyptians were "no longer inheritable", and Ismailia Square became Tahrir Square. [source: URA]
- Juan Cole argues that 'Urabi's was a broad social revolution of guilds, intelligentsia and village notables that the British cut off. Roger Owen's review was sceptical. *Colonialism and Revolution in the Middle East* (Princeton, 1993). [source: https://press.princeton.edu/node/51215]
- Mustafa Kamil (14 August 1874 – 10 February 1908) led the National Party and admired Meiji Japan as an "Eastern" modernizer. In the Denshawai (Dinshaway) incident of 13 June 1906, four peasants were hanged after a clash with British officers hunting pigeons; it galvanized the nationalists. [source: MK; EGN]

## 11. India: the Indian National Congress
- First session in Bombay, 28–31 December 1885, at Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College. It was moved from Poona because of cholera. There were 72 delegates, mostly lawyers, and W. C. Bonnerjee presided. A. O. Hume, a retired British civil servant, organized it with Viceroy Dufferin's approval; he had written an open letter to Calcutta University graduates in 1883. [source: INC]
- INC calls Congress the first modern nationalist movement in the non-European British Empire. [source: INC]
- The "safety valve" theory of Congress's origin (that Hume founded it to avert revolt) has been challenged: the "secret report" volumes behind it are said to have been fictional. This is an open historiographical debate. [source: HUME]
- Early Congress was led by Moderates (Naoroji, Gokhale, R. C. Dutt) who used petitions and asked for "Indianisation" of the services. INC says it was elite-based for its first two decades. Moderates and Extremists ("Lal, Bal, Pal") split by 1905–07. [source: INC]
- Dadabhai Naoroji (4 September 1825 – 30 June 1917) set out the "drain theory" in *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901). He was MP for Finsbury Central from 1892 and Congress President three times. [source: NAO]
- B. G. Tilak: "Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it!" He turned household Ganesh worship into a public festival in 1894 and used Shiv Jayanti to build national spirit beyond the elite. TIL notes this also sharpened Hindu–Muslim divisions. He was jailed for sedition in 1897. He also opposed some social reforms, including a girls' high school in Pune in 1885. [source: TIL]

## 12. Latin American and Haitian national identities
- Haiti: Dessalines proclaimed independence at Gonaïves on 1 January 1804. Haiti was the first independent nation of Latin America and the Caribbean, after the only successful slave revolution. Boisrond-Tonnerre wrote and read the declaration. Julia Gaffield found the only two known printed copies in the UK National Archives in 2010–11. [source: HDI]
- The name "Haiti/Ayiti" was taken from the Taíno name for the island ("land of high mountains") as a tribute to the Indigenous predecessors. [source: HAI; DES]
- Dessalines ordered the 1804 massacre of the remaining white French, killing 3,000–5,000; Polish legionnaires and some Germans were exempted. [source: DES]
- 1805 Constitution, Article 14: Haitians "shall hereafter be known by the generic appellation of blacks". Article 12 barred whites from owning property. Translation details vary: Haytians/Haitians, blacks/Blacks. [source: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Hayti_(1805) ; https://haitidoi.com/2015/12/21/race-and-the-haitian-constitution-of-1805/]
- In 1825 France recognized Haiti only in exchange for an indemnity of 150 million francs (HAI equates this with about $560 million today). [source: HAI]
- Creole patriotism in New Spain: criollos called peninsular Spaniards *gachupines*. Miguel Sánchez's 1648 account of the Virgin of Guadalupe suggested that God had blessed Mexico and its criollos. The 1799 "conspiracy of the machetes" invoked "Mexico and the Virgen de Guadalupe". [source: CRI]
- Bourbon reforms favoured peninsulares and sharpened criollo grievances. Criollos such as Hidalgo, Bolívar and San Martín led independence. [source: CRI]
- Bolívar wrote the Jamaica Letter in September 1815 and stayed in Haiti in 1816. [source: BOL]
- Benito Juárez (21 March 1806 – 18 July 1872 per infobox; the lead says 16 July, a discrepancy) was a Zapotec and the first Indigenous president of Mexico (1858–1872). He was a Liberal who resisted Maximilian's French-backed empire. [source: JUA]
- José Martí (28 January 1853 – 19 May 1895) published "Nuestra América" on 1 January 1891 in New York's *Revista Ilustrada*. He organized Cuban émigré tobacco workers in Tampa and Key West and was killed at Dos Ríos. [source: MAR]
- Indigenismo: in Mexico it was consolidated after the Revolution (1920s–30s) inside a *mestizaje* national narrative that honoured the Indigenous past while seeking to assimilate living Indigenous people. This is a post-1910 development; flag it as later. [source: IND]

## 13. Zionism (Herzl)
- Leon Pinsker, a doctor in Odessa, turned to Jewish self-emancipation after the pogroms of 1871 and 1881, writing the pamphlet *Auto-Emancipation*. The Hovevei Zion (Lovers of Zion) met at the 1884 Katowice Conference. Nathan Birnbaum first used "Zionism" as an "-ism" in 1890. [source: PIN; ZIO]
- Theodor Herzl: 2 May 1860 (Pest) – 3 July 1904. A Viennese journalist, he was Paris correspondent of the *Neue Freie Presse*. [source: HZ]
- *Der Judenstaat* (The Jewish State) was published in February 1896 (dated 14 February) in Leipzig and Vienna. [source: DJ]
- Herzl said the Dreyfus affair (from 1894), with crowds chanting "Death to the Jews!", made him a Zionist. Modern scholars (e.g. Jacques Kornberg) doubt this. Karl Lueger's rise in Vienna in 1895 may have mattered more, and Herzl initially thought Dreyfus guilty. [source: HZ]
- The First Zionist Congress met in Basel on 29–31 August 1897: 208 participants from 17 countries, 69 of them delegates of Zionist societies. Seventeen women attended but could not vote until the Second Congress. The Basel Program: "Zionism seeks to establish a home in Palestine for the Jewish people, secured under public law." [source: FZC]
- Herzl's diary: "At Basel I founded the Jewish State... In five years perhaps, and certainly in fifty years, everyone will perceive it." [source: FZC]
- Herzl appealed unsuccessfully to Wilhelm II and Abdul Hamid II. The 1903 Uganda Scheme (East Africa) was rejected. He died in 1904 and was reburied on Mount Herzl in 1949. [source: HZ]
- Palestinian Arab perspective: Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi (1842–1906), several times mayor of Jerusalem, wrote on 1 March 1899 to Zadoc Kahn, Chief Rabbi of France, intending the letter for Herzl. He said that historically Palestine was the Jews' country, but it was part of the Ottoman Empire and "inhabited by people other than only Israelites". He ended: "in the name of God, let Palestine be left alone" (French: "au nom de Dieu, qu'on laisse tranquille la Palestine"). Herzl replied on 19 March 1899. [source: KHA]

## 14. Theory: modernists, ethno-symbolists, primordialists
- Ernest Renan, "What Is a Nation?" (*Qu'est-ce qu'une nation ?*), a Sorbonne lecture of 1882: a nation is "a daily plebiscite", and "Forgetfulness, and I would even say historical error, are essential in the creation of a nation." [source: REN]
- Ernest Gellner (9 December 1925 – 5 November 1995), *Nations and Nationalism* (1983): "nationalism is primarily a political principle that holds that the political and the national unit should be congruent". Industrial society needs a standardized, literate high culture spread by mass education. Agro-literate societies had no such need. [source: GEL; NN]
- Gellner's earlier *Thought and Change* (1964), ch. 7: "Nationalism is not the awakening of nations to self-consciousness: it invents nations where they do not exist" (p. 169, as quoted by Anderson). The wording is unconfirmed against the original. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought_and_Change ; search results]
- Benedict Anderson (born 26 August 1936, Kunming; died 2015), *Imagined Communities: Reflections on the Origin and Spread of Nationalism* (1983; revised 1991, 2006). A nation is "an imagined political community—and imagined as both inherently limited and sovereign". It is imagined "because the members of even the smallest nation will never know most of their fellow-members... yet in the minds of each lives the image of their communion." His key causes were "print capitalism" and vernacular literacy, plus the decline of divine-right dynasties. Reading the daily newspaper is a "mass ceremony" performed "in the lair of the skull". [source: IC; BA]
- Anderson's "Creole Pioneers": in the 1991 edition he retitled chapter 4 "Creole Pioneers" to stress that the American creole states (creole officials and printers) pioneered nationhood before Europe. [source: search summaries of IC, e.g. https://supersummary.com/imagined-communities-reflections-on-the-origin-and-spread-of-nationalism/chapters-3-4 — secondary; the claim is consistent across sources]
- Partha Chatterjee's critique, "Whose Imagined Community?" (1991, then ch. 1 of *The Nation and Its Fragments*, Princeton 1993): if Asians and Africans can only choose from "modular" forms invented in Europe and the Americas, then "Even our imaginations must remain, forever, colonized" (p. 5). Anti-colonial nationalism first claimed sovereignty in an inner "spiritual" domain. [source: IC; https://muse.jhu.edu/book/74886] (Exact wording and page are attributed via IC; check against the book.)
- Feminist critique (Linda McDowell): Anderson's "horizontal comradeship" carries "connotations of masculine solidarity". [source: IC]
- Eric Hobsbawm (9 June 1917, Alexandria – 1 October 2012): with Terence Ranger he edited *The Invention of Tradition* (1983). Invented traditions foster cohesion, legitimize institutions and instil values. The "curious but understandable paradox: modern nations... generally claim to be the opposite of novel, namely rooted in remotest antiquity". Examples include Scottish tartanry. [source: INV; HOB]
- Hobsbawm, *Nations and Nationalism since 1780: Programme, Myth, Reality* (Cambridge UP, 1990; Wiles Lectures given at Queen's University Belfast in May 1985). [source: https://libcat.library.tamu.edu/Record/in00000912421/Details ; Cambridge preface]
- Critique of "invention" (Peter Burke): the phrase "hides serious ambiguities". Since all traditions change, can genuine and fake traditions really be separated? [source: INV]
- Anthony D. Smith (23 September 1939 – 19 July 2016; LSE, a former student of Gellner): ethno-symbolism. Nationalism is modern, but nations have premodern "ethnic cores" (*ethnies*) with myths of common ancestry, shared memories, symbols and homeland attachment. Smith argued modernist definitions are Eurocentric. [source: ADS; ETH]
- The Warwick debate (24 October 1995), Gellner against Smith on "Nationalism, real or imaginary?", was Gellner's last UK public appearance. Gellner's reply "Do nations have navels?" appeared in *Nations and Nationalism* 2(3) (1996), pp. 366–370: some nations have navels (real ancient roots) and some do not, and it is "inessential". [source: ADS; https://lse.ac.uk/researchAndExpertise/units/gellner/Warwick2.html ; https://scilit.com/publications/8926723ae0333ae197c253d24ff97886]
- John A. Armstrong, *Nations before Nationalism* (1982): "myth-symbol complexes" over the *longue durée*. John Hutchinson, *The Dynamics of Cultural Nationalism* (1987). [source: ETH]
- Primordialism holds that nations or ethnic identities are fixed, natural and ancient. The term is associated with Edward Shils (1957) and Clifford Geertz. PRI says it is widely rejected by scholars, and it is traced philosophically to Herder and Fichte. [source: PRI]
- "Perennialist"/medievalist arguments: Adrian Hastings, *The Construction of Nationhood* (1997), traces European nations to the Middle Ages and stresses the Bible's model of Israel. Liah Greenfeld, *Nationalism: Five Roads to Modernity* (1992), dates the national idea to 16th-century England. [source: HAS; GRE]
- Eugen Weber, *Peasants into Frenchmen: The Modernization of Rural France, 1870–1914* (1976): well into the 19th century many French people spoke Breton, Gascon, Basque, Catalan, Flemish, Alsatian or Corsican. The state made "Frenchmen" through schools, conscription and the courts. [source: WEB]
- John Breuilly, *Nationalism and the State* (1982), sees nationalism as a form of politics oriented to state power. Walker Connor, *Ethnonationalism* (1994), stressed terminological precision. [source: BRE; CON]

## Debates
- **Modern or ancient nations?** Modernists (Gellner, Anderson, Hobsbawm, Breuilly, Weber's case study) say nations are products of industrialization, print capitalism, mass schooling and the state. Ethno-symbolists (Smith, Armstrong, Hutchinson) say nationalism is modern but builds on premodern ethnic cores. Perennialists (Hastings, Greenfeld in her own way) date nations to the Middle Ages or the 16th century. Primordialism (Shils/Geertz, in its popular form) is widely rejected. These positions were staged at Warwick in 1995 ("Do nations have navels?"). [sources: GEL; IC; HOB; ADS; ETH; HAS; GRE; PRI; LSE Warwick page]
- **Where was nationalism invented?** Anderson credits the American "Creole pioneers". Chatterjee rejects the idea of a derivative, "modular" anticolonial nationalism and locates its originality in an inner spiritual domain. [sources: IC; search summaries]
- **Bismarck: master plan or opportunism?** Historians debate it, and GER notes structural factors such as the Zollverein, railways and the army reforms. [source: GER]
- **Risorgimento: liberation or Piedmontese conquest?** The "Piedmontization" and southern "brigandage" critique (Martin Clark, Hearder). Riall shows that Garibaldi's heroism was partly a constructed myth. [source: ITA; GAR]
- **Habsburg Empire: "prison of nations" or viable multinational state?** Judson and Zahra's "national indifference" argue that many people stayed nationally indifferent. Pro-Hungarian and minority readings of 1867 and Magyarization conflict. [source: AHC; MAG; Zahra]
- **Tanzimat: strengthening or fragmenting?** [source: TAN]
- **Congress origins: the "safety valve" theory vs Indian initiative** [source: HUME; INC]
- **'Urabi: officers' mutiny or social revolution?** Cole says social revolution; Owen is sceptical. [source: Cole/Princeton; Cambridge review listing]
- **Herzl and Dreyfus**: did Dreyfus convert him, or was it Vienna and Lueger? [source: HZ]
- **Haiti's 1805 constitution**: radical racialization or Black sovereignty? [source: haitidoi.com; Black Agenda Report listing in search]
- **Greek War massacres**: retaliation, or simultaneous atrocities from the start? [source: GWI]
