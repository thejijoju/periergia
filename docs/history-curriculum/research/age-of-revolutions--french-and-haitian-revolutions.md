# Verified facts — French and Haitian Revolutions (researched 2026-11-11)

Node: history/age-of-revolutions/french-and-haitian-revolutions

Sources key (all English Wikipedia unless stated): FR = https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution ; HR = https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution . The HR article leans heavily on one popular source (Perry 2005) and contains internal errors (see NOT VERIFIED); where possible, Haitian facts below are taken from the more specific articles.

## France: fiscal crisis and the Estates-General (1786–June 1789)
- France paid for the 1778–1783 war with Britain (its intervention in the American Revolutionary War) mainly with loans; the monarchy kept borrowing after the 1783 peace, and by 1788 half of state revenue went on servicing the debt. Caveat: the debt level itself was not high compared with Britain's; the deeper problem was an uneven, inconsistently collected tax system. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- In 1786 finance minister Calonne proposed reforms including a universal land tax; they were rejected by a hand-picked Assembly of Notables and then by the parlements (under his successor Brienne), who argued that only an Estates-General could approve new taxes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- On 8 August 1788 the King and Brienne scheduled the Estates-General for the following May; after Brienne resigned on 24 August, Necker was reappointed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estates_General_of_1789]
- In September 1788 the Parlement of Paris ruled that the Estates-General should meet in the form of 1614, voting by estate rather than by head, so clergy and nobility (less than 5% of the population) could outvote the Third Estate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- Abbé Sieyès's pamphlet *What Is the Third Estate?* argued the Third Estate represented the nation and should sit alone as a National Assembly. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- The Estates-General opened at Versailles on 5 May 1789, the first such meeting since 1614. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estates_General_of_1789]
- Deputies: 1,219 elected (302 First Estate, 289 Second, 611 Third); about 1,208 actually sat. The Third Estate's representation had been doubled; it represented about 98% of a population of roughly 28 million. Only two Third Estate deputies identified themselves as peasants (by their clothing). Caveat: FR gives "610 deputies of the Third Estate". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estates_General_of_1789]
- Grievance lists (*cahiers de doléances*) were drawn up by each region for the deputies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]

## The National Assembly and the Tennis Court Oath (June 1789)
- On 17 June 1789 the Third Estate declared itself the National Assembly; by 19 June it had been joined by more than 100 clergy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- On 20 June 1789, finding their hall locked, deputies met in an indoor tennis court at Versailles and swore "not to separate and to reassemble wherever necessary until the constitution of the kingdom is established". 576 of 577 members took the oath; the only refuser was Joseph Martin-Dauch. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tennis_Court_Oath]
- On 27 June, facing demonstrations and mutinies in the French Guards, Louis XVI ordered the first and second estates to join the National Assembly. On 9 July it declared itself the National Constituent Assembly. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]

## The Bastille, the Great Fear and 4 August
- Louis dismissed Necker on 11 July 1789. On 14 July a crowd, joined by soldiers of the Gardes Françaises, attacked the Bastille, a royal fortress with large stores of arms and ammunition; governor de Launay surrendered after several hours of fighting that killed 83 attackers, and was then killed and his head paraded on a pike. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- The Bastille held only seven prisoners (four forgers, a lunatic, a failed assassin and a deviant nobleman) but was a potent symbol of the old regime; it was demolished in the following weeks. 14 July (Bastille Day) is the French national holiday. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- The Great Fear was a general rural panic, 22 July–6 August 1789, fuelled by rumours of an aristocratic "famine plot"; peasants attacked manors chiefly to find and burn the documents recording seigneurial dues. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Fear]
- Georges Lefebvre studied the Great Fear in detail and wrote: "To get the peasant to rise and revolt, there was no need of the French Revolution ... when the panic came he was already up and away". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Fear]
- On the night of 4 August 1789 (proposals by the Vicomte de Noailles and the Duke of Aiguillon) the Assembly declared: "The National Assembly abolishes the feudal system entirely." The August Decrees were made between 4 and 11 August. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolition_of_feudalism_in_France]
- Over 25% of French farmland was subject to feudal dues; dues and church tithes were cancelled. Former tenants were supposed to pay compensation, but this proved uncollectable and was annulled in 1793. Other decrees: equality before the law, public office open to all, freedom of worship. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]

## The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen
- Published on 26 August 1789 as a statement of principle; 17 articles. Lafayette prepared the principal drafts, consulting Thomas Jefferson; Sieyès and Mirabeau were central to the final version. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Man_and_of_the_Citizen]
- Article I: "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights. Social distinctions may be founded only upon the general good." Article II names the natural rights as "liberty, property, safety and resistance against oppression". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Man_and_of_the_Citizen]
- When the Declaration was adopted, delegates for colonial landowners argued successfully that its principles should not apply in the colonies; slave-owners and merchants formed the Club de Massiac (August 1789) against the abolitionist Society of the Friends of the Blacks (founded February 1788). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- The 1791 constitution limited the vote to men over 25 meeting a property qualification ("active citizens"); the Assembly opposed equal political rights for women. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]

## Women's march on Versailles (5–6 October 1789)
- On 5 October 1789 market women in Paris, angry at the price and shortage of bread, gathered at the Hôtel de Ville; some 7,000 marched on Versailles and entered the Assembly; 15,000 National Guards under Lafayette followed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- On 6 October the crowd, grown to over 60,000, escorted the royal family back to Paris (about nine hours); the King was installed in the Tuileries and the Assembly soon followed to Paris. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_March_on_Versailles]

## Olympe de Gouges
- Born Marie Gouze, 7 May 1748, Montauban; executed by guillotine 3 November 1793. Playwright; one of France's earliest public opponents of slavery (*Réflexions sur les hommes nègres*, 1788; play *Zamore et Mirza*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympe_de_Gouges]
- Wrote the *Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen* (1791) shortly after the 1791 constitution, dedicating it to Marie Antoinette. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympe_de_Gouges]
- Associated with the Girondins; opposed the execution of Louis XVI; arrested 20 July 1793 after her poster *Les Trois Urnes*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympe_de_Gouges]
- Nuance: she did not approve of violent revolution in Saint-Domingue; her 1792 preface to *L'Esclavage des Noirs* criticised the rebels' violence while arguing it mirrored the cruelty of slavery. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympe_de_Gouges]

## Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790)
- Passed 12 July 1790: priests became state employees, bishops and priests were to be elected locally; 83 bishops (one per department) instead of 135. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Constitution_of_the_Clergy]
- When clergy were required to swear loyalty (November 1790) the church split: about 24% complied, the majority refused. Resistance was strongest in Catholic regions such as Normandy, Brittany and the Vendée; "refractory clergy" were persecuted, exiled, deported or executed. Pope Pius VI condemned it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]

## Varennes and war (1791–1792)
- On the night of 20–21 June 1791 the royal family fled Paris in disguise; Louis was recognised (perhaps from his profile on an assignat) and arrested at Varennes on 21 June and brought back. The flight destroyed trust in the King and fed fears of "spies and traitors". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_to_Varennes ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- Declaration of Pillnitz, 27 August 1791 (Austria and Prussia). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- War began on 20 April 1792 against Austria and Prussia, followed by disastrous defeats. The Brunswick Manifesto (news reached Paris 1 August) threatened "unforgettable vengeance". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- On 10 August 1792 the Paris National Guard and provincial fédérés attacked the Tuileries; the Assembly suspended the monarchy. In the September Massacres, 1,100–1,600 prisoners in Paris were killed, most of them common criminals. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- Victory at Valmy, 20 September 1792; on 22 September the Convention replaced the monarchy with the First Republic and a new calendar (1792 = Year One). Caveat: monarchy abolished 21 September, republic dated from 22 September; FR uses 22 September. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]

## The king's execution (1793)
- On 17 January 1793 Louis was sentenced to death: 361 deputies for, 288 against, 72 for death with delaying conditions (often described as a majority of one). Executed by guillotine on 21 January 1793 on the Place de la Révolution (now Place de la Concorde) by Charles-Henri Sanson. France then declared war on Britain and the Dutch Republic (February 1793). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Execution_of_Louis_XVI]

## The Terror, Robespierre and the Vendée
- Revolutionary Tribunal created 10 March 1793; Committee of Public Safety 6 April 1793; after the 31 May–2 June 1793 rising, power passed to the Committee dominated by Jacobins led by Robespierre. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reign_of_Terror ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- Robespierre: born (baptised) 6 May 1758, Arras; executed 28 July 1794. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximilien_Robespierre]
- Law of Suspects, 17 September 1793. From September 1793 to July 1794 around 300,000 were arrested, some 16,600 executed after trial, and perhaps 40,000 more summarily executed or died awaiting trial (FR, citing Gough). Reign of Terror article: 16,594 official death sentences since June 1793, 2,639 of them in Paris, plus an estimated 10,000–20,000 executed without trial or dead in prison. FR lead: "about 16,000" executed. Give as a range. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reign_of_Terror]
- Historians disagree when the Terror began (5 September 1793, 10 March 1793, September 1792, even July 1789). Jean-Clément Martin argues there was no "system of terror". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reign_of_Terror]
- Law of 22 Prairial, 10 June 1794, cut acquittals at the Tribunal from about half to about a quarter. Marie Antoinette guillotined mid-October 1793; Danton executed 5 April 1794. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reign_of_Terror ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- Vendée: revolt began March 1793 against the levy of 300,000 men chosen by lot (voted February 1793), compounded by religious grievances. Republican victory at Cholet 17 October and Le Mans 23 December 1793; insurgency continued to 1796. Over 4,000 were drowned in the Loire at Nantes (Nov 1793–Feb 1794) under Carrier; Turreau's "infernal columns" killed 20,000–50,000 Vendéan civilians (Jan–May 1794). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_the_Vend%C3%A9e]
- Vendée death-toll estimates vary widely: about 170,000 (Hussenet) to 200,000–250,000; overall estimates for both sides range from 117,000 to 450,000 out of about 800,000 people. Reynald Secher (*A French Genocide*, 1986) called it genocide; critics (e.g. Peter McPhee) reject that label; Furet spoke of "massacre and destruction on an unprecedented scale". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_the_Vend%C3%A9e ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]

## Thermidor, the Directory and Brumaire
- 9 Thermidor Year II (27 July 1794): Robespierre attacked in the Convention and arrested; executed 28 July with 19 colleagues including Saint-Just and Couthon, followed by Commune members. The Jacobin Club was closed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermidorian_Reaction ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- A "White Terror" of revenge killings followed in the south; by April 1795 the assignat was worth only 8% of face value and people were starving. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- The Directory (Constitution of Year III, 1795): five Directors, a Council of Five Hundred and a Council of Ancients (250 men over 40); governed from 26 October 1795 (some sources: inaugurated 2 November 1795) to 9 November 1799. Napoleon put down a royalist rising in Paris on 5 October 1795 (13 Vendémiaire). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Directory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermidorian_Reaction]
- Babeuf's Conspiracy of the Equals (1796); Napoleon's invasion of Egypt, July 1798. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- Coup of 18 Brumaire Year VIII (9 November 1799), at Saint-Cloud, engineered by Sieyès with Napoleon and Lucien Bonaparte; the Consulate (Napoleon, Sieyès, Ducos) replaced the Directory, Napoleon as First Consul. Most historians treat it as the end of the Revolution. Sieyès, asked what he had done during the Terror, allegedly answered "I survived". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coup_of_18_Brumaire]

## Saint-Domingue: economy and population
- French colonies held about 700,000 enslaved people in 1789, about 500,000 in Saint-Domingue; colonial products were about a third of France's exports. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- HR figures for 1789: about 40,000 whites, 28,000 free people of colour and free Black people, about 452,000 enslaved; Saint-Domingue produced 60% of the world's coffee and 40% of the sugar imported by France and Britain. Two-thirds of the enslaved were African-born. Caveat: these come via Perry (2005), a popular history; treat as approximate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- About 20,000 enslaved Africans were brought to Saint-Domingue in 1787 alone; deaths exceeded births, so the colony depended on continuous imports. Kongolese were the largest African group (about 40%, citing Dubois). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- Whites were divided into *grands blancs* (planters) and *petits blancs* (artisans, overseers, shopkeepers). Many free people of colour were educated, some owned plantations and enslaved people; colonial laws from 1758 imposed dress codes and barred them from many offices and professions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- François Mackandal led maroon resistance (1751–1757) and was burned at the stake in 1758. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- Laurent Dubois on the free people of colour: "The policing of this social structure depended on a group that occupied a paradoxical place within it: the gens de couleur." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]

## Vincent Ogé and the rights of free people of colour
- Julien Raimond and Vincent Ogé lobbied in Paris for the rights of free people of colour; both were slaveholders and did not seek abolition. Ogé (born c. 1757, Dondon; Bordeaux-trained; the richest merchant of African descent in Le Cap) went home via London (meeting Thomas Clarkson) and Charleston. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_Og%C3%A9]
- In October 1790, with Jean-Baptiste Chavannes, Ogé demanded that the governor apply the March 1790 decree; refused, they raised about 300 free men of colour near Le Cap; defeated, they fled to Spanish Santo Domingo, were handed over, tried and Ogé was broken on the wheel and beheaded (February 1791). Caveat on date: the Ogé article gives 6 February 1791; other sources give 25 February 1791. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_Og%C3%A9 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- On 15 May 1791 the Assembly granted full political rights to people of colour born of two free parents; the colonial assemblies refused to apply it and fighting broke out. Robespierre in the debate: "Perish the colonies, if it will cost you your happiness, your glory, your freedom." (He did not call for immediate abolition.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximilien_Robespierre]
- Decree of 4 April 1792 gave citizenship to all free men of colour; commissioners Sonthonax and Polverel (with Ailhaud) and 6,000 troops were sent; on arrival in September 1792 they announced slavery would remain. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9ger-F%C3%A9licit%C3%A9_Sonthonax ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]

## The August 1791 uprising, Bois Caïman and Boukman
- On 14 August 1791 about 200 slave drivers, coachmen and other elite enslaved men met at Morne-Rouge (Lenormand de Mézy plantation) to plan the revolt. A Vodou ceremony at Bois Caïman, presided over (in tradition) by Dutty Boukman and the mambo Cécile Fatiman, marked its start. The uprising broke out on the night of 22–23 August 1791 on the northern plain (burning of the Noé estate). Caveat on dates: HR says the revolt began 21 August; Geggus dates the Bois Caïman ceremony itself to 21 August at the Choiseul plantation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutty_Boukman ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toussaint_Louverture ; https://sites.duke.edu/blackatlantic/sample-page/storytelling-and-representation-of-bois-caiman/genealogy-of-bois-caiman-textual-sources/debates-about-bois-caiman]
- Within weeks some 100,000 enslaved people had joined in the north; in two months about 4,000 whites were killed and 180 sugar and hundreds of coffee plantations destroyed; white militias killed about 15,000 Black people in reprisal (Perry figures, approximate). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- Many rebel leaders claimed to fight for the King of France, who they believed had decreed reforms suppressed by colonial officials (Dubois). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- Boukman: born c. 1767 in Senegambia, enslaved first in Jamaica; killed 7 November 1791 and his head displayed at Le Cap. His being a Vodou priest is asserted only in modern sources. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutty_Boukman]
- The earliest written account of the ceremony is by the colonist Antoine Dalmas (written 1793–94, published 1814); Hérard Dumesle (1824) recorded the speech from oral testimony without naming Boukman; Schoelcher (1843) is the first to name Boukman. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bois_Ca%C3%AFman ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutty_Boukman ; https://sites.duke.edu/blackatlantic/sample-page/storytelling-and-representation-of-bois-caiman/genealogy-of-bois-caiman-textual-sources/debates-about-bois-caiman]
- From the 1990s some evangelical Christians recast Bois Caïman as a "blood pact with Satan"; this is a modern reinterpretation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bois_Ca%C3%AFman]

## Sonthonax, the 1793 emancipations and 16 Pluviôse Year II
- Britain and Spain entered the war in 1793; Spain backed rebel leaders Jean-François and Biassou; Toussaint joined the Spanish (probably early June 1793). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toussaint_Louverture]
- June 1793: commissioners freed enslaved men who would fight for the Republic (FR: 10,000). Sonthonax proclaimed emancipation in the Northern Province on 29 August 1793; Polverel extended it in the West and South between 27 August and 31 October 1793. The motive was largely strategic: to keep the colony from Britain and Spain. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9ger-F%C3%A9licit%C3%A9_Sonthonax ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_4_February_1794]
- Sonthonax (7 March 1763 – 23 July 1813), lawyer, member of the Society of the Friends of the Blacks, linked to Brissot. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9ger-F%C3%A9licit%C3%A9_Sonthonax]
- Three deputies from Saint-Domingue — the white colonist Louis Dufay, the Black officer Jean-Baptiste Belley and the free man of colour Jean-Baptiste Mills — reached the Convention; on 4 February 1794 (16 Pluviôse Year II) it decreed that "slavery of the Blacks is abolished in all the colonies" and all men in the colonies, without distinction of colour, were French citizens. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_4_February_1794]
- Danton in the debate: "Representatives of the French people, until now our decrees of liberty have been selfish, and only for ourselves. But today we proclaim it to the universe ..." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- Implementation was uneven: applied in Saint-Domingue, Guadeloupe (retaken by Victor Hugues's force, which recruited ex-slaves) and Guiana; Martinique was under British occupation; Réunion and Île de France refused. Freed people were often bound to plantations by labour and vagrancy rules; historian Frédéric Régent: "only whites, persons of color already freed before the decree, and former slaves in the army or on warships really benefited from general emancipation." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_4_February_1794 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_20_May_1802]

## Toussaint Louverture
- Born 20 May 1743, probably on the Bréda plantation, Haut-du-Cap; son of an enslaved man of Allada origin; died 7 April 1803 at Fort de Joux, France. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toussaint_Louverture]
- He was freed before the Revolution (manumitted c. 1772–1776; documented in records of 1776–77; he wrote in 1797 that he had been free for over twenty years) and himself owned enslaved people and coffee plantations. Historians before the later 20th century, including C. L. R. James (1938), believed he had been enslaved until 1791. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toussaint_Louverture]
- Adopted the name Louverture ("the opening") c. 1792–93; 29 August 1793 proclamation of Camp Turel: "Brothers and friends, I am Toussaint Louverture ... I want Liberty and Equality to reign in St. Domingue." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toussaint_Louverture]
- Switched from the Spanish to the French Republic in early May 1794 (HR gives 6 May 1794). Defeated Rigaud in the War of the South (1799–1800); the British withdrew in 1798; he invaded Spanish Santo Domingo and freed its slaves on 3 January 1801. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9ger-F%C3%A9licit%C3%A9_Sonthonax]
- Constitution promulgated 7 July 1801 (drafted by an assembly chosen mainly from white planters): made him governor-general for life with power to choose his successor; Article 3: "There cannot exist slaves [in Saint-Domingue], servitude is therein forever abolished. All men are born, live and die free and French." It kept the colony French, made Catholicism the official religion, and confirmed forced plantation labour and importing workers via the slave trade. Moïse led a revolt against forced labour in October 1801, repressed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toussaint_Louverture]

## Napoleon, Leclerc and the restoration of slavery (1801–1803)
- The Leclerc expedition left Brest 14 December 1801; landed at Le Cap February 1802, where Henri Christophe burned the town rather than surrender it. In all about 31,000 soldiers were sent; only 7,000–8,000 survived; over 20 French generals died. Leclerc died of yellow fever on 1 November 1802; Rochambeau succeeded him and used dogs imported from Cuba. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Domingue_expedition]
- Leclerc had secret instructions to arrest Toussaint and deport Black officers. Toussaint came to terms in May 1802, was seized by General Brunet on 7 June 1802 and deported; on boarding he said "In overthrowing me you have cut down in Saint Domingue only the trunk of the tree of liberty; it will spring up again from the roots, for they are numerous and they are deep." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toussaint_Louverture]
- Law of 20 May 1802 (30 Floréal Year X) maintained slavery in colonies that had never applied the 1794 abolition, chiefly Martinique (returned by Britain under the Treaty of Amiens), Tobago and Saint Lucia. Re-enslavement in Guadeloupe followed by a consular decree of 16 July 1802 (formally re-established 14 May 1803); Guiana by decree of 7 December 1802. Caveat: "restored slavery in Martinique" is loose — slavery had never ended there. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_20_May_1802]
- In Guadeloupe, Louis Delgrès resisted Richepanse's expedition; on 28 May 1802 at Matouba he and his followers blew themselves up rather than surrender. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Delgr%C3%A8s]
- Napoleon, letter to Truguet: "I am for the whites, because I am white; I have no other reason, and this is the right one". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_4_February_1794]
- When it became clear France meant to restore slavery (as in Guadeloupe), Black cultivators revolted in summer 1802; in October 1802 Pétion, Christophe and Dessalines deserted the French. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Domingue_expedition]
- Battle of Vertières, 18 November 1803: Dessalines and François Capois defeated Rochambeau near Le Cap; the French evacuated in late 1803. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Verti%C3%A8res]
- Slavery in the French colonies was finally abolished by decree of 27 April 1848. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_of_slavery_in_France]
- Napoleon abandoned plans for an American empire and sold Louisiana to the United States (1803). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]

## Dessalines, independence and the 1804 massacre
- Jean-Jacques Dessalines: born 20 September 1758 into slavery on the Cormier plantation (born Jean-Jacques Duclos); assassinated 17 October 1806. Declared governor-general for life, then crowned Emperor Jacques I (6 October 1804). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Dessalines ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Domingue_expedition]
- 1 January 1804, Gonaïves: declaration of independence, renaming the country Haiti after an Indigenous (Taíno/Arawak) name. The text was written and read by his secretary Louis Boisrond-Tonnerre. Haiti was the first independent nation of Latin America and the Caribbean and the second in the Americas after the United States. The only two known copies of the original printing were found by Julia Gaffield in the UK National Archives in 2010–2011. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Declaration_of_Independence ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- Boisrond-Tonnerre (attributed): "For our declaration of independence, we should have the skin of a white man for parchment, his skull for an inkwell, his blood for ink, and a bayonet for a pen!" [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Declaration_of_Independence]
- 1804 massacre: from February to 22 April 1804, on Dessalines's orders (decree of 22 February 1804), 3,000–5,000 remaining white French civilians were killed, largely eliminating the white population. Exempted: Polish deserters, German colonists, some doctors and others. Philippe Girard calls it genocide and points to fear of French reinvasion and re-enslavement among motives; Dessalines had also had about 800 sick French soldiers executed in November 1803. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1804_Haiti_massacre ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- The 1805 constitution declared all citizens "Black" (including naturalised Poles and Germans) and barred whites from owning land. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1804_Haiti_massacre ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- Dessalines bound labourers to plantations (abolishing the whip, shortening the working day). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- Death tolls are rough estimates: HR gives about 200,000 Black dead 1791–1803 and French losses of 57,000–75,000; Scheina estimated 350,000 Haitians and 50,000 European troops. British losses 1793–98 about 100,000 dead, wounded or disabled. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]

## The indemnity of 1825
- On 3 July 1825 Baron de Mackau arrived at Port-au-Prince with warships (14 in all, 528 cannon); a royal ordinance of Charles X dated 17 April 1825 recognised Haiti's independence in return for 150 million francs, in five annual instalments of 30 million (first due 31 December 1825), to compensate former colonists, plus a 50% cut in duties on French imports. Haiti's senate accepted on 11 July 1825; President Jean-Pierre Boyer agreed to avoid invasion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_independence_debt]
- The first instalment was about six times Haiti's annual revenue (NYT); Haiti borrowed it from French bank Ternaux Gandolphe — the "double debt". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_independence_debt]
- On 12 February 1838 France reduced the total to 90 million francs (i.e. 60 million remaining) payable over 30 years. Final indemnity payment: 1883 per Wikipedia, 1888 per the NYT; but loans taken to pay it were only paid off in 1947 (last payments to National City Bank of New York). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_independence_debt]
- 2022 New York Times series "The Ransom" (May 2022, Apuzzo, Gebrekidan, Méheut, Porter et al.): Haiti paid about 112 million francs, about $560 million in 2022 dollars; with fifteen economists it estimated the lost growth at $21 billion to $115 billion over two centuries (low figure if the money had stayed in Haiti and grown at its historical pace; high figure if Haiti had grown like its Latin American neighbours). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_independence_debt ; https://eji.org/news/haitis-forced-payments-to-enslavers-cost-economy-21-billion-the-new-york-times-found/]
- Later loans from French bank CIC (1874–75) took 40% of capital in fees; Thomas Piketty called it "neocolonialism through debt". In 2003 President Aristide demanded over $21 billion in restitution; Hollande called the money "the ransom of independence"; in 2016 the French parliament repealed the 1825 ordinance symbolically; in 2025 France created a commission to study the debt's impact. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_independence_debt]
- France's recognition came 21 years after independence; the United States did not recognise Haiti until 1862. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_independence_debt ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]

## Global echoes
- White refugees from Saint-Domingue, often with enslaved people, settled in Philadelphia, Baltimore, New York, Charleston and New Orleans/southern Louisiana — the first US refugee crisis — and raised southern planters' fears of revolt. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1804_Haiti_massacre]
- Jefferson, a slaveholder, refused diplomatic relations and imposed a trade embargo on Haiti, regarding a successful slave revolt as a dangerous example (Dubois). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- Gabriel's planned rebellion near Richmond, Virginia (1800): Gabriel and 25 followers hanged. 1811 German Coast uprising in Louisiana, the largest US slave revolt. Aponte conspiracy, Cuba, 1812, as Cuba expanded sugar slavery to replace Haiti's output. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel%27s_Rebellion ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aponte_conspiracy]
- Pétion gave Simón Bolívar refuge (from late 1815) and arms, men and supplies, asking in return that he abolish slavery; sources disagree on how fully Bolívar kept the pledge. Yet Bolívar left Haiti out of the 1826 Congress of Panama, reportedly to avoid offending the US. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_P%C3%A9tion ; https://daily.jstor.org/bolivar-haiti/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_independence_debt]
- Haitian rulers offered refuge or citizenship to people escaping slavery and to African American emigrants. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- France's revolutionary turbulence also reached Quebec, where early sympathy for the Revolution turned to hostility. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]

## Each revolution exposes the other's contradictions
- The Declaration of 1789 did not say whether its equality applied to women, the enslaved or colonial subjects; planters read it as a chance for colonial self-rule, free people of colour as a claim to equal rights, and the enslaved revolt "radicalized the slavery question and forced French leaders to recognize the full meaning of their stated ideology." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- Abolition was delayed until February 1794 because it clashed with slave-owners' property rights and fears for trade; the Constitution of 1793's new Declaration excluded the colonies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- Edward Said on *The Black Jacobins*: "events in France and in Haiti criss-cross and answer each other like voices in a fugue." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Black_Jacobins]
- Toussaint, imprisoned, wrote: "Without a doubt I owe this treatment to my colour." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toussaint_Louverture]

## Historians
- C. L. R. James (Trinidadian), *The Black Jacobins: Toussaint L'Ouverture and the San Domingo Revolution*, London: Secker & Warburg, 1938; Marxist, written in the shadow of Franco and Stalin; places Haiti within the French Revolution and argues the enslaved embraced revolutionary ideals more consistently than the French. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Black_Jacobins]
- Laurent Dubois (University of Virginia): *Avengers of the New World* (Belknap/Harvard, 2004); *A Colony of Citizens* (2005 Frederick Douglass Prize); *Haiti: The Aftershocks of History* (2012). Reviewers describe *Avengers* as updating James with Atlantic and African scholarship and arguing for the revolution's centrality to world history and universal rights. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurent_Dubois ; https://h-france.net/vol5reviews/sepinwall2.html]
- Lynn Hunt (UCLA, b. 1945): *Politics, Culture, and Class in the French Revolution* (1984); *Inventing Human Rights* (2007), which argues human rights depended on a new capacity to empathise with strangers, fostered in part by novels and revulsion at torture. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynn_Hunt ; https://www.ethicsandinternationalaffairs.org/journal/inventing-human-rights-a-history-by-lynn-hunt]
- François Furet (1927–1997): former Communist (left 1956), led the "revisionist" school; *Penser la Révolution française* (1978; English *Interpreting the French Revolution*, 1981) attacked the Marxist "revolutionary catechism", stressed politics and ideology, and linked revolutionary ideology to 20th-century totalitarianism; argued the revolutionaries' utopian commitment drove the Terror. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Furet ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- Albert Soboul (1914–1982, born in French Algeria): Sorbonne chair; Marxist-republican tradition (Jaurès, Mathiez, Lefebvre); thesis on the Parisian sans-culottes of Year II (1958); saw the Revolution as class struggle and the Terror as a necessary response to external and internal threats. Critics deny the sans-culottes were a class. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Soboul ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- Sudhir Hazareesingh (b. 1961): *Black Spartacus: The Epic Life of Toussaint Louverture* (2020; Allen Lane / FSG), 2021 Wolfson History Prize; emphasises Toussaint's blend of European, African and Caribbean traditions. "Black Spartacus" was Laveaux's name for Toussaint. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudhir_Hazareesingh ; https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/sudhir-hazareesingh/black-spartacus/print]
- Michel-Rolph Trouillot (1949–2012), Haitian-born anthropologist (University of Chicago): *Silencing the Past: Power and the Production of History* (1995), chapter "An Unthinkable History: The Haitian Revolution as a Non-Event". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silencing_the_Past ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel-Rolph_Trouillot]

## Debates
- Marxist vs revisionist: Lefebvre and Soboul read the Revolution as a bourgeois/class revolution with a popular sans-culotte movement and a Terror forced by war and counter-revolution; Alfred Cobban (*The Myth of the French Revolution*, 1955; *Social Interpretation*, 1964) and Furet (with Richet, 1965; *Penser*, 1978) saw primarily political conflict and ideological drift (Furet: ideology made the Terror; a middle position blames circumstances and war). Since the 1990s Western scholars have largely dropped class-struggle explanations without agreeing on a replacement; attention has moved to political culture (Hunt), regions, gender and transnational/colonial history. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Furet ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Soboul]
- Role of ideas: Jonathan Israel says "radical Enlightenment" drove the Revolution; Cobban says revolutionaries mostly improvised practical solutions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- Vendée as "genocide": Secher (1986) and Solzhenitsyn (1993 speech) vs McPhee, Vovelle and most specialists; a 2007 bill to recognise it as genocide was denounced by historians. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_the_Vend%C3%A9e]
- Bois Caïman: Léon-François Hoffmann (1990) argued it was a myth from the hostile colonist Dalmas; Geggus accepts it happened (dating it 21 August, Choiseul plantation) but doubts details such as Boukman's presence; Rachel Beauvoir-Dominique's oral-history work supports its reality. [source: https://sites.duke.edu/blackatlantic/sample-page/storytelling-and-representation-of-bois-caiman/genealogy-of-bois-caiman-textual-sources/debates-about-bois-caiman]
- Was Haiti's revolution an offshoot of France's? James saw them as intertwined; many recent historians stress the independent agency of the enslaved, many African-born, and African and Vodou influences (Hazareesingh stresses Toussaint's hybrid traditions). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Black_Jacobins ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution ; https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/sudhir-hazareesingh/black-spartacus/print]
- Impact on slavery in the Americas: Geggus asks "How much of a difference did it make?" and answers a limited amount, since slavery flourished for decades; W. E. B. Du Bois argued it pushed Britain toward abolition. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- 1804 massacre: Girard and others call it genocide ("subaltern genocide" for Robins, Jones, Moses); Girard notes both sides considered genocidal strategies and stresses fear of reinvasion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1804_Haiti_massacre]
- Abolition of 1794: idealism or expedience? The commissioners acted to keep the colony from Britain and Spain; Régent stresses limited real freedom under labour rules. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_4_February_1794 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution]
- Indemnity and Haiti's poverty: NYT economists' $21–115 billion range is a model-based estimate; some historians and activists call the debt "odious"; Piketty calls it "neocolonialism through debt". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_independence_debt]
- Toussaint's constitution: he abolished slavery for ever yet kept forced plantation labour and allowed importing workers via the slave trade, and made himself governor for life. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toussaint_Louverture]

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use
- C. L. R. James's passage on "the transformation of slaves, trembling in hundreds before a single white man..." — exact wording and location (preface?) not confirmed; use "Rivers of blood..." instead.
- Exact Trouillot definition of "unthinkable" ("that which one cannot conceive within the range of possible alternatives") — not checked against text.

- HR's statement that Sonthonax proclaimed an end to slavery on "29 August 1792" — error (it was 1793).
- HR's statement that on "4 April 1792 the Legislative Assembly granted freedom to slaves" — wrong; the 4 April 1792 decree gave rights to free men of colour only.
- HR's claim that the US embargo on Haiti "lasted until 1862" — not verified (the 1806 embargo appears to have lapsed earlier); only non-recognition until 1862 is safe.
- HR's figures of "2 million" total casualties of the French Revolution and "162,000" Caribbean casualties — unsourced, do not use.
- Paul Fregosi "Haiti was hell, but Haiti was rich" block quote — quoted second-hand via Perry; avoid.
- "Saint-Domingue produced 40% of the world's sugar" variants — only "40% of sugar imported by France and Britain" (HR via Perry) was seen.
- Exact date of the first Bolívar abolition decrees and whether he fully kept his promise to Pétion — sources conflict.
- Dessalines's crowning date (6 October 1804 in two articles; other sources give 8 October) — use "October 1804".
- Labelling Haiti in 1804 "the first Black republic": Dessalines made himself emperor in 1804; the state was formally an empire 1804–06 (a republic in the south from 1806/07). Say "first independent Black-led state" or qualify.
