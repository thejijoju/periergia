## 1. Political order: caudillos, liberals and conservatives, the Church
- **Caudillo**: from Latin *capitellum* ("head"); a personalist leader wielding military and political power, a term historically tied to Spain and post-independence Spanish America; often used pejoratively. The early 19th century is "sometimes called 'The Age of Caudillos'", with Rosas and Santa Anna dominating national politics. [source: CAU]
- Explanations: John Lynch roots *caudillismo* not in the distant Spanish past but in the independence wars, which left a power vacuum. Caudillos imposed order, often violently, in rural areas lacking state institutions, and acted as patrons/providers to followers. [source: CAU]
- E. Bradford Burns called some "folk caudillos" (Facundo Quiroga, Güemes, above all Rosas), popular with the rural poor and despised by Europeanised urban elites; he blamed elite contempt for much of the caudillos' negative image. Political scientist Peter H. Smith calls Rosas, Diego Portales of Chile ("whose system lasted nearly a century") and Porfirio Díaz builders of "integrative dictatorships" that curbed centrifugal "federalism". [source: CAU]
- In Mexico, after the US war, regional caudillos Juan Álvarez (Guerrero) and Santiago Vidaurri (Nuevo León–Coahuila) ousted Santa Anna in the Revolution of Ayutla (1855), bringing liberals to power; François Chevalier called Álvarez a "good *cacique*" who protected the mainly Indigenous and mestizo peasants of Guerrero. [source: CAU]
- **Rosas** (30 March 1793 – 14 March 1877): wealthy Buenos Aires landowner; by 1830 the province's 10th-largest landowner (300,000 cattle, 420,000 acres) with a private gaucho militia. Elected governor of Buenos Aires 6 December 1829 with "extraordinary faculties"; title "Restorer of the Laws"; 1831 Federal Pact (provincial autonomy, Argentine Confederation); term ended 5 December 1832; Desert Campaign against Indigenous peoples 1833–34; returned 7 March 1835 with the *suma del poder público* ("sum of public power"). [source: ROS]
- Rosas's terror: the *Mazorca*, a parapolice arm of the *Sociedad Popular Restauradora*; "Modern estimates report around 2,000 people were killed from 1829 until 1852." Elections became a farce; cult of personality. The Jesuits, who refused to back him, were expelled; most clergy supported him. [source: ROS]
- Rosas and Afro-Argentines: a slave-owner who helped revive the slave trade in 1831 but abolished it in 1839; in 1836 ended automatic conscription of *libertos*; lifted the ban on *candombes*, attended them and donated to Afro-Argentine mutual-aid societies; "He remained popular among blacks and gauchos." [source: ROS]
- Rosas faced the French blockade (late 1830s) and an Anglo-French blockade (most of the late 1840s); by 1848 ruled all Argentina. Brazil's aid to Uruguay led him to declare war (August 1851, Platine War); defeated by Urquiza at Caseros, 3 February 1852; exile in Southampton, where he farmed until his death. [source: ROS; EB]
- Rosas memory: Argentine writers called the era "The Tyranny of Rosas"; a nationalist *revisionismo* (from the 1930s) tried to rehabilitate him; President Menem repatriated his remains on 30 September 1989 and a week later pardoned nearly 300 military personnel convicted of Dirty War abuses. [source: ROS]
- **Santa Anna**: fought as a royalist officer, switched to the insurgents in 1821; repeatedly switched between liberals and conservatives; styled himself "Napoleon of the West" and later "His Most Serene Highness". Historian Alexander Dawson counts eleven times he assumed the presidency (University of Texas Libraries gives the same, adding he was president for only about six years in total). [source: SA]
- Santa Anna lost his left leg defending Veracruz against the French in the Pastry War (1838) and had it buried with military honours; in 1844 a mob dug it up and dragged it through the streets. He sold the Mesilla Valley to the US (Gadsden Purchase, 1853–54), was ousted by the Plan of Ayutla (1855), was allowed back in 1874 and died in Mexico City in 1876. [source: SA; MAW]
- 1833–34: Vice-President Valentín Gómez Farías's anticlerical reforms (abolishing the compulsory tithe, among others); the Catholic priest-intellectual José María Luis Mora saw sale of Church property as the key to making Mexico "a liberal, progressive nation of small landowners". Santa Anna dissolved Congress on 12 June 1834 and formed a "Catholic, centralist and conservative government", with the Church promising 30,000–40,000 pesos a month. [source: SA]
- **Páez** (13 June 1790 – 6 May 1873): of humble origins; "Centaur of the Plains"; led Venezuela's separation from Gran Colombia; president 1830–35, 1839–43, 1861–63; dominated Venezuelan politics for about three decades, in alliance with "the conservative oligarchy", which controlled wealth but lacked popular support, whereas Páez was popular. Died in exile in New York. [source: PAEZ]
- **Rafael Carrera** (1814–1865): mestizo, illiterate, a former swineherd; led a pro-Catholic peasant and Indigenous uprising in Guatemala (1837, during a cholera epidemic) against the liberal government; president 1844–48 and 1851–65, president for life from 1854. [source: CAR]
- **The Church question, Ecuador**: Gabriel García Moreno (1821–1875), conservative president 1861–65 and 1869–75; the 1869 constitution made Catholicism the state religion and required candidates and voters to be Catholic; he alone among the world's rulers protested the Pope's loss of the Papal States, and had Ecuador consecrated to the Sacred Heart; assassinated in 1875. [source: GM]
- **Colombia**: the Thousand Days' War (17 October 1899 – 21 November 1902), liberals against the conservative-led government; an estimated 100,000–180,000 dead, about 2.5–3.8% of the population, the deadliest civil war in Colombian history; it led to the loss of Panama (1903). [source: TDW]

## 2. Brazil: a distinct path, empire to republic
- The Empire of Brazil (1822–1889) was a parliamentary constitutional monarchy under Pedro I and Pedro II; EB contrasts its stability, freedom of speech and comparatively broad elections with the Hispanic republics, "albeit with legal restrictions on women and slaves", who were property, not citizens. [source: EB]
- Pedro II (2 December 1825 – 5 December 1891) became emperor at five when Pedro I abdicated (1831); a weak regency followed. The 1834 *Ato Adicional* gave provinces more autonomy and fed regional revolts, including the Ragamuffin War (1835–1845), the Cabanagem (1835–1840) and the Balaiada (1838–1841). [source: EB; P2]
- On 23 July 1840 the General Assembly declared the 14-year-old Pedro II of age. Roderick Barman: by 1840 politicians "had lost all faith in their ability to rule the country on their own. They accepted Pedro II as an authority figure whose presence was indispensable for the country's survival." [source: P2; EB]
- Brazil won the Platine War (1851–52, which toppled Rosas), the Uruguayan War and the Paraguayan War. [source: EB]
- The 1871 Free Birth law lost the monarchy the ultraconservatives' loyalty; the Golden Law of 13 May 1888 (signed by Princess Isabel while Pedro II was in Europe) was "the final blow", pushing ultraconservatives and the coffee planters behind them toward republicanism. The heir, Isabel, was not considered acceptable as a female monarch. [source: EB]
- Army grievances: after the Paraguayan War officers felt undervalued; Comtean positivism spread in the military schools; the Positivist Church of Brazil (from 1881; Miguel Lemos and Raimundo Teixeira Mendes) campaigned for abolition and a republic. [source: PRB]
- 15 November 1889: a bloodless military coup in Rio led by Marshal Deodoro da Fonseca (himself a monarchist) deposed Pedro II and the Liberal prime minister, the Viscount of Ouro Preto; Deodoro headed the provisional government. Pedro II, after a 58-year reign, accepted exile and died in Paris in 1891. [source: PRB; EB; P2]
- The new flag, adopted 19 November 1889, carries the motto *Ordem e Progresso* ("Order and Progress"), derived from Comte's "L'amour pour principe et l'ordre pour base; le progrès pour but". [source: FLAG]
- The Old Republic (1889–1930): "coffee with milk" (*café com leite*) politics, in which São Paulo (coffee) and Minas Gerais (dairy) oligarchies dominated the presidency. [source: MCP]
- War of Canudos (1896–97), Bahia: a millenarian community led by Antônio Conselheiro, branded "monarchist" by the press; three federal expeditions failed before the fourth destroyed it. Death estimates: Euclides da Cunha (1902) about 30,000; Robert M. Levine about 15,000; Joel Singer about 5,000. [source: CAN]
- Revolt of the Lash (*Revolta da Chibata*), November 1910: a naval mutiny in Rio by Afro-Brazilian and mixed-race sailors against flogging by white officers, led by João Cândido; the trigger was 250 lashes given to the sailor Marcelino Rodrigues Menezes (the number is disputed). [source: LASH]

## 3. Mexico
### The US–Mexican War seen from Mexico
- In Mexico it is called the "United States intervention in Mexico" (*Intervención estadounidense*); 25 April 1846 – 2 February 1848; it followed the US annexation of Texas (1845), which Mexico still regarded as its own. Texas claimed the Rio Grande border, Mexico the Nueces. [source: MAW]
- Polk, elected in 1844 on an expansionist platform, sent John Slidell (November 1845) with an offer of $25 million for the Rio Grande border plus Alta California and New Mexico. [source: MAW]
- Mexican politics during the war: presidents lasted months, weeks or days; José Joaquín de Herrera, willing to talk, was denounced as a *vendepatria* (country-seller) and overthrown by the conservative Mariano Paredes (December 1845). [source: MAW]
- Before the war, Comanche, Apache and Navajo raids reached hundreds of miles into northern Mexico, which the Mexican state did not effectively control. [source: MAW]
- Scott landed at Veracruz (March 1847) and took Mexico City in September 1847. Chapultepec, 13 September 1847: six cadets, the *Niños Héroes*, died; the legend that Juan Escutia leapt from the castle wrapped in the flag is a legend (NH says "Legend has it"). The date is a civic holiday. [source: MAW; NH]
- The San Patricios (Saint Patrick's Battalion), mainly Irish and German Catholic deserters from the US Army, fought for Mexico; about half were hanged after capture at Churubusco (August 1847). [source: MAW]
- Mexican toll: including civilians and disease, Mexican deaths "may have reached 25,000" (Clodfelter), against 13,283 US deaths. [source: MAW]
- Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (2 February 1848): Mexico ceded California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and parts of New Mexico, Colorado and Wyoming and recognised the Rio Grande; the US paid $15 million and assumed $3.25 million of debts owed to US citizens; Mexicans in the ceded lands could move or take US citizenship, but their land grants were often not honoured. [source: TGH]
- Mexican reckoning: in the aftermath, Ignacio Ramírez, Guillermo Prieto, José María Iglesias and others, edited by Ramón Alcaraz, wrote that "the true origin of the war" was "the insatiable ambition of the United States, favored by our weakness"; translated by Albert Ramsey and published in the US in 1850. [source: MAW]
- The Caste War broke out in Yucatán in 1847, during the US war; Yucatán's government even offered sovereignty to the United States in return for protection. [source: MAW; CW]

### La Reforma and Juárez
- Juárez: born 21 March 1806 in San Pablo Guelatao, Oaxaca, to Zapotec peasants; orphaned at three; a shepherd who spoke only Zapotec and could not read until he moved to Oaxaca City at 12, where he worked as a domestic servant; studied for the priesthood, then law; governor of Oaxaca; married Margarita Maza. [source: JUA]
- The Reform laws: Ley Juárez (23 November 1855) ended the jurisdiction of military and Church courts over civil cases (*fueros*); Ley Lerdo (June 1856) forced corporate bodies to sell their real estate, aimed at the Church but also at Indigenous communal lands (*ejidos*); Ley Iglesias (11 April 1857) on parish fees; civil registry (27 January 1857); Constitution of 5 February 1857. [source: REF]
- REF: liberals "were not opposed to the church as a spiritual institution but rather sought a secular state". The Church excommunicated officials who swore to uphold the constitution. [source: REF; RW]
- Reform War (17 December 1857 – 11 January 1861): began with General Zuloaga's Plan of Tacubaya; President Comonfort resigned and Juárez, as president of the Supreme Court, succeeded him constitutionally (January 1858). Called the "worst civil war to hit Mexico between the War of Independence of 1810–21 and the Revolution of 1910–20". [source: RW]
- 7 July 1859: Juárez's programme decreed separation of church and state and promised education, railways, the subdivision of great estates and immigration. He also negotiated the McLane–Ocampo Treaty (perpetual US transit rights across the Isthmus of Tehuantepec; JUA lists it among his controversial measures). [source: JUA]

### The French intervention (1861–1867)
- July 1861: Juárez suspended foreign-debt payments; Spain, France and Britain signed the Convention of London; Spanish General Prim occupied Veracruz on 14 December 1861 with 6,200 troops from Cuba; 2,000 French and 700 British joined on 7 January 1862. Britain and Spain withdrew. [source: FI]
- Mexican conservatives, defeated in the Reform War, invited the intervention; "the Catholic Church, conservatives, much of the upper-class ... and some indigenous communities" collaborated. [source: FI]
- 5 May 1862, Battle of Puebla: about 5,000 Mexicans under Ignacio Zaragoza (with Porfirio Díaz) beat about 6,000 French; French casualties 476, Mexican 227. Napoleon III then sent 30,000 troops; Puebla fell on 17 May 1863 after a siege. [source: FI]
- An "Assembly of Notables" chosen under French control voted for monarchy (8 July 1863) and invited Maximilian of Habsburg. Maximilian "proved to be of liberal inclination" and kept some of Juárez's measures. [source: JUA; FI]
- An Egyptian (Sudanese) battalion of 453 men served with the French around Veracruz from 1863, suffering 126 casualties before withdrawal in 1867. [source: FI]
- October 1865: Maximilian's "Black Decree" ordered death for captured republican fighters; it "backfired by creating liberal martyrs". [source: FI]
- Juárez "never left the national territory"; the US never withdrew recognition of his government. French troops withdrew; Maximilian, Miramón and Mejía were shot on the Cerro de las Campanas, Querétaro, 19 June 1867. [source: FI]
- Infobox casualties: imperial/French side 31,962 killed (including 11,000 executed); Mexican republicans about 14,000 dead (figures from Clodfelter; treat as estimates). [source: FI]
- Juárez's triumphant re-entry into Mexico City: the 500-peso note's caption (quoted in JUA) dates it 13 July 1867; his manifesto is dated 15 July 1867 (sources disagree on which day he entered; say "mid-July 1867"). He was re-elected in 1871; Díaz rebelled (Plan de la Noria); Juárez called him "a latter-day Santa Anna". He died of a heart attack on 18 July 1872. His birthday is a national holiday, the only one for an individual. [source: JUA]

### The Porfiriato (1876–1911) and 1910
- Díaz seized power in 1876 (Plan of Tuxtepec, against Lerdo de Tejada), ruled 1876–80, let Manuel González serve 1880–84, returned 1884 and stayed until 1911; the term *Porfiriato* was coined by Daniel Cosío Villegas. Slogan: "order and progress"; policy *pan o palo* ("bread or bludgeon"). [source: POR]
- The *Rurales* (rural police, created under Juárez) were expanded under Díaz and moved by train to crush revolts; railways and telegraphs, built with foreign capital, tied the regions to the capital. [source: POR]
- The *Científicos*: technocratic advisers grafting Comte's positivism and Spencer's social Darwinism; they saw themselves as superior to a "largely rural, largely indigenous and mixed-race" population. Gabino Barreda, who studied under Comte in Paris (1847–51), founded the National Preparatory School (1868). Finance minister José Yves Limantour was a leading member. [source: POR; CIEN]
- Village lands were expropriated as haciendas (often foreign-owned) expanded; Emiliano Zapata emerged in Anenecuilco, Morelos, to defend village lands. [source: POR]
- US mining capital flowed into the north after the 1873 silver-price fall; Díaz's advisers Matías Romero and Manuel Zamacona advised a "peaceful invasion" of US capital. [source: POR; DIAZ]
- Labour: Cananea copper strike (Sonora, 1906; US-owned mine, Arizona Rangers called in) and Río Blanco textile strike (Veracruz, January 1907). [source: MR; POR]
- 1908 interview with James Creelman (*Pearson's Magazine*): Díaz said Mexico was ready for democracy and he would retire. [source: DIAZ]
- Madero, jailed for challenging Díaz in 1910, issued the Plan of San Luis Potosí (dated 5 October 1910; slogan "Sufragio Efectivo, No Re-elección"), calling for revolt on 20 November 1910. After the fall of Ciudad Juárez, the Treaty of Ciudad Juárez (21 May 1911) sent Díaz into exile in Paris. Zapata's Plan of Ayala followed in November 1911. "Perhaps 1.5 million people died" in the Revolution (1910–1920), with nearly 200,000 refugees. [source: MR]

## 4. Wars between states
### The Paraguayan War (War of the Triple Alliance), 1864–1870
- Paraguay against Argentina, Brazil and Uruguay; "the deadliest inter-state war in Latin American history". It grew out of the Uruguayan War; on 12–13 November 1864 Paraguay seized the Brazilian ship *Marquês de Olinda*. The secret Treaty of the Triple Alliance was signed on 1 May 1865; Article 6 required the war to continue until López's government ceased to exist. [source: PW]
- Battle of Tuyutí (24 May 1866): over 12,000 Paraguayan casualties to 6,000 Allied. By 1867 Paraguay had lost 60,000 men and conscripted another 60,000 from slaves and children. Asunción was sacked by Brazilian troops (1869). [source: PW; SAR]
- López was killed at Cerro Corá on 1 March 1870; Argentine and Brazilian troops occupied Paraguay until 1876; Paraguay ceded disputed territory. [source: PW]
- **Population loss (give as a range):** pre-war population estimates range from 300,000 to 1,337,000 (14 estimates). Vera Blinn Reber (1988) estimated as few as 21,000 deaths (about 7%) and argued losses "have been enormously exaggerated". Thomas Whigham and Barbara Potthast (1999, *Latin American Research Review*, "The Paraguayan Rosetta Stone"; reply 2002) put the 1864 population at 420,000–450,000 and survivors in 1870–71 at 150,000–160,000, only 28,000 of them adult men: 60–70% dead. The figure of 1.2 million dead (90%) rests on an 1857 census "that was a government invention". Infobox: Paraguayan deaths likely 175,000–300,000; Allied 50,000–200,000; total 150,000–500,000. [source: PW]
- The 1871 census: 221,079 inhabitants — 106,254 women, 28,746 men and 86,079 children. [source: PW]
- Women: Paraguayan peasant women became almost the sole agricultural producers and served as nurses and auxiliaries; government claims of women's combat battalions are of "doubtful veracity". Afro-Brazilian *vivandeiras* carried tents, cooked and washed for Brazilian troops, work the government called "service to their male kin, not the nation". [source: PW]
- Brazilian infantry: some Argentine revisionist and Paraguayan accounts disparaged it as recruited largely from slaves and the landless poor (largely Black). [source: PW]
- Guaraní identity: López used nationalist rhetoric in Spanish and Guaraní, binding Guaraní speakers to the national project. [source: PW]

### The War of the Pacific, 1879–1884 (brief; see SIR chapter)
- Chile against a Bolivian–Peruvian alliance over the Atacama; also called the "Saltpeter War" and "Ten Cents War" (after the Bolivian ten-centavo tax). Battle of Iquique (21 May 1879): the Peruvian ironclad *Huáscar* sank the *Esmeralda*. Bolivia withdrew after Tacna (26 May 1880); Chile occupied Lima in January 1881; Peruvian irregulars fought on in the sierra. Treaty of Ancón (20 October 1883): Chile took Tarapacá; Bolivia lost Litoral (truce 1884, treaty 1904), becoming landlocked; Tacna–Arica settled in 1929. [source: WP]
- Chincha Islands War: Spain seized Peru's guano-rich Chincha Islands in an attempt to reassert influence over former colonies; fought against Peru, Chile, Ecuador and Bolivia (CHW gives 1865–1879). [source: CHW]

## 5. Indigenous peoples
- **Caste War of Yucatán (1847–1915; skirmishes to 1933)**: Maya revolt against Yucatecan elites, "one of the most successful modern Native American revolts". Causes: land loss to sugar and henequen estates, debt labour and taxes. Jacinto Pat wrote in 1848: "what we want is liberty and not oppression". Cecilio Chi and Pat attacked Tepich on 30 July 1847; by spring 1848 the Maya held most of the peninsula except Campeche and Mérida. From 1850 the "Talking Cross" inspired the independent Maya state of Chan Santa Cruz, recognised by Britain for trade with British Honduras, which sold it arms. Yucatán's government sold Maya prisoners into slavery in Cuba (INAH identified the slave ship *La Unión* in 2020). The Mexican army occupied Chan Santa Cruz in 1901; formal end 1915 under Governor Salvador Alvarado. [source: CW]
- **Conquest of the Desert (Argentina, 1870s–1880s; main campaign 1878–1885)**: directed by General Julio A. Roca; the 1879 sweep used 6,000 soldiers with Remington rifles, reaching the Río Negro and killing 1,313 and capturing over 15,000 (COD's narrative). COD lead: "more than 1,000 Mapuches" killed, more than 15,000 displaced, part enslaved; captives "became servants or prisoners". Preceded by Calfucurá's 1872 raid (6,000 warriors; 300 settlers killed; 200,000 cattle). Roca became president. Debate: apologists call it a civilising mission and defence; revisionists label it genocide (Jens Andermann: contemporary sources show an intent to exterminate). [source: COD]
- Rosas's earlier Desert Campaign (1833–34) also targeted Indigenous peoples. [source: ROS; COD]
- **Occupation ("Pacification") of Araucanía (Chile, 1861–1883)**: Mapuche territory, de facto independent through the colonial era, was incorporated by military advance and settlement planned by Colonel Cornelio Saavedra on the model of the US frontier; some Mapuche groups allied with Chile, the Arribanos resisted, others stayed neutral; famine in winter 1869 and smallpox; thousands of Mapuche died. Formally ended with the refounding of Villarrica on 1 January 1883. [source: ARA]
- **Yaqui (Sonora)**: Bácum massacre (1868): 120 men, women and children killed when the army shelled a church holding 400 prisoners. Mazocoba massacre (1900): several hundred killed. By 1903 the regime decided to deport both peaceful and rebel Yaqui to Yucatán and Oaxaca; Governor Rafael Izábal led "organized manhunts" 1904–09. Yaqui joined all major factions in the Revolution. [source: YW]
- Yaqui deportation numbers (range; weak sources): YW gives 8,000–15,000 taken prisoner (citation needed); John Kenneth Turner gave about 15,000 rounded up in 1904–09; an INAH study counts 6,432 Yaqui on Yucatán henequen haciendas at the end of 1908, probably an undercount. Say "thousands, perhaps more than ten thousand". [source: YW; search summaries of https://www.mexconnect.com/?p=7741 and https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/diariodecampo/article/download/15594/16649/32667]
- Reform-era land law: the Ley Lerdo forced Indigenous communities to sell communal lands; liberal hopes of yeoman farmers were not met, and community integrity was undermined. [source: REF; POR]
- Peru abolished Indigenous tribute in 1854 in the guano era. [source: HPE]
- Amazonia: the rubber boom (1879–1912) saw enslavement, debt peonage (*enganche*) and slave raids (*correrías*); the Putumayo genocide under Julio César Arana's Peruvian Amazon Company (registered in London 1907) killed an estimated 32,000 to 40,000+ (PUT infobox); exposed by Benjamín Saldaña Rocca, Walter Hardenburg and Roger Casement. Colombia apologised in 2024 for rubber-boom deaths of about 60,000 Indigenous people in its Amazon. ARC cautions that the impact "should not be exaggerated nor extrapolated to the whole region". [source: ARC; PUT]

## 6. Afro-Latin Americans, Cuba and 1898
- Abolition dates: use the Abolition chapter's list (Chile 1823; Mexico 1829; New Granada 1851 [Nationalism chapter]; Argentina 1853; Peru and Venezuela 1854; Puerto Rico 1873; Cuba 1880/1886; Brazil 1888). [cross-ref]
- Afro-Argentines: the 1778 census showed high Black and mixed shares in interior cities (54% Santiago del Estero, 52% Catamarca, 46% Salta, 44% Córdoba and Tucumán). The old story of their "disappearance" (wars, the 1871 yellow fever, low intermarriage) is challenged: Black soldiers "were not the majority" of armies, and reclassification into ambiguous census categories (e.g., *pardo*, *trigueño*) helped make them statistically invisible (Erika Denise Edwards, "The making of a White nation", 2018). [source: AFA]
- **Ten Years' War (1868–1878)**: 10 October 1868, sugar-mill owner Carlos Manuel de Céspedes issued the "10th of October Manifesto" at La Demajagua (the *Grito de Yara*), freed his slaves and called men of all races to fight. Guáimaro constitutional assembly, 10 April 1869. Pact of Zanjón, 10 February 1878. Infobox: about 50,000 Cuban dead (Martí's estimate) and 81,248 Spanish (mostly disease). [source: TYW]
- At Guáimaro, Ana Betancourt (1832–1901) argued that, having freed the enslaved, Cuba should now emancipate women; it is counted among the first Cuban feminist statements. [source: AB]
- **Antonio Maceo** (14 June 1845 – 7 December 1896), "the Bronze Titan", son of the Venezuelan Marcos Maceo and the free mulata Mariana Grajales; rose from private to general; his colour and humble origin delayed promotion because of racism among aristocratic patriots; Spain spread propaganda that he sought a race war. At Los Mangos de Baraguá (15 March 1878) he refused peace without abolition and independence (Protest of Baraguá). Killed near Punta Brava, Havana province. Mariana Grajales (born 1815) ran camp hospitals and is a national icon. [source: MAC; MGC; TYW]
- **Martí** (28 January 1853 – 19 May 1895): founded the Cuban Revolutionary Party (early 1892) and its paper *Patria* (March 1892); his speeches to tobacco workers in Tampa and Key West are counted his greatest political achievement; essay "Mi Raza" treated race as a social construct. Landed with Máximo Gómez at Playitas in April 1895; killed at Dos Ríos, 19 May 1895. *Versos sencillos* supplied the lyrics of "Guantanamera". [source: MARTI; CWI]
- **War of Independence (1895–98)**: began 24 February 1895. About 40% of the insurgent officers were men of colour. Of 71 supply missions from exiles, 33 were stopped by the US Coast Guard (a different passage gives 60 attempts with only one success; figures inconsistent, say "most were intercepted, many by US authorities"). [source: CWI]
- Weyler's *reconcentración* (order of 21 October 1896): rural people forced into fortified towns; John Lawrence Tone estimates 155,000–170,000 civilian deaths, nearly 10% of the population. Weyler was recalled after Cánovas's assassination (8 August 1897). [source: CWI]
- **1898 from Cuba's side**: Cuban forces under Calixto García supported the US siege of Santiago, but Shafter excluded them from the surrender (13–17 July 1898) and barred them from entering the city; García protested in a letter of 17 July and withdrew to the interior. [source: SOS; search summary of https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/educational-magazines/calixto-garcia]
- After the war the US ordered the independence army disbanded. The Teller Amendment barred annexation; the Platt Amendment (Army Appropriations Act, 1901) was written into Cuba's constitution on 12 June 1901 and the 1903 treaty, allowing US intervention and naval-base leases (Guantánamo); used for the 1906–09 occupation; partly dropped in 1934. [source: PLATT; PIC]
- Partido Independiente de Color, founded 1908 by Black veterans; its 1912 revolt in Oriente was crushed; estimates run up to 6,000 dead including civilians, and many Afro-Cubans were killed whether involved or not. [source: PIC]

## 7. The export economy, capital and labour
- **Guano (Peru)**: Humboldt studied guano at Callao in 1802. From 1840 the state nationalised guano (deal brokered by Francisco Quirós), and royalties became Peru's largest source of revenue. Thousands of South Chinese "coolies" worked as "virtual slaves"; by 1852 Chinese were two-thirds of guano miners, on eight-year contracts, many deceived into thinking they were bound for California. [source: GUA]
- The guano era (c. 1845–1866, under Ramón Castilla) paid off foreign debt and built the Lima–Callao railway; in 1849 Castilla's government authorised importing Chinese labour. [source: HPE (largely uncited — use for framing only)]
- Chinese in Peru: 80,000–100,000 contract labourers, about 95% Cantonese and almost all men, 1849–1874, mostly for sugar plantations and guano. Chinese in Cuba: 141,000 in 1847–1874; about 1% women; eight-year contracts. [source: CHP; CHC]
- **Coffee (Brazil)**: by the 1830s coffee was Brazil's largest export and about 30% of world output; by the 1840s 40%; second boom 1880s–1930s; by the early 20th century coffee was about three-quarters of export earnings and 16% of GNP; 1906 Taubaté Agreement (price "valorisation"). Historians debate whether planters were the most powerful actors in politics. [source: CPB]
- **Rubber (Amazon)**: boom c. 1879–1912; Manaus, Belém, Iquitos boomed; the "Acre question" border conflicts. [source: ARC]
- **Beef and grain (Argentina)**: frozen beef exports to Britain became highly profitable after refrigerated ships (1870s); the pampas drove growth 1860–1930; by 1913 Argentina was among the world's ten wealthiest states per capita. British capital in Argentina rose from just over £20 million (1880) to £157 million (1890), about two-thirds of foreign capital; Argentina was the first choice for British investment in Latin America until 1914. [source: EHA]
- Baring crisis (Panic of 1890): Barings nearly failed over Argentine debt (November 1890); a Bank of England consortium rescued it; Argentina's real GDP fell 11% in 1890–91. [source: BAR]
- **Immigration, Argentina**: Alberdi's maxim "to govern is to populate" shaped the 1853 constitution, which mandated promotion of European immigration. 3.8 million Italians and 1.5 million Spaniards arrived 1861–1920 (not all stayed). Population 4 million (1895) to 7.9 million (1914). Immigrants were 52% of the capital's population in 1895; the foreign-born share peaked at almost 30% nationally in 1914. Argentina had the second-largest number of immigrants after the US (8 million vs 27 million, IA's lead; period not specified). [source: IA]
- **Immigration, Brazil**: 1877–1903 about 71,000 a year, 58.49% Italians; the São Paulo immigrants' hostel opened in 1886; subsidised European immigration whitened the census population (non-white share 47% by 1890). Overall arrivals: 1.8 million Portuguese, 1.7 million Italians, 760,000 Spaniards. [source: IB]
- **US intervention after 1898**: Roosevelt Corollary (1904 State of the Union): intervention in any country guilty of "chronic wrongdoing, or an impotence which results in a general loosening of the ties of civilized society"; "international police power". Panama seceded from Colombia on 3 November 1903 with US backing (Bunau-Varilla's $100,000). The "Banana Wars" (term popularised by Lester Langley, 1983): US interventions in Cuba, Panama, Honduras, Nicaragua, Mexico (Veracruz 1914), Haiti (1915–34) and the Dominican Republic until the Good Neighbor policy (1934); United Fruit and Standard Fruit had stakes. [source: RC; PAN; BW]

## 8. Women's lives and roles
- Legal status: Brazil's empire placed "legal restrictions on women". [source: EB]
- Juana Manuela Gorriti (1818–1892): Argentine writer exiled under Rosas, married to the Bolivian Manuel Isidro Belzú; *La quena* (1851) made her recognised as the earliest novelist of what became Argentina; her Lima *tertulias* (salons) gathered Ricardo Palma, Manuel González Prada, Mercedes Cabello de Carbonera and Clorinda Matto de Turner to discuss literature, politics and women's rights. [source: JMG]
- Juana Manso (1819–1875): exiled under Rosas; founded *Álbum de señoritas* (1854); edited *Anales de la educación común* (founded by Sarmiento) from 1865 to 1875; in 1859 became principal of Buenos Aires's first co-educational school. [source: JPM]
- Clorinda Matto de Turner (1852–1909), Cusco: *Aves sin nido* (1889), a novel of Indigenous life and clerical abuse; excommunicated; forced into exile in Argentina. [source: CMT]
- Ana Betancourt (Guáimaro 1869), Mariana Grajales (Cuba), Paraguayan women in the war, Afro-Brazilian *vivandeiras*: see §§4, 6.
- Women in Mexico's Revolution: *soldaderas* ("Adelitas", from the corrido "La Adelita"); a few *coronelas* commanded troops, some dressing and identifying as men. [source: MR]
- Sarmiento brought mostly female teachers from the United States to train Argentine teachers. [source: SAR]

## 9. Intellectual currents
- **Sarmiento** (president of Argentina from 12 October 1868; died in Asunción 11 September 1888): wrote *Facundo* (1845; full title *Civilización i barbarie: Vida de Juan Facundo Quiroga*) in Chilean exile as an attack on Rosas; it opposes "civilisation" (Europe, North America, cities, Unitarians) to "barbarism" (the countryside, gauchos, caudillos, Federalists, Rosas). English translation by Mary Mann, 1868. He founded Latin America's first teacher-training school in Santiago (1842). Railways grew from 573 km (1868) to 1,331 km (1874) under his presidency. [source: FAC; SAR]
- **Positivism**: Mexico's *Científicos* and Barreda (§3); Brazil's army and Positivist Church, and "Order and Progress" on the flag (§2). [source: CIEN; PRB; FLAG]
- **Martí, "Nuestra América"**: published 1 January 1891 in New York's *Revista Ilustrada* (some sources say 10 January) and on 30 January in Mexico's *El Partido Liberal*. [source: MARTI; search summary noting the UPR course page]
- **Rodó, *Ariel* (1900)**: the Uruguayan José Enrique Rodó's "secular sermon to Latin American youth", using Shakespeare's Ariel (spirit, beauty) and Caliban (utilitarian materialism); attacks *nordomanía* (imitation of the United States) and positivism's utilitarianism; written in the aftermath of 1898; part of *modernismo*. [source: ARI; RODO]

## Debates
- **What made the caudillos?** Lynch: a product of the independence wars and the collapse of colonial institutions, not of an ancient Hispanic tradition. Burns: some were "folk caudillos" whose popularity with the rural poor elites misread as barbarism. Peter H. Smith: some (Rosas, Portales, Díaz) built "integrative dictatorships" that strengthened the centre. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caudillo]
- **Rosas: tyrant or national defender?** Mainstream scholarship (Lynch; William Spence Robertson; Dusenberry's "black legend") sees a brutal dictatorship; Argentine nationalist *revisionismo* since the 1930s rehabilitated him as a defender of sovereignty against Britain and France; Michael Goebel notes revisionists' "lack of interest in scholarly standards". Menem's 1989 reburial tied rehabilitation to Dirty War pardons. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Manuel_de_Rosas]
- **Civilisation versus barbarism:** Sarmiento's dichotomy set the terms of debate (González Echevarría: it "gave shape to a polemic that began in the colonial period"); critics note how it cast the countryside, gauchos and Indigenous peoples as barbarian, and later justified campaigns like the Conquest of the Desert. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facundo ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conquest_of_the_Desert]
- **Did Britain cause the Paraguayan War?** Argentine and Paraguayan revisionists (left and right), Eric Hobsbawm in *The Age of Capital*, and Soviet and Russian encyclopedias blamed British interests; Leslie Bethell (1996) and others find "little or no empirical evidence": Britain opposed the war, broke relations with Brazil 1863–65, and already had Egyptian and Indian cotton. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraguayan_War]
- **How many Paraguayans died?** Reber (low, about 7%) vs Whigham and Potthast (60–70%). Steven Pinker: on a 60%+ death rate, proportionally one of the most destructive wars in modern times. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraguayan_War]
- **Dependency and "neocolonialism":** Raúl Prebisch (ECLA) and Hans Singer (1949) argued the terms of trade for raw-material exporters deteriorated (Prebisch–Singer thesis). Andre Gunder Frank's "The Development of Underdevelopment" (*Monthly Review*, September 1966) and *Capitalism and Underdevelopment in Latin America* (1967) held that integration into world capitalism produced underdevelopment. Cardoso and Faletto (1969; English 1979) argued for "dependent development" shaped by internal class alliances as well as external ties. Critics: D. C. M. Platt, "Dependency in Nineteenth-Century Latin America: An Historian Objects" (*Latin American Research Review* 15:1, 1980), argued the evidence did not show that autonomous growth was destroyed, and attacked Stanley and Barbara Stein's *The Colonial Heritage of Latin America*; the Steins replied ("D. C. M. Platt: The Anatomy of 'Autonomy'"). Sanjaya Lall and Tony Smith criticised dependency's vagueness and neglect of domestic politics. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependency_theory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andre_Gunder_Frank ; https://cambridge.org/core/journals/latin-american-research-review/article/dependency-in-nineteenthcentury-latin-america-an-historian-objects/F34CBD762AAC7BB7995A32CDC0DB8A7E ; https://monthlyreview.org/?p=2942 (search summary)]
- **Informal empire:** cross-refer the British Empire chapter (Gallagher and Robinson 1953); Platt's work (above) is the main sceptical counterpoint for Latin America.
- **Conquest of the Desert: civilising defence or genocide?** Apologists vs revisionists (including Osvaldo Bayer); Jens Andermann reads contemporary sources as showing an intent to exterminate. A 2011 *Guardian* piece reported Roca being "recast as genocidal murderer". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conquest_of_the_Desert]
- **Brazil 1889: indifference or consent?** Aristides Lobo's "bestialized" people vs Maria Tereza Chaves de Mello (*A República Consentida*, 2007): the republic was popularly seen as a regime of development, though ordinary people had not demanded it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclamation_of_the_Republic_(Brazil)]
- **Porfiriato:** once treated as a dictatorship swept away in 1910, it is "increasingly ... seen as laying the basis for post-revolutionary Mexico"; benefits were unevenly shared. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porfiriato]
- **1898: whose war?** Louis A. Pérez Jr. (1998) shows how US historiography over a century told the "Spanish–American War" largely without Cuban sources or Cuban agency; Ada Ferrer (1999) puts race and anti-racism at the centre of the independence wars. [source: search results incl. https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/CDDAE9264A63502CF8D7A0C72B7532E9/S0023879100019087a.pdf/history-or-teleology-recent-scholarship-on-cuba-before-1959.pdf ; https://www.perlego.com/book/539973/insurgent-cuba-race-nation-and-revolution-18681898-pdf]
- **Afro-Argentine "disappearance":** the old demographic-catastrophe explanation vs census reclassification and a national project of whiteness (Erika Denise Edwards). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Argentines]
- **Canudos death toll:** da Cunha about 30,000; Levine about 15,000; Singer about 5,000. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_Canudos]
