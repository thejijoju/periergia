## 1. Names and framing
- Names: "Vietnam War" in English; also "Second Indochina War". North Vietnam (and official Vietnamese histories today) called it *Kháng chiến chống Mỹ, cứu nước* — "War of resistance against America to save the nation"; South Vietnam used terms such as "Resistance war against the communists" and "Battle to protect freedom". (Chapter title's "American War" reflects the Vietnamese usage.) [source: VW]
- No consensus start date: the US government officially dated it from 1 November 1955 (MAAG Vietnam established; decided 1998); others choose December 1956, 26 September 1959, 1960, 1954, 1950 or even 1945. End: 30 April 1975. [source: VW]
- VW characterises it simultaneously as a Cold War proxy war, a postcolonial war of national liberation and a civil war, "with civil warfare a defining feature from the outset"; it spilled into the Laotian and Cambodian civil wars, all three countries becoming communist in 1975. [source: VW]

## 2. French Indochina and the First Indochina War (1945–54)
- Vietnam was absorbed into French Indochina in stages, 1858–1887. Nguyen Sinh Cung (later Ho Chi Minh) founded the Indochinese Communist Party in 1930. [source: FIW; VW]
- Bảo Đại abdicated 25 August 1945 (Ho persuaded him to become "supreme advisor"). On 2 September 1945 Ho read the Declaration of Independence of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam to thousands at what is now Ba Đình Square, Hanoi, deliberately echoing the US Declaration of Independence (and also the French Declaration of the Rights of Man). Historian Pierre Asselin reads the references as a strategic bid for Allied (incl. OSS) support. [source: DEC; FIW]
- The US OSS had given the Viet Minh some training and weapons against Japan in 1945. [source: VW]
- Allied occupation: British forces south of the 16th parallel, ~200,000 Chinese Nationalist troops (Lu Han) in the north. On 23 September 1945 French forces, with British support, overthrew the DRV administration in Saigon. [source: VW; FIW]
- Viet Minh also purged and killed rival nationalists and Trotskyists from August 1945 (VW, citing Goscha and others) — part of a Vietnamese civil conflict inside the anticolonial war. [source: VW]
- On 23 November 1946 the French fleet bombarded Haiphong, killing ~6,000 Vietnamese civilians in an afternoon (FIW's figure). Full-scale war from 19 December 1946; FIW dates the war 19 December 1946 – 11 August 1954. [source: FIW]
- Internationalisation: in January 1950 the PRC became the first state to recognise the DRV and persuaded the USSR to follow; the US recognised the French-associated State of Vietnam (Bảo Đại). China's military advisory group (April 1950) turned the Viet Minh into an army. US MAAG set up September 1950. By 1954 the US had spent $1 billion, shouldering 80% of French war costs. [source: VW]
- **Dien Bien Phu**: siege 13 March – 7 May 1954, near the Laotian border; Viet Minh artillery on the surrounding heights; thousands of porters (including Hmong) carried supplies. French losses: 1,142–2,293 killed, 1,606 missing, 11,721 captured. Viet Minh losses: Western estimate ~8,000 dead and 15,000 wounded; Vietnamese figure 13,930 casualties. France and the US discussed using tactical nuclear weapons (Operation Vulture), seriousness unclear. Roughly one-tenth of French Union manpower in Indochina was lost. [source: DBP; VW]
- First Indochina War deaths (FIW infobox): Viet Minh 175,000–300,000 dead or missing (Western) / 191,605 (Vietnamese government); French Union 74,220 dead (20,685 of them Frenchmen); State of Vietnam 58,877 dead or missing; total 400,000–842,707 killed incl. 125,000–400,000 civilians. [source: FIW]

## 3. Geneva and partition (1954–56)
- Geneva Conference 26 April – 21 July 1954; Indochina phase 8 May – 21 July (opened the day after Dien Bien Phu fell). Pierre Mendès France became French PM 18 June pledging a ceasefire within 30 days. Zhou Enlai pressed the Viet Minh to settle, fearing US intervention; VW says the Soviets and Chinese pressured Ho to accept the 17th parallel. [source: GEN; VW]
- Terms: the ceasefire agreement was signed only by the French and Viet Minh military commands; provisional military demarcation line at about the 17th parallel; regroupment of forces; elections "in July 1956" under international supervision — recorded only in the **unsigned** Final Declaration. ICC (India chair, Canada, Poland) to supervise. Neither the US nor the State of Vietnam signed; both had opposed partition. [source: GEN; VW]
- Movement: up to one million northerners moved south in the 300-day period, incl. at least 500,000 Catholics and ~200,000 Buddhists (Goscha); CIA psychological warfare exaggerated anti-Catholic threats but VW says migrants were driven chiefly by their own convictions. 5,000–10,000 Viet Minh cadres stayed in the South (VC article: ~90,000 Viet Minh evacuated north). Viet Thanh Nguyen's parents were among the 1954 northern Catholic refugees. [source: VW; VC; VTN]
- North Vietnamese land reform 1953–56: executions estimated between >13,500 (Hungarian-documents low end) and ~50,000 "accepted by scholars" (some witnesses suggest ~100,000); Hanoi admitted "excesses" in 1956 ("correction of errors"); the Nhân Văn–Giai Phẩm intellectual dissent of 1956 was suppressed. [source: VW]
- Elections never held. Eisenhower later speculated up to 80% would have voted for Ho over Bảo Đại in 1954; in 1957 the ICC said fair elections were impossible and neither side had honoured the armistice. [source: VW]

## 4. Diem's South, 1954–63
- Ngô Đình Diệm (b. 1901, Huế), Catholic nationalist, anti-French and anti-communist; appointed PM by Bảo Đại 16 June 1954. Defeated the Bình Xuyên, Cao Đài and Hòa Hảo armies in 1955. The 23 October 1955 referendum credited him with 98.2% — votes exceeded registered voters by 380,000+; he proclaimed the Republic of Vietnam. Ho's side was reported to win 99% in the North. [source: DIEM; VW]
- Rule was authoritarian and nepotistic: brother Ngô Đình Nhu ran the Cần Lao party and secret police; brother Thục was Archbishop of Huế. "Denounce the Communists" campaign from July 1955; death penalty for communist activity 1956; Law 10/59 (1959); ~40,000 political prisoners by 1959. Hanoi claimed 65,000 imprisoned and 2,148 killed by Nov 1957. [source: VW; DIEM]
- Diem's official ideology was Personalism (from Emmanuel Mounier), a "third way" between liberalism and communism; Edward Miller argues his ideas of democracy and rural development (resettlement rather than land redistribution) were coherent, not merely reactionary. [source: DIEM]
- Insurgency: southern cadres began an assassination campaign ("extermination of traitors") in 1957; Douglas Pike estimated 1,700 assassinations and 2,000 abductions of officials, teachers, health workers etc. 1957–60. Lê Duẩn's "Road to the South" (1956); Hanoi approved "people's war" in January 1959; Group 559 set up May 1959 to build the Ho Chi Minh Trail; first arms delivered August 1959. [source: VW]
- **NLF**: the National Liberation Front of South Vietnam was established 20 December 1960 (at Tân Lập, Tây Ninh province), under Hanoi's direction but including non-communists; "Việt Cộng" = contraction of *Việt Nam cộng sản*, "Vietnamese communist", a term its enemies used. In 1969 it created the Provisional Revolutionary Government (PRG). Women were prominent from the start: Nguyễn Thị Định, a Bến Tre peasant-born veteran, co-founded the NLF and was its deputy commander; she led the 1960 Bến Tre uprising (Đồng Khởi) and the all-female "Long-Haired Army". [source: VC; NTD; WOM]
- Strategic Hamlet Program (1962–64), advised by Britain's Sir Robert Thompson (Malaya veteran): forced relocation of peasants into fortified villages; overseen by Col. Phạm Ngọc Thảo, secretly a communist agent who sabotaged it; it waned by Nov 1963 and ended 1964. [source: SHP; VW]
- Kennedy: from ~900 advisers under Eisenhower to 16,000 by November 1963. Battle of Ấp Bắc (January 1963): VC defeated a larger ARVN force. [source: VW]
- **Buddhist crisis 1963**: 8 May shootings in Huế of Buddhists protesting a ban on Buddhist flags at Vesak (VW: 9 killed). Thích Quảng Đức burned himself to death at a Saigon intersection on 11 June 1963; Malcolm Browne's photographs went round the world; Kennedy said "no news picture in history has generated so much emotion around the world as that one". Madame Nhu said she would "clap hands at seeing another monk barbecue show". August 1963 pagoda raids by Nhu's Special Forces. BC notes the often-quoted "70%+ Buddhist majority" was likely an overestimate (folk religion counted as Buddhism). [source: TQD; BC; VW]
- Coup: Washington signalled (Cable 243) it would not oppose a coup; Diem and Nhu were overthrown and killed on 2 November 1963. Kennedy, shown the news, left the room "with a look of shock and dismay" (Maxwell Taylor); Kennedy himself was assassinated 22 November 1963. A string of coups followed. [source: VW]

## 5. Hanoi's road to war (Vietnamese decision-making)
- Lê Duẩn (1907–1986): General Secretary from the 3rd Party Congress (1960) until his death; with Lê Đức Thọ he marginalised moderates; Lien-Hang Nguyen calls him the "preeminent voice in the Politburo". In December 1963 Lê Duẩn pushed the "General Offensive and General Uprising" strategy; moderates including Ho were coerced to submit. In July 1967 hundreds of pro-Soviet moderates, officers and intellectuals were arrested (the "Revisionist Anti-Party Affair"). [source: LD; VW; TET]
- North Vietnam introduced conscription for men in April 1960; ~40,000 communist soldiers infiltrated south 1961–63; VC strength rose from ~5,000 (early 1959) to ~100,000 (end 1964). [source: VW]
- Aid: China said its total aid was $160 billion (2022 prices), sent some 320,000 troops (engineering and anti-aircraft) over the war, 170,000 at peak (1968); ~1,100 Chinese dead. USSR shipped ~$450 million of arms a year, had up to 3,000 personnel in Vietnam, 16 dead. [source: VW]

## 6. Tonkin and Americanization, 1964–68
- **Gulf of Tonkin**: after South Vietnamese commando raids (OPLAN 34A) on Hòn Mê (30–31 July 1964), USS *Maddox* exchanged fire with North Vietnamese torpedo boats on 2 August 1964; a second "attack" reported 4 August never happened — a 2005-declassified NSA study found it rested on bad intelligence and misrepresented intercepts; Giáp told McNamara in 1995 "Absolutely nothing" happened. McNamara concealed the raids from Congress. Johnson privately: "Hell, those dumb stupid sailors were just shooting at flying fish". [source: GTI; GTR; VW]
- **Resolution**: passed 7 August 1964 (House 416–0, Senate 88–2; dissenters Wayne Morse and Ernest Gruening); signed 10 August. Johnson called it "like grandma's nightshirt — it covered everything". Repealed via the Foreign Military Sales Act signed January 1971; War Powers Resolution passed over Nixon's veto in 1973. [source: GTR]
- Johnson on the campaign trail: he was not "committing American boys to fighting a war that I think ought to be fought by the boys of Asia". [source: VW]
- **Rolling Thunder**: 2 March 1965 – 31 October 1968 (VW infobox note: ends with Johnson's bombing halt). DoD announced 864,000 tons dropped on the North by end-1967 (vs 653,000 tons in the entire Korean War). North Vietnam: ~20,000 soldiers and 30,000–65,000 civilians killed (RT infobox). In 1965 97,000 North Vietnamese civilians worked full time repairing bomb damage, plus 370,000–500,000 part time. [source: RT]
- Ground war: 3,500 US Marines landed near Da Nang on 8 March 1965; ~200,000 troops by December 1965; 184,000 by 1966 (VW lead); peak 543,400 (April 1969). Westmoreland's attrition strategy measured by "body count", promising victory by Dec 1967. First big US–PAVN battle: Ia Drang, November 1965. [source: VW]
- **Search and destroy**: Westmoreland's offensive tool; in practice blurred with "clearing"; generated mass displacement — ~2 million internal refugees in South Vietnam by 1967; Operation Masher alone left 125,000 homeless. VC/PAVN initiated ~90% of large firefights, keeping the initiative. Free-fire zones. [source: VW; SD]
- Saigon politics: Nguyễn Cao Kỳ and Nguyễn Văn Thiệu from mid-1965; Thiệu president from 1967 (rigged election) to 1975. [source: VW]
- **The draft**: 1965–73 over 1.7 million men drafted; ~648,500 draftees served in Vietnam (~a quarter of US forces there); 17,725 draftees died (just over 30% of US combat deaths). College (2-S) deferments let middle-class men avoid service; local boards often lacked Black or Hispanic members. Draft lottery by birth date held 1 December 1969 (first date drawn: 14 September). Last man drafted 30 June 1973. Project 100,000 (from Oct 1966) lowered standards: 246,000 recruited, 41% Black. [source: DRAFT; CAS]
- Black servicemen: in 1965 African Americans were 11% of the population but 14.1% of combat deaths; after reforms from 1967, Black casualties fell to ~13% of combat deaths by war's end. [source: CAS; VW]
- **Credibility gap**: term first applied to Johnson's Vietnam policy in the *New York Herald Tribune* in March 1965; Johnson's "policy of minimum candor"; Westmoreland told the National Press Club in November 1967 that "the end comes into view". [source: CG; VW]

## 7. Allies of Saigon
- "Many Flags": Australia, New Zealand, Thailand, the Philippines sent troops; South Korea joined in return for economic compensation; Britain and Canada declined. Pro-Saigon troop figures (VW infobox): South Korea ~48,000/year, 320,000 total, 5,099 dead; Thailand peak 11,570, 351 dead; NZ peak 552, 37 dead; Philippines 2,061, 9 dead. [source: VW]
- **South Korea**: Park Chung Hee saw the war as a way to secure US aid and dollars (a $150 million development loan among other benefits) and the alliance; Korean marines accused of massacres incl. Phong Nhị/Phong Nhất (Feb 1968, ~70 civilians), Bình Hòa, Hà My. Lai Đại Hàn: an estimated 5,000–30,000 children of Korean fathers and Vietnamese mothers, many conceived by rape. Presidents Kim Dae-jung and Moon Jae-in made apologetic statements short of a full apology. [source: SK; WOM; VW]
- **Australia**: 30 advisers in 1962; Menzies committed a battalion 29 April 1965 ("part of a thrust by Communist China between the Indian and Pacific Oceans"); peak 7,672; ~60,000 served 1962–72; deaths 521 (AUS; AWM) vs 526 (VW, citing DVA 2025) — say "about 520". Conscription by birthday ballot: 15,381 national servicemen served, 202 killed. Battle of Long Tan, 18–19 August 1966 (18 Australians killed, at least 245 VC). Holt's slogan "All the way with LBJ" (30 June 1966). Whitlam withdrew the last advisers by December 1972; combat ended 11 January 1973. [source: AUS; VW]

## 8. Tet and 1968
- **Tet Offensive**: launched prematurely 30 January 1968 in the I/II Corps areas; countrywide 31 January — ~77,000–85,000 PAVN/VC struck more than 100 towns and cities, incl. 36 of 44 provincial capitals and Saigon; a 19-man sapper team entered the US Embassy compound. Hanoi's aim was a general uprising and ARVN defections — neither happened. Communist losses across the 1968 offensives ~45,000 killed. TET: civilian toll 14,300 killed, 24,000 wounded, 630,000 refugees. [source: TET; VW]
- **Huế**: held by PAVN/VC for 26 days; US firepower left ~80% of the city in ruins; communist forces executed civilians, officials and others — estimates 2,800–6,000 (≈2,800 bodies recovered from mass graves; Saigon listed 4,062 murdered or abducted); historian Gareth Porter argued only "several hundred" executions; David Hunt called Pike's US-mission study propaganda. [source: HUE; VW]
- 1 February 1968: Gen. Nguyễn Ngọc Loan executed captured VC officer Nguyễn Văn Lém in front of photographer Eddie Adams ("Saigon Execution", Pulitzer 1969). [source: TET]
- Walter Cronkite (CBS), 27 February 1968: "we are mired in a stalemate that could only be ended by negotiation, not victory." TET notes polls show many Americans still called themselves hawks in early 1968 — the "media lost the war" thesis is contested. [source: TET]
- Johnson's approval fell from 48% to 36%; on 31 March 1968 he announced a partial bombing halt and that he would not run again; Paris talks began May 1968. VW notes US bombing of Indochina as a whole actually increased the next month. Westmoreland replaced by Abrams. US troops peaked at 536,000 (VW lead, 1969) / 543,400 (April 1969, infobox). [source: VW; TET]
- Hanoi then adopted "talking while fighting, fighting while talking". [source: VW]

## 9. My Lai
- 16 March 1968, Sơn Mỹ village, Quảng Ngãi province: C Company, 1/20th Infantry (Capt. Ernest Medina; 1st Platoon under Lt William Calley) killed between 347 (US Army count, excluding Mỹ Khê) and 504 civilians, almost all women, children and old men; rapes and mutilation. Helicopter crew Hugh Thompson Jr., Lawrence Colburn and Glenn Andreotta intervened to stop killings. Covered up as a victory; exposed November 1969 by veteran Ronald Ridenhour and journalist Seymour Hersh. 26 soldiers charged; only Calley convicted (22 murders), sentenced to life, served three and a half years under house arrest. [source: ML]
- Not unique: the Pentagon's Vietnam War Crimes Working Group substantiated 320 incidents (incl. seven massacres 1967–71 killing at least 137); Operation Speedy Express (1968–69) — John Paul Vann called it "many Mỹ Lais", *Newsweek* suggested ≥5,000 civilians killed; Nick Turse ties civilian deaths to body-count pressure and free-fire zones; Phoenix Program killed 26,000–41,000. [source: VW]
- Communist atrocities also systematic: Pentagon estimate of 36,000 murders and 58,000 kidnappings by VC/PAVN 1967–72; Đắk Sơn massacre (252 Montagnards); American POWs tortured in Hỏa Lò ("Hanoi Hilton"). South Vietnam's Côn Sơn "tiger cages" exposed 1970. [source: VW]

## 10. The antiwar movement
- First public draft-card burning at Berkeley, 5 May 1965. Quaker Norman Morrison burned himself to death outside the Pentagon 2 November 1965, consciously imitating Vietnamese Buddhist protest. [source: OPP]
- Martin Luther King Jr., "Beyond Vietnam: A Time to Break Silence", Riverside Church, 4 April 1967; SNCC was the first major civil rights group to oppose the war (1965); Muhammad Ali resisted the draft (1966). Opposition included civil rights, feminist and Chicano movements and labour. [source: OPP]
- March on the Pentagon, October 1967 (~35,000; 682 arrests). National Moratorium, 15 October 1969, hundreds of thousands (VW: "millions"). [source: OPP; VW]
- Kent State, 4 May 1970: Ohio National Guard killed four and wounded nine unarmed students protesting the Cambodian incursion; more than 4 million students joined the strike. On 14 May police killed two students at Jackson State (historically Black), which drew less attention. [source: KS]
- Public opinion: Gallup in Sept 1965 found only 24% thought sending troops a mistake; plurality "mistake" from Oct 1967, majority from Aug 1968; 60% by 1970/1973. The movement itself stayed unpopular even as support fell; historians divide over its influence. [source: VW]
- Within the army: desertions quadrupled from 1966; ~900 fragging incidents investigated (mostly 1969–71); ~50,000 deserted and ~125,000 Americans went to Canada to avoid the draft; Carter pardoned draft evaders in 1977. Kuzmarov argues the "addicted army" image is a Nixon-era myth. [source: VW]

## 11. Nixon: Vietnamization and the wider war, 1969–73
- "Vietnamization" (term from an NSC meeting of 28 January 1969, associated with Defense Secretary Laird): US withdrawals from 1969, ARVN expanded. Nixon's October 1969 nuclear-alert bluff ("madman theory"); appeal to the "silent majority". Ho Chi Minh died 2 September 1969. [source: VZN; VW]
- **Cambodia**: Operation Menu, secret B-52 bombing of PAVN/VC sanctuaries, 18 March 1969 – 26 May 1970: 3,800 sorties, 108,823 tons, hidden by a dual-reporting system; only five members of Congress informed; exposed by William Beecher, *NYT*, 9 May 1969. Lon Nol deposed Sihanouk March 1970; US–ARVN Cambodian campaign 29 April – 22 July 1970, announced by Nixon on TV 30 April. Bombing continued (Freedom Deal) to 1973. VW: 50,000–150,000 Cambodian combatants and civilians killed by US bombing; total Cambodian war dead 275,000–310,000. Ben Kiernan and William Shawcross link bombing to the Khmer Rouge's rise (a debated claim). [source: MENU; CC; VW]
- **Laos**: North Vietnam invaded/occupied eastern Laos 1958–59 for the Trail; 1962 Geneva neutrality agreement ignored by both sides. The CIA's "Secret War": ~30,000 Hmong, Mien and Khmu irregulars (SGUs) led by Gen. Vang Pao. 1964–73 the US dropped ~2 million tons of bombs on Laos (≈ the 2.1 million tons it dropped in all of WWII) in over half a million missions — the most heavily bombed country per capita in history. Lao dead 20,000–62,000. ARVN's Lam Son 719 into Laos (Feb 1971) ended in rout. [source: LCW; VW; UXO]
- **Ho Chi Minh Trail** (*Đường Trường Sơn*): network through Laos and Cambodia, from footpaths and bicycles to truck roads and a 5,000 km fuel pipeline; by 1973 trucks could drive its length. Women were the majority of the Youth Shock Brigades (TNXP) that repaired it; at Đồng Lộc junction 10 young women volunteers were killed by a US bomb on 24 July 1968 (now a shrine). [source: HCMT; WOM]
- **Pentagon Papers**: DoD study 1945–68, 47 volumes (3,000 pp analysis + 4,000 pp documents); leaked by Daniel Ellsberg (with Anthony Russo) to Neil Sheehan; *NYT* began publishing 13 June 1971; Supreme Court ruled 6–3 against prior restraint, 30 June 1971. Ellsberg's charges were dismissed after Watergate-related misconduct emerged. One memo listed US aims as "70% – To avoid a humiliating U.S. defeat". [source: PP]
- Easter Offensive 1972 (~300,000 PAVN with tanks); Linebacker; Kissinger–Lê Đức Thọ secret talks; October 1972 draft deal rejected by Thiệu. **Linebacker II** ("Christmas bombing"; in Vietnam "Điện Biên Phủ in the air"), 18–29 December 1972: 15 B-52s lost (US claim); 1,624 civilians killed (Vietnamese claim); Bạch Mai hospital hit (28 staff killed); Khâm Thiên Street, Hanoi, 26 Dec: 278 killed. [source: LB2; VW]

## 12. Paris Accords and the fall of Saigon
- **Paris Peace Accords** signed 27 January 1973 (Hotel Majestic), by Kissinger, Lê Đức Thọ, PRG Foreign Minister Nguyễn Thị Bình and a reluctant Thiệu's government: ceasefire, US withdrawal in 60 days, POW exchange (591 American POWs returned in Operation Homecoming), PAVN troops (~200,000) allowed to stay in the South. Kissinger and Thọ awarded the 1973 Nobel Peace Prize; Thọ refused it. [source: PPA; VW; USVN]
- Fighting continued ("War of the Flags"); Thiệu declared the war restarted January 1974; Congress passed Case–Church (1973) barring intervention and cut aid from $1 billion to $700 million. Trần Văn Trà's plan; Phước Long fell January 1975. Campaign 275 (Ban Mê Thuột, 10 March 1975) collapsed the Central Highlands ("convoy of tears"); Huế and Da Nang fell in March. Thiệu resigned 21 April, accusing the US of betrayal. [source: VW]
- **30 April 1975**: Operation Frequent Wind (29–30 April) evacuated more than 7,000 by helicopter, the largest helicopter evacuation in history; tanks broke through the Independence Palace gates; President Dương Văn Minh surrendered. ~130,000 Vietnamese associated with the US/RVN evacuated. Reunification as Socialist Republic of Vietnam 2 July 1976; Saigon renamed Ho Chi Minh City. [source: FOS; FW; BOAT; VW]

## 13. Costs (give ranges; explain why they differ)
- US: 58,220 (DoD/SIAD) — VW infobox gives 58,281 (VVMF 2021); 303,644 wounded (incl. 150,341 not hospitalised); 1,584 still missing (2021). Average age of the US dead: 23. ~2.7 million served in Vietnam; ~830,000 veterans suffered PTSD. Cost $168 billion (1953–75). [source: VW]
- **Vietnamese** — the estimates diverge widely:
  - Vietnamese government (1995): 1.1 million PAVN/VC dead and 2 million civilians on both sides (1955–75); with ~300,000 South Vietnamese and allied soldiers → ~3.4 million. 2017 survey: 849,018 PAVN/VC military dead 1960–75 plus 232,000 missing. [source: CAS]
  - Hirschman, Preston & Vu (1995, *Population and Development Review*): 791,000–1,141,000 Vietnamese war deaths 1965–75, most likely 882,000. [source: CAS]
  - BMJ (Obermeyer et al., 2008): 3.8 million violent deaths 1955–2002; 3.1 million 1955–75. [source: CAS]
  - VW lead summary: 970,000–3 million Vietnamese dead; ARVN ~254,000 killed 1960–74 (other estimates 313,000); South Vietnamese civilians 195,000–430,000; northern civilians killed by US bombing 30,000–182,000. [source: VW]
  - Why: US "body counts" were inflated and blurred civilians and combatants; Hanoi long withheld figures. The seed's "up to three million" is within range.
- Bombing tonnage: over 7 million tons on Indochina, more than triple WWII's 2.1 million — ~4 million on South Vietnam, 1 million North Vietnam, 500,000 Cambodia, 2 million Laos. [source: VW]
- **Agent Orange**: Operation Ranch Hand (1962–71) sprayed nearly 20 million US gallons of herbicides; Agent Orange contaminated with TCDD dioxin; >20% of South Vietnam's forests sprayed at least once; suspended 15 April 1970. Vietnam's government says 4 million exposed and up to 3 million ill; Vietnam Red Cross: up to 1 million disabled or ill; the US disputes these figures. US veterans' class action settled 7 May 1984 for $180 million; a Vietnamese victims' suit (VAVA, filed 2004) was dismissed. US–Vietnam dioxin clean-up at Da Nang began 9 August 2012. [source: AO; CAS; VW]
- **Unexploded ordnance**: UXO has killed some 42,000 Vietnamese since the war (Vietnamese government). Laos: about 30% of bomblets failed to detonate; 288 million cluster munitions and ~75 million unexploded bombs left (UXO; VW says "80 million unexploded bombs remain"); over 20,000 Laotians killed or injured since; ~50 casualties a year; clearance could take ~100 years (UXO) or centuries (VW). Laos hosted the first meeting of the Convention on Cluster Munitions (2010). [source: CAS; UXO; VW]

## 14. Aftermath in Indochina
- **Re-education camps**: South Vietnamese were told to report for "three days" (soldiers), "ten days" (junior officers/officials) or "one month" (senior); many stayed years, some over a decade. At least 200,000–300,000 held (other estimates 500,000–1 million passed through); hard labour incl. mine-clearing, starvation, disease; Hanoi called them war criminals under a 1967 law. No mass bloodbath on the scale Nixon predicted; a 65,000-executions estimate (Desbarats & Jackson) has been criticised as methodologically invalid. New Economic Zones relocated ~750,000–1 million+ southerners. [source: REC; VW]
- **Boat people**: exodus from September 1978; ethnic Chinese (Hoa) targeted amid China–Vietnam tensions — ~250,000 Hoa fled to China by end-1979; exit "fees" in gold. Peak arrivals 54,000 in June 1979; July 1979 Geneva conference → Orderly Departure Program. Piracy in the Gulf of Thailand (1981: 349 of 452 boats reaching Thailand attacked). 1989 Comprehensive Plan of Action introduced screening and repatriation. Almost 800,000 boat people reached safety 1975–95; over 1.2 million Vietnamese resettled abroad 1975–97. Deaths at sea: UNHCR estimate 200,000–250,000 (BOAT; VW) — CAS gives "200,000–400,000"; Barry Wain (1981) only 30,000. More than 3 million fled Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia overall; ~300,000 from Laos (incl. ~90% of its intellectuals, technicians and officials, VW). [source: BOAT; IRC; VW; CAS]
- Laos: Pathet Lao took power 2 December 1975 (Lao PDR); ~30,000 former officials sent to re-education camps; Hmong persecuted as traitors. [source: LCW; VW]
- Cambodia: Khmer Rouge 17 April 1975 – 7 January 1979; 1.5–2 million dead (20–25% of the population) per CAMG lead (Kiernan: 1.671–1.871 million). Vietnam invaded December 1978 and ousted them; China attacked Vietnam 17 February – 16 March 1979. [source: CAMG; SVW; VW]
- Đổi Mới market reforms, Sixth Party Congress, December 1986, after Lê Duẩn's death (July 1986) and inflation over 700%. [source: DM]
- US trade embargo from 1975; Clinton lifted it in 1994 (urged by veterans John McCain and John Kerry); diplomatic normalisation 1995. US vetoed Vietnam's UN membership three times. [source: USVN; VW]
- Vietnamese memory: Bảo Ninh's novel *The Sorrow of War* (written 1987, published 1990/91, Writers' Association award 1991, then restricted 1993–2005); the diary of doctor Đặng Thùy Trâm (1942–1970), killed in battle, kept by US intelligence officer Fred Whitehurst and published 2005 (*Last Night I Dreamed of Peace*). Women veterans faced discrimination and erasure from official histories. [source: SOW; DTT; WOM]

## 15. Aftermath in the US
- "Vietnam syndrome": Norman Podhoretz's 1976 *Commentary* argument; Reagan's 1980 VFW speech called the war "a noble cause". 1978 poll: ~72% thought the war "fundamentally wrong and immoral". (VW says Reagan "coined" the term; VS shows it was in circulation earlier — say Reagan popularised it.) [source: VS; VW]
- **Vietnam Veterans Memorial**: fund founded by veteran Jan Scruggs (1979); 1,421 entries; winner no. 1026 by Maya Lin, then a Yale undergraduate (b. 1959, to Chinese immigrant parents); dedicated 13 November 1982; two black granite walls, 246 ft 9 in each, pointing to the Washington Monument and Lincoln Memorial; 57,939 names in 1982, 58,320 by 2018, in chronological order of casualty; called "a black gash of shame and sorrow" by critics; Frederick Hart's *Three Servicemen* statue added as compromise; Vietnam Women's Memorial nearby (8 women — all nurses — on the Wall). ~3 million visitors a year. [source: VVM; LIN; WOM]
- ~11,000 American women served in the military in the war, mostly nurses. [source: WOM]

## Debates
- **Orthodox vs revisionist (US historiography):** the "orthodox" view treats the war as unwise and probably unwinnable from the start; "revisionists" (Mark Moyar, *Triumph Forsaken*, 2006; Keith Taylor's 2004 essay; earlier soldiers' memoirs) argue South Vietnam was viable and the war was lost through US errors — notably backing Diem's overthrow in 1963. Critics (e.g. John Carland, *Journal of Military History*) say revisionism cannot explain key events; Moyar and others dispute the neat dichotomy. Wiest & Doidge (eds.), *Triumph Revisited* (Routledge, 2010) collects the exchange. NB the word "revisionist" here is the opposite of "revisionist" in Cold War historiography (overview chapter). [source: WebSearch: https://muse.jhu.edu/journals/journal_of_military_history/v071/71.2carland.html; https://issforum.org/roundtables/PDF/TriumphForsaken-Moyar.pdf; https://historynet.com/vietnam-book-review-triumph-forsaken/]
- **Vietnamese-centred / international turn:** Lien-Hang Nguyen (*Hanoi's War*, 2012) shows Lê Duẩn and Lê Đức Thọ controlling Hanoi's policy from ~1960, sidelining Ho and Giáp, repressing moderates and gambling on the 1964 and 1968 offensives — a portrait that offended some in Vietnam and in the Western antiwar tradition. Goscha and Pierre Asselin (cited throughout VW) stress the Vietnamese civil war and the communists' violence against rival nationalists; Tuong Vu and Nu-Anh Tran stress South Vietnamese republicanism. [source: WebSearch: https://www.lrb.co.uk/v35/n03/andrew-bacevich/small-nations-take-heed; https://historynet.com/interview-lien-hang-t-nguyen-and-hanois-secrets/; VW]
- **Diem: puppet or nationalist?** Edward Miller (*Misalliance*, 2013) restores Diem and Nhu as rational nation-builders with their own Personalist programme clashing with US ideas; Keith Taylor (New Mandala review) says Miller over-relies on Diem's "faith" in his policies to explain 1963 and underplays the communist challenge. VW: "whatever the failings of Diệm, his credentials as a nationalist had been impeccable". [source: WebSearch: https://www.newmandala.org/2013/05/17/review-of-misalliance-tlc-nmrev-li/; DIEM; VW]
- **Lost chances / escalation:** Logevall (*Choosing War*, 1999; *Embers of War*, 2012) argues US escalation was a choice and that the US walked into France's war knowingly. [source: LOG]
- **Tet and the media:** Tet was a military defeat for Hanoi's uprising strategy but a political blow to Washington; the claim that the press "lost the war" is contested (polls show many Americans still hawkish in Feb 1968; opposition driven by casualties and stalemate). [source: TET; VW]
- **Bombing and the Khmer Rouge:** Kiernan and Shawcross link US bombing of Cambodia to Khmer Rouge recruitment; others dispute the causal weight. [source: MENU]
- **Numbers as politics:** Huế massacre (2,800–6,000 vs Porter's "several hundred"); post-1975 executions (Desbarats/Jackson 65,000 vs Porter & Roberts' critique); Agent Orange victims (Vietnam's millions vs US denial of proven links); boat-people deaths (30,000 to 400,000). Present ranges and who produces them. [source: HUE; VW; AO; BOAT; CAS]
- **Memory:** Viet Thanh Nguyen argues the war is remembered through competing national "memory industries" (US, Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia, South Korea) that each forget others' dead. [source: VTN; WebSearch above]
