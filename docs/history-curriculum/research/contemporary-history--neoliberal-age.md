## 1. The word and the idea
- "Neoliberalism" has multiple, competing definitions and is "often used pejoratively"; it is generally applied to policies that became dominant from the late 20th century promoting market processes and competition. [source: NEO]
- An early English use: French economist Charles Gide in 1898; Milton Friedman wrote an essay "Neo-Liberalism and its Prospects" in 1951. [source: NEO]
- **Colloque Walter Lippmann, Paris, August 1938:** about 25 liberal intellectuals (Lippmann, Hayek, Mises, Röpke, Rüstow, Rougier) met to renew liberalism after the Depression; "neoliberalism" was one name floated. They split: Rüstow and Lippmann wanted strong state supervision of the economy; Mises and Hayek a narrower state. Rüstow called Hayek and Mises relics of the liberalism that caused the Depression; Mises called the ordoliberals "ordo-interventionism". The war overwhelmed the effort, but it was the precursor of Mont Pèlerin. [source: NEO]
- Friedman (1951): neo-liberalism would substitute for laissez-faire "the goal of the competitive order", with a state to "police the system, establish conditions favorable to competition and prevent monopoly, provide a stable monetary framework, and relieve acute misery and distress". By the 1970s the movement (including Friedman) focused far more narrowly on market liberalisation. [source: NEO]
- In Germany "neoliberalism" originally meant the ordoliberal Freiburg School and the "social market economy" (Erhard); the term faded there in the 1950s–60s. Latin American intellectuals in the 1960s used *neoliberalismo* for this German model. Under Pinochet, opposition scholars took up the word for the Chicago Boys' reforms, and from Spanish-language scholarship it diffused into English. [source: NEO]
- A study of 148 scholarly articles found the term "almost never defined" and used for ideology, theory, development theory or reform policy; it has largely become a term of abuse. Historian Elizabeth Shermer: left-leaning academics took it up in the 1970s to "describe and decry" the free-market turn; Phillip Magness dates its academic re-emergence to the mid-1980s after Foucault. Foucault's lectures, *The Birth of Biopolitics* (Collège de France, 1978–79). [source: NEO]
- Sceptics of the term: Daniel Stedman Jones says it "is too often used as a catch-all shorthand for the horrors associated with globalization and recurring financial crises"; Rajesh Venugopal (2015) calls it "a controversial, incoherent and crisis-ridden term"; journalist Will Hutton called "neoliberal" "an unthinking leftist insult". Defenders of the term: Stephen Metcalf (citing the IMF's own 2016 paper "Neoliberalism: Oversold?"); Gary Gerstle calls it a legitimate term, "a creed that calls explicitly for unleashing capitalism's power", distinct from conservatism, which respects traditions. [source: NEO]
- **Harvey's definition** (opening of *A Brief History of Neoliberalism*, OUP 2005): neoliberalism is "in the first instance a theory of political economic practices that proposes that human well-being can best be advanced by liberating individual entrepreneurial freedoms and skills within an institutional framework characterized by strong private property rights, free markets, and free trade". [source: WebSearch: https://my.vanderbilt.edu/anth232/files/2014/04/ABriefHistoryNeoliberalism-1.pdf; https://newlearningonline.com/literacies/chapter-2/harvey-on-neoliberalism] (search excerpts agree; check exact wording against the book if quoting at length)
- Core policy set (NEO, citing Stedman Jones): trade liberalisation, deregulation, privatisation of state-owned enterprises, reduced government spending, monetarism. [source: NEO]

## 2. The intellectual insurgency
- **Hayek, *The Road to Serfdom*:** published by Routledge in Britain, March 1944 (Hayek called it "that unobtainable book" because of paper rationing); rejected by three US publishers, then published by the University of Chicago Press on 18 September 1944 (expected sales 900–3,000). A 20-page condensation in *Reader's Digest*, April 1945, ran to several million copies; a *Look* picture-book version was distributed by General Motors. Dedicated "To the socialists of all parties". Over two million copies sold by 2010. Argument: socialism requires central planning, which leads towards totalitarianism. [source: RTS; HAY]
- **Mont Pèlerin Society:** founded 10 April 1947 at a conference organised by Hayek at the Hôtel du Parc, Mont Pèlerin near Vevey, Switzerland; 39 invited scholars (mostly economists); founding members included Hayek, Friedman, Karl Popper, George Stigler, Mises. Its Statement of Aims (8 April 1947) began "Over large stretches of the Earth's surface the essential conditions of human dignity and freedom have already disappeared." The 1947 meeting was almost half American; Europeans dominated by 1951. Friedman called it a "rallying point". It met while the International Trade Organization charter was being drafted at the other end of Lake Geneva. [source: MPS; NEO]
- Eight MPS members won the economics Nobel: Hayek, Friedman, Stigler, Allais, Buchanan, Coase, Becker, Vernon Smith. MPS members in office included Ludwig Erhard, Luigi Einaudi, Arthur Burns, George Shultz, Václav Klaus. [source: MPS]
- Think tanks: Antony Fisher (MPS) founded the Institute of Economic Affairs, London, 1955, the Manhattan Institute (1977) and the Atlas network; Hayek encouraged the think-tank route. Adam Smith Institute founded 1977 (it relabelled itself "neoliberal" in October 2016). [source: MPS; NEO]
- For decades MPS ideas stayed on the fringes; only the crises of the 1970s brought them into policy. [source: NEO]
- Harvey and Thomas Volscho trace the political rise in the US to Lewis Powell's confidential 1971 memorandum to the Chamber of Commerce, a call to business to build intellectual infrastructure (Heritage, Cato, Business Roundtable, Manhattan Institute etc.). [source: NEO]
- **Friedman:** University of Chicago from 1946 for 30 years; *A Monetary History of the United States* (with Anna Schwartz, 1963); *Capitalism and Freedom* (1962, over 500,000 copies); critique of the Phillips curve and the "natural rate of unemployment" (1968, with Edmund Phelps independently): inflation cannot permanently lower unemployment. "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon" (1963). 1970 *New York Times Magazine* op-ed against corporate "social responsibility". Sole winner of the 1976 economics Nobel, amid protests over his Chile visit. TV series *Free to Choose* (PBS, 1980), whose companion book was the best-selling nonfiction book of 1980. Adviser to Reagan's campaign and Economic Policy Advisory Board; Presidential Medal of Freedom 1988. Also advocated school vouchers (1955), a negative income tax, floating exchange rates, ending conscription. [source: MF]
- **Hayek:** shared the 1974 economics Nobel with Gunnar Myrdal (announced 9 October 1974); the prize brought his ideas great public attention. In 1975 the IEA arranged a meeting with the new Conservative leader Thatcher. [source: HAY]
- **Hayek and Pinochet:** Hayek visited Chile in the 1970s–80s, became honorary chairman of the Centro de Estudios Públicos, and told a Chilean interviewer "Personally I prefer a liberal dictatorship to democratic government devoid of liberalism"; in a letter to *The Times* he wrote he had "not been able to find a single person even in much maligned Chile who did not agree that personal freedom was much greater under Pinochet than it had been under Allende". [source: HAY] (translated via German–Spanish–English; HAY says so.)
- **Friedman and Chile:** seven-day visit, March 1975, lecturing (one lecture "The Fragility of Freedom"); letter to Pinochet, 21 April 1975: "There is only one way to end inflation: by drastically reducing the rate of increase of the quantity of money"; with inflation "raging at 10–20% a month ... gradualism is not feasible". He said he was never an adviser to the regime and argued free markets would undermine its political control. In 1982 he coined "the Miracle of Chile". [source: MF; CHM]
- Chicago Boys: Chilean students invited to the University of Chicago from 1955 (Friedman, Arnold Harberger). [source: NEO; CB] (Chile otherwise cross-refer.)
- Chile poverty: 45% below the poverty line in 1987; Amartya Sen: little net growth 1975–82; after the 1982 banking crash the state controlled more of the economy than under Allende. [source: CHM]

## 3. The 1970s crisis of the postwar order
- **Bretton Woods** (1944 agreement; 44 countries): dollar fixed to gold at $35 an ounce; other currencies pegged to the dollar. After the war the US held about $26 billion of an estimated $40 billion of gold reserves. London Gold Pool from 1961, collapsed March 1968. [source: BW]
- **Nixon shock, 15 August 1971:** after a secret Camp David meeting (13 August) with Burns, Connally, Volcker and others, Nixon suspended dollar–gold convertibility, imposed a 90-day wage and price freeze and a 10% import surcharge. US gold reserves had fallen to about 10,000 tonnes, less than half their peak. Smithsonian Agreement (December 1971) repegged the dollar at $38/oz; after a further 10% devaluation on 12–14 February 1973, Japan and the EEC floated. End formally ratified by the Jamaica Accords, 1976; by the early 1980s all industrialised states floated. [source: NIX; BW]
- **Stagflation:** word generally attributed to Conservative MP Iain Macleod, House of Commons, 1965: "We now have the worst of both worlds—not just inflation on the one side or stagnation on the other, but both of them together. We have a sort of 'stagflation' situation." Keynesian economists had assumed (Phillips curve) that high unemployment and high inflation did not coincide; Friedman and Phelps explained the breakdown through expectations. Some evidence that US inflation began rising before the oil shock (CPI 4.7%→5.6% and unemployment 3.6%→4.9%, 1968–70). [source: STAG]
- **First oil shock:** Egypt and Syria attacked Israel on 6 October 1973 (Yom Kippur/October War); on 16 October OPEC's Gulf producers raised the posted price from $3.01 to $5.12; OAPEC (Arab producers) imposed an embargo on countries that supported Israel (US in particular, after Nixon requested $2.2 billion for Israel). Embargo lifted March 1974; price had risen nearly 300%, from about $3 to nearly $12 a barrel. [source: OIL73]
- **Second oil shock (1979–80):** Iranian Revolution; global supply fell only about 4% but crude more than doubled to $39.50 a barrel within 12 months (highest real price until March 2008); Iran–Iraq War 1980 cut supply further. [source: OIL79]
- Petrodollar recycling: oil exporters deposited earnings in international banks, which lent them as syndicated loans to Latin American governments. [source: LADC]
- UK inflation: RPI peaked at about 24% in 1975; 1976 sterling crisis; Winter of Discontent 1978–79. [source: UKCPI; THA]
- UK top income-tax rate on earned income had been raised to 83% in 1974 (98% on investment income with surcharge). [source: UKTAX]

## 4. The Volcker shock
- Paul Volcker (1927–2019): as Treasury under-secretary he took part in the 1971 gold decision; Carter appointed him Fed chairman, took office 6 August 1979; renominated by Reagan; chairman to 1987. [source: VOL]
- US inflation peaked at 14.8% (March 1980) and fell below 3% by 1983. Fed funds rate, averaging 11.2% in 1979, peaked at 20% in June 1981; prime rate 21.5% in 1981. The 1980–82 recession pushed unemployment over 10%. Policies included monetarist-inspired money-supply targeting. [source: VOL]
- Protests: the strongest political attacks in the Fed's history — indebted farmers blockaded the Fed's Eccles Building with tractors; homebuilders mailed pieces of 2x4 lumber. The shock disturbed the European Monetary System. Policy eased in 1982. [source: VOL]
- Global effect: rising US/European rates from 1979 drove up debt payments of borrowing countries; Arrighi argues the capital scarcity, "heralded by the Mexican default of 1982", created the environment for structural adjustment. [source: LADC; SAP]
- Volcker called the PATCO strike a "watershed" in the fight against inflation. [source: PATCO]

## 5. Thatcher's Britain
- Thatcher elected Conservative leader February 1975; IEA arranged meeting with Hayek; at the Conservative Research Department (summer 1975) she produced Hayek's *The Constitution of Liberty*: "'This', she said sternly, 'is what we believe', and banged Hayek down on the table" (recollection quoted in HAY). [source: HAY; THM]
- Won the May 1979 election ("Labour Isn't Working" posters, after the Winter of Discontent). First budget (1979): top income-tax rate cut from 83% to 60%, basic rate 33%→30%; VAT/indirect taxes raised; top rate cut to 40% in 1988 (basic 25%). Exchange controls abolished 1979 (Geoffrey Howe). [source: UKTAX; THA]
- Monetarism: higher interest rates to control money supply; Medium Term Financial Strategy (1980 budget), whose targets were overshot and revised upward in 1982 — so analysts argue Thatcherites were not strictly monetarist. [source: THA; THM]
- "You turn if you want to. The lady's not for turning." — 1980 Conservative conference, speech written by Ronald Millar. March 1981: 364 economists signed a statement that "present policies will deepen the depression, erode the industrial base of our economy and threaten its social and political stability". Approval fell to 23% (December 1980). [source: THA]
- Inflation down to 8.6% by 1982 from a high of 18%; unemployment over 3 million for the first time since the 1930s, peaking at 3.3 million in 1984. [source: THA]
- **TINA:** the attested Thatcher wording (Conservative Women's Conference, 21 May 1980) is "I believe people accept there's no real alternative"; the slogan "There is no alternative" became associated with her (and later Merkel's *alternativlos*, Cameron, Lagarde). [source: TINA]
- **Privatisation** ("a crucial ingredient of Thatcherism"): accelerated after 1983; more than £29 billion from selling nationalised industries and £18 billion from council houses. Share-owning adults rose from 7% to 25% during her tenure. Natural monopolies (gas, water, electricity) saw little gain in competition; results "mixed". She resisted rail privatisation (done by Major in 1994). [source: THA]
- **Right to Buy:** Housing Act 1980; discounts initially 33–50% (up to 70% for flats); more than a million families bought council homes; owner-occupation rose from 55% to 67%, 1979–90. [source: RTB; THA]
- **Big Bang:** 27 October 1986, London Stock Exchange: fixed commissions abolished, jobber/broker distinction ended, screen-based trading; settled an Office of Fair Trading case (agreement 1983). [source: BB]
- "There is no such thing!" — *Woman's Own* interview, 1987 (full passage: "...who is society? There is no such thing! There are individual men and women and there are families and no government can do anything except through people..."). [source: THA]
- Poll tax (Community Charge): Scotland 1989, England and Wales 1990, among her most unpopular policies. [source: THA]
- Strikes: 4,583 stoppages in 1979 (29 million working days lost); 630 in 1990. UK union membership fell from 13 million (1979) to about 7.3 million (2000); below 6 million in 2012; 6.6 million (22%) in 2025. [source: THA; UKTU]

### The miners' strike, 1984–85
- 6 March 1984: the National Coal Board (chairman Ian MacGregor, appointed 1983; he had halved British Steel's workforce) announced 20 pit closures with 20,000 job losses (of 187,000; 174/175 pits). The walkout began at Cortonwood (Yorkshire). Cabinet papers released in 2014 show MacGregor wanted to close 75 pits over three years (the NUM's Arthur Scargill had claimed over 70; government denied it). [source: MIN; THA]
- The NUM held no national ballot (delegate conference voted 69–54 against, 19 April 1984); the strike was ruled illegal in England and Wales in September 1984. Over a fifth of miners, especially in Nottinghamshire, kept working. [source: MIN]
- Battle of Orgreave, 18 June 1984: about 5,000 pickets and as many police. 11,291 arrests during the strike; 8,392 charged; 150–200 imprisoned. Two pickets (David Jones, Joe Green) killed; three teenagers died picking coal; taxi driver David Wilkie killed (30 November 1984) by strikers dropping concrete. [source: MIN]
- Thatcher, July 1984 (MIN: she called union leaders "the enemy within"; on 19 July 1984 she told the Commons that giving in would surrender parliamentary democracy to "the rule of the mob"): "We had to fight the enemy without in the Falklands. We always have to be aware of the enemy within, which is much more difficult to fight and more dangerous to liberty." [source: MIN]
- Solidarity: Women Against Pit Closures; Lesbians and Gays Support the Miners ("Pits and Perverts" concerts), after which the NUM backed gay rights. Soviet trade unions donated £1.5 million; damaging Libya allegations. [source: MIN]
- Strike ended 3 March 1985; "a defining moment in British industrial relations". Coal privatised December 1994; all 175 pits of 1983 closed by end of 2015; Grimethorpe was the poorest settlement in the country in 1994. [source: MIN]

## 6. Reagan's America
- Inaugural address, 20 January 1981: "In this present crisis, government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem." [source: WebSearch: https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/reagan1.asp; https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/inaugural-address-11]
- Economic Recovery Tax Act (Kemp–Roth), signed 13 August 1981: top marginal rate 70%→50%, lowest 14%→11%. 1982 tax increase undid about a third. Tax Reform Act 1986: Niskanen gives the overall fall in the top rate as 70.1% to 28.4%. Critics called supply-side policy "trickle-down" or "voodoo economics". [source: RE; ERTA]
- Defence spending rose (constant 2000 dollars) from $267.1 billion (4.9% of GDP) in 1980 to $393.1 billion (5.8%) in 1988. Federal debt held by the public nearly tripled, $738 billion → $2.1 trillion; the US went from largest creditor to largest debtor; Reagan called the debt his "greatest disappointment". [source: RE]
- Unemployment averaged 7.5% under Reagan; fell steadily after December 1982 to 5.4% when he left. Real GDP growth averaged 3.5%. Misery index 19.33 → 9.72. Poverty rate 13.0% (1980) → 15.2% (1983) → 13.0% (1988). [source: RE]
- Deregulation began under Carter (trucking, airlines, banking) — useful against a Reagan-only story. [source: NEO]
- **PATCO:** union of ~13,000 air traffic controllers; it had endorsed Reagan in 1980. Struck 7 a.m. 3 August 1981 (demanding a 32-hour week, big raises), illegal for federal employees. Reagan: "They are in violation of the law and if they do not report for work within 48 hours they have forfeited their jobs and will be terminated." On 5 August 11,345 strikers were fired and banned from federal service for life; union decertified 1981. Clinton lifted the ban 12 August 1993; by 2006 only 850 had been rehired. Joseph McCartin, *Collision Course* (2011); Reagan aides argued it emboldened private employers. [source: PATCO]
- US union density peaked at almost 35% in 1954; members peaked at about 21.0 million in 1979. BLS: 20.1% in 1983 (17.7 million) → 10.0% in 2025 (14.7 million); 2025: 32.9% public sector, 5.9% private sector. [source: USLU]
- Top 1% share (CBO, before taxes and transfers): 9.0% (1979) → 13.8% (1986) → 12.3% (1989). [source: RE]

## 7. Global South: debt, adjustment and the Washington Consensus
### Latin American debt crisis and the lost decade
- Latin America's external debt quadrupled from $75 billion (1975) to over $315 billion (1983), about 50% of regional GDP; debt service $12 billion (1975) → $66 billion (1982). Bank debt grew 20.4% a year 1975–82. [source: LADC]
- August 1982: Mexican finance minister Jesús Silva-Herzog declared Mexico could not service its debt (90-day moratorium); banks halted lending. Real urban wages fell 20–40% in the ten years after 1980; regional GDP grew only 2.3% 1980–85 but per capita fell almost 9%; Latin America paid back $108 billion in 1982–85. Known as *la década perdida*, "the lost decade". Most countries abandoned import-substitution industrialisation for IMF-backed export orientation. [source: LADC]
- Mexico was the first country to implement structural adjustment in exchange for loans. [source: SAP]
- Bolivia 1985: Decree 21060 (29 August 1985) under Paz Estenssoro and planning minister Gonzalo Sánchez de Lozada, with advice from Jeffrey Sachs, ended hyperinflation — the first "shock therapy" after Chile. [source: SHK]
- Mexican peso crisis ("tequila crisis"), December 1994, after NAFTA took effect 1 January 1994 and the Zapatista rising. [source: PESO]
- Brazil cut tariffs from 32% (1990) to 14% (1994). [source: NEO]
- Argentina: hailed in 1998 in Washington as the most successful reformer; collapsed 2001; Kirchner and Lula signed the "Buenos Aires Consensus" (2003) against the Washington Consensus. [source: WC]

### The Washington Consensus
- Coined 1989 by John Williamson (Institute for International Economics). Ten points: fiscal discipline; redirect spending from subsidies to education, health, infrastructure; broaden tax base with moderate marginal rates; market-determined positive real interest rates; competitive exchange rates; trade liberalisation; liberalised inward FDI; privatisation; deregulation (except safety, environment, prudential); legal security for property rights. [source: WC]
- Williamson opposed the broad use of the term: he "consciously excluded" capital-account liberalisation and did not mean "monetarism, supply-side economics, or a minimal state ... which I think of as the quintessentially neoliberal ideas". In 2002: "The phrase 'Washington Consensus' is a damaged brand name." He later judged results on growth, employment and poverty "disappointing, to say the least". [source: WC]
- Origins debate: Yergin and Stanislaw say the prescriptions were "developed in Latin America, by Latin Americans"; Birdsall et al. (2011) similar. [source: WC]
- Evidence mixed: a 2020 study found reforms raised real GDP per capita over 5–10 years; a 2021 study of Brazil, Chile, Mexico: macro stability much improved, growth "generally disappointing"; another 2021 study: in sub-Saharan Africa initial declines in per capita growth in the 1980s–90s, notable increases after 2000. Dani Rodrik: China and India grew fast while largely ignoring the Consensus (disputed). World Bank's own 2005 report *Economic Growth in the 1990s*: "there is no unique universal set of rules". [source: WC]

### Structural adjustment in Africa
- **Berg Report** (World Bank, 1981), *Accelerated Development in Sub-Saharan Africa: An Agenda for Action*, written by Elliot Berg after a 1979 request by African governors; answered the OAU's inward-looking Lagos Plan of Action (1980) with outward-looking liberalisation; criticised for blaming domestic policy and downplaying external shocks. (Note: the BERG article's text gives the subtitle as "A Plan for Action", but its cover image and the WC bibliography give "An Agenda for Action" — use the latter.) [source: BERG; WC]
- SAPs: IMF/World Bank loans conditioned on privatisation, trade and investment liberalisation, budget balance, devaluation; during the 1980s packages for most countries of Latin America and sub-Saharan Africa; the debt crisis gave the IMF leverage over "more than 70 developing countries". Replaced in 2002 (in name) by Poverty Reduction Strategy Papers. [source: SAP]
- Critiques: growth in African countries in the 1980s–90s fell below previous decades; agriculture suffered as state support was withdrawn; early industrialisation was "wiped out"; cuts to health and education; utilities privatisation (Cameroon, Ghana etc.); sovereignty and "neo-colonial" critiques; gendered effects. Defences: some studies find SAPs "weakly associated with growth" and reduced inflation; supporters argue governments were otherwise captured by rent-seeking. [source: SAP]
- Malawi: donors long pressed it to cut fertiliser subsidies; after the disastrous 2005 harvest nearly five million of 13 million people needed food aid; President Bingu wa Mutharika restored deep subsidies, producing record maize harvests in 2006–07. [source: WC]
- **Ghana as showcase:** Economic Recovery Programme from April 1983 under Jerry Rawlings' PNDC, with IMF/World Bank support, despite its populist origins; seen as one of few successful adjustments in sub-Saharan Africa; first African SAP with a formal programme to mitigate social costs; critics say poverty and exclusion of non-export sectors persisted. Ghana defaulted again in December 2022 and entered a new IMF programme in 2023. [source: WebSearch: https://countrystudies.us/ghana/70.htm; https://opendocs.ids.ac.uk/opendocs/handle/123456789/5637; https://cdn.odi.org/media/documents/2634.pdf; GHA]
- **African critique:** Thandika Mkandawire (1940–2020, Malawian economist, first chair of African Development at LSE) and Charles Soludo, eds., *Our Continent, Our Future: African Perspectives on Structural Adjustment* (CODESRIA, 1999) — about 30 commissioned studies, mostly by African economists; argued for a broader agenda and a more active state within a market economy. [source: MKA; WebSearch: https://publication.codesria.org/index.php/pub/catalog/book/344]

## 8. Other paths
- **New Zealand, Rogernomics:** Roger Douglas, finance minister 1984–88 in David Lange's *Labour* government: 20% devaluation (July 1984 currency crisis), float, corporatisation, removal of farm subsidies, tariff cuts, GST at 10%. Term coined by the *New Zealand Listener*, February 1985. About 76,000 manufacturing jobs lost 1987–92; food banks ~365 by 1994; child poverty 14% (1982) → 29% (1994). Supporters: inflation (17.15% in 1980) single-digit after his tenure; income tax rates halved. Continued by Ruth Richardson ("Ruthanasia", 1990–93). Lange in 1990: "Where the market results in manifest inequity, or poor economic performance, the Government must get involved." [source: RN] (RN's GNI-per-capita doubling figure is in nominal US dollars — do not use.)
- **India 1991:** reserves fell to about three weeks of imports; Gulf War oil prices, collapse of Soviet trade; India pledged gold — 47 tonnes airlifted to the Bank of England, 20 tonnes to UBS — and borrowed from the IMF. PM P.V. Narasimha Rao and finance minister Manmohan Singh devalued the rupee (9% on 1 July, 11% on 3 July 1991, about 18–20% in all); Singh's budget of 24 July 1991 dismantled most industrial licensing ("Licence Raj"), cut import licensing, allowed 51% foreign equity in priority industries. How far the IMF shaped the reforms is disputed. Growth had averaged ~3.6% 1951–74 ("Hindu rate of growth") and 5.6% in the 1980s. [source: IND91]
- India: growth faster after 1991 and poverty fell sharply (debated how much due to reform); top 10% income share 35% (1991) → 57.1% (2014), bottom 50% 20.1% → 13.1%. [source: INDL; IND91]
- **China (contrast case):** reforms launched at the Third Plenum, 18 December 1978; decollectivised agriculture (household responsibility system), Special Economic Zones (Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Xiamen; Shekou, 31 January 1979), dual-track pricing (1985), township and village enterprises. Leadership rejected shock price reform in 1986 (and again pulled back after 1988 inflation); Tiananmen 1989; Deng's southern tour 1992; mass SOE privatisation 1997–98; WTO 2001. GDP growth ~9.5% a year 1978–2013; extreme poverty reduced by 800 million, 1978–2018. Lin Chun: pre-reform investments in health, education and land ownership also explain success. [source: CHN]
- Isabella Weber, *How China Escaped Shock Therapy: The Market Reform Debate* (Routledge, 2021): China chose gradual dual-track price reform, reforming "the core by reforming at the margins"; it "escaped shock therapy" twice, in 1986 and 1988. [source: WEB; WebSearch: https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/usappblog/2021/10/10/book-review-how-china-escaped-shock-therapy-the-market-reform-debate-by-isabella-m-weber/]
- Harvey's *Brief History* includes China ("neoliberalism with Chinese characteristics") — NOT VERIFIED here; NEO only says China implemented "significant neoliberal reform", which is itself contested.
- **Developmental states:** Chalmers Johnson, *MITI and the Japanese Miracle* (1982) — far-sighted bureaucratic intervention; Alice Amsden: South Korea "deliberately getting relative prices wrong" (1989); Park Chung-hee's Economic Planning Board after 1961; Peter Evans, *Embedded Autonomy* (1995). World Bank's *The East Asian Miracle* (1993) credited export orientation and macro management; critics argued state intervention mattered much more. [source: DEV; TIG]
- **Asian financial crisis 1997:** began in Thailand on 2 July 1997 when the baht peg broke (the "Tom Yum Kung crisis"); hot money and short-term dollar borrowing; IMF programmes ($40 billion initially for Korea, Thailand, Indonesia) with high interest rates and austerity. South Korea received $58.4 billion (then the IMF's largest); foreign-ownership ceiling raised from 26% to 100%. Rupiah ~2,600 → over 14,000 to the dollar (January 1998); Indonesia lost 13.5% of GDP in 1998; Suharto resigned 21 May 1998 after riots. Malaysia (Mahathir, who blamed Soros) refused IMF aid, pegged the ringgit at 3.8 and imposed capital controls (September 1998). Stiglitz and Sachs blamed IMF prescriptions and financial panic. [source: AFC]
- **Post-communist shock therapy (brief):** Poland's Balcerowicz plan (commission September 1989, with Sachs) vs Russia; nearly all post-Soviet states suffered deep recessions; Russia's poverty (<$4/day) rose from ~2% (1987–88) to 50% (1993–95), the worst peacetime mortality increase of any industrial country; debate whether this was Soviet collapse or policy; Sachs says his advice was not followed; advocates cite Poland, critics say Poland was more gradual. Naomi Klein, *The Shock Doctrine* (2007). [source: SHK]

## 9. The Third Way and the zenith of the order
- Third Way: Clinton (1992 campaign), Blair, Schröder, Chrétien; Anthony Giddens, *The Third Way: The Renewal of Social Democracy* (1998). Blair: "The Third Way stands for a modernised social democracy, passionate in its commitment to social justice". [source: TW]
- Clinton, State of the Union, 23 January 1996: "the era of big government is over," continuing "but we cannot go back to the time when our citizens were left to fend for themselves." [source: SOTU96]
- NAFTA; welfare reform (PRWORA), signed 22 August 1996, replaced AFDC with TANF, five-year lifetime limit, fulfilling Clinton's promise to "end welfare as we know it"; Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act, signed 12 November 1999, repealed part of Glass–Steagall (1933), legalising the Citicorp–Travelers merger (Citigroup, 1998); Commodity Futures Modernization Act (2000). [source: NEO; PRWORA; GLBA]
- GLBA and 2008: Obama and critics blamed it; Phil Gramm, Clinton, DeLong, Cowen argued it did not cause and even softened the crisis (Lehman was among the least diversified firms). [source: GLBA]
- Gerstle: Reagan was "the ideological architect" of the neoliberal order, Clinton its "key facilitator"; the order became dominant in the 1990s–2000s. [source: NEO]
- New Labour largely kept Thatcher's reforms (privatisation, union laws, flexible labour markets). [source: THM; NEO]

## 10. Financialisation and inequality
- Financialisation: Gerald Epstein — "the increasing importance of financial markets, financial motives, financial institutions, and financial elites in the operation of the economy"; Greta Krippner — profit-making "increasingly through financial channels rather than through trade and commodity production". [source: FIN]
- US financial sector slightly over 3% of GDP in the 1970s ("boring banking"); commercial-bank assets 53% of GDP (1978) → 84% (2007); broker-dealer assets 1.3% → 22% of GDP. [source: FIN]
- US inequality (Piketty–Saez–Zucman): top 1% share of pre-tax income 12% (1980) → 20% (2014); bottom 50% 20% → 12%; top 1% earned 27 times the bottom-50% average in 1981, 81 times in 2014. Lowest top-1% share in 1913–2016 was 10.9% (1975, 1976, 1980); 19.6% in 2007. CBO: top-1% after-tax income grew 275% 1979–2007 vs 18% for the bottom fifth; after-tax top-1% share ~8% (1979) → over 17% (2007). Different series give different levels (cf. RE's 9.0% for 1979) — cite the series. [source: USINEQ]
- Piketty, *Le Capital au XXIe siècle* (August 2013); English *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (trans. Arthur Goldhammer, April 2014); #1 NYT bestseller; 2.5 million copies by end-2017; argument r > g (later downplayed by Piketty as not the main driver of labour-income inequality). [source: PIK]
- Global extreme poverty fell from about 43% (1990) to 13.4% (2015) by the World Bank line then in use; around 10% (c. 831 million, UN estimate) in 2025 at the $3/day line. Most of the reduction in China, India, Indonesia and Pakistan; extreme poor now concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa. (Lines and estimates have been revised; give round figures.) [source: POV]

## 11. 2008 and the afterlife
- Subprime crisis from early 2007; Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac seized 7 September 2008; Lehman Brothers filed the largest bankruptcy in US history on 15 September 2008; AIG rescue; House rejected TARP 29 September (Dow −777.68 points, then the largest point drop), passed it 3 October ($700 billion). Bernanke reportedly: "If we don't do this, we may not have an economy on Monday." [source: GFC]
- Costs: about 8.7 million US jobs lost; unemployment 5% (2007) → 10% (October 2009); US poverty 12.5% (2007) → 15.1% (2010); household wealth fell $11 trillion; Dodd–Frank (2010); followed by the eurozone crisis (Greece from late 2009) and Iceland's collapse. [source: GFC]
- Austerity and TINA: Merkel's *alternativlos* (2010, "un-word of the year"); Cameron (2013) "there is no alternative"; governments that imposed austerity were voted out in 12 of 15 cases. [source: TINA]
- Mirowski, *Never Let a Serious Crisis Go to Waste: How Neoliberalism Survived the Financial Meltdown* (Verso, 2013): neoliberal thought proved unfalsifiable and survived the crash. [source: MIR] (NEO's bibliography lists a 2014 edition.)
- Industrial policy's return: CHIPS and Science Act, signed 9 August 2022 (~$280 billion authorised, $52.7 billion appropriated for semiconductors); Inflation Reduction Act, signed 16 August 2022 (CBO/JCT: $891 billion spending incl. $391 billion energy and climate — the largest US climate investment). [source: CHIPS; IRA]
- Jake Sullivan, "Renewing American Economic Leadership", Brookings, 27 April 2023: "a modern American industrial strategy"; trade liberalisation's promise to export goods "not jobs and capacity" was "a promise made but not kept"; he referred to what some called a "new Washington consensus" (he did not coin it). [source: WebSearch: https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/speeches-remarks/2023/04/27/remarks-by-national-security-advisor-jake-sullivan-on-renewing-american-economic-leadership-at-the-brookings-institution; https://nationalinterest.org/node/206439]
- Trump's second-term tariffs: average effective US tariff rate rose from 2.5% (January 2025) to an estimated 27% (April 2025), the highest in over a century; "Liberation Day" tariffs (April 2025) under IEEPA. On 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* that IEEPA does not authorise tariffs; Section 232 tariffs (up to 50% on steel, aluminium, cars, copper) remain; Trump then imposed a 10% global tariff under Section 122. Post-ruling effective-rate estimates differ (Yale Budget Lab ~9.1%; TAR: 12.1% as of 21 July 2026). [source: TAR; WebSearch: https://www.cfr.org/articles/the-supreme-court-clipped-trumps-tariff-powers-and-opened-new-trade-battle-fronts; https://taxfoundation.org/blog/supreme-court-trump-tariffs-ruling/] **Caution: the TAR Wikipedia article is tagged as possibly AI-generated (September 2026); keep to the court ruling and the "highest in a century" point.**
- Populism: TINA researchers link the post-1990s "party cartel" consensus to the rise of populist parties (AfD, Orbán). Gerstle links the 2008 crisis to Trump, Sanders and (in summaries) Brexit. [source: TINA; WebSearch: https://www.internationalaffairs.org.au/australianoutlook/book-review-the-rise-and-fall-of-the-neoliberal-order/]

## Debates
- **Is "neoliberalism" a useful term?** Critics of the word (Stedman Jones on "catch-all shorthand"; Venugopal "incoherent"; Hutton "leftist insult"; Boas and Gans-Morse 2009, "From New Liberal Philosophy to Anti-Liberal Slogan") vs defenders (Gerstle; Metcalf citing the IMF's 2016 "Neoliberalism: Oversold?"; Harvey). Its own early champions (Friedman 1951) meant something more state-friendly than the later usage. Defenders of the policies usually reject the label: McCloskey calls her position simply "liberalism" ("humane true liberalism"), crediting the Great Enrichment to liberal ideas. [source: NEO; WebSearch: https://liberalforum.eu/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/09_Liberal-Read_The-Revolutionary-Liberal-Idea.pdf]
- **Class project or idea-driven movement?** Harvey: a "class project" to restore elite power; Duménil and Lévy similar; Volscho: conscious capitalist mobilisation in the 1970s. Stedman Jones and Mirowski/Plehwe ("neoliberal thought collective") stress ideas and networks; Slobodian stresses global institutions — neoliberals sought to "encase" markets through supranational law (GATT/WTO, EU, investment law), not to abolish the state; reviewers (e.g. David Gordon, *QJAE*) dispute details such as Mises's place in the "Geneva School". [source: NEO; WebSearch: https://www.dissentmagazine.org/article/neoliberalism-world-order-review-quinn-slobodian-globalists/; https://qjae.mises.org/api/v1/articles/118908-review-essay-_globalists-the-end-of-empire-and-the-birth-of-neoliberalism_.pdf]
- **Results:** Critics — inequality (Piketty; CBO), financial instability, social costs (miners, Latin America's lost decade, African SAPs, Russia's mortality). Defenders — inflation conquered; long expansions; Norberg and McCloskey on globalisation and the fall in world poverty; Washington Consensus studies showing medium-term growth gains. Counterpoint: much of the poverty fall happened in China and India, which (Rodrik) did not follow the Consensus, and in East Asian developmental states (Johnson, Amsden, Wade) vs the World Bank's 1993 *East Asian Miracle*. [source: WC; DEV; TIG; POV; NOR]
- **Structural adjustment in Africa:** World Bank/IMF and defenders (Berg; Ghana showcase; SAP "weakly associated with growth", lower inflation) vs Mkandawire and Soludo, postcolonial and feminist critics (contraction, deindustrialisation, cuts to health and education, sovereignty). A 2021 study finds early decline then post-2000 growth. [source: SAP; BERG; WC]
- **Was the Washington Consensus imposed?** Williamson and Yergin/Stanislaw: Latin American technocrats' own agenda; critics (Stiglitz, Arrighi): leverage of debt and conditionality. [source: WC; SAP]
- **Shock therapy vs gradualism:** Sachs/Poland vs Russia; China (Weber) as gradualist alternative; whether post-Soviet collapse or policy caused the mortality crisis. [source: SHK; WEB]
- **Has the neoliberal order ended?** Gerstle (2022): a "political order" is a paradigm the opposition accepts; the 2008–09 crash fractured it in the 2010s, opening space for Trump and Sanders; some reviewers read him as treating Covid as a death knell; the *Irish Times* reviewer doubted that a new order will replace it, predicting gridlock. Mirowski: neoliberalism survived 2008. Sullivan's 2023 "new consensus", Biden's industrial policy and Trump's tariffs are cited as evidence of a break, but the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs in 2026 and much of the institutional order (independent central banks, WTO rules, privatised utilities) persists. [source: WebSearch: https://www.internationalaffairs.org.au/australianoutlook/book-review-the-rise-and-fall-of-the-neoliberal-order/; https://www.irishtimes.com/culture/books/the-rise-and-fall-of-the-neoliberal-order-a-compelling-analysis-1.4851633; MIR; BID]
