## The idea of the cryosphere
- "Cryosphere" comes from Greek *kryos* ("cold, frost, ice") and *sphaira* ("globe"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryosphere]
- About 70% of the world's fresh water is frozen in Antarctica; melted, it would raise global sea level by almost 60 m. The Antarctic ice sheet averages about 1.9 km thick; the continent covers about 14.2 million km². [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctica]
- The Greenland ice sheet covers about 1.7 million km² (about 80% of Greenland); if all of it (about 2.9 million km³) melted, global sea level would rise by about 7.4 m (an older estimate gave 7.2 m). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland_ice_sheet]
- Permafrost underlies around 15% of the exposed land of the Northern Hemisphere and 11% of global land surface, about 18 million km². [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permafrost]

## The Indigenous Arctic: deep history
- The Thule people ("proto-Inuit") were the ancestors of all modern Inuit (and, per Wikipedia, of Yupik peoples); they developed in coastal Alaska by about 1000 CE, spread east across northern Canada and reached Greenland by the 13th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thule_people]
- Thule technology: kayaks and umiaks (large skin boats), dog sleds, open-sea whaling in cooperative boat crews; the whaleboat captain (*umialik*) is still a prominent position in Alaskan Arctic communities. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thule_people]
- The Thule displaced the earlier Dorset culture (Paleo-Eskimo; c. 500 BCE to between 1000 and 1500 CE), called the *Tuniit* in Inuktitut; by the 15th century the Thule had replaced the Dorset. Genetic studies find the Dorset distinct from later groups; no Dorset genes have been found in the present Greenlandic population. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorset_culture; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thule_people; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland]
- Norse settlements in Greenland were founded after 986 CE by settlers from Iceland led by Erik the Red; the Thule began moving into Greenland around 1200 and were the dominant population by about 1300. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_settlements_in_Greenland]
- The Norse population at its height is estimated at 2,000–10,000, with recent estimates trending toward the lower figure; about 620 farm ruins are known (500 Eastern, 95 Western, 20 Middle Settlement). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_settlements_in_Greenland]
- The Norse economy exported walrus ivory, narwhal tusks, polar bear and seal skins and walrus-hide rope, much of it hunted in the far north (Norðrsetur, around Disko Bay). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_settlements_in_Greenland]
- The Western Settlement disappeared in the mid-14th century; a 1408 marriage at Hvalsey is the last known written record of the Norse in Greenland; the Eastern Settlement disappeared in the mid-15th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_settlements_in_Greenland]
- By around 1300 seal hunting provided over three-quarters of Norse Greenlanders' food (isotope evidence). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age]
- In 1721 Denmark–Norway sent the missionary Hans Egede to find and re-Christianize the Norse; he found none and missionized the Inuit instead; this begins Danish colonization. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_settlements_in_Greenland; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland]
- Inuit oral tradition preserved three stories about the Norse, recorded in the 19th century and published by Hinrich Rink (1866–71) — the only Inuit memory of them. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_settlements_in_Greenland]

## Sámi, Nenets, Chukchi and other northern peoples
- Sámi: Indigenous people of Sápmi (northern Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia's Kola Peninsula); the exonyms "Lapp/Laplander" are regarded as offensive by some. Population estimates vary widely (Wikipedia infobox: about 80,000; Sápmi 63,831–107,341). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sámi_people]
- Reindeer herding is the best-known Sámi livelihood but as of 2007 only about 10% of Sámi were connected to it (about 2,800 full-time herders in Norway); it is legally reserved for Sámi in some areas. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sámi_people]
- Around 1720 the missionary Thomas von Westen, the "Apostle of the Sámi", burned drums and sacred objects in Norway; of the thousands of Sámi drums that once existed only about 70 are known to survive. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sámi_people]
- The Kautokeino rebellion of 1852 was one of the few Sámi uprisings against the Norwegian state; the skulls of its two executed leaders were taken for research and reburied only in 1997. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sámi_people]
- Norwegianization policy (from roughly the 1850s for about a century) suppressed Sámi (and Kven) languages; the Alta dam controversy of the late 1970s–early 1980s rallied the Sámi rights movement; the Sámi Act (1987) led to the Sámi Parliament of Norway, opened 9 October 1989 at Karasjok. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sámi_people; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sámi_Parliament_of_Norway; https://www.thelocal.no/20230601/how-the-governments-norwegianisation-policies-harmed-indigenous-people-in-norway]
- Norway's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (set up by the Storting in 2018) reported on 1 June 2023 that Norwegianization was wider and more intrusive than previously known. Reports say the Storting apologized in November 2024 (secondary report only; treat as "reported"). [source: https://www.thelocal.no/20230601/how-the-governments-norwegianisation-policies-harmed-indigenous-people-in-norway; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sámi_people]
- In Sweden, 1913–1920s race-biology research collected material from living Sámi; the 1986 Chernobyl fallout contaminated reindeer lichen pastures. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sámi_people]
- Nenets: a Samoyedic people of the Russian Arctic; 2021 census about 49,600–49,800 (the article gives both 49,646 and 49,787), most in the Yamalo-Nenets Autonomous Okrug, where oil and gas development threatens herding. Large-scale reindeer herding emerged in the 18th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nenets]
- Chukchi: Maritime Chukchi (sea-mammal hunters) and Reindeer Chukchi (inland herders). Russians reached the Kolyma (1643) and Anadyr (1649); wars of conquest from 1701; Major Dmitry Pavlutsky's campaigns were notoriously brutal and ended with his death in March 1747 (the Chukchi reputedly kept his head); in 1764 Russia abandoned the Anadyr fort, which had cost about 1,380,000 rubles against 29,150 in taxes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chukchi_people]
- The published Early Modern Russia chapter already covers *yasak* fur tribute and Chukchi resistance "for over a century" — cross-refer.
- Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit (IQ), "that which has long been known by Inuit", is now a guiding principle of Nunavut's government; Nunavut was established in 1999 after the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit_Qaujimajatuqangit; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit]
- "Eskimo" is considered pejorative by many Canadian and Greenlandic Inuit; the old etymology "eater of raw meat" is discredited (probably Innu-aimun "one who laces a snowshoe"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit]

## Colonial cold: relocation, bodies, science
- High Arctic relocation: in August 1953 Canada moved seven or eight Inuit families from Inukjuak (northern Quebec) to Grise Fiord and Resolute, joined by three families from Pond Inlet; 92 Inuit in total over the 1950s. Motives included asserting Arctic sovereignty. Canada refused an apology in the 1990s but set up a C$10 million trust fund (March 1996). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Arctic_relocation] (Canada did formally apologize in 2010 — NOT VERIFIED here; see below.)
- Thule Air Base (now Pituffik Space Base), built by the US 1951–53; in 1953 Denmark relocated about 130 Inughuit of "Old Thule" to a new village (Qaanaaq). Denmark's Supreme Court on 28 November 2003 ruled it an unlawful expropriative intervention and awarded 500,000 kroner to the community and 15,000–25,000 kroner to individuals. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pituffik_Space_Base]
- Project Iceworm: a secret US plan for nuclear missile sites under the Greenland ice, run from Camp Century 1960–66, revealed in documents declassified in 1996. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland]
- Forced contraception: in the 1960s–70s about 4,500 Greenlandic Inuit women and girls (roughly half of fertile females), some as young as 12, were fitted with IUDs by Danish doctors; fertility fell from 7 to 2.3 children per woman between 1966 and 1974. Danish PM Mette Frederiksen apologized in writing on 27 August 2025 and in person in Nuuk on 24 September 2025, announcing a reconciliation fund. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland; https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2025/09/26/world/denmark-greenland-forced-contraception-apology]
- In 1897 Peary brought six Inughuit, including the child Minik, from Greenland to New York; they were studied by the American Museum of Natural History; most died of tuberculosis; the museum faked a burial of Minik's father Qisuk and displayed his skeleton. Minik died in the 1918 flu pandemic; the remains were returned to Greenland in 1993 (Kenn Harper's campaign; book *Give Me My Father's Body*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minik_Wallace]
- Peary removed the Cape York meteorite (reached 1894), which Inuit had used as a source of iron for tools. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Peary]

## Polar exploration: Franklin's lost expedition
- Sir John Franklin's expedition left Greenhithe on 19 May 1845 with HMS *Erebus* and HMS *Terror* to complete the Northwest Passage; final complement 129 men. Last seen by European whalers in Baffin Bay in late July 1845. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin%27s_lost_expedition]
- Wintered 1845–46 at Beechey Island (three graves); beset off King William Island from September 1846; Franklin died 11 June 1847; ships abandoned April 1848 (Victory Point note dated 25 April 1848, signed by Crozier and Fitzjames, the last communication; 105 survivors). No one survived. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin%27s_lost_expedition]
- Causes proposed: hypothermia, starvation, scurvy, lead poisoning (tin-can solder hastily applied by contractor Stephen Goldner, or the ships' water systems), zinc deficiency; the lead hypothesis remains debated. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin%27s_lost_expedition]
- In 1854 John Rae (Hudson's Bay Company), told by Inuit near Pelly Bay of 35–40 white men who starved near the Back River, reported evidence of cannibalism and bought relics (silver cutlery of Franklin, Crozier and others). Lady Jane Franklin enlisted Charles Dickens, who attacked the report in *Household Words* as "the wild tales of a herd of savages". Cut marks on bones later supported Rae and the Inuit. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Rae_(explorer); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin%27s_lost_expedition]
- Lady Franklin funded McClintock's search; Lt William Hobson found the Victory Point note in May 1859. Charles Francis Hall (1860s), with Inuit guides Ipirvik and Taqulittuq, gathered hundreds of pages of Inuit testimony. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin%27s_lost_expedition]
- David Woodman, *Unravelling the Franklin Mystery* (1992) and *Strangers Among Us* (1995), used Inuit testimony to argue many survivors lived past 1848 and re-manned a ship. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin%27s_lost_expedition]
- 1980s: Owen Beattie's forensic project; the permafrost-preserved body of John Torrington (Beechey Island) became famous. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin%27s_lost_expedition]
- The wreck of *Erebus* was found by Parks Canada on 2 September 2014 in Wilmot and Crampton Bay "corroborating Inuit testimony"; Inuk historian Louie Kamookak (died 2018, aged 58) had collected the oral histories. *Terror* was found in 2016 in Terror Bay by the Arctic Research Foundation, after Inuk hunter Sammy Kogvik recalled seeing a mast in the ice years earlier. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Erebus_(1826); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Terror_(1813)]
- In September 2024 researchers (Stenton, Fratpietro, Park) identified a mandible as Captain James Fitzjames's by DNA. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin%27s_lost_expedition]

## Polar exploration: the race to the poles
- Amundsen led the first transit of the Northwest Passage (1903–06, sloop *Gjøa*); wintering at today's Gjoa Haven he learned from Netsilik Inuit to use sled dogs and wear animal skins, which he found invaluable at the South Pole. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roald_Amundsen]
- His polar party wore clothing modelled on Netsilik Inuit garments and sealskin suits from northern Greenland. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amundsen%27s_South_Pole_expedition]
- Amundsen kept his destination secret even from his crew until *Fram* left Madeira on 9 September 1910; base Framheim, Bay of Whales; five men, four sledges and 52 dogs set out 19 October 1911; reached the pole on 14 December 1911 (some sources say 15 December because of the date line), naming it Polheim; back at Framheim 25 January 1912. Dogs were killed for food at the "Butchers' Shop". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amundsen%27s_South_Pole_expedition]
- Scott's Terra Nova party (Scott, Wilson, Bowers, Oates, Edgar Evans) reached the pole on 17 January 1912, 34 days after Amundsen; Evans died 17 February; Oates walked out on about 16–17 March (his 32nd birthday was 17 March); Scott's last diary entry 29 March 1912; the tent with Scott, Wilson and Bowers was found on 12 November 1912, 11 miles south of One Ton Depot. Scott used mixed transport: dogs, motor sledges and Siberian ponies, with man-hauling on the plateau. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_Nova_Expedition; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Oates]
- Terra Nova's science: the winter journey to Cape Crozier (June–July 1911) for emperor penguin eggs — Cherry-Garrard's *The Worst Journey in the World* (1922). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_Nova_Expedition; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Shackleton]
- Shackleton's Nimrod expedition (1907–09) reached 88°23′S, 97 geographical miles from the pole. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Shackleton]
- Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition (1914–17): *Endurance* was beset in the Weddell Sea and sank on 21 November 1915; 28 men camped on the ice, reached Elephant Island by boat; Shackleton and five others sailed the *James Caird* about 800 miles (1,300 km) to South Georgia, crossed the island to the Stromness whaling station (20 May 1916); the Chilean Navy tug *Yelcho* under Luis Pardo rescued the Elephant Island party on 30 August 1916 without loss of life. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Trans-Antarctic_Expedition]
- The Ross Sea party on the other side of the continent lost three men: Arnold Spencer-Smith (scurvy), and Aeneas Mackintosh and Victor Hayward (lost on sea ice, 8 May 1916). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Trans-Antarctic_Expedition]
- The *Endurance* wreck was found on 5 March 2022 by the Endurance22 search, at 3,008 m depth, "in a brilliant state of preservation"; it is a protected historic site. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endurance_(1912_ship)]
- North Pole disputes: Frederick Cook claimed 21 April 1908 (a University of Copenhagen commission found the claim unproven in December 1909; his Denali "summit" photo was taken on a ridge 19 miles away). Robert Peary claimed 6 April 1909, with Matthew Henson and four Inuit — Ootah, Egingwah, Seegloo and Ooqueah. Congress thanked Peary in March 1911. Wally Herbert's reassessment (NGS article 1988; book *The Noose of Laurels*, 1989) concluded Peary did not reach the pole but may have come within about 60 miles. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Peary; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Cook]
- The Peary 1908–09 effort used 19 Inuit, 140 dogs and 28 sledges at its peak. Peary and Henson fathered children with Inuit women (Peary with Aleqasina, beginning when she was about 14; their son Kali; Henson's son Anaukaq). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Peary; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Henson]
- Henson, an African American, published *A Negro Explorer at the North Pole* (1912); became the first African American life member of the Explorers Club (1937); reinterred at Arlington in 1988. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Henson]
- The airship *Norge* (Amundsen, Ellsworth, Nobile and 13 others) flew over the North Pole on 12 May 1926 — the first verified arrival if the Cook, Peary and Byrd claims are rejected. Amundsen disappeared in June 1928 on a rescue flight. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roald_Amundsen]
- Antarctica's ice shelf was first sighted by Bellingshausen and Lazarev's Russian expedition on 27 January 1820, days before Bransfield (30 January). The first confirmed landing on the continental mainland was at Cape Adare in 1895 (earlier claims include the sealer John Davis, February 1821). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctica]

## Ice cores as archives
- Ice cores hold annual snow layers, isotopes recording past temperature, and air bubbles recording past CO₂ and methane; volcanic ash and sulphate, cosmic-ray isotopes (beryllium-10) and bomb tritium from 1950s–60s nuclear tests date the layers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_core]
- Volcanic markers: Laki (Iceland, 1783) appears in Greenland ice but not Antarctic; Tambora (Indonesia, 1815) appears in both. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_core]
- Camp Century (US Army CRREL, Greenland) reached the base of the ice sheet at 1,387 m in July 1966; Camp Century and Dye 3 cores revealed Dansgaard–Oeschger abrupt warming events. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_core]
- Vostok Station (Soviet, founded 16 December 1957 during the International Geophysical Year) holds the lowest reliably measured temperature on Earth, −89.2 °C (21 July 1983). Its core, drilled with French collaboration, stopped at 3,623 m (1998 per the article's wording, after a halt in 1996 over contamination of subglacial Lake Vostok — the dates in the article are muddled; say "in the late 1990s") and gave a 420,000-year record covering four glacial cycles. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vostok_Station]
- Greenland Ice Core Project (GRIP, European) drilled a 3,029 m core to bedrock at Summit, 1989–92. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland_ice_core_project]
- EPICA Dome C (European) completed drilling in December 2004 at 3,270 m; the record now reaches about 800,000 years (initial publication 2004 gave 740,000 years, eight glacial cycles). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Project_for_Ice_Coring_in_Antarctica; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_core]
- Beyond EPICA – Oldest Ice: early 2025 drilling at Little Dome C to 2,800 m yielded a probably continuous record back 1.2 million years; analyses in 2025 confirmed ice over 1.2 million years old. Coordinator Carlo Barbante: CO₂ is now 50% higher than at any time in the past 800,000 years. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Project_for_Ice_Coring_in_Antarctica]
- Allan Hills (Antarctica, 2016): blue-ice cores include ice about 2.7 million years old (not a continuous sequence). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_core]
- In 1969 lead in Greenland ice was found to have risen over 200-fold since pre-industrial times (Clair Patterson's work on leaded petrol pollution). Hong, Candelone, Patterson and Boutron (*Science*, 1994) showed Greek and Roman lead pollution in Greenland ice. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_core; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_economy]
- McConnell et al., *PNAS* 2018 ("Lead pollution recorded in Greenland ice indicates European emissions tracked plagues, wars, and imperial expansion during antiquity", doi 10.1073/pnas.1721818115): an annually dated lead-emission record 1100 BCE–800 CE; emissions rose with Phoenician expansion, accelerated with Carthaginian and Roman mining in Iberia, peaked under the Empire, dipped with Republican wars, and fell with the Antonine Plague, staying low for over 500 years. [source: https://caes.wp.st-andrews.ac.uk/bibliographic-resources/lead-pollution-recorded-in-greenland-ice-indicates-european-emissions-tracked-plagues-wars-and-imperial-expansion-during-antiquity; https://www.sci.news/archaeology/lead-pollution-greenland-ancient-civilizations-06018.html]
- Lead in Greenland ice quadrupled over prehistoric levels in the Imperial era; world lead output would not exceed Roman levels until the mid-18th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_economy]

## The Little Ice Age (brief; mainly cross-refer)
- Term introduced by François Matthes in 1939; conventionally 16th–19th centuries, some prefer about 1300–1850; IPCC AR6 describes a cool period from around the 15th century with global mean temperature 1450–1850 about −0.03 °C [−0.30 to 0.06] relative to 1850–1900; not a globally synchronous cold period. Possibly triggered by the Samalas eruption of 1257. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age]
- The first Thames frost fair was in 1608, the last in 1814. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age]
- Glacier advances in the Alps and elsewhere; Cruikshank's *Do Glaciers Listen?* is set in the Mount Saint Elias region at the end of the Little Ice Age, contrasting Tlingit and Athapaskan oral histories, in which glaciers are sentient and respond to human behaviour, with explorers' scientific measurement. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Do_Glaciers_Listen%3F; https://journalhosting.ucalgary.ca/index.php/arctic/article/download/63312/47249/181126]

## The cold rush: whaling, sealing and fur
- Basque whalers were in Newfoundland/Labrador (Red Bay) by the second quarter of the 16th century; the English Muscovy Company opened Spitsbergen whaling in 1611–13, and English, Dutch, Danish and Basque fleets fought over the grounds until 1638. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_whaling]
- Bering Strait bowhead boom: in 1848 Thomas Welcome Roys returned with a big catch; over 500 whales caught the next year, over 2,000 in 1850, over 220 ships in 1852, collapse by 1853. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_whaling] (Demuth's *Floating Coast* is the key history of this — see reading list.)
- The first Antarctic shore whaling stations opened on South Georgia in 1904 (195 whales that year); stern-slipway factory ships followed; by 1930/31 41 factory ships took over 37,000 whales. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whaling; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_whaling]
- 15 whaling nations signed the International Convention for the Regulation of Whaling (Washington, 1946), creating the IWC; the IWC commercial moratorium took effect in 1986. Japan resumed commercial whaling in its own waters from July 2019 and left Antarctic whaling. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_whaling; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whaling]
- Rocha, Clapham and Ivashchenko (*Marine Fisheries Review* 76(4), 2014) estimate at least 2.9 million large whales were killed in the 20th century (a lower bound), including about 874,000 fin, about 761,000 sperm and about 379,000 blue whales; Soviet catches were corrected for illegal under-reporting. [source: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/first-estimate-number-whales-killed-during-industrial-whaling-1900-1999; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/world-s-whaling-slaughter-tallied-at-3-million/]
- Alaska Natives continue subsistence whaling (bowhead, beluga, narwhal) under IWC aboriginal provisions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_whaling]
- Sealing: in 1778 English sealers brought back as many as 40,000 skins from South Georgia and the Magellan Strait area; fur seals were driven to near extermination; after the South Shetlands were discovered (1819), sealing spread into the Antarctic. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_hunting]
- The North Pacific Fur Seal Convention (7 July 1911; US, Britain, Japan, Russia) curtailed pelagic sealing around the Pribilofs. The EU banned import of whitecoat pup pelts in 1983 and seal products in 2009 (Council approval 27 July 2009); Nunavut Inuit opposed the ban. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_hunting]
- Russian fur expansion: Bering and Chirikov reached North America in 1741; Shelekhov founded the first permanent settlement (Kodiak) in 1784; the Russian-American Company monopoly was chartered in 1799; promyshlenniki forced Aleut (Unangan) hunters into the sea-otter trade; the sea otter was brought near extinction. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_colonization_of_North_America]
- The maritime fur trade (c. 1790–1840) linked the Northwest Coast with China (Canton), Hawaii, Britain and the US; British traders from 1785, Americans from 1788. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_fur_trade]
- Alaska Purchase: treaty signed 30 March 1867 for $7.2 million; transfer 18 October 1867; of about 60,000 inhabitants, only about 10,500 were under the Russian company (about 8,000 Indigenous). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Purchase]

## Refrigeration and the cold chain
- Ice houses go back at least to Akkadian tablets (c. 1750 BCE) on the Euphrates; Persians used the *yakhchal* pit; in 17th-century India ice was brought from the Himalayas. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_trade; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refrigeration]
- Frederic Tudor began the natural ice trade in 1806 with a cargo to Martinique; his ship *Tuscany* reached Calcutta in 1833; regular exports to Calcutta, Madras and Bombay from 1835; he made more than $220,000 profit from Calcutta (1833–50) and drove out Indian and Italian competitors; New England ice reached Hong Kong, the Persian Gulf, Argentina and Peru. At its late-19th-century peak the US ice trade employed about 90,000 people; Norway exported about a million tons a year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_trade]
- Mechanical refrigeration: William Cullen's experiment (1755); Jacob Perkins's first working vapour-compression system (1834); James Harrison's commercial ice machine in Geelong, Australia (1854; patent 1856); Ferdinand Carré's ammonia absorption system (1859); Carl von Linde. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refrigeration]
- The refrigerated ship *Dunedin* sailed for London on 15 February 1882, opening New Zealand's frozen-meat trade (cross-refer to the Second Industrial Revolution chapter, which has the details). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refrigeration]
- GE's Monitor Top (1927) was the first refrigerator to run on electricity; Freon (CFCs) from 1930; US household ownership exceeded 50% by 1940. CFCs damaged the ozone layer; curtailed by the Montreal Protocol (1987). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refrigeration]
- The Antarctic "ozone hole" was reported by British Antarctic Survey scientists Farman, Gardiner and Shanklin in *Nature* in May 1985 (Halley Bay data) — refrigeration's chemistry read in Antarctic air. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_depletion]
- Clarence Birdseye, in Labrador 1912–15, learned from Inuit that fish frozen instantly at −40 °C tasted fresh when thawed; he developed quick-freezing (General Seafood 1924), and in 1929 his business and patents were bought by Goldman Sachs and the Postum Company (later General Foods) for $22 million. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarence_Birdseye]
- Frederick McKinley Jones, a Black American inventor (born 1893 to a Black mother and Irish father), co-founded Thermo King and patented a mobile refrigeration unit for trucks (12 July 1940); he held 61 patents, 40 for refrigeration, and won the National Medal of Technology (posthumously). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_McKinley_Jones; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_chain]
- Vaccine cold chains: ultracold (−70 °C) chains for Ebola and Pfizer–BioNTech COVID-19 vaccines; in 2020 only an estimated 25–30 countries had ultracold infrastructure. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_chain]

## Cold places as prisons: Siberia and the Gulag (brief; cross-refer)
- Dalstroy, founded 13 November 1931, ran gold mining and road building in the Kolyma with prison labour, based at Magadan; tens of thousands died en route or in Kolyma camps 1932–54; the Kolyma Highway is known as the "Road of Bones". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalstroy; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolyma]
- Norillag (Norilsk, from 1935) built the nickel complex; Norillag archives record 16,806 prisoner deaths; the Norilsk uprising of 1953. Wikipedia notes Norilsk prisoner mortality was in most periods lower than across the camp system as a whole. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norilsk; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulag]
- Of 10,000–12,000 Poles sent to Kolyma in 1940–41, only 583 survived to be released in 1942. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulag]
- Norilsk 2020 diesel spill (about 6,000 t into land and 15,000 t into water) after a tank on thawing permafrost collapsed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permafrost]

## Cryosphere geopolitics: Antarctica
- Before the treaty: in 1948 the US proposed a UN trusteeship, rejected by claimants; Argentine troops fired warning shots at Britons at Hope Bay on 1 February 1952; Britain took Argentina and Chile to the International Court of Justice (closed 1956 when they refused jurisdiction); India tried to bring Antarctica to the UN General Assembly in 1956 and 1958. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_Treaty_System]
- The International Geophysical Year (1 July 1957 – 31 December 1958) involved 66 countries; 12 nations active in Antarctica during it (Argentina, Australia, Belgium, Chile, France, Japan, New Zealand, Norway, South Africa, USSR, UK, US) signed the Antarctic Treaty in Washington on 1 December 1959; it entered into force on 23 June 1961. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_Treaty_System]
- The treaty covers everything south of 60°S; bans military activity, nuclear tests and new territorial claims; freezes the seven existing claims (none recognized by the non-claimants). As of 2024: 58 parties, 29 consultative (voting). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_Treaty_System]
- The Madrid Protocol on Environmental Protection was signed 4 October 1991 and entered into force 14 January 1998; Article 7 bans all mineral-resource activity except science. It replaced the failed 1988 mining convention (CRAMRA). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protocol_on_Environmental_Protection_to_the_Antarctic_Treaty]
- 2048: after 50 years any consultative party may request a review conference; the mining ban does NOT lapse automatically in 2048 — amending Article 7 requires further conditions (a common misreading). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protocol_on_Environmental_Protection_to_the_Antarctic_Treaty]
- Antarctica's population is about 5,000 in summer and 1,000 in winter, all at research stations. The US Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station dates from 1956. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctica; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Pole]
- The IWC's Southern Ocean Whale Sanctuary was established in 1994. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctica]

## Cryosphere geopolitics: the Arctic
- Svalbard Treaty signed 9 February 1920, in force 14 August 1925: Norwegian sovereignty, but signatories have equal commercial (mainly coal-mining) rights; military use restricted, not fully demilitarized. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svalbard_Treaty]
- Arctic Council created by the Ottawa Declaration, 19 September 1996 (preceded by the 1991 Arctic Environmental Protection Strategy); 8 member states; six Indigenous Permanent Participants (Aleut International Association, Gwich'in Council International, Inuit Circumpolar Council (about 180,000 Inuit), RAIPON, Saami Council and the Arctic Athabaskan Council — the last name not checked in the extract, see NOT VERIFIED); a footnote excludes military security. China and India became observers in 2013. On 3 March 2022 the seven other members paused participation under Russia's chairmanship after the invasion of Ukraine; Norway chaired 2023–25. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_Council]
- Northern Sea Route: first navigated by Nordenskiöld's *Vega* (1878–79, one wintering); first in a single season by the *Sibiryakov* (Otto Schmidt, 1932); Rosatom has run it since 2018 (directly since 2022). Murmansk–Yokohama is 12,840 nautical miles via Suez vs 5,770 via the NSR. Cargo: 3.87 Mt (2012), 34.0 Mt (2022), 36.25 Mt (2023), 37.9 Mt (2024), with record transit of over 3 Mt in 2024 (most NSR cargo is Russian Arctic export, not transit). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Sea_Route]
- Northwest Passage: Canada claims it as internal waters; the US calls it an international strait. First commercial transit: tanker *Manhattan*, August 1969; the RCMP's *St. Roch* (Henry Larsen) crossed 1940–42; the cruise ship *Crystal Serenity* (1,500 passengers) sailed it in 2016. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Passage]
- Seabed claims: on 2 August 2007 Russian submersibles planted a titanium flag on the seabed at the North Pole (4,261 m); Russia, Denmark (from 2014) and Canada claim the Lomonosov Ridge as continental shelf. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lomonosov_Ridge]
- China's January 2018 Arctic white paper declared it a "near-Arctic state" and promoted a "Polar Silk Road". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_policy_of_China]
- Greenland: colony until the 1953 Danish constitution; home rule from 1 May 1979 (referendum 17 January 1979); left the EEC in 1985; self-government from 21 June 2009, with Greenlandic (Kalaallisut) the sole official language. Population about 56,600 (2022). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland]
- US interest: Seward discussed buying Greenland (1867); the US secretly offered to buy it in 1946; the 1951 defence agreement allows US bases. In 2019 Trump proposed buying it; Frederiksen called it "an absurd discussion". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proposed_United_States_acquisition_of_Greenland; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland_crisis]
- 2025–26 "Greenland crisis" (FAST-MOVING; give dates): Trump sought US control from January 2025; in early 2026 he refused to rule out force and threatened tariffs; on 21 January 2026 he ruled out force and dropped tariffs after talks with NATO's Mark Rutte; a US–Denmark–Greenland security agreement expanding the US military footprint while affirming Danish sovereignty and Greenlandic self-determination was signed on 22 September 2026. Polling cited: 85% of Greenlanders oppose joining the US. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland_crisis; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proposed_United_States_acquisition_of_Greenland]

## The melt: climate change in cold places
- Arctic amplification: long described as twice the global rate; Rantanen et al., *Communications Earth & Environment* (11 August 2022), "The Arctic has warmed nearly four times faster than the globe since 1979" (Arctic Circle definition); with the Arctic defined as north of 60°N, the 1971–2019 figure is about three times (3.1 °C vs 1 °C). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_amplification]
- Arctic sea ice: record September minimum 3.42 million km² on 16 September 2012 (Wikipedia; NSIDC figures vary slightly), about 50% below the 1979–2000 average; the 2012 record still stands. NSIDC's preliminary 2025 minimum was 4.60 million km² (10 September 2025, tenth lowest); the 2026 minimum was about 4.60 million km² on 12 September 2026, tied for tenth lowest (the UK Met Office/OSI SAF gives 5.06 million km², 14th lowest). The 20 years 2007–2026 are the 20 lowest minima on record. NSIDC's Walt Meier: "We've plateaued, but we're still low relative to the earlier part of the record." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_in_the_Arctic; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/arctic-sea-ice-shrinks-to-its-2026-minimum/; https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/approach/monitoring/sea-ice/2026/briefing-on-arctic-sea-ice-minimum-and-antarctic-sea-ice-maximum-report---september-2026]
- Arctic sea-ice volume has fallen by about 80% since 1979 (Wikipedia figure; PIOMAS-based). The first ice-free Arctic summers are expected before 2050, possibly late 2020s–early 2030s; IPCC AR6 (2021): sea ice area likely below 1 million km² in some Septembers before 2050. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_in_the_Arctic; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_sea_ice_decline]
- Earth lost 28 trillion tonnes of ice 1994–2017; mountain glaciers 6.1 trillion tonnes of it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_sea_ice_decline; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retreat_of_glaciers_since_1850]
- Permafrost holds 1,400–1,650 billion tonnes of carbon, about twice the carbon in the atmosphere; nearly 70% of Arctic infrastructure on permafrost is at risk by 2050. Yakutsk and Norilsk are the largest cities on continuous permafrost. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permafrost]
- Pathogens: Wikipedia states pandemic risk from thawed microbes is "considered unlikely". But permafrost preserved the 1918 flu: on 20 August 1997 Johan Hultin recovered virus samples from an Iñupiat woman buried at Brevig Mission, Alaska (the village lost about 85% of its people in November 1918), enabling Taubenberger's sequencing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permafrost; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu_research]
- Glaciers since 1850: retreat until about 1940, a pause 1950–80, then rapid retreat. Himalaya: about 15,000 glaciers in the greater Himalaya; around 1.4 billion people depend on the five main Himalayan rivers; studied glaciers retreating about 18–20 m a year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retreat_of_glaciers_since_1850]
- Andes: Peru holds about 70% of tropical glaciers and lost 29% of glacier area 2000–2016; Bolivia's Chacaltaya glacier (once a ski slope) lost 90% of its mass since 1940 and was expected to vanish by 2010–2015. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retreat_of_glaciers_since_1850]
- Glacial lake outburst floods: Lake Palcacocha burst on 13 December 1941, destroying much of Huaraz, Peru (deaths: Wikipedia about 1,800; a review of Carey cites nearly 5,000 — give a range); 15 million people are exposed to GLOF risk (2023 study), mostly in China, India, Nepal, Pakistan and Peru; Kedarnath (India), June 2013. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacial_lake_outburst_flood; https://bioone.org/journals/Mountain-Research-and-Development/volume-31/issue-1/mrd.mm079/In-the-Shadow-of-Melting-GlaciersClimate-Change-and-Andean-Society/10.1659/mrd.mm079.pdf]
- Lliuya v. RWE: Huaraz farmer Saúl Luciano Lliuya sued German utility RWE for a share of Palcacocha flood defences; on 28 May 2025 the Hamm Higher Regional Court dismissed the claim (flood risk to his house about 1% over 30 years) but accepted in principle that a major emitter can be liable for climate harm abroad. [source: https://www.npr.org/2025/05/28/nx-s1-5414369/peruvian-farmer-loses-landmark-climate-case-against-german-energy-giant; https://www.germanwatch.org/en/climate-case-saul-versus-rwe]
- Sheila Watt-Cloutier (Inuk, born Kuujjuaq) filed with 62 Inuit hunters and elders on 7 December 2005 a petition to the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights — called the first international legal action on climate change; the IACHR declined to hear it but held a hearing on 1 March 2007. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheila_Watt-Cloutier]
- What the ice gives up: Ötzi the Iceman (c. 3350–3105 BCE; Wikipedia gives death c. 3230 BCE) was found in melting ice in the Ötztal Alps on 19 September 1991 by German tourists Helmut and Erika Simon. Glacial ("ice patch") archaeology now recovers artefacts emerging from melting ice — Yukon ice patches (arrows, early metallurgy), Norway (Lendbreen textiles, skis). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ötzi; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacial_archaeology]
- Cross-refer: the Inca chapter has the Llullaillaco children (frozen, found 1999 at over 6,700 m).

## Debates
- **Scott versus Amundsen.** Roland Huntford, *Scott and Amundsen* (1979; TV *The Last Place on Earth*, 1985), blamed Scott's leadership and organization; rebuttals by Ranulph Fiennes, Susan Solomon (scientific analysis of the weather that defeated Scott), David Crane (2005 biography) and Karen May (Scott's dog orders disobeyed). Barczewski: Amundsen succeeded through planning, dogs, skis and stamina. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_Nova_Expedition; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amundsen%27s_South_Pole_expedition]
- **Inuit knowledge borrowed and erased.** Amundsen's dogs and furs, Birdseye's quick-freezing, Peary's Inuit sledgers, and the Franklin wrecks found where Inuit testimony said, against Dickens's dismissal of that testimony. The wrecks' discovery is now widely presented as vindicating Inuit oral history; Kamookak said Parks Canada had ignored local stories about *Terror*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Terror_(1813); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin%27s_lost_expedition]
- **What killed Franklin's men** — lead (from solder or water systems) versus scurvy, starvation, cold; isotopic studies question whether lead "spiked" late. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin%27s_lost_expedition]
- **Who reached the North Pole first** — Cook (unproven), Peary (Herbert: probably short, within about 60 miles), Byrd (1926, disputed), *Norge* (first verified, 1926). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roald_Amundsen; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Peary]
- **Why the Greenland Norse vanished.** Jared Diamond (*Collapse*, 2005): overgrazing, soil erosion, conflict with Inuit, cultural conservatism (refusal to adopt Inuit ways). Others (Jette Arneborg and more recent work): planned emigration as ivory lost value (Russian walrus and African elephant ivory), trade ships stopped, cooling, and land freed up in plague-hit Iceland and Norway; the Norse ate much seal, so "refused to adapt" is contested. The 1379 "skrælings raid" may not refer to Greenland at all. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_settlements_in_Greenland]
- **The Little Ice Age as global or regional** — IPCC and PAGES2k find no globally synchronous cold; Parker (*Global Crisis*, 2013) and Behringer (witch-hunts linked to harvest failure) versus Degroot (adaptation and thriving). Cross-refer to Environmental History. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age]
- **Glaciers as actors or objects** — Cruikshank's argument that Indigenous oral histories treat glaciers as sentient beings that respond to human behaviour, versus explorer-science; "local knowledge" as history. [source: https://journalhosting.ucalgary.ca/index.php/arctic/article/download/63312/47249/181126]
- **Ice cores as economic history** — McConnell et al. treat lead as a proxy for Roman economic activity; historians such as Joseph Manning call it "a new kind of history"; caution: lead tracks silver mining, mostly in Iberia, not the whole economy. [source: https://www.sci.news/archaeology/lead-pollution-greenland-ancient-civilizations-06018.html]
- **Antarctica: science preserve or frozen empire.** The 1959 treaty froze but did not end the seven claims; India (1956, 1958) and later non-aligned states challenged a club of IGY powers; Howkins's work treats claims as imperial. The 2048 review is widely misread as an expiry date. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_Treaty_System; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protocol_on_Environmental_Protection_to_the_Antarctic_Treaty]
- **Arctic warming rate** — "twice" (older), "three times" (60°N, 1971–2019), "nearly four times" (Arctic Circle, 1979–2021; Rantanen 2022); the figure depends on the region and baseline. Sea-ice minima have plateaued since 2007 at low levels: a decline that is not linear. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_amplification]
- **Who pays for melting ice** — Lliuya v. RWE (liability in principle, claim dismissed); Watt-Cloutier's "right to be cold" framing of climate as a human-rights issue. [source: https://www.germanwatch.org/en/climate-case-saul-versus-rwe; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheila_Watt-Cloutier]
- **Gulag in the cold** — cold was part of the punishment, but Norilsk's mortality was in most periods lower than the camp average: the cold places were not always the deadliest. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulag]
