## 1. Background: the wartime conferences
- **Tehran Conference** (code name *Eureka*): 28 November – 1 December 1943, at the Soviet embassy in Tehran. It was the first meeting of the "Big Three". It came just after the Cairo Conference (22–26 November 1943) of the US, UK and China. Iran was neutral but had been invaded jointly by Britain and the USSR in August 1941. [source: TEH]
- Roosevelt and Stalin met for the first time at Tehran. Roosevelt had travelled about 7,000 miles, and his health was "already deteriorating". [source: TEH]
- Main outcome of Tehran: the Anglo-American commitment to a second front. Operation Overlord, the cross-Channel invasion, was to be launched in May 1944 alongside a Soviet offensive (Bagration). Stalin agreed to enter the war against Japan once Germany was defeated. [source: TEH]
- Poland at Tehran: Stalin pressed for an eastern Polish border on a variant of the Curzon Line, and Churchill proposed moving Poland westward with compensation from Germany, which Stalin accepted. Roosevelt asked to be excused from the Polish discussion, citing Polish-American voters before the 1944 election. These decisions were not formally ratified until Yalta and Potsdam. The Polish government-in-exile was not involved: the USSR had broken off recognition of it before the conference. [source: TEH]
- At Tehran, Roosevelt "jokingly" told Stalin that when Soviet forces reoccupied the Baltic countries "he did not intend to go to war with the Soviet Union on this point", but that "the question of referendum and the right of self-determination" mattered to Americans. [source: WEL]
- Tehran also included early talk about a postwar international organisation. Stalin's dinner remark about executing 50,000–100,000 German officers made Churchill storm out; Stalin then said he had been joking. [source: TEH]
- **Dumbarton Oaks** (Washington, August–October 1944) drafted the UN proposals (tentative text agreed 7 October 1944). Per Charles Bohlen, it "settled all but two issues": Security Council voting, and the Soviet demand that all 16 Soviet republics be admitted. [source: DO; VETO]
- **Fourth Moscow Conference** ("Tolstoy"), Churchill and Stalin, 9–19 October 1944. The US ambassador Averell Harriman was an observer but was not at the percentages exchange. Delegations of both the London Polish government-in-exile and the Lublin Committee were also in Moscow. [source: MC44; PCT]
- **Percentages agreement**, the opening proposal as Churchill recorded it in his memoir (MC44's rendering of the "naughty document"):
  - Romania: Russia 90%, "The Others" 10%.
  - Greece: Great Britain "(in accord with US)" 90%, Russia 10%.
  - Yugoslavia: 50–50.
  - Hungary: 50–50.
  - Bulgaria: Russia 75%, "The Others" 25%.

  Poland was discussed only briefly beforehand. Churchill pushed the paper across; Stalin made a large tick in blue pencil and handed it back. [source: MC44; PCT]
- Amended figures after Eden–Molotov haggling (10–11 October): Soviet share in Bulgaria 80% and in Hungary 80%; Romania listed as 100% Soviet in PCT's table. Molotov offered Eden 20% influence in Bulgaria on 11 October. [source: PCT]
- Churchill called it a "naughty document". He published the episode in 1953 in *Triumph and Tragedy*, the last volume of *The Second World War*. [source: PCT]
- **Status of the agreement (important caveat):** it was secret and informal, never signed and never mentioned at Yalta. MC44 says the sole source is Churchill's memoirs and that its authenticity was questioned as early as 1958. However, PCT (drawing on Albert Resis, *American Historical Review*, April 1978) also uses the British transcript of the talks. Soviet historians first acknowledged Churchill's account in 1958, only to deny it: Igor Zemskov called it a "dirty, crude" lie. Churchill himself called the percentages "a method by which in our thoughts we can see how near we are together" and told the War Cabinet on 12 October it was "only an interim guide for the immediate wartime future". [source: MC44; PCT]
- Roosevelt had agreed in June 1944, "rather weakly" (David Carlton), to a three-month trial of an Anglo-Soviet Balkan arrangement. Harriman never mentioned percentages to him, and for months Roosevelt did not know the full contents. [source: PCT]
- At Moscow, Churchill also secured Stalin's promise that the Red Army would not enter Greece. Britain agreed to return all former Soviet citizens liberated from the Germans. [source: PCT; MC44]
- Outcome: every country on the list fell under Communist rule except Greece, where the Communists lost the civil war. Yugoslavia left the Soviet bloc after the 1948 Tito–Stalin split. [source: PCT]
- In a 1956 interview with C. L. Sulzberger, Churchill said: "Stalin never broke his word to me. We agreed on the Balkans. I said he could have Romania and Bulgaria, and he said we could have Greece…" [source: PCT]

## 2. The setting and the participants
- Dates: 4–11 February 1945. Also called the Crimean Conference (code name *Argonaut* in FRUS titles; not verified separately). It was held near Yalta in Crimea. Three palaces were used:
  - Livadia: the conference sessions, plus Roosevelt and the US delegation.
  - Yusupov: the Soviet delegation.
  - Vorontsov (Alupka), about 8 km away: the British.

  [source: YC; LIV]
- Livadia Palace was the summer palace of Nicholas II, designed by Nikolay Krasnov and inaugurated 11 September 1911. [source: LIV]
- Roosevelt had wanted a Mediterranean venue (Malta, Cyprus, Sicily, Athens and Jerusalem were all suggested). Stalin said his doctors opposed long trips and proposed Yalta; his fear of flying also played a part. [source: YC]
- Military situation: the Western Allies had liberated France and Belgium and were on Germany's western border. Soviet forces were about 65 km from Berlin and had driven the Germans from Poland, Romania and Bulgaria. [source: YC]
- De Gaulle was not invited to Yalta or Potsdam, a slight he long resented. The Soviets had also objected to his inclusion. [source: YC; POT]
- The aims as the YC article summarises them:
  - Roosevelt wanted Soviet entry into the Pacific War and Soviet participation in the UN.
  - Churchill pressed for free elections, especially in Poland.
  - Stalin wanted a Soviet sphere in Eastern and Central Europe.

  [source: YC]
- James F. Byrnes, a member of the US delegation: "it was not a question of what we would let the Russians do, but what we could get the Russians to do". [source: YC]
- FDR's health: tests at Bethesda in March 1944 found hypertension, atherosclerosis, coronary artery disease with angina, and congestive heart failure. His physician Ross McIntire denied in the 1944 campaign that his health was poor. On his return from Yalta, observers were "shocked by how old and frail he looked". He addressed Congress (1 March 1945) seated, an unprecedented concession to his disability. He died at Warm Springs, Georgia, on 12 April 1945 of a cerebral haemorrhage. [source: FDR]
- The Joint Chiefs had estimated that an American invasion of Japan could cost as many as one million American casualties. SST's version, "1,000,000 American troops and 500,000 British troops", is an estimate cited by Wikipedia; treat it as a planning estimate, not a fact. [source: FDR; SST]
- Alger Hiss attended Yalta as a member of the State Department delegation under Stettinius. He had helped draft the American text of the Declaration on Liberated Europe. [source: HISS] (See NOT VERIFIED on the "chief negotiator" claim.)

## 3. Poland
- Stalin at Yalta: "For the Soviet government, the question of Poland was one of honor" and of security, since Poland had been a corridor for invasions of Russia. He also said "Poland must be strong". [source: YC]
- **Curzon Line:** the line was first proposed in 1919 (Supreme War Council announcement of 8 December 1919) and named after Lord Curzon's note to the Bolsheviks of 11 July 1920. The 1921 Treaty of Riga had instead given Poland almost 135,000 km² east of it, on average about 250 km east of the line. [source: CUR]
- Yalta confirmed the Curzon Line as Poland's eastern border "with digressions from it in some regions of five to eight kilometres in favour of Poland". Poland was to receive "substantial accessions of territory in the North and West". The new Polish government was to be consulted, and "the final delimitation of the western frontier of Poland should thereafter await the Peace Conference". (Wording confirmed from search extracts of the communiqué/protocol; CUR gives "five-to-eight-kilometre variations".) [source: CUR; search extracts of the Yalta communiqué via https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/node/210023 and https://search.library.wisc.edu/digital/A4ZTPYKEFDL44H8U/text/APGTDKFZPZCJUH9E]
- Churchill and Roosevelt asked Stalin to reconsider, particularly over Lwów (Lviv), but he refused. According to CUR, Stalin asked: "Do you want me to tell the Russian people that I am less Russian than Lord Curzon?" (Wording varies between sources; see NOT VERIFIED.) [source: CUR]
- Western border: Yalta was the first time the Soviets openly backed a German–Polish frontier on the Western (Lusatian) Neisse rather than the Eastern Neisse. Churchill objected that "it would be a pity to stuff the Polish goose so full of German food that it got indigestion." Roosevelt said Soviet generosity on the eastern frontier would "make it easier for me at home". [source: ONL]
- **Lublin government:** the Polish Committee of National Liberation (PKWN, the "Lublin Committee") was proclaimed on 22 July 1944 in Chełm and installed in Lublin on 26 July. It was Soviet-sponsored and Communist-dominated, under Edward Osóbka-Morawski. At the end of December 1944 it became the Provisional Government of the Republic of Poland, which the USSR recognised in January 1945. [source: PKWN]
- Yalta's Polish formula: "the Provisional Government which is now functioning in Poland should therefore be reorganized on a broader democratic basis with the inclusion of democratic leaders from Poland and from Poles abroad". That government was to "be pledged to the holding of free and unfettered elections as soon as possible on the basis of universal suffrage and secret ballot". Language calling for supervised elections was dropped. [source: YC]
- Admiral Leahy told Roosevelt the language was so elastic that the Soviets could "stretch it all the way from Yalta to Washington without ever technically breaking it". [source: YC]
- **Declaration on Liberated Europe:** the signatories promised to help liberated peoples "to create democratic institutions of their own choice", invoking the Atlantic Charter: "the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live". They also promised to help form interim governments "pledged to the earliest possible establishment through free elections of the Governments responsive to the will of the people". The only mechanism was to "consult together". Molotov inserted language weakening enforcement. [source: YC]
- The Polish government-in-exile (Prime Minister Tomasz Arciszewski) declared on 13 February 1945 that the Yalta decisions on Poland could not be recognised and were not binding on the Polish nation, and that the Curzon border would be seen as a new partition, this time by Poland's allies. General Anders' II Corps refused to recognise the "one-sided Crimea decision". (Search-extract level; paraphrase rather than quote.) [source: https://eng.ipn.gov.pl/en/news/9244,77-Years-From-the-Yalta-Conference.html; https://time.com/archive/6599601/poland-order-of-the-day]
- More than 200,000 troops of the Polish Armed Forces in the West were then serving under Allied command. Many came from the Kresy, now assigned to the USSR, and many had been released from the Gulag to form the Anders Army. [source: WB]
- Britain: a three-day Commons debate began on 27 February 1945 and ended in a vote of confidence. Twenty-five MPs (WB names Alec Douglas-Home among them) drafted an amendment protesting the agreement. Henry Strauss, MP for Norwich, resigned in protest. [source: YC; WB]
- Churchill's remark: "Poor Neville Chamberlain believed he could trust Hitler. He was wrong. But I don't think I am wrong about Stalin." [source: YC]
- **After Yalta:**
  - Roosevelt, 1 March 1945: "I come from the Crimea with a firm belief that we have made a start on the road to a world of peace".
  - Roosevelt to Churchill, 11 March: neither the US government nor the people "will support participation in a fraud or a mere whitewash of the Lublin government".
  - Harriman cabled on 21 March that the Soviet programme was "the establishment of totalitarianism".
  - On 23 March Roosevelt began to concede that his view of Stalin had been too optimistic: "Averell is right."

  [source: YC]
- **Trial of the Sixteen:** NKVD general Ivan Serov invited the leaders of the Polish Underground State (Home Army commander Leopold Okulicki, Government Delegate Jan Stanisław Jankowski and others) to talks on joining the government. Despite a guarantee of safety, they were arrested at Pruszków on 27–28 March 1945 and taken to the Lubyanka. They were tried in Moscow on 18–21 June 1945, deliberately at the same time as the Moscow talks on the new government. Twelve were sentenced to four months to ten years; charges against four were dropped. Okulicki died in Butyrka prison on 24 December 1946. [source: T16; TRJN]
- **Provisional Government of National Unity (TRJN):** negotiated in Moscow on 17–21 June 1945 and formed on 28 June. Osóbka-Morawski was prime minister, with Gomułka (PPR) and Stanisław Mikołajczyk (PSL, former exile prime minister) as deputies. The US recognised it on 5 July 1945, and the US and UK withdrew recognition of the exile government on 6 July (PGE gives 5 July). The Vatican was the last state to withdraw recognition of the exile government, on 19 October 1972. [source: TRJN; PGE]
- Poland was absent from the San Francisco conference for want of an agreed government. A space was left for its signature, and it signed the UN Charter on 15 October 1945 as a founding member. [source: UNCIO]
- Elections of 16 January 1947 led to a Communist state by 1949 (YC wording). WB: Molotov delivered an election fair by "Soviet standards". As many as half a million Polish soldiers refused to return. The Polish Resettlement Act 1947 was Britain's first mass immigration law. [source: YC; WB]
- Human cost of the border shift: about 3 million Roman Catholic Poles lived east of the Curzon Line in interwar Poland, and about 2.1–2.2 million of them died, fled, emigrated or were expelled to the former German territories (per CUR, citing "more recent research"). [source: CUR]

## 4. Germany
- The Big Three ratified the European Advisory Commission's zone boundaries for three zones. France would get a fourth zone carved out of the US and UK zones. Churchill argued for French membership of the Allied Control Council, and Stalin gave way when Roosevelt backed Churchill. Berlin was likewise to be divided. [source: YC; POT]
- Agreed aims for Germany: disarm and disband the armed forces, break up the General Staff, eliminate or control war industry, punish war criminals, exact reparations, wipe out the Nazi party ("denazification"). A "Committee on Dismemberment of Germany" was set up to study partition. [source: AOG; YC]
- **Reparations:** the protocol set up an Allied Reparation Commission in Moscow. It was to "take in its initial studies as a basis for discussion the suggestion of the Soviet Government" of a total of 20 billion dollars, 50% of it to the USSR. The British delegation held that no figures should be mentioned before the commission had studied the question. Reparations could include the use of German labour. (Search extract of the protocol; one reproduction gives 22 billion, which is probably a transcription error.) [source: https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/occupation-and-the-emergence-of-two-states-1945-1961/excerpts-from-the-protocol-of-the-proceedings-of-the-yalta-crimea-conference-february-11-1945; YC]
- Later dispute: at Potsdam, Byrnes said neither Roosevelt nor anyone else had agreed to $20 billion. Molotov said the US had accepted it as a basis for discussion. [source: search extract of FRUS 1945 Berlin vol. via the same query; treat as "the US later denied it had agreed a figure"]
- Yalta also agreed that Nazi war criminals were to be tried; and, in a reciprocal protocol, the repatriation of liberated nationals (§6 below). [source: YC]

## 5. The United Nations
- **"Yalta formula" for Security Council voting:** each permanent member could veto any action by the Council but not procedural decisions, so a permanent member could not block debate. At Dumbarton Oaks, the Soviets had wanted an absolute veto even on discussion. [source: VETO]
- In today's Charter (Art. 27) procedural decisions need nine affirmative votes (seven before 1966), and other decisions need nine including "the concurring votes of the permanent members". Parties to a dispute abstain under Chapter VI. [source: VETO]
- Stalin asked for UN membership for all 16 Soviet republics. Roosevelt and Churchill agreed to two, Ukraine and Byelorussia. The US did not ultimately claim extra votes for itself. [source: YC; UNCIO]
- At San Francisco the Big Five made clear "it was either the Charter with the veto or no Charter at all" (Francis O. Wilcox, adviser to the US delegation). [source: VETO]
- The San Francisco Conference (UNCIO) ran from 25 April to 26 June 1945, with delegates of 50 Allied nations and about 3,500 attendees in all. [source: UNCIO]
- Roosevelt wanted to attend the San Francisco conference; he went to Warm Springs on 29 March to rest beforehand and died there. [source: FDR]

## 6. The Far East: the secret Agreement Regarding Japan (11 February 1945)
- Exact opening (from KUR's quotation): "The leaders of the three great powers – the Soviet Union, the United States of America and Great Britain – have agreed that in two or three months after Germany has surrendered and the war in Europe is terminated, the Soviet Union shall enter into war against Japan on the side of the Allies on condition that:" [source: KUR]
- Terms (KUR quotation and SST paraphrase):
  1. The status quo in Outer Mongolia (the Mongolian People's Republic) to be preserved (paraphrase; SST says "maintained").
  2. "The former rights of Russia violated by the treacherous attack of Japan in 1904 shall be restored, viz.:"
     - (a) "The southern part of Sakhalin as well as the islands adjacent to it shall be returned to the Soviet Union."
     - (b) The commercial port of Dairen (Dalian) internationalised, with the "pre-eminent interests" of the USSR safeguarded, and the lease of Port Arthur as a Soviet naval base restored.
     - (c) The Chinese Eastern and South Manchurian railways to be run by a joint Soviet–Chinese company, with Soviet "pre-eminent interests" safeguarded and China retaining "full sovereignty in Manchuria".
  3. "The Kuril islands shall be handed over to the Soviet Union."

  The phrases "pre-eminent interests" and "full sovereignty in Manchuria" were confirmed via search extracts; (b) and (c) are otherwise paraphrased. [source: KUR; SST; https://ndl.go.jp/constitution/e/etc/c04.html (search extract)]
- **Without China's consent:** the clauses on Outer Mongolia, the ports and the railways "will require concurrence of Generalissimo Chiang Kai-shek". The President was to "take measures in order to obtain this concurrence on advice from Marshal Stalin". The three heads of government agreed that the Soviet claims "shall be unquestionably fulfilled after Japan has been defeated". (Phrases confirmed via search extracts of the text: NDL Japan and FRUS.) [source: https://ndl.go.jp/constitution/e/etc/c04.html; https://worldjpn.net/documents/texts/docs/19450211.T1E.html (search extracts); SST]
- The agreement was secret. It was made public on 11/12 February 1946 (a New Zealand parliamentary paper gives 12 February 1946). [source: search extract, https://slbrartest.natlib.govt.nz/parliamentary/appendix-to-the-journals-of-the-house-of-representatives/1947/I/1158]
- Roosevelt gave the Chinese ambassador Wei Tao-ming the outline of the China provisions on 12 March 1945. **Conflict:** SST says ambassador Patrick Hurley conveyed the full contents to Chiang and Wang Shijie on 29 April 1945; FRUS (search extract) says Hurley delivered the terms to Chiang in a message from Truman on 15 June 1945. Safe wording: "China was told only in stages, and Chiang received the full terms only in mid-1945". [source: SST; search extract of FRUS 1945 vol. VII]
- The **Sino-Soviet Treaty of Friendship and Alliance** was signed in Moscow on 14 August 1945 by Wang Shijie and Molotov, as Japan surrendered, and after the Soviet invasion of Manchuria had begun on 9 August. Its terms:
  - China agreed to accept Outer Mongolia's independence if a referendum confirmed it. The referendum of 20 October 1945 recorded 97% for independence.
  - There was to be joint control of the Chinese Eastern Railway.
  - The USSR was to stop aiding the Chinese Communists.
  - Soviet troops were to withdraw from Manchuria within three months.

  The Republic of China later called it an unequal treaty. Its Legislative Yuan, by then on Taiwan, terminated it on 24 February 1953 and rescinded recognition of Mongolia. The UN General Assembly's Resolution 505 (1 February 1952) found the USSR had violated it. [source: SST]
- The mainland concessions (Port Arthur, Dalian, the China Changchun Railway) passed to full PRC control in 1955. South Sakhalin and the Kurils remain Russian. [source: SJW45]
- The USSR denounced the Soviet–Japanese Neutrality Pact on 5 April 1945. Germany surrendered on 9 May (Moscow time), implying a Soviet deadline of 9 August. The USSR declared war on 8 August. [source: SJW45; KUR]
- **Kurils dispute:** Japan holds that the Yalta Agreement "did not determine the final settlement of the territorial problem… Furthermore, Japan is not bound by this document, to which it did not agree." Japan and the US also argue that the southern "Northern Territories" are not part of the Kurils. Truman wrote to Stalin on 27 August 1945 that the Kurils were "Japanese territory, disposition of which must be made at a peace settlement" and that his predecessor had agreed to "support in the peace settlement the Soviet acquisition of those islands". Russia rejects this reading. [source: KUR]
- **Korea:** Korea is absent from the formal record. Declassified records show that on 8 February, without Churchill, Roosevelt proposed a trusteeship of 20–30 years (Soviet, American, Chinese). Stalin said it should be as short as possible and remarked that the British, if excluded, "would most certainly be offended" (the Prime Minister might "kill us"). They agreed that no foreign troops should be stationed in Korea. [source: YC]
- What followed in Korea: on 10 August 1945 Dean Rusk and Charles Bonesteel picked the 38th parallel from a *National Geographic* map, and no Korea experts were consulted. The December 1945 Moscow Conference set a four-power trusteeship of up to five years. This "invigorated" the Korean anti-trusteeship movement demanding immediate independence; the Korean Communist Party, aligned with Moscow, supported trusteeship. [source: KOR]

## 7. The repatriation agreements
- On 11 February 1945 separate Soviet–American and Soviet–British agreements were signed for the mutual return of liberated POWs and civilians. France signed a matching agreement on 26 June 1945. The Western motive was the rapid return of their own servicemen, whom the Red Army was liberating from German camps. Return was mandatory regardless of consent. [source: REP; YC; KEEL]
- Scope: Soviet citizens within the borders of 1 September 1939. Citizens of the Baltic states, western Belarus and western Ukraine (annexed 1939–40) were therefore exempt. Soviet attempts at Potsdam to extend it failed. White Russian émigrés of the Civil War era were not covered either. [source: REP; COS]
- Scale: about 4.2 million Soviet citizens were repatriated (Ostarbeiter, POWs, collaborators, refugees). About 80% of those from pre-1939 borders returned voluntarily. About 500,000 stayed in the West; Viktor Zemskov estimates the figure would have been about 1 million had repatriation been wholly voluntary. [source: REP]
- Fate on return: according to declassified Soviet data cited by REP, 6.5% of returnees were arrested during filtration (14% of POWs, 2% of civilians), and 14.5% were sent to labour ("construction") battalions. REP says this disproves claims of universal persecution. The writer should present these as Soviet archival figures. [source: REP]
- Cossacks, Lienz, May–June 1945: over 45,000 Cossack combatants and dependants surrendered to the British V Corps. On 28 May about 1,800 officers were tricked into attending a "conference" and handed over. About 32,000 were repatriated from the Lienz–Peggetz camps, by force where necessary. British records list nine deaths, while the Cossack cemetery at Lienz has 28 graves. Roughly 3,000 non-Soviet "old émigrés" were deported in breach of the Yalta terms. Pyotr Krasnov and Andrei Shkuro were executed in Moscow in January 1947. [source: COS]
- There were also forced handovers, with suicides and self-harm, at Fort Dix (USA), Kempten, and Plattling (February 1946). **Operation Keelhaul** (14 August 1946 – 9 May 1947), in Italy, sent about 1,000 military collaborators back. It was the last forced transfer. [source: REP; KEEL]
- Historiography: Nicholas Bethell (1974) and later critics charged Allied duplicity; Julius Epstein called it a war crime under the Geneva Convention. The current Wikipedia articles stress that most forcibly returned were collaborators. The writer should present both, and note that the Wikipedia framing is recent and contested. [source: YC; KEEL; COS]

## 8. Those not at the table
- **Poles:** see §3. To many Polish Americans, Yalta "constituted a betrayal" of Poland and the Atlantic Charter. Strobe Talbott: "many countries in the (center and) east suffered half a century under the shadow of Yalta." Polish communists used Yalta to fuel anti-Western feeling ("the West sacrificed us"). Some authors note that the new western lands helped the communists win over nationalists. [source: WB]
- **Chinese:** China was an ally (Cairo, 1943) yet was not consulted. Its sovereign rights in Manchuria and its claim to Outer Mongolia were bargained away, subject to a "concurrence" Roosevelt undertook to obtain. See §6. Rana Mitter argues that China's war is neglected in Western memory (MIT; see reading list). [source: SST; MIT]
- **Baltic states:** Yalta did not name them. The US had refused since the Welles Declaration (23 July 1940) to recognise their 1940 annexation, and that non-recognition lasted five decades. The Yalta repatriation agreements exempted Balts. Soviet deportations after reoccupation exceeded those of 1940–41: about 90,000 Baltic nationals in March 1949 alone (Operation Priboi). The Forest Brothers fought on for years. Post-Soviet Russian officials claim Yalta, Potsdam and Helsinki (1975) gave *de jure* recognition of the incorporation, which Western analysts reject. [source: WEL; BALT; REP]
- **Koreans:** no Korean voice. FDR's trusteeship idea of 20–30 years treated Korea as unready for self-rule, and Koreans largely rejected trusteeship when it was announced in December 1945. See §6. [source: YC; KOR]
- **Japanese** (later): Japan says it is not bound by an agreement it never signed (the Kurils). [source: KUR]
- **French:** excluded; de Gaulle resented it. France occupied Stuttgart beyond its agreed zone and withdrew only under threat of losing US supplies. [source: YC]
- **Iran and others:** Iran had been occupied since 1941. The Tehran declaration promised economic aid and Iranian independence (TEH). Saudi Arabia: Roosevelt met Ibn Saud after Yalta; YC says Roosevelt backed a Jewish state in Palestine over Ibn Saud's opposition. Use with care: YC is the only source read. [source: TEH; YC]

## 9. Potsdam (brief)
- 17 July – 2 August 1945, at Potsdam in the Soviet zone. Truman (Roosevelt had died 12 April) met Stalin; it was the only time they met. The British general election results were announced on 26 July, and Attlee replaced Churchill (Bevin replaced Eden). France was not invited. [source: POT]
- Outcomes:
  - The four zones were confirmed (and four zones in Austria; Berlin and Vienna divided).
  - Germany's eastern border was moved provisionally to the Oder–Neisse line, about 25% of 1937 territory.
  - The Polish TRJN was recognised.
  - Reparations: each power was to take them from its own zone, and the USSR was to get 15% of usable western-zone industrial equipment in exchange for goods, plus 10% free.
  - Article XIII: the "transfer to Germany of German populations… in Poland, Czechoslovakia and Hungary will have to be undertaken" (in an "orderly and humane" manner, wording not checked).
  - Vietnam was to be partitioned at the 16th parallel for the Japanese surrender.
  - The Soviets reaffirmed their Yalta promise to attack Japan.

  [source: POT; AOG]
- The final German–Polish frontier awaited the 1990 settlement (Treaty on the Final Settlement; German–Polish Border Treaty 1990, ratified 1992). East Germany had accepted the line in 1950 (Zgorzelec); West Germany accepted it with reservations in the 1970 Treaty of Warsaw (in force 1972). [source: ONL; POT]
- Potsdam Declaration on Japan, 26 July: already in the WW2 draft. Do not repeat.

## 10. American politics: "Yalta betrayal"
- Yalta came to be seen badly in US opinion, especially among Republicans, conservative Southern and Western Democrats, and Americans with East European roots. [source: YC]
- 1952 Republican platform: a Republican government "will repudiate all commitments contained in secret understandings such as those of Yalta which aid Communist enslavements." [source: https://presidency.ucsb.edu/node/273395 (search extract)]
- After Eisenhower's election, Senate Majority Leader Robert A. Taft and Foreign Relations Republicans tried to have Yalta repudiated. Eisenhower's resolution did not flatly repudiate Yalta, which annoyed Senator Alexander Wiley among others, and the effort "fizzled out after Stalin's death". [source: YC; search extract re Wiley]
- The Hiss case (Whittaker Chambers' testimony, 3 August 1948; Chambers had written a satirical *Time* piece against Yalta in March 1945) fed claims that Yalta was shaped by a Soviet agent. Venona cable #1822 (30 March 1945) mentions an agent "ALES" who attended Yalta and then went to Moscow. Hiss did both, but the identification is disputed (e.g., Lowenthal argues ALES was not at Yalta). [source: HISS]
- George W. Bush, Riga, 7 May 2005: "The agreement at Yalta followed in the unjust tradition of Munich and the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact." John Lewis Gaddis told the *New York Times* the equation was "a bit much". [source: https://georgewbush-whitehouse.archives.gov/news/releases/2005/05/20050507-8.html (search extract); https://www.irishtimes.com/news/bush-s-criticism-of-yalta-reignites-roosevelt-debate-1.445020 (search extract)]

## Debates
- **"Betrayal" vs realism (the central argument):**
  - Critics: Polish émigrés, the 1952 Republicans, Bush in 2005, and "Western betrayal" writing in Central Europe. They hold that Roosevelt and Churchill sold out Poland and Eastern Europe, against the Atlantic Charter.
  - Defenders: they argue *Realpolitik*. The Red Army already occupied the region, and the West could not fight an unwinnable war with its ally. The FDR article: "supporters argue that there was little more that Roosevelt could have done for the Eastern European countries given the Soviet occupation".
  - Churchill's own planners (Operation Unthinkable, report of 22 May 1945) rated the odds of a war to force "a square deal for Poland" unfavourable.

  [source: WB; FDR; YC]
- **Traditional / orthodox:** Thomas A. Bailey (*America Faces Russia*, 1950) argued that Stalin violated his Yalta promises and imposed Soviet regimes. Herbert Feis also blamed Soviet aggression, while holding that Roosevelt's "surrender" to Stalin's demands at Yalta paved the way. [source: HCW]
- **Revisionist:** following William Appleman Williams (*The Tragedy of American Diplomacy*, 1959), revisionists put more responsibility on the US and its "open door" aims. They saw Soviet control of Eastern Europe as defensive. Gar Alperovitz (*Atomic Diplomacy*, 1965) shifted the story's turning point to Potsdam and the bomb. [source: HCW]
- **Post-revisionist / Gaddis:** in 1972 Gaddis held that "neither side can bear sole responsibility". After the Soviet archives opened, *We Now Know* (1997) put prime responsibility on Stalin (quotation above). Leffler says Gaddis thereby "returns to a more traditional interpretation". On Bush's 2005 Yalta–Munich comparison Gaddis said "a bit much". [source: HCW; Irish Times search extract]
- **Plokhy (2010):** the outcome in Eastern Europe was set mainly by Stalin's implacable aims and by the Red Army's presence. The Western leaders did about as well as could be expected, despite missteps. Reviewer Andrew Nagorski accepts the "little they could have done" argument but says Plokhy goes too far in claiming Yalta preserved "some elements of political pluralism" in Poland. Thomas de Waal (Carnegie) says the Red Army's sacrifice "had handed Stalin all the cards". [source: https://bookpage.com/reviews/6318-s-m-plokhy-new-understanding-critical-summit-history/; https://andrewnagorski.com/articles/book-review-yalta; https://carnegieendowment.org/europe/posts/2020/02/the-yalta-conference-was-more-than-a-victors-feast (all search extracts)]
- **Harbutt (2010):** Yalta was the hinge between a Churchill–Stalin "European" arrangement (the October 1944 "Moscow Order") and the American-led East/West order of the Cold War. Before Yalta, Churchill and Stalin looked to each other, not to the US, as "the prospective postwar political partner in Europe". Reviewers note that little of the book concerns the conference itself. *Finest Hour* doubts the implication that the Cold War was avoidable. [source: https://academic.oup.com/ahr/article/116/4/1096/42296 (search extract); https://winstonchurchill.org/publications/finest-hour/finest-hour-159/books-arts-curiosities-chameleons-and-crossroads/ (search extract)]
- **Did Yalta "divide Europe", or recognise where the armies stood?**
  - Recognition: by February 1945 the Red Army held Poland, Romania and Bulgaria and was 65 km from Berlin (YC). Yalta's texts promised free elections, not spheres. Stalin's breach of the texts came afterwards, so the division followed the armies, not the agreement.
  - Division: Naimark, in the *Cambridge History of the Cold War*, writes that Yalta, Potsdam and "the notorious percentages agreement… confirmed that Eastern Europe, initially at least, would lie within the sphere of influence of the Soviet Union". The real spheres deal was the October 1944 Moscow meeting, not Yalta. At Yalta, Roosevelt wanted percentages-type issues settled by the UN, and Leffler says Churchill later "sought to renege" on them.
  - Stalin's reported "Never mind. We'll do it our own way later" points to the texts as cover for a decision made by force.

  [source: YC; PCT]
- **Percentages agreement significance:**
  - Kolko and Geoffrey Roberts: overrated. Stalin struck out sphere language from Churchill's draft the next day.
  - Andrew Roberts: it "saved Greek democracy".
  - Richard Crampton: "infamous", and done without consulting the peoples concerned.
  - Sergey Radchenko: Stalin valued the recognition because it lent legitimacy to his gains.

  [source: PCT]
- **Repatriation:** Bethell, Tolstoy and Epstein charged betrayal and even a war crime. More recent accounts, based on Soviet archives (Zemskov; Polian), stress that most of those forced back were collaborators and that repression was not universal. [source: YC; REP; KEEL]
- **The Far East bargain:** Roosevelt believed Soviet entry would save many American lives (casualty estimates for invading Japan). Critics in China (ROC) and the US saw an unnecessary sacrifice of Chinese sovereignty. Hasegawa argues Soviet entry, more than the bomb, forced Japan's surrender, which partly vindicates Roosevelt's priority. [source: FDR; SST; HAS]
- **Russia today:** officials cite Yalta and Potsdam as legitimising the 1940 Baltic annexation. Western analysts (CHACR) call this a "nefarious" myth. Plokhy, a Ukrainian historian, wrote partly against Soviet/Russian mythologising of Yalta. The 2015 "Big Three" monument at Livadia, in Russian-annexed Crimea, is an example of that use. [source: BALT; YC gallery caption]

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use
- Roosevelt's 1 March 1945 line that Yalta "spells the end of the system of unilateral action, the exclusive alliances, the spheres of influence, the balances of power…": a partial version was seen only in an opinion piece. Exact wording not confirmed. Paraphrase only, or omit.
- Full exact text of the Declaration on Liberated Europe beyond the phrases quoted above, and the full exact text of clauses 2(b) and 2(c) of the Japan agreement (Dairen, Port Arthur, railways). Avalon and FRUS could not be opened. Paraphrase.
- "Do you want me to tell the Russian people that I am less Russian than Lord Curzon?" (CUR). Other versions add Clemenceau ("less Russian than Curzon and Clemenceau"). Only CUR's version was seen. Quote as "reportedly", or paraphrase.
- Stalin to Molotov, "Never mind. We'll do it our own way later." (YC, without a visible source; probably from Molotov's later recollections). Attribute as "reportedly", or omit.
- Alger Hiss as "the chief American negotiator at Yalta" (WB): overstated. HISS shows him only as a member of Stettinius's delegation. Do not use "chief negotiator".
- Hiss as the Venona agent "ALES": disputed. Say "accused; the identification remains contested".
- The Yalta article's claim that the Red Army had "three times" the military power of the Western forces: tagged "citation needed". Omit.
- "All original governments would be restored… except Romania, Bulgaria and Poland" (YC): tagged "citation needed". Omit.
- Roosevelt's support at Yalta for a Jewish state in Palestine (YC only, not cross-checked). Use cautiously or omit.
- Polish exile government's phrase "fifth partition of Poland": not found. Use the IPN paraphrase ("a new partition, this time by Poland's allies").
- Chiang Kai-shek's diary reaction (e.g., feeling "betrayed"): not found. Do not quote.
- Diane Shaver Clemens's thesis (often summarised as "the Soviets made more concessions than the West"): not confirmed. Cite the book without characterising its argument, or omit it from the debate.
- Exact date and manner in which Chiang learned the full Far East terms: 29 April (SST) vs 15 June 1945 (FRUS extract). Say "in stages, fully only by June 1945".
- The 1,000,000 American + 500,000 British casualty estimate (SST): a contested planning figure. Use the FDR article's "as many as one million American casualties", attributed to the Joint Chiefs' estimate.
- Exact Potsdam wording "orderly and humane" on the transfers: not checked. Quote only "the transfer to Germany of German populations… will have to be undertaken".
- Code name "Argonaut" for Yalta: seen only in search-result titles. Omit or verify.
- Bush 2005 follow-on sentences ("the freedom of small nations was somehow expendable…"): not seen. Quote only the verified sentence.
