## 1. From Mughal successor states to Company Raj (c. 1707–1857)
*(Cross-refer to Mughal, Trading Companies and British Empire chapters for Plassey/Buxar/diwani/1770.)*
- Battle of Plassey, 23 June 1757: Robert Clive's victory made Mir Jafar nawab of Bengal; WP calls it the de facto beginning of Company rule. [source: PLAS]
- After Buxar (1764) the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II appointed the Company *diwan* (revenue collector) of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa; the Company was de facto ruler of the lower Gangetic plain by 1773. [source: CRI]
- 1770 famine figures: contemporary Company estimates of about a third of the population (~10 million) dead "have been uncritically reproduced by most modern scholars"; Rajat Datta calls them "inflated" and proposes ~1.2 million (4–5% of the population); Tim Dyson calls the 10 million figure "barely credible". WP infobox range: 1–10 million. Present as a range with this debate. [source: F1770]
- Regulating Act 1773: a compromise under Lord North by which the Company could act as a sovereign power on behalf of the Crown; from 1784 (Pitt's India Act) the British government had the final word on senior appointments. [source: CRI]
- Permanent Settlement, 1793, under Governor-General Cornwallis: fixed Bengal land revenue in perpetuity with zamindars, aiming to create a European-style land market; WP notes many argue it fell short of its goals (fixed revenue meant the Company's income did not grow). [source: PS]
- Anglo-Mysore wars 1767–1799 against Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan; Tipu defeated William Baillie at Pollilur (September 1780); the Fourth War (1798–99) ended with Tipu's death. [source: AMW]
- Anglo-Maratha Wars: First 1775–82, Second 1803–05, Third 1817–19. Anglo-Sikh wars 1845–46 and 1848–49; Punjab annexed 1849. [source: AMAR; ASW; CRI]
- Subsidiary alliances (Wellesley; Hyderabad first to sign, 1798) created the princely states, which in the early 19th century accounted for about two-thirds of the subcontinent; annexations included Delhi (1803), Assam (1828), Sindh (1843), Punjab (1849). [source: CRI]
- Bentinck (1828–35): Bengal Sati Regulation 1829. Dalhousie (1848–56): Second Anglo-Sikh War, annexation of Punjab, railway construction begins (1850), first telegraph (1851), Doctrine of Lapse annexations. [source: CRI]
- Ram Mohan Roy (22 May 1772 – 27 September 1833), co-founder of the Brahmo Sabha (1828), precursor of the Brahmo Samaj; campaigned against sati and child marriage; dubbed "Father of the Indian Renaissance". [source: RMR]
- 1857: the name itself is contested ("Sepoy Mutiny", "Indian Mutiny", "Great Rebellion", "First War of Independence"); in Awadh it took on the character of a patriotic revolt, and taluqdars dispossessed by British revenue policy led much of it (Kunwar Singh in Bihar; Begum Hazrat Mahal, Tatya Tope among leaders); "probably 800,000 Indians were killed, and very likely more". The rebellion led to the Government of India Act 1858, by which the Crown took over; the Company itself was dissolved in 1874. [source: REB; EIC]

## 2. The Raj (1858–1947): state, economy, famine
- British India covered almost all of present-day India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Myanmar, apart from French and Portuguese enclaves; capital Calcutta 1858–1911, then New Delhi. [source: RAJ]
- 562 princely states retained internal autonomy under British paramountcy (WP figure; other sources say 565 — give "over 560"). [source: PII]
- Ilbert Bill (1883), which would have let Indian judges try Europeans, was partly reversed after European protest; WP's Raj article links this to the founding of Congress on 28 December 1885. [source: RAJ]
- Raj reform sequence: Indian Councils Act 1892; Minto–Morley reforms 1909 created separate Muslim electorates; Montagu–Chelmsford report July 1918 → Government of India Act 1919; Rowlatt Committee (1917) → anti-sedition laws. [source: RAJ]
- Government of India Act 1935 (mainly drafted by Samuel Hoare): federal part never took effect because the princes did not accede; provincial provisions took effect with elections in 1937, which showed "overwhelming support for Congress among the Hindu electorate". [source: GOI35; CON]
- Long-run output: Angus Maddison's estimates have India's share of world GDP falling from 24.4% (1700) to 4.2% (1950), while per-capita GDP rose only slightly ($550 → $619, 1990 international dollars) (WP tags this line "citation needed"; consistent with the British Empire chapter's "above 20 per cent to under 5"). GDP grew ~0.55%/yr 1850–1947. India's share of world industrial output: ~25% (1750) → ~2% (1900). [source: ECO]
- Counterweights in the debate: Zareer Masani notes India had ~25% of world population in 1700, so per-capita GDP was low, with "the major drop in India's per capita GDP occurring between 1600 and 1750"; Niall Ferguson stresses British investment (£270 million by the 1880s, £400 million by 1914) and an eightfold increase in irrigated land. William Digby estimated £900 million transferred from India 1870–1900. [source: ECO]
- Handloom yarn output fell from 419 million lb (1850) to 240 million lb (1900). [source: ECO]
- Opium: grown under a government monopoly (Patna and Benares agencies) mainly for China; ~14% of Government of India revenue in 1880, ~7% by 1905; in 1913 India stopped selling opium to China. [source: ECO]
- Tata: denied permission for heavy industry from 1883; the Raj later promised to buy surplus steel (cross-refer to Second Industrial Revolution chapter for TISCO 1907). [source: ECO]
- Famine summary: the last major famine within today's Republic of India was Bengal 1943; within former British India, Bangladesh 1974. Amartya Sen links British-era famines to unequal distribution and the undemocratic nature of rule; Tirthankar Roy attributes them to ecology; Cormac Ó Gráda: famines "less the product of empire per se than the failure of the authorities of the day to act appropriately", noting no famine between the 1900s and 1943, partly due to railways. [source: FAM]
- The 1876–78 El Niño drought was part of a global pattern (India, China, South America, Africa) that killed 19–50 million worldwide. [source: F1876]
- Bengal 1943: Famine Inquiry Commission (Woodhead, 1945) ~1.5 million deaths (Jan 1943–June 1944); K. P. Chattopadhyay (1944) 3.5 million; Greenough (1982) 3.5–3.8 million; Ó Gráda: "scholarly consensus is about 2.1 million". Causes cited include loss of Burmese rice after Rangoon fell (March 1942), the army's "boat denial" policy from 1 May 1942, wartime inflation, and a provincial government that never formally declared famine. [source: F1943] (Cross-refer to British Empire for the Churchill/Mukerjee debate.)

## 3. Social reform, caste and women before 1947
- Jyotirao Phule and Savitribai Phule opened a girls' school in Pune in 1848; Savitribai (3 January 1831 – 10 March 1897) is "widely regarded as the first Indian female teacher"; she worked with Fatima Sheikh. Jyotirao founded the Satyashodhak Samaj on 24 September 1873 for the rights of women, Shudras and Dalits, and wrote *Gulamgiri* (1873). [source: JP; SP]
- Pandita Ramabai (23 April 1858 – 5 April 1922): Sanskrit scholar awarded the titles *Pandita* and *Sarasvati* by Calcutta University faculty; orphaned in the 1876–78 famine; converted to Christianity in Britain (1883); founded the Mukti Mission at Kedgaon. [source: PR]
- Self-Respect Movement founded 1925 in the Tamil country, headed by E. V. Ramasamy (Periyar), against Brahminism and caste. [source: SRM]
- B. R. Ambedkar (14 April 1891 – 6 December 1956), born a Mahar (Dalit) at Mhow; doctorates from Columbia and the LSE; led the Mahad satyagraha (1927) for Dalit access to a public water tank; *Annihilation of Caste* (1936) was an undelivered speech for Lahore's Jat-Pat Todak Mandal, whose organisers found it too controversial; Gandhi replied in *Harijan* (July 1936, "A Vindication of Caste"). [source: BRA; AOC]
- Poona Pact, 24 September 1932, Yerwada jail: Gandhi fasted against separate electorates for the "Depressed Classes" granted by Ramsay MacDonald's Communal Award (August 1932); the pact replaced them with reserved seats in joint electorates — 148 seats in provincial legislatures, nearly double the 71 of the Award. Ambedkar signed reluctantly. [source: PP; BRA]
- Women in the national movement: All India Women's Conference founded 1927; Child Marriage Restraint Act 1929 (minimum age 14 for girls). Sarojini Naidu (1879–1949), poet ("Nightingale of India"), Congress president 1925–26, first governor of the United Provinces after independence. [source: WII; SN]
- Rabindranath Tagore: Nobel Prize in Literature 1913, first non-European laureate; wrote the national anthems of India ("Jana Gana Mana") and Bangladesh ("Amar Shonar Bangla"); renounced his knighthood after Jallianwala Bagh (see British Empire chapter). [source: TAG]

## 4. Nationalism, 1885–1947
- Partition of Bengal announced by Curzon, effective 16 October 1905 (Muslim-majority Eastern Bengal and Assam vs the west); the day of partition coincided with Raksha Bandhan, used in protest; Swadeshi boycott formally launched at Calcutta Town Hall, 7 August 1905; Bengal reunited 1911 and capital moved to New Delhi. [source: POB; SWA]
- All-India Muslim League founded 30 December 1906 at Dacca; a Muslim delegation had met Viceroy Minto in 1906 to ask for separate electorates. Jinnah joined the League in 1913 and was its president from 1934. [source: AIML; RAJ]
- Tilak's slogan: "Swaraj is my birthright, and I shall have it." [source: INM]
- Gandhi (2 October 1869 – 30 January 1948): went to South Africa in 1893, spent 21 years there; first major Indian campaign Champaran (Bihar), 1917, for indigo cultivators; led Congress from 1921; non-cooperation allied with the Khilafat movement, called off after Chauri Chaura (Nehru: ended "suddenly" on 4 February 1922). [source: MKG; NCM]
- Revisionist view of Gandhi in South Africa: Ashwin Desai and Goolam Vahed argue he was at times "a willing part of racial stereotyping and African exploitation" and that his image was later reinvented as saintly. [source: MKG]
- Bhagat Singh (27 September 1907 – 23 March 1931), revolutionary socialist, hanged at Lahore with two comrades, 11 hours ahead of schedule. [source: BHS]
- Quit India: launched 8 August 1942 at the AICC session, Gowalia Tank, Bombay; Gandhi: "Do or Die … We shall either free India or die in the attempt." British estimates: 1,028 killed, over 100,000 arrested; Congress estimates 4,000–10,000 killed. Parallel governments in Satara, Talcher and Tamluk/Midnapore. [source: QIM; INM]
- Subhas Chandra Bose headed the Azad Hind government from 4 July 1943; died 18 August 1945 at Taihoku (Taipei). [source: SCB] (INA detail: Second World War chapter.)
- Jinnah's speech of 11 August 1947 to Pakistan's Constituent Assembly (quoted below) is the classic text for those who argue he envisaged a non-theocratic state. [source: JIN]
- Gandhi was shot by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist, at Birla House on 30 January 1948; the RSS was banned on 4 February 1948 (Godse later claimed he had left it in 1946). [source: MKG; RSS]

## 5. Independent India under Nehru (1947–64)
- Integration of princely states by Vallabhbhai Patel and V. P. Menon using Instruments of Accession (three subjects) and Standstill Agreements; Hyderabad taken by "Operation Polo", 13–18 September 1948 — deaths in the ensuing communal violence estimated from an official 27,000–40,000 to scholarly figures of 200,000 or more. Goa taken from Portugal 18 December 1961 (African leaders urged Nehru to act). Sikkim voted to merge in 1975 (resolution 10 April 1975; 97% in referendum). [source: PII] (Note: WP's integration article mislinks "Menon" to V. K. Krishna Menon; the official involved was V. P. Menon — not separately verified here, so say "Patel and his secretary V. P. Menon" only if the writer confirms, or simply "Patel".)
- Constitution adopted 26 November 1949, in force 26 January 1950 (Republic Day); the longest written national constitution; drafting committee chaired by Ambedkar, building on B. N. Rau's draft and the 1935 Act; the Assembly had 389 members, 299 after partition. [source: CON; BRA]
- Ambedkar resigned from Nehru's cabinet on 27 September 1951 after the Hindu Code Bill was blocked; on 14 October 1956 at Nagpur he converted to Buddhism with about 500,000 followers; d. 6 December 1956. [source: BRA]
- Nehru PM 15 August 1947 – 27 May 1964 (d. in office); five-year plans, public-sector steel plants (Bhilai, Rourkela, Durgapur), IITs, AIIMS. [source: NEH]
- Sino-Indian War, 20 October – 21 November 1962 (Aksai Chin and NEFA); Indian figures 1,383 killed; defence minister Krishna Menon resigned and Nehru sought US aid. [source: SIW; NEH]

## 6. Pakistan's turbulent path
- Language: in 1948 Jinnah declared Urdu (spoken by ~4% of Pakistanis) the national language; the Bengali Language Movement culminated in police firing on students on 21 February 1952 (today International Mother Language Day); Bengali made co-official 1956. [source: BLM; BG]
- First constitution 1956; Ayub Khan's coup, October 1958 (president 27 October 1958 – 25 March 1969); Indus Waters Treaty with India, signed 19 September 1960, brokered by the World Bank. [source: AYUB; IWT]
- 1965 war: began with Operation Gibraltar infiltration into Kashmir (5 August 1965); fighting until 23 September; inconclusive; Tashkent Declaration 10 January 1966. [source: W65]
- Z. A. Bhutto: founded the Pakistan Peoples Party 30 November 1967; leader after 1971; Simla Agreement with Indira Gandhi, 2 July 1972 (93,000 POWs released, LoC); launched the nuclear programme; overthrown by Zia's coup (Operation Fair Play), 5 July 1977; hanged 4 April 1979. [source: ZAB; SIM; ZIA]
- Zia-ul-Haq ruled 1977–17 August 1988 (killed in an air crash); Islamisation including the 1979 Hudood Ordinance; with US and Saudi backing he coordinated the Afghan mujahideen. [source: ZIA]
- Benazir Bhutto: PM 1988–90 and 1993–96, the first woman elected to head a democratic government in a Muslim-majority country; assassinated after a rally in Rawalpindi on 27 December 2007. [source: BB]
- Pervez Musharraf seized power 12 October 1999 after Kargil; allied with the US after 9/11; ruled until 2008. [source: MUSH]
- Imran Khan: first Pakistani PM removed by a no-confidence motion (April 2022); imprisoned since August 2023; sentenced to 14 years (January 2025) and a further 17 years (December 2025); says the cases are politically motivated. [source: IK]

## 7. 1971: the breakup of Pakistan and Bangladesh's birth
- Population: ~75 million Bengalis in East Pakistan vs ~55 million in West Pakistan. Bhola cyclone 12 November 1970 and the slow response fed into the December 1970 election, in which Mujib's Awami League won 167 of East Pakistan's 169 seats, a majority of the 313-seat National Assembly. [source: BG; BLW]
- Operation Searchlight began 25 March 1971; independence proclaimed 26 March 1971 (broadcast by Major Ziaur Rahman on Mujib's behalf). [source: BG; BLW; SMR]
- Death toll — present as a contested range, attributed: Bangladeshi official figure up to 3 million (embedded in national culture); Pakistan's Hamoodur Rahman Commission 26,000 civilians (lowest); Sarmila Bose 50,000–100,000 (her book *Dead Reckoning* strongly criticised); 2008 *BMJ* study (Obermeyer, Murray, Gakidou) 125,000–505,000 civilian deaths; Christian Gerlach (2018) just over half a million, "could not have exceeded 1 million", mostly rural deaths from hunger and exhaustion; WP infobox 300,000–3,000,000. Women raped: 200,000–400,000 (WP infobox). Hindus were especially targeted. [source: BG]
- 8–10 million refugees fled to India. [source: W71]
- Witnesses: Anthony Mascarenhas's *Sunday Times* report "Genocide", 13 June 1971; US consul general Archer Blood's dissent telegram of 28 March 1971 (Nixon/Kissinger backed Yahya Khan, partly because of the China opening). [source: BG; BLOOD]
- Killing of intellectuals by the army and collaborators (notably Jamaat-e-Islami's Al-Badr), especially in the war's last days (14 December). [source: BG]
- War: Pakistani air strikes on Indian airfields 3 December 1971; surrender at Dhaka 16 December 1971 (Niazi); ~93,000 Pakistani prisoners; Indian dead 2,500–3,843, Pakistani ~9,000 (WP infobox). [source: W71]
- Raghavan (*1971*, 2013) argues the breakup was not inevitable but shaped by a global conjuncture and great-power calculations. [source: RAGH]
- Aftermath: Bangladesh famine of 1974 (March–December): government estimate 27,000; one scholar's estimate 1.5 million including post-famine mortality. Mujib killed with most of his family in a coup at dawn on 15 August 1975. [source: BF74; AMUJ]
- 2024: student protests against civil-service job quotas (from July) became a mass uprising; Sheikh Hasina resigned and fled to India on 5 August 2024 after 15 years in power; deaths: "over 1,400" (WP Hasina article, matching UN-reported figure) / "at least 1,500" (WP movement article infobox). Interim government under Muhammad Yunus from 8 August 2024. In November 2025 Hasina was sentenced to death in absentia by the International Crimes Tribunal (OHCHR opposed the death penalty; HRW criticised the in-absentia trial). ## 8. Indira Gandhi, the Emergency, Punjab
- Indira Gandhi PM 24 January 1966 – 1977 and 14 January 1980 – 31 October 1984; split Congress 1969; nationalised the 14 largest banks (1969); "Garibi Hatao" (remove poverty) campaign (1971). [source: IG]
- Green Revolution from 1968 under Indira Gandhi, led by M. S. Swaminathan: high-yielding wheat, fertiliser; concentrated in Punjab, Haryana and western UP. [source: GRI]
- First nuclear test "Smiling Buddha" (Pokhran-I), 18 May 1974, described as a "peaceful nuclear explosion". [source: SMB]
- Emergency: Allahabad High Court (Justice Sinha) on 12 June 1975 voided Indira's 1971 election on Raj Narain's petition; Jayaprakash Narayan had called for "total revolution" (1974); President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed proclaimed the Emergency under Article 352 on the night of 25 June 1975; it lasted until 21 March 1977; WP infobox gives 110,806 arrests (34,988 under MISA); press censorship; Sanjay Gandhi's sterilisation drive — 8.3 million sterilisations in 1976–77, "most of them forced", up from 2.7 million the previous year; elections called 1977, which Congress lost to the Janata Party (Morarji Desai). [source: EMG]
- Operation Blue Star, 1–10 June 1984, to remove Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale and armed militants from the Golden Temple; official figure 493 militant and civilian deaths, army 83 dead (later figures higher; Rajiv Gandhi disclosed 700 soldiers killed; independent estimates of civilians in the complex up to 4,712–5,000+). [source: OBS; ASR]
- Indira Gandhi was shot by her Sikh bodyguards Satwant Singh and Beant Singh on 31 October 1984. Anti-Sikh pogroms followed: Indian government figure 3,350 dead; other estimates 8,000–17,000; first high-profile conviction (Congress leader Sajjan Kumar) only in December 2018. [source: IG; ASR]
- Air India Flight 182 bombed by Sikh militants off Ireland, 23 June 1985: 329 killed, 268 of them Canadian citizens. [source: KHAL]
- Bhopal, night of 2–3 December 1984: methyl isocyanate leak from Union Carbide India's plant; at least 3,787 deaths officially, over 16,000 claimed; 558,125+ injuries. [source: BHO]

## 10. Kashmir and the nuclear rivalry
- After the 1987 state election (results disputed), armed insurgency began in 1989 in the Kashmir Valley, with Pakistani logistical support. [source: KC]
- Kashmiri Pandit exodus from January–March 1990: of ~300,000–600,000 Hindus in the Valley in 1990, only 2,000–3,000 remained in 2016. [source: KC]
- Death tolls (attributed): authorities said 43,000 killed by 2008; J&K government (2011) 43,460 over 21 years; Amit Shah (2019) 35,000 security personnel; a separatist group claims 80,000. [source: KC]

## 11. Sri Lanka
- Independence as the Dominion of Ceylon, 4 February 1948; republic and renamed Sri Lanka 22 May 1972. Population ~74% Sinhalese, ~12% Sri Lankan Tamils, ~10.5% Moors, ~2.8% Indian (Hill Country) Tamils. [source: SL]
- Ceylon Citizenship Act (1948) made citizenship virtually impossible for ~700,000 Indian Tamil plantation workers; disenfranchised 1949. [source: SLCW; HSL]
- Official Language ("Sinhala Only") Act No. 33 of 1956 under S. W. R. D. Bandaranaike; partially offset by the Tamil Language (Special Provisions) Act 1958. [source: SOA]
- Sirimavo Bandaranaike: first woman in the world democratically elected prime minister (1960); PM 1960–65, 1970–77, 1994–2000. [source: SB]
- LTTE founded 5 May 1976 under Velupillai Prabhakaran; suicide unit (Black Tigers); assassinated Rajiv Gandhi in 1991; designated terrorist by 33 countries. [source: LTTE]
- Black July: after an LTTE ambush on 23 July 1983, anti-Tamil pogrom from 24 July in Colombo; deaths estimated at 400–3,000 (government said 300; TCHR 5,638); 150,000 homeless. Civil war dated 23 July 1983 – 18 May 2009. [source: BJ; SLCW]
- Indo-Sri Lanka Accord 29 July 1987 (Rajiv Gandhi–Jayewardene); Indian Peace Keeping Force 1987–90, peak 100,000; 1,165 Indian soldiers killed. [source: SLCW]
- Death toll (attributed): UN, 20 May 2009: 80,000–100,000 over the war; UN Secretary-General's Panel of Experts (2011): "as many as 40,000 civilian deaths" in the final phase; other estimates much higher; Sri Lankan government has refused an independent international investigation. [source: SLCW]
- Aragalaya (2022): economic collapse led to mass protests; President Gotabaya Rajapaksa fled to the Maldives on 13 July 2022 and resigned; Ranil Wickremesinghe elected by parliament; left-wing Anura Kumara Dissanayake won the presidency, sworn in 23 September 2024. [source: ARA; SL]

## Debates
- **Cambridge School vs nationalist vs Subaltern readings of Indian nationalism.** The "Cambridge School" (Anil Seal, *The Emergence of Indian Nationalism: Competition and Collaboration in the Later Nineteenth Century*, 1968; Gordon Johnson, Richard Gordon, David Washbrook; *Locality, Province and Nation*, 1973, ed. Gallagher, Johnson and Seal) treated nationalism as competition among local elites and factions for patronage and office, downplaying ideology. Critics: Howard Spodek (*AHR*, 1979) praised its regional pluralism but faulted its reliance on British documents, downplaying of ideology and "excessive emphasis on Indian self-seeking"; Tapan Raychaudhuri's review "Indian Nationalism as Animal Politics" (*Historical Journal*, 1979) gave the critique its name. Nationalist-Marxist historians led by Bipan Chandra (*India's Struggle for Independence*, 1989) described a mass, multi-class anti-colonial movement. Subaltern Studies (from 1982; Ranajit Guha, after Gramsci) attacked both "elitist" historiographies, colonial and nationalist, to recover peasant insurgency; later critiques: Sumit Sarkar's "Decline of the Subaltern in Subaltern Studies" (turn to Foucauldian discourse), Vivek Chibber (*Postcolonial Theory and the Specter of Capital*) on neglect of class. Related: Partha Chatterjee, *Nationalist Thought and the Colonial World* (1986); Dipesh Chakrabarty, *Provincializing Europe* (2000). [source: CAMB; HBE; SUB; GUHA; BIP; PCH; DCH; https://cambridge.org/core/journals/modern-asian-studies/article/emergence-of-indian-nationalism-competition-and-collaboration-in-the-later-nineteenth-century-by-anil-seal-cambridge-university-press-cambridge1968-pp-xvi-416-sketch-maps-70s/B970F49EC3B5D89028AB8939080FC83A ; https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/journals/historical-journal/article/indian-nationalism-as-animal-politics/7F1779D9043A598DC222E5AB26765A05]
- **Economic impact of colonial rule.** Drain (Naoroji, Dutt, Digby's £900 million 1870–1900; Patnaik's compounded $45 trillion) and deindustrialisation (handloom collapse; Parthasarathi) vs Maddison/Masani's point that per-capita decline began 1600–1750 and Ferguson's investment/irrigation case; Tirthankar Roy's ecological reading of famine vs Sen's entitlement/democracy argument vs Ó Gráda's "failure of the authorities to act". Reuse British Empire chapter for the Patnaik/Roy numbers. [source: ECO; FAM]
- **1770 and 1943 famine tolls** — see sections 1–2 (Datta and Dyson vs the Company's one-third; Woodhead 1.5m vs Greenough 3.5–3.8m vs consensus ~2.1m).
- **Gandhi.** Saint of non-violence vs Desai and Vahed's account of his racial attitudes in South Africa; Ambedkar's charge (1955 BBC interview) that Gandhi wrote against caste in English but in support of it in Gujarati; the Poona Pact as Dalit defeat or compromise. [source: MKG; BRA; PP]
- **Pakistan's origins.** Jalal (*The Sole Spokesman*): Jinnah used the Pakistan demand as a bargaining counter for Muslim parity in a united India; Dhulipala (in Partition chapter): popular "New Medina" vision. [source: JAL]
- **Bangladesh 1971.** Genocide (Bangladeshi narrative, 3 million) vs Sarmila Bose's revisionist low count and emphasis on Bengali violence against Biharis (heavily criticised by Naeem Mohaiemen, Nayanika Mookherjee and others); demographic estimates (BMJ 2008; Gerlach 2018) sit between. Raghavan: the breakup was not inevitable. [source: BG; RAGH]
- **India's growth.** 1991 reforms vs the 1980s "pro-business" turn (Rodrik & Subramanian); how far reforms explain poverty decline. [source: LIB]
- **Hindu nationalism and democracy.** Jaffrelot's "ethnic democracy" thesis (*Modi's India*, 2021) — praised for documentation, criticised methodologically; 2002 Gujarat as "riot" vs "pogrom"; SIT exoneration of Modi (2012). [source: JAFF; GUJ]
