## 1. What digital history is
- Digital history is "the use of digital media to further historical analysis, presentation, and research"; Wikipedia calls it a branch of the digital humanities and an extension of quantitative history, cliometrics and computing. It has two broad sides: digital public history (engaging online audiences) and digital research methods. Outputs include digital archives, online exhibits, data visualisations, interactive maps, timelines, audio and virtual worlds. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_history]
- Recent methods it lists: text mining, corpus linguistics, network analysis, 3D modelling and big-data analysis. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_history]
- Digital humanities grew out of "humanities computing". The change of name is credited to John Unsworth, Susan Schreibman and Ray Siemens, editors of *A Companion to Digital Humanities* (2004), who wanted to stop the field being seen as "mere digitization". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_humanities]

## 2. Roots: humanities computing (Busa) and quantitative history
- Roberto Busa SJ (28 Nov 1913 – 9 Aug 2011), an Italian Jesuit born in Vicenza, was a pioneer of using computers for linguistic and literary analysis. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roberto_Busa]
- Busa planned the *Index Thomisticus* in 1946, a complete lemmatised concordance of the works of Thomas Aquinas. In 1949 he met IBM founder Thomas J. Watson and persuaded him to sponsor it. IBM agreed to sponsor it until it was finished and assigned the executive Paul Tasman to work with Busa. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roberto_Busa ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_Thomisticus]
- Scope: 179 texts (118 by Aquinas; 61 attributed to him or completions of his works); 10,631,980 words indexed over about 34 years, first on punched cards; 56 printed volumes and over 70,000 pages. The first volume appeared in 1974 and the last in 1980. It used about 1,500 km of tape and took an estimated 10,000 hours of computer time and 1 million hours of human work. A website followed in 2005, and a new version of the site was released in 2026. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_Thomisticus]
- CD-ROM date CONFLICT: the Index Thomisticus article says the CD-ROM came out in 1992, but the Busa article says 1989. Say "around 1990" or leave the date out. [source: both URLs above]
- The data entry (punching the text) was done between 1950 and 1966 by a team of women keypunch operators in Gallarate, Italy, with 70 workers at the peak in 1962. Julianne Nyhan's book *Hidden and Devalued Feminized Labour in the Digital Humanities: On the Index Thomisticus Project 1954–67* (Routledge, 2023) recovers their story, which makes a good "ordinary people" angle. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_Thomisticus]
- In 2020 *The Economist* called the project "the creation story of the digital humanities". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_Thomisticus]
- The Alliance of Digital Humanities Organizations awards the "Busa Prize", first given to Busa himself in 1998. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roberto_Busa]
- The English professor Josephine Miles also began computer concordance work in the early 1950s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_humanities]
- Quantitative history in the 1960s and 70s used computers mainly on demographic and social data such as censuses, election returns and city directories, and it was rooted in the rise of social history. In the late 1980s quantifiers founded the Association for History and Computing, which helped drive the rise of digital history in the 1990s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_history]
- Quantitative history journals and their start dates: *Historical Methods* (1967–), *Journal of Interdisciplinary History* (1968–), *Social Science History* (1976–) and *Cliodynamics* (2010–). The largest data repository is the University of Michigan's Inter-university Consortium for Political and Social Research (ICPSR). "Nominal record linkage" means matching one person's name across censuses, directories and other lists. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantitative_history]
- Cliometrics: the "new economic history" began with Alfred Conrad and John Meyer's *The Economics of Slavery in the Antebellum South* (1958). Stanley Reiter coined the term "cliometrics" in 1960. Fogel and North shared the Nobel economics prize in October 1993. The Cliometric Society was founded in 1983. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliometrics]
- Text Encoding Initiative (TEI): launched in 1987, it published the first full TEI Guidelines in May 1994. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_humanities]

## 3. The web era: CD-ROMs, centres and pioneers (Rosenzweig)
- In 1982 the Library of Congress began an Optical Disk Pilot Project (laserdiscs and CD-ROMs). It launched its first online exhibit in 1992 with "Selected Civil War Photographs". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_history]
- In 1993 Roy Rosenzweig, Steve Brier and Josh Brown produced the CD-ROM *Who Built America?* for the American Social History Project. It won the American Historical Association's James Harvey Robinson Prize. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_history ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Rosenzweig]
- The Valley of the Shadow was conceived in 1991 by Edward L. Ayers at the University of Virginia. It compares Augusta County, Virginia, with Franklin County, Pennsylvania, through the American Civil War. Ayers and William G. Thomas III published "The Differences Slavery Made" online and in the *American Historical Review* (2003), using XML and GIS. In 1997 Ayers and Thomas used the term "digital history" when they founded the Virginia Center for Digital History, the earliest centre devoted exclusively to history. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_history]
- Roy Alan Rosenzweig (born New York City, 6 August 1950; died Arlington, Virginia, 11 October 2007, of lung cancer, aged 57) founded the Center for History and New Media (CHNM) at George Mason University in the fall of 1994 and directed it until his death. It was renamed the Roy Rosenzweig Center for History and New Media on 15 April 2011. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Rosenzweig ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Rosenzweig_Center_for_History_and_New_Media]
- Its stated aim was to use digital media "to democratize history: to incorporate multiple voices, reach diverse audiences, and encourage popular participation in presenting and preserving the past" (Wikipedia's wording, not a quotation from Rosenzweig). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Rosenzweig_Center_for_History_and_New_Media]
- CHNM's tools: Zotero (reference manager; Thomson Reuters, maker of EndNote, sued over it in 2008 and the suit was dismissed in 2009); Omeka (first released 2008; IMLS funding 2007); Tropy (2017). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Rosenzweig_Center_for_History_and_New_Media]
- With the American Social History Project at CUNY, CHNM built the September 11 Digital Archive. It holds more than 150,000 first-hand accounts, emails, images and other materials, and it became the Library of Congress's first major digital acquisition. It inspired the Hurricane Digital Memory Bank (Katrina, Rita, Wilma). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Rosenzweig_Center_for_History_and_New_Media]
- Rosenzweig's essays: "Scarcity or Abundance? Preserving the Past in a Digital Era", *American Historical Review* 108, no. 3 (2003), 735–62; "Can History Be Open Source? Wikipedia and the Future of the Past", *Journal of American History* (June 2006). Posthumous collection: *Clio Wired: The Future of the Past in the Digital Age* (Columbia UP, 2011). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Rosenzweig]
- Daniel J. Cohen and Roy Rosenzweig, *Digital History: A Guide to Gathering, Preserving, and Presenting the Past on the Web* (University of Pennsylvania Press). DATE CONFLICT: retail listings give a print release date of 27 September 2005, but the copyright and catalogue year is 2006, and Rosenzweig's Wikipedia page says 2006. Use "2005" with "(dated 2006)" or say "in the mid-2000s". A free online edition is hosted by GMU. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Cohen_(academic) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Rosenzweig ; via search: https://mars.gmu.edu/handle/1920/11906]
- Dan Cohen led RRCHNM for about 12 years and left in 2013 to become founding executive director of the Digital Public Library of America. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Cohen_(academic)]
- Wikipedia was launched on 15 January 2001. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia]

## 4. Mass digitisation: books, newspapers, archives
- Google Books began in 2002 under the code name "Project Ocean". When Larry Page and Marissa Mayer experimented in 2002, a 300-page book took 40 minutes to scan. Google Print was announced at the Frankfurt Book Fair in October 2004, and the Library Project in December 2004 with Michigan, Harvard, Stanford, Oxford (Bodleian) and the New York Public Library. By October 2019 Google said it had scanned more than 40 million titles. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Books]
- *Authors Guild v. Google* was filed in 2005. The District Court ruled for Google (fair use) on 14 November 2013, and the Second Circuit affirmed on 16 October 2015. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authors_Guild,_Inc._v._Google,_Inc.]
- European critics, including Jean-Noël Jeanneney, attacked Google Books on "linguistic imperialism" grounds because it was dominated by English. Jeanneney's book in English is *Google and the Myth of Universal Knowledge: A View from Europe* (University of Chicago Press, 2008). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Books]
- HathiTrust was founded in October 2008 by the 12 universities of the Committee on Institutional Cooperation and the 11 University of California libraries, and it is run by the University of Michigan. "Hathi" is Hindi for "elephant", an animal known for its memory. In September 2024 it held more than 18 million volumes, of which 6.7 million were public domain in the US. *Authors Guild v. HathiTrust*: fair use ruling October 2012, affirmed by the Second Circuit on 10 June 2014. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HathiTrust]
- Europeana began with an April 2005 letter from President Jacques Chirac and the heads of government of Germany, Spain, Italy, Poland and Hungary to European Commission President Barroso, urging a virtual European library. Its prototype, the European Digital Library Network, launched on 20 November 2008 with 4.5 million objects. By 2024 it gave access to records of more than 50 million objects from over 3,000 institutions. It holds metadata only, and the objects stay with the institutions that own them. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europeana]
- Chronicling America is a US newspaper database from the Library of Congress and the National Endowment for the Humanities (National Digital Newspaper Program, founded 2005). It launched publicly in March 2007. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronicling_America]
- Trove (National Library of Australia) was first released in late 2009. Since August 2008 the public has been able to correct the OCR text of digitised newspapers by crowdsourcing; by January 2022, 5.82% of articles had at least one correction. Some volunteers have corrected millions of lines. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trove]
- Google Books Ngram Viewer was released on 16 December 2010. It was developed with the Harvard researchers Jean-Baptiste Michel and Erez Lieberman Aiden, whose 2010 *Science* paper "Quantitative Analysis of Culture Using Millions of Digitized Books" coined "culturomics". Caveat: the Ngram corpus is not an unbiased sample and lacks metadata; for example, medical literature makes up a large and shifting share. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Books_Ngram_Viewer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culturomics]
- Other digital libraries beyond the West:
  - Chinese Text Project, created by Donald Sturgeon and launched in 2006. Its pre-Qin and Han section holds about 5.7 million characters, and its post-Han section over 20 million. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Text_Project]
  - Qatar Digital Library, launched in October 2014 by the Qatar Foundation, Qatar National Library and the British Library. It is bilingual (English and Arabic) and has about 500,000 pages, including about 475,000 from the India Office Records (mid-18th century to 1951) on the Gulf. It is an example of colonial-era records held in London being made freely available to the region they describe. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatar_Digital_Library]

## 5. Inequality of digitisation; endangered archives in the Global South
- The British Library's Endangered Archives Programme has been funded by the Arcadia Fund since 2004. Researchers digitise endangered archives *in situ*: the originals stay in their country of origin, and projects often include local training. It focuses on material from before the mid-20th century. By 2019 it had funded over 400 projects. Its collections hold over 7 million images and 25,000 sound files from Africa, Asia, the Americas and the Caribbean, Russia and Eastern Europe. Examples: magical texts from Djenné, Mali; the Islamic libraries of Timbuktu; Sri Lankan palm-leaf manuscripts; Tangut Buddhist texts (EAP140); Shui manuscripts from Guizhou, China (EAP143). Sam van Schaik became the first head of EAP in February 2019. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endangered_Archives_Programme]
- Timbuktu manuscripts: 4,203 manuscripts were burned or stolen in 2012–13 during the Mali War. Some 350,000 were moved to safety, and 300,000 were still in Bamako in 2022. The University of Oslo's Timbuktu Manuscripts Project ran from 1999 to 2007 to preserve, digitise and catalogue them. From 2013 the Hill Museum & Manuscript Library (Minnesota), with SAVAMA-DCI and Arcadia funding, photographed more than 150,000 manuscripts. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbuktu_Manuscripts]
- HathiTrust limits access outside the US to works published outside the US from 1896 onwards, an example of how copyright law shapes who can read what. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HathiTrust]
- "Digital colonialism": the concept that powerful states and corporations (US, also China) build digital infrastructure in the Global South and extract data from it, with a few corporations dominating. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_colonialism]
- Lara Putnam, "The Transnational and the Text-Searchable: Digitized Sources and the Shadows They Cast", *American Historical Review* 121, no. 2 (April 2016), 377–402. She argues that keyword search lets historians make transnational connections without the place-based local knowledge archive visits once required. Because digitised newspapers and periodicals dominate, the people who did not move, write or get written about become relatively less visible. [via search: https://www.historians.org/perspectives-article/in-the-april-issue-of-the-american-historical-review-april-2016/ ; abstract-level only, full text not read]

## 6. Databases as historical tools (worked examples)
- **Voyages: The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database (SlaveVoyages)**
  - Roots: in 1990 David Eltis and Stephen Behrendt were separately collecting British slaving data at the Public Record Office.
  - 1999: Cambridge University Press published it as a CD-ROM (Eltis, Behrendt, Richardson, Klein) covering 27,233 voyages. Some catalogues give about 25,000, and the high price limited its use.
  - December 2008: relaunched as a free open-access website ("Voyages") with about 35,000 voyages.
  - [via search: https://www.neh.gov/humanities/2010/septemberoctober/feature/gross-injustice ; https://hutchinscenter.fas.harvard.edu/trans-atlantic-slave-trade-database]
  - Wikipedia: by 2008 it covered nearly 35,000 voyages from 1501 to 1867, about 80% of the whole trade. It estimates that about 12.5 million captives embarked and 10.7 million landed, and that the Middle Passage killed more than 1.8 million people. It has a sister project, African Origins. Hosting is unclear: the article says Rice University, but its short description says Emory, so avoid naming the host or say "now hosted at Rice University". By flag of carrier, Portugal (5,848,266) and Great Britain (3,259,441) carried the most. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyages:_The_Trans-Atlantic_Slave_Trade_Database]
- **Old Bailey Proceedings Online**
  - Fully searchable and digitised: all surviving editions of the Old Bailey Proceedings 1674–1913, plus the Ordinary of Newgate's Accounts 1679–1772.
  - Over 197,000 trials, and biographical details of about 2,500 people executed at Tyburn.
  - Partners: the universities of Hertfordshire and Sheffield and the Open University, with AHRC funding.
  - [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arts_and_Humanities_Research_Council#Old_Bailey_Proceedings_Archive]
  - Launched in 2003 with trials from 1714 to 1759 and later extended to the full run. Edited by Tim Hitchcock and Robert Shoemaker. [via search: https://francistowne.ac.uk/collection/bibliography/old-bailey-online-2003/page/10 ; https://mapoflondon.uvic.ca/OLDB3.htm]
- **China Biographical Database (CBDB)**
  - A relational database of Chinese historical figures from the 7th to 19th centuries, with about 360,000 individuals as of April 2015.
  - Begun by Robert M. Hartwell to study the networks of Song officials. He left more than 25,000 individuals to the Harvard-Yenching Institute.
  - Now a joint project of Harvard's Fairbank Center, Academia Sinica's Institute of History and Philology, and Peking University. Associated scholars: Michael Fuller and Peter Bol.
  - Sources: official histories, funerary essays, epitaphs, local gazetteers and similar records.
  - Stated limitation: career data are biased toward higher offices, and errors in the primary sources are carried into the database.
  - [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_Biographical_Database]
- **China Historical GIS**: a Harvard–Fudan University historical GIS project on imperial China. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_geographic_information_system]
- **Other early or notable examples**: Patrick Manning's "African Slave Demography Project" simulated population decline from the slave trade in West and Central Africa (1730–1850) and East Africa (1820–1890). The Quilt Index is a collaborative database used in women's history. British History Online began as a 2002 pilot and now has over 1,200 volumes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_history]

## 7. Distant reading, text mining, topic modelling
- "Distant reading" is generally credited to Franco Moretti's article "Conjectures on World Literature" (*New Left Review* 1, Jan–Feb 2000, 54–68). He contrasted it with the close reading of a small canon and spoke of "the great unread". He first meant it as a way of working from other scholars' research, and only later did it come to mean computer analysis of the primary texts. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distant_reading ; via search: https://newleftreview.org/issues/ii1/articles/franco-moretti-conjectures-on-world-literature]
- Franco Moretti, born 1950 in Sondrio, Italy, taught at Stanford from 2000 to 2016 and founded the Stanford Literary Lab with Matthew Jockers in 2010. His books include *Graphs, Maps, Trees* (Verso, 2005) and *Distant Reading* (Verso, 2013). In 2017 he was publicly accused of sexual assault, which he denied; no formal proceeding was opened, and Stanford took no action. This is a sensitive fact, and the chapter does not need to mention it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco_Moretti]
- Example: Moretti's "Style, Inc." studied the titles of about 7,000 British novels from 1740 to 1850 and showed that titles grew shorter. Ted Underwood traces distant reading back long before the internet, to Raymond Williams in the 1960s and Janice Radway in the 1980s. Lauren Klein's "The Image of Absence" used computational methods on Jefferson's papers to trace James Hemings, Jefferson's enslaved chef, and the "silences" in the archive of slavery. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distant_reading]
- Topic modelling: latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) was developed by David Blei, Andrew Ng and Michael I. Jordan, published in January 2003. An early historical use: Block and Newman's study of topics in the *Pennsylvania Gazette*, 1728–1800. Common tools include MALLET. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topic_model ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_history]
- Jo Guldi, *The Dangerous Art of Text Mining: A Methodology for Digital History* (Cambridge UP, 2023). It warns that textual data from the past is "dangerous" unless historians read its silences and gaps, and it rejects treating models as prediction machines. [via search: https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009263016]

## 8. GIS and spatial history
- A historical GIS stores and analyses past geographies. Methods include georeferencing old maps, "rubbersheeting" them onto modern maps, and mapping census or parish microdata. Examples: Great Britain Historical GIS (Portsmouth, census period 1801–2001), the US National Historical GIS, and China Historical GIS. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_geographic_information_system]
- Richard White (born 28 May 1947) taught at Stanford from 1998 to 2020 and won a MacArthur Fellowship in 1995. He was the founding director of Stanford's Spatial History Project. He won the Parkman Prize twice and was a Pulitzer finalist twice, for *The Middle Ground* (1991) and *Railroaded* (2011). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_White_(historian)]
- The Spatial History Project was founded in 2007, with Mellon Foundation funds, within Stanford's Center for Spatial and Textual Analysis. It was active from 2007 to 2022, and its website is now a static copy. Its "Shaping the West" strand mapped how railroads reshaped people's experience of space and time, and it fed into *Railroaded*. White's working paper "What is Spatial History?" is dated 1 February 2010. [via search: https://web.stanford.edu/group/spatialhistory/about.html ; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/587591]

## 9. Network analysis of correspondence and trade
- The Republic of Letters was the long-distance intellectual community of the early 16th to early 19th centuries. The Latin term is first found in a letter of 6 July 1417 from Francesco Barbaro to Poggio Bracciolini, and Pierre Bayle's journal *Nouvelles de la République des Lettres* (1684) spread it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Letters]
- Mapping the Republic of Letters (Stanford) was begun in 2008 and is led by Paula Findlen and Dan Edelstein, with Nicole Coleman. It drew on the Electronic Enlightenment archive of about 55,000 letters among about 6,400 correspondents. Jeffrey Heer's students built the visualisations, and the Palladio tool grew out of the project. Findings: Voltaire's network was strongly Franco-centric and Locke's centred on the British Isles, and Franklin's most prolific correspondents were not leading European figures. The project's mapping showed less than 12% of Voltaire's roughly 19,000 letters, because many lacked location data, which is a good illustration of data limits. Reflection article: Edelstein, Findlen, Ceserani, Winterer and Coleman, "Historical Research in a Digital Age: Reflections from the Mapping the Republic of Letters Project", *AHR* 122:2 (2017), 400–424. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Letters ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_humanities ; via search: https://www.neh.gov/humanities/2013/novemberdecember/feature/mapping-the-republic-letters]
- Wikipedia's Digital history article illustrates network analysis with a visualisation of the archives of the League of Nations' International Committee on Intellectual Cooperation (interwar period). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_history]
- Trade networks: CBDB (kinship and social networks) and SlaveVoyages (ports and routes) serve as the "trade" examples. No dedicated trade-network project was separately verified.

## 10. Crowdsourcing and public digital history
- Transcribe Bentham was launched in September 2010 by UCL's Bentham Project with a 12-month AHRC grant, and it had Mellon funding from October 2012 (with the British Library joining). It runs on a customised MediaWiki and uses TEI-XML encoding. UCL's Bentham papers run to about 85,000 manuscript pages (about 30 million words), and the British Library holds about 15,000 more. Volunteers' transcripts feed *The Collected Works of Jeremy Bentham*. It won an Award of Distinction (Digital Communities) at the Prix Ars Electronica in September 2011. Volunteers transcribed recipes for Bentham's panopticon prison, published in 2014 as *Jeremy Bentham's Prison Cooking*. Wikipedia says that by November 2026 volunteers had worked on over 45,000 pages, 94% of them to the required standard. That date stamp looks odd, so the writer should say "tens of thousands of pages". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcribe_Bentham]
- Zooniverse grew from Galaxy Zoo (2007) and launched on 12 December 2009. It had more than 1 million registered volunteers in February 2014 and over 2.7 million by March 2025. Old Weather, a Zooniverse project launched in October 2010, has volunteers transcribe weather observations from Royal Navy and later US ship logbooks for climate reconstruction. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zooniverse ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Weather]
- The 1947 Partition Archive was founded by Guneeta Singh Bhalla, who began recording interviews in the San Francisco Bay Area in 2010; it was formalised in 2011. It is a nonprofit in Berkeley and a trust in Delhi. It crowdsources oral histories through "Citizen Historians" and trains volunteers. By June 2025 it had over 12,000 interviews from more than 450 cities and villages in 14 countries, including India, Pakistan and Bangladesh. Part of the collection streams through Stanford University Libraries, and full access is restricted because of sensitivity. Its book *10,000 Memories* appeared on 8 August 2024. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_1947_Partition_Archive]
- Online exhibits and collecting: Omeka was built so collecting websites didn't have to be made from scratch each time, and it was later used for COVID-19 collecting ("Pandemic Religion", "Collecting these Times"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Rosenzweig_Center_for_History_and_New_Media]
- An all-ages Canadian example: "Great Unsolved Mysteries in Canadian History" (University of Victoria, Université de Sherbrooke and OISE). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_history]

## 11. Indigenous data sovereignty and digital repatriation
- Mukurtu CMS was originally designed in 2007 for the Warumungu people in Australia. It is free and open source, and it lets communities set access by cultural protocol, with parallel sets of metadata and no single standard hierarchy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_repatriation#Mukurtu_CMS]
- "Mukurtu" is the Warumungu word (Tennant Creek, Northern Territory) for "dilly bag", which the project presents as a "safe keeping place". It was developed by the anthropologist Kimberly Christen (then at Washington State University) with the Warumungu community through the Nyinkka Nyunyu Art and Culture Centre. Example protocols: men may not view women's rituals, and family members may not view images of deceased relatives. One source spells her name "Christian", but WSU sources give "Christen". [via search: https://docs.mukurtu.org/about-mukurtu/WhatDoesMukurtuMean/ ; https://archive.news.wsu.edu/?p=82683]
- Local Contexts began in 2010 and issues Traditional Knowledge (TK) Labels, such as "TK Secret/Sacred". The Passamaquoddy digital archive (2014, built on Mukurtu) repatriated wax-cylinder recordings made in 1890. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_repatriation]
- The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance were created in 2019 by the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group of the Research Data Alliance (Global Indigenous Data Alliance). CARE stands for Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics. They complement the FAIR principles (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable), are framed by UNDRIP, and were inspired by Canada's First Nations OCAP principles. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CARE_Principles_for_Indigenous_Data_Governance ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_data_governance]

## 12. Born-digital sources and web archives
- "Born-digital" means material that originates in digital form, as opposed to digitised analogue material. The term is said to have been coined independently by the web developer Rafi Metz in 1993. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born-digital]
- Brewster Kahle founded the Internet Archive in May 1996, with Bruce Gilliat. Its mission is "universal access to all knowledge". Its headquarters have been at 300 Funston Avenue, San Francisco (a former Christian Science church), since 2009. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Archive]
- The Wayback Machine opened to the public on 25 October 2001 at a ceremony at UC Berkeley, by which time it already held over 10 billion pages; from 1996 to 2001 the data sat on tape. It is named after the time machine in the *Rocky and Bullwinkle* "Peabody's Improbable History" segment. "Save Page Now" was added in October 2013. In October 2025 the Internet Archive celebrated its first trillion archived web pages. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayback_Machine ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Archive]
- Fragility:
  - *Hachette v. Internet Archive* (filed 1 June 2020) ended in a ruling for the publishers on 25 March 2023.
  - In May 2024 the Archive suffered DDoS attacks, and from 9 October 2024 it suffered DDoS attacks, defacement and a data breach. The Wayback Machine came back read-only on 13 October.
  - By February 2026 some news organisations, including *The Guardian* and *The New York Times*, had begun blocking the Wayback Machine over AI-scraping concerns.
  - [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Archive ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayback_Machine]
- The International Internet Preservation Consortium was founded in 2003. Several national libraries may legally archive parts of the web under legal deposit (examples: the UK Web Archive and Australia's Pandora). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_archiving]
- Digital obsolescence: the BBC Domesday Project (1986) stored a survey of the UK on LaserDisc 900 years after the Domesday Book. The discs became hard to read, and the CAMiLEON project migrated them in 2002. The original Domesday Book of 1086 remains legible. In 2015, at the AAAS annual meeting, Vint Cerf warned of a "digital dark age" (the warning is attributed to him; see NOT VERIFIED for wording). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_dark_age]

## 13. Text recognition, AI and machine learning
- Transkribus is a platform for text recognition (including handwritten text recognition, HTR) and layout analysis of historical documents. It came out of the EU projects tranScriptorium (2013–15) and READ (Recognition and Enrichment of Archival Documents, 2016–19), was developed at the University of Innsbruck, and has been run since 1 July 2019 by READ-COOP, a non-profit cooperative. Alternatives include eScriptorium and OCR4All. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transkribus]
- Transkribus's own homepage claims "500,000+ users" and "200M+ pages". These are self-reported. A 2022 release said 90,000 users, and a 2022 academic paper estimated about 1,700 regular monthly users. The National Archives of Finland made more than 2 million handwritten documents searchable. [via search: https://transkribus.org ; https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10502-022-09397-0]
- Trove began pilot-testing handwritten text recognition in October 2023. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trove]
- Herculaneum papyri:
  - More than 1,800 scrolls were carbonised in the eruption of Vesuvius in 79 AD and found from 1752 in the Villa of the Papyri.
  - Many are works of the Epicurean Philodemus.
  - Brent Seales (University of Kentucky) used "virtual unrolling" on the En-Gedi scroll in 2016 and on Herculaneum material in 2018.
  - [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herculaneum_papyri]
- Vesuvius Challenge:
  - Announced in 2023 by Nat Friedman, Daniel Gross and Brent Seales.
  - On 12 October 2023 Luke Farritor, a 21-year-old University of Nebraska student, won $40,000 for detecting the first word in an unopened scroll: *porphyras* ("purple").
  - On 5 February 2024 the $700,000 Grand Prize went to Youssef Nader, Luke Farritor and Julian Schilliger. They recovered more than 2,000 characters in 15 columns, about 5% of the scroll PHerc. Paris. 4, which was previously unknown text, probably by Philodemus, on pleasures such as music and food.
  - In June 2026 the team announced the first complete virtual unwrapping and reading of a rolled scroll, PHerc. 1667 ("Scroll 4"): 22 columns of a Stoic treatise possibly by Chrysippus. This was announced in a preprint, so say "reported" or "announced".
  - [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herculaneum_papyri#Vesuvius_Challenge ; via search: https://scrollprize.org/grandprize ; https://uknow.uky.edu/research/grand-prize-discovery-made-2000-year-old-herculaneum-scrolls]
- On 29 July 2025 the American Historical Association's Council approved *Guiding Principles for Artificial Intelligence in History Education* (14 principles under five conclusions). It warns that generative AI output may be false unless verified, and that imitation of historians' work should not be mistaken for teaching or learning. A separate AHA committee covers research and publication. [via search: https://www.historians.org/news/aha-publishes-guiding-principles-for-artificial-intelligence-in-history-education/]

## 14. Sustainability (seed topic)
- The Index Thomisticus migrated from punched cards to print (1974–80), then CD-ROM (around 1989–92), then the web (2005), then a new site (2026). This is a case study in keeping a project alive across formats. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_Thomisticus]
- Stanford's Spatial History Project now survives only as a static copy, after being active from 2007 to 2022. [via search: https://web.stanford.edu/group/spatialhistory/about.html]
- From 2015 RRCHNM shared software maintenance with the Corporation for Digital Scholarship, and in February 2021 it announced that Tropy, Omeka and Zotero were moving to CDS entirely. Its *Journal of Digital Humanities* ran from 2011 to 2014, and *Digital Humanities Now* from 2009 to 2021. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Rosenzweig_Center_for_History_and_New_Media]
- The BBC Domesday Project and the Internet Archive's lawsuits and cyberattacks (above) are further examples.

## Debates
- **Abundance versus scarcity.** Rosenzweig (2003) argued that historians face both unprecedented abundance of records and the risk of losing born-digital ones, and that the centuries-old system of preservation was "in crisis" (paraphrase). [via search: https://rrchnm.org/essays/scarcity-or-abundance-preserving-the-past-in-a-digital-era/]
- **Search and digitisation distort what historians see.**
  - Tim Hitchcock, "Confronting the Digital: or how academic history writing lost the plot", *Cultural and Social History* 10:1 (March 2013), 9–23. He argues that algorithmic discovery, misleading search, poor OCR and the selection bias of digitised print archives mean historians keep the forms of scholarship while ignoring its principles. [via search: https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Confronting_the_digital_or_how_academic_history_writing_lost_the_plot/23423972]
  - Lara Putnam (2016) argues that keyword search lets researchers skip place-based knowledge and makes people who did not write or travel less visible. [via search, see §5]
- **Limits of quantification and visualisation.**
  - Johanna Drucker: data are "capta", taken and constructed, not given. Visualisations wrongly present interpreted material as neutral data.
  - Stanley Fish: tool-driven "run the numbers" method.
  - Stephen Marche: generalisations fail on "literary questions proper".
  - Earlier cliometrics quarrels (*Time on the Cross*) are covered in Social History.
  - Francesco Boldizzoni argues that neoclassical economic laws do not fit non-capitalist or premodern economies. Claude Diebolt (2016) defends cliometrics as an "indispensable tool".
  - [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_humanities ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distant_reading ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliometrics ; via search for capta]
- **Who digital history is for, and who it leaves out.** In 2012 Matthew K. Gold summarised criticisms of digital humanities: "a lack of attention to issues of race, class, gender, and sexuality", a preference for research over teaching, "an absence of political commitment", little diversity among practitioners, an inability to deal with texts under copyright, and "an institutional concentration in well-funded research universities". Tara McPherson links the field's lack of racial diversity to the design of UNIX and computing. Roopika Risam's postcolonial critique is covered in the reading list. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_humanities]
- **Digital colonialism versus in-situ digitisation.** One side holds that data extraction and English-language dominance reproduce colonial asymmetries (Jeanneney's "linguistic imperialism" critique of Google; the digital colonialism concept). The other side points to models such as EAP (originals stay in place, with local training), the Qatar Digital Library, and Mukurtu and the CARE principles (community control). [sources in §4, §5, §11]
- **What counts as an argument.** Wikipedia's Digital history article says that combining archives with tools to make a scholarly analysis is a "hallmark" of digital history, and that differences in how projects do this point to "ongoing debates over what digital history can and should be". The flagged section is unreferenced. A concrete example is "The Differences Slavery Made" (AHR 2003), an argument published online and in print. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_history]
- **Prediction and failure of the computer revolution.** In 1968 Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie predicted that historians would have to become programmers. A 1979 *New York Review of Books* review called this a failed prophecy. Exact wording is unverified, so paraphrase only (see NOT VERIFIED). [via search: https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1979/11/08/in-the-alleys-of-mentalite/]
- **Distant reading's genealogy.** Ted Underwood argues that distant reading has a pre-computer, social-science genealogy (Raymond Williams, Janice Radway) that the Moretti origin story hides. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distant_reading]
