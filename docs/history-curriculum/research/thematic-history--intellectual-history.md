## What intellectual history is
- Intellectual history, or the history of ideas, studies the history of human thought and of intellectuals; its premise is that ideas do not develop in isolation from the people who conceive and use them, so ideas are studied both as arguments and in their concrete settings of culture, life and history. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_history]
- The field grew out of the history of philosophy and cultural history (*Kulturgeschichte*, *Geistesgeschichte*); the history of reading and the history of the book developed from it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_history]
- Over the 20th century "history of ideas" gave way to "intellectual history" as a label, a shift reflecting the move toward contextualism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_history (citing Melvin Richter, JHI 1987)]
- Peter E. Gordon (Harvard), in "What Is Intellectual History? A Frankly Partisan Introduction to a Frequently Misunderstood Field" (2012), says intellectual historians "tend to be more relaxed about crossing the boundary between philosophical texts and non-philosophical contexts" and treat the line between "philosophy" and "non-philosophy" as itself historically conditioned. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_history]
- Anyone who set down their thoughts can be the subject of an intellectual history, not only professional intellectuals; the idea of "the intellectual" as a public figure dates from the 19th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_history]
- Worked example of "ordinary" intellectual history: Carlo Ginzburg's *The Cheese and the Worms* (Italian *Il formaggio e i vermi*, 1976) reconstructs the cosmology of Menocchio (Domenico Scandella, 1532–1599), a miller of Montereale in Friuli, from his Inquisition trials; he was burned as a heresiarch. Called "probably the most popular and widely read work of microhistory". Ginzburg found the case in 1963 while researching witchcraft trials and returned to the documents in 1970. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cheese_and_the_Worms]

## Origins: Lovejoy and the "history of ideas"
- Arthur Oncken Lovejoy (10 October 1873 – 30 December 1962), American philosopher, born in Berlin; studied at Berkeley and at Harvard under William James and Josiah Royce; never took a PhD. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Oncken_Lovejoy]
- Lovejoy was professor of philosophy at Johns Hopkins 1910–1938 (another Wikipedia article says 1910–1939; see Not verified) and founded and long presided over its History of Ideas Club. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Oncken_Lovejoy]
- *The Great Chain of Being: A Study of the History of an Idea* (Harvard University Press, 1936) grew out of the William James Lectures at Harvard in 1933 (one source says 1932–33). It has been called "probably the single most influential work in the history of ideas in the United States during the last half century" (Knuuttila, ed., 2013). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Oncken_Lovejoy; lectures: search, https://www.routledge.com/The-Great-Chain-of-Being-A-Study-of-the-History-of-an-Idea/Lovejoy/p/book/9781412810265 and https://libarch.nmu.org.ua/bitstream/handle/GenofondUA/24685/da21a657702b32cb3b956423de30e0ad.pdf]
- The book traces three principles that combined in the "chain of being" conception, plenitude, continuity and gradation (or "graduation"), from Plato, Aristotle and the Neoplatonists; the chain ran from God through angels, humans, animals and plants to minerals. Lovejoy argued the chain was dismantled at the end of the 18th century (endorsed by W. F. Bynum, 1975). [source: search as above; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_chain_of_being; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Oncken_Lovejoy]
- "Unit-ideas": Lovejoy held that big "-isms" (pragmatism, idealism, rationalism) are compounds of analytically separate ideas, which the historian must separate out and then follow as they combine and recombine over time. His essay "The Thirteen Pragmatisms" appeared in 1908. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Oncken_Lovejoy; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_history]
- In 1940 Lovejoy co-founded the *Journal of the History of Ideas* with Philip P. Wiener; it has been published by the University of Pennsylvania Press since 2006 and runs a blog (since 2015). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_the_History_of_Ideas]
- Lovejoy helped found the American Association of University Professors and the Maryland ACLU, but in 1952 argued that Communist Party membership was grounds for dismissal from academic posts. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Oncken_Lovejoy]
- Other mid-century practitioners: Isaiah Berlin (*Against the Current: Essays in the History of Ideas*). Berlin's "Two Concepts of Liberty" was his Oxford inaugural lecture, 31 October 1958, distinguishing "negative" liberty ("freedom from" interference) and "positive" liberty ("freedom to", self-mastery). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Concepts_of_Liberty]

## The Cambridge School: texts as actions
- The Cambridge School is a loose, contextualist movement in the history of political thought associated with Quentin Skinner, J. G. A. Pocock, Peter Laslett, John Dunn, James Tully, David Runciman and Raymond Geuss. It opposes the anachronism of reading old texts through modern understandings. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_School_(intellectual_history)]
- Its founding manifesto is usually taken to be Skinner's article "Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas", *History and Theory* 8 (1) (1969), pp. 3–53, reprinted in his *Visions of Politics*, vol. 1: *Regarding Method* (2002). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_history; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quentin_Skinner]
- In that essay Skinner attacked two "orthodoxies": "perennialism" (philosophers have always debated the same fundamental questions) and the view that a text can be understood without its context. He criticised Lovejoy's unit-ideas as a "reification of doctrines". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_School_(intellectual_history); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_history]
- The essay names a set of "mythologies" of bad interpretation: of doctrines (turning scattered remarks into a full doctrine on an expected theme), of coherence, of prolepsis (reading a work for a significance only later visible), and of parochialism. [search: https://typeset.io/papers/meaning-and-understanding-in-the-history-of-ideas-4bw0flhcjo and related summaries; wording of the definitions is paraphrase, not quotation]
- Skinner's method draws on Wittgenstein and J. L. Austin's speech-act theory: a text is an intervention in a live debate, and the question is what its author "was doing" in writing it (supporting, discrediting, legitimising). It requires reading lesser-known contemporaries. Early on it was aimed especially at Leo Strauss and his followers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quentin_Skinner]
- J. L. Austin's *How to Do Things with Words* (published 1962, edited by J. O. Urmson) came from his William James Lectures at Harvard in 1955; it introduced the "performative utterance" and the distinction between locutionary, illocutionary and perlocutionary acts. Austin died in 1960. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Do_Things_with_Words]
- Quentin Skinner: born 26 November 1940, Oldham; Cambridge lecturer from 1965; Institute for Advanced Study, Princeton, 1974–79; Regius Professor of History at Cambridge 1996–2008; Wolfson History Prize 1979, Balzan Prize 2006; reportedly refused a knighthood. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quentin_Skinner]
- Skinner's main books: *The Foundations of Modern Political Thought* (2 vols, 1978; on the TLS list of the 100 most influential books since the Second World War, 1995); *Machiavelli* (1981; *Very Short Introduction* 2000, 2019); *Liberty before Liberalism* (1998); *Visions of Politics* (3 vols, 2002); *Hobbes and Republican Liberty* (2008). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quentin_Skinner]
- *Liberty before Liberalism* argues for a shift from an older ("neo-roman" / republican) view that liberty means not being subject to arbitrary power to today's view that it simply means absence of restraint. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quentin_Skinner]
- J. G. A. Pocock (7 March 1924 – 12 December 2023): born in London, raised in New Zealand from 1927 (father professor of Classics at Canterbury College); moved to the USA in 1966; taught at Johns Hopkins 1975–2011; died in Baltimore aged 99. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._G._A._Pocock]
- Pocock's books: *The Ancient Constitution and the Feudal Law* (1957), on the English "common law mind" (Edward Coke); *The Machiavellian Moment: Florentine Political Thought and the Atlantic Republican Tradition* (Princeton UP, 1975); *Barbarism and Religion* (6 vols, 1999–2015), on Gibbon and Enlightenment historiography, vol. 1 titled *The Enlightenments of Edward Gibbon* (1999). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._G._A._Pocock; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Machiavellian_Moment]
- Pocock's distinctive emphasis was on political "languages", defined as "idioms, rhetorics, specialised vocabularies and grammars"; Skinner's on authorial intention; Dunn's on biography. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._G._A._Pocock]
- Pocock said "long ago, I decided that I would no longer describe what I was doing by the then conventional term 'history of ideas'", and warned of the danger of translating the Greek *polis*, Chinese *kuo*, Latin *civitas*, Florentine *stato* etc. all as "state". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_School_(intellectual_history)]
- Skinner suggested the title *The Machiavellian Moment*; the two corresponded while writing it and *Foundations*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Machiavellian_Moment]
- John Dunn (born 9 September 1940), King's College, Cambridge; professor of political theory from 1987; *The Political Thought of John Locke* (1969); later *Western Political Theory in the Face of the Future* (1979) and an edited *West African States: Failure and Promise* (1978). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dunn_(political_theorist)]
- Cambridge University Press's *Ideas in Context* series began in 1984 with *Philosophy in History* (eds Richard Rorty, J. B. Schneewind, Quentin Skinner); Skinner was general editor for over two decades. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_School_(intellectual_history)]

## Begriffsgeschichte: the history of concepts
- Conceptual history (*Begriffsgeschichte*) studies the historical semantics of terms such as "liberty" or "reform"; its pioneers include Joachim Ritter, Otto Brunner, Werner Conze, Reinhart Koselleck and Erich Rothacker; Raymond Williams was the leading English-language figure. It is carried on by the History of Concepts Group and the journal *Contributions to the History of Concepts*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceptual_history]
- Reinhart Koselleck (23 April 1923 – 4 February 2006): Hitler Youth member and wartime soldier; captured by the Soviet army in May 1945, set to work clearing debris at Auschwitz, then held as a POW in Kazakhstan for 15 months. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinhart_Koselleck]
- His Heidelberg dissertation *Kritik und Krise* (1954; book 1959; English *Critique and Crisis*, MIT Press 1988) argued, under Carl Schmitt's influence, that the Enlightenment's moral critique of the absolutist state, nurtured in the Republic of Letters and secret societies like the Freemasons, was utopian and bred "permanent crisis". It is often read against Habermas's more positive account. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinhart_Koselleck; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critique_and_Crisis]
- *Geschichtliche Grundbegriffe: Historisches Lexikon zur politisch-sozialen Sprache in Deutschland*, edited by Otto Brunner, Werner Conze and Koselleck, published by Klett(-Cotta), Stuttgart, 1972–1997, in 8 volumes (A–Z) plus a two-part index. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinhart_Koselleck; search: https://www.sudoc.fr/023102918, https://econbiz.de/Record/geschichtliche-grundbegriffe-historisches-lexikon-zur-politisch-sozialen-sprache-in-deutschland-brunner-otto/10000099301]
- Its leading hypothesis was the *Sattelzeit* ("saddle time", threshold period), about 1750–1850, when the German political-social vocabulary turned into the language of modernity. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinhart_Koselleck]
- In *Vergangene Zukunft* (1979; English *Futures Past: On the Semantics of Historical Time*, usually dated 1985; Columbia UP edition 2004, trans. Keith Tribe) Koselleck proposed the paired categories "space of experience" and "horizon of expectation"; modernity is marked by a growing gap between them, expectations no longer derived from past experience. [source: search: https://www.jhiblog.org/?p=29361, https://dl1.cuni.cz/pluginfile.php/738777/mod_resource/content/2/Koselleck%20-%20Future%20Past.pdf; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinhart_Koselleck]
- Pocock accepted that the *Sattelzeit*, "as Professor Koselleck has described", showed concepts inside an ongoing history of debate, but insisted conceptual history, history of ideas and history of discourses "can be confronted, compared, and combined, but not homogenized". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_School_(intellectual_history)]
- Raymond Williams's *Keywords: A Vocabulary of Culture and Society* (Croom Helm, 1976; revised 1983) traced over a hundred words such as culture, society, nature, work; he called "culture" one of the most complicated words in English, originally meaning husbandry, "the tending of natural growth". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keywords:_A_Vocabulary_of_Culture_and_Society]

## Foucault: archaeology and genealogy
- Michel Foucault (15 October 1926 – 25 June 1984); Collège de France from 1970 (chair in the History of Systems of Thought). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Foucault]
- *The Order of Things* (*Les Mots et les Choses*, 1966; English 1970) argued each period has an *episteme*, underlying rules setting what can be thought and said to be true; it opens with an analysis of Velázquez's *Las Meninas* (1656). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Order_of_Things]
- *The Archaeology of Knowledge* (*L'archéologie du savoir*, 1969) set out the method used in *Madness and Civilization* (1961), *The Birth of the Clinic* (1963) and *The Order of Things*; it attacked the history of ideas' reliance on narrative continuity and stressed discontinuity and "discursive formations". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Archaeology_of_Knowledge; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_history]
- "Genealogy", after Nietzsche's *On the Genealogy of Morals*, was developed in Foucault's essay "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" and his *Discipline and Punish* (1975) and *History of Sexuality* (1976): not a search for origins but a showing of contingent, power-laden pasts of things "we tend to feel [are] without history". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genealogy_(philosophy); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Foucault]

## History of the book and of reading
- The history of the book became an academic discipline in the later 20th century, fostered by Lucien Febvre and Henri-Jean Martin, *L'apparition du livre* (1958; English *The Coming of the Book: The Impact of Printing 1450–1800*, 1976), William Ivins (1953) and Marshall McLuhan, *The Gutenberg Galaxy* (1962); Robert Darnton is a leading pioneer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_books]
- Robert Darnton (born 10 May 1939, New York): Harvard AB 1960; Oxford DPhil 1964 (Rhodes scholar); *New York Times* reporter 1964–65; Princeton professor; director of the Harvard University Library 2007–2016 and co-founder of the Digital Public Library of America. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Darnton]
- Darnton's books: *The Business of Enlightenment: A Publishing History of the Encyclopédie, 1775–1800* (1979); *The Literary Underground of the Old Regime* (1982); *The Great Cat Massacre and Other Episodes in French Cultural History* (1984); *The Forbidden Best-Sellers of Pre-Revolutionary France* (1996, National Book Critics Circle Award for criticism); *Censors at Work* (2014). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Darnton]
- Darnton's essay "What Is the History of Books?", *Daedalus* 111 (3) (Summer 1982), pp. 65–83, proposed the "communications circuit", a loop linking production, distribution and reception in which the reader completes the circuit by influencing the author; he revisited it in *Modern Intellectual History* (2007). [search: https://typeset.io/pdf/what-is-the-history-of-books-2a1qdcpdm3.pdf and related; the conventional list of stages (author, publisher, printers, shippers, booksellers, readers) was NOT confirmed verbatim]
- The cat-massacre chapter: apprentice printers in Jacques Vincent's shop on the rue Saint-Séverin, Paris, in the late 1730s (date uncertain) held mock trials and killed cats, including the mistress's favourite *la grise*; the only source is the account by Nicolas Contat ("Jérôme") in *Anecdotes typographiques*, written some twenty years later. Darnton, influenced by Clifford Geertz's "thick description", read it as a symbolic workers' protest. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Cat_Massacre; search: https://www.historytoday.com/print/pdf/node/50144/debug]
- Critics: Roger Chartier debated Darnton; Dominic LaCapra mocked the "Great Symbol Massacre"; Harold Mah (1991) argued Darnton "suppressed" the nature of the source. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Cat_Massacre]
- Roger Chartier (born 9 December 1945, Lyon), Annales school historian of books, publishing and reading; EHESS; Collège de France chair "Written Culture in Modern Europe" from 2006. Key concept: "appropriation" (how readers make texts their own, shaped by reading skills, communities and formats). Books include *The Cultural Origins of the French Revolution* (1991), *The Order of Books* (1994), and *A History of Reading in the West* (co-ed. with Guglielmo Cavallo). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Chartier]
- Jürgen Habermas, *Strukturwandel der Öffentlichkeit* (1962; English *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere*, 1989), a key text on salons, coffeehouses and print in the making of a "public". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Structural_Transformation_of_the_Public_Sphere]

## Global intellectual history
- Samuel Moyn and Andrew Sartori (eds), *Global Intellectual History* (Columbia University Press, 2013), opens with their chapter "Approaches to Global Intellectual History"; the Routledge journal *Global Intellectual History* was founded in 2016 (editor Richard Whatmore). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_history; search: https://laviedesidees.fr/Global-Thinking]
- Pocock and Dunn have argued for a more global approach against Eurocentrism (Dunn, "Why We Need a Global History of Political Thought", 2013). Christopher Goto-Jones argued the Cambridge School had developed in an orientalist direction by neglecting non-Western thought; Dag Herbjørnsrud warned "the Skinner perspective is in danger of shutting the door to comparative philosophy". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_history; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_School_(intellectual_history)]
- Andrew Sartori, *Bengal in Global Concept History: Culturalism in the Age of Capital* (University of Chicago Press, 2008), follows the concept of "culture" from 18th-century Germany through England to 19th–20th-century Bengal, arguing that global capitalism drove its spread. [search: https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/B/bo5834728.html]
- Samuel Moyn (born 1972), Yale; *The Last Utopia: Human Rights in History* (Harvard/Belknap, 2010) argues human rights became a mass moral cause only in the 1970s (stressing 1977), once rival utopias (socialism, anticolonial nationalism) faltered; earlier 1940s rights talk had been bypassed. Critics argue rights were on the agenda well before. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Moyn; search: https://www.pbs.org/wnet/religionandethics/2010/12/09/an-unconventional-history-of-human-rights/7641]
- Dipesh Chakrabarty (born 1948, Kolkata), *Provincializing Europe: Postcolonial Thought and Historical Difference* (2000); Toynbee Prize 2014. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipesh_Chakrabarty]
- Lydia H. Liu, *Translingual Practice: Literature, National Culture, and Translated Modernity—China, 1900–1937* (Stanford UP, 1995), studies how new words and meanings arose in Chinese through contact with European languages and Japanese; its appendices list missionary neologisms and Sino-Japanese "return graphic loans" (classical Chinese terms reimported via Japanese kanji usage). [search: https://muse.jhu.edu/journals/the_comparatist/summary/v021/21.scott.html]
- Tomoko Masuzawa (Michigan), *The Invention of World Religions, or, How European Universalism Was Preserved in the Language of Pluralism* (2005); Jason Ananda Josephson, *The Invention of Religion in Japan* (2012). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomoko_Masuzawa; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaozheng (bibliography)]

## Translating concepts: liberty, civilization, culture, religion
- Fukuzawa Yukichi (10 January 1835 – 3 February 1901), founder of Keio Gijuku: *Seiyō Jijō* (*Conditions in the West*, first volume 1866, about 250,000 copies sold); *Gakumon no Susume* (*An Encouragement of Learning*, 1872–76); *Bunmeiron no Gairyaku* (*An Outline of a Theory of Civilization*, 1875), drawing on François Guizot. He was a leading voice of *bunmei kaika* ("civilization and enlightenment"); his idea of "civilization" was first shaped by American geography textbooks (Samuel Mitchell, Sarah Cornell) with a ladder of stages. Member of the Meirokusha (1874). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukuzawa_Yukichi]
- Fukuzawa's final chapter of the *Outline* (1875) put national independence ahead of civilization as an abstract ideal; the 1885 editorial "Datsu-A Ron" ("Leaving Asia") urged distance from "backward" neighbours. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukuzawa_Yukichi] (Authorship: see Conflicts.)
- Nakamura Masanao translated Mill's *On Liberty* as *Jiyū no kotowari* (自由之理, 1872); "liberty" was among the first terms to dominate Japanese debate in the early 1870s. [search: https://museumcollection.tokyo/en/works/6450225, https://www.ndl.go.jp/portrait/e/datas/305.html]
- Yan Fu (8 January 1854 – 27 October 1921) studied at the Royal Naval College, Greenwich, 1877–79; after the 1894–95 defeat by Japan he translated Huxley's *Evolution and Ethics* (天演論), Adam Smith, Mill's *On Liberty*, Spencer and others (1898–1909), introducing natural selection (天擇 *tianze*) and Social Darwinism; his preface named three difficulties of translation, "faithfulness, expressiveness, and elegance" (信達雅). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yan_Fu]
- Yan Fu's *On Liberty* (1903) was titled 羣己權界論 *Qunji quanjie lun*, "On the boundary between the self and the group" — a title that recasts Mill's individual liberty as a boundary between individual and collective. [search: https://data.ctext.org/entity/545504 and related]
- Rifa'a al-Tahtawi (1801–1873): sent to Paris in 1826 as imam to Muhammad Ali's student mission; returned 1831; *Takhlis al-Ibriz fi Talkhis Bariz* (1834); founded the School of Languages (translators) in Cairo, 1835. Regarded as the pioneering figure of the Nahda. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rifa%27a_at-Tahtawi; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahda]
- Butrus al-Bustani (1819–1893) founded the secular National School in Beirut in 1863 after the 1860 conflict, worked on the Smith–Van Dyck Arabic Bible, and created the first modern Arabic encyclopedia. Ahmad Faris al-Shidyaq's *Leg Over Leg* (Paris, 1855) defended women's rights. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahda]
- Older scholarship tied the Nahda to the shock of Napoleon's 1798 invasion; more recent work sees it as "autogenetic" as much as Western-inspired, linked to the Tanzimat and later the Young Turk Revolution's press boom. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahda]
- Positivism in Latin America: Leopoldo Zea (30 June 1912 – 8 June 2004), *El positivismo en México* (1943); Brazil's flag motto "Ordem e Progresso" (adopted by decree of 19 November 1889) is adapted from Auguste Comte's positivist maxim ("love as the principle, order as the basis, progress as the end"); several leaders of the 1889 republican coup, notably Benjamin Constant Botelho de Magalhães, were positivists. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopoldo_Zea_Aguilar; search: https://library.brown.edu/create/fivecenturiesofchange/chapters/chapter-4/positivism/]
- José Martí (28 January 1853 – 19 May 1895): essay "Nuestra América" published 1 January 1891 in New York's *Revista Ilustrada* and on 30 January in Mexico's *El Partido Liberal*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Mart%C3%AD]
- José Carlos Mariátegui (14 June 1894 – 16 April 1930), *Seven Interpretive Essays on Peruvian Reality* (1928); founded the Peruvian Socialist Party (1928), a socialism "without tracing or copying". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Carlos_Mari%C3%A1tegui]

## Non-Western traditions and their own intellectual histories
- Ibn Khaldun (27 May 1332 – 17 March 1406) drafted the *Muqaddimah* in 1377 at Qal'at ibn Salama, as the introduction to his universal history *Kitab al-'Ibar*; it proposes a new science of *'umran* (civilization/society) and the concept of *'asabiyya* (group feeling, solidarity) to explain the rise and fall of dynasties. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muqaddimah; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Khaldun]
- Reception of the *Muqaddimah* (a worked example of a text travelling): Ottoman historians Kâtip Çelebi (d. 1657) and Mustafa Naima (d. 1716) valued it; Pirizade Mehmed (d. 1749) translated chapters 1–5 into Ottoman Turkish and Ahmed Cevdet the sixth (complete translation published 1860/61); first printed at Bulaq, Cairo, in 1857 with Tahtawi's support; Quatremère's Arabic edition, Paris 1858; de Slane's French translation 1863 (three volumes); Franz Rosenthal's English translation 1958. Abdelmajid Hannoum argues de Slane read a colonial "racial" opposition of Arabs and Berbers into the text. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muqaddimah]
- *Kaozheng* ("evidential research"), the Qing philological movement c. 1600–1850, also called the Qian–Jia school; it criticised Wang Yangming's subjectivism and Song neo-Confucianism and returned to the classics with textual-critical methods. Early figures include Gu Yanwu (1613–1682), author of the *Rizhilu* (*Record of Daily Knowledge*), and Huang Zongxi. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaozheng; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gu_Yanwu]
- Dai Zhen (19 January 1724 – 1 July 1777) criticised neo-Confucian introspection and its hostility to human desire. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dai_Zhen]
- Interpretations of *kaozheng*: Liang Qichao, Hu Shih and Gu Jiegang saw it as a step toward scientific scholarship; Carsun Chang and Xu Fuguan as sterile; Yu Ying-shih stressed continuity with neo-Confucianism; Benjamin Elman argued it was "an empirical revolution". Its methods passed to Edo Japan as *kōshōgaku* (e.g. Keichū's edition of the *Man'yōshū*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaozheng]
- Benjamin A. Elman (born 1946), Princeton: *From Philosophy to Philology: Intellectual and Social Aspects of Change in Late Imperial China* (1984); *A Cultural History of Civil Examinations in Late Imperial China* (2000); *On Their Own Terms: Science in China, 1550–1900* (2005). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_A._Elman]
- Sheldon Pollock (born 1948), Columbia; *The Language of the Gods in the World of Men: Sanskrit, Culture, and Power in Premodern India* (University of California Press, 2006): around the start of the Common Era Sanskrit was reinvented from a ritual language into a code of literary and political expression across a "Sanskrit cosmopolis" from Afghanistan to Java (by the fifth century "power in India now had a Sanskrit voice"); from around the start of the second millennium a "vernacular millennium" saw regional languages displace it. Founding editor of the Murty Classical Library of India. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheldon_Pollock; search: https://bookshop.org/book/9780520245006]
- C. A. Bayly (18 May 1945 – 18 April 2015), Vere Harmsworth Professor at Cambridge 1992–2013; *The Birth of the Modern World, 1780–1914* (2004); *Recovering Liberties: Indian Thought in the Age of Liberalism and Empire* (Cambridge UP, 2012; from the Wiles Lectures, Belfast, 2007), tracing Indian liberalism from Rammohan Roy in the 1820s through Dadabhai Naoroji (1880s) to G. K. Gokhale (1900s), and showing Mill, Comte, Spencer and Marx being reworked by Indian thinkers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Bayly; search: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/recovering-liberties/advent-of-liberal-thought-in-india-and-beyond/31C55295F39288EAEF2E3AB687D5AB1B]
- Timbuktu manuscripts: mostly Arabic, some in African languages in Arabic script (*Ajami*: Fula, Songhay, Tamasheq, Bambara, Soninke); dated late 13th to early 20th century; subjects include law, theology, astronomy, medicine, history and debates on polygamy, moneylending and slavery. - Shamil Jeppie and Souleymane Bachir Diagne (eds), *The Meanings of Timbuktu* (HSRC Press with CODESRIA, 2008), challenges the idea that African history survived only orally. [search: https://hsrcpress.ac.za/?p=9582]
- Souleymane Bachir Diagne (born 8 November 1955, Saint-Louis, Senegal), Columbia University; *African Art as Philosophy: Senghor, Bergson, and the Idea of Negritude* (Eng. 2011); *Open to Reason: Muslim Philosophers in Conversation with the Western Tradition* (Eng. 2018); a book on Muhammad Iqbal (2001). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Souleymane_Bachir_Diagne]

## Worked example: Machiavelli
- *The Prince* was written in 1513 (Latin working title *De Principatibus*), the year after the Medici returned and months after Machiavelli's arrest and torture; dedicated to Lorenzo di Piero de' Medici; printed only in 1532, five years after his death, with Pope Clement VII's permission. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince]
- It is written in the form of a traditional "mirror for princes"; Felix Gilbert (1938) showed that its closing exhortation was a normal feature of that genre — the kind of contextual reading the Cambridge School generalised. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince]
- Contrast of readings: Leo Strauss (*Thoughts on Machiavelli*, 1958) — even granting he was a patriot or scientist, "we would not be forced to deny that he was a teacher of evil"; Skinner (*Foundations*, 1978; *Machiavelli*, 1981) and Pocock (*The Machiavellian Moment*, 1975) place him in Florentine humanist and republican debate, Pocock following a republican language from Florence through Harrington's *Oceana* (1656) to the American Revolution. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Machiavellian_Moment]

## Worked example: Locke
- *Two Treatises of Government* was published anonymously in December 1689 (title page dated 1690), after the Glorious Revolution; the First Treatise attacks Sir Robert Filmer's *Patriarcha*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Treatises_of_Government]
- Peter Laslett (18 December 1915 – 8 November 2001), in his critical edition (1960), showed the bulk was written during the Exclusion Crisis (1679–81) and revised later, so that "*Two Treatises* is an Exclusion Tract, not a Revolution Pamphlet". Laslett had found much of Locke's library and helped move it to the Bodleian. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Laslett; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Treatises_of_Government]
- Reception: around 200 tracts on the Revolution's legitimacy appeared 1689–94, only three mentioning Locke; the 1691 French translation omitted the First Treatise, and it was in that form that Montesquieu, Voltaire and Rousseau read it; the work was not widely read until the 1760s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Treatises_of_Government (citing Mark Goldie, Laslett)]
- John Dunn's *The Political Thought of John Locke* (1969) argued that Locke's politics were fundamentally theological (duty to the Creator), so that liberal and Marxist readings of him as an ancestor missed his meaning; he later said that theological universe has been lost to a secular age. [search: https://thegreatthinkers.org/locke/commentary/the-political-thought-of-john-locke/; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dunn_(political_theorist)]

## Worked example: the uses of the Enlightenment
- Koselleck (*Critique and Crisis*, 1954/1959) blamed Enlightenment utopian critique for modern ideological "permanent crisis"; Habermas (1962) held up the 18th-century public sphere as a model of deliberative politics. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinhart_Koselleck]
- Pocock wrote of "Enlightenments" in the plural (*Barbarism and Religion*, vol. 1: *The Enlightenments of Edward Gibbon*, 1999). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._G._A._Pocock]
- Liang Qichao and Hu Shih claimed *kaozheng* as China's own scientific/critical tradition — an example of an "Enlightenment" being found, or made, in a non-European past. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaozheng]
- For Kant's 1784 essay, Horkheimer and Adorno (1944), Israel, Pagden and Conrad, see the published Enlightenment chapter (cross-refer; not re-verified here).

## Debates
- Unit-ideas vs context: Lovejoy followed ideas across centuries; Skinner (1969) called this reification and demanded that texts be read in their own debates. Critics of Skinner: Andreas Dorschel (ideas also live in music and architecture, not only words); Herbjørnsrud (contextualism blocks comparison across cultures); Goto-Jones (orientalist neglect of non-Western thought). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_history; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_School_(intellectual_history)]
- Presentism vs contextualism: the Cambridge School rejects reading old texts as answers to our questions; Pocock later conceded contextualists are better at the short-run synchronic picture than at long-run change, and Bernard Bailyn's 1995 "Context in History" lecture (echoed by Gordon Wood) criticised over-strict contextualism. The perennialist/Straussian view holds that great texts address permanent questions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_School_(intellectual_history); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quentin_Skinner]
- Ideas vs material forces: social historians criticised the Cambridge School's focus on ideas; Mark Bevir says Skinner and colleagues defended the history of political theory "against both reductionists who dismissed ideas as mere epiphenomena and canonical theorists who approached texts as timeless philosophical works". Sartori argues the global spread of "culture" was driven by capitalism; Foucault ties knowledge to power. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_School_(intellectual_history); search: Sartori as above]
- Begriffsgeschichte vs Cambridge: Koselleck's long-run semantic history (*Sattelzeit*) vs Skinner's short-run speech acts; Pocock: "confronted, compared, and combined, but not homogenized". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_School_(intellectual_history)]
- Book history: Darnton's symbolic reading of the cat massacre vs Chartier, LaCapra and Mah. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Cat_Massacre]
- Locke: Laslett (Exclusion tract, not 1688 pamphlet); Dunn (a theologically grounded thinker, not a liberal ancestor); disputed influence on the American Revolution (cross-refer American Revolution chapter: Bailyn, Wood, Pocock vs Kramnick, Appleby). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Treatises_of_Government; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Laslett]
- Machiavelli: "teacher of evil" (Strauss) vs republican/humanist contextual readings (Skinner, Pocock); Hans Baron argued he changed his mind between *The Prince* and the *Discourses*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince]
- Human rights chronology: Moyn's 1970s vs older accounts from 1948 or the 18th-century revolutions. [search: Moyn as above]
- *Kaozheng*: empirical revolution (Elman) vs continuity (Yu Ying-shih) vs sterility (Carsun Chang, Xu Fuguan). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaozheng]
- Nahda: shock of 1798 vs autogenetic Ottoman reform. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahda]
- Who counts as an intellectual / the canon: Ginzburg's miller; Timbuktu's scholars; Appiah, Diagne and Pollock widening the canon. [sources above]
