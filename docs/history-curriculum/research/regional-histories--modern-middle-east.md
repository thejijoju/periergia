## 1. Late Ottoman reform: Tanzimat, constitution, Abdülhamid II, Young Turks
- The Edict of Gülhane (Gülhane Hatt-ı Şerifi), proclaimed by Sultan Abdülmecid I in 1839 at the behest of the reformist Mustafa Reşid Pasha (image caption dates it 3 November 1839), launched the Tanzimat ("reorganisation"). It promised security of life, property and honour, fair taxation and conscription, public trials, abolition of tax farming (iltizam), and rights for all subjects regardless of religion. WP notes it made promises rather than legal changes and was never fully implemented. [source: GUL; TANZ]
- Debate on its roots: Butrus Abu-Manneh argued the edict had Islamic roots and no Western influence; Stanford Shaw saw the influence of the 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man. [source: TANZ]
- Context: the 1838 Treaty of Balta Liman with Britain dismantled Ottoman trade monopolies; the Tanzimat built on Mahmud II's reforms. [source: TANZ]
- After the Crimean War, Western pressure produced the Imperial Reform Edict (Hatt-ı Hümayun) of 18 February 1856. [source: GUL] (The Nationality Law of 1869 creating a common Ottoman citizenship is already in the *Nationalism* chapter.)
- Tanzimat measures included: first paper banknotes (1840), new ministries, provincial councils (1845), closure of the Istanbul slave market (1847), first universities/teacher schools (1848), mixed commercial courts as "the first secular legal system" (1850). [source: TANZ]
- The 1876 constitution (Kanun-ı Esasi), drafted chiefly by Midhat Pasha and the Young Ottomans, was in force 1876–78 and 1908–22. Abdülhamid II prorogued parliament on 14 February 1878 after defeat in the Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78. [source: CONST]
- Abdülhamid II (r. 1876–1909) ruled as an autocrat for three decades, stressed his title of caliph (pan-Islamic policy), built a secret police and censorship, but also expanded schools, railways (Hejaz Railway, Baghdad Railway with German help); the empire's finances came under the Ottoman Public Debt Administration after its loan default in the Great Eastern Crisis. The Hamidian massacres of Armenians (1894–96; WP figure "300,000" killed — estimates vary) earned him the name "Red Sultan". Recent assessments stress his promotion of education and public works; his cult revived under the AKP. [source: AH2]
- Young Turk Revolution, July 1908: Committee of Union and Progress (CUP) officers in Macedonia (Ahmed Niyazi, Enver) forced Abdülhamid to restore the constitution; after the counter-revolutionary "31 March incident" (1909) he was deposed for Mehmed V. Bulgaria declared independence and Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia immediately after. [source: YTR]
- CUP (active 1889–1926): began as a liberal reform movement; seized full power in 1913 (Raid on the Sublime Porte, then after Mahmud Şevket Pasha's assassination, June 1913); triumvirate of Talât, Enver and Cemal Pasha; WP: ruled as a dictatorship 1913–18 and carried out genocide of Armenians, Pontic Greeks and Assyrians. Most ex-Unionists later joined Mustafa Kemal's movement; the Republican People's Party continued one-party rule until 1946. [source: CUP]
- (Armenian genocide: already covered in *First World War* (Armenian population "estimated at 1.5 to 2 million") and *Ottomans* ("around one million … by many estimates more" killed) — keep brief and cross-refer.)

## 2. Egypt from Muhammad Ali to the British occupation
- Muhammad Ali (4 March 1769 – 2 August 1849), an Albanian-born Ottoman commander, became governor (wali) of Egypt in 1805 and de facto ruler to 1848; "widely considered the founder of modern Egypt". He massacred the Mamluk leaders at the Cairo Citadel on 1 March 1811; introduced long-staple cotton as a cash crop; conscripted peasants (many fled their villages). He conquered Sudan, fought the Wahhabis in Arabia for the sultan, invaded Syria in 1831 and again 1839–40, threatening Istanbul; European intervention forced withdrawal, and in return his family received hereditary rule over Egypt and Sudan. The dynasty ruled until the 1952 revolution. [source: MA]
- Khedive Isma'il (r. 1863–1879) obtained the title khedive in 1867, invested heavily (WP: about £46 million on irrigation canals), was defeated by Ethiopia at Gundet (1875) and Gura (1876), sold Egypt's Suez Canal shares to Britain in 1875 for £3,976,582, and was removed in 1879 under British and French pressure. [source: ISM]
- The Suez Canal (193.3 km) was built by de Lesseps' company 1859–69 and opened 17 November 1869; it was closed by Egypt from 5 June 1967 for eight years, reopening 5 June 1975. [source: SUEZC]
- The 'Urabi revolt (1879–82), led by Colonel Ahmed 'Urabi, sought to depose Khedive Tewfik and end Anglo-French control; Britain bombarded Alexandria and defeated 'Urabi at Tell el-Kebir (1882), beginning the British occupation. [source: URABI]
- ('Urabi revolt 1882, Cromer's rule 1883–1907, Denshawai 1906, the 1919 revolution, 1922 "independence" and the 1936 treaty are covered in the *Nationalism*, *British Empire* and *Interwar* chapters — keep brief.)

## 3. The Nahda and Islamic modernism
- The Nahda ("Awakening"/"Renaissance") was a cultural movement in Arab regions of the Ottoman Empire, especially Syria, Lebanon, Egypt and Tunisia, in the second half of the 19th and early 20th centuries. Traditional scholarship tied it to the shock of Napoleon's invasion of Egypt (1798) and Muhammad Ali's reforms; more recent scholarship sees it as "autogenetic" as much as Western-inspired, linked to the Tanzimat and the press boom after 1908. [source: NAHDA]
- Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (1838/39 – 9 March 1897): founder of Islamic modernism and pan-Islamic activist; involved, through his follower Mirza Reza Kermani, in the assassination of Naser al-Din Shah (1896). [source: AFG]
- Muhammad Abduh (1849 – 11 July 1905): Egyptian scholar, Grand Mufti of Egypt from 1899, central to the Nahda and Islamic modernism; published *al-'Urwa al-Wuthqa* with al-Afghani; wrote *Risalat al-Tawhid*. [source: ABDUH]

## 4. Qajar Iran, the Constitutional Revolution and Pahlavi Iran
- Qajar Iran, 1789–1925, founded by Agha Mohammad Khan; lost Caucasian territories (eastern Georgia, Dagestan, Azerbaijan, Armenia) to Russia in the early 19th century; the 1907 Anglo-Russian Convention split Iran into Russian and British zones of influence and a neutral zone. [source: QAJ]
- Tobacco Protest: against an 1890 concession by Naser al-Din Shah to Major G. F. Talbot (a British subject) giving control of the growth, sale and export of tobacco; merchants joined clergy; climaxed in Mirza Shirazi's widely obeyed fatwa against tobacco (December 1891). [source: TOB]
- Constitutional Revolution, 1905–11: Mozaffar ad-Din Shah signed the 1906 constitution, creating the Majles; Mohammad Ali Shah bombarded parliament in 1908; constitutionalists retook Tehran and deposed him in 1909; it ended in December 1911 when deputies were expelled "with the support of 12,000 Russian troops". WP calls it "the first of its kind in the Islamic world, earlier than the revolution of the Young Turks in 1908". [source: PCR]
- Oil: William Knox D'Arcy obtained an oil concession in 1901; oil was struck at Masjed Soleyman on 26 May 1908; the Anglo-Persian Oil Company was founded in 1909; the British government bought 51% in 1914; renamed Anglo-Iranian (AIOC) in 1935 and BP in 1954. [source: APOC]
- Reza Khan (1878–1944), Cossack Brigade officer, seized Tehran in February 1921, became prime minister in 1923, and in 1925 the Majles deposed Ahmad Shah and made him Shah, founding the Pahlavi dynasty; he centralised, Persianised and forcibly sedentarised tribes, clashed with clergy, and was forced to abdicate after the Anglo-Soviet invasion of 1941. [source: RS]
- Women and Reza Shah: the "Women's Awakening" (1936–41); the Kashf-e hijab decree (1936) banned the chador in public. [source: RS]
- (Mosaddegh, oil nationalisation 1951 and the 1953 coup are in *Global Cold War* — CIA history says "under CIA direction"; debate over Kashani. Keep brief.)
- The White Revolution was launched by Mohammad Reza Shah on 26 January 1963: land reform, literacy corps, enfranchisement of women, profit-sharing. The referendum recorded 5,598,711 for and 4,115 against (boycotted by opponents). Women voted for the first time in 1963. It aroused clerical opposition led by Khomeini. [source: WR]
- Khomeini denounced the Shah and the US on 26 October 1964 and was exiled (Turkey, then Najaf, Iraq), where his lectures on velayat-e faqih (Guardianship of the Jurist) became *Islamic Government*. [source: KHO]

## 5. The Iranian Revolution (1978–79) and the Iran–Iraq War
- Protests began October 1977; on 19 August 1978 the Cinema Rex fire in Abadan killed "around 400" (WP lead attributes it to Islamic militants; many at the time blamed SAVAK); "Black Friday" (8 September 1978) — clashes in Tehran; WP gives an opposition death toll of 89 for the day. The Shah left on 16 January 1979; Khomeini returned 1 February; the monarchy fell 11 February 1979; a March 1979 referendum approved an Islamic republic (98%); constitution approved 2–3 December 1979. [source: IR; PCR]
- Deaths in the revolution, January 1978 – February 1979: consensus estimates (Tucker) between 532 and 2,781; the regime's figure of 60,000 is rejected as "grossly overstated". [source: IR]
- Repression after: by January 1980 "at least 582" executed; Tucker: 1980–85 between 8,000 and 9,500 executed (UN sources note far higher estimates). [source: IR]
- Hostage crisis: on 4 November 1979 students ("Followers of the Imam's Line") seized the US embassy, taking 66 Americans; 52 were held until 20 January 1981 (minutes after Reagan's inauguration, after the Algiers Accords); the failed rescue (Operation Eagle Claw, 24 April 1980) killed eight US servicemen. It strengthened Khomeini and the theocrats. [source: HOST]
- Ali Shariati (1933–1977), sociologist, offered a revolutionary, progressive reading of Shi'ism; called the "ideologue of the Islamic Revolution", though his ideas did not become the Islamic Republic's foundation. [source: SHAR]
- Iran–Iraq War: Iraq invaded on 22 September 1980; ceasefire under UNSCR 598 effective 20 August 1988. Around 500,000 deaths (WP lead, excluding Anfal); WP also quotes Britannica: casualties (killed and wounded) "range from 1,000,000 to twice that number". Iraq's aims: stop export of Khomeini's revolution, retake the Shatt al-Arab (ceded in the 1975 Algiers Agreement). Iran recaptured its territory by June 1982 and then invaded Iraq. No border changes, no reparations. Iraqi chemical attacks. Iraq was backed by the US, USSR, France, most Arab states; Iran by Syria, Libya, North Korea, China and others (incl. Israel). [source: IIW]

## 6. The First World War's "triple promises" (keep the war itself brief — see *First World War*)
- McMahon–Hussein correspondence: ten letters, July 1915 – March 1916, between Sharif Hussein bin Ali of Mecca and Sir Henry McMahon, British High Commissioner in Egypt. Britain agreed to recognise Arab independence in exchange for an Arab revolt. Excluded: "portions of Syria" lying west of "the districts of Damascus, Homs, Hama and Aleppo" — whether that excluded Palestine has been disputed ever since (British later said yes; Arabs read it as excluding only the Lebanese coast). WP: the main British motive was to counter the Ottoman call to jihad and keep the loyalty of India's ~70 million Muslims. Key letter dated 24 October 1915; George Antonius published the letters in full in *The Arab Awakening* (1938); official publication 1939 (Cmd. 5957). [source: MMC; SP]
- Hussein refused to sign the Versailles treaty or a 1921 British treaty accepting the mandates, saying he could not "affix his name to a document assigning Palestine to the Zionists and Syria to foreigners"; Britain then dropped him in favour of Ibn Saud, who conquered the Hejaz. [source: MMC]
- Sykes–Picot Agreement: secret Anglo-French agreement (Russian and Italian assent); negotiations 23 November 1915 – 3 January 1916 (memorandum initialled by Mark Sykes and François Georges-Picot), ratified 9 and 16 May 1916. Britain: southern Mesopotamia, Haifa and Acre, and what is now Jordan and southern Israel; France: south-east Anatolia, northern Iraq (Mosul), Syria, Lebanon; Palestine (smaller than the later mandate) under "international administration". Russia was promised Constantinople, the Straits and Western Armenia. Published by the Bolsheviks on 23 November 1917 (Manchester Guardian, 26 November). After the war France ceded Palestine and Mosul to Britain. [source: SP]
- Balfour Declaration: letter of 2 November 1917 from Arthur Balfour to Lord Rothschild, published 9 November 1917; "national home" had no precedent in international law and was deliberately vague; the Cabinet drafts involved Zionist and anti-Zionist Jews but no representation of Palestine's Arab majority. The British government acknowledged in 1939 that local wishes should have been considered and in 2018 that it should have protected Palestinian Arabs' political rights. It became part of the Mandate for Palestine. [source: BAL] (Already quoted in *First World War* and *Partition*.)
- Sharif Hussein launched the Arab Revolt in June 1916 (covered in *First World War*, including the historians' qualification that most Arabs stayed loyal).

## 7. The post-war settlement and the mandates
- San Remo conference, 19–26 April 1920; resolution of 25 April 1920 allocated Class "A" mandates for "Palestine", "Syria" and "Mesopotamia", with boundaries to be fixed later. [source: SANR]
- Faisal (1885–1933), proclaimed king by the Syrian National Congress in March 1920, was expelled by France after the Battle of Maysalun (24 July 1920, a four-hour battle west of Damascus) in which his war minister Yusuf al-Azma was killed; Maysalun is remembered across the Arab world as a symbol of resistance. [source: FAI; MAYS]
- Before the war: the Arab Congress met in Paris, 18–23 June 1913 (25 official delegates), seeking more autonomy within the Ottoman Empire, not independence. [source: AC13]
- Cairo Conference, 12–30 March 1921, called by Colonial Secretary Winston Churchill: the "Sharifian Solution" — Faisal king of a new Kingdom of Iraq, his brother Abdullah to administer Transjordan; Lebanon and Syria left to France; Britain keeps Palestine west of the Jordan and support for the Jewish national home; Ibn Saud left in Najd. [source: CAIRO]
- Iraq: the 1920 revolt against the proposed British mandate (Sunnis and Shias together, per *Interwar*) led to a British-sponsored kingdom (Faisal crowned 23 August 1921), the 1922 Anglo-Iraqi Treaty, RAF control; independence and League membership in 1932 (the only mandate independent before WWII — consistent with *Interwar*). WP notes Iraq "had long existed as a distinct region under the Ottoman Empire" (provinces of Mosul, Baghdad, Basra), countering the idea it was a British invention. Iraq Petroleum Company received a 75-year concession. [source: MIRAQ; FAI]
- French Mandate for Syria and Lebanon (League mandate formally 29 September 1923; lasted to 1946): France divided the territory into a State of Syria, Greater Lebanon, an Alawite State, a Jabal Druze State; Hatay (Alexandretta) was annexed by Turkey in 1939; French troops left in 1946. (Great Syrian Revolt 1925–27: *Interwar*; details for the separate *Modern Syria* chapter.) [source: MSL]
- Transjordan: emirate established 11 April 1921 under Abdullah; independent kingdom 25 May 1946 (full independence on exchange of ratifications 17 June 1946); renamed Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan 1950 after annexing the West Bank. [source: TJ]
- Treaty of Sèvres, signed 10 August 1920, never ratified: would have ceded much Ottoman territory and created zones of occupation; provided for an Armenian state and for prosecution of those responsible for the Armenian genocide (see Kurds section for its Kurdish provisions). [source: SEV]

## 8. Atatürk and the Turkish Republic
- Turkish War of Independence, 15 May 1919 (Greek landing at Smyrna/İzmir) – 24 July 1923. Mustafa Kemal (c. 1881 – 10 November 1938) organised a nationalist government in Ankara; victories against Armenia (Treaty of Kars 1921), France in the south, and Greece (Sakarya 1921, the Great Offensive 1922, recapture of İzmir); Armistice of Mudanya 11 October 1922. WP: the nationalist movement also carried out massacres and deportations of Christians; Anatolia's Muslim share rose from 80% to 98%. [source: TWI; SEV]
- Treaty of Lausanne, signed 24 July 1923 (in force 6 August 1924), superseded Sèvres; it included an amnesty for crimes committed 1914–22. [source: LAU]
- Greek–Turkish population exchange: convention signed at Lausanne 30 January 1923; about 1,221,489 Greek Orthodox Christians and 355,000–400,000 Muslims; based on religion, not language; most Greeks had already fled by late 1922. Some scholars call it legalised mutual ethnic cleansing (e.g. Naimark); others defend it. [source: PEX]
- Atatürk's reforms (dates): sultanate abolished 1 November 1922; republic proclaimed 29 October 1923; caliphate abolished and education unified 3 March 1924; Sufi lodges closed and Hat Law 1925 (around 50 sentenced to death over hat protests, Nov 1925 – Feb 1926, per Adanır); Swiss-based Civil Code 1926 (debate on how far it was a mere translation); Islam removed as state religion 1928; Latin alphabet 1 November 1928; women vote in municipal elections 1930 and nationally (law of 5 December 1934); first women judges 1930; Surname Law 21 June 1934. Ideology: Kemalism; motto "Peace at home, peace in the world". One-party rule (CHP) until 1946. [source: AREF; ATA; CUP]
## 9. Saudi Arabia, oil and the making of the Gulf
- Ibn Saud (Abdulaziz Al Saud, 15 January 1876 – 9 November 1953): the family was exiled to Kuwait in the 1890s; he retook Riyadh in 1902; consolidated Najd by 1921; conquered the Hejaz in 1925 (Kingdom of Hejaz and Najd from 1926); proclaimed the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia 23 September 1932; consolidation ended with the Saudi–Yemeni war of 1934. In line with Wahhabi doctrine he had shrines and the al-Baqi cemetery demolished. Casualty claims of 400,000–800,000 for unification are often repeated, but recent research suggests far fewer. [source: IBS; USA]
- Britain signed the Treaty of Darin with Ibn Saud (1915; negotiated via Captain William Shakespear) making his lands a British protectorate; the Uqair conference (1922) set Kuwait–Najd boundaries. The Ikhwan (Wahhabi tribal militia) had been taught that non-Wahhabis were infidels; their revolt was crushed (fighting to 1929). [source: USA]
- Oil: Bahrain Petroleum (a SoCal subsidiary) struck the first Gulf oil outside Iran in 1932; SoCal received a Saudi concession in 1933 (CASOC); commercial oil found at Dammam No. 7 on 3 March 1938; company renamed Aramco 31 January 1944; Exxon (30%) and Mobil (10%) joined in 1947; in 1950 the king's threat to nationalise forced a 50/50 profit split; Saudi government took 25% "participation" in 1973 and 60% in 1974; Saudi Aramco created by royal decree in November 1988. Oil was also discovered in Kuwait in 1938. [source: ARAMCO; D7; KUW]
- OPEC founded 14 September 1960 at Baghdad by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and Venezuela, on the initiative of Saudi Arabia's Abdullah Tariki, Venezuela's Juan Pablo Pérez Alfonzo and Iraq's Abd al-Karim Qasim; it shifted power from the "Seven Sisters" to producing states. WP (current as of 2026) reports the UAE left OPEC and OPEC+ in 2026 — very recent; double-check before use. [source: OPEC]
- (1973 embargo — OAPEC, price from ~$3 to nearly $12; 1979–80 second shock: covered in *Neoliberal Age*. Do not repeat beyond a sentence.)
- Gulf sheikhdoms: Trucial States signed protective treaties with Britain 1820–1892; treaties ended 1 December 1971; next day six emirates formed the UAE; Ras al-Khaimah joined 10 February 1972. Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan was first president 1971–2004. Only about 11% of UAE residents are Emirati citizens. [source: TRU; UAE]

Critics: Michael Herb argues that, controlling for oil's effect, oil states fit the same patterns as others; Luciani himself later criticised the theory's dominance. [source: RENT; web search on Mahdavy/Beblawi–Luciani]

## 10. Arab nationalism, Nasser and the Ba'th
- Precursors: Abd al-Rahman al-Kawakibi (a follower of Abduh) argued the empire should be both Turkish and Arab with Arabs leading religiously; the secret society al-Fatat hosted the Arab Congress of 1913 in Paris; Sati' al-Husri defined the Arab nation by language and sentiment, an idea taken up by the Ba'th. [source: ARNAT]
- Egyptian revolution: Free Officers led by Mohamed Naguib and Gamal Abdel Nasser overthrew King Farouk on 23 July 1952; abolished monarchy, ended British occupation, land reform (1952–53), later "Arab socialism" and a centrally planned economy; one-party rule. [source: E52; NAS]
- Nasser (15 January 1918 – 28 September 1970): after a 1954 Muslim Brotherhood assassination attempt he crushed the Brotherhood and sidelined Naguib; elected president June 1956. The US and UK withdrew Aswan Dam financing on 19 July 1956; on 26 July 1956 he announced nationalisation of the Suez Canal Company to fund the dam. Rogan: "Nasser conquered the Arab world by radio" (Voice of the Arabs). After the 1967 defeat he resigned but was recalled by mass demonstrations; his funeral drew five to six million mourners. Book: *The Philosophy of the Revolution* (1955). [source: NAS] (Suez war itself: *Decolonization*/*Global Cold War* — Egyptian losses 1,650–3,000 soldiers and ~1,000 civilians.)
- United Arab Republic: Egypt–Syria union 1 February 1958 – Syrian secession 28 September 1961; Egypt kept the name until 1971. [source: UAR]
- Nasser's intervention in the North Yemen civil war (from 1962) and the "Arab Cold War" with Saudi Arabia. [source: NAS]
- Ba'th Party: founded 7 April 1947 in Damascus by Michel Aflaq (a Christian, 1910–1989) and Salah al-Din al-Bitar with followers of Zaki al-Arsuzi; motto "Unity, Freedom, Socialism"; merged with Akram al-Hawrani's Arab Socialist movement (1953 per the party article; 1952 per Aflaq's article — discrepancy). Took power in Iraq (Ramadan Revolution, February 1963, briefly) and Syria (March 1963); the 1966 Syrian coup (Salah Jadid, Hafez al-Assad) split the party into Syrian- and Iraqi-led wings. Aflaq called Islam proof of "Arab genius". No Ba'thist state remains after the fall of the Syrian regime on 8 December 2024. [source: BAATH; AFLAQ]
- Iraq, 14 July 1958: Free Officers under Abd al-Karim Qasim and Abd al-Salam Arif overthrew the Hashemite monarchy; King Faisal II and Crown Prince Abd al-Ilah were killed; Nuri al-Said was killed a day later; Iraq left the Baghdad Pact by March 1959. [source: IQ58]
## 11. Israel's founding and the Nakba (cover briefly — see *Partition*)

## 12. The wars of 1967 and 1973, Camp David
- Six-Day War, 5–10 June 1967 (Arabic: *al-Naksa*, "the Setback"). Background: April 1967 Israeli–Syrian air clash; false Soviet reports of an Israeli attack plan against Syria; in mid-May Nasser moved 100,000 troops into Sinai and had UN peacekeepers withdrawn, then closed the Straits of Tiran to Israeli shipping (Israel had said this would be an act of war); Egypt signed a defence pact with Jordan on 30 May. Israel struck first on 5 June (Operation Focus), destroying nearly all Egypt's air force. Israel took Sinai and Gaza (Egypt), the West Bank and East Jerusalem (Jordan), and the Golan Heights (Syria). Deaths: more than 15,000 Arab, fewer than 1,000 Israeli; 34 US personnel died in the USS *Liberty* incident. 280,000–325,000 Palestinians and about 100,000 Syrians fled or were expelled. [source: SDW]
- (Caution: WP quotes Nasser on 26 May 1967 saying "our basic objective will be to destroy Israel"; whether Nasser actually intended war is a long-standing historians' debate — present as contested, do not use the quote without further checking.) [source: SDW]
- Aftermath: Khartoum Arab Summit (September 1967): "no peace, no recognition and no negotiation with Israel" — though Avraham Sela argues Khartoum marked a shift from contesting Israel's legitimacy to contesting territory. UNSC Resolution 242 (22 November 1967), accepted by Egypt and Jordan: "every state in the area to live in peace within secure and recognized boundaries". Suez Canal closed 1967–75. [source: SDW; SUEZC]
- October/Yom Kippur/Ramadan War, 6–25 October 1973: Egypt and Syria attacked on Yom Kippur (10th of Ramadan); Egypt crossed the Suez Canal (Operation Badr); Israel counter-attacked, crossed the canal and threatened the Egyptian Third Army; ceasefires 22 and 25 October; massive US and Soviet resupply. Casualties: Israel 2,521–2,800 dead; Egypt 5,000–15,000 dead (WP infobox; Syrian figures also large). The Arab world felt "psychologically vindicated". [source: YKW] (Oil embargo: *Neoliberal Age*.)
- Sadat (25 December 1918 – 6 October 1981): president from 1970; reintroduced multi-party politics and the *Infitah* (economic opening); visited Jerusalem in 1977 and addressed the Knesset — the first official visit by an Arab leader to Israeli-controlled territory; Camp David Accords signed with Menachem Begin on 17 September 1978 after 12 days of talks hosted by Jimmy Carter; Egypt–Israel peace treaty 26 March 1979 returned Sinai; the Palestinian framework was drafted without Palestinians and condemned by the UN; Egypt suspended from the Arab League 1979–89; Sadat assassinated by militants led by Khalid al-Islambuli at the 6 October 1981 parade. Shared 1978 Nobel Peace Prize with Begin. [source: CDA; SADAT]

## 13. Lebanon
- Independence 22 November 1943 (National Pact power-sharing among confessions). 1958 crisis: US troops landed at President Camille Chamoun's request for about three months. [source: LCW; LEB58]
- Civil war 13 April 1975 – 13 October 1990: began 13 April 1975 when gunmen fired on a church in the Christian suburb of Ain el-Rummaneh, killing four, setting off clashes; Christian militias vs the PLO with Lebanese Muslim, leftist and pan-Arab allies; Syrian, Israeli (invasion of June 1982) and Iranian interventions. Deaths: WP lead "an estimated 120,000"; AP in 1989 reported 150,000 over 14½ years; another source "around 150,000" plus 100,000 permanently handicapped; nearly one million displaced. 1991 general amnesty. [source: LCW]
- Sabra and Shatila, 16–18 September 1982: 1,300–3,500 civilians, mostly Palestinians and Lebanese Shias, killed by the (Christian) Lebanese Forces militia under Elie Hobeika, while the IDF surrounded the camps and fired flares; Israel's Kahan Commission (February 1983) found Israel indirectly responsible and forced Ariel Sharon's resignation as defence minister; the MacBride commission found the IDF bore responsibility as occupying power. Context: Israel's June 1982 invasion to expel the PLO; PLO evacuated by 30 August 1982; President-elect Bashir Gemayel assassinated 14 September. [source: SABRA]
- Taif Agreement, signed 22 October 1989 (ratified 5 November 1989): parliament of 128 seats split 50:50 between Christians and Muslims (previously 6:5 favouring Christians), more power to the Sunni prime minister; Syrian forces were to leave within two years but stayed until 2005. [source: TAIF]
- Hezbollah founded 1982 by Shia clerics with Iranian help (about 1,500 IRGC instructors); 1985 manifesto; blamed for the 1983 US embassy and barracks bombings and the 2005 Hariri assassination; became a major party and armed force. WP reports 2025–26 cabinet decisions to disarm it and a 2026 Lebanon war — very recent; leave to *Modern Conflicts* or check before use. [source: HEZ]

## 14. Palestinians from 1967 to Oslo's collapse (brief; Gaza since 2023 is in *Modern Conflicts*)
- First Intifada began 9 December 1987 at Jabalia camp (Gaza) after a traffic collision killed four Palestinian workers; strikes, boycotts, tax refusal and stone-throwing; Israel deployed some 80,000 soldiers; at least 1,087 Palestinians (240 children) killed by the Israeli army over six years; 100 Israeli civilians and 60 soldiers killed; an estimated 822 Palestinians killed as alleged collaborators (1988–April 1994). Ended with Madrid (1991) or Oslo (1993). [source: INT1]
- Hamas founded 1987 by Sheikh Ahmed Yassin, from his Muslim Brotherhood-affiliated charity (1973); WP says Israel initially discreetly supported it as a counterweight to the PLO; won the 2006 legislative election; seized Gaza from Fatah in 2007; 1988 charter widely described as antisemitic; 2017 charter accepted a state on 1967 lines without recognising Israel (debated whether policy changed). [source: HAMAS]
- Oslo Accords: Declaration of Principles signed 13 September 1993 (Washington); Oslo II 1995 (Taba); mutual recognition of Israel and the PLO; created the Palestinian Authority; no Palestinian state; Edward Said called them a "Palestinian Versailles"; Rabin assassinated by an Israeli extremist in 1995. [source: OSLO]
- Second (al-Aqsa) Intifada, September 2000 – Sharm el-Sheikh summit 2005: after the failed Camp David summit (July 2000) and Ariel Sharon's Temple Mount visit; ~138 suicide bombings after March 2001; about 3,000 Palestinians and 1,000 Israelis killed (infobox: 3,179–3,354 Palestinians; ~1,010 Israelis). [source: INT2]
## 15. Political Islam
- Wahhabism: 18th-century Hanbali reformer Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab; pact with Muhammad bin Saud in 1744 founded the politico-religious alliance of the Saudi states. ("Wahhabism" is an exonym; adherents say Salafi/Muwahhidun.) Since 2022 Saudi Arabia celebrates a Founding Day (22 February) dated to Muhammad bin Saud's accession in 1727, de-emphasising the 1744 pact; some describe a "post-Wahhabi era" under Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman. [source: WAH]
- Muslim Brotherhood: founded in Egypt in 1928 by the schoolteacher Hassan al-Banna (1906 – assassinated by the Egyptian secret police 12 February 1949); slogan "Islam is the solution"; social services plus politics; members assassinated Prime Minister Nokrashy (1948); WP: it remained marginal until the 1967 defeat discredited secular Arab nationalism. Al-Banna saw Islam as a comprehensive system of life and rejected (secular) Arab nationalism. [source: MB; BANNA]
- Sayyid Qutb (1906 – hanged 29 August 1966): *Milestones* (1964), *In the Shade of the Qur'an*; convicted of plotting to assassinate Nasser; seen as an originator of militant Islamism (takfir). The Brotherhood renounced violence in the 1970s. [source: QUTB; MB]
- Egyptian Brotherhood legalised in 2011; Mohamed Morsi won the 2012 presidential election; overthrown by the military in 2013; the group banned as terrorist in Egypt (also Saudi Arabia, UAE). [source: MB] (Arab Spring detail is in *Modern Conflicts*.)
- Grand Mosque seizure, Mecca, 20 November – 4 December 1979: up to 600 militants under Juhayman al-Otaybi, proclaiming a Mahdi; French GIGN advisers helped; 270 died; 68 later executed; afterwards King Khalid gave the ulama more power and the religious police grew more assertive. [source: GMS]
- Iran's revolution (section 5), Hezbollah (1982) and Hamas (1987) belong here too. Shia political Islam vs Sunni Brotherhood: the writer can note that 1979 (Iran, Mecca, Soviet invasion of Afghanistan) is often treated as a turning point.

## 16. The Kurds
- Estimated 30–45 million Kurds in West Asia plus 1–2 million in diaspora; the fourth-largest ethnic group in the Middle East (after Arabs, Persians, Turks); often called "the largest ethnic group without a state". [source: KURDS]
- The 1920 Treaty of Sèvres provided for possible Kurdish autonomy/independence; it was never ratified and Lausanne made no such provision. [source: KURDS; SEV]
- Turkey: Sheikh Said rebellion, February–March 1925, Kurdish nationalist and Islamist (mainly Zaza) against the new secular republic; Robert Olson calls it "the first large-scale nationalist rebellion by the Kurds"; its religious vs national character is debated. Republic of Ararat declared 1927. After the 1980 coup Kurdish was banned in public and private life. [source: SSR; KURDS; PKK]
- PKK founded 27 November 1978; insurgency from 15 August 1984; more than 40,000 dead, most of them Kurdish civilians in Turkey; Öcalan captured 1999; ceasefire 2013–15; ceasefire 1 March 2025 and announced dissolution 12 May 2025 (WP also reports a 2026 Turkish pardon law — recent, check). Both sides accused of atrocities; Turkey depopulated thousands of Kurdish villages. [source: PKK]
- Iran: Republic of Mahabad, 22 January – 15 December 1946, Soviet-backed Kurdish republic. [source: MAH]
- Iraq: Anfal campaign, February–September 1988, under Ali Hassan al-Majid on Saddam's orders; Human Rights Watch (1993) estimated 50,000–100,000 killed and called it genocide; Kurdish leaders cite 182,000 (al-Majid reportedly replied "it couldn't have been more than 100,000"); a 2007 Hague court found the documents insufficient to establish genocide (Iraqi tribunal later did). Halabja, 16 March 1988: chemical attack (mustard gas and nerve agents) killing 3,200–5,000 and injuring 7,000–10,000 — the largest chemical attack on a civilian population; the US DIA initially blamed Iran. After 1991, elections in May 1992 created the Kurdistan Regional Government. [source: ANFAL; HAL; KURDS] (1991 uprising and no-fly zone: *Modern Conflicts*.)
- Syria's Kurds (YPG, Rojava) belong to the *Modern Syria* chapter.

## 17. Women and social change
- Qasim Amin (1863–1908), Egyptian jurist: *The Liberation of Women* (1899), *The New Woman* (1900) argued against veiling, seclusion, early marriage; called one of the Arab world's "first feminists", though his feminism is debated (Leila Ahmed is a noted critic). [source: QA]
- Huda Sha'arawi (23 June 1879 – 12 December 1947) founded the Egyptian Feminist Union in 1923 (first president); returning from the International Woman Suffrage Alliance congress in Rome (1923) she publicly removed her face veil. (EFU founding also in *Interwar*.) [source: HUDA]
- Iran: Kashf-e hijab decree, 8 January 1936, banned the veil, enforced for five years (1936–41); compulsory veiling returned after 1979. Women voted from 1963 (White Revolution). [source: KASHF; WR]
- Turkey: women's municipal vote 1930, national vote 1934; Islamic courts and canon law gave way to a secular code based on the Swiss Civil Code (1926). [source: AREF]
- Saudi Arabia: women were barred from driving until June 2018; dozens of women who drove in Riyadh in 1990 were arrested; campaigns in 2011 (Manal al-Sharif) and 2013; King Salman's order of 26 September 2017; ban lifted 24 June 2018 — while activists such as Loujain al-Hathloul had been detained in May 2018. [source: W2D]
- Iran: protests after the death in morality-police custody of Mahsa Amini (a young woman from Saqqez, arrested 13 September 2022 for wearing her hijab "improperly", died three days later) began 16 September 2022; at least 551 killed incl. 68 minors (to September 2023); about 19,262 arrested; a UN fact-finding mission (February 2024) found crimes against humanity. [source: MAMINI] (The slogan "Woman, Life, Freedom" was not checked in the fetched text — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Pattern for the writer: states imposed women's dress from above in both directions — unveiling in Reza Shah's Iran (1936) and Atatürk's dress laws, compulsory veiling after 1979 — while women's own movements (Sha'arawi's EFU, Saudi drivers, Iranian protesters) pushed from below.

## Debates
- **Said's *Orientalism* (1978) and its critics.** Said (born Jerusalem 1 November 1935 – died 2003) argued Western scholarship on the "Orient" was bound up with imperial power. Bernard Lewis called the thesis "anti-Western" and attacked it in "The Question of Orientalism" (*New York Review of Books*, 24 June 1982), comparing Arab critics of Orientalism to imagined Greeks rejecting classical scholarship; Said replied (NYRB, 12 August 1982) that classics and Orientalism were not comparable because Orientalism served empire. Other critics: Sadiq Jalal al-Azm and Aijaz Ahmad (from the left), Robert Irwin (*For Lust of Knowing*, 2006), Nikki Keddie; Albert Hourani also had reservations. Lewis's essay "The Roots of Muslim Rage" (1990) is credited with the phrase "clash of civilizations" (though he may have used it in 1957), later popularised by Samuel Huntington. [source: ORI; SAID; LEWIS; https://www.nybooks.com/?p=24353; https://somereading.blogspot.com/2012/01/bernard-lewis-question-of-orientalism.html]
- **The "Sykes–Picot" myth.** Popular and jihadist rhetoric (ISIS's 2014 "End of Sykes–Picot" video; al-Baghdadi) blames the 1916 agreement for "artificial" borders. Many historians reply that the agreement was never implemented as drawn: France ceded Mosul and Palestine to Britain after the war; San Remo (1920), Lausanne (1923) and other later agreements set the borders; Henry Laurens says the borders were largely renegotiated 1916–22 and the original map "bears no resemblance" to today's; Aslı Bâli notes some borders followed Ottoman administrative lines or local demands; Sara Pursley ("Lines Drawn on an Empty Map", 2015) argues the "artificial state" story of Iraq is a legend (WP itself notes Iraq existed as an Ottoman region of Mosul, Baghdad and Basra). The counterpoint: the wartime partition logic, and Sykes–Picot as a symbol of betrayal (published by the Bolsheviks 23 November 1917), remain historically real. [source: SP; MIRAQ; web search: https://www.jadaliyya.com/Details/32140; https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2016/05/century-remains-sykes-picot-160516070206616.html; https://www.allazimuth.com/2019/03/29/deconstructing-the-sykes-picot-myth-frontiers-boundaries-borders-and-the-evolution-of-ottoman-territoriality/]
- **Did the McMahon letters include Palestine?** Britain later said Palestine lay "west of Damascus" and was excluded; Arabs read the exclusion as the Lebanese coast only. The dispute "haunted Anglo-Arab relations"; Antonius (1938) made the Arab case public. [source: MMC; SP]
- **Rentier-state theory.** Mahdavy (1970, Iran) and Beblawi & Luciani (*The Rentier State*, 1987, Gulf): oil rents free rulers from taxing citizens, weakening demands for representation and blocking productive development; critics (Michael Herb; later Luciani himself) argue oil states fit general patterns once oil's effect is controlled for. Useful for the Gulf monarchies, Pahlavi Iran, and why the Arab Spring toppled mostly non-oil, non-hereditary regimes (WP Arab Spring). [source: RENT; AS; web search: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rentier_state; https://www.routledge.com/The-Rentier-State/Beblawi-Luciani/p/book/9781138923720]
- **Sectarianism: ancient or modern?** Makdisi (*The Culture of Sectarianism*, 2000) argues sectarianism in 19th-century Mount Lebanon was a modern product of contested Ottoman reform (Tanzimat) and European intervention, not a primordial hatred — nor solely a colonial invention; *Age of Coexistence* (2019) argues "every history of sectarianism is also a history of coexistence", though critics (e.g. Carter Barnett) say it over-generalises. [source: MAKD; web search: https://www.ucpress.edu/books/the-culture-of-sectarianism/paper; https://www.jadaliyya.com/Details/40355; https://notevenpast.org/age-of-coexistence-the-ecumenical-frame-and-the-making-of-the-modern-arab-world-by-ussama-makdisi-2019/]
- **Was the Nahda Western-inspired?** Older scholarship ties it to Napoleon's 1798 invasion and Western influence; newer work sees it as "autogenetic", rooted in the Tanzimat and local changes. Similarly for the Gülhane edict: Abu-Manneh (Islamic roots) vs Shaw (French Declaration of Rights). [source: NAHDA; TANZ]
- **Ottoman "decline" and Abdülhamid II.** The old view of a stagnant "Red Sultan" vs recent assessments of him as the culminator of the Tanzimat (education, railways); the AKP-era revival of his cult as a counterweight to Atatürk. Historians also debate whether the Tanzimat strengthened the state or hastened fragmentation (*Nationalism* chapter). [source: AH2]
- **Gelvin's framework** — the modern Middle East shaped by two global processes, the world economy and the nation-state system — vs Rogan's narrative of Arabs living under others' rules (Ottoman, European, American). Use both to frame the chapter's "patterns". [source: web search: https://international.ucla.edu/institute/article/15140; ROGAN]
- **Explaining 1979.** WP notes the revolution was "unusual" — no war defeat, financial crisis, peasant revolt or disaffected army, in a time of relative prosperity — vs explanations stressing the Shah's repression, dependence on the US/UK, the 1953 coup's legacy and the White Revolution's dislocations. Death toll: 532–2,781 (Tucker) vs the regime's 60,000. [source: IR]
- **1967: who started it?** Israel struck first on 5 June, but Nasser's mobilisation, the UNEF expulsion and the Tiran closure (which Israel had called a casus belli) preceded it; historians debate whether Nasser intended war or was brinkmanship that escaped control (WP gives the casus belli framing). [source: SDW]
- **Population exchange 1923**: "legalised mutual ethnic cleansing" (Naimark, Shelton) vs a defence that it prevented further killing. [source: PEX]
- **Anfal as genocide**: HRW (1993) and the Iraqi High Tribunal said yes; a 2007 Hague court found the documentary evidence insufficient to establish genocide. [source: ANFAL]
- **Kemalist reforms**: liberation vs authoritarian imposition (e.g. ~50 death sentences after the Hat Law protests; Kurdish revolts); Miller disputes that the 1926 Civil Code was simply a translation of the Swiss code; Maksudyan finds racial ideas in 1925–39 Turkish nationalism. [source: AREF]
