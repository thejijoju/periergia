## 1. Definitions and periodisation
- The Victorian era is the reign of Queen Victoria, from 20 June 1837 until her death on 22 January 1901 (some definitions differ slightly). Her reign was 63 years and seven months (QV: 63 years and 216 days), longer than any predecessor's. "Victorian" was used by contemporaries to describe the era. [source: VE; QV]
- Michael Sadleir: "in truth, the Victorian period is three periods, and not one": early Victorian (1837–1850, socially and politically unsettled), mid-Victorian (1851 to the 1870s, prosperity, domestic prudery, complacency), and late Victorian (from 1880, aestheticism and imperialism). G. M. Trevelyan spoke of the "mid-Victorian decades of quiet politics and roaring prosperity". [source: VE]
- Historians call 1850–1870 Britain's "Golden Years": national income per person rose by half. Real wages were 65 per cent higher in 1901 than in 1871. Savings-bank depositors rose from 430,000 (1831) to 5.2 million (1887). [source: VE]
- Britain was called the "workshop of the world". Cooke and Wheatstone's telegraph (1837) spread to every town; within about a decade of Bell's 1876 patent there were 26,000 telephones in Britain. [source: VE]

## 2. Queen Victoria
- Born Alexandrina Victoria at Kensington Palace on 24 May 1819, the only child of the Duke of Kent (fourth son of George III). She was raised under the "Kensington System" devised by her mother and the comptroller Sir John Conroy, and later called her childhood "rather melancholy". [source: QV]
- She turned 18 on 24 May 1837, so no regency was needed. William IV died on 20 June 1837. Diary: "...and consequently that *I* am *Queen*." Under Salic law Hanover passed to her uncle Ernest Augustus, ending the union of the British and Hanoverian crowns begun in 1714. [source: QV]
- Coronation: 28 June 1838, Westminster Abbey; over 400,000 visitors came to London. She was the first sovereign to live at Buckingham Palace. [source: QV]
- She married her first cousin Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha on 10 February 1840 (she proposed on 15 October 1839). They had nine children, who married into European royal houses, hence "grandmother of Europe". [source: QV; VE]
- She used chloroform in childbirth in 1853 (Leopold) and 1857 (Beatrice), despite clerical and medical opposition. [source: QV]
- There were several attempts on her life. Edward Oxford fired twice in 1840; he was found not guilty by reason of insanity. Others: John Francis (1842), John William Bean (1842), William Hamilton (1849), Robert Pate (1850, struck her with a cane), and Roderick Maclean (2 March 1882). After the 1882 attempt she said it was "worth being shot at – to see how much one is loved". [source: QV]
- Albert died on 14 December 1861; Sir William Jenner diagnosed typhoid fever. She wore black for the rest of her life and withdrew from public life, earning the name "widow of Windsor". Republicanism grew. In March 1864 a protester posted a notice on the railings of Buckingham Palace: "these commanding premises to be let or sold in consequence of the late occupant's declining business". After the Prince of Wales recovered from typhoid, a thanksgiving at St Paul's on 27 February 1872 helped republican feeling subside. [source: QV; VE]
- Revolutionary scare of April 1848: the royal family left London for Osborne House (Isle of Wight, bought in 1845). Her first visit to Ireland in 1849 was a public-relations success but had no lasting effect on Irish nationalism. [source: QV]
- Golden Jubilee in 1887 (banquet with 50 kings and princes). Diamond Jubilee procession on 22 June 1897: six miles through London with troops from across the empire. Joseph Chamberlain made it a "Festival of the British Empire"; dominion prime ministers were invited and foreign heads of state were not, partly to avoid inviting Wilhelm II. On 23 September 1896 she passed George III as the longest-reigning British monarch. [source: QV; DJ]
- Diamond Jubilee: a bank holiday in Britain, Ireland and India. Chamberlain spoke of a monarch ruling over 450 million people. Telegraphed message: "From my heart I thank my beloved people. May God bless them." Memorials included the Cunningham Clock Tower in Peshawar. [source: DJ]
- Jubilee in Ireland: unionists celebrated and nationalists largely ignored it. In Dublin, James Connolly's Irish Republican Socialist Party led a mock funeral procession with a coffin marked "British Empire", attended by Maud Gonne and W. B. Yeats. The coffin was thrown into the Liffey and Connolly was arrested. [source: DJ]
- Empress of India: Disraeli pushed the Royal Titles Act 1876 through Parliament. She took the title from 1 May 1876, proclaimed at the Delhi Durbar of 1 January 1877. Gladstone and the Liberals opposed it as despotic and "un-British". Victoria had pressed for the title partly because her daughter, married to the German heir, would otherwise outrank her. [source: QV; RTA]
- Abdul Karim (1863–1909), from near Jhansi, was engaged as a waiter on 23 June 1887 and promoted to "Munshi", teaching her Urdu and acting as clerk. Her household was appalled; she dismissed their complaints as racial prejudice. After her death Edward VII sent him back to India and ordered his correspondence with the Queen destroyed. [source: QV; AK]
- She died at Osborne House at 6:30 pm on 22 January 1901, aged 81, with her son Albert Edward (Edward VII) and grandson Wilhelm II present. State funeral 2 February 1901; burial at Frogmore on 4 February. She was the last monarch of the House of Hanover. [source: QV; DF]
- She kept a journal from 1832, 122 volumes in all; Princess Beatrice transcribed and edited it, burning the originals. Lytton Strachey's *Queen Victoria* (1921) is considered out of date. Longford (1964), Woodham-Smith (1972) and Weintraub (1987) are admired. [source: QV]
- In her later years she was about 5 ft tall. She was unpopular early in her widowhood and well liked in the 1880s–90s, when she embodied the empire as a "benevolent matriarchal figure". [source: QV]

## 3. Politics and the franchise
- **Reform Act 1832** (before the reign; background): defined voters as "male person", formally excluding women. It enfranchised £10 householders in boroughs. Estimates: about 400,000 English voters before and 650,000 after. Rodney Mace estimates 1 per cent of the population before and 7 per cent after. The Scottish electorate went from about 5,000 to 65,000. Rotten boroughs had included Old Sarum and Gatton (7 voters). [source: RA32]
- **Chartism** (1838–1857; strongest in 1839, 1842 and 1848) was a working-class movement named after the People's Charter of 1838. The Charter was drafted by a committee of six MPs and six working men, including William Lovett of the London Working Men's Association. It made six demands: a vote for every man, the secret ballot, no property qualification for MPs, payment of MPs, equal constituencies, and annual parliaments. [source: CH]
- The *Northern Star* was in 1839 the best-selling provincial newspaper in Britain, with a circulation of 50,000, and was often read aloud. It denounced the First Opium War. [source: CH]
- 1839: the first petition, of 1.3 million signatures, was rejected in June. (CUF gives "over a million" and a vote of 235–46.) Newport Rising, 3–4 November 1839: John Frost led several thousand marchers on the Westgate Hotel, Newport. CH says "more than twenty were killed"; CUF says 22. Frost, Zephaniah Williams and William Jones were transported (to Tasmania). [source: CH; CUF]
- 1842: the second petition, of over three million signatures, was rejected in May. Strikes followed in 14 English and 8 Scottish counties. Dorothy Thompson: "1842 was the year in which more energy was hurled against the authorities than in any other of the 19th century". [source: CH]
- 10 April 1848, Kennington Common: the government recruited 100,000 special constables. Feargus O'Connor cancelled the procession and the petition went to Parliament in three cabs. [source: CH]
- 1848 petition signatures: O'Connor claimed 5,706,000. The Commons committee counted about 1.98 million (1,975,496), many plainly forged ("Mr Punch", "No Cheese", and the names of Victoria and Wellington among them). Caveat: the counted figure is the total counted, not the number of fakes. [source: WebSearch: https://www.historyhome.co.uk/peel/chartism/debate48.htm; https://www.historytoday.com/archive/failed-chartist-demonstration-london]
- **William Cuffay** (1788 – July 1870) was a Black Chartist leader. Born in Chatham/Gillingham, Kent, he was the son of an English woman and Chatham Cuffay, a formerly enslaved man from St Kitts. He was a tailor, about 4 ft 11 in tall, joined the Chartist National Executive in 1842, and was one of the organisers of the Kennington Common rally. He was convicted of "conspiring to levy war" against the Queen and transported to Tasmania, arriving 29 November 1849. He was pardoned three years later but stayed and continued organising. The *Times*, *Illustrated London News* and *Standard* used racist language against him. [source: CUF; CH]
- O'Connor represented a significant Irish working-class contribution to Chartism. In 1842 all eighteen of O'Connell's Irish MPs voted for the Chartist petition, which also demanded repeal of the Union. [source: CH]
- Chartist legacy: the secret ballot came in 1872, payment of MPs in 1911, and full manhood suffrage in 1918; annual parliaments never. The 1854 Eureka Stockade miners at Ballarat raised Chartist demands, and Victoria (Australia) held early elections with near-universal male suffrage and the secret ballot. [source: CH]
- **Repeal of the Corn Laws**: tariffs on imported grain, 1815–1846. The Anti-Corn Law League was formed in Manchester in 1839. Peel achieved repeal in 1846 (Importation Act, royal assent 26 June 1846) with Whig support against most of his own party, which split; Peel fell. The Irish famine "forced a resolution". CL says the League played a minor role in the final passage. [source: CL; GFI]
- **Reform Act 1867** (Second Reform Act, Derby/Disraeli): before it, one million of seven million adult men in England and Wales could vote; the Act immediately doubled that. Context: Hyde Park demonstrations (1866 "riots"; a banned May 1867 meeting) and the Reform League. Disraeli accepted amendments that made it more radical than intended ("leap in the dark", *Punch* cartoon). The Conservatives then lost the 1868 election. [source: RA67]
- **Third Reform Act 1884** (Gladstone; royal assent 6 December 1884): extended the household franchise to the counties. Registered voters were 3,040,050 in 1880 and 5,708,030 in 1885. All women and 40 per cent of men were still without the vote. Two in three adult men could vote in England and Wales, three in five in Scotland, one in two in Ireland. [source: RA84]
- Secret ballot 1872; "cities were given greater political autonomy, and the labour movement was legalised". Historian Bruce L. Kinzer describes the reforms as putting the UK "on the path towards becoming a democracy". [source: VE; CH]
- Prime ministers of the reign: Melbourne, Peel, Russell, Derby, Aberdeen, Palmerston, Disraeli, Gladstone, Salisbury, Rosebery. [source: VE infobox]
- Pax Britannica: Britain's only war against another great power in this period was the Crimean War (1853–56). Between 1815 and 1914 about 10 million square miles and roughly 400 million people were added to the empire, and Britain developed an "informal empire" through trade and finance. [source: VE; PAX]

## 4. Poverty, the New Poor Law and the workhouse
- The Poor Law Amendment Act 1834 ("New Poor Law") came from the 1832 Royal Commission (Chadwick, Nassau Senior, John Bird Sumner). Its principles were "less eligibility" (workhouse conditions worse than the worst outside) and the "workhouse test" (relief only inside the workhouse). Critics called the workhouses "Poor Law bastilles". VE says it "had been deliberately designed to punish" the poor and remained the basis of welfare into the 20th century. [source: PLAA; WH; VE]
- New workhouses separated inmates into at least four classes (aged and impotent, children, able-bodied men, able-bodied women), so families were split. An amendment by William Cobbett to forbid separating husband and wife was defeated. [source: WH; PLAA]
- Andover scandal (1845): starving paupers fought over the rotting bones they were supposed to crush, to suck out the marrow. Bone-crushing was withdrawn, and in 1847 the Poor Law Commission was replaced by the Poor Law Board. [source: WH]
- Workhouses were formally abolished on 1 April 1930 (Local Government Act 1929), but many continued as Public Assistance Institutions. The Poor Law's last vestiges went with the National Assistance Act 1948. [source: WH]
- Dickens, *Oliver Twist* (1838): "Please, sir, I want some more." Dickens attacked the separation of couples: "instead of compelling a man to support his family [they] took his family from him, and made him a bachelor!" [source: WH; PLAA]
- Poverty fell from as much as two-thirds of the population in 1800 to under a third by 1901. 1890s studies suggested almost 10 per cent of the urban population lacked enough food to maintain basic physical function. [source: VE]
- Henry Mayhew (1812–1887), co-founder of *Punch* (1841), wrote *Morning Chronicle* articles collected as *London Labour and the London Poor* (3 vols 1851; a 4th volume in 1861). He wrote: "I shall consider the whole of the metropolitan poor under three separate phases, according as they *will* work, they *can't* work, and they *won't* work". [source: MAY]
- Charles Booth (b. Liverpool 1840), a shipowner, began his survey of London in 1886. *Life and Labour of the People* appeared in 1889, with 9 volumes 1892–97 and 17 volumes 1902–03. His researchers included Beatrice Potter (Webb) and Clara Collet. His colour-coded poverty maps ranged from "well-to-do" to "semi-criminal". He argued for old-age pensions ("limited socialism"). Booth's survey found 30.7 per cent of Londoners in poverty. [source: BOOTH; WebSearch: https://londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/how-charles-booth-mapped-london-poverty] (BOOTH's "35% in the East End" is tagged dubious; do not use it.)
- Samuel Smiles, *Self-Help* (1859, self-published): sold 20,000 copies in its first year and over a quarter of a million by his death in 1904. It was called "the bible of mid-Victorian liberalism" and argued that poverty was largely caused by irresponsible habits. [source: SMI]

## 5. Factory reform and working-class organisation
- Factories Act 1847 ("Ten Hours Act"): limited women and young persons (13–18) in textile mills to 10 hours a day. Defective drafting needed the 1850 Act to make it effective. It followed a campaign of almost fifteen years by Richard Oastler, Lord Ashley (later 7th Earl of Shaftesbury), John Doherty and the mill-owner John Fielden; free-trade Liberals such as John Bright opposed it. (The *Industrial Revolution* chapter mentions it; cross-refer.) [source: FA47]
- Children's Employment Commission (Mines) report, 1842: illustrations of child "drawers" pulling coal tubs. [source: VE]
- Harold Perkin: the moral change led to "diminished cruelty to animals, criminals, lunatics, and children (in that order)". [source: VE; VM]
- Elementary Education Act 1870 (Forster's Act): school boards for children aged 5–12 in England and Wales where provision was short. Recent commentators stress that it "brought neither free nor compulsory education". Literacy was nearly universal by 1900. [source: EEA; VE]
- **Matchgirls' strike, 1888**: at Bryant & May's factory in Bow, workers were mostly women and girls, many Irish or of Irish descent. Annie Besant's article in *The Link* (23 June 1888) led management to demand a repudiation; when a worker was dismissed (about 2 July), about 1,400 women and girls struck, and by 6 July the whole factory had stopped. A deputation included Sarah Chapman. Grievances were fines (3d for dirty feet, 5d for lateness, a shilling for a burnt match) and phossy jaw from white phosphorus (mortality in about 20 per cent of cases). The strike won. The Union of Women Matchmakers became the largest union of women and girls in the country. Besant did not start the strike; she "knew nothing of it until the deputation called". [source: MG]
- Earlier, in 1871, up to 10,000 matchmakers marched on Parliament against a proposed match tax, which was withdrawn the day after the march. [source: MG]
- **London dock strike, 1889** (August–14 September): about 100,000 strikers won the "dockers' tanner" (6d an hour). Leaders were Ben Tillett, John Burns, Tom Mann and Will Thorne; Cardinal Manning mediated. It was inspired by the matchgirls and the gasworkers and marks the rise of "New Unionism" of unskilled workers. **Global link:** £30,000 came from Australia, versus £16,075 raised by the British public and unions. Gandhi, then a law student in London, visited Manning to thank him. Caveat: DOCK gives the start as 14 August in its lead and 12 August in its narrative. [source: DOCK; MKG]

## 6. Cities, disease and the sanitary reformers
- Urbanisation: by the 1901 census more than three in four people lived in urban areas, against one in five a century earlier (Soloway: "Great Britain had become the most urbanized country in the West"). Overcrowding of seven or eight to a room was common, and sanitation was inadequate until at least the 1880s. [source: VE]
- Life expectancy rose from about 42 to 55 for women and from 40 to 56 for men; the crude death rate fell only from 20.8 per thousand (1850) to 18.2. [source: VE]
- Edwin Chadwick's *Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population of Great Britain* (begun 1839, published 1842 at his own expense) became the best-selling publication of the Stationery Office to that date. It led to the Public Health Act 1848, "the first instance of the British government taking responsibility for the health of its citizens". [source: CHAD]
- London cholera: 6,536 died in 1831/32; 14,137 in 1848–49; 10,738 in 1853–54; 5,596 in 1866, confined to the East End. [source: GS] (BSC gives 1832 and 1849 combined as 14,137; use GS's breakdown cautiously, or say "tens of thousands".)
- 1854 Broad Street (Soho) outbreak: began 31 August; 127 died in three days and 420 by the end. John Snow, helped by Rev. Henry Whitehead, traced it to the pump. After Snow addressed the St James's parish Board of Guardians on 7 September, the handle was removed the next day. Officials afterwards rejected his theory; miasma theory still dominated. *Vibrio cholerae* was isolated in 1854 but not accepted for decades. [source: BSC] Caveat: GS says Snow himself removed the handle, but BSC quotes Snow saying the Guardians did. Use BSC's version.
- The Great Stink, July–August 1858: the Thames smell prompted Parliament to adopt Joseph Bazalgette's intercepting sewers, built 1859–1875, which ended cholera outbreaks in central London. Michael Faraday's 1855 letter to *The Times*: "the whole river was for the time a real sewer." [source: GS]
- Engels on Manchester and Liverpool death rates (1845): see the *Industrial Revolution* chapter. The English translation (by Florence Kelley) appeared in 1887 (New York) and 1892 (London). [source: ENG]

## 7. Middle class, respectability, domesticity and separate spheres
- A rapidly growing middle class became the dominant cultural influence, "to a significant extent replacing the aristocracy". Home and workplace were separated; the home was a private refuge; romantic love as the basis of marriage gained ground; women were expected to focus on the home and rely on male earners. [source: VE]
- Evangelicalism and utilitarianism were allied in reform. Nonconformists were about half of English churchgoers in 1851. [source: VE]
- Coventry Patmore, *The Angel in the House* (1854, expanded to 1862), was an idealised account of his wife Emily. The phrase came to stand for the self-sacrificing, submissive Victorian wife. Virginia Woolf: "She was intensely sympathetic. She was immensely charming. She was utterly unselfish. ... If there was a chicken, she took the leg; if there was a draught she sat in it ... Above all, she was pure." She also wrote that the Angel "bothered me and wasted my time and so tormented me that at last I killed her". [source: AIH] (Woolf's essay title not confirmed; see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Coverture: on marriage a wife's property passed to her husband, and she could not make a will or sue in her own name. The Married Women's Property Acts of 1870 and 1882 extended married women's rights to own and control property. [source: MWPA; CN]
- Caroline Norton, denied access to her three sons after a failed adultery case against her husband, campaigned (including by letter to the Queen) for the Custody of Infants Act 1839 (mothers' custody up to age seven) and the Matrimonial Causes Act 1857 (divorce). [source: CN]
- The double standard of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1857: a man could divorce for adultery alone; a woman had to prove adultery plus another offence (incest, cruelty, bigamy, desertion), or cruelty alone. [source: VM]
- Contagious Diseases Acts (1864, 1866, 1869): police in garrison and port towns (18 "subjected districts" by 1869) could detain women suspected of prostitution for compulsory genital examination, with internment in lock hospitals for up to a year (1869). Men were not examined. [source: CDA; JB]
- Josephine Butler (1828–1906) co-founded the Ladies National Association (late 1869) after the men's repeal association excluded women. She called the examinations "surgical" or "steel rape". The Acts were repealed in 1886. Her last campaign, in the late 1890s, was against the Acts' continuing application in British India. She worked with W. T. Stead, whose "Maiden Tribute" exposé contributed to the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885, raising the age of consent from 13 to 16. [source: JB; VE]
- Women's suffrage: Barbara Leigh Smith Bodichon's committee gathered nearly 1,500 signatures in 1866. The Manchester Society for Women's Suffrage (February 1867, secretary Lydia Becker). Unmarried women ratepayers got the municipal vote in 1869; by 1900 over a million women were registered for local elections. The NUWSS (Millicent Fawcett) was founded in 1897. Women gained the parliamentary vote in 1918 (over 30, with a property qualification) and on equal terms in 1928. [source: WSUK]
- J. S. Mill moved on 20 May 1867 to replace "man" with "person" in the Reform Bill. It was defeated by 196 to 73 (sources vary by one or two votes). [source: WebSearch: https://historyofparliament.com/2025/03/21/womens-suffrage-petition/; parliament.uk living-heritage pages]
- Mill, *The Subjection of Women* (1869; written by 1861; developed with Harriet Taylor Mill, whose *The Enfranchisement of Women* appeared in 1851): "the legal subordination of one sex to another ... is wrong in itself, and now one of the chief hindrances to human improvement". [source: SOW]

## 8. Morality and its hypocrisies
- Harold Perkin (1969): "Between 1780 and 1850 the English ceased to be one of the most aggressive, brutal, rowdy, outspoken, riotous, cruel and bloodthirsty nations in the world and became one of the most inhibited, polite, orderly, tender-minded, prudish and hypocritical." [source: VM (Perkin, *Origins of Modern English Society*, p. 280)]
- VE notes that, contrary to popular belief, Victorians understood that men and women both enjoyed sex. Chastity was expected of women, with more tolerance for men. Less than 5 per cent of working-class couples lived together before marriage. Male homosexual acts were all outlawed (the Labouchère Amendment, 1885). [source: VE; VM]
- William Acton estimated 8,600 prostitutes in London in 1857. Judith Walkowitz shows prostitution was varied, often short-term, and driven by poverty and the scarcity of women's work. [source: VM]
- Victorian erotica: *My Secret Life* ("Walter") and the magazine *The Pearl*. [source: VM]
- Lytton Strachey, *Eminent Victorians* (9 May 1918), irreverent lives of Manning, Nightingale, Thomas Arnold and Gordon. In 1912 he called the Victorians "a set of mouth bungled hypocrites". Some historians trace the myth of Victorian repression to Strachey. [source: EV; VM]

## 9. Science and the crisis of faith
- William Whewell coined "scientist" in 1833 (VE; CT says the term "did not emerge until 1834"). The Royal Society admitted only professionals from 1847. [source: VE; CT]
- Tennyson, *In Memoriam A.H.H.* (1850), an elegy for Arthur Hallam (d. 1833), written over 17 years, includes "Tho' Nature, red in tooth and claw". It predates Darwin's *Origin* by nine years. [source: IM]
- Darwin received Alfred Russel Wallace's paper from the Maluku Islands on 18 June 1858 and wrote that "all my originality ... will be smashed". The joint papers were read at the Linnean Society on 1 July 1858. [source: OOS]
- *On the Origin of Species* was published on Thursday 24 November 1859 (John Murray), priced at 15 shillings, in 1,250 copies; all available copies were taken up at the trade sale. Mudie's Library took 500. The 2nd edition (3,000 copies, 7 January 1860) added "by the Creator" to the closing sentence. *The Descent of Man* followed in 1871. [source: OOS]
- The Oxford meeting of the British Association: the exchange came on Saturday 30 June 1860 at the Oxford University Museum, after a dull paper by John William Draper, with Henslow in the chair; more than a thousand attended and Darwin was too ill to come. Wilberforce supposedly asked Huxley whether he descended from a monkey through his grandfather or grandmother, but "what Huxley and Wilberforce actually said is uncertain"; no verbatim account exists. Caveat: OXD's lead says 7 July 1860, while its body and all detailed accounts say 30 June. Use 30 June. [source: OXD]
- The "conflict thesis" of an inevitable war between science and religion came from Draper (*History of the Conflict between Religion and Science*, 1874) and A. D. White (*A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom*, 1896). Historians of science now regard it as discredited and favour a "complexity thesis". Note that Draper was the speaker at the 1860 meeting. [source: CT]
- Matthew Arnold, "Dover Beach" (published 1867, probably written 1851): the "Sea of Faith" with "Its melancholy, long, withdrawing roar". [source: DB]
- Secularism and doubt about the Old Testament grew among the more educated. [source: VE]

## 10. Literature as social witness
- Dickens (7 February 1812 – 9 June 1870) left school at 12 to work ten-hour days at Warren's Blacking warehouse while his father was in the Marshalsea debtors' prison. Works: *Oliver Twist* (1838), *A Christmas Carol* (1843), *Bleak House* (1853), *Hard Times* (serialised in *Household Words*, 1 April – 12 August 1854; set in "Coketown", partly based on Preston; Gradgrind: "Now, what I want is Facts"). [source: DICK; HT]
- Dickens's darker side: after 1857 he wished he were commander-in-chief in India so he could "do my utmost to exterminate the Race upon whom the stain of the late cruelties rested". He was criticised for acquiescing in Governor Eyre's Jamaica crackdown and supported the Eyre Defence Committee. [source: DICK; MB]
- Elizabeth Gaskell, *Mary Barton: A Tale of Manchester Life* (18 October 1848), set in Manchester in 1839–42; John Barton turns to Chartism and trade unionism. [source: MBAR]
- Disraeli, *Sybil, or The Two Nations* (1845), on the "Condition of England question". The "two nations" were the privileged few and the deprived working classes. Disraeli later became the figurehead of "one-nation" conservatism. [source: SYB; DIS]

## 11. The Great Exhibition, 1851
- "Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations", Hyde Park, 1 May – 15 October 1851, organised by Henry Cole and Prince Albert. Joseph Paxton's Crystal Palace, of cast iron and glass (1,848 × 454 ft), went from plan to opening in nine months. [source: GE]
- Visitors: 6,039,722 (infobox), "equivalent to a third of the entire population of England and Wales". The one-shilling days made it popular with working people. Thomas Cook brought 150,000. The surplus of £186,000 founded the V&A, the Science Museum and the Natural History Museum ("Albertopolis"). Victoria visited three times with her family and 34 times alone. [source: GE]
- Its aim was to make "clear to the world [Britain's] role as industrial leader". Colonial raw materials had prestige places. The Koh-i-Noor diamond was a star attraction of the India exhibit, alongside Sindhi gold and enamel work. George Jennings's public flush toilets had 827,280 users ("spending a penny"). [source: GE]
- Global note for the writer: the Koh-i-Noor's display is a natural pointer to empire. Its seizure from the Sikh state is not verified here; leave it to the British Empire chapter or verify separately.

## 12. Ireland: the Great Famine and after
- Great Famine (*an Gorta Mór*), 1845–1852; the worst year was 1847 ("Black '47"). Excess deaths range from 775,000 to 1.5 million; the most widely accepted estimate is one million. A million or more emigrated (at least 2.1 million left 1845–1855). The population fell 20–25 per cent between 1841 and 1871. Census figures: 8,175,124 in 1841 and 6,552,385 in 1851. More died of disease than of starvation. [source: GFI]
- Blight (*Phytophthora*; HERB-1 strain, traced to the Toluca Valley, Mexico) reached Ireland in September 1845. One-third to one-half of the 1845 crop was lost. Before the famine, two-thirds of people depended on agriculture and many relied on the Irish Lumper potato. [source: GFI]
- Relief: Peel secretly bought £100,000 of American maize ("Peel's brimstone") and set up public works. Russell's Whig government (from June 1846) relied on the Poor Law, rates and the market. The "Gregory clause" (June 1847) denied relief to anyone holding more than a quarter-acre, driving people off the land; James Donnelly called it "indirectly a death-dealing instrument". [source: GFI]
- Food exports: Woodham-Smith called the export of "huge quantities of food" from Ireland during the starvation the most embittering issue. GFI also notes that at the height of the famine four times as much wheat was imported as exported. [source: GFI]
- Charles Trevelyan, the civil servant most responsible, wrote in 1848 that the famine was "a direct stroke of an all-wise and all-merciful Providence" and "the sharp but effectual remedy by which the cure is likely to be effected". [source: GFI]
- Lord Clarendon (Lord Lieutenant) to Russell, 26 April 1849: "I don't think there is another legislature in Europe that would disregard such suffering as now exists in the west of Ireland, or coldly persist in a policy of extermination." [source: GFI]
- Peter Gray: the government spent £7 million on Irish relief, 1845–50, "less than half of one per cent of the British gross national product over five years". Contemporaries contrasted this with the £20 million paid to West Indian slave-owners. [source: GFI]
- Evictions: police recorded almost 250,000 people officially evicted between 1849 and 1854. Donnelly thinks the true figure, including "voluntary" surrenders, would exceed half a million. [source: GFI]
- Charity from beyond Europe: Calcutta made the first larger donations in 1846 (about £14,000), including from Irish soldiers there. The Choctaw Nation sent $170 in 1847, 16 years after the Trail of Tears (commemorated by the *Kindred Spirits* sculpture, Midleton, 2017). Sultan Abdülmecid reportedly offered £10,000 and was asked to reduce it to £1,000 so as not to exceed the Queen; GFI labels this "according to legend". [source: GFI]
- Victoria gave £2,000 to the British Relief Association (QV: January 1847), more than any other individual donor, yet in Ireland she was called "The Famine Queen". The story that she gave only £5 (and the same to Battersea Dogs Home) is a myth from the late 19th century. [source: QV; GFI]
- Liverpool was at least a quarter Irish-born by 1851. [source: GFI]
- John Mitchel, *The Last Conquest of Ireland (Perhaps)* (1861): "The Almighty, indeed, sent the potato blight, but the English created the Famine." He was transported (to Bermuda) under the Treason Felony Act 1848. The Young Irelander rising of 1848 failed. [source: GFI]

## 13. India: rebellion, Crown rule, famine and Indian voices in Britain
- The rebellion began on 10 May 1857 at Meerut with the mutiny of sepoys. The 81-year-old Mughal Bahadur Shah Zafar was declared Emperor of Hindustan in Delhi. Leaders included Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi and Nana Sahib. It was contained with the rebels' defeat at Gwalior on 20 June 1858. The British estimated about 6,000 of some 40,000 Europeans in India were killed. Probably 800,000 Indians were killed, "and very likely more", including famine and epidemic. [source: IR57]
- Causes: the greased-cartridge rumour (cow and pig fat; the Pattern 1853 Enfield). Kim Wagner argues there is little direct evidence that greased cartridges were widely issued; the belief was what mattered. The doctrine of lapse, land taxes and reforms also played a part. Many Indians fought for the British, notably Sikh princes in the Punjab, and Bengal, Bombay and Madras stayed largely calm. [source: IR57]
- The Bibighar massacre at Cawnpore (five men and 206 women and children had been confined there; 25 died of disease before the killings) and British reprisals: Neill burned villages and hanged their inhabitants, and prisoners were "blown from cannon". The British press demanded revenge and mocked "Clemency Canning". Lurid rape stories (e.g. in *The Times*) were later found to lack convincing evidence by official enquiry; Karl Marx called one such story false propaganda. [source: IR57]
- Government of India Act 1858 (2 August 1858) liquidated the East India Company's rule. India was governed in the Crown's name through a Secretary of State. On 1 November 1858 Victoria's proclamation promised rights similar to other British subjects and, at her insistence, religious toleration in place of a reference to "undermining of native religions and customs". She wrote of "her feelings of horror and regret at the result of this bloody civil war". [source: GOI; IR57; QV]
- The rebellion's name is contested ("Sepoy Mutiny", "Great Rebellion", "First War of Independence"). V. D. Savarkar, *The Indian War of Independence* (1909). (The *Imperial Expansion* chapter mentions the naming debate.) [source: IR57]
- Great Famine of 1876–1878 (southern and western India): 5.6–9.6 million excess deaths, with a recent estimate of 8.2 million. Lytton's government let a record 6.4 million cwt (320,000 tons) of wheat be exported to England. Sir Richard Temple's "Temple wage" of 1 lb of grain plus one anna for a day's hard labour was opposed by W. R. Cornish (who argued for 1.5 lb) and William Digby. Temple: "everything must be subordinated to the financial consideration of disbursing the smallest sum of money." Digby: "a famine can scarcely be said to be adequately controlled which leaves one-fourth of the people dead." [source: IF76]
- Juxtaposition for the writer (both dates verified): the Delhi Durbar proclaiming Victoria Empress of India met on 1 January 1877, during this famine. [source: QV; IF76]
- The same 1877–78 El Niño struck China: the Northern Chinese ("Dingwu") Famine of 1876–1879 killed 9.5–13 million (5.5 million in Shanxi). The British missionary Timothy Richard publicised it. Relief raised abroad, mostly from England and foreign firms, totalled 204,000 taels. Chinese philanthropists in Shanghai circulated the pamphlet "Pictures to Draw Tears from Iron". [source: CF76]
- Dadabhai Naoroji (4 September 1825 – 30 June 1917) was elected Liberal MP for Finsbury Central in 1892 with a majority of 5, the first British Indian MP. (NAO also calls him the second MP of Asian descent, after David Ochterlony Dyce Sombre.) He took the oath on the Khordeh Avesta as a Zoroastrian, spoke on Irish Home Rule and Indian conditions, and lost in 1895. In 1888 Lord Salisbury said of his earlier candidacy: "I doubt if we have yet got to the point where a British constituency will elect a black man to represent them"; the Queen criticised the remark. His drain theory and *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901) are already in the Nationalism chapter. [source: NAO]
- Cornelia Sorabji (1866–1954) was the first woman to study law at Oxford. In 1892, by special decree, she took the Bachelor of Civil Law exam at Somerville, the first woman to do so. [source: SOR]
- Gandhi left for London in August 1888 aged 18 to train at the Inner Temple. He joined the London Vegetarian Society, read the Bhagavad Gita with Theosophists, thanked Cardinal Manning after the 1889 dock strike, and was called to the bar in June 1891. [source: MKG]
- Opium and the Victorian conscience: opium was one of the chief revenues of British India, mostly from exports to China. Under anti-opium pressure, Gladstone's government appointed the Royal Commission on Opium (1893–95; seven British and two Indian members, chaired by Lord Brassey). Its roughly 2,000-page report defended the system, likening opium in Asia to alcohol in Europe. Henry J. Wilson wrote a minute of dissent. (Opium Wars themselves: cross-refer.) [source: RCO]

## 14. The Caribbean, Africa and China in Britain's mirror
- **Morant Bay, Jamaica**, 11 October 1865: Paul Bogle's protest march; militia killed seven, and the courthouse was burned. Under Governor Edward Eyre's martial law, soldiers killed 439 directly and arrested 354, who were later executed, many without proper trials. More than 600 were flogged and thousands of homes burned. George William Gordon, a mixed-race Assembly member, was taken from Kingston to Morant Bay to be tried under martial law and hanged on 23 October. Gordon had said: "If we are to be governed by such a Governor much longer, the people will have to fly to arms and become self-governing." The Jamaica Assembly then gave up its charter and Jamaica became a Crown Colony. [source: MB]
- The **Eyre controversy** split Victorian intellectuals. The Jamaica Committee (December 1865: John Bright, Charles Buxton, Darwin, Huxley, Goldwin Smith, T. H. Green, A. V. Dicey and others) sought to have Eyre prosecuted. The Eyre Defence Committee (August 1866) included Carlyle, Ruskin, Tennyson, Kingsley, Dickens and Tyndall. The grand jury did not indict, and *Phillips v Eyre* went for Eyre. Catherine Hall's *Civilising Subjects* covers this terrain (see Debates). [source: MB; CHALL] (J. S. Mill's chairing of the Jamaica Committee is commonly stated but not in MB; see NOT VERIFIED.)
- **Lancashire Cotton Famine**, 1861–65: overproduction plus the American Civil War blockade threw cotton workers out of work. Mills turned to Indian Surat cotton, and Egypt and Russian Turkestan boomed. On 31 December 1862 a meeting of working people at the Free Trade Hall, Manchester, wrote to Lincoln supporting the Union against "that foul blot on civilisation and Christianity – chattel slavery". Lincoln replied on 19 January 1863, calling their stand "an instance of sublime Christian heroism which has not been surpassed in any age or in any country". Caveat: Confederate flags were also flown in many cotton towns, and many owners and workers resented the blockade, so working-class opinion was not uniform. [source: LCF]
- **Mary Seacole** (23 November 1805, Kingston – 14 May 1881): a Jamaican "Creole" of Scottish and African descent and a healer. She went to the Crimea in 1855 and ran the "British Hotel" near the front. Her *Wonderful Adventures of Mrs. Seacole in Many Lands* (1857) is described as the first autobiography by a Black woman in Britain. Quote: "I have a few shades of deeper brown upon my skin which shows me related – and I am proud of the relationship – to those poor mortals whom you once held enslaved". She was largely forgotten for a century. Her statue at St Thomas' Hospital (30 June 2016) caused controversy among Nightingale's defenders. [source: SEA]
- **Sarah Forbes Bonetta** (born Aina, c. 1843, Oke-Odan, Yorubaland – 15 August 1880): orphaned when Dahomey's army destroyed her town in 1848, she was held at King Gezo's court. Gezo gave her as a diplomatic "gift" to Captain Frederick Forbes in 1850 during his failed mission to end Dahomey's slave trade. She was presented to Victoria, who became her guardian, and married James Pinson Labulo Davies in 1862. [source: SFB]
- **Cetshwayo**, the Zulu king defeated in 1879, visited England in 1882 and had audiences with Victoria. His cause had been taken up by Bishop Colenso and Lady Florence Dixie. [source: CET]
- **Khama III** of the Bamangwato, with Bathoen I and Sebele I, travelled to Britain in 1895 to lobby the Queen against annexation by Rhodes's British South Africa Company, touring evangelical audiences. The campaign succeeded after the Jameson Raid (1896). Neil Parsons's book is cited by KHA (see reading list). [source: KHA]
- **Guo Songtao**, the first Qing minister stationed in a Western country (London 1877–79; also Paris from 1878), visited the Ransomes & Rapier works at Ipswich in July 1877 and admired British railways. His travel record *Shixi jicheng* praised the West and was ordered burned by imperial edict under conservative pressure. Guo retired on his return. [source: GUO]

## 15. Additional context for the end of the era
- In 1900 the Queen visited Ireland for the first time since 1861, partly to thank the Irish regiments fighting in South Africa. [source: QV]
- Within the empire in this period came "the complete abolition of slavery in the African possessions", the end of convict transportation to Australia, and responsible government in some territories (for the British Empire chapter). [source: VE]
- Ireland's population "shrank significantly", while Great Britain's grew rapidly with no catastrophic famine or pandemic, "for the first time in history". [source: VE]

## Debates
- **Was Victorian Britain an imperial society at home? (the Porter–MacKenzie debate).** Bernard Porter, *The Absent-Minded Imperialists* (2004), argued that empire had little influence on ordinary Britons and popular culture, 1800–1940. John MacKenzie (*Propaganda and Empire*, 1984; reply 2008) argued that empire saturated popular culture: "an assemblage of such shards ... implies the existence of a culture, not its absence." Catherine Hall, Antoinette Burton and Jeffrey Richards are counted on MacKenzie's side; Andrew Thompson broadly with Porter (no single "imperial culture"). This debate frames the chapter's global angle. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter%E2%80%93MacKenzie_debate; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Porter]
- **Separate spheres.** Davidoff and Hall's *Family Fortunes* (1987) tied separate spheres to industrialisation and middle-class formation, 1780–1850. Amanda Vickery, "Golden Age to Separate Spheres?" (*Historical Journal* 36:2, 1993, pp. 383–414), questioned both the "golden age" decline story and the chronology and premises of separate spheres. Barbara Welter's "Cult of True Womanhood" is the US analogue. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separate_spheres (Vickery citation); WebSearch: https://reviews.history.ac.uk/review/57/print]
- **Repression or hypocrisy?** Strachey's *Eminent Victorians* (1918) began the image of Victorian hypocrisy. Steven Marcus, *The Other Victorians* (1966), read Victorian sexuality through Acton and pornography. Michel Foucault rejected this "repressive hypothesis", titling a part of his *History of Sexuality* "We 'Other' Victorians". Peter Gay and Michael Mason stress that Victorian reticence was not ignorance. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Marcus; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_morality; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eminent_Victorians]
- **Causes of the moral change:** Asa Briggs stresses reaction against the French Revolution and the evangelical movement; Perkin describes the transformation; Christie Davies links falling crime to moral education such as Sunday schools. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_morality; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_era]
- **Was the Irish Famine genocide?** Most historians reject the claim. Cormac Ó Gráda: "no academic historian continues to take the claim of 'genocide' seriously", while "a case of neglect is easier to sustain". Christine Kinealy: the famine was "neither inevitable nor unavoidable" and the government used it to "facilitate various long-desired changes within Ireland". Some genocide scholars (Robbie McVeigh 2008; Neysa King on the Russell administration, 1846–49) argue otherwise. New Jersey placed it in a Holocaust and Genocide curriculum in 1996. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_(Ireland)]
- **Famine and liberal empire.** Mike Davis blames the 30–60 million late-19th-century famine deaths on laissez-faire and Malthusian ideology. Amartya Sen calls Davis's conclusions overly reductive ("In exploding one myth, we have to be careful not to fall for another") but praises the book. Alexander Morrison criticised Davis's "ignorance of Indian history". Tirthankar Roy argues that better documentation under the Raj does not mean famines became more frequent, and that excess mortality fell by 1915. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Victorian_Holocausts]
- **What was Chartism?** Gareth Stedman Jones's *Languages of Class* (1983, including his "Rethinking Chartism") read it as a political movement in the language of radicalism. Dorothy Thompson's *The Chartists* (1984) re-centred class, the Irish and women. On the 1842 strikes, Mick Jenkins gives a Marxist reading of them as organised and political; how far they were Chartist "was then, as now, a subject of much controversy". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gareth_Stedman_Jones; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Thompson_(historian); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chartism] (The content of "Rethinking Chartism" is summarised from general knowledge of the title only; keep the wording to "language of political radicalism" or omit; see NOT VERIFIED.)
- **Science versus religion.** The Draper–White "conflict thesis" was shaped by episodes like the 1860 Oxford exchange, but historians of science now regard it as discredited and prefer a "complexity thesis". Huxley's famous retort survives only in later, inconsistent accounts. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conflict_thesis; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1860_Oxford_evolution_debate]
- **1857: mutiny or war of independence?** Michael Adas (1971) identifies four approaches: Indian nationalist, Marxist, traditionalist rebellion, and local studies. Savarkar (1909) made the "war of independence" reading. Marx wrote on it in the *New York Tribune*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Rebellion_of_1857]
- **Seacole and Nightingale.** The 2016 statue revived a dispute over Seacole's role and whether she was a "pioneer" of nursing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Seacole]
- **Did the matchgirls make their own strike?** Older accounts credit Annie Besant; Louise Raw (2011) and others stress the women's own organisation. MG states Besant knew nothing until the deputation came. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matchgirls%27_strike]
