## The crisis of 1808 and the collapse of Spanish authority
- The abdications of Bayonne took place on 5, 6 and 7 May 1808 at the castle of Marracq, Bayonne: Napoleon forced Charles IV and his son Ferdinand VII to renounce the Spanish throne in his favour. Charles IV had already abdicated once, on 19 March 1808 (at Aranjuez, under pressure), and later protested that this was forced. On 10 May Napoleon wrote to his brother Joseph (then King of Naples) transferring the Spanish crown to him. [source: BAY]
- News of Bayonne reached Spain in mid-May 1808 and an anti-French rising spread; Joseph I did not reach Madrid until late July 1808. [source: BAY]
- Napoleon also imposed the Bayonne Statute and summoned American representatives to the Assembly of Bayonne, and sent commissioners to the viceroyalties. The Spanish world "almost uniformly rejected" Joseph. [source: SAWI]
- Drawing on traditional contractual theories of monarchy (e.g. Francisco Suárez), Spanish provinces formed juntas; the Junta of Seville claimed authority over the empire. A Supreme Central and Governing Junta of Spain and the Indies was formed 25 September 1808: two representatives per peninsular kingdom but only one each for the overseas viceroyalties/captaincies (New Spain, Peru, New Granada, Buenos Aires, Cuba, Puerto Rico, Guatemala, Chile, Venezuela, Philippines) — criticised in America as unequal. [source: SAWI]
- 1809: short-lived American juntas at Chuquisaca, La Paz and Quito, all suppressed within the year; a junta attempt in New Spain was stopped (in Mexico City the peninsular elite carried out a coup, also in Ferdinand's name). [source: SAWI; GRITO]
- After the Battle of Ocaña the Central Junta fled to Cádiz and dissolved itself on 29 January 1810, handing power to a five-member Regency; this set off a second wave of American juntas in 1810 (e.g. Caracas, 19 April 1810; Buenos Aires, 25 May 1810; Chile, 1810; New Granada). Most claimed to govern in the name of the captive Ferdinand VII. [source: SAWI]
- "There was little interest in outright independence" at first; most supporters of the juntas saw them as a way to preserve autonomy from the French. Few areas declared independence soon after 1810: Venezuela and New Granada in 1811, Paraguay 14–15 May 1811; Río de la Plata waited until 1816 and Chile until 1818. Some historians call the early professions of loyalty the "mask of Ferdinand VII". [source: SAWI]

## The Cortes of Cádiz and the Constitution of 1812
- The General and Extraordinary Cortes opened in Cádiz (under French siege) on 24 September 1810; 104 deputies present, 30 of them for overseas territories (substitutes living in Spain). It was the first national assembly to claim sovereignty in Spain and met as one body representing the whole empire. [source: CORTES; SAWI]
- Of 303 representatives in total, 37 were born overseas (several as substitute deputies elected by American refugees in Cádiz). [source: C1812]
- Constitution ratified/promulgated 19 March 1812 ("La Pepa"); signed by 174 deputies. Affirmed national sovereignty, separation of powers, press freedom, abolished fueros, made Catholicism the sole religion, set suffrage without property qualifications. [source: C1812; CORTES]
- Race and citizenship: American (mostly creole) deputies wanted suffrage for all Indigenous, mixed-race and free Black people — which would have given America a majority in future Cortes; peninsular deputies resisted. Result: Indigenous people were included as citizens, but people of African descent (castas) could obtain citizenship only by naturalisation, and slaves were excluded. Deputies José Miguel Guridi y Alcocer and Agustín Argüelles argued unsuccessfully for abolishing slavery. [source: C1812; ABOL]
- Peninsular deputies largely opposed the federalist ideas of many overseas deputies. [source: C1812]
- Ferdinand VII, restored after the Treaty of Valençay, abolished the Constitution on 4 May 1814 and arrested liberal leaders on 10 May; 69 deputies had signed the "Manifesto of the Persians" urging absolutism. [source: C1812; SAWI]
- The 1812 Constitution served as a model for the Portuguese constitution of 1822 and Mexico's of 1824, and for Norway 1814. [source: C1812]
- 1 January 1820: Rafael Riego led the troops assembled at Cádiz for a new American expedition in revolt, demanding the 1812 Constitution; Ferdinand accepted it on 10 March 1820 (Trienio Liberal). The expedition never sailed; reinforcements never reached America. [source: SAWI]
- Spain's largest force sent to America (1815, under Pablo Morillo): 10,500 troops and nearly 60 ships. [source: SAWI]
- Indian tribute: the Cortes abolished Indian tribute in 1811 (and the mita in 1812); it was re-established after 1815 as an indigenous "contribution". (Per a search summary of Peruvian scholarship; exact Cortes decree date not confirmed.) [source: WebSearch summary citing https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/historica/article/download/1299/1253 and https://dia.upenn.edu/es/content/TL009Tributo]

## Mexico: Hidalgo, Morelos, Iturbide
- Grito de Dolores: around 2:30 a.m. on 16 September 1810, after the discovery of the Querétaro conspiracy (organised by corregidor Miguel Domínguez and his wife Josefa Ortiz de Domínguez with officers Ignacio Allende, Juan Aldama, Mariano Abasolo), parish priest Miguel Hidalgo rang the church bells at Dolores and called his congregation to arms. [source: GRITO; HID]
- At Atotonilco Hidalgo took an image of the Virgin of Guadalupe as his banner. [source: HID]
- Mestizos and Indigenous people joined in such numbers that the creole aims of the Querétaro group were overshadowed; the revolt drew on years of bad harvests in the Bajío. [source: HID; SAWI]
- Alhóndiga de Granaditas, Guanajuato (September 1810): insurgents overwhelmed the granary where peninsulares and creoles had taken refuge and killed an estimated 400–600 people (MWI: "more than 500"). [source: HID; MWI]
- After Monte de las Cruces (30 October 1810) Hidalgo had some 100,000 insurgents near Mexico City but did not attack it. [source: HID]
- At Guadalajara Hidalgo decreed the abolition of slavery (6 December 1810, on pain of death for non-compliance) and of Indigenous tribute. [source: HID; ABOL]
- Defeated by Félix Calleja at Calderón Bridge (January 1811); captured at the Wells of Baján 21 March 1811; defrocked and shot at Chihuahua, 30 July 1811. The heads of Hidalgo, Allende, Aldama and Jiménez hung at the corners of the Alhóndiga for ten years. [source: HID; MWI]
- José María Morelos (30 September 1765 – 22 December 1815), priest, took leadership after Hidalgo; took Acapulco (12 April 1813, except the fort of San Diego); endured a 72-day royalist siege at Cuautla. [source: MOR; MWI]
- Congress of Chilpancingo (Anáhuac) declared independence on 6 November 1813. Morelos's Sentimientos de la Nación (1813), influenced by the Cádiz constitution, made Catholicism the sole religion and prohibited "slavery forever, as [well as] the distinction of caste". He refused the title "Generalissimo" and asked to be called "Siervo de la Nación" (Servant of the Nation). [source: MOR; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentimientos_de_la_Nación]
- Morelos was tried by the Inquisition, defrocked, and executed at San Cristóbal Ecatepec, 22 December 1815. [source: MOR]
- Morelos's ancestry is disputed: his baptismal register classifies him as español, though he is often described as mixed-race or Afro-Mexican (AFROMX and MWI call him mixed-race/Afro-Mexican). Present it as contested. [source: MOR; AFROMX]
- Vicente Guerrero, a former muleteer of African descent (often described as mulatto), led the southern insurgency; Guadalupe Victoria led guerrillas in Puebla. Guerrero objected to the Cádiz constitution's exclusion of Afro-Mexicans from citizenship; he and other Afro-Mexican insurgents joined the Plan of Iguala only once Iturbide accepted equal citizenship. [source: MWI; AFROMX; SAWI]
- Agustín de Iturbide (27 September 1783 – 19 July 1824), royalist officer who had fought the insurgents (he wrote in 1814 of executing 300 rebels to celebrate Good Friday), allied with Guerrero. Plan of Iguala, 24 February 1821 ("Three Guarantees"): religion (Catholicism), independence (constitutional monarchy under Ferdinand VII or another Bourbon), and union/equality of all inhabitants, peninsular and American. [source: ITUR; IGUALA; SAWI]
- Treaty of Córdoba signed 24 August 1821 with the last Spanish chief Juan O'Donojú; the Army of the Three Guarantees entered Mexico City 27 September 1821; independence declared 28 September. Spain's Cortes and Ferdinand rejected the treaty; Iturbide became Emperor Agustín I (Congress conferred the throne 19 May 1822; crowned 21 July 1822); abdicated 19 March 1823; executed on return in July 1824. [source: SAWI; ITUR; GRITO]
- Spain did not recognise Mexican independence until 1836; a last reconquest attempt (Barradas) failed at Tampico in 1829. [source: SAWI; MWI]
- Death toll of the Mexican war: 250,000 to 600,000 total deaths (estimate range in MWI infobox). [source: MWI]
- Central America declared independence 15 September 1821, joined Iturbide's empire January 1822, seceded (except Chiapas) 1 July 1823 as the Federal Republic of Central America, which broke apart by about 1840. [source: SAWI]
- In SAWI's words, "it was the royal army in Mexico that ultimately brought about that nation's independence"; in New Spain and Central America coalitions of conservative and liberal royalists created the new states. [source: SAWI]

## Bolívar and northern South America
- Simón Bolívar: born Caracas 24 July 1783, died Santa Marta 17 December 1830 (tuberculosis). John Lynch described his childhood as "at once privileged and deprived". [source: BOL]
- Cartagena Manifesto, 15 December 1812, written after the fall of Venezuela's First Republic; blamed among other things the federal system (weak in wartime) and the 1812 Caracas earthquake. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartagena_Manifesto]
- Decree of War to the Death, issued during the Admirable Campaign (1813): death to Spaniards not actively aiding the patriots; American-born royalists to be spared. [source: BOL; SAWI]
- Bolívar was dictator of the Second Republic from January 1814; José Tomás Boves (b. Oviedo, Asturias, 1782; killed at Urica, 5 December 1814) raised a royalist llanero army, mixed-race plains people and slaves, partly by attacking the white landowning class, and destroyed the Second Republic (Battle of La Puerta, 15 June 1814). [source: BOL; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José_Tomás_Boves; SAWI]
- Jamaica Letter: written in Kingston (Bolívar lived there May–December 1815), nominally a reply to merchant Henry Cullen, aimed at winning British support; finished 7 September 1815 per Wikipedia (often given as 6 September — see NOT VERIFIED). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaica_Letter]
- Haiti: Alexandre Pétion gave Bolívar sanctuary and material and military support; Bolívar promised to abolish slavery. The expedition left Les Cayes 31 March 1816. Bolívar decreed freedom of slaves on 2 June 1816 (at Carúpano). He returned to Haiti in September 1816 and Pétion helped him a second time. [source: BOL; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_Pétion; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haiti–Venezuela_relations]
- Manuel Piar (b. Willemstad, Curaçao, 28 April 1774), son of a Canarian merchant seaman and a Curaçao woman of partly African descent, described as a "quadroon" who faced colonial racial discrimination; won the Battle of San Félix (11 April 1817); arrested 28 September 1817 (BOL: 27 Sept.), court-martialled and shot at Angostura 16 October 1817. Execution of Piar is a lasting criticism of Bolívar. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Piar; BOL]
- Angostura fell to the patriots 17 July 1817; Congress of Angostura opened 15 February 1819, where Bolívar urged centralised government and racial equality. [source: BOL]
- Páez (13 June 1790 – 6 May 1873) recognised Bolívar's leadership in January 1818 and led llanero cavalry; the llaneros, royalist under Boves, switched to the patriots after 1815. Páez later served three times as president of Venezuela (1830–35, 1839–43, 1861–63) and dominated its politics; died in New York. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José_Antonio_Páez; SAWI; BOL]
- 1819 campaign: Bolívar crossed flooded plains and the Andes (Páramo de Pisba) June–July 1819 with llaneros, New Granadan exiles under Santander, and British volunteers; a quarter of the British Legion died. [source: SAWI; BOL]
- Battle of Boyacá, 7 August 1819: patriots c. 2,850 vs royalists c. 2,670; patriot losses 13 killed, 53 wounded; royalists 100 killed, 150 wounded, 1,600 captured. Bolívar took Bogotá and its treasury. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Boyacá; SAWI]
- Gran Colombia (Republic of Colombia) proclaimed at Angostura 1819; constituted by the Congress of Cúcuta's constitution (1821); comprised today's Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Panama. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gran_Colombia]
- Battle of Carabobo, 24 June 1821: patriots 6,500–8,000 vs royalists 4,000–5,000; patriot losses about 300 killed and wounded including 119 British Legionaries dead; royalists 2,908 killed, wounded and captured. Secured Venezuela (except Puerto Cabello). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Carabobo; SAWI]
- José Prudencio Padilla (b. Riohacha 19 March 1784; executed Bogotá 2 October 1828) won the naval Battle of Lake Maracaibo, 24 July 1823, leading to the royalist capitulation of 3 August 1823. He was executed for conspiracy/treason after the September 1828 crisis, though not involved in the assassination attempt. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José_Prudencio_Padilla; BOL]
- Battle of Pichincha, 24 May 1822 (Sucre): secured Quito for Gran Colombia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Pichincha; BOL]
- Antonio José de Sucre (Cumaná, 3 February 1795 – assassinated at Berruecos, 4 June 1830), "Grand Marshal of Ayacucho", president of Bolivia 1825–1828. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_José_de_Sucre; DGC]

## San Martín, Río de la Plata, Chile, Paraguay, Peru
- May Revolution, 18–25 May 1810, Buenos Aires: an open cabildo (22 May) led to the removal of Viceroy Baltasar Hidalgo de Cisneros and the Primera Junta, 25 May 1810. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Revolution]
- Congress of Tucumán declared independence of the United Provinces on 9 July 1816. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_Declaration_of_Independence]
- Three Buenos Aires campaigns into Upper Peru failed; there guerrilla "republiquetas" allied with rural and Indigenous people held the countryside but never the cities. [source: SAWI]
- Paraguay: Buenos Aires sent Manuel Belgrano to subdue it (1810); defeated at Paraguarí and Tacuarí (early 1811). Paraguayan officers deposed Governor Velasco in May 1811 (14–15 May). José Gaspar Rodríguez de Francia ruled as "Supreme and Perpetual Dictator" (El Supremo) 1814–1840 and isolated the country. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_of_Paraguay; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José_Gaspar_Rodríguez_de_Francia; SAWI]
- José de San Martín (b. Yapeyú, a former Guaraní Jesuit mission, 25 February 1778 [year disputed]; d. Boulogne-sur-Mer 17 August 1850): veteran of the Spanish army in the Peninsular War; governor of Cuyo from 1814, built the Army of the Andes at Mendoza. [source: SM; SAWI]
- Army of the Andes: estimates 3,500–6,000 men plus about 1,200 auxiliaries. San Martín drafted slaves aged 16–30, later extending emancipation-for-service to ages 14–55. Black soldiers formed most of the 7th, 8th and 11th infantry; Wikipedia states they were about two-thirds of the army and estimates 2,000–3,000 freedmen crossed the Andes, citing Lynch's figure of 1,554 freedmen recruits. [source: AOA; SM]
- Chile: Patria Vieja 1810–1814 ended at Rancagua (1–2 October 1814); royalists held Chile 1814–1817. Crossing of the Andes January–February 1817; Battle of Chacabuco, 12 February 1817 (patriots 3,600–4,000; royalist losses c. 500 killed/wounded, 600 captured). Chile declared independence 12 February 1818 (first anniversary of Chacabuco); O'Higgins Supreme Director 1817–1823. Battle of Maipú, 5 April 1818, secured central Chile. Spain recognised Chile in 1844. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilean_War_of_Independence; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Chacabuco; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Maipú; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernardo_O'Higgins]
- Mapuche groups fought on both sides in Chile. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilean_War_of_Independence]
- Thomas Cochrane, ex-Royal Navy, commanded the Chilean navy; took Valdivia and captured the frigate Esmeralda at Callao (1820); later commanded Brazil's navy. [source: SAWI]
- Peru: San Martín's expedition landed at Paracas 7 September 1820 hoping for a Peruvian rising; Viceroy La Serna abandoned Lima; San Martín entered 12 July 1821, independence proclaimed 28 July 1821, San Martín named Protector. He offered an independent monarchy in negotiations. He promised freedom to slaves deserting Peruvian masters, but they did not come in expected numbers. [source: SAWI; SM]
- Guayaquil meeting with Bolívar, 26–27 July 1822, after which San Martín withdrew and went into exile. [source: SAWI; SM]
- Peru remained strongly royalist in part from rivalry with Río de la Plata. [source: SAWI]
- Battle of Junín, 6 August 1824 (cavalry). Battle of Ayacucho, 9 December 1824: patriots 5,780–8,500 under Sucre; patriot losses 979 killed and wounded; royalists c. 2,500 killed and wounded and 2,000–3,000 captured; Viceroy La Serna wounded; Canterac signed the capitulation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Junín; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ayacucho; SAWI]
- Upper Peru declared independent at Chuquisaca (renamed Sucre) on 6 August 1825 and named Bolivia after Bolívar. Last royalist footholds Callao and Chiloé held until 1826. [source: SAWI; BOL]

## Who fought: soldiers of colour, Indigenous peoples, royalists
- Both sides recruited Indigenous communities, castas (pardos, cholos etc.) and even enslaved Africans, promising social improvements; captured soldiers were often absorbed into the enemy army. [source: SAWI]
- Europeans formed only about a tenth of royalist armies in Spanish America. Estimated European share of royalist forces: about a quarter at Maipú, about a fifth at Carabobo, under 1% at Ayacucho. In 1820 Morillo had only 2,000 European soldiers. [source: SAWI]
- Royalist army in Venezuela, 1820: 843 white, 5,378 casta, and 980 Indigenous soldiers. [source: SAWI]
- Mateo Pumacahua (1740–1815), Indigenous noble of Cuzco, led royalist Indigenous militias that helped defeat Túpac Amaru II (1780–81) and won Guaqui (1811) for the crown; in 1814 he joined the Cuzco rebellion demanding full implementation of the 1812 Constitution and was executed (1815). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mateo_Pumacahua]
- Pasto (southern New Granada) was a royalist stronghold blocking the road to Quito; Agustín Agualongo (1780–1824), a mestizo royalist commander, fought on until captured and executed at Popayán, 13 July 1824. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agustín_Agualongo; BOL]
- The Pincheira brothers, a multiethnic royalist band allied with Indigenous groups, held out in Patagonia until 1832. [source: SAWI; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royalist_(Spanish_American_independence)]
- British Legions: volunteer units, many Napoleonic veterans; SAWI gives upwards of 6,000 men, and around 10,000 men from the British Isles serving in South America by 1819; the Irish Legion of John Devereux enlisted about 1,700. [source: SAWI; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Legions]

## Women
- Manuela Sáenz (b. Quito 27 December 1797; d. Paita, Peru, 1856), married English merchant/doctor James Thorne in 1817, Bolívar's companion from 1822; foiled the assassination attempt of 25 September 1828 in Bogotá, after which Bolívar called her "Libertadora del Libertador". Wore a colonel's uniform in public, attended by two Black servants, Jonatás and Nathán. Died in poverty in Paita during a diphtheria epidemic. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuela_Sáenz; BOL]
- Juana Azurduy de Padilla (Chuquisaca, 12 July 1780 – 25 May 1862), mother a chola (mestiza/Indigenous); fought with husband Manuel Ascencio Padilla in the Upper Peru republiquetas; captured Cerro Rico of Potosí briefly (March 1816); made lieutenant colonel by Pueyrredón, 16 August 1816; husband killed after the Battle of La Laguna (1816). Bolívar granted her a pension in 1825; it was revoked in 1857; she died impoverished. Posthumously promoted general of the Argentine Army (2009). In 2015 a statue of her replaced one of Christopher Columbus in Buenos Aires (in front of the Centro Cultural Kirchner); Bolivia's Evo Morales contributed funding. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juana_Azurduy_de_Padilla]
- Policarpa Salavarrieta, "La Pola" (c. 26 January 1795 – 14 November 1817), seamstress from Guaduas, spied for the patriots during the Spanish reconquest of New Granada; shot in Bogotá's main square (now Bolívar Square) with her lover Alejo Sabaraín and others; reportedly berated the soldiers and refused to kneel. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policarpa_Salavarrieta]
- In New Spain, Leona Vicario and Josefa Ortiz de Domínguez acted as conspirators/spies; Javiera Carrera in Chile organised women and is credited with sewing the first Chilean flag. Most women's participation was supportive (supplies, nursing, fundraising, espionage). [source: SAWI]

## Slavery, abolition and free-womb laws
- Haiti declared independence and abolished slavery (1804). [source: ABOL]
- Chile 1811: First National Congress (proposal of Manuel de Salas) declared "freedom of wombs", banned the slave trade; slavery abolished 1823. [source: ABOL]
- Río de la Plata: Assembly of Year XIII passed a free-womb law — children born after 31 January 1813 to be freed at marriage or at 16 (women)/20 (men). Argentina abolished slavery with the 1853 Constitution. [source: ABOL]
- Gran Colombia 1821 (Congress of Cúcuta, José Félix de Restrepo): children born to slave mothers from 21 July 1821 free, plus compensated manumission scheme. New Granada abolished slavery 21 May 1851 (with compensation to owners); Ecuador 1851; Venezuela 1854. [source: ABOL; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Colombia]
- Peru: 1821 slave-trade ban and gradual plan; slave imports re-legalised in the 1830s (Salaverry); slavery and Indigenous tribute abolished by Ramón Castilla in 1854. [source: ABOL; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramón_Castilla]
- Mexico: the 1824 constitution effectively abolished slavery; last slaves freed 1829 under President Vicente Guerrero. Central America also abolished early (SAWI: almost immediately where slavery was not a main labour source — Mexico, Central America, Chile). Bolivia 1831; Uruguay 1830 (also 1842 in some accounts — not checked); Paraguay gradual law 1842. [source: ABOL; SAWI]
- "By the early 1850s, slavery had been abolished in the independent nations of Spanish America" (SAWI's summary; note Venezuela 1854, Peru 1854, Paraguay later). [source: SAWI; ABOL]
- Spanish colonies: Moret Law (free wombs, 1870); Puerto Rico abolished slavery 22 March 1873; Cuba by royal decree 7 October 1886. Brazil: Rio Branco Law (Law of Free Birth) 1871; Lei Áurea signed by Princess Isabel 13 May 1888 — Brazil the last country in the Americas to abolish slavery. [source: ABOL; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro–Puerto_Ricans; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Cuba; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lei_Áurea]
- As part of Portugal's recognition of Brazil (1825), Brazil agreed with Britain to ban the Atlantic slave trade; the 1831 ban was rarely enforced before the 1850 Eusébio de Queirós Law. [source: BRIND; ABOL]
- Rio's slave population increased by about 200% between 1808 and 1822. [source: COURT]

## Brazil
- The Portuguese court sailed from Lisbon 29 November 1807 (embarked 27 Nov.) under British naval escort; arrived Salvador 22 January 1808 and Rio de Janeiro 7 March 1808. Prince Regent João opened Brazil's ports to "friendly nations". [source: COURT]
- 16 December 1815: Brazil raised to a kingdom in the United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil and the Algarves; Rio was the empire's capital for 13 years (a "metropolitan reversal"). João granted 145 titles of nobility 1808–1821. [source: COURT]
- After Portugal's Liberal Revolution of 1820, João VI returned to Lisbon (26 April 1821), leaving his son Pedro as regent. The Lisbon Cortes ordered Pedro home; on 9 January 1822 (Dia do Fico) he refused, after an 8,000-signature petition. [source: COURT; PEDRO]
- 7 September 1822, by the Ipiranga brook near São Paulo: Pedro proclaimed independence ("Independence or death!"), encouraged by José Bonifácio and Princess Leopoldina. Acclaimed emperor 12 October 1822; crowned 1 December 1822. [source: BRIND; PEDRO]
- War against Portuguese garrisons in Bahia, Cisplatina, Piauí, Maranhão, Grão-Pará; Brazil freed slaves to enlist; Cochrane hired to command the navy. Portugal recognised independence by treaty of 29 August 1825, with Brazil paying compensation. [source: BRIND]
- Pedro I abdicated 7 April 1831 in favour of Pedro II. [source: PEDRO]

## Cuba, Puerto Rico, foreign powers
- Cuba, Puerto Rico (and briefly Santo Domingo) remained Spanish until 1898; Caribbean junta plots were denounced early. Spain renounced sovereignty over continental America in 1836, recognising states individually afterward. [source: SAWI]
- Britain: covert support and volunteers; Castlereagh and Canning blocked European aid to Spain. Britain recognised Mexico, Gran Colombia and Río de la Plata in 1825; the USA recognised Chile, Río de la Plata, Peru, Gran Colombia and Mexico in 1822. Portugal claimed to be first to recognise (1821). [source: SAWI]
- Monroe Doctrine: stated by President James Monroe on 2 December 1823 in his annual message, written chiefly by Secretary of State John Quincy Adams; not called the "Monroe Doctrine" until 1850. For years the Royal Navy was in practice its only enforcer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monroe_Doctrine]

## Gran Colombia's failure, the Congress of Panama, caudillos
- Congress of Panama, 22 June – 15 July 1826, convened by Bolívar ("Amphictyonic Congress"): attended by Gran Colombia, Peru, the United Provinces of Central America and Mexico; Chile and Argentina declined from mistrust of Bolívar; Brazil stayed away (Cisplatine War); Paraguay not invited; the US delegation was delayed by Southern congressmen worried by the slavery issue. Its "Treaty of Union, League, and Perpetual Confederation" was ratified only by Gran Colombia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Panama]
- Bolívar's Bolivian constitution (1826) provided a president for life; Bolívar was proclaimed so under it. Bolívar intended to be a life president partly to prevent "pardo" rule (Arana's interpretation). [source: BOL]
- La Cosiata: Páez rebelled against Bogotá on 30 April 1826 (triggered by Santander's 1824 levy); Bolívar amnestied him. Federalist–centralist conflict; failed Convention of Ocaña (1828); assassination attempt 25 September 1828; Santander exiled. [source: DGC; BOL]
- Venezuela's Congress of Valencia (opened 6 May 1830) made Páez president; separation consummated with its constitution, 22 September 1830. Ecuador (Southern District) seceded 13 May 1830 under Juan José Flores. Sucre assassinated 4 June 1830. Bolívar resigned (accepted 27 April 1830) and died 17 December 1830; Gran Colombia formally ended 1831. [source: DGC; BOL]
- The Federal Republic of Central America also collapsed (by ~1840); Argentina did not consolidate politically until the 1860s. [source: SAWI]
- Caudillos: wartime armies did not fully disband and officers sought rewards, producing caudillos. John Lynch: "Before 1810, the caudillo was unknown … The caudillo entered history as a local hero whom larger events promoted to a military chieftain." Examples: Rosas (Argentina), Santa Anna (Mexico), Páez (Venezuela). [source: SAWI; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caudillo]

## What changed and what didn't
- Wars destroyed the civilian bureaucracy (audiencias abolished, peninsular officials and bishops fled); Church weakened, then attacked by liberals. [source: SAWI]
- Intra-regional trade fell below colonial levels; protective barriers ended and foreign textiles undercut local (often Indigenous) producers; new states borrowed abroad and fell into debt; Spanish America entered world markets as exporter of raw materials. [source: SAWI]
- Liberal reforms often hurt Native communities, which had benefited from protections in Spanish law (e.g. corporate land). [source: SAWI]
- Indigenous tribute survived independence under new names: abolished in Peru only in 1854 (Castilla). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramón_Castilla]
- Creole elites retained control while conceding some demands of other groups. [source: SAWI]

## Debates
- Cause or consequence: Jeremy Adelman argues the emergence of Latin American nations "was the effect, and not the cause, of the breakdown of European empires"; colonies abandoned loyalty not mainly because they sought nationhood; Ferdinand VII's repression after 1814 bred revolution. [source: WebSearch summary of Princeton UP description and reviews, https://press.princeton.edu/node/58751]
- Cádiz emphasis: Jaime Rodríguez O. treats independence as part of a Hispanic political revolution centred on Cádiz, with continuity rather than rupture; Brian Hamnett (HAHR 2000) disputes his claim that the Cortes offered American autonomists a peaceful route to home rule. Rodríguez sees the 1812 Constitution as the main model for Mexico's 1824 federal constitution. [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/12318; https://sup.org/books/history/we-are-now-true-spaniards]
- John Lynch's older synthesis stresses creole grievances (Bourbon reforms), and elite fear of social revolution; caudillos are products of the wars. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caudillo; SAWI]
- "Mask of Ferdinand VII": were early junta loyalties sincere or a tactic? [source: SAWI]
- Did Hidalgo invoke Ferdinand VII? Alamán and conservative writers said yes; participant testimonies (Aldama) and historians like Carlos Herrejón suggest no. [source: HID]
- Race and republic: Marixa Lasso argues free pardos actively joined and led the patriot cause in Cartagena; patriots promised legal racial equality, producing a "myth of racial harmony" that made complaints of racism seem unpatriotic and let elites brand pardo movements as Haitian-style race war. Aline Helg asks why race did not become an organising category in Caribbean Colombia, citing class divisions, silence on race and individual/local resistance; she highlights Bolívar's fear of "pardocracia" in the fate of Padilla (executed 1828). [source: https://upittpress.org/?p=86152; https://muse.jhu.edu/book/28812; https://uncpress.org/book/9780807855409/liberty-and-equality-in-caribbean-colombia-1770-1835/]
- Royalism from below: Rebecca Earle argues Spain's royalists inadvertently engineered their own defeat (harsh reconquest 1815–20), and studies the royalist guerrillas of Pasto; Wikipedia's "Royalist" article stresses that royalist forces were mostly Native/American-born. [source: https://gwonline.unc.edu/node/1521; SAWI]
- Popular politics: Sarah Chambers shows honour, a colonial value, became a resource for ordinary Arequipeños resisting republican authorities, and that protection of men's honour came at women's expense. [source: https://www.psupress.org/books/titles/978-0-271-01901-7.html]
- Bolívar's reputation: early criticism (Piar's execution, betrayal of Miranda, authoritarianism) versus the later "cult of Bolívar" promoted by the Venezuelan state from 1842. [source: BOL]
