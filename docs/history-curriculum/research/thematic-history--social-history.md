## 1. What social history is; the phrase "history from below"
- Social history, "often called history from below", is "a field of history that looks at the lived experience of the past". It came to prominence in the 1960s, spreading from British and French schools of thought that held the "Great Man" view of history could not explain how societies changed. [source: SH]
- Charles Tilly's definition of the social historian's tasks: "documenting large structural changes; 2) reconstructing the experiences of ordinary people in the course of those changes; and (3) connecting the two" (as quoted in SH). Useful framing for the chapter. [source: SH]
- G. M. Trevelyan: "[w]ithout social history, economic history is barren and political history unintelligible" (as quoted in SH). The field has been dismissed as "history with the politics left out" and defended as "history with the people put back in" (SH, unattributed phrases — present as common sayings, not as anyone's quotation). [source: SH]
- Origin of the phrase: Georges Lefebvre in 1932 used "histoire vue d'en bas et non d'en haut" (history seen from below and not from above), praising Albert Mathiez for writing "histoire des masses et non de vedettes" (history of the masses and not of stars/celebrities). "People's history" first appeared in a title in A. L. Morton's *A People's History of England* (1938). Thompson's essay "History from Below" in the *Times Literary Supplement* (1966) brought the phrase to the forefront; the title was supplied by an anonymous editor. [source: SH; PH]
- In the US the equivalent is "history from the bottom up" or "people's history", popularly associated with Howard Zinn's *A People's History of the United States* (1980). [source: SH; PH]
- Size of the field: in US universities the share of history professors identifying with social history rose from 31% to 41% between 1975 and 1995, while political historians fell from 40% to 30%. In British and Irish university history departments in 2014, 878 of 3,410 faculty (26%) identified with social history, narrowly ahead of political history (841, 25%). [source: SH]
- Older precedent: W. E. B. Du Bois's *Black Reconstruction in America* (1935) was "rediscovered" in the 1960s as a pioneering work of this kind. [source: SH]
- After 1990, social history was increasingly challenged by cultural history, which emphasises language, beliefs and assumptions. [source: SH]

## 2. The Annales school (France)
- The journal *Annales d'histoire économique et sociale* was founded in **1929** by **Marc Bloch** and **Lucien Febvre**, then teaching at the University of Strasbourg. Successive names: *Annales d'histoire sociale* (1939–42, 1945), *Mélanges d'histoire sociale* (1942–44), *Annales. Economies, sociétés, civilisations* (1946–94), *Annales. Histoire, Sciences Sociales* (1994– ). An English-language edition began in 2013. [source: ANN]
- The Annales rejected the emphasis on politics, diplomacy and war of the "Sorbonnistes" (Febvre's term) and combined geography, history and Durkheimian sociology; aimed at *histoire totale*. Little attention to political, diplomatic or military history or to biographies of famous men. [source: ANN]
- Generations: founders Febvre (1878–1956), Bloch (1886–1944), Henri Hauser; second generation led by Fernand Braudel (1902–1985) with Georges Duby, Pierre Goubert, Robert Mandrou, Pierre Chaunu, Jacques Le Goff, Ernest Labrousse; third generation led by Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie (1929–2023) with Jacques Revel and Philippe Ariès (joined 1978), stressing *mentalités*; fourth generation led by Roger Chartier (born 1945), who moved from *mentalités* to the cultural and linguistic turn and the history of the book and reading. [source: ANN]
- Georges Duby on the history he taught: it "relegated the sensational to the sidelines and was reluctant to give a simple accounting of events, but strove on the contrary to pose and solve problems and, neglecting surface disturbances, to observe the long and medium-term evolution of economy, society and civilisation." [source: ANN]
- **Marc Bloch**: born 6 July 1886; medievalist; *Les Rois thaumaturges* (1924) on the belief that French and English kings could cure scrofula by touch — asked why people believed it, like an anthropologist; *French Rural History* (*Les caractères originaux de l'histoire rurale française*, 1931); first volume of *Feudal Society* autumn 1939. Jewish; served in both world wars (mobilised again 24 August 1939, aged 53); evacuated from Dunkirk 1940 and returned to France; joined the Franc-Tireur Resistance movement in Lyon after November 1942; captured by the Gestapo in 1944 and shot with 27 others near Saint-Didier-de-Formans on the night of **16 June 1944**. *The Historian's Craft* (*Apologie pour l'histoire*, 1949) and *Strange Defeat* (*L'Étrange défaite*, 1946) were posthumous. Epitaph: *dilexi veritatem* ("I have loved the truth"). [source: MB]
- **Lucien Febvre**: born Nancy 22 July 1878, died 11 September 1956; thesis on Philip II and the Franche-Comté (MB/LF: published 1911; ANN says 1912 — say "1911–12" or omit the year); *Le problème de l'incroyance au 16e siècle: la religion de Rabelais* (1942; English *The Problem of Unbelief in the Sixteenth Century*, 1982) — a classic of the history of mentalities (argues what it was possible to think in an era). [source: LF; ANN]
- *Mentalités* = "the ways in which historical people thought about, interacted with, and classified the world around them"; origin in Bloch, taken up by Duby, Mandrou and Chartier. Critics: Marxists said it relied on faith over reason; Ginzburg criticised its "decidedly classless character". [source: MEN]
- **Fernand Braudel** (24 August 1902 – 27 November 1985): taught in Algeria (1923–32) and at the new University of São Paulo (1935–37); began his thesis on Philip II's Mediterranean with archive work at Simancas in 1927. Taken prisoner by the Germans on 29 June 1940; drafted *La Méditerranée* in POW camps in Mainz and (from June 1942) near Lübeck, largely from memory, sending notebooks to Febvre. He later called the long view partly an "existential" reaction to the war and the camp. Defended the thesis 1947; *La Méditerranée et le monde méditerranéen à l'époque de Philippe II* published **1949** (2nd revised ed. 1966). Head of the EPHE Sixth Section after Febvre's death (1956); editor of *Annales* (FB: 1957; LD: 1956 — say "from the mid-1950s"). [source: FB; LD]
- Braudel's tiers of time: short-term *événements* (the "evental history", *histoire événementielle*, a term from François Simiand), medium-term *conjonctures* (decades/centuries of economic and social cycles) and the *longue durée* — slow, almost permanent structures (geography, climate, mental frameworks). His programmatic article: "Histoire et sciences sociales: La longue durée" (*Annales*, **1958**). Example from *La Méditerranée* vol. 1: the tension between mountain dwellers and plain dwellers as a feature of Mediterranean history over thousands of years. [source: LD] (Terminology caveat: see Conflicts section.)
- *Civilisation matérielle, économie et capitalisme, XVe–XVIIIe siècle* (*Civilization and Capitalism, 15th–18th Century*): vol. 1 1967 (English 1973), completed 1979 in three volumes; a history of the pre-industrial world "focusing on how regular people made economies work", with thick description of food, fashion and everyday custom — **good link for the seed's "food, housing" coverage**. Vol. 1 later titled *The Structures of Everyday Life*. [source: FB; LD] (Vol. 1 English subtitle not verified in a fetched source — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- In a 2011 *History Today* poll Braudel was named the most important historian of the previous 60 years, Thompson second. [source: EPT]
- Annales critics: Marxists called them conservatives for stressing inertia over revolution; by 1980 postmodernism undercut confidence in metanarratives; Jacques Revel: no longer "any implicit consensus on which to base the unity of the social". [source: ANN]
- Global reach: well received in Italy and Poland (Franciszek Bujak, Jan Rutkowski, journal founded 1931); in Venezuela (Federico Brito Figueroa from the 1950s), Spain (Jaume Vicens Vives from 1950), and Mexico (Spanish Republican exiles at El Colegio de México); influenced Immanuel Wallerstein's world-systems theory. British historians, apart from a few Marxists, were "generally hostile". [source: ANN]
- Regional studies: Le Roy Ladurie's *The Peasants of Languedoc*; Pierre Goubert on Beauvais (1960). [source: ANN]
- Bloch quotation (verified via quote box in MB, citing Bloch 1963 p. 39): see Quotations.

## 3. Worked example: *Montaillou* (Annales "historical anthropology")
- Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie, *Montaillou, village occitan de 1294 à 1324* (Gallimard, **1975**; English trans. Barbara Bray, 1978). Reconstructs a Pyrenean village of about 250 people from the **Fournier Register** — interrogations by the inquisitor Jacques Fournier (later Pope Benedict XII — not in source; omit) during the campaign against Catharism in the Ariège, **1318–1325**. Part 1: material life (the Clergue brothers, the shepherd Pierre Maury); Part 2: *mentalité*. Sold more than 250,000 copies; seen as a precursor to microhistory and linked to oral history (interrogation records as voices). [source: MON]

## 4. British Marxist historians and "agency from below"
- The **Communist Party Historians' Group** formally existed from **1946** (informal roots at Cambridge in the 1930s under the economist Maurice Dobb); heyday 1946–56; members included Christopher Hill, Eric Hobsbawm, Rodney Hilton, Dona Torr, E. P. Thompson, Raphael Samuel, A. L. Morton. In **1952** members founded the journal *Past and Present*. Most left the Communist Party after the Soviet invasion of Hungary in 1956; Hobsbawm stayed. Their dictum, paraphrasing Marx, that men make their own history but not in conditions of their own choosing. Their techniques influenced feminist historians and the Subaltern Studies group. [source: CPHG; EPT]
- **E. P. Thompson** (Edward Palmer Thompson, 3 February 1924 – 28 August 1993): born Oxford to Methodist missionary parents; served in a tank unit in Italy; left the Communist Party in 1956; founded *The New Reasoner* (1957), merged into *New Left Review* (1960); taught at Warwick until 1971; later a leading figure of European Nuclear Disarmament (1980 Appeal). Married historian Dorothy Towers (Dorothy Thompson), historian of women in Chartism, in 1948. [source: EPT]
- ***The Making of the English Working Class*** (Victor Gollancz, **1963**; revised Pelican edition 1968; about 848 pages in paperback) — English artisan and working-class society "in its formative years 1780 to 1832"; covers the London Corresponding Society, Luddism, Methodism; Thompson uses "working class" (singular) to stress the growth of consciousness. Placed 30th in the Modern Library 100 Best Nonfiction of the 20th century. [source: MEWC]
- Preface claim (verified wording in MEWC): "in the years between 1780 and 1832 most English working people came to feel an identity of interests as between themselves, and as against other men whose interests are different from (and usually opposed to) theirs". [source: MEWC]
- Reception: Sidney Pollard: "without a doubt, a landmark in English historiography"; David Eastwood: it "transformed the study of labour, class and political radicalism in Britain and America and is incontestably the single most influential work of history of the post-war period". Geoffrey Best's critique: Thompson neglected the "flag-saluting, foreigner-hating, peer-respecting side of the plebeian mind". Joan Scott argued the book was androcentric, treating class as male; Sheila Rowbotham replied that the critique was ahistorical for a 1963 book. [source: MEWC; EPT]
- Other Thompson works for crime/custom: "The moral economy of the English crowd in the eighteenth century", *Past & Present* 50 (**1971**) — food riots as defence of customary norms; "Time, work-discipline and industrial capitalism", *Past & Present* 38 (1967); *Whigs and Hunters: The Origin of the Black Act* (1975) on the 1723 Waltham Black Act; *Albion's Fatal Tree: Crime and Society in Eighteenth-Century England* (1975, with Douglas Hay, Peter Linebaugh and others); *The Poverty of Theory* (1978) attacking Althusser's structural Marxism. [source: EPT; PLB]
- **Eric Hobsbawm** (9 June 1917, Alexandria – 1 October 2012): Birkbeck from 1947; *Primitive Rebels* (1959; US title *Social Bandits and Primitive Rebels*, 1960) and *Bandits* (1969) — "social banditry" as a historically explicable form of rebellion; *Captain Swing* (1969, with George Rudé) on the English agricultural uprising of 1830; the "Age of" tetralogy; *The Invention of Tradition* (1983, with Terence Ranger). [source: EH; GR; TR] (Captain Swing first-publication year 1969 is from memory; GR lists only a 1985 Penguin edition — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- **Christopher Hill** (6 February 1912 – 23 February 2003): Master of Balliol 1965–78; *The World Turned Upside Down: Radical Ideas During the English Revolution* (**1972**) — Diggers, Ranters and other radicals of the 1640s–50s. [source: CH]
- **George Rudé**: *The Crowd in the French Revolution* (first-edition year not confirmed — GR lists an OUP 1967 printing; London PhD thesis on the subject 1950) and *The Crowd in History, 1730–1848* (1964): put "faces in the crowd", arguing rioters were "ordinary, sober citizens, not half-crazed animals, not criminals" (GR's paraphrase/quotation of Rudé). Rudé taught in Adelaide, Flinders and Montreal; wrote on protesters transported to Australia. [source: GR]
- **History Workshop**: Raphael Samuel (26 December 1934 – 9 December 1996) taught at Ruskin College (the trade-union college in Oxford) from 1962 and founded the History Workshop movement there (Workshops from the late 1960s; flourished until the mid-1980s); co-founders/leaders included Sheila Rowbotham, Sally Alexander, Anna Davin. *History Workshop Journal* founded **1976**, subtitled "A Journal of Socialist Historians", then "...of Socialist and Feminist Historians", subtitle dropped 1994. Samuel: history should be "a collaborative enterprise" in which researcher, archivist, curator, teacher, "do-it-yourself" enthusiast, local historian and family history societies are "equally engaged" (quoted in HWJ). The movement inspired History Workshops in Europe, South Africa and America. [source: RS; HWJ; SR]
- Sheila Rowbotham (born 1943), *Hidden from History* (1973) and *Women, Resistance and Revolution* (1972); helped organise the first National Women's Liberation Conference (1970). [source: SR]

## 5. Quantitative and demographic history; family and childhood
- Social history was "revolutionized in the 1960s" by quantitative and demographic methods using individual data from censuses and local registers of births, marriages, deaths and taxes. [source: SH]
- **Family reconstitution**: transcribing parish registers of christenings, marriages and burials and rebuilding them into family histories, then linking tax, land and probate records. Pioneered by the French demographer **Louis Henry** (1911–1991): with Michel Fleury, *Des registres paroissiaux à l'histoire de la population* (**1956**); by 1959 proposed reconstituting France's population 1670–1829. [source: OPS]
- **Cambridge Group for the History of Population and Social Structure (CAMPOP)**: co-founded **1964** by **Peter Laslett** and **Tony (E. A.) Wrigley**; worked with amateur volunteers on local records; founded the journal *Local Population Studies*; integrated into Cambridge's Geography Department in 2001. [source: PL; OPS; CAMPOP]
- Peter Laslett (18 December 1915 – 8 November 2001): *The World We Have Lost: England Before the Industrial Age* (**1965**; 3rd ed. retitled *The World We Have Lost: Further Explored*, 1983); began historical demography from 17th-century listings of the inhabitants of Clayworth and Cogenhoe; edited *Household and Family in Past Time* (1972); helped found the Open University and the University of the Third Age (1982). [source: PL]
- Wrigley (born Manchester 17 August 1931; died 2022) and Roger Schofield, *The Population History of England, 1541–1871: A Reconstruction* (**1981**); Wrigley, Davies, Oeppen and Schofield, *English Population History from Family Reconstitution 1580–1837* (TW lists a 2005 CUP printing; give no year). [source: TW]
- Lawrence Stone, *The Family, Sex and Marriage in England, 1500–1800* (1977) used quantitative methods for family life; his 1979 *Past and Present* essay "The Revival of Narrative: Reflections on a New Old History" diagnosed a turn back from social-science history to narrative. [source: LS]
- Louise Tilly and Joan Scott, *Women, Work, and Family* (1978): women's wage labour in France and England, linking production and reproduction — joined labour and family history. [source: SH; JWS]
- History of the family emerged as a separate field in the 1970s, especially in the US and Canada. [source: SH]
- **Childhood — Philippe Ariès**, *L'enfant et la vie familiale sous l'ancien régime* (**1960**; English *Centuries of Childhood*, 1962): argued childhood as a separate stage of life was a modern idea (not understood as such until about the 15th century), using medieval paintings showing children as small adults. Heavily criticised: Geoffrey Elton (children were dressed as adults only for portraits); Hugh Cunningham notes the English translation rendered *sentiment* ("feeling") as "idea", producing the famous (mis)line "in medieval society the idea of childhood did not exist"; Nicholas Orme's *Medieval Children* refuted the thesis ("medieval children were ourselves, five hundred or a thousand years ago"). **Good worked example of a social-history thesis tested and overturned.** [source: CC]
- **Cliometrics** ("new economic history"): began with Conrad and Meyer, *The Economics of Slavery in the Antebellum South* (1958); term coined 1960 by Stanley Reiter; Robert Fogel and Douglass North won the 1993 Nobel economics prize "for having renewed research in economic history". [source: CLIO]
- Fogel and Stanley Engerman, ***Time on the Cross*** (1974): argued US slavery was efficient and that slaves' material conditions were better than presumed (field hands received "approximately ninety percent of the income produced"). Fierce criticism; Thomas Haskell (1975) said it now "appears at least to be severely flawed". Herbert Gutman (1928–1985) answered with *Slavery and the Numbers Game* (1975 — title/year not in fetched page) and ***The Black Family in Slavery and Freedom, 1750–1925*** (1976/1977 — HG infobox says 1977; see NOT VERIFIED). **Good debate example: numbers vs. experience.** [source: TOC; HG]
- Paul E. Johnson's recollection of 1960s UCLA training: "literary" evidence was deemed "inherently elitist and untrustworthy"; "One well-known quantifier said that anyone who did not know statistics at least through multiple regression should not hold a job in a history department." (quoted in SH) [source: SH]
- Social Science History Association founded 1976; *Journal of Social History* edited since 1967 by Peter Stearns. [source: SH]

## 6. Italian microhistory and its relatives
- Microhistory "asks large questions in small places" (Charles Joyner's phrase, as quoted in MH). It became popular in **Italy in the 1970s**; pioneers Carlo Ginzburg, Giovanni Levi, Edoardo Grendi, Carlo Poni (MH spells "Carlo Ponti" — wrong link; use "Carlo Poni" only if verified; safer to name Ginzburg, Levi, Grendi); journal *Quaderni Storici* (founded 1966); Einaudi's *Microstorie* series (Ginzburg first heard the term around 1977). A reaction against grand narratives and social-science generalisations; it treats gaps in the sources as part of the story and the researcher's point of view as part of the account. Since the 2010s, "global microhistory". [source: MH]
- Earlier uses of the word: George R. Stewart's *Pickett's Charge: A Microhistory...* (1959); Braudel (pejoratively); and the Mexican historian **Luis González y González**, *Pueblo en vilo: Microhistoria de San José de Gracia* (**1968**) — a Latin American pioneer. [source: MH]
- **Carlo Ginzburg** (born Turin 15 April 1939; **died Bologna 17 June 2026**, aged 87): son of the novelist Natalia Ginzburg and of Leone Ginzburg, an antifascist murdered by the Gestapo in 1944; PhD Pisa 1961; taught at Bologna, UCLA (1988–2006) and the Scuola Normale, Pisa. *The Night Battles* (1966) on the *benandanti* of Friuli; *Ecstasies* (1989). In 1979 he wrote to Pope John Paul II asking that the Inquisition archives be opened; they formally opened to qualified researchers in January 1998, and Cardinal Ratzinger credited his letter. [source: CG; death confirmed by AP-based reports and UCLA History, https://history.ucla.edu/2026/06/19/professor-emeritus-carlo-ginzburg-passes-away/ and https://aarome.org/news/features/memoriam-carlo-ginzburg]
- ***The Cheese and the Worms*** (*Il formaggio e i vermi*, Einaudi, **1976**; English Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1980): Domenico Scandella, called **Menocchio** (1532–1599), a miller of Montereale in Friuli, tried twice by the Roman Inquisition and **burned at the stake** as a heresiarch. Ginzburg found the case in 1963 while researching witch trials, returned to the documents in 1970. Menocchio had read books including a vernacular Bible and *Il Fioretto della Bibbia*; believed the world arose from chaos as cheese from milk, with worms that were the angels (full quotation below). Ginzburg's argument (drawing on Bakhtin): peasant and learned culture formed "a circular relationship composed of reciprocal influences, which traveled from low to high as well as from high to low". Called "probably the most popular and widely read work of microhistory". [source: CW; MH] (Menocchio trial documents: 1583–1599, per the Del Col edition cited in CW.)
- **Giovanni Levi** (born 29 April 1939): professor emeritus of economic history, Ca' Foscari Venice; *L'eredità immateriale. Carriera di un esorcista nel Piemonte del Seicento* (Einaudi, Microstorie series, **1985**; English *Inheriting Power: The Story of an Exorcist*, University of Chicago Press, 1988, trans. Lydia G. Cochrane) — the village of Santena, Piedmont, in the late 17th century; essay "On Microhistory" (1991/1992). [source: GL; WebSearch results e.g. https://iris.unive.it/retrieve/e4239ddb-4ec1-7180-e053-3705fe0a3322/Microstoria_II_Bozze.pdf]
- **Natalie Zemon Davis** (8 November 1928, Detroit – 21 October 2023, Toronto): Princeton 1978–96; co-founded with Jill Ker Conway one of North America's first women's history courses (Toronto, 1971); *Society and Culture in Early Modern France* (1975 — artisans and peasants, carnivals, religious violence); *Fiction in the Archives: Pardon Tales and their Tellers in 16th-century France* (1987 — court pardon pleas as storytelling; **use for "crime from below"**); *Women on the Margins* (1995 — Glikl of Hameln, Marie de l'Incarnation, Maria Sibylla Merian); *Trickster Travels* (2006 — al-Hasan al-Wazzan / Leo Africanus; Davis's turn to a North African, Muslim subject); unfinished *Braided Histories* on 18th-century Suriname. Second woman president of the AHA; Holberg Prize 2010. Used judicial records, notarial records, tax rolls, pamphlets, folk tales. [source: NZD]
- ***The Return of Martin Guerre***: Davis was historical consultant on Daniel Vigne's film *Le Retour de Martin Guerre* (released 14 May 1982; Gérard Depardieu, Nathalie Baye); her book first appeared in French in 1982 and in English in **1983**; translated into 22 languages. The case: Martin Guerre, a peasant of **Artigat** in southwestern France, left his wife Bertrande de Rols; years later Arnaud du Tilh ("Pansette") took his place and was accepted; accused by Martin's uncle, tried before the Parlement of Toulouse (judge Jean de Coras); the real Martin returned; Arnaud was executed. Davis argued Bertrande was an active accomplice, not a dupe. [source: RMGb; RMGf; NZD]
- Debate: Robert Finlay, "The Refashioning of Martin Guerre", and Davis's reply "On the Lame", *American Historical Review* 93:3 (June **1988**). Finlay: Bertrande was deceived; Davis's evidence was weak and her reading anachronistic; he extended the charge to the new social history's methods. Davis: her sources are circumstantial "signs", the evidence allows "complex possibilities". [source: WebSearch, e.g. https://blogs.ubc.ca/history548/files/2015/09/Finlay-Martin-Guerre.pdf ; RMGb ("criticized for making claims without adequate evidence")]
- **Robert Darnton** (born 1939), *The Great Cat Massacre and Other Episodes in French Cultural History* (**1984**): Paris printing apprentices on the Rue Saint-Séverin in the late 1730s, fed worse than their master's wife's cats, staged a mock trial and hanging of cats; Darnton, influenced by Clifford Geertz's "thick description", reads the joke as worker protest — the point being that what is not funny to us opens an alien mentality. Translated into 18 languages. [source: GCM; MEN; RD]
- Laurel Thatcher Ulrich, ***A Midwife's Tale: The Life of Martha Ballard, Based on Her Diary, 1785–1812*** (Knopf, **1990**): built on a diary long dismissed as repetitive and ordinary; Pulitzer Prize for History and Bancroft Prize, 1991. (MT text says "1989"; publisher records and press give 1990.) [source: MT; WebSearch https://www.neh.gov/project/midwifes-tale]

## 7. Women's and gender history
- Women's history "exploded into prominence in the 1970s", led by second-wave feminist historians in the US and Britain. Joan Kelly criticised early social history as too focused on male experience; her essay "Did Women Have a Renaissance?" (**1977**) asked whether the periodisation applied to women at all. [source: SH; GH]
- **Joan Wallach Scott** (born 18 December 1941), "**Gender: A Useful Category of Historical Analysis**", *American Historical Review* 91:5 (December **1986**), pp. 1053–1075; reprinted in *Gender and the Politics of History* (1988); "undoubtedly one of the most widely read and cited articles in the journal's history". Her two-part definition (paraphrase only — exact wording not seen): gender is based on perceived differences between the sexes, and is also a primary way of signifying relationships of power. William Sewell called the second part "important and contentious". Scott argued gender explains not only women's history but all history. Trained as a labour historian (*The Glassworkers of Carmaux*, 1974); IAS Princeton from 1985. [source: JWS; GUC; SH]
- History of masculinity emerged as a specialty in the 1990s. [source: GH]
- Non-Western example: Chen Dongyuan's *Zhongguo funü shenghuo shi* (*History of the Lives of Chinese Women*), written 1928, published by the Commercial Press 1937 — the first systematic Chinese women's history; framed women's history as a history of abuse, in the spirit of the New Culture Movement. [source: WH]
- Gail Hershatter, *The Gender of Memory: Rural Women and China's Collective Past* (University of California Press, **2011**): with Shaanxi-born collaborator Gao Xiaoxian, ten years of interviews with 72 elderly rural women in Shaanxi about the 1950s–60s. [source: WebSearch https://muse.jhu.edu/book/25936 ; https://chinafile.com/node/269]
- Saidiya Hartman: "critical fabulation" (from her essay "Venus in Two Acts") — combining archival research, critical theory and fictional narrative to address silences in the archive of Atlantic slavery about enslaved women; *Wayward Lives, Beautiful Experiments: Intimate Histories of Social Upheaval* (**2019**); MacArthur Fellow 2019. Useful for the frontier (and controversy) of recovering lives the archive omits. [source: SHart]

## 8. Oral history and other "archives of the inarticulate"
- WPA Federal Writers' Project slave narratives: interviews of formerly enslaved people **1936–1938** (infobox; text says 1936–1939), assembled 1941; about **2,300** first-person accounts, more than 10,000 typed pages, in 17 states; mostly white interviewers, rendered in phonetic dialect — John Blassingame warned of "a simplistic and distorted view of the plantation"; George Rawick published them as *The American Slave: A Composite Autobiography* from 1972 (eventually 41 volumes), which "revolutionized" slavery studies. [source: WPA]
- Columbia University's Oral History Research Office founded **1948** by Allan Nevins — one of the earliest academic oral history programmes (nearly 8,000 taped memoirs). Oral History Association (US) 1967; Oral History Society (UK) 1969. [source: OH]
- **Alessandro Portelli**: oral history "tells us less about events as such than about their meaning ... the unique and precious element which oral sources force upon the historian ... is the speaker's subjectivity" (quoted in OH). Compared workers' experiences in Harlan County, Kentucky, and Terni, Italy. [source: OH]
- Supreme Court of Canada, *Delgamuukw v. British Columbia* (**1997**): in Aboriginal title claims, oral histories as important as written testimony. [source: OH]
- The 1947 Partition Archive founded 2010 by Guneeta Singh Bhalla (Berkeley) to record Partition survivors; Chinese Association of Oral History Studies founded December 2004; Uzbekistan collectivisation project (Kamp and Zanca, 2003–04) uncovered famine stories absent from the state archive. [source: OH]
- **Jan Vansina** (14 September 1929 – 8 February 2017): pioneer of method for African oral tradition; *Oral Tradition* (1965 English edition; French original *De la tradition orale* 1961 — French year not in fetched page) and *Oral Tradition as History* (1985); taught at Wisconsin. [source: JV]
- Mass-Observation (Britain): founded 1937 by Tom Harrisson, Charles Madge and Humphrey Jennings; about 500 volunteer diarists and paid observers recording everyday life (Bolton = "Worktown"); revived 1981 at the University of Sussex. **Useful for "leisure and everyday life"**. [source: MO]
- Studs Terkel's oral histories, e.g. *Hard Times* (Great Depression) and *"The Good War"* (1985 Pulitzer for General Nonfiction). [source: ST]
- Court and Inquisition records as sources: Fournier Register (Montaillou); Menocchio's trials; Davis's pardon tales; the **Old Bailey Online** (proceedings 1674–1913; DH sentence carries a citation-needed tag). [source: MON; CW; NZD; DH]

## 9. Crime and punishment from below
- Thompson's *Whigs and Hunters* (1975) and *Albion's Fatal Tree* (1975; Douglas Hay, Peter Linebaugh, E. P. Thompson et al.) — eighteenth-century English criminal law as an instrument of class rule and the "crimes" of the poor (poaching, wrecking) as customary rights. [source: EPT; PLB]
- Peter Linebaugh, *The London Hanged: Crime and Civil Society in the Eighteenth Century* (1991); with Marcus Rediker, *The Many-Headed Hydra: Sailors, Slaves, Commoners, and the Hidden History of the Revolutionary Atlantic* (2000/2001 — PLB lists Beacon 2001; US first edition commonly given as 2000; say 2000). [source: PLB]
- Michel Foucault, *Surveiller et punir: Naissance de la prison* (Gallimard **1975**; English *Discipline and Punish*, 1977): opens by contrasting the public torture of Robert-François Damiens (attempted regicide, 1757 — year from memory; PLB/DP say "mid-18th century") with the timetable of an early 19th-century prison (Mettray); argues the prison rose not simply from humanitarian reform but from new "disciplines" of power. [source: DP]
- Rudé's crowd studies and Hobsbawm's social bandits (above) belong here too.
- Global example: van Onselen's *The Small Matter of a Horse: The Life of 'Nongoloza' Mathebula, 1867–1948* (1984) — a South African bandit-gang leader. [source: CVO]

## 10. The cultural and linguistic turn, and its critics
- The "linguistic turn": from the 1970s, emphasis on language and discourse as constituting social reality rather than reflecting it; term popularised by Richard Rorty's anthology *The Linguistic Turn* (1967); Foucault on discourse and power; Hayden White, *Metahistory* (1973). [source: LT]
- In social history: Joan Scott and William Sewell emphasised "the constitutive role of language and discourse"; Gareth Stedman Jones, *Languages of Class: Studies in English Working Class History, 1832–1982* (Cambridge, **1983**) (after *Outcast London*, 1971). [source: SH; GSJ]
- Germany: the Bielefeld School (Hans-Ulrich Wehler, 1931–2014; Jürgen Kocka) built *Gesellschaftsgeschichte* (history of society) using modernisation theory in the 1960s–70s; from the 1980s criticised by the cultural turn for reducing individuals to structures. Kocka's recollection: social history "gave priority to ... classes and movements, urbanization and industrialization, family and education, work and leisure, mobility, inequality, conflicts and revolutions. It stressed structures and processes over actors and events." [source: SH]
- Critics: Bryan D. Palmer, *Descent into Discourse: The Reification of Language and the Writing of Social History* (Temple University Press, **1990**), argued from historical materialism that poststructuralism's reliance on discourse obscures oppression and resistance; quoted: "There is nothing to be gained by claiming that everything is discourse." Elizabeth Fox-Genovese and Eugene Genovese complained that post-Marxist social history became concerned with "the bedrooms, bathrooms, and kitchens of each one's favourite victims". [source: WebSearch https://www.chronicle.com/article/historians-debate-the-discourse-theory/ ; SH]
- Lawrence Stone, "The Revival of Narrative" (1979) — see §5. [source: LS]

## 11. Non-Western social history

### South Asia: Subaltern Studies
- "Subaltern" comes from Antonio Gramsci (1891–1937); Guha used it first for peasants not integrated into industrial capitalism; later any group of inferior rank by class, caste, gender, race, religion. [source: SS]
- **Ranajit Guha** (23 May 1923, Siddhakati, Bengal – 28 April 2023, Purkersdorf near Vienna, aged 99): taught at Sussex (Reader from 1962) and ANU (1981–88); *A Rule of Property for Bengal* (1963); ***Elementary Aspects of Peasant Insurgency in Colonial India*** (Oxford University Press, Delhi, **1983**). Edited *Subaltern Studies* I (**1982**, "Writings on South Asian History and Society", OUP Delhi) through III (1984); the series ran to vol. 12 (2005). Group began at Sussex; influenced by Eric Stokes and Guha. [source: RG; SS]
- Their target: both colonialist and nationalist (including traditional Marxist) histories that focused on elite consciousness inspiring the masses. [source: SS]
- Spivak (born 24 February 1942; Columbia University), "**Can the Subaltern Speak?**" in Cary Nelson and Lawrence Grossberg (eds), *Marxism and the Interpretation of Culture* (University of Illinois Press, **1988**), pp. 271–313; discusses sati and the 1926 suicide in North Calcutta of the young Bhubaneswari Bhaduri (who, Spivak argues, timed it during menstruation so it could not be read as illicit pregnancy; a letter later revealed she belonged to an armed independence group) — her act was still read as illicit love: the subaltern woman, even when she "speaks", is not heard. Spivak later called her own conclusion "the subaltern cannot speak" an "inadvisable remark" (in the 1999 revision, *A Critique of Postcolonial Reason*). Spivak co-edited *Selected Subaltern Studies* with Guha (1988). [source: GCS; SUB; WebSearch https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.ucsc.edu/dist/1/400/files/2015/01/spivak-critique-of-postcolonial-reason.pdf]
- **Dipesh Chakrabarty** (born 1948, Kolkata; University of Chicago): *Rethinking Working-Class History: Bengal 1890–1940* (1989; DC lists it as "Rethinking Working Class History (1989)"; subtitle not verified); ***Provincializing Europe: Postcolonial Thought and Historical Difference*** (**2000**); "The Climate of History: Four Theses" (2009); Toynbee Prize 2014. [source: DC]
- Critics: Sumit Sarkar, "Decline of the Subaltern in Subaltern Studies" (turn to Foucauldian discourse abandoned the empirical, Marxist early volumes); Vivek Chibber, *Postcolonial Theory and the Specter of Capital* (2013 — year from memory) — excised class exploitation, re-created the "Orient" as culturally exceptional. [source: SS]

### Africa
- **Ibadan School** (Nigeria, 1950s–1970s): Kenneth Dike (1917–83), London PhD, first African professor of history at University College Ibadan; *Trade and Politics in the Niger Delta 1830–1885*; promoted oral evidence; with J. F. Ade Ajayi, Saburi Biobaku, E. A. Ayandele, Obaro Ikime, Tekena Tamuno and others. Nationalist and largely **political** history, but modern in sources (oral history, interdisciplinarity). Declined in the 1970s; social, economic and cultural history rose (e.g. the Lagos school), plus a neo-Marxist school and Ahmadu Bello University's "Islamic Legitimist" school. **Caveat: Ibadan was a pioneer of African-centred history and oral sources rather than "social history" in the Thompson sense.** [source: IB]
- **Dar es Salaam school** (Tanzania, 1960s–70s): historians working under Terence Ranger at University College, Dar es Salaam; emblem: Isaria Kimambo and Arnold Temu (eds), *A History of Tanzania* (1969); stressed African agency and initiative; later charged with naïve celebration of nationalist governments (Ranger replied the school's commitment was to African agency). (Search-only evidence; no Wikipedia page.) [source: WebSearch https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/critical-examination-of-isaria-kimambos-ideas-through-time/3560C33D6278CAF8BBE56686A181A038 ; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/187886]
- **Terence Ranger** (1929–2015): deported from Southern Rhodesia in 1963, moved to Dar es Salaam (colleagues John Iliffe, John Lonsdale); *Revolt in Southern Rhodesia, 1896–97* (1967); *Peasant Consciousness and Guerrilla War in Zimbabwe* (1985); *The Invention of Tradition* (1983, with Hobsbawm); *Voices from the Rocks* (1999). [source: TR]
- **Charles van Onselen** (born 14 August 1944, Boksburg; University of Pretoria): *Chibaro: African Mine Labour in Southern Rhodesia, 1900–1933* (Pluto, **1976**); *Studies in the Social and Economic History of the Witwatersrand, 1886–1914* (Ravan, **1982**; vols *New Babylon* and *New Nineveh*); ***The Seed is Mine: The Life of Kas Maine, a South African Sharecropper, 1894–1985*** (James Currey/Oxford, **1996**; Alan Paton Award 1997) — a whole life reconstructed largely from years of interviews (oral method from memory; CVO describes it as the effect of South Africa's land laws on one man and his family). The History Workshop movement inspired a South African History Workshop (HWJ); the Wits History Workshop link to van Onselen is from memory — see NOT VERIFIED. [source: CVO; HWJ]
- **Frederick Cooper** (BA Stanford 1969, PhD Yale 1974; NYU): *Plantation Slavery on the East Coast of Africa* (1977); *Decolonization and African Society: The Labor Question in French and British Africa* (1996); *Africa since 1940: The Past of the Present* (2002; the "gatekeeper state"); *Colonialism in Question* (2005); with Jane Burbank, *Empires in World History* (2010). Began as a historian of East African labour. [source: FC]

### Latin America
- Gilberto Freyre (1900–1987), *Casa-Grande & Senzala* (**1933**; English *The Masters and the Slaves*) — early social history of Brazilian plantation domestic life; now criticised for portraying slavery as mild and consolidating the myth of "racial democracy". [source: GF]
- Luis González y González, *Pueblo en vilo* (1968) — Mexican microhistory (see §6). [source: MH]
- Florencia Mallon (born Santiago, Chile; Wisconsin), ***Peasant and Nation: The Making of Postcolonial Mexico and Peru*** (University of California Press, **1995**) — peasant political cultures in Puebla/Morelos (against the French intervention) and Junín/Cajamarca (against the Chilean invasion); Bryce Wood Award. Mallon, "The Promise and Dilemma of Subaltern Studies: Perspectives from Latin American History", *AHR* 99:5 (**1994**), pp. 1491–1515. [source: FM; WebSearch https://www.ucpress.edu/books/peasant-and-nation/paper ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_American_subaltern_studies]
- Latin American Subaltern Studies Group: founded **1992** by John Beverley and Ileana Rodríguez; "Founding Statement" in *boundary 2* 20:3 (1993); mostly literary scholars (with historian Patricia Seed); folded over scholarly and political differences; *Latin American Subaltern Studies Reader* (Duke, 2001). [source: LASS]

### China and East/Southeast Asia
- **Fei Xiaotong** (1910–2005): studied under Malinowski at LSE 1936–38; *Peasant Life in China* (1939) from fieldwork in Kaixiangong village (Jiangsu); *From the Soil* (*Xiangtu Zhongguo*; FX gives 1948 — commonly dated 1947; say late 1940s). A foundational village study used by social historians. [source: FX]
- **Philip C. C. Huang** (UCLA 1966–2004; founding editor of *Modern China*): *The Peasant Economy and Social Change in North China* (Stanford, **1985**); *The Peasant Family and Rural Development in the Yangzi Delta, 1350–1988* (Stanford, **1990**) — "involution": population pressure drove labour into ever more intensive, low-return work, with no rise in output per workday until the 1980s rural industries; later a critic of Pomeranz's *Great Divergence* (2002). [source: PCH; WebSearch https://sup.org/books/cite/?id=2761]
- James C. Scott, ***Weapons of the Weak: Everyday Forms of Peasant Resistance*** (Yale, 1985): about 70 families in a Malaysian village transformed by irrigation and double-cropping; "everyday resistance" — foot-dragging, false compliance, pilfering, feigned ignorance, slander, sabotage, arson. [source: JCS]
- Alf Lüdtke had regular exchanges with South Korean scholars from the late 1990s (Mass Dictatorship conferences in Seoul from 2005). [source: AL]

### Germany: Alltagsgeschichte
- *Alltagsgeschichte* ("history of everyday life") emerged among West German historians in the **1980s**, founded by **Alf Lüdtke** (18 October 1943, Dresden – 29 January 2019, Göttingen; Max Planck Institute for History from 1975) and **Hans Medick** (born 1939); challenged *Strukturgeschichte* (the Bielefeld history of structures); related to microhistory. Lüdtke's key concept **Eigen-Sinn** (roughly "self-will", workers' own sense of themselves, expressed in factory-floor horseplay, breaks, etc.) — *Eigen-Sinn. Fabrikalltag, Arbeitererfahrungen und Politik vom Kaiserreich bis in den Faschismus* (1993); also how ordinary Germans took part in and acquiesced to dictatorship. Martin Broszat's *Bayern in der NS-Zeit* (1977–83) was influenced by the method. [source: ALL; AL]

## 12. Global turn, labour beyond Europe, digital social history
- Eric Wolf, ***Europe and the People Without History*** (**1982**): global history from 1400 of how the peoples "without history" (absent from Western accounts) were active participants in the world created by commercial empire; kin-ordered, tributary and capitalist modes of production. [source: EW]
- **International Institute of Social History** (Amsterdam), founded 1935 by Nicolaas Posthumus; one of the world's largest labour and social history archives (papers of Marx, Bakunin, Emma Goldman...); collections looted by the Nazis, mostly recovered in Hanover 1946 and some from Moscow in 1991. [source: IISH]
- Marcel van der Linden (IISH), ***Workers of the World: Essays toward a Global Labor History*** (Brill, **2008**; Studies in Global Social History vol. 1) — global labour history beyond Eurocentrism and "methodological nationalism", integrating slavery and indentured labour with wage labour. [source: WebSearch https://iisg.amsterdam/nl/node/24 ; Journal of Global History review on cambridge.org]
- Digital history: grew from 1960s–70s computer analysis of censuses, election returns and city directories in social history; Association for History and Computing (late 1980s); Edward Ayers's *Valley of the Shadow* (conceived 1991) comparing two Civil War counties, Augusta (Virginia) and Franklin (Pennsylvania), and the 2003 *AHR* article "The Differences Slavery Made"; Roy Rosenzweig's Center for History and New Media (1994); text mining, network analysis, GIS, big data; Patrick Manning's "African Slave Demography Project". [source: DH]
- Microhistory's "global microhistory" since the 2010s. [source: MH]
- After 1991 the opening of Soviet archives made Soviet social history flourish; in Hungary after 1989, demographic and urban history. [source: SH]

## Debates
- **Structure vs. agency**: Annales structures and the *longue durée* (history "beyond the reach of conscious actors", ANN) vs. Thompson's working class "present at its own making" and agency from below; Annales historians rejected the Marxist idea that history should foment revolution, and Marxists called them conservative. [source: ANN; FB]
- **Numbers vs. lives**: quantitative social-science history (Cambridge Group, cliometrics, *Time on the Cross*) vs. humanist recovery of experience (Thompson, Gutman); Stone's "revival of narrative" (1979); Paul Johnson's recollection of quantifiers' "mad-scientist bravado". [source: SH; TOC; LS]
- **Thompson and gender**: Joan Scott's charge of androcentrism vs. Rowbotham's defence. [source: EPT]
- **Microhistory and evidence**: Finlay vs. Davis (AHR 1988) on how far a historian may infer motives ("perhaps", "she may have thought") from circumstantial evidence. [source: WebSearch AHR forum; NZD]
- **Linguistic turn**: Scott, Stedman Jones (language constitutes class) vs. Palmer, the Genoveses (discourse obscures material oppression). [source: SH; GSJ; Palmer search]
- **Subaltern Studies**: from recovery of peasant agency to discourse analysis; Sarkar's "Decline of the Subaltern"; Chibber's class critique; Spivak's question whether the subaltern can be heard at all through rulers' archives. [source: SS; GCS]
- **African historiography**: Ibadan/Dar es Salaam nationalist histories of African agency vs. later charges of celebrating nationalist states; turn to social, economic and labour history (van Onselen, Cooper). [source: IB; TR; Dar es Salaam search]
- **Ariès and childhood**: thesis vs. refutation (Orme, Ozment) — a model of how social-history claims are tested. [source: CC]
- **Critical fabulation** (Hartman): legitimate response to archival silence, or a step beyond evidence? (Wikipedia gives the method; the critique framing is the writer's to present as a live debate, not attribute to named critics.) [source: SHart]

## Conflicts with published chapters
- **No factual contradiction found** between these sources and the published chapters (What is History?, Industrial Revolution, Modern South Asia, Modern Southeast Asia, 20th Century overview). Dates match: Annales 1929, Braudel 1949, Thompson 1963, Ginzburg 1976, Scott 1986, Subaltern Studies 1982, Spivak 1988, Thompson's time-discipline essay 1967, Bloch's *Historian's Craft* published 1949.
- **Terminology nuance (not a factual error):** What is History? calls the "near-motionless" layer of mountains, seas and climate the *longue durée*. The Annales school article (ANN) instead labels the geographic layer *l'histoire quasi immobile* and attaches *longue durée* to the history of social/economic structures; the Longue durée article (LD) treats *longue durée* as the slowest of three tiers (événements / conjonctures / longue durée) and includes geography in it. Usage genuinely varies. Recommend: in this chapter, present Braudel's three speeds (events / conjunctures / near-motionless geographic time) and say *longue durée* is the general name for the slow, structural time-scale — consistent with What is History?.
- **Ginzburg is now dead (17 June 2026).** What is History? does not give his life dates, so no conflict, but the writer must use the past tense for Ginzburg (and for Davis, d. 2023; Guha, d. 2023; Le Roy Ladurie, d. 2023).
- What is History? says Menocchio's trials "eventually had him executed"; CW says "burned at the stake". Consistent.
