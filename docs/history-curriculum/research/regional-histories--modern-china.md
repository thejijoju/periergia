## Late Qing in brief (for a bridging paragraph only)
- Japan presented the Twenty-One Demands to Yuan Shikai's government on 18 January 1915; Britain and the US forced Japan to drop section 5; the demands provoked a nationwide boycott and contributed to the May Fourth Movement. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty-One_Demands]
- The name "Xinhai Revolution" comes from the sexagenary-cycle label (xinhai) for 1911. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1911_Revolution]

## The 1911 Revolution and Sun Yat-sen
- The Wuchang Uprising by New Army soldiers began on 10 October 1911; revolts followed around the country. On 1 November 1911 the Qing court appointed Yuan Shikai (leader of the Beiyang Army) prime minister. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1911_Revolution]
- Sun Yat-sen "had no direct part in the uprising" and was travelling in the United States raising support among overseas Chinese. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1911_Revolution]
- On 1 January 1912 the Republic of China was declared in Nanjing with Sun, leader of the Tongmenghui, as (provisional) president; Sun resigned in favour of Yuan in return for Yuan securing the abdication; the edict of abdication of the six-year-old Xuantong Emperor (Puyi) was promulgated 12 February 1912; Yuan was sworn in as president on 10 March 1912. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1911_Revolution]
- Sun founded the Tongmenghui (Revolutionary/United League) in Tokyo in August 1905, uniting the Revive China Society, Huaxinghui and Guangfuhui; all the uprisings he backed before Wuchang failed (he may have participated in 8–10). Japanese sympathisers, including the ultranationalist Black Dragon Society, supported the anti-Qing revolutionaries. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1911_Revolution]
- Sun's 1904–05 programme: "expel the Tatar barbarians, revive Zhonghua, establish a Republic, distribute land equally". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Yat-sen]
- The failed Yellow Flower Mound (Second Guangzhou) uprising of 27 April 1911, led by Huang Xing: 72 martyrs identified among 86 bodies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Yat-sen]
- Sun (12 November 1866 – 12 March 1925, died in Beijing) is called "Father of the Nation" in the ROC/Taiwan and "Forerunner of the Revolution" in the PRC. His Three Principles of the People: nationalism, democracy and the people's livelihood. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Yat-sen]
- In February 1913 the KMT won the National Assembly elections; in March 1913 Song Jiaoren, expected to become premier, was assassinated in Shanghai (Yuan's involvement "alleged"); Sun's "Second Revolution" of summer 1913 was crushed; Yuan outlawed the KMT and dissolved the assembly. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuan_Shikai]
- Yuan proclaimed himself Hongxian Emperor (from 12 December 1915), abdicated in March 1916 after opposition, and died 6 June 1916. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuan_Shikai ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1911_Revolution]
- Lu Xun on 1911, writing in 1925: "before the revolution, I was a slave, but shortly after the revolution, I have been cheated by slaves and have become their slave." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lu_Xun]

## Warlords (1916–1928)
- The Warlord Era ran from Yuan's death (June 1916) to December 1928; control was divided between military cliques: the Zhili clique (Feng Guozhang), the Anhui clique (Duan Qirui) and the Fengtian clique of Zhang Zuolin in Manchuria, plus southern factions (Sichuan, Yunnan, Guangxi etc.). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warlord_Era]
- About half a million soldiers in 1916; the number tripled by 1922 and tripled again by 1924. Opium sales were a major warlord revenue source. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warlord_Era]
- Several former warlords stayed powerful after 1928, e.g. in the Central Plains War of 1929–30 (Feng Yuxiang, Yan Xishan). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warlord_Era]

## New Culture and May Fourth
- Chen Duxiu founded *Youth Magazine* in Shanghai in 1915 (first issue 15 September 1915, with his "A Letter to Youth"); renamed *New Youth* (*La Jeunesse*) in September 1916; moved to Beijing in January 1917 when Chen joined Peking University; published until 1926. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Youth]
- New Culture leaders blamed Confucian values for China's weakness and called for "Mr. Science" and "Mr. Democracy" (Chen Duxiu: to replace "Mr. Confucius"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Fourth_Movement ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Culture_Movement]
- Hu Shih argued for written vernacular (baihua) literature, especially in his *New Youth* essay of January 1917, "Preliminary Discussion on Literary Reform". He studied at Cornell and under John Dewey at Columbia; in 1919 he urged "more study of questions, less study of isms" against Li Dazhao. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Culture_Movement ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hu_Shih]
- Hu Shih (b. 17 December 1891) was ROC ambassador to the US from 29 October 1938 to 1 September 1942, later president of Academia Sinica, and died in Nankang, Taipei, in February 1962 (Wikipedia gives both 24 and 25 February — use "February 1962"). His work was in disrepute on the mainland until a 1986 article by Ji Xianlin. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hu_Shih]
- Lu Xun (pen name of Zhou Shuren; 25 September 1881 – 19 October 1936, Shanghai) studied medicine at Sendai in Japan before turning to literature. "Diary of a Madman" appeared in the April 1918 issue of *New Youth*; it is often called "China's first modern short story". Its madman sees "eat people" written between the lines of the classics and ends with a plea to "save the children" (救救孩子). Other works: "Kong Yiji" (1919), *The True Story of Ah Q* (1921). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lu_Xun ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diary_of_a_Madman_(Lu_Xun_short_story)]
- Caveat: Chen Hengzhe's vernacular story "One Day" (1917) preceded Lu Xun's; do not call "Diary of a Madman" the first vernacular fiction, and it is a short story, not a novel. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Culture_Movement ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diary_of_a_Madman_(Lu_Xun_short_story)]
- Mao called Lu Xun "the saint of modern China" after his death and in 1940 "not only a great writer, but also a great thinker and a great revolutionary". Lu Xun himself kept his distance from the CCP. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lu_Xun]
- China had entered the First World War on the Allied side in 1917 and sent some 140,000 labourers to the Western Front, but the Versailles settlement gave Germany's Shandong rights to Japan. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Fourth_Movement]
- On 4 May 1919 students from Peking University, Yenching and other schools gathered at Tiananmen (Wikipedia gives "over 4,000" in the text and "approximately 3,000 students from 13 Beijing universities" in a caption — say "several thousand"), with slogans including "Give Qingdao back to us!" After arrests on 3 June, merchants and workers in Shanghai struck; on 12 June the government dismissed Cao Rulin, Zhang Zongxiang and Lu Zongyu; China's delegates refused to sign the Treaty of Versailles. Women students took part. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Fourth_Movement]
- Rana Mitter: the "atmosphere and political mood that emerged around 1919 are at the center of a set of ideas that has shaped China's momentous twentieth century." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Fourth_Movement]
- The CCP was founded at its First National Congress in the Shanghai French Concession, which began on 23 July 1921 (the party later fixed 1 July as its anniversary); 13 Chinese delegates attended and the party had 57 members. Chen Duxiu and Li Dazhao were its founders, with Comintern help; Chen was its first leader (general secretary), resigned in mid-1927, turned to Trotskyism and was expelled in 1929; died 27 May 1942. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Party ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Duxiu]

## United Front, Northern Expedition, 1927
- Sun Yat-sen and the Soviet envoy Adolph Joffe signed the Sun–Joffe Manifesto in January 1923; Sun admitted Communists into the KMT (First United Front), took Comintern adviser Mikhail Borodin, and founded the Whampoa Military Academy near Guangzhou in 1924 with Chiang Kai-shek as commandant. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Yat-sen]
- The Northern Expedition ran from 9 July 1926 to 29 December 1928; Zhang Zuolin was assassinated by the Japanese (Huanggutun incident, 1928) as he fled Beijing, and in December 1928 his son Zhang Xueliang accepted Nanjing's authority, completing nominal reunification. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Expedition ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warlord_Era]
- The Shanghai massacre began on 12 April 1927, carried out by Chiang's forces with Green Gang gangsters (Du Yuesheng) against Communists, union leaders and workers' militias. The toll "was never precisely established": some sources give over 1,000 arrested, some 300 executed and more than 5,000 missing; others 5,000–10,000 killed in the following purges. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai_massacre]
- Over several years after 1927 the KMT's anti-communist "White Terror" killed an estimated 300,000 to one million people, mainly peasants (Barnouin and Yu). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Civil_War]

## The Nanjing decade (1927–1937)
- Also called the "Golden Decade"; far more stable than the Warlord Era, with economic growth and government projects, some later taken up by the PRC. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanjing_decade]
- Foreign tariff restrictions were largely lifted by 1930 (tariff autonomy), raising central revenue. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanjing_decade]
- The government built 82,000 km of roads in the decade, for a total of 115,000 km; central education funding rose from 23.29 million yuan (1933) to 55.4 million yuan (1936). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanjing_decade]
- The New Life Movement began in 1934, mixing Confucian morality, Christianity and nationalism; Frederic Wakeman called it "Confucian fascism"; the Blue Shirts imitated European fascist movements; Jay Taylor and A. James Gregor reject calling Chiang's regime fascist. Close ties with Nazi Germany brought German military advice in the mid-1930s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiang_Kai-shek]
- Lloyd Eastman's *The Abortive Revolution: China under Nationalist Rule, 1927–1937* (Harvard, 1974) and *Seeds of Destruction* (1984) represent the older, critical "conventional wisdom" on the Nationalists. [source: WebSearch — https://cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-asian-studies/article/abortive-revolution-china-under-nationalist-rule-19271937-by-lloyd-e-eastman-cambridge-harvard-university-press-1974-xvii-398-pp-bibliography-index-1650/D9B442A62AF21AC72675D6E886F6B0C6 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Republic_of_China]

## Women and family change
- Foot binding: the new Republic banned it in 1912, but the ban was not actively enforced; provincial leaders (e.g. Yan Xishan in Shanxi) used inspectors and fines, and Nanjing-era governments enforced it. A 1929 survey in one province found 95% of girls born after 1910 unbound; in Dingxian, where over 99% of women had once been bound, no new cases among those born after 1919. Most modern historians put women with bound feet at the practice's height at 10–20 million. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_binding]
- The last case of foot binding was reportedly in 1957. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_China]
- The Women's Suffrage Alliance was founded in 1912. Under the KMT Civil Code (1931 per Wikipedia), women gained equal inheritance rights; women's suffrage was in the 1936 draft constitution, implemented only in 1947. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_China]
- Women were admitted to Peking University in 1920. In 1919 the death of Li Chao, a student at the Beijing Higher Normal School for Women cut off by her family, became a catalyst for feminist agitation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_China ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Culture_Movement]
- The PRC's Marriage Law, passed 1 May 1950, required consent of both parties, set minimum ages (20 men, 18 women), let women initiate divorce, and banned arranged marriage, concubinage, bigamy and child betrothal; it was promoted by mass campaigns and the All-China Women's Federation; superseded 1980. Mao's slogan "Women hold up half the sky". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Marriage_Law ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_China]
- During land reform party activists specifically mobilised rural women; Deng Yingchao stressed this at a 1947 policy meeting. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_Reform_Movement]
- Qiu Jin (feminist revolutionary, executed 1907) is covered in the Taiping/Late Qing chapter. [source: supabase/content/history--modern-era-19th-century--taiping-late-qing.sql]

## The CCP's survival: Jiangxi, the Long March, Xi'an
- After Chiang's Fifth Encirclement Campaign, the First Front Red Army (about 86,000 troops) left the Jiangxi Soviet in October 1934; it had 36,000 left after crossing the Xiang River on 1 December. The Zunyi Conference (January 1935) established Mao as de facto leader. The march (c. 10,000 km) ended 19 October 1935 in northern Shaanxi with about 8,000 survivors (infobox: 69,000 → 7,000; some counts 130,000 including civilians at the start). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_March]
- Caveat: Wikipedia's lead says the army "arrived in Yan'an" on 19 October 1935, but its own timeline puts the end at Wuqizhen, 145 km from Yan'an; Yan'an became the capital only in 1937 (Maoism chapter). Write "northern Shaanxi". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_March]
- Xi'an Incident, 12–26 December 1936: Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng detained Chiang, demanding an end to civil war and an alliance with the CCP against Japan; result: the Second United Front; Zhang was afterwards arrested by Chiang. Historiography is "widely ranging and often partisan". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xi%27an_Incident]

## The war with Japan, from the Chinese side
- Since 2017 the CCP's official historiography dates a "Fourteen Years' War of Resistance" from 18 September 1931. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Sino-Japanese_War]
- Battle of Shanghai: 13 August – 26 November 1937 (a Chinese report of 5 November counted 187,200 killed and wounded). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Shanghai]
- Nanjing: Japanese troops entered on 13 December 1937; the massacre is traditionally dated six weeks from then (atrocities in the area 4 December 1937 – 28 March 1938). Estimates: IMTFE more than 200,000; newer estimates 100,000–200,000; range 40,000 (city only) to over 340,000 (whole Shanghai–Nanjing region); rapes 4,000 to over 80,000, about 20,000 most common; China's official figure 300,000; the 1947 Nanjing War Crimes Tribunal counted "more than 190,000" plus "more than 150,000" victims. The Nanking Safety Zone committee (John Rabe; Minnie Vautrin sheltered up to 10,000 women at Ginling College) is credited with saving at least 200,000 lives. China held its first Nanjing Massacre Memorial Day on 13 December 2014. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanjing_Massacre]
- Taierzhuang (22 March – 7 April 1938) was the war's first major Chinese victory. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Taierzhuang]
- 1938 Yellow River flood: on Chiang's sanction (1 June 1938), Nationalist troops breached the dike at Huayuankou, Henan, on 5 and 7 June ("using water as a substitute for soldiers"); the first floodwaters reached Zhongmu county on 13 June. Immediate drownings 30,000 (Kuo Tai-chun, 2015) to 89,000 (Chinese Academy of Sciences, 1995); total deaths from flood, famine and plague 400,000–500,000 (Wang Zhibin 1986; Bi Chunfu 1995); the Nationalists' 1948 figure of 893,303 is discredited but was later upheld by Communist sources; about 5 million lived on inundated land until the breach was closed (repairs completed 1947). The Nationalists first blamed Japanese bombing. Falkenhausen's 1935 report had recommended such a flood. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1938_Yellow_River_flood]
- The government retreated to Chongqing; Wuhan fell in 1938 (Second World War chapter). Wang Jingwei's collaborationist "Reorganized National Government" in Nanjing existed 30 March 1940 – 16 August 1945. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_Jingwei_regime]
- Henan famine 1942–43: Anthony Garnaut "well under one million", probably around 700,000; Lloyd Eastman 2–3 million; official 1943 count 1,484,983 (judged exaggerated). Theodore White reported it in *Time* (March 1943). Mitter blames corrupt or incompetent officials who collected more grain than Chiang's reduced quota. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_famine_of_1942%E2%80%931943]
- The CCP's Hundred Regiments Offensive, 20 August 1940 – 24 January 1941. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_Regiments_Offensive]
- Operation Ichi-Go (1944), the largest Japanese campaign of the war (500,000 troops), cost Chinese armies about 300,000 casualties and much territory. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Sino-Japanese_War]
- Chinese war dead: Wikipedia's infobox, citing Mitter (2013, p. 381), gives 12+ million civilian deaths and 14 million total deaths, with other estimates up to 22 million (Clodfelter); China claims 35 million total casualties. Reviews of *Forgotten Ally* report Mitter's estimate as 14–20 million dead and 80–100 million refugees. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Sino-Japanese_War ; WebSearch — https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2014/jan/1/book-review-forgotten-ally/ ; https://search.icpl.org/Record/1424948]
- China was recognised as one of the "Big Four" Allies; in 1943 the US and Britain revoked the unequal treaties and extraterritoriality. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Sino-Japanese_War ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Century_of_humiliation]

## The civil war (1945–1949)
- Chiang and Mao negotiated in Chongqing from 28 August to 10 October 1945; at war's end the CCP had about 1.2 million regular troops plus about 2 million militia. The Soviet army delayed leaving Manchuria (into 1946) and let CCP forces move in behind it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Civil_War]
- Land reform was the CCP's "most effective political reform" in the war; by victory over 25 million hectares had been redistributed in Communist areas. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Civil_War ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_Reform_Movement]
- Siege of Changchun, 23 May – 19 October 1948: the PLA kept civilians from leaving to starve the garrison; civilian deaths ~150,000 (Lary: up to 200,000); largely unknown in China until the 1989 book *White Snow, Red Blood*, since censored. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Changchun]
- The three decisive campaigns: Liaoshen (from 12 September 1948, Lin Biao), Huaihai (6 November 1948 – 10 January 1949, the war's largest operation) and Pingjin, all ended by January 1949. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Civil_War ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huaihai_campaign]
- On 21 September 1949 Mao announced the PRC in a speech to the first plenary session of the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference — the speech known as "The Chinese people have stood up". On 1 October 1949, at 15:00 from the Tiananmen Gate, he proclaimed: "the Central People's Government of the People's Republic of China was established today!" [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclamation_of_the_People%27s_Republic_of_China]
- In December 1949 Chiang proclaimed Taipei the temporary capital (the ROC government's retreat to Taiwan is dated 7 December 1949); approximately two million mainlanders went to Taiwan. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Civil_War]
- Why the Nationalists lost is debated: some Chinese historians stress Chiang's decisions (70% of spending on the military, overconfidence in US-armed forces, collapsing morale); others (following Jay Taylor) stress external factors — US withdrawal of aid, Marshall's arms embargo, the push for a coalition, Soviet aid to the CCP. Liang Shuming said Chiang's "greatest contribution was to make the CCP successful". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiang_Kai-shek]

## Taiwan's separate path
- Japan ruled Taiwan 1895–1945 (see Japan's Empire chapter); the island passed to ROC administration in 1945. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_28_incident]
- February 28 incident (1947): on 27 February in Taipei, Monopoly Bureau agents struck a widow, Lin Jiang-mai, over contraband cigarettes; an officer fired into the crowd, killing a man; on 28 February soldiers fired on protesters; the uprising spread by radio. Governor Chen Yi waited for troops from Fujian, who launched the crackdown on 8 March. Mainland civilians were also attacked by mobs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_28_incident]
- 228 death toll: no accurate figure; the most frequently cited range is 8,000–28,000; the 18,000–28,000 range comes from the commission set up in 1990/1992 under Premier Hau Pei-tsun (report 1992, adopted as official in 2007), challenged by Hau himself in 2012; a Taiwanese delegation at the time cited 3,000–4,000 executed; George Kerr (*Formosa Betrayed*) cited 10,000. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_28_incident]
- Lee Teng-hui was the first president to discuss the incident publicly on its anniversary (1995); 28 February is now Peace Memorial Day; the National 228 Memorial Museum opened 28 February 1997; in 2019 the Transitional Justice Commission exonerated those convicted. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_28_incident]
- Martial law was declared 19 May 1949 (effective 20 May) and lifted 15 July 1987 — 38 years and 57 days, then the world's longest. The White Terror (generally 1949 to 1987 or to the repeal of Criminal Code Article 100 on 21 September 1992) saw at least 140,000 imprisoned and 3,000–4,000 executed (not counting 228). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martial_law_in_Taiwan ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Terror_(Taiwan)]
- KMT land reform in Taiwan began in 1949. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Terror_(Taiwan)]
- Kaohsiung (Formosa) Incident, 10 December 1979 (Human Rights Day): a *Formosa Magazine* rally was crushed and opposition leaders, including Annette Lu, were arrested. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaohsiung_Incident]
- The Democratic Progressive Party was founded 28 September 1986 (while martial law was still in force). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party]
- Lee Teng-hui (president 13 January 1988 – 20 May 2000) was the first Taiwan-born president; he succeeded Chiang Ching-kuo on his death in 1988. Martial law was lifted in July 1987 under Chiang Ching-kuo, before Lee took office. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Teng-hui ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martial_law_in_Taiwan]
- Wild Lily student movement, 16–22 March 1990, coincided with Lee's indirect re-election by 641 National Assembly members. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_Lily_student_movement]
- First direct presidential election 23 March 1996, after the Third Taiwan Strait Crisis; Lee won 54%. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Taiwanese_presidential_election]
- 18 March 2000: the DPP's Chen Shui-bian won with 39.3%, ending some 50 years of KMT rule — the first peaceful transfer of power to an opposition party under a democratic system in Chinese history. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_Taiwanese_presidential_election]
- Hsiao-ting Lin argues Taiwan's separate state was not planned but the product of ad hoc half-measures, especially in Nationalist dealings with Washington (*Accidental State*, 2016). [source: WebSearch — https://fsi.stanford.edu/node/12627]

## Hong Kong
- Britain acquired Hong Kong Island (Treaty of Nanking, 1842), Kowloon (Convention of Peking, 1860) and leased the New Territories for 99 years (1898). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handover_of_Hong_Kong]
- 1967 riots (May–December), leftist and pro-CCP during the Cultural Revolution: 51 dead, 832 injured, 4,979 arrested. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1967_Hong_Kong_riots]
- Sino-British Joint Declaration signed 19 December 1984 in the Great Hall of the People by Margaret Thatcher and Zhao Ziyang: a special administrative region with a high degree of autonomy except in foreign affairs and defence, "one country, two systems", policies unchanged for 50 years. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-British_Joint_Declaration]
- Handover at midnight on 1 July 1997, ending 156 years of British rule. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handover_of_Hong_Kong]
- 2019–20 protests against an extradition bill: the bill was suspended 15 June and withdrawn 23 October 2019; the 16 June march drew almost 2 million by the organisers' (CHRF) estimate, 338,000 at peak by police estimate; 10,279 arrests by August 2022. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019%E2%80%932020_Hong_Kong_protests]
- The National Security Law was passed by Beijing on 30 June 2020, creating crimes of secession, subversion, terrorism and collusion with foreign forces; a local Safeguarding National Security Ordinance followed in 2024. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Hong_Kong_national_security_law]

## Land reform and the campaigns of the 1950s (keep brief; Maoism chapter has land reform)
- Land reform (tugai) ran from the civil war to 1953 (completed except in Xinjiang, Tibet, Qinghai and Sichuan); landlords were subjected to struggle sessions and "speaking bitterness"; it turned "lurched violently to the left" in May 1951 during the Korean War (Philip Short). Death estimates (mostly land reform plus the counter-revolutionary campaign together) range 200,000 to 5,000,000: Stavis (1978) 200,000–800,000; Roberts (2006) 200,000–2 million; Twitchett, Fairbank and MacFarquhar (1987) 1–2 million executed; Meisner (1999) 2 million executed 1950–52; Feigon (2002) 2–5 million landlords. A 1952 CIA-linked claim of 14–15 million cited no sources. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_Reform_Movement]
- Campaign to Suppress Counterrevolutionaries (zhenfan), 1950–53: Deputy Public Security Minister Xu Zirong reported (1954) 712,000 executed, 1.29 million imprisoned and 1.2 million placed under control; the historian Yang Kuisong (2008) argues the real number executed "was much larger than the reported 712,000". In 1957 Mao spoke of 700,000 killed 1950–52 and 70,000–80,000 more in 1953–56. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_Reform_Movement ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campaign_to_Suppress_Counterrevolutionaries]
- Three-anti (1951) and Five-anti (1952) campaigns against corruption and the urban bourgeoisie, linked to Korean War costs; they caused many suicides among businessmen (Short: "hundreds of thousands of suicides"; precise figures unreliable). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-anti_and_Five-anti_Campaigns]
- Hundred Flowers (1956–57) was followed by the Anti-Rightist Campaign (1957–59): officially at least 550,000 persecuted; some researchers 1–2 million or more; Deng Xiaoping and Peng Zhen were among its most enthusiastic prosecutors; the 1981 Resolution called it "correct" and "necessary" but "too broad". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Rightist_Campaign]
- Dikötter (*The Tragedy of Liberation*, 2013) describes 1945–57 as "calculated terror and systematic violence" with some 5 million unnatural deaths in the PRC's first decade; critics: Felix Wemheuer, Adam Cathcart (misuse of sources), Brian DeMare (implying landlords were an invention). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tragedy_of_Liberation]
- Great Leap Forward and Cultural Revolution: cross-refer to the Maoism and Cultural Revolution chapters; use their figures (famine c. 15 to 45+ million, most recent estimates 30–45 million; CR deaths commonly 1–2 million).

## Tibet and Xinjiang (brief, attributed)
- After 1912 central Tibet operated autonomously and was de facto independent by about 1917. The PLA campaign began 6 October 1950 (Chamdo); the Seventeen Point Agreement was signed in 1951 and ratified by the 14th Dalai Lama on 24 October 1951. Beijing calls it the "Peaceful Liberation of Tibet"; the exile government calls it an invasion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1951_Chinese_annexation_of_Tibet]
- Armed resistance broke out in Amdo and eastern Kham in 1956 after land reform there. The Lhasa uprising began 10 March 1959; the Dalai Lama fled on 17 March to India (Dharamsala) and repudiated the agreement as signed under duress. Deaths: Peace Research Institute Oslo about 10,000 Tibetan and 2,000 PLA battle deaths; the Tibetan government in exile claims 85,000–87,000; estimates for 1956–59 including areas outside Lhasa around 60,000 civilian and 40,000 military deaths. Since 2005 the Dalai Lama has sought greater autonomy within China. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1959_Tibetan_uprising ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1951_Chinese_annexation_of_Tibet]
- Xinjiang: July 2009 Ürümqi riots (over 100 deaths) and later attacks; the "Strike Hard Campaign Against Violent Terrorism" from 2014; from 2017 under party secretary Chen Quanguo, an estimated one million or more Uyghurs and other Turkic Muslims held without legal process in camps officially called "vocational education and training centers" (Adrian Zenz: up to 1.8 million); camps wound down from 2019. The government denies abuses. The US State Department declared genocide in 2021; the UN human rights office (OHCHR), 31 August 2022, said the policies may constitute crimes against humanity, without using "genocide". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Uyghurs_in_China ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang_re-education_camps]

## Reform, 1989, and the Xi era (cross-refer to China Today)
- 1989: protests 15 April – 4 June, spreading to some 400 cities; martial law declared 20 May, with as many as 300,000 troops mobilised; troops cleared the city in the early hours of 4 June, most killings along Chang'an Avenue in the western suburbs. Death estimates: official CCP tally 241 (including 23 soldiers and police); Tiananmen Mothers identified 202; hospital records 478; Amnesty International up to 1,000; British ambassador Alan Donald's cable claimed at least 10,000, later revised to 2,700–3,400 (Timothy Brook calls 10,000 "extravagant"); a rumoured Chinese Red Cross figure of 2,600 was denied. Overall "several hundreds to the low thousands". "Tank Man" stood before tanks on 5 June. Zhao Ziyang was removed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1989_Tiananmen_Square_protests_and_massacre]
- WTO member 11 December 2001. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_and_the_World_Trade_Organization]
- Xi Jinping became general secretary on 15 November 2012 and president on 14 March 2013; a March 2018 constitutional amendment removed presidential term limits; he took a third term as general secretary in October 2022 and as president in 2023, the first to serve more than two. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xi_Jinping]
- Under Xi the "century of humiliation" became central to party messaging; the 2023 Patriotic Education Law made it a required theme. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Century_of_humiliation]

## Debates
- **The "century of humiliation"**: a Chinese historiographical concept from the First Opium War to 1949; the idea of national humiliation arose after the 1894–95 defeat and scramble for concessions, was popularised by both the KMT and the CCP in the 1920s, became common during the Japanese invasion (Callahan), and is now central to CCP messaging and the 2023 Patriotic Education Law. Zheng Wang (2012) analyses its political uses. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Century_of_humiliation]
- **Fairbank vs Cohen**: John King Fairbank (1907–1991; Harvard 1936–77), with Teng Ssu-yu in *China's Response to the West* (1954), framed modern China as "impact–response". Paul A. Cohen (1934–2025), Fairbank's own student, in *Discovering History in China* (1984; 2010) criticised the "impact-response", "tradition and modernity" and "imperialism" paradigms for casting China as passive, and called for a "China-centered history of China". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovering_History_in_China ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_King_Fairbank ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Cohen_(historian)]
- **Rehabilitating the Nationalist state**: the older view (Eastman, *The Abortive Revolution*, 1974; *Seeds of Destruction*, 1984; Theodore White and the "Stilwell–White paradigm") stressed failure, corruption and repression. William C. Kirby (*Germany and Republican China*, 1984; "Engineering China: The Origins of the Chinese Developmental State", 2000) argues the pre-1949 decades were "a formidable period of state- and institution-building" and that 1949 was not an unbridgeable divide; David Pietz and Megan Greene follow this line. Hans van de Ven (*War and Nationalism in China, 1925–1945*, 2003) attacks the Stilwell–White paradigm and argues neither Britain nor the US valued China enough to fund the Nationalists. Mitter (2013) puts China's war at the centre and notes the PRC's own more positive treatment of Chiang since the 2000s. Dikötter (*The Age of Openness*, 2008) calls the Republic a "golden age of openness"; Jonathan Fenby says his case sometimes rests on "fragile foundations". Chiang's diaries (1917–1976), deposited at the Hoover Institution in 2004 and opened from 2006, have spurred reassessment by mainland and Taiwanese historians alike. [source: WebSearch — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/china-quarterly/article/engineering-the-state-the-huai-river-and-reconstruction-in-nationalist-china-1927-1937-by-david-a-pietz-new-york-and-london-routledge-2002-ix142-pp-4500-isbn-0415933889/DC923A302520AB9D701CD6F3F29099E0 ; https://exhibits.lib.ku.edu/items/show/6388 ; https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/china-quarterly/article/war-and-nationalism-in-china-19251945-by-hans-j-van-de-ven-london-and-new-york-routledgecurzon-2003-xii377-pp-7000-isbn-0415145716/C9F77111208ADA6796B10105A7093E69 ; https://www.thecipherbrief.com/book-review/reassessing-chiang-kai-shek ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_C._Kirby ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Age_of_Openness ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiang_Kai-shek]
- **Was Chiang a fascist?** Wakeman ("Confucian fascism"), Eastman (Blue Shirts as a Chinese fascist organisation) vs Jay Taylor, A. James Gregor and Stanley Payne. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiang_Kai-shek ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuomintang]
- **Dikötter's People's Trilogy and its critics**: *Mao's Great Famine* (2010; Samuel Johnson Prize 2011; praised by MacFarquhar), *The Tragedy of Liberation* (2013), *The Cultural Revolution* (2016). Critics: Felix Wemheuer (local variation, method), Daniel Leese (neglect of ideology), Philip Short ("Dikötter's errors are strangely consistent. They all serve to strengthen his case against Mao"), Adam Cathcart and Brian DeMare (misuse of sources); Pankaj Mishra called him "boldly and engagingly revisionist". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Dik%C3%B6tter ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tragedy_of_Liberation]
- **Why the CCP won**: land reform and peasant mobilisation (DeMare), Japanese invasion weakening the Nationalists (Mitter, van de Ven), Chiang's own decisions vs external factors (Taylor). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Civil_War ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiang_Kai-shek]
- **Naming**: Tiananmen as "counter-revolutionary rebellion" → "turmoil" in PRC usage vs "June Fourth Massacre"; Tibet 1950–51 as "Peaceful Liberation" vs "invasion"; Hong Kong 1997 "handover" vs "transfer of sovereignty" vs Beijing's "resumption of sovereignty"; 228 framed by Beijing as a "patriotic, anti-imperialist struggle" vs the foundational event of Taiwanese identity. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1989_Tiananmen_Square_protests_and_massacre ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1951_Chinese_annexation_of_Tibet ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handover_of_Hong_Kong ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_28_incident]

## Conflicts with published chapters
- **Mitter's figure for Chinese war dead.** The Second World War chapter says "Rana Mitter putting the most likely figure at about 15–16 million" and calls the PRC's official figure "over 20 million". Wikipedia's infobox cites Mitter (2013, p. 381) for 14 million total dead (12+ million civilians), and reviews give Mitter's estimate as 14–20 million; the Japan's Empire chapter says Westad gives about 14 million, "which Rana Mitter considers conservative". The PRC claim on Wikipedia is 35 million casualties (dead and wounded), as the Japan chapter says. Recommend: "estimates of Chinese war dead run from about 14 million to 20 million or more" and do not attribute "15–16 million" to Mitter without checking the book.
