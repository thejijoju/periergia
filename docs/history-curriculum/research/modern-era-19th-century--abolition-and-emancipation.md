## Britain: the trade, 1807
- The Society/Committee for the Abolition of the Slave Trade was founded in 1787 by Evangelicals allied with Quakers; Thomas Clarkson and Granville Sharp were founders; Quakers made up most of the Committee and, as Dissenters, could not sit as MPs; Wilberforce led the parliamentary campaign. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_Trade_Act_1807 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_Kingdom]
- Clarkson built a network of c.1,200 local abolition groups; a 1792 petition gained 400,000 signatures and one in 1814 gained 1,375,000. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_Kingdom]
- Olaudah Equiano's autobiography went through nine editions in his lifetime; the Wedgwood anti-slavery medallion (1787) and the *Brookes* slave-ship engraving were key images. (Covered in the slave-trade chapter; mention only.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_Kingdom]
- Somerset's case (1772): Lord Mansfield's judgment was generally taken to mean slavery did not exist under English common law (legal implications debated); it did not apply to the colonies. Knight v. Wedderburn (1778) did the same for Scotland. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_Kingdom]
- Commons votes: 1791 motion defeated 163–88; 1792 motion passed with Dundas's "gradual" amendment (230–85), then stalled in the Lords. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_Trade_Act_1807]
- 1807 Act: passed under Grenville's "Ministry of All the Talents"; Grenville led in the Lords, Charles Grey in the Commons; the Commons second reading on 23 February 1807 passed 283–16; royal assent 25 March 1807; in force 1 May 1807. Fines up to £100 per enslaved person found aboard. It did not free anyone already enslaved. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_Trade_Act_1807]
- The 100 Irish MPs added by the Acts of Union 1800 mostly supported abolition. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_Trade_Act_1807]
- The USA passed its Act Prohibiting Importation of Slaves on 2 March 1807 (effective 1808, the earliest date the Constitution allowed); the internal/coastwise US slave trade continued. Denmark-Norway's 1792 ban on its transatlantic trade took effect 1 January 1803. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_Trade_Act_1807 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom]
- Britain paid for other powers' trade bans: Portugal banned the trade north of the Equator in return for £750,000 (1815); Spain's cédula of 1817 banned the trade from 1820 in return for £400,000 (smuggling continued). The Congress of Vienna (1815) declared opposition to the trade. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom]

## The West Africa Squadron and its limits
- Formed 1808 (also called the Preventive Squadron); independent command until 1856 and again 1866–67; absorbed into the Cape of Good Hope Station in 1867. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa_Squadron]
- Seized about 1,600 slave ships and freed about 150,000 Africans, 1808–1860; it captured only about 6% of transatlantic slave ships. Almost 1,600 sailors died on duty 1830–1865, mainly of disease; one of the worst postings in the Navy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa_Squadron ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_Trade_Act_1807]
- Prize courts: Vice Admiralty Court at Freetown (1807); Mixed Commission Courts from 1817 (Anglo-Portuguese, Anglo-Spanish, Anglo-Dutch) in Sierra Leone; naval station at Freetown from 1819. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa_Squadron]
- More than 80,000 "liberated Africans" (recaptives) were emancipated before courts in Freetown between 1808 and 1871 (when the last mixed commission closed); villages such as Leicester (1809), Wilberforce (1810), Regent (1812), Gloucester (1814) were founded for them. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberated_Africans_in_Sierra_Leone]
- Up to a quarter of those rescued who could neither stay in Freetown nor easily return home are estimated to have died before being fully released. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa_Squadron]
- African labour: the squadron "depended on Africans for the day to day operation" (Mary Wills), notably Kru sailors; John Rankin: "African and diaspora sailors made up one-fifth of shipboard personnel." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa_Squadron]
- Debate: Pape and Kaufmann call it "the most expensive international humanitarian intervention in modern history"; a 2021 *International Journal of Maritime History* paper says it was under 5% of Navy warships and "unfit and inadequate"; Howard W. French says its impact is overstated as a "central prop" of a positive self-image of British history. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa_Squadron]
- Traders switched to fast Baltimore clippers; HMS *Black Joke*, a captured Brazilian clipper, took 11 slavers in one year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa_Squadron]
- Aberdeen Act (Slave Trade (Brazil) Act 1845, 9 August 1845) let the Navy stop and search Brazilian ships on the high seas; Brazilian historiography sees it as retaliation for the 1844 Alves Branco tariff. Imports to Brazil actually rose briefly (c.50,324 in 1846, c.60,000 in 1848, per Caio Prado Júnior). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberdeen_Act ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_Brazil]
- 1841 Quintuple Treaty (Britain, France, Russia, Prussia, Austria) to suppress the trade. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom]

## Britain: rebellions as causes (brief — see slave-wars chapter)
- Demerara (now Guyana), 18–20 August 1823: between 9,000 and 12,000 enslaved people (number debated; Viotti da Costa's publisher says some 12,000); led by Jack Gladstone and his father Quamina, a deacon of missionary John Smith's church; triggered by a mistaken belief that Parliament had abolished slavery and it was being withheld; "largely nonviolent". 200–500 killed in the suppression; 45 sentenced to death, 27 executed, bodies displayed for months. John Smith, sentenced to death, died in jail awaiting appeal; news of his death strengthened British abolitionism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demerara_rebellion_of_1823]
- Jack Gladstone was enslaved on "Success" plantation, owned by Sir John Gladstone, father of the future prime minister William Gladstone; John Gladstone had expanded its workforce from 160 to over 330 after switching from coffee to sugar. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demerara_rebellion_of_1823]
- Demerara had c.77,000 enslaved, 2,500 whites, 2,500 free Black people (Viotti da Costa figures). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demerara_rebellion_of_1823]
- Baptist War (Jamaica), 25 December 1831 – 5 January 1832: up to 60,000 of Jamaica's 300,000 enslaved; led by Samuel Sharpe (1801–23 May 1832), Baptist deacon; planned as a peaceful general strike demanding a wage of "half the going wage rate"; Kensington estate burned 27 December. 14 whites killed; c.500 rebels killed (207 outright), and an estimated 310–340 executed afterwards, some for stealing a pig or a cow. Missionaries William Knibb and Henry Bleby were attacked; chapels destroyed by white mobs. Sharpe proclaimed National Hero of Jamaica in 1982; on the $50 note. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptist_War ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Sharpe]
- Accompong Maroons helped suppress the Baptist War. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptist_War]
- Mary Prince (born Bermuda c.1788): *The History of Mary Prince* (1831), dictated in London to Susanna Strickland, was the first account of the life of a Black enslaved woman published in Britain. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Prince]
- Elizabeth Heyrick (1769–1831), Leicester Quaker: pamphlet *Immediate, Not Gradual Abolition* (1824), when leading abolitionists still favoured gradualism. (Fuller treatment belongs to the "Abolitionist Women" node.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Heyrick]
- Anti-Slavery Society founded 1823 (Buxton, Brougham, Sturge, Clarkson, Wilberforce, Heyrick, etc.; also Jamaican mixed-race campaigners Louis Celeste Lecesne and Richard Hill). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_Abolition_Act_1833]

## Britain: the 1833 Act, apprenticeship, compensation
- Slavery Abolition Act 1833: Earl Grey's Whig government; Commons second reading unopposed 22 July 1833 (a week before Wilberforce died); royal assent 28 August 1833; in force 1 August 1834. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_Abolition_Act_1833]
- Exclusions: territories of the East India Company, Ceylon, St Helena; India dealt with by the Indian Slavery Act 1843 (Act V); slavery in British protectorates lasted longer (e.g. Bahrain to 1937). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_Abolition_Act_1833]
- Numbers freed (estimate): c.700,000 in the West Indies, 20,000 in Mauritius, 40,000 at the Cape (South Africa). A contemporary estimate gave 800,000–1,000,000 for all British colonies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_India]
- Stanley's bill introduced apprenticeship and compensation; negotiation cut apprenticeship from 12 to 6 years and raised compensation from £15m to £20m. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_Abolition_Act_1833]
- Children under six freed at once. Others became "apprentices": praedial (field) apprentices to serve to 1 August 1840 (six years); non-praedial (e.g. house servants) to 1 August 1838. Up to 45 hours a week unpaid work in return for food, clothing, housing, medical care; right to buy out. Enforced by "stipendiary magistrates" (initially only 100 authorised). Antigua and Bermuda chose immediate full freedom in 1834. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_Abolition_Act_1833]
- Apprentices' protest: on 1 August 1834 in Port of Spain, Trinidad, mostly elderly, unarmed people drowned out the governor chanting "Pas de six ans. Point de six ans" ("Not six years"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_Kingdom]
- Early end: abolitionist campaign 1837–38 (fact-finding mission 1836, incl. Joseph Sturge); March 1838 amendment banned flogging of apprentices; Barbados first legislated an end on 1 August 1838 (law of 16 May), others followed, Jamaica last; all apprenticeship ended 1 August 1838. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_Abolition_Act_1833 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptist_War]
- Compensation: "the Sum of Twenty Million Pounds Sterling" to owners, nothing to the enslaved. £20m = c.40% of the Treasury's annual income (or spending) and c.5% of GDP. Financed by a £15m loan (finalised 3 August 1835) underwritten by a syndicate led by Nathan Mayer Rothschild and Moses Montefiore, plus £5m paid directly in government stock. Paid under the Slave Compensation Act 1837 (royal assent 23 December 1837); more than 40,000 awards based on the census of owners of 1 August 1834. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_Abolition_Act_1833 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_Compensation_Act_1837]
- About 42,000 claimants in the colonies and 3,500 resident in Britain; about half the money went to absentee owners/creditors in Britain. Largest single recipient: Sir John Gladstone, £106,769. Bishop Henry Phillpotts and co-trustees received £12,700 for 665 enslaved people. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_Abolition_Act_1833 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_Compensation_Act_1837]
- The "taxpayers paid until 2015" claim: the debt was folded into a 4% consolidated loan (1927) and only certainly extinguished when undated gilts were redeemed in 2015; Wikipedia calls the claim "based on a technicality". Present-day equivalent given as c.£16.5bn (one source) or c.£17bn (LBS page). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_Abolition_Act_1833 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_Compensation_Act_1837 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_for_the_Study_of_the_Legacies_of_British_Slavery]
- Legacies of British Slavery (UCL): Centre for the Study of the Legacies of British Slavery (formerly "...Slave-ownership"); projects 2009–2012 (ESRC) and 2013–2015 (ESRC/AHRC); inaugural director Nicholas Draper, chair Catherine Hall; Matthew J. Smith (formerly University of the West Indies) director from June 2020. Free online database starting from the compensation records; identified some 46,000 compensated people; aims to identify all slave-owners at the end of slavery. Draper and Hall: purpose is to counter "selective forgetting" (remembering abolition but not slavery). In 2023 the Trevelyan family apologised and set up a reparations fund for Grenada. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_for_the_Study_of_the_Legacies_of_British_Slavery]
- Sugar Duties Acts 1846 equalised duties, removing protection for British West Indian sugar against Cuban and Brazilian slave-grown sugar (Williams discusses this). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism_and_Slavery ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system]
- Freedom's limits in Jamaica: Morant Bay rebellion, 11 October 1865, led by Baptist preacher Paul Bogle over poverty, poll taxes excluding most from the vote, and land; Governor Edward John Eyre's martial law killed more than 400 Black people and arrested more than 300; George William Gordon hanged. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morant_Bay_rebellion]

## France, 1848
- First abolition 4 February 1794 (Convention); repealed by the law of 20 May 1802 under Napoleon; Napoleon abolished the trade in 1815 (Hundred Days). (Haiti in the French Revolution/Haiti chapter.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_of_slavery_in_France ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decree_of_the_Abolition_of_Slavery_of_April_27,_1848]
- Victor Schœlcher (22 July 1804 – 25 December 1893): porcelain manufacturer's son; saw slavery in Mexico, Cuba and the US South in 1828–30; first article "Des noirs" (1830) proposed gradual abolition, later became an immediatist; first European abolitionist to visit independent Haiti; opposed the Haiti indemnity. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Sch%C5%93lcher]
- After the February 1848 revolution, Arago (Minister of the Navy and Colonies) appointed him under-secretary of state for the colonies (4 March) and president of the abolition commission; Schœlcher argued delay would cause revolt and bloodshed. He drafted the decree of 27 April 1848 abolishing slavery in all colonies and granting citizenship to the freed. Deputy for Martinique and Guadeloupe from 1848. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Sch%C5%93lcher]
- Decree's preamble: "slavery is an attack on human dignity ... a flagrant violation of the republican motto: 'Liberty – Equality – Fraternity'". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decree_of_the_Abolition_of_Slavery_of_April_27,_1848]
- The enslaved forced the pace: on 21 May 1848 in Saint-Pierre, Martinique, an enslaved man, Romain, was arrested for drumming; an uprising spread and the governor declared abolition on 23 May, before the decree arrived. Guadeloupe proclaimed 27 May; French Guiana 10 June (effective 10 August); Réunion only on 20 December. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decree_of_the_Abolition_of_Slavery_of_April_27,_1848]
- 251,019 freed (based on owners' indemnity claims): Guadeloupe 87,087; Martinique 74,447; Réunion 60,651; French Guiana 12,251; Senegal 10,350; Mayotte 2,733; Nosy Be and Sainte-Marie 3,500. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decree_of_the_Abolition_of_Slavery_of_April_27,_1848]
- Compensation to owners: law of 30 April 1849, 126 million gold francs (Réunion highest at 711 francs per person); symbolically repealed by amendment in June 2016. Nothing to the freed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decree_of_the_Abolition_of_Slavery_of_April_27,_1848]
- Article 8 forbade French citizens anywhere to own slaves on pain of losing citizenship (affecting c.20,000 people, half the owners in Louisiana); weakened in 1851 and 1858. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decree_of_the_Abolition_of_Slavery_of_April_27,_1848]
- Enforcement was weak in Senegal and Algeria; slavery persisted in French Senegal to 1904 and French Sudan to 1894 (per one source); France formally abolished slavery in most of French West Africa in 1905. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_of_slavery_in_France]
- Taubira law, 10 May 2001: slavery and the Atlantic trade recognised as a crime against humanity; 10 May is France's day of remembrance. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_of_slavery_in_France]
- Schœlcher's remains were moved to the Panthéon in May 1949 together with Félix Éboué's, at the initiative of Guianese senator Gaston Monnerville (date given as 20 May in most sources, 19 May in some). His statue in Cayenne (Barrias, 1896), criticised as paternalist, was toppled on 18 July 2020. [source: WebSearch results incl. https://www.herodote.net/Victor_Schoelcher_1804_1893_-synthese-2772.php ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decree_of_the_Abolition_of_Slavery_of_April_27,_1848]
- Danish West Indies: 1847 edict for gradual abolition over 12 years provoked a revolt on St Croix; Governor Peter von Scholten declared immediate emancipation on 3 July 1848 (he was recalled and tried for treason, charges later dropped). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decree_of_the_Abolition_of_Slavery_of_April_27,_1848]

## The Netherlands, 1863
- Dutch abolished the trade in 1814; slavery in the colonies abolished 1 July 1863, freeing c.33,000 in Suriname, 12,000 in Curaçao and dependencies, and an indeterminate number in the East Indies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom]
- Owners compensated ƒ9,867,780 for 32,911 people in Suriname (c.€250m in 2020 terms). The freed were obliged to a 10-year transition of state-supervised plantation labour, so not fully free until 1873; pass laws applied. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ketikoti]
- Ketikoti ("the chain is cut", Sranan Tongo), 1 July; public holiday in Suriname since 1955; Kwakoe statue in Paramaribo (1963); National Slavery Monument in Amsterdam's Oosterpark (2002). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ketikoti]
- After 1873 Suriname imported Indian indentured labour (1873–1916) and later Javanese (from Dutch East Indies). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Suriname]
- Apologies: Prime Minister Mark Rutte apologised on behalf of the state on 19 December 2022 (National Archives, The Hague); King Willem-Alexander apologised at the Keti Koti commemoration in Amsterdam on 1 July 2023 (150 years after 1873). No compensation offered. [source: WebSearch results incl. https://www.dutchnews.nl/2022/12/rutte-apologises-for-slave-trade-human-dignity-was-trampled-underfoot ; https://www.iamexpat.nl/expat-info/dutch-expat-news/keti-koti-2023-why-did-dutch-king-apologise-slavery]

## United States
- Slave population peaked at almost four million in 1861. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution]
- Self-emancipation: May 1861 General Benjamin Butler declared three men who escaped to Union lines at Fort Monroe "contraband of war"; First Confiscation Act 6 August 1861; Second Confiscation Act 17 July 1862. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emancipation_Proclamation]
- Preliminary Proclamation 22 September 1862 (five days after Antietam); final Emancipation Proclamation 1 January 1863, applying to ten states in rebellion and changing the legal status of more than 3.5 million; it excluded the nearly 500,000 enslaved in the loyal border states (Maryland, Delaware, Missouri, Kentucky) and Tennessee; justified as "military necessity". It let freedmen enlist; nearly 200,000 Black men, most formerly enslaved, served in the Union army. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emancipation_Proclamation]
- Juneteenth: 19 June 1865, Major General Gordon Granger's General Order No. 3 at Galveston, Texas; federal holiday since 2021. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juneteenth]
- Thirteenth Amendment: Senate passed 8 April 1864 (38–6); House failed 15 June 1864 (93–65); House passed 31 January 1865 (119–56) after Lincoln's administration promised posts and favours; ratified by 27 of 36 states on 6 December 1865; proclaimed 18 December 1865. Text: "Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States..." (the "punishment for crime" exception, from the Northwest Ordinance 1787, later enabled convict leasing). No compensation to owners for emancipation by Proclamation/amendment. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution]
- Indian Territory: 1866 treaties required the Five Tribes, who had allied with the Confederacy, to abolish slavery. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom]
- Frederick Douglass (born Frederick Augustus Washington Bailey, c. February 1818 – 20 February 1895), Talbot County, Maryland; escaped 3 September 1838 by train from Baltimore, helped by Anna Murray, a free Black woman he married; *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave* (1845), *My Bondage and My Freedom* (1855); toured Ireland and Britain 1845–47 (met Clarkson in 1846); British supporters led by Anna and Ellen Richardson of Newcastle bought his legal freedom from Thomas Auld; founded the *North Star* in Rochester (1847) with £500 from English supporters; motto "Right is of no sex, Truth is of no color...". Speech of 5 July 1852, Corinthian Hall, Rochester, for the Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Douglass]
- Sojourner Truth (born Isabella Bomefree, c.1797 – 26 November 1883), enslaved in Ulster County, New York; Dutch was her first language; walked to freedom with her infant daughter in late 1826; in 1828 won a lawsuit in New York to recover her son Peter, illegally sold to Alabama — one of the first Black women to win such a case against a white man; took the name Sojourner Truth in 1843; *The Narrative of Sojourner Truth* (1850); 1851 speech at the Ohio Women's Convention, Akron. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sojourner_Truth]
- Harriet Tubman (born Araminta Ross, c. March 1822 – 10 March 1913), Dorchester County, Maryland; escaped 1849; made some 13 missions rescuing about 70 people (not hundreds); "never lost a passenger"; during the war a scout and spy, guiding the Combahee Ferry raid (June 1863) that freed more than 700 people. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriet_Tubman]
- Black abolitionism was central (Sinha's argument, see Debates).

## Russia, 1861 (parallel)
- Alexander II to the Moscow nobility, 30 March 1856: "It is better to abolish serfdom from above, than to wait for that time when it starts to abolish itself from below." Defeat in the Crimean War sharpened the case. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emancipation_reform_of_1861]
- Emancipation Manifesto of 3 March 1861 (19 February Old Style): more than 23 million people freed; privately owned serfs were an estimated 38% of the population (Pipes). Household serfs got freedom but no land. State peasants emancipated 1866 on better terms; Georgia 1864. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emancipation_reform_of_1861]
- Terms: two-year transition; "cut-off lands" (otrezki) kept by landlords; peasants paid redemption payments (state loans paid to landlords); land held through the mir commune; redemption made compulsory 1881; redemption payments abolished only in 1907. Unrest followed, e.g. peasants killed at Bezdna, 12 April 1861. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emancipation_reform_of_1861 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serfdom_in_Russia]
- Comparison point: Russian lords were compensated via peasants' redemption payments; US owners got nothing (except in DC, not verified); British, French, Dutch owners were compensated by the state; the freed nowhere received compensation. (Synthesis of facts above.)
- Also: Prussian (Stein-Hardenberg) reforms abolished serfdom from 1807; Hungary's April Laws 1848. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom]

## Spanish empire: Puerto Rico 1873, Cuba 1886
- Cuba imported enslaved Africans long after Spain's 1817 ban; c.90,000 imported 1856–60; the Cuban trade ended only in 1867. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Cuba ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Years%27_War]
- Ten Years' War (1868–78): Carlos Manuel de Céspedes freed his own slaves on proclaiming independence in 1868; peace freed slaves who fought on either side (manumission for those who fought for Spain). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Years%27_War ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom]
- Moret Law, approved 4 July 1870 (Segismundo Moret, Minister of Overseas Territories): freed children born after 18 September 1868, those who served in the Spanish army, the elderly (age given as over 60 on the Moret Law page, over 65 on the timeline — conflict), and state-owned slaves; owners paid 125 pesetas each. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moret_Law ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom]
- Puerto Rico: slavery abolished 1873. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom]
- Law of 13 February 1880 replaced slavery in Cuba with the *patronato*, a bound-labour transition (eight-year limit); *patrocinados* got small stipends and could buy freedom; by 1886 only c.25,000 remained; patronato abolished early by royal decree of 7 October 1886 (some cite 8 October in the Gaceta). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Cuba ; WebSearch results incl. Rebecca Scott, "Gradual Abolition and the Dynamics of Slave Emancipation in Cuba, 1868–86", HAHR 63:3 https://dup.silverchair.com/hahr/article/63/3/449/148684/Gradual-Abolition-and-the-Dynamics-of-Slave]
- Chinese indentured workers to Cuba: c.141,000 in 1847–1874 (Wikipedia elsewhere says "more than 100,000"); almost all men (c.1% women); some 2,000 fought for the rebels in the Ten Years' War (Havana monument: "There was not one Cuban Chinese deserter, not one Cuban Chinese traitor"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Cubans ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Cuba]

## Brazil, 1850–1888
- Brazil received c.5.5 million of the c.12 million Africans brought to the Americas (1540s–1860s), more than any country. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Brazil]
- Feijó law of 7 November 1831 banned imports and freed illegally imported people but was not enforced ("para inglês ver" not verified). Eusébio de Queirós Law (Law 581, 4 September 1850) effectively ended the trade; last known landings 1856. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_Brazil ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom]
- Malê revolt, Salvador, Bahia, night of 24–25 January 1835, during Ramadan: enslaved and freed African Muslims, mostly Nagô (Yoruba) plus Hausa and Nupe, many literate in Arabic; leaders included Ahuna, Pacífico Licutan (in jail; freeing him was a goal), Manoel Calafate, Luís Sanim; c.600 rebels; 70+ rebels killed; betrayed by the ex-slave Sabina da Cruz's report. Aftermath (figures vary): four sentenced to death (infobox says 5 executed), many flogged, some 200 deported to the Bight of Benin. Reis (publisher): more than 500 sentenced to death, prison, whipping or deportation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mal%C3%AA_revolt ; Reis book listing via WebSearch]
- Paraguayan War (1864–70): enslaved soldiers' service turned many officers against slavery; later the army asked not to be used to hunt fugitives. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lei_%C3%81urea ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_Brazil]
- Rio Branco ("Free Womb") Law, 28 September 1871: children of enslaved mothers free but owed service to the mother's owner to age 21; by 1885 only 118 of c.400,000 *ingênuos* had been handed to the state. Chamber vote 65–45. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_Brazil ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lei_%C3%81urea]
- 1872 census: population 10 million, 15% enslaved; about three-quarters of Black and mixed-race Brazilians already free. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Brazil]
- Abolitionists: Brazilian Society Against Slavery founded in Rio 1880 by Joaquim Nabuco and José do Patrocínio; Brazilian Abolitionist Confederation 13 August 1883; André Rebouças; Ave Libertas Society (women, Pernambuco, 1884) freed 200 in its first year; c.230 abolitionist organisations in the 1870s–80s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_Brazil ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lei_%C3%81urea]
- Luís Gama (21 June 1830/31 – 24 August 1882): son of the freed African Luísa Mahin; sold into slavery by his father aged 10; illiterate until 17; won his own freedom in court and became a lawyer freeing captives — "more than 500" (Luís Gama page) or "more than a thousand" (Abolitionism in Brazil page); conflict, use "hundreds". Died six years before abolition. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lu%C3%ADs_Gama ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_Brazil]
- Provincial abolition: Ceará abolished slavery in 1884 (Amazonas same year; one passage on the same page says 1885 — prefer 1884). The 1877–78 Great Drought had driven sales of northeastern slaves south. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Brazil ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_Brazil]
- Saraiva-Cotegipe (Sexagenarian) Law, 28 September 1885: freed those over 60, with further service to 65. Flogging of slaves outlawed 1886 (Law 310, 15 October 1886). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_Brazil ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lei_%C3%81urea]
- Mass flight: after 1885, thousands fled São Paulo plantations, helped by Antônio Bento's *caifazes*, to the Jabaquara quilombo at Santos; slogan "Abolition without compensation". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_Brazil]
- Lei Áurea (Law 3,353): passed Chamber 10 May, Senate 13 May 1888, signed 13 May 1888 by Princess Isabel (regent). Two articles: "From the date of this law, slavery is declared extinct in Brazil. ... All dispositions to the contrary are revoked." No compensation; planter anger contributed to the fall of the monarchy and the Republic of 15 November 1889. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lei_%C3%81urea]
- Number freed in 1888: commonly c.700,000 (one cited count 723,419; some say c.600,000). Give as "roughly 700,000". [source: WebSearch results, Brazilian sources incl. https://vermelho.org.br/2016/05/13/a-abolicao-uma-revolucao-pela-metade/ ; https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/golden-law]
- Memory: the Black movement in Brazil contests 13 May as a gift from Princess Isabel. Nabuco (1883): abolitionism is "a political movement ... born from a different thought: that of rebuilding Brazil on free labor and the union of races in freedom." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_Brazil]
- European (mainly Italian) immigrant labour replaced the enslaved in São Paulo coffee. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_Brazil]

## Africa and the Islamic world
- Paradox of "legitimate commerce": Patrick Manning — if African societies were ever organised around slave production, 1850–1900 "was it"; ending the Atlantic trade redirected slaves into domestic plantation labour producing exports. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Africa]
- Ottoman Empire, gradual and nominal reforms: Istanbul slave market closed 1847 (moved indoors, "cosmetic"); Persian Gulf import ban 1847; Circassian/Georgian trade banned 1854 (then quietly allowed); Firman of 1857 banned the African trade (Hejaz exempted after the Hejaz rebellion); Anglo-Ottoman Convention 1880 on the Red Sea; Kanunname of 30 December 1889 against the African trade; 1908 constitution excluded slavery; women still openly sold in 1908. One source dates a firman declaring slavery not legally recognised to 1887 (timeline says 1882 — conflict). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_Ottoman_Empire ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom]
- Tunisia abolished slavery in 1846 under Ahmed Bey (an early Muslim-world abolition). Egypt: Anglo-Egyptian Slave Trade Convention 1877; slavery abolished 1895. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom]
- Brussels Anti-Slavery Conference, 18 November 1889 – 2 July 1890, produced the Brussels Conference Act 1890 (17 signatories incl. Ottoman Empire), used to legitimise colonial expansion ("lip service to humanitarian goals"). League of Nations Slavery Convention signed 25 September 1926. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels_Anti-Slavery_Conference_1889%E2%80%9390 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1926_Slavery_Convention ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_Ottoman_Empire]
- Zanzibar: Omani-ruled clove and coconut plantations; enslaved people up to two-thirds of the population at peak (estimate); slave trade a third of state income. Moresby Treaty 1822; Frere Treaty 1873 (Sultan Barghash) banned imports from the mainland and closed the Stone Town market (the Anglican cathedral was later built on its site — not verified here); 1890 decree banned sale and made children born after 1890 free; 1897 decree removed the legal status of slavery, but concubines were excluded until 1909. Only c.12,000 applied for freedom 1897–1909, a minority. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Zanzibar]
- Colonial Africa: Gold Coast 1874; Madagascar 1896; British East Africa early 1900s (exact year unclear in the timeline table); French West Africa formal abolition 1905; Sierra Leone domestic slavery 1928; Northern Nigeria 1936 (c.2–2.5 million enslaved in the Sokoto Caliphate area at British conquest); Bechuanaland 1936. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Africa]
- Banamba exodus (French Sudan, now Mali): mass departures of enslaved people began spring 1905 and resumed 1906; Klein argues the exodus, not French policy, destroyed slavery as a labour system there; estimates of numbers leaving vary widely (c.200,000 to up to a million in French West Africa). [source: WebSearch results incl. https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/CBO9780511584138A020/type/BOOK_PART ; https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/banamba-slave-exodus-of-1905-and-the-decline-of-slavery-in-the-western-sudan/7BCFA09F4A279029ACB17C379CB69A28]
- Northern Nigeria: Lovejoy and Hogendorn argue colonial rule abolished the legal status of slavery and encouraged self-purchase rather than freeing people outright; slavery finally abolished 1936. [source: https://www.labyrinthbooks.com/slow-death-for-slavery/ (via WebSearch)]
- Ethiopia: trade abolished on joining the League (1923); Anti-Slavery Society estimated 2 million enslaved in early 1930s; Italian occupation abolished it 1935; reconfirmed 1942. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Africa]
- Arabian peninsula and beyond: Bahrain 1937; Qatar 1952; Saudi Arabia and North Yemen 1962; South Yemen 1967; Oman 1970. Also Qing China 1910 (law of 1906, effective 31 January 1910), Korea 1894, Siam 1912 (trade 1905), Afghanistan 1923, Nepal 1926, Persia 1929. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_abolition_of_slavery_and_serfdom]
- Mauritania: French declared an end in 1905 but did little; 1981 presidential decree made it the last country to abolish slavery, without criminal penalties; criminalised in 2007; 2015 law broadened. Global Slavery Index estimated 90,000 (2.1%) in 2018, down from 155,600 (4%) in 2014. Only one slave-owner, Oumoulmoumnine Mint Bakar Vall, prosecuted (by that report). Hereditary status affects Haratin ("Black Moors") held by Beidane. Activists: SOS Esclaves (Boubacar Messaoud, Abdel Nasser Ould Ethmane); IRA-Mauritania led by Biram Dah Abeid, a descendant of slaves. African Union reprimand 2018. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Mauritania]
- India: Indian Slavery Act 1843 (Act V) barred courts from enforcing slave status and banned sale; Indian Penal Code (1860/61) criminalised slavery; a contemporary estimate put India's slave population in 1841 at 8–9 million, most debt slaves; many were never told of the change and slid into debt bondage. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Slavery_Act,_1843 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_India]

## Indenture: "a new system of slavery"
- More than 1.6 million Indians were transported under indenture (UNESCO Memory of the World nomination figure), from 1834 to the 1920s (ended 1917 for most colonies; Natal 1911). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system]
- Réunion terms of 18 January 1826 (five years, 8 rupees a month); first organised British shipment: the *Atlas*, Calcutta to Mauritius, 1834, 36 labourers; over 25,000 within four years; early-voyage mortality over 17%. First Indian ships to British Guiana and Trinidad 1838 (396 arrived in British Guiana in 1838 — Sir John Gladstone was an early promoter). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coolie]
- Emigration banned 29 May 1839 after an inquiry; resumed to Mauritius 1842 and West Indies 1845. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system]
- Lord John Russell, Colonial Secretary, letter to Governor Light of British Guiana, 15 February 1840: "I am not prepared to encounter the responsibility of a measure which may lead to a dreadful loss of life on the one hand, or, on the other, to a new system of slavery." Prompted by the British and Foreign Anti-Slavery Society's campaign. [source: https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/a-new-system-of-slavery-the-british-west-indies-and-the-origins-of-indian-indenture]
- Hugh Tinker, *A New System of Slavery: The Export of Indian Labour Overseas 1830–1920* (OUP, 1974): argued voluntariness was a legal fiction; reforms cosmetic. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system]
- Conditions: five-year contracts ("girmit"; workers "girmitiyas"; a Fiji emigration notice let emigrants return at their own expense after five years' "industrial residence"); penal labour laws (absence a criminal offence — e.g. Demerara ordinance of 1864); barracks housing; skewed sex ratios (as few as one woman per 40 men on early ships); resistance through strikes, desertion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_India]
- Numbers by colony: Mauritius 453,063; British Guiana 238,909; Natal 152,184; Trinidad 147,596; Réunion 120,507; Fiji 60,965; Jamaica 36,412; Dutch Suriname 34,304; East Africa Protectorate 32,000. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system]
- End: Gopal Krishna Gokhale's 1910 resolution ended indenture to Natal (effective July 1911); the whole system ended 1917 under nationalist pressure and declining profitability. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system]
- Chinese "coolie" trade: 1806 Trinidad experiment; Macau the hub — c.215,000 shipped 1851–1874, mainly to Cuba and Peru; deaths up to 40% on some voyages; kidnapping and debt entrapment; called the "pig trade"; banned at Macau under British pressure (1873/74). Abolitionists (BFASS, Garrison) criticised it; US pro-slavery writers cited it to defend slavery. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coolie]
- Indenture kept growing after 1863 into Dutch Suriname (Indians from 1873, Javanese after 1916). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Suriname]
- Sharecropping, convict leasing (post-US) belong mainly to the Reconstruction/US chapters; here mention only the 13th Amendment's punishment exception.

## Debates
- Economic decline (Williams, 1944) vs "econocide" (Drescher, 1977): Williams argued abolition followed the decline of the West Indian sugar economy (soil exhaustion, falling profits from 1823, rising free-trade interests), and attacked Reginald Coupland's moralised Oxford account ("poetic sentimentality"). Drescher's *Econocide: British Slavery in the Era of Abolition* argued British slavery was still valuable and expanding in 1807, so abolition was a sacrifice driven by public mobilisation. Williams's dissertation (Oxford, supervised by Vincent Harlow, topic suggested by C. L. R. James; *The Black Jacobins* 1938 stated the thesis first) was refused by British publishers; first UK edition 1964 (André Deutsch); Penguin Modern Classics 2022 bestseller. Later positions: Engerman (slave-trade plus plantation profits under 5% of the British economy in any year); David Beck Ryden (2009, evidence of a profitability crisis in the early 19th century, supporting Williams); Selwyn Carrington (defended both theses); Hilary Beckles (2004) on the respect the book enjoys in the Caribbean; Catherine Hall et al. (2014) note a schism between Anglo-American and Caribbean historians; Blackburn calls Williams's schema "mechanical"; Eltis (2025, *Atlantic Cataclysm*) stresses value shifts driven by newspapers. NOTE: the slave-trade chapter already covers the basic Williams/Drescher contrast. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism_and_Slavery ; https://cambridge.org/core/journals/business-history-review/article/econocide-british-slavery-in-the-era-of-abolition-by-seymour-drescher-pittsburgh-university-of-pittsburgh-press-1977-pp-xiv-279-1695/240DFC25B44795F85011F07AEB903522]
- Moral capital (Brown, 2006): rejects both the economic and the "bourgeois humanitarianism" explanations; the crisis of empire around the American Revolution made antislavery a way to claim virtue for Britain and for individuals; abolition mattered to its advocates as much as to the enslaved. Won AHA Rawley Prize 2006 and Frederick Douglass Prize 2007. [source: https://oieahc.wm.edu/publications/books/available-titles/moral-capital/ ; WebSearch summaries]
- The role of slave resistance: Williams himself linked abolition to "self-emancipation of slave rebels" from Haiti to Jamaica 1831; Gelien Matthews (2006) argues the revolts (Barbados 1816, Demerara 1823, Jamaica 1831–32) reshaped British abolitionism, pushing it from "not fit for freedom" gradualism toward immediate emancipation, though abolitionists' responses stayed ambivalent; historians argue the brutality of the Jamaican reprisals and missionaries' testimony (Knibb, Burchell) accelerated the 1833 Act; in Brazil and French Martinique, mass flight and uprising forced the timing (1888; 23 May 1848); in Africa, Klein argues the Banamba exodus, not French policy, ended slavery as a labour system; in the US, flight to Union lines ("contrabands") preceded and shaped the Proclamation. Sinha centres Black abolitionists. [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/books/9780807148907 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism_and_Slavery ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptist_War ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decree_of_the_Abolition_of_Slavery_of_April_27,_1848 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_Brazil ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emancipation_Proclamation]
- Did the establishment resist? Michael Taylor (2020) argues the pro-slavery "West India Interest" had near-universal establishment backing 1823–33. The London Society of West India Planters and Merchants (from 1780; now the West India Committee) organised opposition. [source: https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/439452/the-interest-by-taylor-michael/9781473590441 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_Kingdom]
- Was the West Africa Squadron humanitarian or imperial? (Pape/Kaufmann vs French, Wills, 2021 IJMH paper — see above.) Similarly the Brussels Act 1890 is seen as anti-slavery rhetoric legitimising the Scramble for Africa. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa_Squadron ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels_Anti-Slavery_Conference_1889%E2%80%9390]
- Indenture: "new system of slavery" (Russell 1840, Tinker 1974) vs a harsh but distinct contract system; Evelyn Hu-DeHart frames Chinese labour in Cuba as "free labour or neo-slavery?"; descendants celebrate resilience while historians stress trauma (Bahadur). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Cuba ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coolie]
- Memory and reparations: LBS's "selective forgetting"; French 2001 Taubira law and 2016 symbolic repeal of the 1849 compensation law; Dutch apologies 2022–23; Brazilian Black movement's critique of 13 May; Trevelyan family reparations (2023). [sources above]
