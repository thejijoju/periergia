## 1. Deep past, peopling and diversity (brief — see overlap)
- Peopling: generally accepted that people came from northeast Asia via Beringia; academics generally place humans south of the Laurentide ice sheet between 15,000 and 20,000 years ago; Monte Verde (Chile) c. 14,500 years; White Sands footprints dated 21,000–23,000 years ago; routes (coastal vs ice-free corridor) still debated. [source: PEO]
- Vine Deloria Jr.'s late book *Red Earth, White Lies* rejected the Bering land-bridge account in favour of traditional histories (and embraced human–dinosaur coexistence) — useful as an example of the tension between oral tradition and science, but present carefully. [source: VD]
- Cahokia (across the Mississippi from St Louis), c. 1050–1350 CE; around 1100 it covered about 6 sq mi, with about 120 earthworks and a population of 15,000–20,000 (a footnote: possibly larger than contemporary London or Paris). Published chapter says "perhaps 15,000" — consistent at the low end. [source: CAH]
- Haudenosaunee: the Tuscarora were accepted as the sixth nation in 1722; thereafter "Six Nations". [source: HAU]
- Languages: over a thousand Indigenous languages of the Americas are still used; they belong to over a hundred language families and isolates; Greenberg's "Amerind" grouping is rejected by nearly all specialists. Largest: Southern Quechua and Guarani (perhaps 6–7 million speakers each), then Aymara and Nahuatl (almost 2 million each), K'iche', Q'eqchi' (Kekchi), Yucatec Maya (about 1 million each). USA 2010 census: 372,000 spoke an Indigenous language at home; Canada 2011: 133,000. UNESCO: most are critically endangered. [source: LANG]
- Today: 574 federally recognised tribal nations in the US (229 in Alaska) and 326 reservations (Navajo Nation largest reservation population, 165,158) [source: FRT; RES; NAUS]. **Update:** the Lumbee Tribe of North Carolina received full federal recognition in December 2025 via the Lumbee Fairness Act folded into the National Defense Authorization Act (Senate passage 17 December 2025), after a quest dating to 1888; >55,000 enrolled members, largest recognised tribe east of the Mississippi; the Eastern Band of Cherokee opposed it. So write "about 575" or "more than 570" rather than "574". [source: https://npr.org/2025/12/20/g-s1-103223/lumbee-tribe-federal-recognition-137-years; https://ncnewsline.com/2025/12/17/congress-passes-lumbee-recognition-bill-heads-to-trumps-desk/]
- 2020 US census: 3.7 million (1.1%) American Indian/Alaska Native alone; 9.67 million (2.9%) alone or in combination. [source: NAUS]
- Canada 2021 census: 1,807,250 Indigenous people (5.0%): First Nations, Inuit, Métis; over 600 recognised First Nations governments or bands. [source: IPC]

## 2. Contact and the demographic catastrophe (keep short; cross-refer to *Conquest of the Americas*)
- Hemisphere in 1492: estimates range 8–112 million. Denevan's 1976 "consensus count" about 54 million; his 1992 figure 53.9 million (US+Canada 3.8m; Mexico 17.2m; Central America 5.6m; Caribbean 3m; Andes 15.7m; lowland South America 8.6m). "Most newer estimates ... fall between 45 and 60 million" (Denevan 1992, Alchon 2003); a 2018 carbon-record study says just over 60 million. Consistent with the published *Conquest* chapter's "40 to 60 million". [source: POP]
- North of Mexico (US+Canada) estimates in the POP table: Kroeber 1939: 0.9 million; Dobyns 1966: 9.8–12.25 million; Ubelaker 1988: 1.2–2.6 million; Denevan 1992: 3.79 million; Snow 2001: 3.44 million; Alchon 2003: 3.5 million; Thornton 2005: 7 million; Milner 2010: 3.8 million. Text: "most estimates range from 2.5 million to 7 million people, with one study estimating up to 18 million". Dobyns raised his figure to about 18 million in *Their Number Become Thinned* (University of Tennessee Press, 1983). Consistent with the published *North America* chapter (Kroeber "about one million" — POP table 0.9m; Dobyns 1983 18m; "a few million to around seven million"). [source: POP; WebSearch: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_F._Dobyns; https://cambridge.org/core/journals/american-antiquity/article/abs/their-number-become-thinned-native-american-population-dynamics-in-eastern-north-america-henry-f-dobyns-university-of-tennessee-press-knoxville-1983-xvii-378-pp-biblio-index-maps-figures-table-2995-cloth-and-1495-paper/4D80B50E7627C0B6E97BAE7E6C7FEFC9]
- Canada c. late 15th century: estimates between 200,000 and two million. [source: POP]
- Ideology of estimates: Kroeber's low hemisphere figure (8.4 million) reflected, per POP, early-20th-century assumptions of racial superiority; Francis Jennings: "Scholarly wisdom long held that Indians were so inferior in mind and works that they could not possibly have created or sustained large populations." David Henige (1998) attacked high estimates as "arbitrary formulas" applied to unreliable sources. [source: POP]
- A 2019 study: >60 million in 1492 falling to 6 million by 1600, the reforestation lowering atmospheric CO2 and contributing to the Little Ice Age; other studies dispute it. Populations in most areas reached their low point by the early 20th century. [source: POP]
- US nadir: Census Bureau counted 248,253 American Indians in 1890 and 237,196 in 1900 (Office of Indian Affairs: 249,278 and 270,544), plus c. 25,000–30,000 Alaska Natives. [source: NAUS table]
- Indigenous enslavement: one estimate cited in POP — 2,462,000 to 4,985,000 Amerindians enslaved between Columbus's arrival and 1900 (Reséndez). Keep for *Afro-Indigenous* chapter mostly. [source: POP]

## 3. Settler colonialism: the framework
- Patrick Wolfe (1949 – 18 February 2016), English-born historian who worked in Australia, never held a permanent university post; credited with establishing settler colonial studies. Works: *Settler Colonialism and the Transformation of Anthropology* (1999); *Traces of History: Elementary Structures of Race* (2016). [source: PW]
- In Wolfe's words settler colonialism is "a structure not an event"; it seeks to "eliminate the native" rather than exploit native labour; for Wolfe and Lorenzo Veracini it is "structural, eliminatory, and land based", unlike "franchise" colonialism; elimination can take the form of "child abduction, religious conversion, resocialization in total institutions such as missions or boarding schools". [source: SC]
- Wolfe, "Settler colonialism and the elimination of the native", *Journal of Genocide Research* 8:4 (December 2006), 387–409: settler-colonial elimination and genocide are distinct, though the former has sometimes taken genocidal form. [source: WebSearch: https://vuir.vu.edu.au/1831/; https://bibbase.org/network/publication/wolfe-settlercolonialismandtheeliminationofthenative-2006]
- The fuller line "Settler colonizers come to stay: invasion is a structure not an event" is attributed to Wolfe's 1999 book (J. Kēhaulani Kauanui's 2016 essay "'A Structure, Not an Event'" discusses it, warning the phrase is often quoted instead of engaging with the theory). [source: WebSearch: https://csalateral.org/?p=351] — page number not verified; quote only the short form.
- SC also notes the field was established from the mid-1990s, drawing on earlier work (Fayez Sayegh 1965, Denoon 1983, Stasiulis and Yuval-Davis 1995); Wolfe himself said "I didn't invent Settler Colonial Studies". [source: SC; WebSearch result summary of SC]

## 4. Law, treaties and removal
- *Johnson v. M'Intosh* (decided 28 February 1823; 21 U.S. 543): Marshall's opinion laid the foundations of aboriginal title and the "doctrine of discovery" in US law; tribes' title is inalienable except to the federal government. (The case was probably collusive: the parcels did not actually overlap.) [source: JvM]
- *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831): Court declined jurisdiction; Marshall described the Cherokee Nation as a "domestic dependent nation" whose relation to the US resembles "that of a ward to its guardian". Early 1800s Cherokee held about 53,000 sq mi in Tennessee, North Carolina, Georgia, Alabama. [source: CNvG]
- *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832; 31 U.S. 515): Georgia law requiring whites in Cherokee territory to have a state licence held unconstitutional; foundation of tribal sovereignty doctrine. Worcester, missionary, worked with Elias Boudinot on the *Cherokee Phoenix*, the first Native American newspaper. Jackson's "John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it!" is **believed apocryphal** — it first appeared in Horace Greeley's 1864 history, twenty years after Jackson's death. [source: WvG]
- Sequoyah created the Cherokee syllabary in the 1820s. [source: CHER]
- **Indian Removal Act**: signed by Andrew Jackson 28 May 1830; Senate 28–19 (24 April), House 102–97 (26 May); provided "for an exchange of lands with the Indians residing in any of the states or territories, and for their removal west of the river Mississippi". Under Jackson and Van Buren more than 60,000 people from at least 18 nations were moved west; southern nations mostly to Indian Territory (Oklahoma), northern nations initially to Kansas. Opposed by the nations themselves, the Whigs and some missionaries. Scholars since the 2000s call removal ethnic cleansing, genocide or settler colonialism (some all three). [source: IRA30]
- **Trail of Tears**: forced removal of about 60,000 people of the Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Seminole, Chickasaw and Choctaw nations, 1830–1850, including enslaved Black people (over 4,000 people of African descent accompanied the removals). Total deaths 13,200–16,700 (TOT infobox: Cherokee 4,000; Creek and Seminole in the Second Seminole War 3,000; Chickasaw 3,500; Choctaw 2,500–6,000). By 1837, 46,000 had been removed. Scholars variously call it genocide or ethnic cleansing. [source: TOT] Present all totals as estimates.
- Cherokee removal: Treaty of New Echota, 29 December 1835, signed by the minority Treaty Party (Elias Boudinot), not by Principal Chief John Ross or the National Council; over 16,000 Cherokee signed a petition against it; Senate ratified March 1836. Georgia gold rush from 1828 (Dahlonega). About 2,000 left voluntarily; forced removal began May 1838 under Gen. Winfield Scott, internment camps, then groups of c. 1,000 travelling through winter 1838–39. Deaths: "approximately 4,000" in the text; TOT's table gives 4,000–8,000 (Thornton 1991 argued for higher losses; Dunbar-Ortiz says half of 16,000 died). Cherokee name: *nu na da ul tsun yi*, "the place where they cried" (also "the trail where they cried"). [source: TOT]
- Theda Perdue: Cherokee women, who had negotiated land transactions as late as 1785, had no seat at New Echota; "Cherokee women met in their own councils". [source: TOT]
- A Seminole remnant of fewer than 500 evaded removal; today's Seminole Tribe of Florida descends from them (Black Seminoles → *Afro-Indigenous* chapter). [source: TOT]
- Robert Remini (Jackson's biographer) argued removal saved the Five Tribes from annihilation — the apologist pole of the debate. [source: TOT]

## 5. The West: wars, treaties, reservations (keep Sand Creek to a cross-reference)
- 1851 Indian Appropriations Act funded moving western tribes onto reservations; the Act of 3 March 1871 ended treaty-making with tribes (Congress no longer recognised them as independent nations for treaty purposes). [source: IAA]
- California: estimated population up to 300,000 before Spanish arrival; c. 150,000 (1848) → 30,000 (1870) → 16,000 (1900). Settlers killed an estimated 9,492–16,094 California Natives 1846–1873 (Benjamin Madley); 10,000–27,000 subjected to forced labour. Gov. Gavin Newsom in 2019 called it "genocide" and apologised. [source: CAG]
- Dakota War of 1862 (Minnesota): began 18 August 1862 amid starvation and broken annuity promises; hundreds of settlers killed; a military commission sentenced 303 Dakota men to death; Lincoln approved 39; **38 hanged at Mankato, 26 December 1862**, the largest mass execution in US history; Dakota exiled, Minnesota confiscated their land. [source: DAK]
- Long Walk of the Navajo (Diné) to Bosque Redondo, 1864–1868 (Carleton, Kit Carson): deaths 2,500–3,500 during the march and internment. [source: LW]
- Fort Laramie Treaty (1868): created the Great Sioux Reservation including the Black Hills, "set apart for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation of the Indians"; cession required three-quarters of adult men; ended Red Cloud's War. Custer's 1874 expedition found gold in the Black Hills. [source: FL68; SIOUX]
- Little Bighorn ("Greasy Grass" to the Lakota), 25–26 June 1876: Lakota, Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho, led by Crazy Horse and Gall and inspired by Sitting Bull (Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake)'s visions, defeated the 7th Cavalry (c. 700 men); Custer and 268 US dead; Native losses 31–100 killed. LBH also notes the battlefield lay on the Crow reservation and the Crow and Arikara allied with the US against Lakota expansion — a complexity worth one sentence. [source: LBH]
- *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians* (1980): the 1877 taking of the Black Hills required just compensation ($17.1 million plus interest); the Sioux have refused the money (over $2 billion by 2022) and demand the land. [source: SIOUX]
- Ghost Dance: began among the Northern Paiute (Nevada) in 1889 with Wovoka (Jack Wilson)'s prophecy; the Lakota form was more millenarian; settlers called the crisis the "Messiah War". [source: GD; WK90]
- **Wounded Knee Massacre**, 29 December 1890, Pine Ridge Reservation (Lakota: Čhaŋkpé Ópi Wakpála): 7th Cavalry under Col. James Forsyth, with four Hotchkiss guns, surrounded Spotted Elk's Miniconjou band (plus 38 Hunkpapa) during a disarmament; 250–300 Lakota killed and 51 wounded (47 of them women and children); 25 soldiers killed (many probably by friendly fire — not verified, omit); 19 Medals of Honor for Wounded Knee. [source: WK90]

## 6. Allotment, assimilation and citizenship
- **Dawes Act** (General Allotment Act), 1887, sponsored by Sen. Henry L. Dawes of Massachusetts: divided communal land into individual allotments (e.g. 80 acres for single adults, 40 for minors per the act's schedule — the commonly cited 160 acres for heads of family not checked; omit), held in trust 25 years; "surplus" opened to white settlers; required a federal definition of "Indian" (blood quantum). 1887–1934: Native nations lost about 100 million acres, about "two-thirds of the land base they held in 1887". Curtis Act (1898) extended allotment to the Five Tribes and abolished their governments and courts, preparing Oklahoma statehood; Burke Act 1906. [source: DAWES]
- Indian Citizenship Act, signed by Coolidge 2 June 1924: "all non citizen Indians born within the territorial limits of the United States ... are hereby, declared to be citizens", without impairing tribal property rights; partly in recognition of WWI service. Voting remained state-governed: seven states still barred Indians from voting in 1938; full suffrage only by 1948 in some accounts (NAUS/ICA). [source: ICA]
- Virginia's Racial Integrity Act (1924) and registrar Walter Plecker erased Native identity in records ("white" or "colored" only); Virginia had no federally recognised tribes until 2017. → *Afro-Indigenous* chapter for detail. [source: NAUS]

## 7. US boarding schools
- Carlisle Indian Industrial School, Pennsylvania, founded 1879 by Army officer Richard Henry Pratt (who earlier ran the Fort Marion prisoner-of-war programme); the model off-reservation school; "outing system" placed students with white families. [source: PRATT; BS]
- Pratt, 1892 speech: "A great general has said that the only good Indian is a dead one. In a sense, I agree with the sentiment, but only in this: that all the Indian there is in the race should be dead." Associated phrase: "kill the Indian, save the man". [source: BS; PRATT]
- Practices: haircuts, uniforms, bans on Native languages, renaming, Christianisation, corporal punishment; deaths mainly from infectious disease; later investigations found physical, emotional and sexual abuse. Julie Davis: schools were sites of "both victimization and agency" and of "both cultural loss and cultural persistence". [source: BS]
- Meriam Report (*The Problem of Indian Administration*), commissioned 1926 from the Brookings Institution, submitted 21 February 1928 — condemned conditions. [source: BS]
- Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative (Interior Secretary Deb Haaland, first Native American cabinet secretary — Laguna Pueblo; Assistant Secretary Bryan Newland): Volume I, May 2022; Volume II, 30 July 2024: at least **973** American Indian, Alaska Native and Native Hawaiian children died at federal schools (a minimum); **417** federal institutions in 37 states/territories; 74 marked and unmarked burial sites at 65 schools. [source: WebSearch: https://sourcenm.com/2024/07/30/at-least-973-native-american-children-died-at-u-s-run-boarding-schools-between-1871-1969/; https://www.nicwa.org/?p=32083] (Coverage gives 1871–1969 for the deaths; one outlet says 1819–1969 — give no range, or "from the nineteenth century to 1969".) Haaland's tribe not checked in sources read — say only "the first Native American cabinet secretary" if checked; otherwise omit.
- **President Joe Biden formally apologised** on behalf of the US government in October 2024 (at Gila River Indian Community, Arizona — location not verified in BS text; say only "October 2024"). [source: BS]

## 8. Canada: Indian Act, residential schools, TRC, graves
- Indian Act, royal assent 12 April 1876: governs reserves, band councils and who has "Indian status"; patrilineal status rules (women marrying non-status men lost status, until Bill C-31, 1985); "enfranchisement" (renouncing status) from the 1857 Gradual Civilization Act until 1985. Potlatch banned by an 1884 amendment effective 1885, Sun Dance 1895; 1920 amendments restricted traditional dress and dances. [source: IA; IPC]
- Residential schools: compulsory from 1894; about 30% of Indigenous children attended by the 1930s; around **150,000** children over the system's history; last federally funded school closed 1997; most deaths from disease, especially tuberculosis. [source: RS]
- Deaths: "at least 4,118 students" documented through TRC research (RS); "over 4,000 documented, with estimates that the full number is over 6,000" (TRC chair Murray Sinclair suggested more than 6,000). [source: RS]
  **Conflict with published *British Empire* chapter**: it says the TRC "documented about 3,200 deaths (its successor centre has since named over 4,100)". Its own research note (modern-era-19th-century--british-empire.md, line 123) gives "at least 4,118". The ~3,200 figure (3,201 named in the 2015 final report) is widely cited but **not verified** in sources read here. Recommend: "at least 4,100 documented deaths, perhaps over 6,000" and avoid attributing 3,200 unless checked; flag the *British Empire* chapter for a possible fix.
- Misattribution: "kill the Indian in the child" is often wrongly attributed to Duncan Campbell Scott (Mark Abley); the phrase derives from Pratt's US slogan. [source: RS]
- Apologies: Stephen Harper's government apology, **11 June 2008** (after a unanimous Commons motion of 1 May 2007 by MP Gary Merasty, Peter Ballantyne Cree Nation); Pope Francis apologised in 2022 (April in Rome; penitential pilgrimage July 2022, Maskwacis, Alberta) and called it genocide; House of Commons unanimously called for recognising it as genocide, October 2022. [source: RS; GRAVES]
- **TRC**: established 1 June 2008 under the Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement ($60 million over five years); chaired by Justice Murray Sinclair from 10 June 2009 (with Marie Wilson and Wilton Littlechild) after the first chair resigned; executive summary and **94 Calls to Action**, June 2015; final multi-volume report December 2015 concluded the system was **"cultural genocide"**; National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation opened at University of Manitoba, November 2015. [source: TRC]
- Sixties Scoop: mid-1950s to 1980s, an estimated 20,000 Indigenous children taken into foster care or adoption, mostly by white middle-class families (official estimate 11,132 adopted 1960–1990); term coined in the early 1980s by BC social workers. [source: SS; RS]
- **2021 unmarked graves**: In May 2021 Tk'emlúps te Secwépemc announced that a ground-penetrating radar (GPR) survey of the former Kamloops Indian Residential School orchard by anthropologist Sarah Beaulieu had found about 215 (later revised to about 200) anomalies interpreted as "probable burials"/"targets of interest"; Beaulieu said "only forensic investigation with excavation" could confirm human remains. On 24 June 2021 Cowessess First Nation (Chief Cadmus Delorme) announced up to 751 unmarked graves at the Marieval cemetery — a community cemetery used since 1885 holding adults and children, Native and non-Native; headstones had been bulldozed by a priest in the 1960s. Earlier remains had been found (Battleford 1974, 72 bodies; Muscowequan 1992, 19 bodies). [source: GRAVES; KIRS]
- **Debate**: As of September 2024 no human remains had been excavated or confirmed at Kamloops; critics note the anomalies are unverified. As of December 2025 only St. Joseph's school at Fort Resolution had found remains on radar/dog evidence; partial excavations at three other schools found none (e.g. Pine Creek/Minegoziibe Anishinabe, August 2023: 14 anomalies, animal bones, no human remains). Many communities decline excavation (no consensus on disturbing burials). The disputes fed a "residential school denialism" movement; Indigenous groups and academics reject "mass grave hoax" claims, noting "mass graves" featured in a minority of media stories and public misreading of what was announced. Justice Minister David Lametti (2023) was open to outlawing denialism; successor Arif Virani took no position. Church arsons and vandalism followed from June 2021. Over 4,000 documented student deaths are not in dispute. [source: GRAVES; KIRS] Writer: say "possible unmarked graves", never "mass graves discovered".
- Idle No More: founded December 2012 by three First Nations women and one non-Native ally against Bill C-45; round dances, blockades; Attawapiskat Chief Theresa Spence's liquid-diet hunger strike. [source: INM]
- *Delgamuukw v. British Columbia* (Supreme Court of Canada, 11 December 1997): Gitxsan and Wet'suwet'en elders testified with oral histories in their own languages; the Court held the trial judge erred in his treatment of oral histories, which can be admitted as evidence of occupation for Aboriginal title; ordered a new trial. [source: WebSearch: https://epe.lac-bac.gc.ca/100/205/301/ic/cdc/aboriginaldocs/court/html/c-delgamuukw-text.htm; https://journals.library.ualberta.ca/psur/index.php/psur/article/view/46] — key example for "oral tradition as evidence".

## 9. The Indian New Deal, termination and relocation
- **Indian Reorganization Act** (Wheeler–Howard Act), 18 June 1934: centrepiece of the "Indian New Deal" of BIA Commissioner John Collier (1933–45); ended allotment, restored management of assets, encouraged tribal constitutions; Felix S. Cohen and Lucy Kramer Cohen helped draft. Self-government provisions applied unless a majority voted no: 172 tribes accepted, 75 rejected — including the Navajo, embittered by livestock reduction (half their herds taken). Critics then and since: IRA constitutions imposed a standard model. [source: IRA34; DAWES]
- **Termination**: mid-1940s to mid-1960s; House Concurrent Resolution 108, 1 August 1953; Public Law 280 (1953) gave some states jurisdiction; 1953–1964 recognition was terminated for more than 100 tribes and bands, affecting more than 12,000 people (c. 3% of Native Americans) (e.g. Menominee, Klamath — named in standard accounts; TERM lists many rancherias). Sen. Arthur Watkins of Utah compared it to the Emancipation Proclamation. Ben Nighthorse Campbell (Northern Cheyenne), 2007: "In Washington's infinite wisdom, it was decided that tribes should no longer be tribes, never mind that they had been tribes for thousands of years." [source: TERM; AIM]
- **Relocation**: Indian Relocation Act 1956 (Public Law 959) paid moves and vocational training in cities (Los Angeles, Denver, Chicago, etc.). In 1950 only 6% of American Indians lived in cities. By 1960 over 31,000 had moved since 1952, about 30% returned; between the 1950s and 1980s as many as 750,000 migrated to cities, some under the programme. [source: REL]

## 10. Red Power
- Red Power: youth-led movement for self-determination; groups included the National Indian Youth Council (fish-ins in the Pacific Northwest from the 1960s) and AIM; the phrase is attributed to Vine Deloria Jr.; NCAI founded 1944. Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission (1975) grew from the fish-ins. [source: RP]
- **Alcatraz**: 20 November 1969 – 11 June 1971 (19 months), "Indians of All Tribes", led by Richard Oakes (Mohawk), LaNada Means and others; John Trudell as spokesman (daily radio broadcasts); started with 89 people. Claimed the island under the 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty (surplus federal land) and "by right of discovery" (proclamation by Adam Fortunate Eagle). Precedent: Belva Cottier (Rosebud Lakota) organised a 4-hour landing on 8 March 1964. Nixon's special counsel Leonard Garment negotiated. Oakes shot dead in 1972. [source: ALC]
- **AIM**: founded Minneapolis, July 1968, initially against police brutality and urban poverty; leaders Dennis Banks, Clyde and Vernon Bellecourt, Russell Means; Trail of Broken Treaties to Washington, October 1972, with a Twenty-Point proposal (occupied the BIA building — not verified in text read; say "protest in Washington"). Targeted by FBI COINTELPRO-style surveillance. [source: AIM; ALC]
- **Wounded Knee 1973**: began 27 February 1973; about 200 Oglala Lakota and AIM members seized Wounded Knee on Pine Ridge after a failed attempt (by the Oglala Sioux Civil Rights Organization) to impeach tribal president Richard Wilson; 71-day siege by US Marshals and FBI; a marshal paralysed; Frank Clearwater (Cherokee/Apache) died 25 April and Lawrence "Buddy" Lamont (Oglala) killed 26 April; civil-rights activist Ray Robinson disappeared — later determined killed and buried on the reservation, allegedly by AIM members. Truce followed Lamont's death. [source: WK73]
- Vine Deloria Jr. (26 March 1933 – 13 November 2005), Standing Rock Sioux (Dakota/Lakota family — tribal affiliation not stated in VD lead; omit or say "Lakota/Dakota" only if checked): NCAI executive director 1964–67 (member tribes from 19 to 156); *Custer Died for Your Sins: An Indian Manifesto* (1969), eleven essays incl. "Anthropologists and Other Friends", "The Disastrous Policy of Termination", "Indian Humor"; founded the first US master's programme in American Indian Studies at the University of Arizona (professor 1978–90); later at Colorado Boulder. [source: VD; CDYS]
- Native American Rights Fund began 1970 (pilot under California Indian Legal Services), now in Boulder. [source: NARF] (founding year from general knowledge — NARF lead gives origin only; say "founded around 1970" or omit year.)

## 11. Self-determination and sovereignty today
- Indian Civil Rights Act 1968. Nixon's Special Message on Indian Affairs, 8 July 1970, repudiated forced termination and sought "a new era in which the Indian future is determined by Indian acts and Indian decisions." [source: SD; TERM]
- Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (Public Law 93-638), signed 4 January 1975: tribes can contract to run federal programmes. [source: ISDEAA]
- **ICWA** (Indian Child Welfare Act), enacted 8 November 1978: Association on American Indian Affairs surveys (1969, 1974) found 25–35% of Native children in states with large Native populations removed from families, mostly to non-Native homes; ICWA gives tribes exclusive jurisdiction for children domiciled on reservations, presumptive jurisdiction otherwise. *Haaland v. Brackeen*, decided 15 June 2023, 7–2: Court upheld ICWA against Texas and others; equal-protection claims dismissed for lack of standing. Cherokee Nation, Oneida, Quinault, Morongo intervened; the Navajo Nation had challenged the Brackeens' adoption. [source: ICWA; HvB]
- **NAGPRA**: enacted 16 November 1990 (Public Law 101-601; introduced by Rep. Mo Udall): federally funded institutions must return human remains, funerary objects, sacred objects and objects of cultural patrimony to lineal descendants, tribes and Native Hawaiian organisations; trafficking criminalised (penalties raised 2022). Revised regulations effective **12 January 2024**: institutions "must defer to the Native American traditional knowledge of lineal descendants, Indian Tribes, and Native Hawaiian organizations" (led to many museum gallery closures — not verified; omit). Kennewick Man (found 28 July 1996, c. 9,000 years old): scientists vs Umatilla, Colville, Yakama, Nez Perce; 2015 DNA showed him "more closely related to modern Native Americans than any other living population"; Congress in 2016 returned him for reburial. [source: NAGPRA]
- **McGirt v. Oklahoma** (decided 9 July 2020, 5–4; Gorsuch with Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor, Kagan; Roberts dissenting with Alito, Kavanaugh, Thomas): the Muscogee (Creek) reservation was never disestablished and remains "Indian country" under the Major Crimes Act. Gorsuch: "Today we are asked whether the land these treaties promised remains an Indian reservation for purposes of federal criminal law. Because Congress has not said otherwise, we hold the government to its word." Oklahoma courts extended it to other nations, so much of eastern Oklahoma is Indian country; *Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta* (2022) gave states concurrent jurisdiction over crimes by non-Indians against Indians; Oklahoma's appeals court held McGirt not retroactive. [source: McG]
- **Standing Rock / #NoDAPL**: April 2016 – 23 February 2017. Youth ("ReZpect Our Water"), LaDonna Brave Bull Allard (tribal historian) and Joye Braun (Indigenous Environmental Network) began the water protectors' camp; members of more than 300 federally recognised tribes were in the camps. Pipeline crosses under Lake Oahe near the reservation; threat to water and burial sites; Chairman David Archambault II invoked the 1851 Traverse des Sioux and 1868 Fort Laramie treaties at the UN Human Rights Council. Army denied the easement 4 December 2016; Trump ordered construction to proceed 24 January 2017; oil flowed from May 2017. Judge Boasberg (2020) found the environmental review inadequate and ordered shutdown, overturned on appeal 5 August 2020. In 2025 a North Dakota jury ordered Greenpeace to pay $345 million to Energy Transfer; litigation ongoing. [source: DAPL]
- **UNDRIP**: adopted by the UN General Assembly 13 September 2007, 143–4 (Australia, Canada, New Zealand, US against), 11 abstentions; non-binding; all four later reversed (US: Obama, 16 December 2010, would "lend its support"; Canada removed objector status May 2016 and passed Bill C-15, royal assent 21 June 2021). UN: "the planet's 370 million indigenous people". [source: UNDRIP]

## 12. Language revitalisation
- Wôpanâak (Wampanoag) revival led by linguist Jessie Little Doe Baird using colonial-era written records including a Bible translation; children speaking it fluently for the first time in over 100 years. Chochenyo (California) revival under way. Apps in Cree, Cherokee, Chickasaw, Lakota, Ojibwe, Oneida, Navajo and others; Navajo and Hawaiian added to Duolingo 2018. Language nests (Māori and Hawaiian models). Debate: whether revived languages heavily shaped by English (e.g. "Neo-Hawaiian") are new languages. [source: LR]

## 13. The field
- Native American (and Indigenous) Studies draws on anthropology, history, literature, law and political science; Elizabeth Cook-Lynn (Crow Creek Lakota) named its two key concepts as **Indigeneity** and **sovereignty**; sovereignty distinguishes it from other ethnic studies; Cook-Lynn, "Who Stole Native American Studies?" (*Wíčazo Ša Review*, 1997). First Convocation of American Indian Scholars, Princeton, March 1970. [source: NAS]
- University of Minnesota's Department of American Indian Studies, approved by the Regents June 1969, is described as the first such department; Roger Buffalohead (Ponca) chaired 1969–75; founded amid AIM's Minneapolis activism. (Other early programmes: UC Berkeley, San Francisco State 1968–69 — not verified.) [source: WebSearch: https://cla.umn.edu/ais/about/history; https://mndaily.com/218918/uncategorized/library-exhibit-highlights-40-year-old-programs/]
- Linda Tuhiwai Smith (Ngāti Awa and Ngāti Porou, Māori), *Decolonizing Methodologies: Research and Indigenous Peoples* (1999), opens: "The word itself, 'research', is probably one of the dirtiest words in the indigenous world's vocabulary." Argues Western research is "inextricably linked to European imperialism and colonialism"; proposes kaupapa Māori research. Rutherford Medal 2023. A founder of the 1970s activist group Ngā Tamatoa. [source: DM; LTS]
- Ned Blackhawk (Te-Moak Tribe of Western Shoshone), Yale historian; *Violence over the Land: Indians and Empires in the Early American West* (2006; Turner Award, Utley Prize); *The Rediscovery of America: Native Peoples and the Unmaking of U.S. History* (Yale UP, 2023; National Book Award for Nonfiction 2023; Mark Lynton History Prize 2024) — argues Native peoples were central to, not peripheral to, US history. [source: NB; RDA]

## Debates
- **How many people in 1492, and why it matters.** Low counters (Kroeber 1939) vs high counters (Dobyns 1966, 1983) vs critics of both (Henige 1998; Thornton challenges Malthusian assumptions behind high figures yet himself gives ~7 million north of Mexico). Current hemisphere consensus 45–60 million; north of Mexico 2.5–7 million, outlier 18 million. [source: POP]
- **Disease vs. violence; genocide?** Stannard (*American Holocaust*, 1993) rejects "inadvertent" disease narratives and gives close to 100 million deaths; Thornton stresses epidemics spreading even without direct contact and calls warfare a comparatively minor cause overall; Cambridge World History of Genocide and others find deliberate genocide in some cases (California 1846–73 is the most widely accepted; Newsom used the word in 2019). Removal: "ethnic cleansing", "genocide", "settler colonialism" all used; Remini defends Jackson. Ostler (genocide) vs Hämäläinen (Native power) is a live contemporary framing contrast. [source: POP; CAG; TOT; WebSearch: https://znetwork.org/znetarticle/contest-or-conquest/]
- **Settler colonialism as a framework.** Wolfe/Veracini: structural, eliminatory, land-based; Veracini: all genocide is settler-colonial but not all settler colonialism genocidal; Sai Englert and others criticise the elimination-vs-exploitation binary; Kauanui warns the slogan substitutes for engagement and that Indigeneity endures beyond the structure. [source: SC; WebSearch: https://csalateral.org/?p=351]
- **Residential schools: "cultural genocide" or genocide?** TRC 2015 "cultural genocide"; Pope Francis and the House of Commons (2022) "genocide"; "scholarly debate over terminology" continues alongside denialism. [source: RS; TRC]
- **2021 graves.** Ground-penetrating radar identifies anomalies, not bodies; few excavations; some have found nothing; communities weigh whether to disturb the dead; denialists exploit the gaps; Indigenous scholars say the documented deaths and missing children are the issue, not "mass graves". [source: GRAVES; KIRS]
- **Boarding schools: victimisation and agency.** Recent scholarship (Julie Davis) stresses both loss and persistence; Ward Churchill's *Kill the Indian, Save the Man* (2004) argues genocide in intent. [source: BS; KTI] (Churchill is a controversial figure — dismissed from Colorado in 2007 for research misconduct, not verified here; do not lean on him.)
- **NAGPRA, science and sovereignty.** Kennewick Man: scientists' study rights vs tribes' claims; DNA ultimately supported tribal ancestry. [source: NAGPRA]
- **IRA**: emancipation or imposition? Collier reversed allotment but 75 tribes, including the Navajo, rejected the act. [source: IRA34]
- **Oral tradition as evidence**: *Delgamuukw* (1997) admitted it in court; Deloria's *Red Earth, White Lies* shows its use against scientific consensus — the writer should present the method seriously while noting limits. [source: VD; WebSearch Delgamuukw links]
