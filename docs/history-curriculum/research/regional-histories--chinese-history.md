**Conflicts and consistency notes (published text compared with the sources read here):**

4. **Erlitou dates.** Ancient China gives 1900–1500 BCE. ERL notes that radiocarbon studies since 2007 narrow it to **1750–1500 BC**. Say "c. 1750–1500 BCE (older estimates c. 1900)".

## 1. Neolithic and Bronze Age beginnings
- The Neolithic in China is considered to have begun about 10,000 years ago, at different times in different regions. The earliest evidence of cultivated rice, found by the Yangtze, is carbon-dated to about 8,000 years ago; millet farming in the Yellow River valley to about 7000 BC. Jiahu (7000–5800 BC) is one of the best-preserved early farming villages. [source: HC]
- So China had at least two farming heartlands: rice in the Yangzi basin and millet in the Yellow River valley. Pigs and dogs were the earliest domesticated animals; cattle, sheep and wheat arrived from Western Asia after about 3000 BC. [source: HC]
- The Yangshao culture, on the middle Yellow River, dates c. 5000–3000 BC and is named after a site found in 1921 by the Swedish geologist Johan Gunnar Andersson. His theory of a western origin is now considered weak. The later Longshan culture dates c. 3000–2000 BC. [source: YS; HC]
- The Liangzhu culture of the Yangtze delta (3300–2300 BC) was highly stratified: jade, silk, ivory and lacquer appear only in elite burials. It had a walled urban centre near Hangzhou, paddy rice and irrigation, and dam-like structures thought to be flood defences. It is described as perhaps among the oldest state societies in East Asia. The ruins of Liangzhu City became a UNESCO World Heritage Site on 6 July 2019. [source: LZ]
- The Liangzhu culture disappeared from the Taihu area after about 2300 BC. Floods are the leading explanation; a 2021 study links its end to decades of extreme rainfall. [source: LZ]
- Erlitou (Henan), excavated from 1959, dates to 1750–1500 BC by radiocarbon studies since 2007 (earlier estimate 1900–1500). Most archaeologists consider it the first state-level society in China. Chinese archaeologists generally identify it with the later Xia; some Western scholars call this premature because no writing has been found there. [source: ERL]
- Sanxingdui (Sichuan) represents a previously unknown Bronze Age culture; the site was first found in 1929 and rediscovered in 1986. [source: HC]
- HC stresses that Xia, Shang and early Zhou may have been overlapping polities rather than strict successors; the single line of dynasties is a later construction. [source: HC]

## 2. Shang: oracle bones and the origins of writing (covered in *Ancient China*; keep short)
- Shang c. 1600–1046 BC. The Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project dates the beginning c. 1600 BC (a walled Erligang city at Yanshi) and the end to 1046 BC. Alternative end dates include 1045 (Nivison and Shaughnessy), 1027 (from the "old text" Bamboo Annals) and the traditional 1122 (calculated by Liu Xin in the Han). [source: SHANG; ZHOU]
- The final capital was Yin (Yinxu, near Anyang), from the reign of Wu Ding, c. 1250 BC. At least 29 kings reigned. [source: HC]
- Oracle bones: ox shoulder blades and turtle plastrons were heated and the cracks read; the question and the interpretation were carved on the same piece. About 150,000 inscriptions are known, most from Yinxu. Inscriptions from Wu Ding's reign are radiocarbon-dated to 1254–1197 BC (±10 years). [source: OBS]
- The script contains about 4,000 distinct characters; "the majority … still remain undeciphered", and scholars can read about 1,500–2,000. It is the direct ancestor of the Chinese script and of "over a dozen East Asian writing systems". [source: OBS]
- Wang Yirong recognised the inscriptions as ancient writing in 1899; pharmacists had been selling them as "dragon bones". [source: OBS; SHANG]
- The Shang practised human sacrifice; royal tombs and sacrificial remains have been excavated at Yinxu. [source: SHANG]

## 3. Zhou and the Mandate of Heaven (covered; keep short)
- The Zhou lasted c. 1046–256 BC, the longest span of any Chinese dynasty. At Muye, King Wu's coalition (traditionally 45,000 men and 300 chariots) defeated the last Shang king. [source: ZHOU]
- The Mandate of Heaven (*tianming*) was first used to justify the Zhou overthrow of the Shang. It made rule conditional on just government, and successful revolt was read as proof that the mandate had passed. It did not require noble birth: the Han and Ming were founded by commoners. Non-Han dynasties such as the Qing also claimed it. [source: MOH]
- Corollary: a right of rebellion. The classic statement, in the *Mencius*, "was often edited out of that text." [source: MOH]
- Japan adopted Tang institutions in the Taihō Code (703) but omitted the Mandate of Heaven. Its emperor was treated as divine, not as conditionally mandated. [source: MOH] (This is a useful East Asian comparison.)
- The Western Zhou fell in 771 BC (King You was defeated by rebel states and the Quanrong). The Eastern Zhou then divides into the Spring and Autumn period (HC: 722–476; the Ancient China chapter uses 771–476; both conventions exist) and the Warring States period (476/475–221). [source: HC]
- Commanderies and counties from this period survive in the terminology of today's *sheng* and *xian* (provinces and counties). [source: HC]

## 4. The Hundred Schools (covered; keep short)
- Confucius, c. 551–479 BCE, born Kong Qiu in Lu (modern Qufu, Shandong). The *Analects* were compiled "many years after his death". [source: CONF]
- Laozi: traditionally a 6th-century BC archivist at the Zhou court and an elder contemporary of Confucius. "Modern scholars remain divided over his identity, dates, and precise relationship to the received text"; some regard him as legendary. "Laozi" is an honorific meaning "old teacher". The Tang dynasty claimed him as an ancestor. [source: LAO]
- Legalism was first adopted institutionally in Qin under Shang Yang's reforms in the 4th century BCE. Han Fei is considered by many the greatest Legalist thinker, and he drew on the Daoist *wu wei*. [source: LEG]

## 5. Qin and Han: the first empires (covered; keep short)
- In 221 BC Ying Zheng took the title *Shi Huangdi* ("First Emperor"). The *huangdi* title remained standard until 1912. The name "Qin" probably gave rise to the Western word "China". [source: HC]
- Qin administration: 36 commanderies at first, with more added by 210 (Bodde); standardised seal script, weights, measures and coinage (Ban Liang); about 4,250 miles of imperial highways; Meng Tian's campaign against the Xiongnu with reportedly 300,000 troops; existing walls joined into the first "Great Wall". [source: HC]
- Book burning: Sima Qian reports some 460 scholars buried alive. "Modern historians doubt the details of the story", which first appears more than a century later. Martin Kern notes that Qin and early Han writings freely cite the Classics, which would have been impossible had they been burned. [source: QSH; BB]
- The First Emperor died in 210 BC; the dynasty fell in 207/206 BC. [source: QSH; HC]
- Terracotta Army: found 29 March 1974 by farmers digging a well (Yang Zhifa, his five brothers and Wang Puzhi). The three pits hold more than 8,000 soldiers, 130 chariots with 520 horses and 150 cavalry horses (2007 estimate), most still in situ. [source: TA]
- Great Wall: the first walls date to the 7th century BC and were joined under the Qin. The best-known sections are Ming (1368–1644), built in brick and stone after Tumu (1449). A 2012 national survey counted 21,196 km of walls, trenches and related sections. NASA says it is barely visible from low Earth orbit and only under near-perfect conditions; it is **not** visible to the naked eye from the Moon. [source: GW] (This corrects a common belief.)
- Han: the first nationwide census, taken in 2 AD, registered 57,671,400 people in 12,366,470 households. [source: HAN]
- In 136 BC Emperor Wu abolished academic chairs not concerned with the Five Classics; in 124 BC he founded the Imperial University. [source: HAN]
- Cai Lun's standard papermaking process dates to 105 AD (earlier paper exists; see the published Han chapter). [source: HAN]
- Sima Qian (c. 145–c. 86 BC): the *Shiji* covers more than two thousand years. In 99 BC he defended the general Li Ling and, offered death or castration, chose castration in order to finish his history. The Twenty-Four official dynastic histories all copied his format. [source: SMQ; CHH]
- Steppe frontier: Thomas Barfield (*The Perilous Frontier*, 1989) describes nomad strategies towards China. When the Xiongnu split in 60 BC, the weaker party adopted what Barfield calls the "inner frontier strategy" (alliance with Han). [source: XN] Reviews summarise his "outer frontier strategy": nomad empires extracted subsidies and trade from China rather than conquering it, and so depended on Chinese dynasties. Nicola Di Cosmo (2002) and Christopher Beckwith (2009) criticise this "needy nomad" model. [source: https://asianreviewofbooks.com/content/shadow-empires-an-alternative-imperial-history-by-thomas-j-barfield/ ; WebSearch summary of reviews, see Debates]

## 6. Division, Buddhism, Sui–Tang (Tang covered; keep short)
- After 220: Three Kingdoms (220–280); Jin reunification in 280, soon broken by the War of the Eight Princes. Northern China fragmented into the Sixteen Kingdoms (304–439), most founded by Xiongnu, Xianbei, Jie, Di and Qiang rulers, and Han Chinese migrated en masse to the Yangtze. Northern and Southern dynasties 420–589: the Xianbei Northern Wei unified the north in 439 and its elite adopted Han surnames, while southern dynasties ruled from Jiankang (Nanjing). [source: HC]
- Buddhism reached China via the Silk Roads in the 1st century CE. The Dunhuang, Yungang and Longmen cave complexes are landmarks of Buddhist art. The Great Anti-Buddhist Persecution of 841–845 under Emperor Wuzong of Tang weakened Buddhist institutions. [source: BUD] (BUD's phrase "via Tibet" is doubtful and is omitted.)
- Sui (581–618): reunified China in 589; built the Grand Canal; failed against Goguryeo by 614; Emperor Yang was assassinated in 618. In 607 Emperor Yang created the *jinshi* ("presented scholar") examination; IE dates the concept of the bureaucratic examinations to 605. [source: SUI; IE]
- Tang (618–907): Chang'an was "the world's most populous city for much of the dynasty". Its metropolitan census of 742 recorded 1,960,188 persons including nearby small cities, so "about a million within the walls" (published) is conservative but compatible. Empire-wide censuses show about 50 million; estimates reach 75–80 million. [source: TANG; ALR]
- Battle of Talas, 751: the Abbasids defeated a Tang army under Gao Xianzhi, helped by the defection of the Karluk Turks. [source: ALR]
- An Lushan rebellion (755–763) and its death toll, **disputed**: the census fell from 52,919,309 (755) to 16,900,000 (764), a paper gap of about 36 million. Steven Pinker used 36 million (calling it controversial). His source, Matthew White, later revised his estimate to **13 million**. Scholars attribute much of the drop to the collapse of registration and the removal of untaxed groups, and Hans Bielenstein (1947) called a literal reading "comically naive". Writer: say "deaths certainly in the millions — one estimate is about 13 million — though census figures overstate them". [source: ALR]
- Huang Chao rebellion (874–884): sacked Guangzhou in 879 with a massacre of its foreign merchant community (HC), then both capitals. A medieval source's claim of 8 million dead is not reliable. [source: HC; TANG]
- Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms (907–960): the Sixteen Prefectures were ceded to the Khitan Liao, and Vietnam gained lasting independence. [source: HC]

## 7. Song: the commercial and technological revolution (covered; keep short)
- Founded 960 by Zhao Kuangyin (Taizu). The Chanyuan Treaty (1004/1005) required payments to the Liao. HC notes the silver flowed back through trade, which "expanded the Song economy". [source: HC]
- Population: the Northern Song census recorded 20 million households, double the Han and Tang. POP estimates 100–123 million for 1103, while SONG gives an estimate of about 90 million. Kaifeng and Hangzhou were each over a million. [source: SONG; POP; HC]
- Champa rice was introduced in 1011 from Champa (Vietnam), drought-resistant and allowing two harvests a year. [source: SONG]
- Paper money: *jiaozi* promissory notes appeared in Chengdu, Sichuan, around the 11th century, first issued by private merchants. After bankruptcies the government took over and founded the Jiaozi bureau in 1023. Numismatists regard it as the first paper money. [source: JZ]
- Wang Anshi's New Policies (from 1069) aimed to cut spending and strengthen the northern military; they were opposed by the historian Sima Guang. Sima Guang's *Zizhi Tongjian* was compiled 1065–1084. [source: WAS; SONG; CHH]
- The first standing navy in China was formed in 1132, in the Southern Song. [source: HC]
- The "four great inventions" (compass, gunpowder, papermaking, printing). Francis Bacon wrote of "printing, gunpowder, and the magnet" (without knowing they were Chinese); see Quotations. Joseph Edkins (1823–1905) listed all four. [source: FGI]
- Li Qingzhao (1084–1151), a woman poet famous in her lifetime. [source: SONG]
- The Mongol conquest: before it the dynasties reported about 120 million people; the 1300 census reported about 60 million. Explanations are **disputed**: Mote says the figures show a failure to record people; Brook says enserfment removed people from the census; McNeill and Morgan point to plague. Plague in the 14th century may have killed about a quarter of the population. [source: HC]

## 8. Yuan (covered in *The Mongol Empire*; keep short)
- Kublai proclaimed the "Great Yuan" in 1271 and claimed the Mandate of Heaven, succeeding the line of Chinese dynasties. He moved the capital from Karakorum to Khanbaliq/Dadu (Beijing), building from 1266. The Song fell in 1279. [source: YUAN]
- The Yuan was the first conquest dynasty to rule all of China proper. Its population was ranked in four descending classes: Mongols, Semuren (western and Central Asian immigrants), Han (northern Chinese) and Nanren (southerners). [source: YUAN; HC]
- Examinations were suspended, then revived in 1315 (decree 1313) with quotas favouring Mongols. [source: IE]
- Paper currency (*jiaochao*) became the predominant medium of exchange; the silver standard was abandoned in 1310, and late-Yuan over-issue caused hyperinflation. [source: HC; ECON]
- The Red Turban Rebellion began in 1351. Zhu Yuanzhang took Khanbaliq in 1368; Yuan remnants ruled from Mongolia as the Northern Yuan until 1635. [source: YUAN]
- Timothy Brook, *Great State* (2019), argues that China's sense of itself as a unified "great state" derives from the Mongol *yeke ulus* rather than the Qin, and that the Ming, Qing, Republic and PRC inherited the imperial form the Mongols and Manchus built. [source: WebSearch summary of reviews, https://www.axios.com/2020/02/26/china-mongols-borders ; https://kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/timothy-brook/great-state/print]

## 9. Ming (covered; keep short — *Ming-Qing History* goes deeper)
- Founded 1368 by Zhu Yuanzhang (the Hongwu emperor), "the only founder of a Chinese dynasty who was also of peasant origin" (HC; contrast Liu Bang, whom MOH calls a commoner). The early Ming had a standing army of over a million and the world's largest dockyards at Nanjing. [source: HC; MING]
- Zheng He (1371–1433/1435), a Muslim eunuch admiral, led seven voyages, 1405–1433. The first left on 11 July 1405 with 317 ships and almost 28,000 crew. [source: ZH] (The published chapter says "over 300 ships … some 27,000 men", which is consistent.)
- Tumu, 1449: Esen's Oirat Mongols captured the Zhengtong emperor; the Ming turned defensive and built most of the surviving Great Wall. [source: HC]
- The 1556 Shaanxi earthquake killed about 830,000 people, the deadliest earthquake on record. [source: HC]
- The Portuguese settled at Macau in 1557 (it stayed Portuguese until 1999). American silver arrived via Manila, and Japanese silver came too. [source: HC; ECON]
- Late-Ming population is estimated at 160–200 million. [source: MING]
- The Ming–Qing transition, with Little Ice Age disasters and epidemics, saw "the loss of as many as 25 million lives". [source: HC]

## 10. Qing at its height (covered partly in *Absolutism* and *Taiping*; *Ming-Qing History* goes deeper)
- Hong Taiji proclaimed the Great Qing in 1636. The Qing took Beijing in 1644 after the Ming general Wu Sangui opened the Shanhai Pass (gates opened 25 May 1644 per GW). It ruled until 1912. [source: QING; GW]
- The Qing was the second conquest dynasty to rule all of China proper and "roughly doubled the territory controlled by the Ming". Its area peaked at about 14.7 million km² in 1790 (QING infobox). [source: HC; QING]
- The queue order forced Han Chinese men to adopt the Manchu hairstyle. [source: HC]
- Kangxi (born 1654, reigned 1661–1722; enthroned at seven): his 61-year reign is the longest of any Chinese emperor. He suppressed the Three Feudatories (1673–81), took Taiwan from Koxinga's heirs (1683) and agreed the Treaty of Nerchinsk with Russia (1689). He welcomed the Jesuits until the Rites controversy. [source: KX; HC]
- Qianlong (1711–1799; reigned 1735–1796, then ruled as emperor emeritus until 1799) abdicated after 60 years so as not to exceed his grandfather Kangxi. He sponsored the *Siku Quanshu* while literary inquisitions suppressed some 3,100 works. [source: QL]
- At the end of Qianlong's reign the Qing "ruled more than one-third of the world's population, and had the largest economy in the world" (HC; Maddison-type estimates, not exact). [source: HC]
- The Ten Great Campaigns: the conquest of the Dzungars (1755–57) and Xinjiang (1758–59) were the significant ones, alongside Jinchuan, Taiwan, Burma (1765–69), Vietnam (1788–89) and the Gurkhas (1790–92). [source: TGC]
- Dzungar genocide: Qianlong ordered it. The Qing scholar Wei Yuan estimated that of about 600,000 Dzungars, 40% died of smallpox, 20% fled and 30% were killed by Qing troops. [source: QING]
- The Qing defined "China" (Dulimbai gurun) as including Manchuria, Mongolia, Xinjiang and Tibet, a multi-ethnic state. When Dzungaria was conquered in 1759, a Manchu memorial declared it absorbed into "China". [source: QING]
- Population (estimates, POP table): about 160 million in 1700, 225 million in 1750, 303 million in 1791, about 384 million in 1821 and 408–436 million in 1851. [source: POP]
- Macartney Embassy, 1793, the first British mission, received at Chengde (Jehol). The kowtow dispute was **not** the reason it failed, "as is sometimes believed". Qianlong's edict to George III: "no need to import the manufactures of outside barbarians" (see Quotations). [source: MAC]

## 11. The nineteenth-century crises (the "modern rupture"; one short section, cross-refer to *The Opium Wars*, *Taiping*, *Modern China*)
- First Opium War (1839/40–42); Treaty of Nanking (1842) ceded Hong Kong. [source: HC]
- Taiping (1850/51–1864) controlled about a third of China proper. The most widely cited death toll is 20–30 million (5–10% of the population); estimates up to 73–100 million exist and are contested. It is often called the deadliest civil war in history. Thirty million people fled. [source: TR; HC]
- The Tongzhi Restoration and Self-Strengthening (1860s) followed; defeat by Japan came in 1894–95; the Hundred Days' Reform of 1898 was ended by Cixi (1835–1908). [source: HC; QING]
- The Boxer Protocol of 7 September 1901 imposed 450 million taels, "more than the government's annual tax revenue", reportedly set at about one tael per head of population. [source: BOX]
- Examinations abolished in 1905. [source: IE]
- 1911: the Wuchang Uprising of 10 October; the Republic was declared on 1 January 1912; the abdication edict of the six-year-old Xuantong emperor (Puyi) was issued on 12 February 1912, ending "over two millennia of imperial rule". Yuan Shikai's monarchy lasted from December 1915 to March 1916. [source: XH]
- The "century of humiliation" (1839–1949) is a historiographical concept, prominent in Xi-era patriotic education. [source: COH]

## 12. Republic and People's Republic (closing survey only; *Modern China* and the 20th-century chapters carry this)
- The May Fourth Movement of 1919 was a reaction to the Versailles award of Shandong to Japan. [source: HC]
- On 1 October 1949 Mao proclaimed the PRC from Tiananmen; the ROC government retreated to Taiwan. [source: PRC; HC]
- Land reform killed between 1 and 2 million landlords (PRC article: "state-tolerated executions"). [source: PRC]
- Great Leap Forward famine: PRC gives "15 to 55 million deaths". Published *Maoism* uses about 15 million (low) to 45 million (Dikötter). Keep the published range and cross-refer. [source: PRC]
- Population rose from 550 million (1950) to 900 million (1974). [source: PRC]
- In 1971/72 the PRC took China's UN seat, and Nixon visited in 1972. Reform and opening under Deng began in 1978; Tiananmen was 4 June 1989; China joined the WTO in 2001; Hong Kong returned in 1997 and Macau in 1999. [source: HC]
- China became the world's second-largest economy under Hu Jintao (overtaking Japan). [source: PRC]

## 13. The machinery of empire: examinations and scholar-officials
- The system lasted about 1,300 years, from the Sui (concept 605; *jinshi* 607) to its abolition in 1905. [source: IE]
- Han antecedents: recommendation of the "filial and incorrupt" (*xiaolian*) with a quota of one candidate per 200,000 households; examinations of *xiaolian* candidates from 132 AD. [source: IE]
- It was expanded greatly under Wu Zetian. In the Ming the content narrowed to Neo-Confucian orthodoxy and the *jinshi* became essential for high office. [source: IE]
- Passing rates: "only a 1% passing rate among the two or three million annual applicants" (late imperial). Examinations always coexisted with other routes: nomination, purchase of titles, clerical promotion, and special procedures for eunuchs. [source: IE]
- Social mobility (**debated**): MING notes that in practice 90% of the population was ineligible for lack of education. Ping-ti Ho, *The Ladder of Success in Imperial China* (1962), used *jinshi* lists to argue for substantial upward mobility in the Ming. A later study of 15,528 cases found 56% of Ming *jinshi* came from families with no previous degree holder, while the share from non-elite backgrounds fell to 19.1% in the Qing. [source: MING; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-asian-studies/article/ladder-of-success-in-imperial-china-aspects-of-social-mobility-13681911-by-pingti-ho-new-york-and-london-columbia-university-press-1962-xviii-385-glossary-bibliography-index-800/2BD9E74D279240B705E8F5BDB72ABD05 ; WebSearch summary]
- Neighbours copied the system (Korea, Vietnam, Ryukyu, and Japan briefly in the 8th–10th centuries). Reports of it influenced the British East India Company, France and Germany. Macaulay and Stafford Northcote (of the Northcote–Trevelyan Report) knew Chinese institutions. In 1853 Lord Monteagle opposed open examinations in Parliament because it was "a Chinese system" and China was not "enlightened". [source: IE]
- Neo-Confucianism: forerunners Han Yu and Li Ao (Tang); synthesised by Zhu Xi (1130–1200) in the Cheng–Zhu school. The rival Lu–Wang school was led by Wang Yangming (1472–1529). [source: NC]

## 14. Recurring themes and how historians frame them
- **Dynastic cycle.** The traditional model runs from founding and mandate, through prosperity, population growth, corruption, disaster and famine, to rebellion, loss of the mandate and a new dynasty. Ban Gu's *Book of Han* set the single-dynasty model; from the Tang, writing official histories became a state undertaking. [source: DC; CHH]
- **Critics.** John K. Fairbank: the concept "has been a major block to the understanding of the fundamental dynamics of Chinese history", valid only as "a somewhat superficial political pattern" over deeper change. [source: DC]
- **Unity and division.** The proverb from *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*: "After a long split, a union will occur; after a long union, a split will occur". [source: DC] Examples: the 220–589 division and Sui–Tang reunion; Five Dynasties; Song–Liao–Jin–Xia. Unlike the western Roman empire, China was repeatedly reunified (published Han chapter).
- **The CCP and the cycle.** In 1945 Huang Yanpei asked Mao how to escape the cycle; Mao answered "democracy … as long as the public maintains their oversight of the government". In 2021 the CCP's third historical resolution named the party's "self-revolution" as "the second answer". [source: DC]
- **Steppe and frontier.** The Xiongnu and Han; nomad-founded northern regimes 304–589; Khitan Liao (907–1125), Tangut Xia (1032–1227), Jurchen Jin (1115–1234); Mongol Yuan; Manchu Qing. HC calls the Yuan and Qing the two "conquest dynasties" that ruled all of China proper. Barfield's model is in Section 5. [source: HC; XN]
- **Tribute system.** The "tribute system" model was created by Fairbank and Teng Ssu-yu in the early 1940s and became influential with Fairbank's edited volume *The Chinese World Order* (1968). Peter Perdue criticises it: *gong* is an "inadequate translation", and the model "turned a flexible practice … into an overly formalized ritual system". In practice Japan, Korea, Ryukyu and Vietnam pursued their own agendas while sending tribute. Ryukyu sent 57 missions in 1372–98. [source: TRIB]
- **What is "China"? The New Qing History.** This US school of the mid-1990s (Rawski, Elliott, Crossley, Perdue, Millward and others) used Manchu-language archives to show the Qing as an Inner Asian as well as a Chinese empire. It began with Evelyn Rawski's 1996 Association for Asian Studies address "Re-envisioning the Qing", against Ping-ti Ho's 1967 sinicization thesis. Ho replied with "In Defense of Sinicization". Some Chinese scholars accuse the school of imperialist motives, while others welcome it. Mark Elliott says it does not separate the Qing from "China". [source: NQH] Ge Zhaoguang's *What Is China?* (2018) offers a Chinese historian's view that the meaning of "China" has changed over time. [source: WebSearch, https://libcat.wellesley.edu/Record/on1028731466]
- **Needham question.** Joseph Needham (1900–1995) launched *Science and Civilisation in China*; vol. 1 appeared in 1954. He asked why modern science "take[s] its meteoric rise only in the West" when China had been "much more efficient than occidental in applying" natural knowledge (*The Grand Titration*, 1969). [source: NEED]
- **Tang–Song transition.** Naitō Konan (1866–1934) argued that changes between the mid-Tang and early Song marked the transition from China's "medieval" to its "early modern" period. [source: NAI]
- **High-level equilibrium trap.** Mark Elvin, *The Pattern of the Chinese Past* (1973). [source: ELV]
- **Great Divergence.** The term was coined by Samuel Huntington in 1996 and used by Kenneth Pomeranz in *The Great Divergence: China, Europe, and the Making of the Modern World Economy* (Princeton, 2000; Fairbank Prize 2001; joint WHA book of the year). The "California school" argues that the Yangzi Delta was comparable to the most advanced parts of Europe until about 1800, with coal and colonies making the difference. Pomeranz stressed that China's coal lay in the northwest, far from the post-Song economic core in the south. Against this: Broadberry finds English silver wages three times the Yangzi Delta's by 1600 (wheat wages 15% higher), and Broadberry and Zhai date the divergence to about 1700. Goldstone's 2021 review finds it only after 1750/1800. Maddison argues that western Europe's per-capita output had already surpassed all other regions. [source: GD; POM; ECON] Eric Jones's *The European Miracle* (1981) is the older Eurocentric framing. [source: GD]
- **China and world trade.** Song coinage served as a de facto currency in maritime Asia (HC). Ming and Qing China absorbed Japanese and American silver (ECON). Quanzhou and Hangzhou were among the largest ports in the world under the Yuan (HC). [source: HC; ECON]
- **Share of world population (POP estimates).** About 27% (Han), 23–35% (Tang, 755), 31–36% (Northern Song, 1103), 17% (Yuan, 1290), about 32–35% (1851), 22% (1950). Use as "between a fifth and a third of humanity for most of the last two thousand years". [source: POP]

## Debates
- **Was there a Xia?** Chinese archaeologists generally equate Erlitou with the late Xia; many Western scholars call this premature because there are no texts. [source: ERL; HC]
- **Did the Qin burn the books and bury scholars?** The traditional account comes from Sima Qian. Kern, Neininger and Li Kaiyuan (2010, "a half-faked history") doubt it. [source: BB]
- **An Lushan death toll:** census-gap readings (36 million, used by Pinker) against registration collapse (Bielenstein; White's revised 13 million). [source: ALR]
- **Mongol-era population fall** (120 million to 60 million): Mote (failure to record), Brook (enserfment), McNeill and Morgan (plague). [source: HC]
- **When did "China" begin as a unified state?** The conventional answer is the Qin in 221 BC; Brook's *Great State* argues for the Mongol *yeke ulus* (reported as unpopular in China). [source: https://www.axios.com/2020/02/26/china-mongols-borders]
- **Sinicization against the New Qing History:** Ho Ping-ti against Rawski, Elliott and Crossley. [source: NQH]
- **Tribute system:** Fairbank's model against Perdue and others. [source: TRIB]
- **Exams and mobility:** Ho's "ladder of success" against the view that 90% were excluded by lack of education. [source: MING; IE]
- **Dynastic cycle:** traditional and CCP uses against Fairbank's critique. [source: DC]
- **Great Divergence:** the California school (Pomeranz, Goldstone) against Broadberry, Maddison and older "European miracle" accounts (Jones). [source: GD]
- **Nomads:** Barfield's "needy nomad" or "outer frontier" model against Di Cosmo (2002) and Beckwith (2009). [source: WebSearch summary of reviews, https://asianreviewofbooks.com/content/shadow-empires-an-alternative-imperial-history-by-thomas-j-barfield/]
- **Ming "turn inward":** the old isolation narrative against Brook and Pomeranz (published Ming chapter); HC still describes the post-Yongle Ming at sea as "increasingly isolationist". Present it as a debate. [source: HC]
