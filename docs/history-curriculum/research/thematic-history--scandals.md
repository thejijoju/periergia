## 1. The concept: moral panic, scandal and the public sphere
- Stanley Cohen (23 February 1942 – 7 January 2013) was born in Johannesburg, South Africa. He studied at Witwatersrand, was active in anti-apartheid issues, came to London in 1963 and did his PhD at the LSE on social reactions to juvenile delinquency. He later held the Martin White chair of sociology at the LSE. [source: WP:Stanley_Cohen_(sociologist)]
- Cohen introduced the phrase "moral panic" in his 1967–69 PhD thesis, which became *Folk Devils and Moral Panics: The Creation of the Mods and Rockers* (1972). Caveat: he did not invent the words. Earlier uses are found in the *Quarterly Christian Spectator* (1830) and in Marshall McLuhan (1964), and Jock Young used the term in his 1967–69 study of drug-takers in Porthmadog, Wales. [source: WP:Moral_panic; WP:Stanley_Cohen_(sociologist)]
- Cohen's definition: a moral panic occurs when "a condition, episode, person or group of persons emerges to become defined as a threat to societal values and interests" (Cohen 2011, p. 1). The claims "exaggerate the seriousness, extent, typicality and/or inevitability of harm". The groups blamed are "folk devils", and those who raise the alarm are "moral entrepreneurs". [source: WP:Moral_panic]
- Cohen's five stages: (1) something is defined as a threat; (2) the media amplify it in simplified, symbolic form; (3) public anxiety is aroused; (4) "gatekeepers" (editors, clergy, politicians, experts) respond, sometimes with new laws or policies; (5) the panic recedes, or leaves lasting change. In Cohen's words (2002, p. 9), a panic sometimes "has more serious and long-lasting repercussions and might produce such changes as those in legal and social policy or even in the way the society conceives itself." [source: WP:Moral_panic]
- Cohen named four kinds of actors: the mass media, moral entrepreneurs, the "societal control culture" (police, courts, politicians) and the public. [source: WP:Moral_panic]
- Mods and rockers: the clashes at Clacton and Hastings over Easter 1964, and in May 1964 at Margate, Brighton and other resorts, drew lurid press coverage. The *Birmingham Post* called the youths "internal enemies" in May 1964. Cohen argued that the fighting was no different from the brawls between ordinary youths at resorts and football matches in the 1950s and early 1960s. [source: WP:Mods_and_rockers; WP:Moral_panic]
- Erich Goode and Nachman Ben-Yehuda, *Moral Panics: The Social Construction of Deviance* (1994), set out five criteria: concern, hostility, consensus, disproportionality and volatility. In their words, "the concept of moral panic *rests* on disproportion". [source: WP:Moral_panic]
- Stuart Hall, Chas Critcher, Tony Jefferson, John Clarke and Brian Roberts, *Policing the Crisis: Mugging, the State and Law and Order* (Macmillan, 1978), treated Britain's early-1970s "mugging" scare as a moral panic. They argued that it was fuelled, and to an extent created, by the institutions charged with controlling it; that it singled out young Black men; and that it helped build an "authoritarian consensus", a drift toward a law-and-order society. The case study chapter is titled "Cashing in on Handsworth". [source: WP:Stuart_Hall_(cultural_theorist); https://oro.open.ac.uk/37679 ; publisher summaries via search]
- Jürgen Habermas, *Strukturwandel der Öffentlichkeit* (1962; English translation *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere*, 1989, by Thomas Burger and Frederick Lawrence), traced a "public sphere" of debate in coffee houses, salons and print that served as a counterweight to political authority. Scandal-as-publicity arguments build on this. [source: WP:The_Structural_Transformation_of_the_Public_Sphere]
- John B. Thompson, *Political Scandal: Power and Visibility in the Media Age* (Polity, 2000), links the rise of political scandal to changes in communication media that altered visibility and blurred public and private life. He distinguishes sex scandals, financial scandals and "power scandals", with case material from Britain and the United States. [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/32084 ; https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/86307]
- Ari Adut, *On Scandal: Moral Disturbances in Society, Politics, and Art* (Cambridge University Press), treats scandal as a disruption caused by the *publicity* of a transgression, with cases including Wilde, Watergate and Lewinsky. Bibliographies give 2008 or 2009; the copyright year appears to be 2008. [source: https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780521895897]
- Later scholars have applied moral panic to Satanic ritual abuse, video games, AIDS and drugs. A Royal Society of Arts commission said Britain's Misuse of Drugs Act 1971 was "driven more by 'moral panic' than by a practical desire to reduce harm". [source: WP:Moral_panic]

## 2. Imposture and fraud: the Tichborne claimant (1866–1898)
- Roger Tichborne, heir to an old Catholic baronetcy in Hampshire, was presumed lost when the *Bella* sank off Brazil in April 1854. His mother, Lady Tichborne, advertised for news, including through a Sydney "Missing Friends Agency" in Australian papers from 1865. [source: WP:Tichborne_case]
- In 1866 a bankrupt butcher in Wagga Wagga, New South Wales, known as Thomas Castro, came forward as Roger. Lady Tichborne accepted him in Paris in January 1867. Most of the family called him a fraud, and evidence suggested he was Arthur Orton, a butcher's son from Wapping. [source: WP:Tichborne_case]
- Andrew Bogle, a formerly enslaved man from a Jamaican plantation and a long-time Tichborne servant, backed the claimant. His son Henry George Bogle was the claimant's constant companion at the criminal trial. [source: WP:Tichborne_case]
- The civil case, *Tichborne v. Lushington*, began on 11 May 1871 and failed. The criminal trial, *Regina v. Castro*, ran from 21 April 1873 to 28 February 1874, 188 court days, and was one of the longest in English history. He was convicted of perjury and sentenced to 14 years. His counsel Edward Kenealy was disbarred. [source: WP:Tichborne_case]
- The verdict "swelled the popular tide" in his favour. Kenealy launched the Magna Charta Association in April 1874 and was elected to Parliament in 1875. George Bernard Shaw noted the paradox that the claimant was seen both as a rightful baronet and as a working-class man denied his rights by an elite. He was released in 1884, confessed to being Orton in 1895, retracted almost at once, and died destitute in 1898. [source: WP:Tichborne_case]
- Debate: most writers accept the court's verdict, but "some analysts believe that an element of doubt remains". Rohan McWilliam (2007) reads the affair as popular radical politics. [source: WP:Tichborne_case]

## 3. Frontier violence on trial: Myall Creek (1838)
- On 10 June 1838 at Myall Creek, northern New South Wales, a party of stockmen killed at least 28 unarmed Wirraayaraay (Kamilaroi) people, mostly women, children and old men. The party was led by the free settler John Henry Fleming and made up of assigned convicts and ex-convicts, one of them John Johnstone, a Black African. [source: WP:Myall_Creek_massacre]
- The first trial ended in acquittal after twenty minutes, although Justice Dowling told the jury the law made no distinction between killing an Aboriginal and a European person. At a second trial in November 1838, seven men were convicted, and they were hanged at Darlinghurst Gaol on 18 December 1838. Fleming was never tried. Governor Gipps refused clemency. [source: WP:Myall_Creek_massacre]
- *The Sydney Morning Herald* opposed the executions in editorials that "openly advocated genocide". A secret "Black Association" led by a local magistrate told the accused to "stick together and say nothing". [source: WP:Myall_Creek_massacre]
- Journalist Bruce Elder (*Blood on the Wattle*, 1998) argues that the convictions led settlers to make pacts of silence so that later massacres could not be proven. [source: WP:Myall_Creek_massacre]
- A memorial was opened in June 2000 "in an act of reconciliation", and the site was added to the National Heritage List on 7 June 2008. [source: WP:Myall_Creek_Massacre_and_Memorial_Site]

## 4. Convict-system exposés and the end of transportation
- In 1837 Sir William Molesworth chaired a Commons Select Committee on Transportation. It sat in London and heard 23 witnesses, mostly opponents of the system, and reported in August 1838. It found that transportation failed both to deter and to reform, that assignment to private masters was arbitrary, and that the system was "morally corrupting" to colonial society. [source: WP:Sir_William_Molesworth,_8th_Baronet]
- Transportation to New South Wales was abolished in 1840 and assignment ended by 1841. Van Diemen's Land received convicts until 1853 and Western Australia until 1868. In all, about 162,000 convicts were transported to Australia. [source: WP:Sir_William_Molesworth,_8th_Baronet; WP:Penal_transportation]
- Debate: historians note that the committee was stacked against assignment. Hamish Maxwell-Stewart's quantitative work argues that its influence on punishment practice such as flogging has been overstated. [source: WP:Sir_William_Molesworth,_8th_Baronet]
- Colonists also resisted. The Anti-Transportation League campaigned in Australia, and in the Cape Colony the "Convict Crisis" of 1849 stopped southern Africa from becoming a penal colony. [source: WP:Penal_transportation]

## 5. Repression and its critics: Morant Bay and Governor Eyre (1865–1870)
- On 11 October 1865 the Baptist deacon Paul Bogle led hundreds of people to the Morant Bay courthouse, Jamaica. The militia fired, and the crowd burned the courthouse; more than 25 people died that day. [source: WP:Morant_Bay_rebellion]
- Under Governor Edward John Eyre's martial law, troops killed 439 Black Jamaicans directly and arrested 354 more, who were later executed, many without proper trials. More than 600 men and women were flogged and about 1,000 homes burned. See Conflicts: some sources treat 439 as the total death toll. [source: WP:Morant_Bay_rebellion; WP:Edward_John_Eyre]
- George William Gordon, a mixed-race member of the Assembly and a critic of Eyre, was tried by court-martial and hanged on 23 October 1865. Bogle was hanged two days later with 14 others. [source: WP:Edward_John_Eyre]
- A Royal Commission (Russell Gurney and J. B. Maule, with Sir Henry Storks as acting governor) reported in 1866. It found the punishments "excessive", the floggings "reckless" and the burnings wanton. It found no sufficient proof of Gordon's complicity, but did not condemn martial law itself. Colonial Secretary Cardwell held that Eyre could not resume the governorship, and Eyre left Jamaica in July 1866. [source: https://victorianweb.org/history/empire/westindies/eyre2.html]
- In Britain, the Jamaica Committee (organised by John Stuart Mill, with John Bright, Darwin, Huxley, Herbert Spencer and others) sought Eyre's prosecution. Carlyle's Eyre Defence Committee (with Ruskin, Kingsley, Tennyson and Dickens) defended him. Grand juries declined to indict, and the civil suit *Phillips v Eyre* (1870), brought by a Black Jamaican, failed. [source: WP:Edward_John_Eyre; WP:Morant_Bay_rebellion]
- Imperial link: Eyre had earlier been an explorer in Australia, crossing the Great Australian Bight and Nullarbor overland in 1840–41. [source: WP:Edward_John_Eyre]
- Outcome: the Jamaican Assembly gave up its charter and Jamaica became a Crown Colony. Bogle and Gordon were named National Heroes of Jamaica in 1969. [source: WP:Morant_Bay_rebellion]

## 6. Stead's "Maiden Tribute" (1885), the age-of-consent panic and the Labouchère Amendment
- "The Maiden Tribute of Modern Babylon" ran in the *Pall Mall Gazette* on 6–10 July 1885, written by its editor W. T. Stead (5 July 1849 – 15 April 1912; he died on the *Titanic*). Its crossheads included "The Violation of Virgins" and "Strapping Girls Down". [source: WP:The_Maiden_Tribute_of_Modern_Babylon; WP:Criminal_Law_Amendment_Act_1885; WP:W._T._Stead]
- Background: the bill had passed the Lords in 1883 and 1884 but stalled in the Commons. On 23 May 1885 Benjamin Scott asked Stead to campaign for it. Stead had opposed the Contagious Diseases Acts since the 1870s, alongside Josephine Butler. [source: WP:Criminal_Law_Amendment_Act_1885; WP:W._T._Stead]
- With Bramwell Booth of the Salvation Army and the former prostitute Rebecca Jarrett, Stead "bought" 13-year-old Eliza Armstrong, a chimney sweep's daughter from Lisson Grove, and called her "Lily" in print. He was convicted of abduction on the technical ground that the father had not consented, and served three months in Coldbath Fields and Holloway. [source: WP:Criminal_Law_Amendment_Act_1885; WP:W._T._Stead]
- The *Gazette* sold out. Copies changed hands for 20 times their price, 10,000 people besieged the office, and paper had to be borrowed from the rival *Globe*. Matthew Arnold dubbed Stead's style "the New Journalism". [source: WP:W._T._Stead]
- The Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885 ("Stead's Act"), which received royal assent on 14 August 1885, raised the age of consent for girls from 13 to 16 and allowed summary proceedings against brothels. [source: WP:Criminal_Law_Amendment_Act_1885]
- Section 11, the Labouchère Amendment, was added late on 6 August 1885 by Henry Labouchère, a Liberal MP and editor of *Truth*. It made "gross indecency" between males, "in public or private", punishable by up to two years with or without hard labour. Lawyers dubbed it the "blackmailer's charter". In England and Wales it was re-enacted in 1956, amended in 1967 (partial decriminalisation) and repealed in 2003. [source: WP:Labouchère_Amendment]
- Debate: Frank Harris and some historians say Labouchère meant it as a wrecking amendment; others see a sincere attempt to police male homosexuality. Judith Walkowitz (*City of Dreadful Delight*, 1992) analyses the "Maiden Tribute" as a narrative of sexual danger. Roland Pearsall called Stead's method "using the weapons of pornography to right a wrong". [source: WP:Labouchère_Amendment; WP:The_Maiden_Tribute_of_Modern_Babylon]

## 7. The Wilde trials (1895)
- Oscar Wilde (16 October 1854 – 30 November 1900) prosecuted the Marquess of Queensberry for criminal libel. The libel hearings produced evidence that led to Wilde's own arrest for gross indecency under section 11. The first jury could not agree. On 25 May 1895 Wilde and Alfred Taylor were convicted and given two years' hard labour, the maximum, which Justice Wills called "totally inadequate". [source: WP:Oscar_Wilde; WP:Labouchère_Amendment]
- He was held from 25 May 1895 to 18 May 1897 in Newgate, Pentonville, Wandsworth and Reading. He wrote *De Profundis* in prison and *The Ballad of Reading Gaol* (1898) in exile in France. [source: WP:Oscar_Wilde]
- The same law convicted Alan Turing (pardoned posthumously in 2013). [source: WP:Labouchère_Amendment]

## 8. Larrikin panics in colonial Australia
- "Larrikin" was a West Midlands dialect word. It spread in Melbourne's streets from the late 1860s, and a *Geelong Advertiser* letter of November 1870 complained of the "larrikin nuisance". It first meant "a lout, a hoodlum", a young urban rough. [source: WP:Larrikin]
- Melissa Bellanta (*Larrikins: A History*, 2012) argues that around 1870 "larrikin" was a term of abuse for teenage working-class hell-raisers, and that "crucially, the early larrikins were female as well as male". After the First World War the "larrikin digger" made the word affectionate. [source: WP:Larrikin]
- The Mount Rennie case: on 9 September 1886, 16-year-old Mary Jane Hicks was gang-raped at Mount Rennie, Waterloo, Sydney, by youths linked to the "Waterloo Push". Nine were convicted and sentenced to death, five sentences were commuted, and four (Read, Duffy, Martin and Boyce) were hanged at Darlinghurst Gaol on 7 January 1887. The press reacted with a "media frenzy". The *Bulletin* attacked Hicks's character and likened the case to British oppression of the Irish. [source: WP:Mount_Rennie_rape_case]

## 9. War crimes and empire: Breaker Morant (1902)
- Harry "Breaker" Morant (born Edwin Henry Murrant, 9 December 1864 – 27 February 1902) was an English-born horseman and bush balladeer in Australia. He was a lieutenant in the Bushveldt Carbineers, an irregular British unit with many Australians, in the northern Transvaal. [source: WP:Breaker_Morant]
- He was convicted of murdering prisoners and captured civilians in 1901, including the wounded prisoner Floris Visser and eight surrendered Boers (four Afrikaners and four Dutch schoolteachers) near Elim Hospital. Kitchener's telegram of 5 April 1902 said 12 of 20 murders charged were proved. Leach notes that three Black African witnesses were also shot. Morant and Peter Handcock were acquitted of the murder of the missionary Rev. C. A. D. Heese. [source: WP:Breaker_Morant]
- The case began with a letter of 4 October 1901 from 15 Carbineers to their commander, and with African servants' testimony gathered by "native scouts". South African historian Charles Leach records the view that without the Heese killing none of the murders would have gone to trial. [source: WP:Breaker_Morant]
- Morant and Handcock were shot in Pretoria on 27 February 1902. George Witton's death sentence was commuted, and his release was announced in the Commons on 10 August 1904. Witton's book *Scapegoats of the Empire* appeared in 1907. [source: WP:Breaker_Morant]
- The newly federated Australian government demanded an explanation. It later insisted that Australian soldiers not be court-martialled by the British in the First World War, which is partly credited for no Australian deserters being executed. [source: WP:Breaker_Morant]
- Memory: Bruce Beresford's 1980 film made Morant a folk hero of Australian independence. Craig Wilcox calls *Scapegoats* a "half-truth". A petition for pardon (James Unkles, October 2009) was rejected by the UK Ministry of Defence in November 2010. In May 2012 Attorney-General Nicola Roxon said Australia would not pursue it further. Descendants of victims in South Africa criticised the campaign. [source: WP:Breaker_Morant]

## 10. Emily Hobhouse and the Boer War camps (1900–1902)
- Hobhouse sailed for the Cape on 7 December 1900 for the South African Women and Children Distress Fund and reached the Bloemfontein camp on 24 January 1901. Her report, *Report of a Visit to the Camps of Women and Children in the Cape and Orange River Colonies*, was published in June 1901. [source: WP:Emily_Hobhouse; WP:Second_Boer_War_concentration_camps]
- Her words: "I call this camp system a wholesale cruelty… To keep these Camps going is murder to the children." [source: WP:Emily_Hobhouse]
- The Liberal leader Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman denounced "methods of barbarism" (June 1901). The government won the Commons debate 252 to 149 but appointed the all-female Fawcett Commission under Millicent Fawcett, which corroborated her account. Hobhouse was refused landing at Cape Town in October 1901 and deported. [source: WP:Emily_Hobhouse; WP:Second_Boer_War_concentration_camps]
- Deaths: about 27,927 Boers, and at least 14,154 (an estimated 20,000) Black Africans in separate camps. More than 100,000 Black South Africans (107,000 interned) were held in worse conditions, and "their treatment elicited little outrage". Ranges differ; see Conflicts. [source: WP:Second_Boer_War_concentration_camps]
- After civil authorities took over (November 1901), the annual white death rate fell to 6.9 per cent by February 1902 and later to 2 per cent; improvements in the Black camps were slower. [source: WP:Second_Boer_War_concentration_camps]
- Debate: Niall Ferguson argues that the deaths came from incompetence, not a "deliberately genocidal policy". [source: WP:Second_Boer_War_concentration_camps]
- Afterlife: Hobhouse died on 9 June 1926 and her ashes lie in the National Women's Monument, Bloemfontein. She met Gandhi in Cape Town in 1913. A South African Navy submarine named after her in 1969 was renamed SAS *Umkhonto* in 1994. [source: WP:Emily_Hobhouse]

## 11. The Congo atrocities campaign (1890–1908)
- George Washington Williams, an African American historian, wrote "An Open Letter to His Serene Majesty Leopold II" from Stanley Falls on 18 July 1890. The letter "popularized the term 'crimes against humanity'" (per the source's attribution to Hochschild). [source: WP:George_Washington_Williams]
- The Stokes Affair: in January 1895 the Free State officer Hubert Lothaire hanged the Dublin-born trader Charles Stokes. The British press outcry, raised in August 1895 by the *Pall Mall Gazette*, helped turn opinion against Leopold. [source: WP:Casement_Report]
- E. D. Morel, a shipping clerk for Elder Dempster in Antwerp, noticed that ships brought rubber out but sent mainly arms and officers in. Roger Casement, British consul at Boma, wrote the Casement Report (1904) for the Foreign Office. The two founded the Congo Reform Association (1904–1913) with Henry Grattan Guinness. [source: WP:Congo_Reform_Association; WP:Casement_Report]
- In 1904 the missionary Alice Seeley Harris, at Baringa, photographed Nsala of Wala with the severed hand and foot of his five-year-old daughter, killed by ABIR company sentries. Her lantern-slide shows toured Britain. [source: WP:Alice_Seeley_Harris]
- Belgium annexed the Free State in November 1908. [source: WP:Alice_Seeley_Harris; WP:Casement_Report]
- Debate: Morel favoured "indirect rule" and free trade (Nathan Alexander); the CRA sought Belgian annexation, not the end of colonialism. Felix Lösing argues that racist anxieties about white prestige, not only humanitarianism, drove its success. Leopold ran a covert Press Bureau from 1904. [source: WP:Congo_Reform_Association]
- Casement (1 September 1864 – 3 August 1916) then investigated the Putumayo rubber abuses in Peru (1910) and was knighted for it. He was hanged in 1916 for seeking German aid for the Easter Rising. [source: WP:Roger_Casement]

## 12. Amritsar / Jallianwala Bagh (1919)
- On 13 April 1919, Baisakhi day, Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer's troops fired on a crowd in the walled Jallianwala Bagh, Amritsar. The crowd was protesting the Rowlatt Act and the arrest of Saifuddin Kitchlew and Satyapal. Dyer reported: "I have heard that between 200 and 300 of the crowd were killed. My party fired 1,650 rounds". [source: WP:Jallianwala_Bagh_massacre]
- Toll: the official figure was 379; the Congress inquiry gave about 1,000 killed and more than 1,500 casualties. Infobox range 379–700. The Hunter Commission estimated the crowd at 10,000–20,000; Dyer said about 6,000. [source: WP:Jallianwala_Bagh_massacre]
- The Disorders Inquiry Committee (Hunter Commission) was announced on 14 October 1919. Dyer told it he could have dispersed the crowd without firing "but they would have come back again and laughed, and I would have made, what I consider, a fool of myself." [source: WP:Jallianwala_Bagh_massacre]
- Dyer was relieved of command (23 March 1920) but never prosecuted. Churchill called it "unutterably monstrous", and the Commons voted 247 to 37 against Dyer on 8 July 1920; many Britons still "thought him a hero". Tagore renounced his knighthood. Udham Singh shot Michael O'Dwyer in London on 13 March 1940. [source: WP:Jallianwala_Bagh_massacre]
- Apologies: David Cameron (February 2013) called it "a deeply shameful event"; Theresa May (April 2019) called it a "shameful scar". Neither gave a formal apology. [source: WP:Jallianwala_Bagh_massacre]
- Historian Kim A. Wagner (*Amritsar 1919*, Yale, 2019) argues that Dyer's firing was an act of fear rooted in British memories of 1857, not an aberration. [source: https://newbooksnetwork.com/kim-a-wagner-amritsar-1919-an-empire-of-fear-and-the-making-of-a-massacre-yale-up-2019]

## 13. The Petrov Affair (1954)
- Vladimir Petrov, a KGB colonel at the Soviet embassy in Canberra, defected on 3 April 1954; his wife Evdokia followed on 20 April 1954. On 19 April crowds at Sydney's Mascot airport tried to stop her being flown out, and she was freed from her guards at Darwin. [source: WP:Petrov_Affair; WP:Royal_Commission_on_Espionage]
- Robert Menzies announced a Royal Commission on Espionage (1954–55) shortly before the 1954 election, which Labor had been expected to win. No one was charged and no major spy ring was found. [source: WP:Petrov_Affair]
- Labor leader H. V. Evatt's conspiracy claims (he read Molotov's denial to Parliament) and his attack on B. A. Santamaria's "Movement" in October 1954 led to the 1955 Labor split. Labor did not win office again until 1972. [source: WP:Petrov_Affair]
- Historiography: ASIO files opened in 1984. Robert Manne (*The Petrov Affair*, 1987) showed there was no conspiracy, that the documents (e.g. "Document J" by Rupert Lockwood) were mostly political gossip, and that Evatt's conduct drove events. The earlier "left" account was Whitlam and Stubbs, *Nest of Traitors* (1974). [source: WP:Petrov_Affair]

## 14. Trial by media: the Chamberlain "dingo" case (1980–2012)
- Nine-week-old Azaria Chamberlain disappeared from a campsite at Uluru on 17 August 1980. Her parents, Lindy and Michael (Seventh-day Adventists), said a dingo took her. [source: WP:Death_of_Azaria_Chamberlain]
- The first inquest (findings on 20 February 1981, Australia's first live telecast of court proceedings) found a dingo attack. A second inquest followed. Lindy was convicted of murder on 29 October 1982 and given life; Michael received an 18-month suspended sentence. [source: WP:Death_of_Azaria_Chamberlain]
- Azaria's matinee jacket was found near dingo lairs on 2 February 1986, and Lindy was released on 7 February 1986. The Morling Royal Commission (report 1987) found reasonable doubt; the "foetal blood" evidence proved to be a sound-deadening compound. All convictions were quashed on 15 September 1988. [source: WP:Lindy_Chamberlain-Creighton; WP:Death_of_Azaria_Chamberlain]
- The Chamberlains received $1.3 million, under a third of their legal costs. A fourth inquest (12 June 2012, Coroner Elizabeth Morris) found that a dingo took Azaria. [source: WP:Death_of_Azaria_Chamberlain]
- The case is now a standard example of "trial by media"; rumours and "sickening jokes" polarised opinion. [source: WP:Death_of_Azaria_Chamberlain]

## 15. The Stolen Generations and institutional-abuse royal commissions
- Removals of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children took place under laws such as the NT Aboriginals Act 1910 and the NSW Aborigines Protection Amending Act 1915. Official estimates: "between one in ten and one in three" Indigenous children were taken in some regions between 1910 and 1970. [source: WP:Stolen_Generations]
- The historian Peter Read coined "the stolen generation(s)" (*The Stolen Generations*, 1981). [source: WP:Stolen_Generations]
- *Bringing Them Home* (1997) was the report of the National Inquiry by the Human Rights and Equal Opportunity Commission (Sir Ronald Wilson and Mick Dodson). It was established on 11 May 1995 under Attorney-General Michael Lavarch and reported on 26 May 1997, with 777 submissions, 535 of them Indigenous. It described the removals as genocide. Note: it was not a royal commission. [source: WP:Bringing_Them_Home]
- John Howard refused an apology ("Australians of this generation should not be required to accept guilt and blame for past actions and policies") and moved a Motion of Reconciliation (regret) on 26 August 1999. The first National Sorry Day was 26 May 1998. [source: WP:Stolen_Generations; WP:Bringing_Them_Home]
- Kevin Rudd's National Apology came on 13 February 2008: "For the pain, suffering, and hurt of these Stolen Generations, their descendants and for their families left behind, we say sorry." [source: WP:Apology_to_Australia's_Indigenous_peoples]
- Bruce Trevorrow became the first member of the Stolen Generations to win court compensation (A$525,000) on 1 August 2007. [source: WP:Stolen_Generations]
- The Royal Commission into Institutional Responses to Child Sexual Abuse was announced by Julia Gillard on 12 November 2012 and chaired by Justice Peter McClellan. Joanne McCarthy's reporting in the *Newcastle Herald* ("Shine the Light" campaign) was a catalyst. [source: WP:Royal_Commission_into_Institutional_Responses_to_Child_Sexual_Abuse]
- Its final report (15 December 2017) contained 409 recommendations in total. It drew on about 8,000 private sessions (one summary gives 8,013 sessions with 7,981 survivors) and 57 public hearings. [source: https://www.childabuseroyalcommission.gov.au/media-releases/final-report-released ; search summaries]
- On 22 October 2018 Scott Morrison gave a National Apology and announced a National Redress Scheme. [source: WP:Royal_Commission_into_Institutional_Responses_to_Child_Sexual_Abuse]

## 16. Beyond the empire: Dreyfus (1894–1906)
- Captain Alfred Dreyfus, an Alsatian Jewish artillery officer, was convicted of treason in December 1894 and sent to Devil's Island. The antisemitic *La Libre Parole* (Édouard Drumont) broke the story on 29 October 1894. [source: WP:Dreyfus_affair]
- Georges Picquart identified the real culprit, Ferdinand Walsin Esterhazy, in 1896, but Esterhazy was acquitted. Émile Zola's "J'Accuse…!" appeared in Clemenceau's *L'Aurore* on 13 January 1898; the paper, normally about 30,000 copies, printed nearly 300,000. Zola was convicted of libel and fled to England. [source: WP:Dreyfus_affair; WP:J'Accuse...!]
- The Rennes retrial of 1899 again convicted Dreyfus (10 years), but he was pardoned; he was exonerated in 1906. The press "played a crucial role" on both sides. [source: WP:Dreyfus_affair]
- Global echo: in 1925 the Palestinian Arab newspaper *Filastin* headed an editorial against the Balfour Declaration "J'Accuse!". [source: WP:J'Accuse...!]

## 17. Teapot Dome (1921–1929)
- Interior Secretary Albert B. Fall leased naval oil reserves at Teapot Dome (Wyoming) to Harry Sinclair and Elk Hills (California) to Edward Doheny without competitive bidding. He received a $100,000 interest-free loan from Doheny and other gifts. Senator Thomas J. Walsh led the investigation. [source: WP:Teapot_Dome_scandal]
- Fall was convicted on 24 October 1929 and jailed for a year, the first cabinet officer imprisoned for misconduct in office; no one was convicted of paying the bribes. *McGrain v. Daugherty* (1927) confirmed Congress's power to compel testimony. [source: WP:Albert_B._Fall; WP:Teapot_Dome_scandal]

## 18. Profumo (1963)
- War Secretary John Profumo, 46, had an affair with Christine Keeler, 19, from 1961. Keeler also knew the Soviet naval attaché Yevgeny Ivanov. [source: WP:Profumo_affair]
- The story surfaced after Johnny Edgecombe, an Antiguan ex-seaman, fired shots at Stephen Ward's flat. Edgecombe got seven years, and the case drew in the Jamaican singer "Lucky" Gordon. Profumo denied the affair to the Commons on 22 March 1963 and resigned on 5 June 1963. [source: WP:Profumo_affair]
- Stephen Ward took a fatal overdose during his trial; his conviction is widely seen as establishment revenge. Lord Denning's report (26 September 1963) found no security breach, and 4,000 copies sold in the first hour. Macmillan resigned in October 1963 and Labour won in 1964. [source: WP:Profumo_affair]
- Mandy Rice-Davies, told that Lord Astor denied knowing her: "Well he would, wouldn't he?" [source: WP:Mandy_Rice-Davies]

## 19. Watergate (1972–1974)
- The break-in at the DNC offices in the Watergate complex took place on 17 June 1972. Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein of *The Washington Post* were guided by "Deep Throat", revealed in 2005 as FBI associate director Mark Felt. [source: WP:Watergate_scandal]
- Events included the "Saturday Night Massacre", the *United States v. Nixon* tapes ruling (July 1974) and the "smoking gun" tape. On 9 August 1974 Nixon became the first U.S. president to resign. Sixty-nine people were charged. Ford pardoned Nixon on 8 September 1974. [source: WP:Watergate_scandal]
- Reforms: the Presidential Records Act and the Ethics in Government Act, plus the Church Committee. The suffix "-gate" entered the global lexicon. [source: WP:Watergate_scandal]

## 20. Iran–Contra (1985–1987)
- Secret U.S. arms sales to Iran (sought to free seven hostages in Lebanon) had proceeds diverted to the Contras in breach of the Boland Amendments. The story was broken by the Lebanese magazine *Ash-Shiraa* in November 1986. [source: WP:Iran–Contra_affair]
- Investigated by the Tower Commission, Congress and Independent Counsel Lawrence Walsh. Eleven convictions resulted, some vacated on appeal. Reagan (March 1987): "what began as a strategic opening to Iran deteriorated, in its implementation, into trading arms for hostages." George H. W. Bush pardoned the rest. [source: WP:Iran–Contra_affair]

## 21. Phone hacking and Leveson (2002–2018)
- The Information Commissioner's Operation Motorman (2002) found 305 journalists buying private data; the *Daily Mail* led with 952 transactions and the *News of the World* was fifth. [source: WP:News_International_phone_hacking_scandal]
- In July 2011 *The Guardian* (Nick Davies) revealed that murdered schoolgirl Milly Dowler's phone had been hacked. The *News of the World* closed on 10 July 2011 after 168 years. Andy Coulson was jailed for 18 months on 4 July 2014. [source: WP:News_International_phone_hacking_scandal]
- David Cameron announced the Leveson Inquiry on 6 July 2011. Its 2,000-page report appeared on 29 November 2012. Part 2 was cancelled by Matt Hancock on 1 March 2018. [source: WP:Leveson_Inquiry]

## 22. Global comparisons (non-Western scandals)
- **Japan, Siemens scandal (1914):** kickbacks from Siemens (15 per cent) and Vickers via Mitsui (25 per cent) to naval officers over the battlecruiser *Kongō* were exposed via Reuters. Tokyo protests of up to 50,000 turned violent on 10 and 14 February 1914. The Diet refused the navy budget and Prime Minister Yamamoto Gonnohyōe resigned on 24 March 1914. [source: WP:Siemens_scandal]
- **Lockheed (1976):** a Frank Church Senate subcommittee found $22 million in foreign bribes. In Japan, Prime Minister Kakuei Tanaka was arrested on 27 July 1976 and convicted on 12 October 1983 (of foreign-exchange violations, not bribery); he was sentenced to four years but remained free on appeal until his death in 1993. Prince Bernhard of the Netherlands was forced from public roles. Italy's President Giovanni Leone resigned on 15 June 1978. The scandal helped produce the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (19 December 1977). [source: WP:Lockheed_bribery_scandals]
- **Japan, Recruit (1988):** shares were given cheaply to politicians before going public; Prime Minister Noboru Takeshita's cabinet resigned. [source: WP:Recruit_scandal]
- **India, Bofors (1987):** a $285 million howitzer contract signed on 24 March 1986. Swedish Radio alleged kickbacks on 16 April 1987, and Chitra Subramaniam and N. Ram of *The Hindu* pursued the story with 350+ documents. It contributed to Congress's defeat in 1989. The Delhi High Court quashed charges against Rajiv Gandhi on 5 February 2004; the middleman Ottavio Quattrocchi died in 2013. [source: WP:Bofors_scandal]
- **South Africa, Arms Deal (1999):** contracts worth about R30 billion were signed on 3 December 1999. PAC MP Patricia de Lille raised the "De Lille dossier" in Parliament in 1999. Only Tony Yengeni and Schabir Shaik were convicted. The Seriti Commission (2011–16) dismissed most allegations, but its findings were overturned in 2019; a case against Zuma and Thales was still ongoing per the source. [source: WP:South_African_Arms_Deal]
- **Brazil, Lava Jato (2014–2021):** began in March 2014 over money laundering via a car wash in Brasília. It exposed Petrobras kickbacks (1–5 per cent) and an Odebrecht-led cartel. Odebrecht admitted bribing officials in twelve countries with some $788 million, and prosecutors cooperated with 61 countries. Lula was jailed in April 2018, released on 8 November 2019 after 580 days, and had his convictions annulled by Justice Edson Fachin on 8 March 2021. On 23 March 2021 the Supreme Court ruled 3–2 that Judge Sergio Moro was biased. Moro had become Bolsonaro's justice minister in 2019, and the "Vaza Jato" leaks (*The Intercept Brasil*, 2019) showed coordination between him and prosecutors. The task force was disbanded on 1 February 2021, and Lula won the presidency again in 2022. [source: WP:Operation_Car_Wash; WP:Luiz_Inácio_Lula_da_Silva]
- **Malaysia, 1MDB (2009–2025):** a sovereign fund set up in 2009 under Najib Razak. A 2015 leak reported by *The Edge*, *Sarawak Report* and *The Wall Street Journal* showed about US$700 million in his accounts. The U.S. DOJ called it the "largest kleptocracy case to date" (2016) and estimated over US$4.5 billion diverted (through Jho Low, with Goldman Sachs bond deals); some of the money financed *The Wolf of Wall Street*. Najib's coalition lost the election of 9 May 2018, its first defeat. He was convicted in the SRC case on 28 July 2020 (12 years; upheld 23 August 2022), and on 26 December 2025 on all 25 main 1MDB counts (15 years; appeal pending). [source: WP:1MDB_scandal]

## 23. Cross-referenced panics (covered elsewhere; brief use only)
- **Satanic panic:** it began with *Michelle Remembers* (1980). The *New York Times* (1994): of more than 12,000 accusations, police could not substantiate any allegation of organised cult abuse. [source: WP:Satanic_panic]
- **McMartin preschool trial:** accusations began in 1983 and trials ran 1987–1990, with no convictions; it was the longest and most expensive criminal trial series in U.S. history. [source: WP:McMartin_preschool_trial]
- **Lavender Scare:** in February 1950 Under Secretary John Peurifoy said 91 homosexuals had been allowed to resign. In 1953 the State Department reported 425 fired. Executive Order 10450 (1953) barred gay people from federal jobs, about 5,000 lost them, and it stayed partly in force until 1995. The term was popularised by David K. Johnson, *The Lavender Scare* (2004). [source: WP:Lavender_Scare]
- **#MeToo:** Tarana Burke used "Me Too" around 2006. After the Weinstein exposés (*New York Times*: Jodi Kantor and Megan Twohey; *New Yorker*: Ronan Farrow) in October 2017, Alyssa Milano's tweet of 15 October 2017 made it a hashtag that trended in at least 85 countries. [source: WP:MeToo_movement]

## Debates
- **Is "moral panic" a useful concept?** Cohen himself conceded that "panic" connotes irrationality and kept it as a metaphor. Goode and Ben-Yehuda offer measurable criteria. Hall et al. tie panics to state authority and race. The concept is a lens, and calling something a panic does not prove the danger was imaginary: Satanic ritual abuse was baseless, but institutional child abuse was real and vast. [source: WP:Moral_panic; WP:Satanic_panic; WP:Royal_Commission_into_Institutional_Responses_to_Child_Sexual_Abuse]
- **Did scandal drive reform, or did reform ride on panic?** The Maiden Tribute raised the age of consent, but the same Act criminalised gay men (Labouchère; whether a wrecking amendment is debated). Molesworth's influence on convict punishment is contested (Maxwell-Stewart). Watergate produced lasting statutes, while Teapot Dome left no one convicted of paying bribes. [source: as in sections above]
- **Humanitarian outrage and empire:** the Congo campaign sought Belgian annexation, not decolonisation (Alexander; Lösing on racial motives). The Boer camp outcry focused on white victims while Black deaths "elicited little outrage". Myall Creek convictions may have produced pacts of silence (Elder). [source: WP:Congo_Reform_Association; WP:Second_Boer_War_concentration_camps; WP:Myall_Creek_massacre]
- **Amritsar:** an aberration or the logic of colonial rule? Wagner sees fear rooted in 1857; Dyer's British supporters saw a saviour. Apology politics continue. [source: WP:Jallianwala_Bagh_massacre; Wagner]
- **Morant:** scapegoat of empire or war criminal? Australian pardon campaigners against Wilcox, Leach, and South African descendants of the victims. [source: WP:Breaker_Morant]
- **Petrov:** conspiracy (Whitlam and Stubbs, 1974) against Manne's archival verdict (1987). [source: WP:Petrov_Affair]
- **Stolen Generations:** whether the term and the "genocide" finding are apt (Herron, Brunton) against Read, Manne and Wilson. [source: WP:Stolen_Generations; WP:Bringing_Them_Home]
- **Anti-corruption crusades and lawfare:** Lava Jato as a model anti-corruption probe, or politicised (Moro found biased; Mendes, Toffoli). [source: WP:Operation_Car_Wash]
- **Tichborne:** Orton or Roger? Most accept Orton; some see residual doubt; McWilliam stresses class politics. [source: WP:Tichborne_case]

## Conflicts with published chapters
- **Boer War camp deaths.** British Empire chapter: "between 18,000 and 26,000 Boer civilians died in concentration camps, and in separate camps for Black Africans at least 14,000, and probably about 20,000". The Black figures match WP. For Boers, the WP camps article gives 27,927, just above the published upper bound. The Hobhouse article gives "some 34,000 white women and children" (an outlier), and Pakenham (quoted) "at least twenty thousand whites". Suggest the writer give "about 26,000–28,000" attributed, or reuse the published range and note the higher count. The published chapter should perhaps be checked (candidate for KNOWN_ISSUES).
- **Morant Bay toll.** Victorian Era: "soldiers killed 439 people directly, 354 more were arrested and later executed" (matches WP:Morant_Bay_rebellion). British Empire: "439 people outright and hundreds more were executed". But WP:Edward_John_Eyre says "up to 439 … killed in the reprisals", and Victorian Web says the Royal Commission established 439 as the approximate number of deaths, which may include executions. Also note that the Morant Bay lead gives "more than 400" killed and "more than 300" arrested. Do not add 439 + 354 into a total; say "about 439 killed, many after summary courts-martial (figures differ on how many were executed)".
- **Bringing Them Home:** WP:Stolen_Generations calls it a "Royal Commission report". It was a HREOC National Inquiry; do not call it a royal commission.
