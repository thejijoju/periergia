## Queens, queen mothers and the kandakes of Kush
- *Kandake* (Meroitic *kdke*, Greek *Kandakē*, Latin *Candace*) was a Meroitic term for a queen or queen mother of Kush. It is attested for six or seven women. Some ruled; others were probably royal wives or mothers. A kandake who ruled in her own right also bore the male ruler's title *qore*. Greek and Roman writers wrongly took it for a personal name. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandake]
- At least ten queens regnant of Meroë are counted between about 260 BCE and 320 CE. Royal mothers had a recognised part in Kushite enthronement: the stelae of Tanwetamani, Harsiyotef and Nastasen show the king with his mother and wife. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandake]
- The kentake Shanakdakheto (about 170 BCE) appears in reliefs in armour holding a spear, as an independent ruler. Note that this sentence is flagged "citation needed" on Wikipedia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandake]
- Amanirenas ruled Kush in the mid-to-late 1st century BCE (Wikipedia's infobox gives about 40–10 BCE). Inscriptions call her both *qore* and *kandake*, which suggests she ruled in her own right. Meroitic is still not fully understood. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amanirenas]
- War with Rome, 25–22 BCE (Wikipedia's dates). While the prefect Aelius Gallus was away in Arabia in 25 BCE, Kushite forces took Syene (Aswan), Elephantine and Philae and pulled down statues of Augustus. The prefect Gaius Petronius drove them back and razed Napata. Envoys from Amanirenas were sent to Augustus at Samos. Augustus dropped the tribute demand, and the frontier was fixed at Hiere Sycaminos (Maharraqa). The peace lasted largely until the late 3rd century CE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amanirenas]
- Strabo (*Geography* 17.53–54) describes "Queen Candace" as "a masculine sort of woman, and blind in one eye". He is almost certainly describing Amanirenas. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amanirenas]
- The bronze Meroë Head of Augustus was buried under the steps of a temple at Meroë, probably a victory shrine. John Garstang found it in 1910, and it has been in the British Museum since 1911. The Hamadab Stela (found 1914) names Amanirenas and Akinidad and may be a Kushite account of the war. It may contain the word "Areme" (Rome). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amanirenas]
- The story that a "Candace of Meroë" faced Alexander the Great comes from the *Alexander Romance* and is legend. Alexander never attacked Nubia. The Candace of Acts 8:27 (the Ethiopian eunuch) is where the English given name comes from. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandake]
- "Queen mother" is an English umbrella term for many different African offices. Examples: Akan *ohemmaa* ("female ruler"), Benin *iyoba*, Yoruba *iya oba*, Dahomey *kpojito*, Burundi *mugabekazi*. Among the Akan, queen mothers rule beside the chief, keep genealogical knowledge, nominate candidates for a vacant stool and hold courts for disputes brought by women, and some have led in war. Benin's first iyoba was Queen Idia, after a succession conflict around the end of the 15th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_mother_(Africa)]
- Colonial officials dealt mainly with titled men and often recorded queen mothers only as "sisters" of rulers. After independence, Ghana's leaders in 1957 also worked only with male chiefs. Article 277 of Ghana's 1992 constitution recognises a queen mother as a customary office. In 2010 the National House of Chiefs announced the inclusion of 20 queen mothers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_mother_(Africa)]
- In the Asante system, each male stool (*ohene*, *omanhene*) had a female counterpart, the *ohemaa*, of the same matrilineage. The Asantehemaa sat on the Kotoko council. Female stool-holders took part in judicial and legislative business, in making war and peace, and in distributing land. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaa_Asantewaa]

## Amina of Zazzau (and the debate over her historicity)
- Amina (Aminatu) of Zazzau (Zaria, now in Kaduna State, Nigeria) is a Hausa figure. Palace tradition calls her queen (*sarauniya*); other traditions call her a princess (*gimbiya*). Her name is missing from the regnal lists in the *Chronicle of Abuja* and from two lists published by E. J. Arnett. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amina_of_Zazzau]
- Her dates are disputed:
  - The *Kano Chronicle* (late 19th century, drawing on earlier material) makes her a contemporary of Muhammad Dauda of Kano (1421–1438), with a 34-year reign. Some scholars therefore date her to the 15th century.
  - Oral tradition calls her a daughter of Bakwa Turunku, whom the Zaria chronicle dates to 1492–1522, giving an early 16th-century date.
  - The historian Abdullahi Smith placed her reign after 1576. The popular dates are "reigned 1576–1610" or "born c. 1533".

  Present all of these as traditions, not facts. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amina_of_Zazzau]
- Sultan Muhammad Bello of Sokoto, in *Infaq al-Maysur*, credited her with conquests and with tribute from Katsina and Kano. Modern historians dispute the tribute claim. Wikipedia dates the work both to 1812 and to "around 1836", so avoid giving a year. Tradition also credits her with the earthen city walls known as *ganuwar Amina* and with introducing kola nuts. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amina_of_Zazzau]
- The historian Michael Crowder observed that after Amina, ruling-class Hausa women "experienced a steady diminution in their influence and were systematically deprived of their authority and autonomy." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amina_of_Zazzau]

## Njinga of Ndongo and Matamba (consistent with the published *Kongo, Asante, and Dahomey* chapter)
- Njinga (Nzinga) Mbande lived about 1583 to 17 December 1663. She ruled Ndongo (1624–1663) and Matamba (1631–1663) in present-day northern Angola. Her subjects called her Ngola Njinga; *ngola* is the root of "Angola". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nzinga_of_Ndongo_and_Matamba]
- Her brother sent her as his envoy to Luanda in 1621–22. She was baptised there as Dona Ana de Sousa, after the governor João Correia de Sousa and his wife, and returned in late 1622 with a treaty. The "popular story" says she was offered only a mat while the Portuguese had chairs, and an attendant made herself into a seat for her. Present it as a story. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nzinga_of_Ndongo_and_Matamba]
- Timeline:
  - 1626: Portugal declared war.
  - 1628: her forces were driven into exile.
  - She allied with the Imbangala through marriage to the warlord Kasanje.
  - 1631–1635: she conquered Matamba.
  - 1641: allied with the Dutch West India Company after the Dutch took Luanda.
  - 1641–44: retook much of Ndongo.
  - 1648: the Portuguese recapture of Luanda ended the Dutch alliance.
  - Late 1656: peace treaty, with the Lucala River as the border.

  [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nzinga_of_Ndongo_and_Matamba]
- Njinga was also a large-scale slave trader. Wikipedia reports that the Dutch bought as many as 13,000 enslaved people a year from her kingdom, and the 1656 peace concentrated the slave trade at a market in her capital. Use this for the seed's "women in and against the slave trades". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nzinga_of_Ndongo_and_Matamba]
- In wartime she took the masculine dress and manner of an Imbangala warlord. After the peace her court became more feminine, she abolished concubinage, and she married her favourite consort in a Christian ceremony. The tale that she kept 50–60 men dressed as women and had lovers killed comes from Dapper's *Description of Africa* and is called "legend (having no proof)". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nzinga_of_Ndongo_and_Matamba]
- Historiography:
  - Joseph C. Miller's 1975 *Journal of African History* essay criticised her autocratic methods.
  - Linda Heywood (2017) warns against making her either a populist hero or a tyrant.
  - Angola's nationalists made her a symbol during the independence war. On 23 December 2014 Angola issued a 20-kwanza coin in her honour.

  [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nzinga_of_Ndongo_and_Matamba]

## Yaa Asantewaa and the War of the Golden Stool (consistent with the published *Imperial Expansion* chapter)
- Yaa Asantewaa lived about 1840 to 17 October 1921. She was Queen Mother of Ejisu (Edweso) in the Asante kingdom, appointed by her brother the Ejisuhene. As queen mother she nominated her grandson as Ejisuhene. When the British exiled him to the Seychelles in 1896, along with Prempeh I, she became regent. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaa_Asantewaa]
- On 25 March 1900 Governor Frederick Hodgson demanded the Golden Stool at Kumasi. The rising began in March 1900 and besieged the British in the Kumasi fort. A relief force under Major James Willcocks relieved the fort on 15 July 1900. Britain annexed Asante on 1 January 1902. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Golden_Stool] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaa_Asantewaa]
- Wikipedia calls her choice as war leader by several Asante chiefs "the first and only example of a woman being given that role in Asante history". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaa_Asantewaa]
- She and about 15 advisers were exiled to the Seychelles in 1901. Sources differ on the date of her capture (late 1900 or 3 March 1901) and of her transport (22 March or 22 May 1901). She died there on 17 October 1921. Prempeh I and the remaining exiles were allowed home on 17 December 1924 and brought her remains back for burial. The British never got the Golden Stool. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaa_Asantewaa] [source: https://eaumf.org/ejm-blog/2017/10/17/october-17-1921-yaa-asantewaa-queenmother-of-ejisu-dies-in-the-seychelles]
- The centenary in 2000 was marked by a Yaa Asantewaa festival and museum. Asante is matrilineal: lineage runs through a common female ancestor. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaa_Asantewaa]

## Matrilineal societies and the Lovedu Rain Queen
- The Rain Queen (Modjadji) is the hereditary queen of the Balobedu (Lovedu) of Limpopo Province, South Africa. She is believed to control rain. The queenship descends by matrilineal primogeniture, from mother to eldest daughter. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rain_Queen]
- The first Rain Queen, Maselekwane Modjadji I, reigned from 1800 to 1854; a male line had reigned before 1800. The founding traditions trace the dynasty to 16th-century Monomotapa (south-eastern Zimbabwe). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rain_Queen]
- The queen does not marry, but councils send her "wives" from subject chiefs' households as a form of diplomacy. Eileen and Jack Krige's fieldwork in the 1930s produced *The Realm of a Rain-Queen* (1943). She is said to have inspired H. Rider Haggard's *She* (attributed). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rain_Queen]
- Recent succession:
  - Makobo Modjadji VI was crowned on 16 April 2003 at 25 and died on 12 June 2005.
  - Her uncle (Makobo's brother) Prince Mpapatla was regent from 2005 to 20 January 2023.
  - Masalanabo Modjadji VII (born 20 January 2005) succeeded in 2023 after a dispute with a male claimant and was legally recognised in December 2024. Her coronation, planned for August 2025, was cancelled.

  [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rain_Queen]
- "Matrilineal belt" is the name for a band of south-central Africa where societies tend to trace descent through women. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrilineal_belt]

## The women soldiers of Dahomey (Agojie / Mino)
- Europeans called them "Amazons". The Fon names include *Agojie*, *Mino* ("our mothers") and *ahosi* ("king's wives"). The corps existed from the 17th century to the 1890s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahomey_Amazons]
- Origin traditions, all attributed:
  - King Houegbadja (1645–1685) organised women elephant hunters (*gbeto*).
  - Queen Hangbe (about 1716–1718) formed a female bodyguard; some doubt she existed.
  - Agaja used the women soldiers against Whydah (Savi) in 1727.
  - Ghezo (1818–1858) expanded them into a serious military force.

  [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahomey_Amazons]
- Visitors' reports put them at 1,000 to 6,000 by the mid-19th century, about a third of the army; present this as an attributed range. They were recruited from palace women, from captives and from free Dahomean women. They were legally married to the king and forbidden to have children while serving. In the Grand Council, 1840s–1870s, most Mino favoured raiding smaller neighbours over attacking Abeokuta. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahomey_Amazons]
- They took part in slave raiding. Survivors of the *Clotilda*, the last ship known to have carried enslaved Africans to the United States, said the women soldiers took part in their capture. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahomey_Amazons]
- At Adégon, 6 October 1892, in the Second Franco-Dahomean War, the corps was largely destroyed in hand-to-hand combat. The figures given are 86 Dahomean regulars and 417 Mino killed, against 6 French dead. The corps was disbanded when Dahomey became a French protectorate in 1894. Nawi, thought to be the last veteran, was interviewed in 1978 and died in November 1979. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahomey_Amazons]
- Dahomey's queen mother, the *kpojito* (for example Hwanjile), counselled the king and pleaded before him in capital cases. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_mother_(Africa)]

## Market women, merchant queens and the slave trades
- In Igbo dual-sex government the *Omu* ("mother of the society") headed a council of women. She regulated the market, set prices and settled disputes, and held her title independently of male relatives. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_Nigeria]
- Omu Okwei of Ossomari (Okwei Opene, 1872–1943) was an Igbo merchant on the Niger who traded with European firms. She was crowned Omu of Ossomari in August 1935, chaired the Council of Mothers, and is described as the last such merchant queen before the British displaced the council's oversight of retail trade. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omu_Okwei]
- The Yoruba *Iyalode* is a high-ranking female chieftaincy title, sometimes called "king of the women". Efunroye Tinubu (about 1810–1887) was a Lagos aristocrat and merchant. She traded palm oil, tobacco, salt, firearms and enslaved people, and allegedly owned more than 360 enslaved people. She kept trading slaves after the 1852 Anglo-Lagos treaty, was expelled to Abeokuta under British pressure, and became Iyalode of Egbaland. Tinubu Square in Lagos is named after her. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efunroye_Tinubu] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iyalode]
- Signares were African and Afro-European women of Saint-Louis and Gorée in Senegal who gained wealth and status through marriages "à la mode du pays" (by local custom) to European traders. Many owned property and enslaved people. Preneau de Pommegorge (in West Africa until 1765) wrote that "many African women own thirty to forty slaves which they hire to the company." The Portuguese/Luso-African equivalent was the *nhara*, for example Bibiana Vaz. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signare]
- Anne Pépin (about 1747–1837) was the richest woman on Gorée. From 1786 she was associated with Governor Stanislas de Boufflers, who moved the capital to Gorée. Her brother Nicolas built the house now called the *Maison des Esclaves*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_P%C3%A9pin]
- Gender in the slave trades:
  - Robertson and Klein's *Women and Slavery in Africa* (1983) argued that women were the majority of enslaved people inside Africa, partly because of internal demand.
  - The Atlantic trade carried more men. Eltis-based data put the female share at roughly a third for much of the 18th century and about 31 per cent in the 19th, higher in the earliest period. These are database estimates and may be revised.

  [source: https://gwonline.unc.edu/node/3934] [source: https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-3-030-28099-4_6]
- Kumasi Central Market is described by its publisher as probably the largest market of its kind in West Africa. It is run largely by Asante women traders and their "market queens", as studied by Gracia Clark in *Onions Are My Husband* (University of Chicago Press, 1994). [source: https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/O/bo3629805.html]
- On 18 August 1979 Rawlings's government destroyed Accra's Makola Market, blaming market women for Ghana's economic problems. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makola_Market]
- Market women formed part of the base of Nkrumah's Convention People's Party, founded in June 1949; see the published *Decolonization* chapter. [source: published chapter history--20th-century-history--decolonization]

## Spiritual authority: Nana Asma'u, Kimpa Vita and Nehanda
- **Nana Asma'u** (1793–1864) was the daughter of Usman dan Fodio. She wrote in Arabic, Fulfulde, Hausa and Tamasheq and left more than 60 surviving works over 40 years, many in *ajami* (African languages in Arabic script). From about 1830 she trained women teachers, the *jajis*, who taught women in their homes; their students formed the *'yan taru* ("those who congregate together"). This is consistent with the published *Africa to 1860* chapter, which gives the same four languages and dates. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nana_Asma%CA%BCu]
- **Kimpa Vita (Dona Beatriz)** lived 1684 to 2 July 1706. She was a Kongolese noblewoman trained as an *nganga marinda* (religious medium). In 1704 she claimed possession by Saint Anthony and taught that Jesus, Mary and St Francis were Kongolese. She reoccupied São Salvador in 1705 and adapted the Salve Regina as the *Salve Antoniana*. She was tried as a witch and heretic under Kongo law, with the counsel of the Capuchins Bernardo da Gallo and Lorenzo da Lucca, and burned at Evululu in 1706 by forces of Pedro IV. This matches the published chapter. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimpa_Vita]
- **Mbuya Nehanda (Charwe Nyakasikana)** lived about 1840 to 27 April 1898. She was a *svikiro* (spirit medium) of the Nehanda *mhondoro* (royal ancestral spirit) among the Zezuru Shona. The historian D. N. Beach suggests she was possessed by the spirit from 1884. With the medium Kaguvi she was a leader of the First Chimurenga (1896–97) against the British South Africa Company. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nehanda_Charwe_Nyakasikana]
- She was convicted of ordering the killing of Native Commissioner Henry Hawkins Pollard and hanged on 27 April 1898. The Wikipedia text also says "March 1898"; use 27 April, the date in Father Francis Richartz's eyewitness account. The trial records in the National Archives of Zimbabwe were added to UNESCO's Memory of the World register in 2015. A statue was unveiled in Harare in May 2021. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nehanda_Charwe_Nyakasikana]
- Mekatilili wa Menza led the Giriama rising of 1913–14 in Kenya. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mau_Mau_rebellion]

## Colonial rule and women's status
- Under indirect rule, women were largely shut out of the bodies the British created. Abeokuta's councils had traditionally included at least one woman; the Egba United Government set up after 1897 was all-male. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abeokuta_Women%27s_Revolt]
- Colonial officials negotiated with titled men, and queen mothers and other female office-holders lost "social, religious, constitutional, and political privileges and rights". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_mother_(Africa)]
- "Sitting on a man" (or "making war on a man") was an Igbo sanction in which women gathered at a man's compound, sang and listed their grievances, and could damage his house. Judith Van Allen's essay "'Sitting on a Man': Colonialism and the Lost Political Institutions of Igbo Women" (*Canadian Journal of African Studies* 6.2, 1972) is the classic statement that colonialism destroyed these institutions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sitting_on_a_man] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_War]
- Ahebi Ugbabe (died 1948) was warrant chief and king (*eze*) of Enugu-Ezike. Nwando Achebe calls her the only female king in colonial Nigeria and writes that she was "a strong leader of her people, yet also a collaborator empowered by and serving the British colonial regime". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahebi_Ugbabe]

## The Women's War, 1929 (consistent with the published *Interwar* and *British Empire* chapters)
- The Igbo name is *Ogu Umunwanyi*; the Ibibio and Annang name is *Ekong Iban*. Both mean "women's war". British records called it the "Aba Riots", and scholars such as Judith Van Allen (1975) argued that the name depoliticised it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_War]
- Causes: direct tax on men from April 1928; a recount of the nominal roll in Bende division by Captain J. Cook from September 1929, which listed wives, children and livestock; and the slump that hit women's trade. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_War]
- At Oloko, the census-taker Mark Emereuwa (spelt variously), working for warrant chief Okugo, told Nwanyeruwa to count her goats, sheep and people. She replied, "Was your widowed mother counted?" Wikipedia dates this to 18 November 1929. History Today gives 23 November and calls the events "contested". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_War] [source: https://www.historytoday.com/archive/months-past/womens-war-breaks-out]
- About 10,000 women gathered at Oloko, led by the "Oloko Trio" Ikonnia, Nwannedia and Nwugo. Nwanyeruwa summarised the demands as "women will not pay tax till the world ends [and] Chiefs were not to exist any more." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_War]
- The revolt spread over about 6,000 square miles of Owerri and Calabar provinces, home to about 2 million people. Courts attacked: Wikipedia's lead says 16 Native Courts were attacked, most of them destroyed; its body says ten were destroyed and others damaged. European factories at Imo River, Aba, Mbawsi and Amata were looted. The last troops left Owerri on 27 December 1929 and the last patrol withdrew on 9 January 1930. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_War]
- Deaths are attributed estimates:
  - Wikipedia gives "about fifty-five women" killed by troops.
  - Search summaries of other accounts give 51 women and 1 man.
  - The UK National Archives blog reports 16 killed at Utu Etim Ekpo on 15 December; Encyclopedia.com gives about 18 dead and 19 wounded there (14 December).
  - At Opobo (Ikot Abasi) on 16 December, 31 women and 1 man were killed and 31 women wounded.
  - T. Obinkaram Echewa's oral accounts suggest about 100 women killed at Aba alone.

  Recommended wording: "more than fifty women". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_War] [source: https://encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/igbo-womens-war] (National Archives figures via search snippet; the page itself could not be fetched.)
- The Aba Commission of Inquiry (1930) sat for 38 days and heard 485 witnesses, only about 103 of them women. The warrant-chief system was abolished and women were appointed to Native Courts for the first time. Mary Okezie, the only woman to submit a memorandum to the commission, later led the Ngwa Women's Association. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_War]

## The Abeokuta Women's Revolt and Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti
- Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti, born Frances Abigail Olufunmilayo Thomas, lived from 25 October 1900 to 13 April 1978, born in Abeokuta. Her children included Olikoye (later health minister), the musician Fela and the activist Beko. She was the first woman in Abeokuta to drive a car (mid-1930s). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funmilayo_Ransome-Kuti]
- She helped found the Abeokuta Ladies' Club in 1932, which in the 1940s became the Abeokuta Women's Union (AWU) and united educated women with market women. Grace Eniola Soyinka was also a leader. The AWU campaigned against flat-rate taxes on women and against the Alake (Ladapo Ademola) and the Sole Native Authority. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abeokuta_Women%27s_Revolt]
- Women in Abeokuta had paid a separate tax since 1 January 1918.
- The protests:
  - Mid-October 1946: nearly 1,000 women marched on the palace.
  - 1947: protests of over 10,000 women, including a camp-out outside the palace on 8–10 December.
  - When permits were refused, women held "picnics" and "festivals".
  - On 3 January 1949 the Alake abdicated; Ransome-Kuti's own article says "temporarily", and he later returned. Four women got seats in the reformed administration, and the tax on women was suspended (later partly reversed).

  [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abeokuta_Women%27s_Revolt] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funmilayo_Ransome-Kuti]
- The *West African Pilot* called her the "Lioness of Lisabi". She was the only woman in the 1947 NCNC delegation to London, where she wrote "We Had Equality 'til Britain Came" in the *Daily Worker*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funmilayo_Ransome-Kuti]
- She founded the Nigerian Women's Union (May 1949) and the Federation of Nigerian Women's Societies (1953). She was vice-president of the Women's International Democratic Federation from 1953, and was the first woman appointed to the Western House of Chiefs. Britain refused to renew her passport in 1957 and the US denied her a visa in 1958. She received the Lenin Peace Prize in 1970. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funmilayo_Ransome-Kuti]
- Northern Nigerian women did not get the vote at independence; it came by military decree in 1976. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funmilayo_Ransome-Kuti]
- On 18 February 1977 about 1,000 soldiers stormed Fela's Kalakuta Republic compound. She was thrown from a second-floor window and died of her injuries on 13 April 1978. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funmilayo_Ransome-Kuti]

## Women in liberation struggles
- **Kenya.** Women formed a core part of Mau Mau, mainly by keeping supply and intelligence lines open; a smaller number fought. Mũthoni Kĩrĩma (1931 – 4 September 2023) was the only woman to reach the Mau Mau rank of field marshal. A few thousand women and girls were held at the all-women Kamiti camp. Oathing was extended to women around 1943 at Olenguruone. Women in Murang'a revolted against forced labour in 1947. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mau_Mau_rebellion] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muthoni_Kirima]
- **Mozambique.** Josina Machel (10 August 1945 – 7 April 1971) joined FRELIMO's Women's Detachment (*Destacamento Feminino*), headed its Department of Social Affairs in 1969 and married Samora Machel the same year. The anniversary of her death, 7 April, is Mozambique's Women's Day. FRELIMO founded the Organisation of Mozambican Women (OMM) in March 1973. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josina_Machel]
- Samora Machel opened the First Conference of Mozambican Women on 4 March 1973 with a speech titled (Portuguese) *A libertação da mulher é uma necessidade da revolução, garantia da sua continuidade, condição do seu triunfo*. [source: https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/machel/1973/liberation-women.htm]
- **Guinea-Bissau.** Cabral's PAIGC set up a women's union and required women on village councils; this is already in the published *Decolonization* chapter.
- **South Africa: Charlotte Maxeke.** Maxeke (7 April 1871 – 16 October 1939) took a BSc at Wilberforce University, Ohio, in 1903. She was the first Black South African woman to graduate from a university, and she founded the Bantu Women's League in 1918 (see Conflicts below). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Maxeke]
- **South Africa: the ANC and its Women's League.** Women in the Orange Free State campaigned against passes for women around 1912–13 and burned passes. Women became full ANC members in 1943, and the ANC Women's League was created in 1948 with Madie Hall Xuma as its first president. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_National_Congress_Women%27s_League]
- **South Africa: FEDSAW.** The Federation of South African Women was founded on 17 April 1954 at a conference of about 150 delegates, which adopted a Women's Charter. It contributed a *What Women Demand* document to the 1955 Congress of the People. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federation_of_South_African_Women] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_March_(South_Africa)]
- **South Africa: the Women's March.** On 9 August 1956 an estimated 20,000 women marched to the Union Buildings in Pretoria. It was a Thursday, domestic workers' day off. They were led by Lilian Ngoyi, Helen Joseph, Rahima Moosa and Sophia Williams-De Bruyn and carried about 14,000 petitions. Strijdom was absent, and his secretary took them. The women stood 30 minutes in silence, then sang "Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika" and "Wathint' abafazi". The day is now National Women's Day, and a monument was unveiled on 9 August 2000. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_March_(South_Africa)]
- **South Africa: Lilian Ngoyi.** Ngoyi (25 September 1911 – 13 March 1980) was a garment machinist (1945–56). She was the first woman elected to the ANC's national executive and was among the 19 women arrested in the 1956 Treason Trial. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lillian_Ngoyi]

## Post-independence politics
- In Ghana, the 1957 independence leaders worked only with male chiefs and left out queen mothers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_mother_(Africa)]
- Bolanle Awe was the first chair of Nigeria's National Commission for Women (1990–92). She resigned when the commission was placed under the First Lady, Maryam Babangida. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolanle_Awe]
- **Liberia: Women of Liberia Mass Action for Peace.** Started in 2003 by Leymah Gbowee, Comfort Freeman and Crystal Roh Gawding, it brought Christian and Muslim women together in nonviolent protest, including a sex strike, to end the Second Liberian Civil War. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_of_Liberia_Mass_Action_for_Peace]
- **Liberia: Ellen Johnson Sirleaf's election.** Sirleaf, born 29 October 1938, is a Harvard-trained economist and was finance minister 1979–80. She came second to George Weah in the first round of 2005 and won the run-off with about 59 per cent to 40. She took office on 16 January 2006 as Africa's first *elected* female head of state, and was re-elected in 2011. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellen_Johnson_Sirleaf]
- **Liberia: the TRC and the Nobel Prize.**
  - In June 2009 Liberia's Truth and Reconciliation Commission listed her among 50 people who should be barred from public office for 30 years, because of her early financial support for Charles Taylor.
  - In 2011 she shared the Nobel Peace Prize with Leymah Gbowee and Tawakkol Karman (Yemen).

  [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellen_Johnson_Sirleaf]
- **Rwanda: the quota.** The 2003 constitution set a quota of at least 30 per cent women and reserves 24 of the 80 seats in the Chamber of Deputies for women elected by provincial councils. Rwanda ranked 24th in women's representation in 1995 and rose to 1st in 2003. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamber_of_Deputies_(Rwanda)] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_quota]
- **Rwanda: women's share of seats.**
  - 2008: about 56 per cent, the first women's majority in any parliament.
  - 2018: 61.25–61.3 per cent.
  - July 2024: 51 of 80 seats (63.75 per cent, which the IPU rounds to 63.8). That is 25 directly elected and 26 indirectly elected.
  - The Senate is about 46 per cent women.

  Recommended wording: "about 64 per cent after the 2024 election". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_quota] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_government] [source: https://iknowpolitics.org/en/news/number-women-rwandas-lower-house-parliament-rises-638] [source: https://www.representwomen.org/july_s_parliamentary_elections_reaffirm_rwanda_as_a_global_leader_in_women_s_representation]

## Wangari Maathai and the Green Belt Movement
- Wangarĩ Maathai (1 April 1940 – 25 September 2011) was born in Ihithe, Tetu, Kenya. In 1971 she received a PhD in veterinary anatomy from the University College of Nairobi, becoming the first woman in East and Central Africa to earn a doctorate. She became associate professor in 1977. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wangar%C4%A9_Maathai]
- She founded the Green Belt Movement in 1977 under the National Council of Women of Kenya. The first tree planting was on World Environment Day, 5 June 1977. The movement reports more than 51 million trees planted and over 30,000 women trained. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Belt_Movement] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wangar%C4%A9_Maathai]
- In 1989 she fought the planned 60-storey Kenya Times Media Trust tower in Uhuru Park. President Moi said its opponents had "insects in their heads". In 1992 she was beaten unconscious by police at the "Freedom Corner" hunger strike for political prisoners (28 February – 3 March). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wangar%C4%A9_Maathai]
- In December 2002 she won Tetu with about 98 per cent of the vote. She was Assistant Minister for Environment from January 2003. She received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004, the first African woman to do so. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wangar%C4%A9_Maathai]

## Recovering women's voices: African feminist thought and scholarship
- **Ifi Amadiume** (born 23 April 1947 in Kaduna, of Igbo parents) took a PhD in social anthropology at SOAS in 1983. *Male Daughters, Female Husbands: Gender and Sex in an African Society* (Zed, 1987) is based on fieldwork in Nnobi (Idemili). It argues that in pre-colonial Igbo society gender and sex did not necessarily coincide:
  - Daughters could become "male daughters" with rights as social sons.
  - Wealthy women could become "female husbands" through woman-to-woman marriage.
  - Colonialism and Christianity eroded women's power.

  The book's parts run 19th century, colonial period ("Colonialism and the Erosion of Women's Power"), post-independence. It was named one of Africa's 100 Best Books of the 20th Century (Zimbabwe International Book Fair, 2002). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Male_Daughters,_Female_Husbands:_Gender_and_Sex_in_an_African_Society] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ifi_Amadiume]
- **Oyèrónkẹ́ Oyěwùmí** (born 10 November 1957, Ogbomoso) studied at Ibadan and Berkeley and teaches at Stony Brook. *The Invention of Women: Making an African Sense of Western Gender Discourses* (University of Minnesota Press, 1997) argues that among the Oyo-Yoruba the main organising principle was seniority, not gender. It notes that Yoruba pronouns and kin terms are not gendered, and argues that "woman" as a social category was a colonial imposition. The book won the American Sociological Association's 1998 Distinguished Book Award (Sex and Gender). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oy%C3%A8r%C3%B3nk%E1%BA%B9%CC%81_Oy%C4%9Bw%C3%B9m%C3%AD]
- **Molara Ogundipe (Ogundipe-Leslie)** (27 December 1940 – 18 June 2019) was the first woman to take a first-class BA in English at University College Ibadan. She coined STIWA, "Social Transformation Including Women in Africa", set out in *Re-Creating Ourselves: African Women & Critical Transformations* (1994). Her contribution to *Sisterhood Is Global* (1984) was "Not Spinning on the Axis of Maleness". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molara_Ogundipe]
- **Amina Mama**, born in Kaduna to a Nigerian father and English mother, took a PhD at Birkbeck in 1987. She wrote *Beyond the Masks: Race, Gender and Subjectivity* (Routledge, 1995), directed the African Gender Institute at the University of Cape Town, and co-founded *Feminist Africa*, the first continental journal of gender studies. She has been at UC Davis since 2010. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amina_Mama]
- **Patricia McFadden** (born 1952, eSwatini) is a radical feminist sociologist. She edited the *Southern African Feminist Review* (1995–2000), worked at SARIPS in Zimbabwe (1993–2005) and wrote "Becoming Postcolonial: African Women Changing the Meaning of Citizenship" (2005). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patricia_McFadden]
- **Other African frameworks.**
  - Chikwenye Okonjo Ogunyemi: African womanism.
  - Catherine Acholonu: *Motherism: The Afrocentric Alternative to Feminism*.
  - Obioma Nnaemeka: nego-feminism, "the feminism of negotiation" and "no ego" feminism.
  - The African Feminist Forum, launched in Accra, published a *Charter of Feminist Principles for African Feminists*.

  [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_feminism] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter_of_Feminist_Principles_for_African_Feminists]
- **Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie** (born 15 September 1977) gave the TEDx talk "We Should All Be Feminists" at TEDxEuston in 2012; it was published as a book by Fourth Estate in 2014. Beyoncé sampled it in "Flawless" (2013). Her other works include the talk "The Danger of a Single Story" (2009) and *Dear Ijeawele, or A Feminist Manifesto in Fifteen Suggestions* (2017). Her comments on trans women since 2017 drew criticism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/We_Should_All_Be_Feminists] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimamanda_Ngozi_Adichie]
- **Nwando Achebe** (Michigan State University) wrote:
  - *Farmers, Traders, Warriors, and Kings: Female Power and Authority in Northern Igboland, 1900–1960* (2005).
  - *The Female King of Colonial Nigeria: Ahebi Ugbabe* (2011).
  - *Holding the World Together: African Women in Changing Perspective* (2019, co-edited with Claire Robertson).
  - *Female Monarchs and Merchant Queens in Africa* (2020).

  [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nwando_Achebe]
- Bolanle Awe edited *Nigerian Women in Historical Perspective* (1992). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolanle_Awe]
- Cheryl Johnson-Odim and Nina Emma Mba wrote the standard biography of Ransome-Kuti. Johnson-Odim said that "no other Nigerian woman of her time ranked as such a national figure or had [such] international exposure and connections." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funmilayo_Ransome-Kuti]

## Debates
- **Did colonialism "invent" women's subordination?**
  - Amadiume (1987) argues that Igbo gender was flexible ("male daughters", "female husbands") and that colonialism and Christianity eroded women's power.
  - Oyěwùmí (1997) goes further: gender was not an organising category among the Oyo-Yoruba, where seniority was, and "women" as a subordinate category was a colonial imposition.
  - Bibi Bakare-Yusuf's review "'Yorubas don't do gender'" (*African Identities*, 2003) accepts the critique of Western universalism but charges Oyěwùmí with linguistic determinism and essentialism. She notes that translation risks run both ways.
  - Carole Boyce Davies calls the picture static and short on pre-colonial historical evidence.
  - Nwando Achebe says her own Igbo findings, and Amadiume's, differ from Oyěwùmí's.
  - Olalade Balogun notes that Yoruba proverbs belittle women.
  - Sefinatu Dogo argues that one ethnic group cannot stand for pre-colonial Africa.
  - Camilla Gibb charges Amadiume's matriarchy thesis with essentialism.

  Present this as a live debate led by African women scholars. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oy%C3%A8r%C3%B3nk%E1%BA%B9%CC%81_Oy%C4%9Bw%C3%B9m%C3%AD] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ifi_Amadiume] [source: https://feministafrica.net/2026/02/17/putting-gender-where-it-belongs-reimagining-social-organisation-and-categories-from-mozambique/]
- **Woman-to-woman marriage and sexuality.** Amadiume and Oyěwùmí argue that institutions such as "female husbands" should not be read as evidence of homosexuality. Achebe calls the exchange "contentious" and holds that Western scholars often misread indigenous institutions through modern categories. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oy%C3%A8r%C3%B3nk%E1%BA%B9%CC%81_Oy%C4%9Bw%C3%B9m%C3%AD]
- **Amina's historicity.** Sources disagree:
  - She is absent from the Zaria and Abuja regnal lists.
  - The *Kano Chronicle* puts her in the 15th century.
  - Oral tradition points to the early 16th century.
  - Abdullahi Smith dates her after 1576.
  - Much of her popular image (lovers beheaded, the walls, kola) comes from late oral tradition and Bello's 19th-century account.

  [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amina_of_Zazzau]
- **Nehanda.** D. N. Beach, "An Innocent Woman, Unjustly Accused? Charwe, Medium of the Nehanda Mhondoro Spirit, and the 1896–97 Central Shona Rising in Zimbabwe" (*History in Africa*, 1998), argued that her central role was overstated. In his account she was convicted by male witnesses and a male bench as a scapegoat, and Kaguvi was more central. Ruramisai Charumbira, "Nehanda and Gender Victimhood in the Central Mashonaland 1896–97 Rebellions: Revisiting the Evidence" (*History in Africa* 35, 2008, pp. 103–131), replied that Beach ignored women's and gender history. [source: https://cambridge.org/core/journals/history-in-africa/article/abs/an-innocent-woman-unjustly-accused-charwe-medium-of-the-nehanda-mhondoro-spirit-and-the-189697-central-shona-rising-in-zimbabwe/466CA1C5269F9CAB07A83F64E45FBAF1] [source: https://www.ufrgs.br/africanas/charwe-1862-1898/]
- **Njinga: hero or tyrant?**
  - Joseph Miller (1975) stressed her autocracy.
  - John Thornton places her in the making of an Atlantic Creole culture.
  - Heywood (2017) presents a complex politician who used culture, diplomacy, religion and war.
  - The Angolan nationalist memory makes her "Mother of Angola".

  [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nzinga_of_Ndongo_and_Matamba]
- **Women as slave traders and slave owners.** Njinga, Tinubu, the signares (Anne Pépin's family's *Maison des Esclaves*) and the Dahomey women soldiers all profited from or took part in slave trading. Robertson and Klein (1983) showed that women were the majority of the enslaved inside Africa. Women's history should not present elite women only as victims or heroines. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efunroye_Tinubu] [source: https://gwonline.unc.edu/node/3934]
- **Naming the 1929 events.** "Aba Riots", from the colonial records, versus "Women's War", from the Igbo and Ibibio names. Judith Van Allen, "Aba Riots or Igbo Women's War?" (*Ufahamu*, 1975). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_War]
- **Is "feminism" the right word for Africa?**
  - Ama Ata Aidoo, Buchi Emecheta, Ogundipe, Micere Mugo, Amina Mama and Obioma Nnaemeka voiced reservations or sought alternatives: stiwanism, nego-feminism, motherism, (African) womanism.
  - Adichie popularised the word itself.
  - McFadden and Mama argue for a radical, explicitly feminist politics.

  [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_feminism]
- **Rwanda's women's majority.** Commentators credit the 2003 quota and post-genocide reconstruction; female legislators shaped laws on inheritance, property and gender-based violence. Note that the quota operates in an authoritarian political system. That is commentary, not verified here, so attribute any such judgement. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_quota]

## Conflicts with published chapters
- **Women's War deaths** (*Interwar Period* and *British Empire* chapters say 55, or "about 55", women were killed). Wikipedia supports this, but other sources give 51 women and 1 man, list separate tolls (16–18 at Utu Etim Ekpo, 31 women and 1 man at Opobo), or, from oral accounts, about 100 at Aba alone. Suggest "more than fifty women (official counts; oral accounts suggest more)". Not a hard contradiction.
- **Women's War courts** (*Interwar* says "attacked sixteen Native Courts"). This matches Wikipedia's lead, but Wikipedia's body says ten were destroyed and others damaged. Keep "attacked sixteen".
- **Women's War date.** Wikipedia says 18 November 1929; History Today says 23 November and calls it contested. The published chapters give only "November 1929", so there is no conflict if the writer avoids a day.
- **Founding of the Bantu Women's League** (*Apartheid* chapter says 1918). The Wikipedia article on the ANC Women's League says 1913, while the Charlotte Maxeke article says 1918. The 1913 date appears to conflate the league with the Free State women's anti-pass campaign of 1912–13. Recommend keeping 1918 for the league and dating the anti-pass campaign 1913.
- **Sirleaf as "first female head of state"** (*Modern Africa* chapter: "Africa's first female head of state"). Wikipedia's wording is "first *elected* female head of state". Recommend adding "elected" for consistency; the published wording may be challengeable (earlier interim or appointed heads not verified here).
- **Dahomey's women soldiers** (*Kongo, Asante, and Dahomey* says they were "drawn from the palace women" and numbered "several thousand"). Wikipedia says recruitment was from palace women, captives and free women, and gives visitors' estimates of 1,000–6,000. This is compatible, but this chapter should give the range.
- **Rwanda genocide toll.** Wikipedia's "Women in government" and "Gender quota" articles say "800,000 Tutsis killed". The *Modern Africa* chapter gives 500,000–662,000 (scholarly estimates). Do not import the 800,000 figure.
- No contradictions were found for Njinga (baptism 1622, died 1663, Matamba, Dutch alliance 1641–48, peace 1656), Kimpa Vita (1704, burned 1706), Nana Asma'u (1793–1864, jajis from about 1830, four languages), Yaa Asantewaa (1900, Seychelles, died 1921), Amanirenas (about 25 BCE, treaty about 21–20 BCE, Meroë Head found 1910), the Women's March (20,000, 9 August 1956, the four leaders, the petition line) or Ngoyi (first woman on the ANC national executive).

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use
- "My bones will rise again", Nehanda's supposed last words. These are popular attribution only, with no contemporary record found. Richartz's eyewitness account describes her resisting, not prophesying. If mentioned, call it a later nationalist tradition.
- Yaa Asantewaa's speeches ("If you, the chiefs of Asante, are going to behave like cowards… exchange your loincloths for my undergarments", and the longer "Brave men of Ashanti" speech). These are traditions of uncertain provenance. Use only as "tradition holds that she said…".
- "1,000 British troops killed" in the 1900 war, and "army of 5,000" (Yaa Asantewaa article). The War of the Golden Stool article gives "1,070 casualties" with "citation needed".
- Amina's precise dates (1533–1610, reign 1576–1610), her army of "20,000 foot and 1,000 cavalry", and the beheaded lovers. Oral traditions only.
- That the Dahomey corps were "the only female army in modern history" (Wikipedia lead; unsupported).
- Rider Haggard's *She* being inspired by the second Rain Queen. Attributed in Wikipedia only; use "said to have inspired".
- Ogundipe-Leslie's "six mountains on the back of the African woman". Not confirmed in sources read.
- A precise Atlantic male:female ratio (for example "two to one"). Only "roughly a third female in the 18th century" was found, from secondary reproductions of Eltis data.
- Ransome-Kuti's retort to a district officer ("You may have been born, but you were not bred!"). Wikipedia presents it as "according to one story"; use only with that caveat.
- Whether Yaa Asantewaa was captured in 1900 or arrested on 3 March 1901, and whether she was shipped on 22 March or 22 May 1901. Sources conflict, so give "exiled in 1901".
- Earlier female heads of state before Sirleaf (for example interim leaders). Not checked.
