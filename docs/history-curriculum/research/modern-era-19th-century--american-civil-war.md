## 1. Slavery and the sectional crisis, 1820–1859
- On the eve of the war about four million people were enslaved; the 1860 census population of the eleven Confederate states was 9,103,332, of whom 3,521,110 were enslaved (CSA infobox). McPherson: slaves were 47% of the population of the first seven Confederate states but 24% in the upper South. [source: ACW; CSA]
- By 1860 cotton was almost 60% of US exports (nearly $200 million a year) and Southern plantations supplied 75% of the world's cotton. [source: KC]
- **Missouri Compromise (1820):** passed by Congress 3 March 1820, signed by Monroe 6 March 1820. It admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state and banned slavery in the rest of the Louisiana Purchase north of 36°30′. It began with James Tallmadge Jr.'s amendments of February 1819 restricting slavery in Missouri; the Senate was split 11 states to 11. Henry Clay steered it through the House. Repealed in effect by the Kansas–Nebraska Act (1854) and declared unconstitutional in *Dred Scott* (1857). [source: MC]
- **Nullification crisis (1832–33):** South Carolina's convention adopted an Ordinance of Nullification on 24 November 1832, declaring the tariffs of 1828 and 1832 void in the state; John C. Calhoun was the theory's leading proponent and broke with President Andrew Jackson. On 1 March 1833 Congress passed the Force Bill and the Compromise Tariff; South Carolina repealed nullification on 15 March 1833 but then "nullified" the Force Bill. By the 1850s slavery in the territories had replaced nullification as the central states'-rights conflict. [source: NULL]
- **Wilmot Proviso:** introduced by Congressman David Wilmot (Pennsylvania) on 8 August 1846 as a rider to a $2 million appropriations bill, to ban slavery in territory gained from Mexico. It passed the House on sectional lines and failed in the Senate (again in 1847). [source: WIL]
- The Mexican War (1846–48) cost roughly 13,000 American deaths, fewer than 2,000 in battle. [source: ACW]
- **Compromise of 1850:** five bills passed in September 1850, designed by Henry Clay and Stephen A. Douglas with President Millard Fillmore's support (after Zachary Taylor, who opposed it, died). California entered as a free state; New Mexico and Utah territories were organised under "popular sovereignty"; Texas gave up claims in exchange for federal assumption of its debt; the slave trade (not slavery) was banned in Washington, D.C.; and a stringent Fugitive Slave Act was passed. [source: C1850]
- **Fugitive Slave Act:** passed 18 September 1850. It required that escaped slaves be returned and that officials and citizens of free states cooperate; it heightened Northern fears of a "slave power conspiracy" and helped lead to the Republican Party. [source: FSA]
- **Kansas–Nebraska Act (1854):** drafted by Senator Stephen A. Douglas, signed by President Franklin Pierce; organised Kansas and Nebraska territories under popular sovereignty, effectively repealing the Missouri Compromise; it destroyed the Whig Party, whose Northern remnants formed the Republican Party. The first anti-Nebraska meeting at which "Republican" was suggested as the name of a new anti-slavery party was in a schoolhouse in Ripon, Wisconsin, on 20 March 1854. [source: KN]
- **Bleeding Kansas (1854–59):** rival pro- and anti-slavery capitals, constitutions and legislatures (Lecompton vs Topeka/Lawrence); Presidents Pierce and Buchanan backed the proslavery side; 56 political killings documented, possibly up to 200. Kansas was admitted as a free state effective 29 January 1861, after enough Southern senators had left Congress. [source: BK]
- **Dred Scott v. Sandford (March 1857):** 7–2 decision, opinion by Chief Justice Roger B. Taney: people of African descent "are not included, and were not intended to be included, under the word 'citizens' in the Constitution"; the Missouri Compromise ban was struck down as a violation of slaveholders' Fifth Amendment property rights. Widely considered the Court's worst decision; Charles Evans Hughes called it the Court's "greatest self-inflicted wound". Superseded by the 13th and 14th Amendments. [source: DRED]
- **Lincoln's "House Divided" speech** (1858, on accepting the Republican Senate nomination in Illinois), and his debates with Douglas in the 1858 Senate campaign gave him a national audience. [source: AL] (Exact date of the speech, 16 June 1858, not confirmed in the text read — say "1858".)
- **John Brown's raid, 16–18 October 1859, Harpers Ferry, Virginia:** party of 22 (including Black raiders such as Shields Green, John Anthony Copeland and Dangerfield Newby); ten killed in the raid, seven tried and executed, five escaped. Robert E. Lee commanded the operation that retook the armory with US Marines under Lt. Israel Greene. Harriet Tubman (prevented by illness) and Frederick Douglass (who thought the plan suicidal) had been invited and did not join; Shields Green, who was living in Douglass's house, chose to go: "I believe I will go with the old man". First national crisis spread by the telegraph. Brown's final note, 2 December 1859 (the day of his execution): see Quotations. [source: JB]

## 2. Lincoln's election and secession, 1860–61
- Election of 6 November 1860: four candidates — Abraham Lincoln (Republican), Stephen A. Douglas (Northern Democrat), John C. Breckinridge (Southern Democrat), John Bell (Constitutional Union). The Republican platform promised not to interfere with slavery where it existed but opposed its extension to the territories. Lincoln won 18 states, 180 electoral votes and 39.7% of the popular vote, all his electoral votes from free states. [source: E1860]
- Caveat for the writer (a common misconception): Lincoln received no votes in ten Southern states not because he was "removed from the ballot" but because the Republican Party was absent there and parties, not states, printed ballots. [source: E1860]
- South Carolina's convention voted unanimously to secede on 20 December 1860; Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana and Texas followed in January–February 1861. The Confederate States were formed on 4 February 1861 (ACW). After Fort Sumter and Lincoln's call for 75,000 militia (15 April), Virginia, Tennessee, Arkansas and North Carolina seceded; the capital moved to Richmond. Eleven states in all; Jefferson Davis was president and Alexander H. Stephens vice president. [source: ACW; ORD]
- Georgia seceded on 22 January 1861 by a vote of 208–89. Tennessee voters rejected a convention on 9 February 1861 and approved secession in a vote on 6 June 1861 (ORD; other dates are sometimes given, so "June 1861" is safest). [source: ORD]
- Secession followed the density of slavery: McPherson notes that Virginia counties with a slave population of only 2.5% opposed secession by three to one, while the rest of the state (36% enslaved) supported it by more than ten to one; East Tennessee (8% enslaved) rejected it by more than two to one. West Virginia was admitted on 20 June 1863. [source: ACW]
- Border states: Maryland, Delaware, Missouri and Kentucky stayed in the Union with slavery. In 1861 Lincoln wrote that "to lose Kentucky is nearly the same as to lose the whole game". [source: ACW]
- Compromise attempts failed: the Crittenden Compromise (December 1860, to restore the 36°30′ line) was rejected by Lincoln and the Republicans; the Corwin Amendment (protecting slavery where it existed) was judged insufficient by the South. [source: ACW]
- Lincoln's first inaugural (4 March 1861) called secession "legally void", promised no invasion and no interference with slavery where it existed, and closed with "the mystic chords of memory". [source: ACW]
- **Fort Sumter:** at 4:30 a.m. on 12 April 1861 Confederate forces opened fire (some 4,000 shells); the fort fell the next day. [source: ACW]

### The seceding states' own declarations of causes
- Four states — South Carolina, Mississippi, Georgia and Texas — issued separate declarations justifying secession, all blaming the movement to abolish slavery; among the ordinances themselves, those of Texas, Alabama and Virginia mentioned the slaveholding states' plight. [source: ACW; ORD]
- **South Carolina**, *Declaration of the Immediate Causes Which Induce and Justify the Secession of South Carolina from the Federal Union*, adopted 24 December 1860 (ordinance 20 December), drafted by a committee led by Christopher Memminger. It complains of "an increasing hostility on the part of the non-slaveholding States to the institution of slavery" and of Northern states' refusal to enforce the Fugitive Slave Act — i.e. it objected to Northern states' rights. It did not mention tariffs (except the three-fifths taxation clause). It omitted the Declaration of Independence's "all men are created equal" and "consent of the governed" (Harry V. Jaffa, *A New Birth of Freedom*, 2000). 15,000 copies were ordered printed. [source: SCD]
- **Mississippi** (January 1861): "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery—the greatest material interest of the world." [source: ACW; ORIG]
- **Texas** (*A Declaration of the Causes which Impel the State of Texas to Secede from the Federal Union*, February 1861): see Quotations. [source: TXCW]
- **Stephens's Cornerstone Speech**, Athenaeum, Savannah, Georgia, 21 March 1861, delivered extemporaneously by the Confederate vice president; called slavery "the immediate cause of the late rupture and present revolution". After the war (1865 prison diary; 1868 *A Constitutional View of the Late War between the States*) Stephens claimed the reporter's notes were "very imperfect"; one scholar finds the alleged misquotations "so numerous as to be highly unlikely". [source: CORN]

## 3. The war: strategy, campaigns, turning points
- Dates: 12 April 1861 – 26 May 1865 (Kirby Smith's Trans-Mississippi surrender; often cited as the effective end); legally ended 20 August 1866 by Johnson's proclamation. [source: ACW]
- Strength: Union about 2.2 million served (698,000 at peak); Confederacy 750,000–1,000,000 (360,000 at peak). The US Army had 16,000 men at the start. About 237 named battles; John Keegan: "one of the most ferocious wars ever fought". [source: ACW]
- Union immigrants: 177,000 German-born and 144,000 Irish-born soldiers; about 50,000 Canadians (around 2,500 Black). [source: ACW]
- **Union strategy:** Winfield Scott's Anaconda Plan (blockade to strangle the South), adopted only in part; Lincoln proclaimed a blockade of Southern ports in April 1861; by June 1861 warships were off the main ports and a year later nearly 300 ships were in service; the South could export less than 10% of its cotton. The Union navy reached 671 vessels by 1865. [source: ACW]
- **Confederate strategy:** a defensive war to outlast the Union's will; McPherson argues Confederate victory was possible but Northern advantages made it likely. In 1861 Southerners voluntarily embargoed cotton to force European intervention. [source: ACW]
- Industrial war: railroads, telegraph, ironclads (Hampton Roads, *Monitor* vs *Virginia*, 8–9 March 1862), repeating rifles, Gatling gun, observation balloons. [source: ACW]
- 1862 West: Forts Henry (6 February) and Donelson (11–16 February) to Grant; Shiloh (April 1862); New Orleans, the South's largest city, captured by Farragut's fleet in April 1862; Memphis fell 6 June 1862. [source: ACW]
- 1862 East: First Bull Run (July 1861) Confederate victory; McClellan's Peninsula campaign; Lee took command June 1862 (Seven Days); Second Bull Run. **Antietam** (Sharpsburg, Maryland), 17 September 1862: the bloodiest single day in American history, 22,726 dead, wounded or missing; a strategic Union victory that gave Lincoln the occasion for the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation and discouraged British and French recognition. Fredericksburg (13 December 1862), a Union defeat. [source: ANT; ACW]
- 1863: Chancellorsville (30 April – 6 May), Lee's "perfect battle", Stonewall Jackson mortally wounded by friendly fire. **Gettysburg**, 1–3 July 1863: over 50,000 casualties (Lee about 28,000, Meade about 23,000); Pickett's Charge on 3 July, the "high-water mark of the Confederacy". **Vicksburg**: siege 18 May – 4 July 1863, Pemberton surrendered to Grant on 4 July; Port Hudson fell 9 July; the Union controlled the Mississippi and the Confederacy was cut in two. Lincoln called Vicksburg "the key to the war". [source: ACW; VIC]
- **Gettysburg Address**, 19 November 1863, dedication of the Soldiers' National Cemetery. Several versions exist; the Bliss copy is usually treated as standard. [source: GA]
- 1864: Grant made general-in-chief; Overland Campaign (Wilderness, Spotsylvania, Cold Harbor); siege of Petersburg (over nine months). Atlanta fell 2 September 1864, securing Lincoln's re-election (he defeated McClellan). **Sherman's March to the Sea**, 15 November – 21 December 1864, Atlanta to Savannah, a "hard war" against infrastructure and property; it laid waste about 20% of Georgia's farms (ACW) and was followed by thousands of freed people; the Carolinas campaign followed. Sheridan devastated the Shenandoah Valley in autumn 1864. [source: ACW; SHER]
- 1865: Richmond fell 2–3 April, entered by the Union XXV Corps, composed of Black troops. Lee surrendered at Appomattox Court House on 9 April 1865 (McLean House). Lincoln was shot by John Wilkes Booth on 14 April and died the next morning; Andrew Johnson became president. Johnston surrendered nearly 90,000 men at Bennett Place, NC, on 26 April (the largest surrender). Jefferson Davis captured 10 May. Last land battle Palmito Ranch, Texas, 13 May. Stand Watie (Cherokee) was the last Confederate general to surrender, 23 June 1865. CSS *Shenandoah* surrendered at Liverpool on 6 November 1865. [source: ACW]
- **Juneteenth:** on 19 June 1865 Gen. Gordon Granger's General Order No. 3 brought the Proclamation into force in Texas. [source: ACW] (Already in Abolition chapter.)
- Prisoners: exchanges collapsed in 1863 when the Confederacy refused to exchange Black prisoners; about 56,000 of 409,000 POWs died in prison camps. [source: ACW]
- Lincoln suspended habeas corpus in Maryland; Chief Justice Taney (*Ex parte Merryman*) ruled only Congress could do so; Lincoln ignored the ruling. [source: ACW]
- Wartime Republican legislation: Morrill Tariff, land-grant colleges, Homestead Act, transcontinental railroad, National Bank Act, legal-tender paper money (1862), end of slavery in D.C., ban on slavery in the territories, a federal income tax (Revenue Act of 1861). [source: ACW]
- Southern Unionists: as many as 100,000 men from Confederate-controlled states served in the Union army or pro-Union guerrilla groups. [source: ACW]

## 4. The dead: casualty estimates (give as ranges)
- Traditional figure: about 620,000 (commonly 360,000 Union and 260,000 Confederate army deaths), from 19th-century counts. These counts missed deaths after discharge and many Confederate deaths. [source: ACW]
- Francis Amasa Walker (1870 census superintendent) had estimated a minimum of 850,000 military deaths (500,000 Union, 350,000 Confederate). [source: ACW]
- **Hacker (2011):** J. David Hacker, "A Census-Based Count of the Civil War Dead", *Civil War History* 57:4 (December 2011), pp. 307–348, used a 1% sample of the 1850–1880 censuses: most probable total 752,000 military deaths, upper bound about 851,000 — about 20% above 620,000; rounded to "750,000" and widely adopted. A secondary source gives his range as 650,000–850,000 (the lower bound is not confirmed in Hacker's own text). Military deaths only. [source: ACW; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/465917/summary ; search summary of https://www.historynet.com/interview-j-david-hacker-awful-tally-goes-higher/]
- **Barceló, Jensen, Peisakhin and Zhai (2024):** "New Estimates of US Civil War mortality from full-census records", *PNAS* 121(48), published November 2024, using full-count census records: about 698,000 soldier deaths. Excess mortality among military-age native-born white men was about 13% in the Confederacy vs 5% in the Union; male mortality as high as 19% in Louisiana and about 16.6–16.7% in Georgia and South Carolina. The paper gives a confidence interval for its native-born-white-male estimate (about 462,000–530,000 excess deaths) but none was found for the 698,000 total. [source: ACW; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11621511/ (via search summary)]
- Writer's framing: "between about 620,000 and 850,000 soldiers died; recent census-based estimates put it at roughly 700,000–750,000" — this covers the seed's "750,000" while showing the 2024 revision. ACW's own lead now says "an estimated 700,000 soldiers dead".
- Two-thirds of soldier deaths were from disease. About 60,000 soldiers lost limbs. 1860 census: 8% of white men aged 13–43 died (6% North, 18% South) (Vinovskis). McPherson: the war's "cost in American lives was as great as in all of the nation's other wars combined through Vietnam". [source: ACW]
- Union Army dead 359,528: 110,070 killed in action or died of wounds; 199,790 disease; 24,866 in Confederate prisons. [source: ACW]
- Freed and fleeing enslaved people: no reliable count; Jim Downs states that tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands died of disease, starvation or exposure, which would push the total above one million (ACW infobox: "60,000 identified enslaved people" plus "tens of thousands" unidentified). No count of civilian deaths was kept. [source: ACW]

## 5. Enslaved people's self-emancipation, contrabands, and the Emancipation Proclamation
- On 24 May 1861 Frank Baker, Shepard Mallory and James Townsend, who had been forced to build a Confederate battery at Sewell's Point, crossed Hampton Roads at night to Fort Monroe; Gen. Benjamin Butler refused to return them, treating them as "contraband of war" (the "Fort Monroe Doctrine"). In August 1861 the army and Congress (First Confiscation Act) adopted the policy. Contrabands at Fort Monroe were paid $8 a month (men) and $4 (women). [source: CON]
- Self-emancipated people set up camps near Union forces; by the war's end more than 250 contraband camps operated, including the Freedmen's Colony of Roanoke Island (about 3,500 people). A camp at Natchez held 6,000. Historians describe them as "simultaneously humanitarian crises and incubators for a new relationship between African Americans and the U.S. government". [source: CON]
- Scale: historians commonly estimate that at least about 500,000 enslaved people (roughly one in eight) fled to Union lines; Chandra Manning cites about 400,000 to nearly half a million. Records are incomplete. [source: search summary of https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/refugees-during-the-civil-war/ ; https://hnn.us/article/186012] (Use "hundreds of thousands, perhaps half a million".)
- Robert Smalls (1839–1915), enslaved pilot of the Confederate transport *Planter* in Charleston harbour, sailed it out to the Union blockade on 13 May 1862 with seven of the eight enslaved crewmen and their families; later a Reconstruction congressman. His example helped convince Lincoln to accept Black soldiers. [source: SMA]
- Lincoln reversed early emancipation orders by Frémont, Hunter and Cameron; urged gradual compensated emancipation with voluntary colonisation (only D.C. got compensated emancipation); met five Black representatives on 14 August 1862 and urged colonisation. His public letter to Horace Greeley, 22 August 1862 (see Quotations). Seward advised waiting for a victory so it would not seem "our last shriek on the retreat". [source: ACW; EP]
- Preliminary Proclamation 22 September 1862 (five days after Antietam); final Proclamation 1 January 1863, applying to states in rebellion; justified "upon military necessity"; it declared Black men could be "received into the armed service of the United States". It did not cover the nearly 500,000 enslaved in the border states or Tennessee and Union-held areas. Estimates of the number freed immediately are uncertain (EP: around 25,000 to 75,000); freedom spread as Union armies advanced, ultimately for about 3.5 million. [source: EP; ACW]
- The Confederacy answered with the "Twenty Negro Law" (Second Conscription Act, 1862, 19 days after the preliminary Proclamation): one white man exempted for every twenty slaves on a plantation, out of fear of slave rebellion; it fed the cry "rich man's war, poor man's fight". [source: TWN]
- Lincoln's letter to Albert G. Hodges (1864): "If slavery is not wrong, nothing is wrong.... I claim not to have controlled events, but confess plainly that events have controlled me." [source: ACW]
- Haiti: the US recognised Haiti on 5 June 1862, after Southerners left Congress. [source: DIP]
- 13th Amendment ratified 6 December 1865 (detail in Abolition notes). Missouri and Maryland abolished slavery themselves; Kentucky and Delaware only by the amendment. [source: ACW; 13A]

## 6. Black soldiers and sailors
- 175 USCT regiments by 1865, about one-tenth of the army (Kelly Mezurek); "approximately 180,000" in the USCT, of whom over 36,000 died (about 20%, a rate about 35% higher than white Union troops). ACW: about 190,000 volunteered; USCT were 10% of Union dead, 15% of disease deaths. 21% of USCT died vs 15% of US Volunteers and 9% of white Regulars. [source: USCT; ACW]
- Medal of Honor: USCT article says 16 in its lead and lists "Eighteen" in a later section — say "more than a dozen", e.g. Sgt. William Harvey Carney (54th Massachusetts, Fort Wagner). [source: USCT]
- Unequal pay: Black soldiers were offered $10 a month with $3 withheld for clothing ($7 net), vs $13 for white privates; they and supporters lobbied and won equal pay. [source: 54; USCT]
- 54th Massachusetts: recruitment from February 1863 (Gov. John Andrew; Col. Robert Gould Shaw); Douglass recruited and two of his sons enlisted. It was the second Black regiment raised in the North after the 1st Kansas Colored. At the Second Battle of Fort Wagner, 18 July 1863, the 54th lost 20 killed, 125 wounded, 102 missing (~40%); Shaw was killed. The Union lost 1,515 overall vs 174 Confederate. Film *Glory* (1989). [source: 54; ACW]
- 1st Kansas Colored Infantry fought at Honey Springs, Indian Territory (17 July 1863) alongside Union Indian Home Guard regiments, against a mainly Native Confederate force. [source: HS]
- Fort Pillow, Tennessee, 12 April 1864: Confederates under Nathan Bedford Forrest massacred Union soldiers, many of them USCT, attempting to surrender; the garrison was about 600, roughly half Black. [source: PIL]
- The Confederacy did not enlist Black soldiers on any scale "for fear of undermining the legitimacy of slavery". (A last-ditch March 1865 law exists but was not confirmed in the text read — see NOT VERIFIED.) [source: ACW]
- Black troops of the XXV Corps entered Richmond, April 1865. [source: ACW]

## 7. Women, home fronts, the draft and riots
- Elizabeth D. Leonard: 500–1,000 women enlisted disguised as men (e.g. Jennie Hodgers as "Albert D. J. Cashier"). Mary Edwards Walker remains the only woman awarded the Medal of Honor. [source: ACW]
- US Sanitary Commission: private relief agency created by federal legislation on 18 June 1861. Dorothea Dix (superintendent of women nurses) placed women in some 350 hospitals; Clara Barton (1821–1912) became famous for wartime nursing and later founded the American Red Cross. [source: USSC; GEN]
- Women's Loyal National League (1863, Susan B. Anthony and Elizabeth Cady Stanton), the first national women's political organisation in the US, collected nearly 400,000 signatures for abolition, the largest petition drive in the nation's history to that time. [source: WS]
- After the war the 14th Amendment's second section protected only male voters, and the women's movement split over the 15th Amendment. [source: 15A]
- Confederate women ran plantations and dealt with "increasingly rebellious slaves"; enslaved women and children became the core plantation workforce as enslaved men were impressed for army labour. [source: ACW]
- Southern bread riots, March–April 1863, mostly by women: Petersburg (1 April), Richmond (2 April, the largest). Richmond grew from 38,000 (1860) to over 100,000 (1863). [source: BREAD]
- Conscription: Confederate draft April 1862 (ages 18–35), the first in US history; the Union's Enrollment Act, signed 3 March 1863, allowed a substitute or a $300 commutation fee. Of 168,649 men procured through the Union draft, 117,986 were substitutes. About 120,000 Northerners evaded the draft and 280,000 deserted; at least 100,000 Southerners deserted. The Confederate Congress abolished substitution in December 1863 amid the cry "rich man's war and a poor man's fight". [source: ENR; ACW; CSA]
- New York City draft riots, 13–16 July 1863: began at the second draft drawing, turned into a race riot by mainly Irish-American rioters against Black New Yorkers; the Colored Orphan Asylum (Fifth Avenue; sheltered about 230 children, all escorted out) was burned; troops came from Gettysburg. Official death toll 119 or 120; other estimates much higher; one historian counts 18 known killed by rioters, 11 of them Black. Manhattan's Black population fell below 11,000 by 1865. Toby Joyce calls it a "civil war" within the Irish community (much of the police and many soldiers were also Irish). [source: DR]

## 8. Native nations
- **Dakota War of 1862 (Minnesota):** began 18 August 1862 after crop failure, late annuities and the agent's withholding of food; trader Andrew Myrick's reported reply was "So far as I am concerned, if they are hungry let them eat grass or their own dung" (attribute as "reportedly"; Myrick was among the first killed). Lasted about five weeks; Little Crow defeated at Wood Lake, 23 September 1862. Several hundred settlers killed (see the conflict above). About 2,000 Dakota were interned at Fort Snelling, including at least 1,658 non-combatants. A military commission sentenced 303 men to death; Lincoln approved 39; 38 were hanged at Mankato on 26 December 1862, the largest one-day mass execution in US history. In 1863 Congress abolished the eastern Dakota and Ho-Chunk reservations in Minnesota and they were exiled. [source: DAK]
- **Bear River Massacre**, 29 January 1863 (present-day Idaho): Col. Patrick Edward Connor's California Volunteers killed an estimated 250–493 Shoshone; 21 soldiers died. [source: BEAR] (Optional.)
- **Long Walk of the Navajo**, August 1864 – end of 1866: some 53 forced marches; about 10,000 Navajo and 500 Mescalero Apache confined at Bosque Redondo; up to 3,500 died; return under the 1868 treaty. [source: NAV]
- **Sand Creek massacre**, 29 November 1864, southeastern Colorado Territory: about 675 men of the Third Colorado Cavalry under Col. John Chivington attacked the Cheyenne and Arapaho camp of Black Kettle, who had come in under a peace parley and flew a US flag with a white flag beneath it. Estimates range from 70 to over 600 killed; most sources say about 150, about two-thirds women and children. [source: SAND]
- **Indian Territory and the Five Tribes (Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Creek, Seminole):** most tribal leaders aligned with the Confederacy; Albert Pike negotiated treaties from July 1861 (Creek) to October 1861 (Cherokee); at least 7,860 Native men from the Territory served the Confederacy. Cherokee Principal Chief John Ross first insisted on neutrality. The Creek leader Opothleyahola led Union-loyal Creeks north and was driven into Kansas in a snowstorm after an attack on 26 December 1861. The Union raised Indian Home Guard regiments. [source: IT; CHER]
- The Cherokee had enslaved people of African descent and laws excluding Black people from citizenship. On 18 February 1863 the (Union-aligned) Cherokee National Council passed a resolution to emancipate all slaves in the Nation. Stand Watie, a slaveholder and the Treaty Party's survivor, became a Confederate brigadier general and the last Confederate general to surrender (23 June 1865). Postwar treaties (1866) required the tribes to abolish slavery and adopt their freedpeople; the US negotiated with the Union-aligned Cherokee and recognised Ross. [source: CHER; Abolition notes]
- Bridge to later chapters: the wartime Homestead Act and Pacific railroad laws (above) opened the West to settlers and set up the postwar Plains wars.

## 9. The war in the world
- **King Cotton:** Senator James Henry Hammond's 1858 boast (see Quotations). In early 1861 planters spontaneously withheld cotton; in 1862 the Confederate Congress authorised burning cotton threatened by Union advance. [source: KC]
- Why it failed: Britain had large stocks (enough for over a year, DIP); Europe's 1860–62 crop failures made Northern grain vital ("King Corn was more powerful than King Cotton"; US grain rose from a quarter to almost half of British imports); Britain turned to India, Egypt and Brazil. [source: ACW; DIP; KC]
- **British neutrality:** a proclamation of neutrality on 13 May 1861 recognised Confederate belligerency (allowing use of ports) but Britain never recognised the Confederacy, signed no treaty and exchanged no ambassadors. No foreign government ever recognised the Confederacy. Brazil, too, recognised belligerency and has been called an unofficial Confederate ally. Hawaii declared neutrality on 26 August 1861. [source: UK; DIP; ACW]
- **Trent Affair** (November–December 1861): the US Navy seized Confederate envoys James Mason and John Slidell from the British mail ship *Trent*; war loomed until Lincoln released them; Prince Albert, near death, toned down the British note. [source: ACW; KC]
- In autumn 1862 the British cabinet considered mediation; Chancellor of the Exchequer W. E. Gladstone, whose family fortune came from West Indian slavery, supported the Confederacy. Antietam and the Emancipation Proclamation ended the moment; Palmerston rejected Napoleon III's proposals of joint mediation. [source: UK; DIP; ACW]
- British arms and ships: the London Armoury Company and others supplied the Confederacy; blockade runners worked via Bermuda, Nassau and Cuba; British-built raiders, chiefly CSS *Alabama* (more than sixty prizes, sunk off France in 1864). After the Treaty of Washington, a tribunal at Geneva (1872) awarded the US $15.5 million for "direct damages"; it set a precedent for international arbitration. [source: ACW; UK; ALA]
- **Lancashire Cotton Famine (1861–65):** In 1860 the region had 2,650 mills employing 440,000 people (56% female). The famine came from pre-war overproduction plus the blockade; raw cotton rose from about 6½d a pound (1861) to 27½d (1864). Indian Surat cotton was short-fibred, ran looms at about 40% of former output and slashed piece-rate wages. Relief: Mansion House Fund (est. 16 May 1862; £473,749 collected April 1862–April 1863) and Manchester Central Committee (20 June 1862); sewing schools (60,000–70,000 women in March 1863); Stalybridge riot over relief tickets, 20 March 1863; public works under the 1864 Act ("Cotton Famine Road"). [source: LCF]
- **The Manchester workers' letter:** on 31 December 1862 a meeting at the Free Trade Hall, Manchester, resolved to support the Union against slavery and wrote to Lincoln; Lincoln replied on 19 January 1863 praising "sublime Christian heroism". A monument in Lincoln Square, Brazennose Street, quotes both. The US sent food ships (first the *George Griswold*). [source: LCF] (Mayor Abel Haywood's address is quoted in EP.)
- **Caveat/debate:** support was not uniform: many mill owners and workers saw the blockade as the problem and Confederate flags flew in many cotton towns (LCF); Mary Ellison, *Support for Secession: Lancashire and the American Civil War* (1972), argued for Lancashire sympathy for the South; R. J. M. Blackett (*Divided Hearts*) finds great variation, with the working class and nonconformists inclined to the Union and Confederate support among conservatives and high-church Anglicans. UK article: "most Britons did not express an opinion". [source: LCF; DIP; UK]
- **France:** Napoleon III stayed officially neutral and never recognised the Confederacy; he would not act without Britain; the French public "showed more interest in Mexico". [source: DIP]
- **Mexico and Maximilian:** Napoleon III used the Civil War as an opportunity; the French army landed in January 1862 (with Britain and Spain initially, ostensibly over debts) and was defeated at Puebla on 5 May 1862 (Cinco de Mayo). Maximilian of Habsburg accepted the crown at Miramar on 10 April 1864 after a bogus referendum. He sought an alliance with the Confederacy, but France blocked it. The US never stopped recognising Juárez's republic; after April 1865 it sent material aid and demobilised Union veterans went south; in January 1866 African American troops from Clarksville, Texas raided Bagdad and fired on French vessels (the US disciplined them). French troops withdrew from 1866; Maximilian, Miramón and Mejía were shot on the Cerro de las Campanas, Querétaro, on 19 June 1867. [source: MEX; MAX]
- **Russia:** supported the Union as a counterweight to Britain; in 1863 its Baltic and Pacific fleets wintered in New York and San Francisco. Russia freed its serfs in 1861 (cross-refer Abolition chapter). [source: ACW; DIP]
- **Egypt:** the cotton boom led Egypt to quadruple its cotton production and exports by 1865 (study of the 1861–65 boom in the *Journal of Economic History*, via search summary); EGC says Egypt ceased nearly all non-cotton agriculture between 1861 and 1864. Isma'il Pasha came to power in 1863, at the height of the boom. The 1865 price collapse hit Egypt hard. [source: EGC; search summary of https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/E2856D1AF5D5A75DD41204C20A67C752/S002205072400038Xa.pdf ; https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/how-american-civil-war-built-egypts-vaunted-cotton-industry-and-changed-country-forever-180959967/]
- **India:** Britain pushed cultivators in India, Egypt and elsewhere into export cotton, often at the expense of food crops; when the war ended much of that cotton was no longer wanted, which LCF links to impoverishment and later famines (treat as a contested claim). The Bombay merchant Premchand Roychand ("Cotton King") made great profits in the 1861–65 boom and later lost most of his fortune (Back Bay reclamation). [source: LCF; PRC]
- Lancashire's cotton prices and Chinese demand: the textile trade with China lost two-thirds of its value from 1861 to 1862. [source: LCF]
- Don H. Doyle argues the Union victory energised popular democratic forces worldwide; a Confederate victory would have meant "a new birth of slavery". The UK article links the Union cause to the British Reform Act of 1867. [source: ACW; UK]

## 10. Reconstruction (1865–77) and its violent overthrow
- **Freedmen's Bureau** (Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands): established 3 March 1865; operated until November 1872. [source: FB]
- **"Forty acres and a mule":** Sherman's Special Field Orders No. 15, 16 January 1865, set aside about 400,000 acres along the coast of South Carolina, Georgia and Florida in plots of up to 40 acres for about 18,000 families; Andrew Johnson reversed it and almost all land was restored to former owners. Black landownership nonetheless peaked at about 15 million acres in 1910. [source: 40A]
- **Black Codes** (1865–66): vagrancy laws let authorities arrest freedpeople and force them into labour; the start of convict leasing ("slavery by another name", Douglas Blackmon, 2008). [source: BC]
- Massacres in 1866: **Memphis**, 1–3 May 1866: 46 Black and 2 white people killed; every Black church and school burned (91 homes, 4 churches, 8 schools); New Orleans massacre, July 1866. They strengthened the Radicals' case for the 14th Amendment and Reconstruction Acts. [source: MEM]
- **14th Amendment**, adopted 9 July 1868: birthright citizenship (overturning *Dred Scott*), privileges or immunities, due process, equal protection; Section 2 replaced the three-fifths clause; Section 4 refused compensation to slaveholders. Ex-Confederate states had to ratify it to regain representation. [source: 14A]
- **Reconstruction Acts**, 2 March 1867 – 11 March 1868, passed over Johnson's vetoes: military rule and conditions for readmission ("Radical" or "Congressional" Reconstruction). Tennessee, readmitted 24 July 1866, was exempt. Johnson was impeached but not convicted. [source: RA; ACW]
- **15th Amendment**, ratified 3 February 1870 (certified 30 March 1870): no denial of the vote "on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude"; no Democrat in Congress voted for it. [source: 15A]
- Black political power: more than 1,500 African Americans held public office in the South during Reconstruction; within three or four years of 1867 "about 15 percent of the officeholders in the South were Black—a larger proportion than in 1990" (Foner, quoted in REC). Hiram Revels (Mississippi, 1870) was the first African American in either house of Congress. Reconstruction conventions created tax-funded public schools (segregated except in New Orleans). In 1868 Grant was aided by some 500,000 Black votes. [source: REC; REV]
- **Ku Klux Klan:** founded in Pulaski, Tennessee, on 24 December 1865 by six former Confederate officers; first Klan 1865–1872. **Enforcement Acts** 1870–71 (under Grant) made it a federal crime to conspire against voting, office-holding, jury service and equal protection. [source: KKK; ENF]
- **Colfax massacre**, Easter Sunday 13 April 1873, Grant Parish, Louisiana: between 62 and 153 Black men killed while surrendering; three whites died; Foner calls it the worst instance of racial violence in Reconstruction. ***United States v. Cruikshank*** (1876) overturned the federal convictions, ruling the 14th Amendment bound state governments, not individuals — crippling Enforcement Act prosecutions. The *Slaughter-House Cases* (1873) read the Privileges or Immunities Clause narrowly. [source: COL; CRU; 14A]
- **Mississippi Plan** (1874–75): Democrats' organised violence and intimidation to retake the state "peaceably if we can, forcibly if we must"; the 1890 Mississippi constitution later disfranchised most Black voters. [source: MSP]
- **Hamburg massacre**, South Carolina, July 1876: Red Shirt rifle clubs killed six Black men (one white man died); 94 white men indicted, none prosecuted; an estimated 100 Black people killed at Ellenton; Wade Hampton III "redeemed" the state. [source: HAM]
- **Compromise of 1877:** an informal deal settling the disputed Hayes–Tilden election; Hayes withdrew the last federal troops from the South; Redeemer Democrats took control. No written evidence of the deal exists; C. Vann Woodward (*Reunion and Reaction*, 1951) named it, and its details are debated. By 1905 most African Americans in the South were effectively disfranchised. [source: C1877]
- Southern per-capita income fell to less than 40% of the North's and stayed low into the 20th century. [source: ACW]

## 11. Memory and historiography
- **The Lost Cause:** a pseudo-historical myth that the Confederate cause was just, heroic and not about slavery; named by Edward A. Pollard's *The Lost Cause: A New Southern History of the War of the Confederates* (1866), which pressed states' rights and Northern aggression. It peaked around 1900 (monuments, textbooks; United Daughters of the Confederacy, Sons of Confederate Veterans) and again during the civil rights era. Alan T. Nolan: a rationalisation and cover-up, "a caricature of the truth". Gary Gallagher shows how Douglas Southall Freeman's four-volume *R. E. Lee* (1934) "cemented" Jubal Early's heroic Lee. Hollywood: *The Birth of a Nation* (1915), *Gone with the Wind* (1939). Monuments on Richmond's Monument Avenue, including Lee's (removed 2021), came down in 2020–21; Florida passed a law in 2024 barring removals. [source: LC; ACW]
- The seceding states' own declarations and Stephens's speech are the standard rebuttal (ACW: the Lost Cause view is "disproven by historical evidence, including the seceding states' own secession documents"). [source: ACW]
- **The Dunning School** (William A. Dunning, Columbia, e.g. *Reconstruction, Political and Economic, 1865–1877*): portrayed Reconstruction as a failure of corrupt "negro rule", resting on an assumption of "negro incapacity"; Black people barely figured as actors. [source: DUN]
- **W. E. B. Du Bois, *Black Reconstruction in America*** (1935) challenged Dunning, stressing Black agency; its chapter "The General Strike" argues that enslaved people's withdrawal of labour and flight to Union lines was a general strike of four million; he framed Reconstruction as a promise of workers' democracy. [source: BRA]
- **Eric Foner**, *Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution, 1863–1877* (1988; Bancroft Prize 1989), affirmed Du Bois's emphasis, calling *Black Reconstruction* "a monumental study" that "anticipated the findings of modern scholarship". Foner (born 1943, Columbia) also wrote *The Fiery Trial: Abraham Lincoln and American Slavery* (2010; Pulitzer, Lincoln and Bancroft prizes). [source: BRA; FON]
- **David W. Blight**, *Race and Reunion* (2001): sectional reconciliation was achieved at the expense of the memory of slavery; the "reconciliationist" memory beat the "emancipationist" one. [source: BLI; LC]
- **Steven Hahn**, *A Nation Under Our Feet* (2003; Pulitzer 2004): Black political struggles in the rural South from slavery to the Great Migration. [source: HAHN]

## Debates
- **Causes:** 21st-century historians overwhelmingly agree slavery was central, at least for the South; they disagree on why the North refused to allow secession (Union, American nationalism). The Lost Cause claim of "states' rights" is undercut by the declarations, which attack Northern states' resistance to the Fugitive Slave Act. The Beards' economic interpretation (*The Rise of American Civilization*, 1927) downplayed slavery; abandoned by historians by the 1950s (ACW links it to the Lost Cause, a claim the article itself flags as needing better sourcing). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Carolina_Declaration_of_Secession]
- **Who freed the slaves?** Ira Berlin ("How the Slaves Freed Themselves", 1992; *Who Freed the Slaves?*, 1997) emphasises self-emancipation while calling the Proclamation's place "secure"; James McPherson ("Who Freed the Slaves?", 1995) keeps Lincoln as the primary agent and warns that challenging the "myth" that Lincoln freed the slaves risks creating another. Du Bois's "general strike" is the classic version of the self-emancipation case. No reputable scholar denies a role to either. [source: search summary of https://cblevins.github.io/sp19-cw-rec/downloads/McPherson_WhoFreedTheSlaves.pdf and https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/cialit1e/cialit1e_ch07_87.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Reconstruction_in_America]
- **Could the Confederacy have won?** McPherson: contingency, victory possible; Shelby Foote: "the North fought that war with one hand behind its back"; E. Merton Coulter's minority view that Southerners "did not will hard enough and long enough" is rejected by most (McPherson, Gallagher). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War]
- **How many died?** 620,000 (traditional) vs Hacker's ~752,000 (2011) vs ~698,000 (Barceló et al., 2024); Downs on freedpeople's deaths. [source: section 4]
- **Reconstruction:** Dunning School (failure, "negro rule") vs Du Bois (1935) and Foner (1988) (an unfinished revolution, overthrown by violence); a further school blames the failure mainly on the inability to suppress white violence and Northern abandonment of Southern Republicans (Etcheson 2009, as cited in REC). The Compromise of 1877 itself is debated: Woodward's "bargain" vs critics who doubt any deal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunning_School ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_era ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compromise_of_1877]
- **Memory:** Blight's reconciliation-at-the-expense-of-emancipation thesis; Nolan and Gallagher on the Lost Cause; Bruce Catton's view that the legend aided national reconciliation.
