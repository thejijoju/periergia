## 1. Kyivan Rus' and the contested inheritance
- Kievan (Kyivan) Rus' was the first East Slavic state and later "an amalgam of principalities", from the late 9th to the mid-13th century, ruled by the Rurik dynasty, multi-ethnic (East Slavic, Norse, Finnic). In its own time it was called simply "Rus'"; the compound name "Kievan Rus'" is first found in a 1582 Polish text and was popularised by 19th-century Russian imperial historiography. [source: KR]
- At its greatest extent (mid-11th century) it stretched from the White Sea to the Black Sea and from the Carpathians to the Taman Peninsula; Kiev was its capital c. 882–1240. [source: KR]
- Per the *Primary Chronicle* (a 12th-century text), the Varangian prince Oleg took Kiev; Sviatoslav I fought the Khazars (965–969); Vladimir the Great converted c. 988; Yaroslav the Wise built St Sophia in Kiev, and his sons issued the first law code, the *Russkaya Pravda*. [source: KR]
- Scholarly estimates of Kiev's population around 1200: 36,000–50,000 (Paris then c. 50,000; London c. 30,000). Yaroslav's daughters became queens of Hungary, France and Norway. [source: KR]
- Decline: fragmentation through the 12th century; in March 1169 Andrei Bogolyubsky of Vladimir sacked Kiev; the Mongol siege of Kiev in 1240 "is generally understood as the end of Kievan Rus'". [source: KR] (Mongol chapter already says 1240.)
- Successors: Galicia–Volhynia in the south-west (Roman Mstislavych united them in 1199; his son Daniel accepted a crown from the papacy); in the north-east Vladimir-Suzdal, later Muscovy; Novgorod. The Grand Duchy of Lithuania, "largely Ruthenized", absorbed much of present Belarus and Ukraine and drew on Rus' legal and cultural traditions; from 1398 to 1569 its full name was "Grand Duchy of Lithuania, Ruthenia and Samogitia". [source: KR]
- Lithuania took Polotsk, Volhynia, Chernihiv and Kyiv after the Battle of Blue Waters (1362/63); Ruthenian (an ancestor of modern Ukrainian and Belarusian) was a chancery language of the Grand Duchy. [source: HU; HB; UKR]
- "The modern nations of Belarus, Russia, and Ukraine all claim Kievan Rus' as their cultural ancestor, with Belarus and Russia deriving their names from it." [source: KR]
- Muscovy's claim: in 1328 Ivan I persuaded Metropolitan Theognost of Kiev to settle in Moscow; Ivan III in 1493 took the title "Sovereign of All Russia"; by the mid-17th century the tsar's title was "Tsar of All Great, Little, and White Rus'". [source: ARN]
- The Normanist controversy (were the Rus' Scandinavians or Slavs?) is "to some extent ... related to the foundation myths of modern states in the region"; scholarly attention has moved to how quickly a Norse elite assimilated. [source: KR]
- The terms "Little" and "Great" Rus' came from Byzantine church usage (first in church documents in 1335) distinguishing the metropolitanates of Halych (closer, "Little") and Moscow (further, "Great"); they were geographic, not hierarchical, in origin. [source: ARN]

## 2. Lithuania, Poland, the Cossacks and the Hetmanate
- Union of Lublin, 1 July 1569: created the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth; most of present Ukraine passed to the Polish Crown, while Belarusian lands remained in the Grand Duchy of Lithuania. [source: UL; HU; HB]
- Union of Brest (1595–96): Orthodox bishops in the Commonwealth's Ruthenian lands accepted papal authority while keeping the Eastern rite — the origin of today's Ukrainian and Belarusian Greek Catholic Churches. [source: UB]
- Cossacks: Ruthenian peasants fleeing serfdom, organised around the Zaporozhian Sich on the lower Dnieper (16th–18th centuries); the Sich's population c. 1650 was about 100,000. [source: HU; ZS]
- The Khmelnytsky Uprising (1648–1657; in Ukraine the "National Liberation War"), led by Hetman Bohdan Khmelnytsky and initially allied with the Crimean Khanate, created the Cossack Hetmanate; it was accompanied by mass atrocities against Poles, Jews and Catholic and Uniate clergy. [source: KU]
- Jewish deaths 1648–49 (estimates revised downward over time): Jewish chroniclers and early-20th-century historians gave 100,000–500,000 (Dubnow, 1916, cited both); 1960s–80s historians commonly used 100,000; more recent estimates are 40,000–100,000, or lower — one 1996 estimate 15,000–30,000 killed or taken captive and 300 communities destroyed; a 2014 estimate 18,000–20,000 dead in 1648–49, including 3,000–6,000 at Nemyriv. Remembered in Jewish tradition as *Gzeyres Takh Vetat* (the decrees of 1648–49). [source: KU]
- Crimean Tatars allied with Khmelnytsky took captives for the slave markets; the alliance terms formally allowed them to enslave only Poles and Jews. [source: KU]
- The Hetmanate existed 1648–1764 (its administrative system to 1781); recognised in the Commonwealth by the Treaty of Zboriv, 18 August 1649. Its official name was the "Zaporozhian Host"; "Hetmanate" is a late-19th-century historians' term. Internally the land was called "Ukraine" and the people the "Ruthenian nation"; in dealings with Moscow "Little Russia". [source: CH]
- Treaty of Andrusovo (1667), concluded without Cossack representation, divided the Hetmanate along the Dnieper between Poland and Russia. [source: CH; PA]
- Mazepa: in 1708 Hetman Ivan Mazepa sided with Charles XII of Sweden; Menshikov's troops sacked the hetman's capital Baturyn on 13 November 1708, killing up to 15,000 people; defeat at Poltava (1709). [source: CH; HU]
- Pylyp Orlyk, elected hetman in exile in April 1710, issued the "Pacts and Constitutions" of the Zaporozhian Host (the Constitution of Pylyp Orlyk), which calls the land both "Little Russia" and "Ukraine". [source: CH]
- Catherine II abolished the hetmancy in 1764 (Little Russia Governorate under Rumyantsev, to 1781); Russian forces destroyed the Zaporozhian Sich in June 1775. [source: CH; ZS; HU]
- After the partitions of Poland (1772, 1793, 1795) the far west (Galicia) went to Austria and the rest to Russia. [source: HU]

## 3. Pereiaslav 1654 — event and rival readings
- January 1654 (council on 18 January N.S.) at Pereiaslav: Cossack leaders swore allegiance to Tsar Alexis; the tsar's envoy Vasily Buturlin refused to swear an oath on the tsar's behalf, which left it uncertain whether the agreement was legally binding. The detailed "March Articles" were finalised in Moscow in April 1654 (March, Old Style), granting broad autonomy. Russian reckoning: 127,000 people, including 64,000 Cossacks, took the oath. [source: PA]
- It precipitated the Russo-Polish War of 1654–67; the 1686 Perpetual Peace confirmed Russian sovereignty over Left-Bank Ukraine and Kyiv. [source: PA]
- Legal-historical readings vary: incorporation (Odinets, Rosenfeld, Miakotin); autonomy within the tsardom (Nolde); real union (Diakonov and others); personal union (Sergeevich, Lashchenko); vassalage (Korkunov, Pokrovsky, Hrushevsky); protectorate (Doroshenko, Krypiakevych); military alliance. [source: PA]
- Soviet historiography shifted: early Soviet historians treated it in class terms (a feudal pact) and Khmelnytsky as a feudal lord; from the 1930s under Stalin it became the "unification of two brotherly peoples", and Khmelnytsky a patriot. [source: PA]
- 1954 tercentenary of "Ukraine's reunification with Russia": nearly 44 million roubles for festivities; the CPSU "Theses" asserted the common Rus' descent of Russians, Ukrainians and Belarusians; the transfer of Crimea to Ukraine was presented as part of the commemoration. [source: PA; TCU]
- Ukrainian nationalist readings view Pereiaslav negatively, as a failed opportunity for independence (WP characterisation). [source: PA]
- An early statement of common Rus' identity, the *Kievan Synopsis*, is attributed to Innocent Gizel, archimandrite of the Kyiv Caves monastery, and is "seen as the earliest historical record of a common Rus' ethnic identity". (Date: see NOT VERIFIED.) [source: ARN]

## 4. Empire, "Little Russia" and the national revival (19th century)
- The "All-Russian nation" / "triune Russian people" (Great, Little and White Russians = Russians, Ukrainians, Belarusians) was the imperial Russian ideology; the concept gained political weight in the late 18th century to legitimise claims to the partitioned Commonwealth's eastern lands. In the 19th century the official and dominant view treated Ukrainian and Belarusian as dialects of Russian. [source: ARN]
- Identities were often layered rather than exclusive: "for many residents of Dnieper Ukraine it was perfectly normal to be both a Little Russian and Russian"; Gogol is cited as an example. In modern Ukrainian "Little Russian" has become derogatory. [source: ARN]
- Taras Shevchenko (9 March 1814 – 10 March 1861), born a serf, poet of the *Kobzar* (regarded as the foundation of modern Ukrainian literature); exiled to Central Asia for his association with the Brotherhood of Saints Cyril and Methodius; wrote poetry in Ukrainian, prose in Russian. [source: TS]
- The Brotherhood of Sts Cyril and Methodius in Kyiv was broken up in March 1847 and its founder Mykola Kostomarov arrested/exiled. Kostomarov's essay "Two Russian Nationalities" (in *Osnova*, 1861–62) argued for two Rus' peoples and languages. [source: EU; ARN]
- Valuev Circular, 18 (30) July 1863, issued by Interior Minister Pyotr Valuev: banned religious, educational and primer books in "Little Russian", allowing belles-lettres; it quoted the Kiev Censorship Committee that "a separate Little Russian language never existed, does not exist, and shall not exist, and the tongue used by commoners is nothing but Russian corrupted by the influence of Poland" (wording as in WP VC; matches the published *Imperial Russia* chapter). It blamed "the Poles' political interests" and "separatist intentions of some of the Little Russians". [source: VC]
- Ems Ukaz, signed by Alexander II at Bad Ems on 18 May (30 May N.S.) 1876: banned Ukrainian-language printing except reprints of old documents, imports of Ukrainian publications, and plays, lectures and song lyrics in Ukrainian. No Ukrainian books were printed in 1877; the Kyiv Hromada fell from c. 100 members to 14; Drahomanov emigrated. The ban was eased by 1905 but never formally revoked; enforcement was incomplete and Galician Ukrainian papers were smuggled in. [source: EU]
- Galicia, under Austria, became the free centre of the movement: Hrushevsky took the new chair of Ukrainian history at Lviv in 1894 (on Antonovych's recommendation), led the Shevchenko Scientific Society from 1897, and co-founded the National Democratic Party in 1899. [source: MH; EU]
- Russian liberal Peter Struve championed the all-Russian nation, fearing a separate Ukrainian identity meant provincialism; Pushkin's line "will not all the Slavic streams merge into the Russian sea?" is cited as the pan-Slavist idea. [source: ARN; EU]

## 5. Hrushevsky and Ukrainian national historiography
- Mykhailo Hrushevsky (29 September 1866, Chełm – 24 November 1934, Kislovodsk): "often considered the country's greatest modern historian"; author of the ten-volume *History of Ukraine-Rus'* (in Ukrainian, prehistory to the 1660s); head of the Central Rada 1917–18. [source: MH]
- His scheme: continuity from the steppe cultures through Kyivan Rus' to the Cossacks; Galicia–Volhynia (not Vladimir-Suzdal) as the legitimate heir of Kyivan Rus'; emphasis on the "popular masses". Critics (e.g. Harvard's Omeljan Pritsak) argued his neglect of political elites disrupted the national narrative. [source: MH]
- His 1904 essay "The Traditional Scheme of 'Russian' History and the Problem of a Rational Organization of the History of the East Slavs", published in the Russian Imperial Academy of Sciences' *Sbornik statei po slavianovedeniiu*, argued that Ukrainian history is distinct from Russian in origin and development; his periodisation was largely adopted by Ukrainian historians (including in Soviet Ukraine until 1929). English translation 1965 (Winnipeg). [source: web search summarising Encyclopedia of Ukraine / CIUS pages — https://www.encyclopediaofukraine.com/pages%5CH%5CR%5CHrushevskyMykhailo.htm ; https://www.ualberta.ca/canadian-institute-of-ukrainian-studies/centres-and-programs/jacyk-centre/hrushevsky-translation-project/who-was-mykhailo-hrushevsky.html] (Title as given in search results; exact English punctuation should be checked by an editor.)
- Fate: returned to Soviet Ukraine in 1924 (context: Ukrainization); exiled to Moscow in 1931, arrested as part of a supposed "Ukrainian nationalist centre"; died in 1934 under GPU surveillance. His portrait is on the 50-hryvnia note. [source: MH]

## 6. Revolution and statehood, 1917–21
- The Central Rada was formed in March 1917; Hrushevsky elected its head on 17 March 1917. First Universal (24 June 1917): autonomy within Russia. Third Universal (20 November 1917): proclaimed the Ukrainian People's Republic (UPR) within a federal Russia, abolished the death penalty, and granted minorities national-personal autonomy. Fourth Universal (22 January 1918): independence. [source: UPR; MH]
- A rival Soviet Ukrainian government in Kharkiv, backed by Soviet Russia, fought the UPR; Battle of Kruty, 29 January 1918. The UPR signed a separate Treaty of Brest-Litovsk with the Central Powers on 9 February 1918. [source: UPR]
- German-backed coup of 29 April 1918: Pavlo Skoropadsky proclaimed Hetman (the "Ukrainian State"); overthrown by the Directory in December 1918. [source: UPR]
- Unification Act with the West Ukrainian People's Republic (Galicia), 22 January 1919. [source: UPR]
- Treaty of Warsaw, 22 April 1920: Petliura allied with Piłsudski's Poland (border on the Zbruch), condemned by Hrushevsky and Vynnychenko; Polish–Ukrainian forces briefly took Kyiv (7 May 1920). Peace of Riga, 18 March 1921, partitioned Ukrainian lands between Poland and Soviet Ukraine; the UPR continued in exile; Petliura was assassinated in Paris in 1926 by Sholem Schwarzbard. [source: UPR]
- Pogroms: per WP UPR article, an estimated 60,000 Jews were killed in pogroms during 1917–21 by different sides; Nahum Gergel counted over 1,000 pogroms, most in 1919. Units of the UPR army committed major pogroms (Proskuriv, Berdychiv, Zhytomyr, February 1919), against Petliura's orders per this article; Denikin's Volunteer Army committed many (1,500 Jews murdered at Fastiv, September 1919); independent otamans (Hryhoriv, Zelenyi) too. (The published *Soviet Union* chapter gives the wider range 35,000–250,000 — consistent; use that range.) [source: UPR]
- Cheka records list 268 peasant uprisings in Ukraine 1917–32. [source: UPR]
- The UPR government in exile transferred its symbols of authority to the president of independent Ukraine in 1992 (article: "following the Declaration of Independence"). [source: UPR]
- Plokhy on Putin's claim that Lenin created Ukraine: "Of course, Lenin did not create Ukraine. In 1918, he started a war against an independent Ukrainian state and then replaced it with a puppet state called the Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic. ... Lenin was the creator of modern Russia, not Ukraine". [source: PUT]

## 7. Soviet Ukrainization, the Executed Renaissance and the Holodomor
- Ukrainization began with the Sovnarkom decree of 27 July 1923 (part of korenizatsiia). By 1929 over 97% of high-school students in the republic were taught in Ukrainian; illiteracy fell from 47% (1926) to 8% (1934). Ukrainian share of Kharkiv (then the capital) rose from 38% to 50% (1923–33), of Kyiv from 27.1% to 42.1%. By 1931, 66 of 88 theatres were Ukrainian (12 Yiddish, 9 Russian); 373 of 426 newspapers were Ukrainian. [source: UKR]
- Mykola Khvylovy, in the 1925–28 literary discussion, urged Ukrainian literature to turn away from Moscow (towards Europe), drawing Stalin's complaint to Kaganovich; Khvylovy killed himself in 1933; Skrypnyk, commissar of education, also killed himself in 1933. [source: UKR; ER]
- "Executed Renaissance" (term coined in 1959 by Jerzy Giedroyc for Yuriy Lavrinenko's anthology): the 1930 show trial of the "Union for the Liberation of Ukraine" convicted 45 intellectuals; up to 30,000 more were arrested, deported or executed over the decade; almost 300 representatives of the Ukrainian cultural renaissance were shot at Sandarmokh (Karelia), 27 October–4 November 1937. [source: ER]
- The 14 December 1932 grain-procurement decree effectively ended Ukrainization in the RSFSR's Ukrainian-populated regions (Kuban). [source: UKR]
- Holodomor ("death by hunger"): man-made famine in Soviet Ukraine, 1932–33, part of the wider Soviet famine of 1930–33. Estimates: a November 2003 joint statement to the UN by 25 countries said 7–10 million; "more recent scholarship" 3.5–5 million (WP lead; = published chapters). Body of WP gives a lower spread too: Kulchytsky (2002) ~3.2 million (3–3.5 million); Vallin et al. (2002) 2.582 million direct deaths in 1933 (about 2.2 million from famine after adjustment); Wheatcroft minimum 1.8 million registered excess deaths, 2.8–4.8 million with unregistered; Snyder (2010) ~3.3 million; Russian historian Shubin 1–2 million direct deaths. See "Conflicts" below. [source: HOL]
- Snyder: "President Viktor Yushchenko does his country a grave disservice by claiming ten million deaths, thus exaggerating the number of Ukrainians killed by a factor of three; but it is true that the famine in Ukraine of 1932–1933 was a result of purposeful political decisions, and killed about three million people." Yushchenko told the US Congress the Holodomor "took away 20 million lives". [source: HOL]
- Snyder thinks it meets the Genocide Convention's criteria but prefers "mass killing" for public clarity. [source: HOL]
- Babyonyshev (as "Sergei Maksudov", 1981) estimated victims in the Ukrainian SSR as 81.3% ethnic Ukrainians, 4.5% Russians, 1.4% Jews, 1.1% Poles. Gorbachev recalled nearly half his Stavropol village, Privolnoye, starving in 1933. [source: HOL]
- Memory: the word *holodomor* became standard in the diaspora by the 1980s; first used publicly in Ukraine by Ivan Drach in 1986; discussion banned in the USSR until glasnost. Since 2006 recognised as genocide by Ukraine and 33 other UN member states, the European Parliament and 35 US states; the Russian Duma (2008) condemned the Soviet regime "that has neglected the lives of people for the achievement of economic and political goals". [source: HOL] (Genocide debate: already in *Soviet Union* chapter — cross-refer.)

## 8. The Second World War: bloodlands, OUN/UPA, Volhynia, Jews
- Snyder's *Bloodlands* (2010): Stalin's and Hitler's regimes killed an estimated 14 million non-combatants in 1933–45 in a region of Poland, Belarus, Ukraine, the Baltic states and western Russia. Snyder: Germans deliberately killed about 11 million (12+ million with foreseeable deaths); Soviets ~6 million (9 million). Critics: Richard Evans ("Snyder's book is of no use", for lack of causal argument), Omer Bartov ("presents no new evidence and makes no new arguments"). (In a 2016 interview Snyder spoke of "13 million civilians".) [source: BL; SNY]
- Ukrainian losses: civilian losses in Ukraine during the war and occupation "estimated at seven million, including over a million Jews"; about 1.7 million (16%) of Soviet soldiers killed were ethnic Ukrainians. Erlikman's table: Ukrainian SSR 6.85 million dead (16.3% of 1940 population); Byelorussian SSR 2.29 million (25.3%). History of Belarus: Belarus lost "a quarter of its pre-war population", about 9,200 villages destroyed; Minsk and Vitsebsk lost over 80% of buildings. [source: MHU; WWC; HB] (Estimates vary — give as "about 7 million" / "a quarter".)
- OUN split in 1940 into OUN-M (Melnyk) and the more radical OUN-B (Bandera). On 30 June 1941 in German-occupied Lviv, the OUN-B's Yaroslav Stetsko proclaimed a Ukrainian state that pledged cooperation with Nazi Germany; the Germans rejected it and Bandera was held in Sachsenhausen from January 1942 to September 1944. [source: BAN]
- Snyder characterises Bandera's goal as "a one-party fascist dictatorship free of national minorities". Bandera (1909–1959) was assassinated by KGB agent Bohdan Stashynsky in Munich on 15 October 1959. [source: BAN]
- Lviv pogroms, 30 June–2 July and 25–29 July 1941: perpetrated by Ukrainian nationalists (OUN militia), Einsatzgruppen and local residents; thousands of Jews killed. Triggered by the discovery of NKVD prison massacre victims (NKVD records ~9,000 prisoners killed in Ukrainian SSR; historians 10,000–40,000 in Western Ukraine), blamed on "Jewish Bolsheviks". The extent of Ukrainian nationalist responsibility is "widely debated". No evidence that Bandera personally took part. [source: LVP; BAN]
- UPA founded by the OUN-B on 14 October 1942; fought Germans, Soviets and Poles; formally disbanded 1949, with units active into the 1950s. [source: UPA]
- Volhynia and Eastern Galicia, 1943–45: UPA ethnic cleansing of Poles, peaking July–August 1943; Volhynia commander Dmytro Klyachkivsky ordered in June 1943 the "general physical liquidation of the entire Polish population". Deaths: ~50,000–60,000 Poles in Volhynia; 20,000–25,000 in Eastern Galicia (OUN-B/UPA, 1943–46); Poland's IPN estimates up to 100,000 overall; Ferguson 60,000–80,000; Rossoliński-Liebe 70,000–100,000; Himka "perhaps a hundred thousand". Ukrainian victims of Polish retaliation: 2,000–3,000 in Volhynia (Rudling); 10,000–20,000 overall (Rossoliński-Liebe); 10,000–30,000 for 1943–47 (Wolczuk). [source: VOL]
- Classification: Poland's Sejm called them "crimes bearing the hallmarks of genocide" (2008) and "ethnic cleansing with the hallmarks of genocide" (2013); on 22 July 2016 it made 11 July a national day of remembrance for victims of "genocide"; Ukraine and some non-Polish historians call it ethnic cleansing. Reconciliation gestures: Poroshenko at the Warsaw monument (2016); Duda and Zelenskyy together in Lutsk, July 2023. [source: VOL]
- Memory politics in Ukraine: Yushchenko made Bandera a Hero of Ukraine on 22 January 2010 (internationally condemned; annulled in 2011 under Yanukovych); 2015 law on "fighters for the independence of Ukraine" (signed 15 May 2015) included the UPA; surviving UPA members got veteran status in March 2019; polling after 2022 showed rising favourable views of Bandera. In Soviet and Russian usage "Banderite" became a slur for Ukrainian nationalists. [source: BAN; UPA]
- The Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church was outlawed in 1946 (absorbed into Russian Orthodoxy) and allowed to function again officially in December 1989. [source: UGCC]

## 9. Crimea and the Crimean Tatars
- The Crimean Khanate ruled 1441–1783; annexed by Russia in 1783. Crimean Tatars were 98% of Crimea's population in 1783, 34.1% in 1897, 19.4% in 1939. Emigration waves: about 300,000 left for the Ottoman Empire in 1784–90; after the Crimean War, at least 500,000 (possibly up to 900,000) Muslims left Russia in 1855–66, at least a third from Crimea. [source: DCT]
- Deportation (*Sürgünlik*), 18–20 May 1944, ordered by Stalin and supervised by Beria: at least 191,044 Crimean Tatars sent mainly to the Uzbek SSR, including party members and Red Army soldiers. Pretext: collaboration — but about 20,000 Crimean Tatars served with the Axis versus about 40,000 in the Red Army. Nearly 8,000 died in transit. Exile deaths: estimates 18% (Ediev, 34,300) to the NKVD's 27%; Crimean Tatar activists' figure 46% (KGB published 22%); Williams 40,000–44,000; Travis 40,000–80,000. Also deported: 9,620 Armenians, 12,420 Bulgarians, 15,040 Greeks. [source: DCT]
- Return banned until perestroika; ~260,000 returned; on 14 November 1989 the USSR Supreme Soviet declared the deportations a crime; by 2004 Crimean Tatars were ~12% of Crimea. Recognised as genocide (2015–25) by Ukraine, Lithuania, Latvia, Canada, Poland, Estonia, Czech Republic and Netherlands. [source: DCT]
- 19 February 1954: the Presidium of the USSR Supreme Soviet transferred Crimea from the RSFSR to the Ukrainian SSR (announced in *Pravda* 27 February), presented as marking the Pereiaslav tercentenary; attributed to Khrushchev though signed by Voroshilov. In January 1992 Russia's Supreme Soviet questioned its constitutionality. [source: TCU]
- In 1991 Crimea voted 54.19% for Ukrainian independence (37% of electorate); Sevastopol 57.07% (36%). [source: REF91]
- 2014: Crimean Tatars "mostly boycotted" the March 2014 referendum; after annexation the Mejlis leadership was barred from entering Crimea and FSB raids on Tatar homes were reported. [source: CR14]

## 10. 1991 and divergent paths
- Ukraine's parliament declared independence on 24 August 1991; referendum 1 December 1991: turnout 84.18%; 28,804,071 (92.3%) voted yes; majorities in all 27 regions (by votes cast) — Ternopil 98.67%, Lviv 97.46%, Donetsk 83.90%, Luhansk 83.86%, Crimea 54.19%; about 55% of ethnic Russians in Ukraine voted yes (WP). Kravchuk elected president the same day; Yeltsin recognised independence on 2 December. Solzhenitsyn had advocated partitioning Ukraine along voting lines. [source: REF91]
- Budapest Memorandum, 5 December 1994 (Russia, US, UK; France and China separately): security assurances in return for Ukraine, Belarus and Kazakhstan joining the NPT as non-nuclear states; Ukraine had held the world's third-largest nuclear stockpile; weapons removed 1993–96. WP: Russia violated it in 2014 and 2022. [source: BM]
- Kuchma, *Ukraine Is Not Russia* (Moscow, Vremya, 2003), argued Russians misunderstood Ukraine as "historically an inseparable part of Russia"; reissued in November 2023 as "Ukraine is not Russia. Twenty years later". Authorship (ghost-written?) disputed. [source: web search — https://sites.utu.fi/bre/ukraine-is-not-russia-vs-one-nation-political-prose-as-the-prelude-to-the-russo-ukrainian-war/ ; https://en.socportal.info/en/news/ukraine-is-not-russia-twenty-years-later-kuchma-republished-a-famous-book]
- Belarus (brief): BNR proclaimed under German occupation on 25 March 1918 (Third Constituent Charter), its Rada going into exile after the Red Army took Minsk on 10 December 1918; its white-red-white flag and Pahonia arms were the Republic of Belarus's symbols 1991–95. Belarusian national intelligentsia were largely executed in the Great Purge, many buried at Kurapaty. Lukashenko elected 1994; the 14 May 1995 referendum (official turnout 64.8%) approved Russian as an equal state language, new Soviet-style symbols and economic integration with Russia — the OSCE PA said it violated international standards; 1996 referendum curtailed parliament. Disputed 2020 election: Tsikhanouskaya claimed 60–70%; UN cited 450+ documented cases of torture/ill-treatment of detainees. In 2022 Russia invaded Ukraine partly from Belarusian territory. [source: HB; BNR; BR95; BP20; RUW22]
- Orthodox Church of Ukraine granted autocephaly by the Ecumenical Patriarch on 6 January 2019. [source: OCU]
- Decommunization laws signed 15 May 2015 (removal of Soviet monuments and names; 22 cities and 44 villages to be renamed; communist and Nazi symbols banned). [source: DEC]
- Zelenskyy won the 21 April 2019 run-off with 73.23%. [source: MHU]

## 11. Orange Revolution, Maidan, 2014 and 2022
- Orange Revolution, late November 2004–January 2005: protests against a rigged 21 November 2004 run-off (Yushchenko v. Yanukovych); Yushchenko had suffered dioxin poisoning in September 2004; the Supreme Court ordered a re-run on 26 December 2004 — Yushchenko ~52%, Yanukovych 44%; Yushchenko inaugurated 23 January 2005. Yanukovych won the 2010 election, judged fair. [source: OR]
- Euromaidan began 21 November 2013 after Yanukovych declined to sign the EU Association Agreement under Russian pressure; violent dispersal on 30 November widened it; called "the largest democratic mass movement in Europe since 1989". [source: EM; RD]
- Revolution of Dignity: 108 protesters and 13 police officers killed in January–February 2014, most protesters on 18–20 February (48 killed on Instytutska Street on 20 February); the dead are remembered as the "Heavenly Hundred". 21 February agreement; Yanukovych fled that night; on 22 February parliament voted 328–0 to remove him. [source: RD]
- Rival narratives (attribute): Russian state narrative of a US-organised "coup" by "neo-fascists" (Putin, 18 March 2014: "Nationalists, neo-Nazis, Russophobes and anti-Semites"); WP: small radical groups such as Right Sector were a minority whose role was "greatly exaggerated in Russian state media and in some Western media"; Nuland's $5 billion (democracy programmes since 1991) remark was cited by Moscow. [source: RD]
- Crimea 2014: soldiers without insignia seized sites from 27 February (Putin later admitted they were Russian troops); official referendum result 95.5% for joining Russia on 83% turnout; Evgeny Bobrov (Russian presidential human rights council) suggested 50–60% on 30–50% turnout; annexed 18 March 2014. UNGA Resolution 68/262 (27 March 2014) affirmed Ukraine's territorial integrity: 100 for, 11 against, 58 abstentions. [source: CR14; UN68]
- Donbas: Russian-backed separatists proclaimed the Donetsk and Luhansk "People's Republics" (referendums 11 May 2014); Russia covertly sent troops (Ilovaisk, August 2014); Minsk I (September 2014) and Minsk II failed. Casualties: use published *Modern Conflicts* figures. [source: MINSK; MHU]
- 2022: on 24 February Putin announced a "special military operation" to "demilitarise and denazify" Ukraine; WP: he "claimed without evidence" Ukraine's government were neo-Nazis committing genocide. Invasion fronts from Belarus, Crimea and Donbas. Russia annexed four partly occupied oblasts on 30 September 2022 (generally unrecognised). ICC warrant for Putin, 17 March 2023, over deportation of children. Population 41 million; ~8 million internally displaced; 6–7 million fled. [source: RUW22; RUW] (Cross-refer to *Modern Conflicts* for casualties.)

## 12. The war over history
- Putin, *On the Historical Unity of Russians and Ukrainians* (Russian: *Ob istoricheskom yedinstve russkikh i ukraintsev*), published on Kremlin.ru on 12 July 2021 (also in German in *Osteuropa* 71/7); argues Russians, Ukrainians and Belarusians are one people (the "triune Russian nation"); questions Ukraine's 1954–1991 borders; calls modern Ukraine an "anti-Russia project"; compares "the formation of an ethnically pure Ukrainian state, aggressive towards Russia" to weapons of mass destruction against Russians; blames "followers of Bandera". Reported to be required reading in the Russian military. [source: PUT]
- Reactions: Zelenskyy (13 July 2021) compared Putin's "brotherhood" to Cain and Abel; the Institute of History of Ukraine said it reproduced Russian imperial views; Carnegie: a "historical, political, and security predicate for invading"; Anders Åslund: "one step short of a declaration of war"; Snyder: "bad history" (2022 Yale lecture); a May 2022 report by 35 legal and genocide experts cited it as groundwork for incitement to genocide by "denying the existence of the Ukrainian group". [source: PUT]
- Putin, 21 February 2022: "modern Ukraine was wholly and fully created by Bolshevik, communist Russia"; Ukraine "has never had its own authentic statehood" and is "an integral part of our own history, culture, [and] spiritual space". [source: PUT; ARN]
- Medvedev (during the war) wrote that "Ukraine is NOT a country, but artificially collected territories" and Ukrainian "is NOT a language" but a "mongrel dialect". [source: ARN]
- RIA Novosti, 3 April 2022, "What Russia Should Do with Ukraine" (Timofei Sergeitsev — author name not checked) called for "denazification" of the nation as a whole; a pre-written RIA article (26 Feb 2022, Petr Akopov) celebrated "gathering the Russian world" of "Great Russians, Belarusians and Little Russians". [source: PUT]
- Poll: April 2022 (Rating group): 91% of Ukrainians (outside occupied areas) did not support the thesis that "Russians and Ukrainians are one people". [source: ARN]
- Plokhy (2006) proposed to "denationalize" Kievan Rus' — separating it "as a multi-ethnic state from the national histories of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus"; Halperin (2010): this relegates rival claims "entirely to the realm of ideology". [source: KR]
- Alexei Miller (Russian historian), *The Ukrainian Question: The Russian Empire and Nationalism in the Nineteenth Century* (CEU Press, 2003): framed 19th-century Russian and Ukrainian nation-building as competing, mutually exclusive projects (a chapter on the "All-Russian Nation" project). WP says that since 2014 Miller has supported the Kremlin line, and that his 2024 Academy of Sciences anthology *The Ukrainian Question and Identity Politics* is seen as the official Russian view (WP article is short and polemical — attribute carefully). [source: MIL; web search https://www.aup.nl/en/book/9789639241602]
- Hosking's thesis (*Russia: People and Empire*): in Russia "state-building had impeded nation-building"; the needs of empire prevented the creation of a Russian nation. Peter Gatrell called it polemical "and perhaps one-sided". [source: web search — https://archives.history.ac.uk/history-in-focus/Empire/reviews/gatrellreply.html]
- Mark von Hagen, "Does Ukraine Have a History?", *Slavic Review* 54:3 (Fall 1995), 658–73 — argued Ukrainian history lacked standing in Western academia, prompting a forum; Georgiy Kasianov revisited it in 2024 as "Does Ukraine Have Histories?". [source: web search — https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/F0654D3B064DD3EA0CA7037B1955AC60]
- Realist critic: John Mearsheimer, "Why the Ukraine Crisis Is the West's Fault: The Liberal Delusions That Provoked Putin", *Foreign Affairs* 93:5 (Sept/Oct 2014) — blamed NATO and EU enlargement and Western backing of the Orange Revolution; Alexander Motyl and others rebutted (e.g. no more than a fifth of Ukrainians wanted NATO membership before 2014). [source: web search — https://europeanleadershipnetwork.org/commentary/the-ukraine-crisis-according-to-john-j-mearsheimer-impeccable-logic-wrong-facts/]
- Critics of Ukrainian nationalist memory: Grzegorz Rossoliński-Liebe (*Stepan Bandera: The Life and Afterlife of a Ukrainian Nationalist: Fascism, Genocide, and Cult*, 2014) and Per Anders Rudling; John-Paul Himka. [source: BAN; VOL; HOL citations]

## Debates
- **Whose Rus'?** Russian imperial/Soviet "common cradle" (Kyivan Rus' as cradle of one Russian people, codified in the 1937 textbooks under Zhdanov; 1954 "Theses") vs. Hrushevsky's separate Ukrainian line via Galicia–Volhynia vs. Plokhy's "denationalised", multi-ethnic Rus'. Putin's 2021 essay restates the triune nation. [source: ARN; MH; KR; PUT]
- **Pereiaslav 1654:** incorporation, autonomy, real or personal union, vassalage, protectorate or military alliance; Soviet "reunification" from the 1930s; Ukrainian national reading as a lost chance. [source: PA]
- **Was 19th-century Ukrainian identity "made" by repression or by Austria?** Imperial officials blamed a "Polish intrigue" and Austria/Germany; Ukrainian historians stress Kostomarov, Shevchenko and the Ems ban's failure (Shevelov); Miller frames it as competing nation-building projects. [source: EU; ARN; MIL]
- **Holodomor numbers and genocide** — cross-refer; add that memory politics raised figures (Yushchenko's 10–20 million, Harper's 10 million) which Snyder and Wheatcroft criticised. [source: HOL]
- **OUN/UPA:** heroes of anti-Soviet and anti-Nazi resistance (Ukrainian state commemoration, 2015/2019 laws) vs. perpetrators of ethnic cleansing of Poles and participants in anti-Jewish violence (Polish Sejm, Rossoliński-Liebe, Rudling, Himka); Poland says genocide, Ukraine ethnic cleansing; Russian propaganda's "Banderite/Nazi" label for all Ukrainian patriotism. [source: VOL; UPA; BAN; RD]
- **Maidan: revolution or coup?** Russian state narrative vs. WP-cited evidence (parliamentary vote, Yanukovych's flight, free 2014 elections); Karatnycky's argument that Moscow triggered Yanukovych's exit. [source: RD]
- **Causes of the war:** Russian imperial/identity explanation (Snyder, Plokhy) vs. realist NATO-expansion explanation (Mearsheimer, 2014); rebuttals by Motyl. [source: SNY; web search above]
- **Russia as empire vs nation:** Hosking — state-building impeded nation-building. [source: web search above]
- **Bloodlands as a frame:** Snyder vs. Evans, Bartov. [source: SNY]

## Conflicts with published chapters
1. **Holodomor death toll.** Published chapters (*Soviet Union*, *Interwar*) say "3.5–5 million according to recent scholarship". This matches the WP lead, but the WP body shows many recent demographic estimates *below* 3.5 million: Kulchytsky 3–3.5 million (~3.2 m), Snyder ~3.3 million (and "about three million" elsewhere), Vallin et al. ~2.6 million direct 1933 deaths, Wheatcroft 1.8 million registered/2.8–4.8 million with unregistered, Shubin 1–2 million. Not a flat contradiction, but "3.5–5 million" understates the lower end. Recommendation: in this chapter say "most scholarly estimates fall between about 3 and 5 million" or "about 3.5–5 million (some demographers put it lower, around 2.5–3.5 million)" and keep consistent with the *Soviet Union* chapter by attributing. Also note WP's *Modern history of Ukraine* article gives "6 to 8 million" and "up to 10 million" — do not use those.
2. **Pogrom deaths 1917–21.** *Soviet Union* gives 35,000–250,000 (all of Ukraine, all sides); WP UPR article gives "an estimated 60 thousand". Compatible — use the published range, mention 1,000+ pogroms.
3. **Bloodlands figure.** *Holocaust* says 14 million (= book's own figure, WP). Snyder said "13 million civilians" in a 2016 interview — use 14 million.
4. **Valuev quotation.** *Imperial Russia* matches WP VC exactly. WP's Ems Ukaz article paraphrases it as "the Ukrainian language never existed, does not exist, and shall never exist" — use the VC/published wording.
5. **Crimean Tatar deportation**, **1991 referendum (92.3%)**, **Crimea annexed 18 March 2014**, **Kiev sacked 1240**, **Vladimir's conversion 988** — all consistent with published chapters. No other contradictions found.
