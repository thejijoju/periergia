# Verified facts — Pacific Voyagers: the Settlement of Oceania (researched 2026-11-11)

Node: `history/regional-histories/oceania-and-the-pacific/pacific-voyagers-the-settlement-of-oceania`

Notes for the writer. Dates for Pacific settlement are being revised all the time, and specialists disagree. Give every settlement date as a range and say who proposed it. Do not give a single year for the first settlement of any island group unless these notes do. Wikipedia articles disagree with each other on several dates (sweet potato, Hawai'i, Rapa Nui, and the older "Polynesians" timeline). These conflicts are listed under each topic. Where they occur, prefer the revised short chronology (Wilmshurst et al. 2011) and say that it is the current majority view, not a settled fact.

## Near Oceania: the deep past (Sahul, New Guinea, Bismarcks, Solomons)
- During the Pleistocene, sea level fell at times to about 130 m below today's, which joined New Guinea, Tasmania and the Aru Islands to Australia in a continent called **Sahul**. The islands of Wallacea (Sulawesi, the Lesser Sundas, Halmahera and others) "always remained an island world", so reaching Sahul required crossing open water. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_Oceania_and_Remote_Oceania]
- The Bismarck Archipelago and the Solomons were never joined to Sahul by dry land, because deep-water trenches separate them from the Australian continental shelf. Reaching them also required voyaging. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_Oceania_and_Remote_Oceania]
- The human colonisation of Sahul and Near Oceania "was most likely effected during the interval between 60,000 and 40,000 years ago", although some researchers propose earlier dates. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_Oceania_and_Remote_Oceania]
- The terms **Near Oceania** and **Remote Oceania** were proposed by Andrew Pawley and Roger Green in 1973 and elaborated by Green in 1991. They were meant to replace Dumont d'Urville's 19th-century categories of Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia. Near Oceania = New Guinea, the Bismarck Archipelago and the Solomon Islands (except the Santa Cruz Islands). Remote Oceania = the rest of Melanesia (Santa Cruz, Vanuatu, Fiji, New Caledonia), plus Polynesia and Micronesia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_Oceania_and_Remote_Oceania]
- The first inhabitants of the Bismarck Archipelago arrived "around 30,000–40,000 years ago". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bismarck_Archipelago]
- **Kilu Cave**, on Buka Island (Bougainville, Papua New Guinea), is the earliest known site in the Solomon Islands archipelago, occupied from about 29,000 to 20,000 BP. Its earliest radiocarbon date is 28,740 ± 280 BP, which calibrates to about 29,850–31,560 BC. Reaching Buka from Nissan Island required crossing at least 60 km of open sea. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilu_Cave]
- Kilu Cave contains starch grains of taro (*Colocasia* and *Alocasia*) on Pleistocene stone tools, the only evidence in Melanesia of plant use by the region's first inhabitants. About 20% of the Pleistocene fish bones are from open-ocean (pelagic) species. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilu_Cave]
- Archaeologist Matthew Spriggs (*The Island Melanesians*, 1997) called the Pleistocene settlement of **Manus** (Admiralty Islands) "a real threshold in voyaging ability". It is the only island settled in the Pleistocene beyond one-way intervisibility, and getting there meant "a blind crossing of some 60–90 km in a 200–300 km voyage". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_Oceania_and_Remote_Oceania]
- Western Melanesia was continuously occupied by Papuan peoples from 30,000–40,000 years ago. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapita_culture]

## Austronesian origins and the Lapita expansion into Remote Oceania
- The Austronesian expansion began in Taiwan "between about 5,000 and 6,000 years ago". Other articles date the movement out of Taiwan to about 3000–1500 BCE. Austronesians reached the Batanes Islands (northern Philippines) by about 2200 BCE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapita_culture; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micronesia]
- Austronesian sailing technology included the catamaran (double canoe), the outrigger boat, lashed-lug plank construction, and the **crab-claw sail**, which developed by about 1500 BCE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_vessels; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micronesia]
- **Lapita** is a Neolithic Austronesian archaeological culture that settled Island Melanesia by sea at around 1600–500 BCE. Its distinctive dentate-stamped pottery closely resembles pottery from the Nagsabaran site in northern Luzon (Philippines). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapita_culture]
- The name comes from a 1952 excavation by Edward W. Gifford and Richard Shutler Jr. at the type site on the Foué peninsula, Grande Terre, New Caledonia. Archaeologists misheard the local Haveke word *xapeta'a* ("to dig a hole"). The first recorded Lapita find was by the missionary Otto Meyer on Watom Island in 1909. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapita_culture]
- "Classic" Lapita pottery was produced between about 1600 and 1200 BCE in the Bismarck Archipelago. Later Lapita material is found in the Solomons, Vanuatu and New Caledonia, and Lapita styles from about 1000 BCE are found in Fiji and Western Polynesia. Around 3,500 years ago the Lapita complex appears "suddenly, as a fully-developed archaeological horizon" in the Bismarcks, with no earlier developmental stages. This is evidence that it was brought by migrants rather than (as Jim Allen and J. Peter White proposed in the 1980s–90s) developing locally. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapita_culture]
- Between about 1300 and 900 BCE, Lapita spread about 6,000 km east from the Bismarck Archipelago as far as Tonga and Samoa. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]
- Remote Oceania was "not settled until around the 12th century BC", when Lapita voyagers arrived. Ancient DNA shows that the first settlers came from Neolithic populations of Taiwan and the northern Philippines. Many populations of western Remote Oceania today have strong Papuan ancestry from a second expansion beginning around the 1st millennium BC. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_Oceania_and_Remote_Oceania]
- Studies from 2016 and 2017 suggest that the earliest Lapita settlers mostly bypassed New Guinea. The Papuan admixture seen in modern Polynesians and Island Melanesians occurred after Tonga and Vanuatu were settled. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesians]
- 2016 DNA analysis of four Lapita skeletons from Vanuatu and Tonga showed descent from people of Taiwan and the northern Philippines. A 2020 study found that the early settlers of the Mariana Islands and early Lapita individuals shared the same Philippine Austronesian source population. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapita_culture]
- The "Triple-I" model (intrusion, innovation, integration) is the orthodox view and is accepted by most specialists. It is advocated by Roger Green and Peter Bellwood. In 2011 Bellwood proposed a route from the northern Philippines east to the Marianas and then south to the Bismarcks, possibly alongside a route through eastern Indonesia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapita_culture]
- Lapita people kept pigs, dogs and chickens and grew taro, yam, coconut, bananas and breadfruit. They traded obsidian, adzes and shells over long distances. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapita_culture]
- **Teouma** (Éfaté, Vanuatu) holds the oldest known cemetery in the Pacific Islands, about 3,200–3,000 years old. In 2003 it yielded 26 burials containing 36 individuals. The skulls had been removed after burial. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teouma]
- The **Mana skeleton**, found on Moturiki, Fiji, in 2002, is a Lapita woman who lived in 800 BCE or earlier. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapita_culture]
- **Tonga** was reached by about 2850 BP (about 900 BCE), roughly three centuries after the Lapita dispersal began, landing first on Tongatapu and soon after in Ha'apai. One shell date from Pea, Tongatapu, is 3180 ± 100 BP. Initial dates are "still subject to debate". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Tonga]
- Patrick Kirch has suggested that Lapita culture encouraged younger sons, who did not inherit land, to emigrate and find new islands. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Tonga]
- Lapita pottery became progressively simpler in Western Polynesia, giving way to "Polynesian Plain Ware". It seems to have stopped in Samoa by about 2,800 years ago (another statement: plainware ceases in Samoa around 1 CE) and in Tonga by about 2,000 years ago (History of Tonga gives about 400 CE). Pottery was never made in East Polynesia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapita_culture; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Tonga] (The sources disagree on when Samoan pottery ended. Say only that pottery-making faded out in Western Polynesia during the first millennium BCE and early first millennium CE.)

## The "long pause" and the revised chronology of East Polynesia
- After reaching Fiji, Samoa and Tonga by about 900 BCE, the eastward expansion halted for a long period, the so-called "long pause". The Science Learning Hub (New Zealand) describes it as roughly a thousand years between the settlement of West and East Polynesia. Computer modelling of winds and currents suggests that shifts in climate may help explain it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesians; https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/2896-the-long-pause]
- **Revised short chronology:** Janet Wilmshurst, Terry Hunt, Carl Lipo and Atholl Anderson, "High-precision radiocarbon dating shows recent and rapid initial human colonization of East Polynesia", *PNAS* 108(5), 2011, pp. 1815–1820. Their meta-analysis of 1,434 radiocarbon dates from 47 islands kept only the most reliable (short-lived) samples. It found that the earliest East Polynesian settlement was in the Society Islands at about **AD 1025–1120**, roughly four centuries later than previously assumed. After a gap of 70–265 years, settlement continued "in one major pulse to all remaining islands" at about **AD 1190–1290**. [source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3033261; https://manoa.hawaii.edu/news/article.php?aId=4097]
- This chronology was contested. Mulrooney et al. criticised it in *PNAS*, and Wilmshurst et al. replied in "Accepting lower precision radiocarbon dates results in longer colonization chronologies for East Polynesia" (*PNAS* 108(23), E195, 2011). [source: https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/reply-to-mulrooney-et-al-accepting-lower-precision-radiocarbon-da/]
- "Rectifications in radiocarbon dating have changed almost all of the previously posited early settlement dates in Polynesia." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island]
- The **older long chronology**, still found in some sources, has East Polynesia settled from about AD 700, Hawai'i by 900, Rapa Nui by 1000 and New Zealand by 1200 (Wikipedia "Polynesians"). Present it only as the older view that the revised dating has largely superseded. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesians]
- The Polynesian Triangle has corners at Hawai'i, Rapa Nui and Aotearoa New Zealand. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]
- **Polynesian outliers** are Polynesian-speaking societies in Melanesia and Micronesia settled by back-migration. Anuta and Tikopia (Solomon Islands) were settled between the 10th and 13th centuries, and Nukuoro (Federated States of Micronesia) only in the 18th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_outlier]
- The Pacific rat (*Rattus exulans*, kiore) cannot swim long distances, so it is used as a marker of human migration across the Pacific. Lisa (Elizabeth) Matisoo-Smith's 1996 University of Auckland doctoral thesis used mtDNA variation in *Rattus exulans* as a model for human colonisation and contact in prehistoric Polynesia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_rat; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisa_Matisoo-Smith]

## Hawai'i
- Polynesians arrived in Hawai'i "between 940 and 1200 AD". "The most recent survey of carbon-dating evidence puts the arrival of people at around 940–1130." Kirch's earlier textbooks dated first settlement to about 300 CE, his later estimates to as late as 600, and others to 700–800. All of these earlier dates are now generally rejected. Wilmshurst et al. 2011 place Hawai'i within the AD 1190–1290 pulse. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Hawaii; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3033261] (Give the range and attribute it: about 940–1130 by one survey, about 1190–1290 by Wilmshurst et al.)
- Hawaiian chiefly genealogies say the Tahitian priest Pa'ao brought a new religious and social order, traditionally around 1200. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Hawaii]
- Patrick Kirch, *How Chiefs Became Kings: Divine Kingship and the Rise of Archaic States in Ancient Hawai'i* (University of California Press, 2010), argues that Ancestral Polynesian societies were chiefdoms. By the time Europeans arrived, he says, chiefs on Hawai'i had been replaced by divine kings ruling archaic states. Hawai'i is valuable evidence because Cook's voyagers observed these recently formed kingdoms. [source: https://www.ucpress.edu/books/how-chiefs-became-kings/paper; https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cliodynamics/article/38126/galley/28693/download]
- The Native Hawaiian population before 1778 is unknown; one estimate is about 300,000. It fell to about 142,000 in the 1820s (missionary census), 82,203 in 1850, 40,622 in 1890 and 37,656 in 1900. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Hawaii]
- Aided by firearms and ships, Kamehameha I conquered most of the islands by 1795. Unification was completed in 1810 when Kaua'i and Ni'ihau joined the kingdom. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Hawaii]

## Rapa Nui (Easter Island): settlement
- Estimates of first settlement have ranged from 400 to 1300 CE. The "current best estimate for colonization is in the 12th century". Hunt and Lipo's radiocarbon dates from Anakena imply settlement "late, about CE 1200" (published as a 2006 study; Wikipedia also cites a "2007 study"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_L._Hunt]
- Settlers probably came from Mangareva (about 2,600 km away) or the Marquesas (about 3,200 km), possibly by way of Pitcairn and Henderson. In 1999 a reconstructed Polynesian voyaging canoe reached Rapa Nui from Mangareva in about 17½ days. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island]
- The *moai* were carved in about 1100–1680 CE (rectified radiocarbon dates). 887 statues have been inventoried, about 95% of them carved from tuff at the Rano Raraku quarry. Only about a quarter were installed and nearly half remained in the quarry. The largest moai erected on a platform, "Paro", weighs about 82 tonnes and is 9.89 m long. Of 313 known *ahu* (platforms), 125 carried moai. Ahu Tongariki had 15. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island]
- At European contact in 1722 the population was estimated at 2,000–3,000. In 2017, 7,750 people lived on the island, of whom 3,512 (45%) identified as Rapa Nui. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island]

## Aotearoa New Zealand
- No human remains, artefacts or structures are confidently dated earlier than the Kaharoa Tephra, the ash layer from the Mount Tarawera eruption of about 1314 CE. Mitochondrial DNA variation among Māori suggests first settlement between 1250 and 1300. Wikipedia's lead dates the start of the "main settlement period" to between 1320 and 1350. 1999 dates of kiore bones to as early as 100 CE were later shown to be wrong. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_New_Zealand] (Write "about 1250–1300, with the main settlement in the decades after", attributed to archaeological and genetic studies. The seed summary's "c. 1250" is acceptable as an approximation.)
- Prehistorians broadly agree that the settlement of New Zealand was "planned and deliberate". There is no evidence of any pre-Polynesian people. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_New_Zealand]
- An early European synthesis of Māori traditions had Kupe discovering New Zealand around 750 CE and a single "great fleet" from Hawaiki around 1350. "From the late 20th century, this synthesis has been increasingly relegated to the realm of myth." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_New_Zealand]
- **Wairau Bar** (Te Pokohiwi), Marlborough, is one of the earliest known settlements. A 2009–2010 study using moa eggshell dated it to **1288–1300 CE**. DNA and isotope work by Lisa Matisoo-Smith and Michael Knapp showed that some of those buried there had been raised in East Polynesia. The DNA diversity indicated a "reasonably large group", which is consistent with planned migration rather than chance arrivals. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wairau_Bar]
- Settlers hunted moa to extinction by about 1500 and cleared about 40% of the original forest. They brought kūmara (sweet potato), taro, gourds and yams from East Polynesia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_New_Zealand]
- Polynesians reached the outlying "South Polynesia" islands (Atholl Anderson's term): the Kermadecs, Chathams, Auckland Islands and Norfolk Island. Remains of a settlement on Enderby Island (Auckland Islands) date to the 13th century, and remains of an ocean-going waka found on Chatham Island in 2024 date to 1440–1470. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]
- The Moriori of the Chatham Islands (Rēkohu) came from mainland New Zealand by at least 1500 AD. They developed a pacifist code, the law of *Nunuku*, which made them vulnerable to Taranaki Māori invaders in the 1830s during the Musket Wars. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moriori]

## Micronesia
- The Mariana Islands were the first islands in Oceania settled by Austronesians, by voyagers sailing east from the Philippines in about 1500 BCE. Palau and Yap were settled by about 1000 BCE, probably from elsewhere in Island Southeast Asia. Eastern Micronesia was settled around 200 BCE by Lapita-descended people moving north from Island Melanesia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micronesia]
- Around 800 CE a second wave reached the Marianas and began the Latte period, named for the capped stone pillars (*latte*/*haligi*). The Marianas were the only islands in Oceania where rice was grown before European contact. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micronesia]
- Construction of **Nan Madol**, a megalithic complex of basalt "logs" on Pohnpei, began around 1180 CE, followed by Leluh on Kosrae around 1200. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micronesia]
- Marshallese **stick charts**, made from coconut-frond midribs with shells marking islands, mapped ocean swell patterns and how islands disrupted them. They were studied on shore, not carried at sea, and each could be fully read only by its maker. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Islands_stick_chart; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]

## Voyaging technology and wayfinding
- Voyagers used outrigger canoes and double-hulled canoes, two equal hulls lashed side by side with a platform between them for food and gear. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]
- Wayfinding methods were: the rising and setting points of stars (a memorised sequence of stars for each route), the sun, ocean swells (more reliable than local wind waves), the flight of birds (white terns and noddies fly out to fish in the morning and return to land at night), cloud formations and the colour reflected on clouds by lagoons (for example the green tint over Anaa atoll), and wind and weather. Knowledge was passed orally from master to apprentice, often in song. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]
- Some star compass systems specify as many as 150 stars with known bearings, though most have only a few dozen. Navigators also used star altitudes for latitude and "sailed down the latitude" to a target island. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]
- Hōkūle'a's name means "star of gladness": Arcturus, a zenith star for Hawai'i that passes directly overhead at Hawai'i's latitude. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hōkūleʻa]
- There is no evidence that Polynesian navigators carried instruments on board. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]
- Large Tongan double canoes of the *kalia* type could carry up to 100 people. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuʻi_Tonga_Empire]

## Sweet potato and contact with South America
- The sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*) is native to the Americas and was widely grown in Central and South America by 2500 BC. In Polynesia the earliest archaeological evidence is carbonised fragments from one site on **Mangaia** (southern Cook Islands), dated **988–1155 AD**. Cultivation probably began in central Polynesia about 1000–1100 AD and spread to Rapa Nui, Hawai'i and New Zealand. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweet_potato_cultivation_in_Polynesia] (Wikipedia is inconsistent here. "Sweet potato" gives a Cook Islands date of 1210–1400 CE, "Pre-Columbian transoceanic contact theories" says 1000 CE with introduction "c. 700 CE", and "Polynesians" says 700 AD. Use "by about 1000–1200 CE, on the evidence from the Cook Islands" and do not use 700 CE.)
- The word: Proto-Polynesian *\*kumala* (Rapa Nui *kumara*, Hawaiian *'uala*, Māori *kūmara*) resembles Quechua and Aymara *k'umar ~ k'umara*. The linguists Willem Adelaar and Pieter Muysken call the similarity "near proof of incidental contact between inhabitants of the Andean region and the South Pacific", but not necessarily of migration. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_transoceanic_contact_theories]
- The "tripartite hypothesis" (developed in the 1950s–60s) holds that an original *kumara* lineage came from western South America around 1000 AD and was later partly replaced by *camote* and *batata* lineages introduced by Spanish and Portuguese ships around 1500. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweet_potato_cultivation_in_Polynesia]
- **Dissent:** a 2018 genetic analysis of sweet potato collected in the Society Islands by Joseph Banks in 1769 found that the lineage diverged from South American varieties at least 111,500 years ago. Its authors argued for natural dispersal (by birds or floating seeds) without humans. Other researchers pointed to methodological flaws, and "the present scholarly consensus favours the pre-Columbian contact model". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweet_potato_cultivation_in_Polynesia; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweet_potato; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island]
- **The 2020 genetic study:** Alexander G. Ioannidis et al., "Native American gene flow into Polynesia predating Easter Island settlement", *Nature* 583, 572–577 (July 2020). They analysed genome-wide data from 807 people in 17 Pacific island populations and 15 Pacific-coast Native American groups. They found Native American ancestry in East Polynesians (Marquesas, including Fatu Hiva in the southern Marquesas, plus Mangareva, the Palliser Islands and Rapa Nui). They dated a single initial contact event to about **1150–1230 CE**, with later admixture on Rapa Nui about 1380. The closest match was to present-day Indigenous people of Colombia's Pacific coast; Wikipedia names the Zenú. The authors allowed that Polynesians may have reached South America and returned. Several outside scholars thought contact in South America more likely. [source: https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2020/07/polynesians-and-native-americans-made-early-contact.html; https://www.sciencenews.org/article/south-americans-may-have-traveled-polynesia-800-years-ago; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_transoceanic_contact_theories]
- **2024 ancient-DNA study:** J. Víctor Moreno-Mayar, Anna-Sapfo Malaspinas and colleagues, *Nature* 633, 389–397 (2024), sequenced 15 ancient Rapanui genomes (individuals dated 1670–1950 CE). They found about 10% Native American ancestry (6.0–11.4% in Schiffels's commentary), from admixture dated to about **1250–1430 CE**. The study does not establish where the contact took place. [source: https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/no-evidence-for-17th-century-population-collapse-on-rapa-nui; https://www.sci.news/genetics/ancient-easter-islander-genomes-13254.html]
- A 2007 *PNAS* study claimed pre-Columbian Polynesian chickens at El Arenal, Chile (bones dated 1304–1424). A later re-analysis disputed this, and the original authors replied defending it. The question remains unresolved. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]
- Proposed Hawaiian–Chumash contact (Southern California, 400–800 CE, based on sewn-plank canoes and the word *tomolo'o*) is rejected by most archaeologists of the Chumash and Tongva. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]

## Societies that followed: chiefdoms and Tonga's maritime polity
- Tongan expansion under the **Tu'i Tonga** line, centred at Mu'a (Lapaha) on Tongatapu, is said to have begun about 950 CE with the first Tu'i Tonga, 'Aho'eitu, and to have peaked in about 1200–1500. Wikipedia's "History of Tonga" says it reached from Tikopia in the west to Niue in the east. Tributary chiefdoms brought annual tribute (*'inasi*) to Mu'a after the harvest, and Cook witnessed an *'inasi* ceremony in 1777. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuʻi_Tonga_Empire; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Tonga]
- Tongan oral traditions on this are discussed by the Tongan scholar 'Okusitino Mahina. Archaeology, anthropology and linguistics confirm Tongan influence in 'Uvea, Rotuma, Futuna, Samoa, Niue, parts of Micronesia, Vanuatu and New Caledonia. "Some academics prefer the term 'maritime chiefdom'", while others regard "empire" as the most convenient term. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuʻi_Tonga_Empire] (The Wikipedia article is weak and relies on traditions. Call it a "maritime chiefdom" or a "far-reaching Tongan polity", note the label is debated, and avoid exact reign dates.)
- In the 13th or 14th century, Samoans led by the progenitors of the Malietoa family expelled the Tongans. After Tu'i Tonga Takalaua was assassinated in about 1535, power was divided: the Tu'i Ha'atakalaua took over everyday government. In about 1610 the office of Tu'i Kanokupolu was created for the half-Samoan Ngata. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuʻi_Tonga_Empire]
- Tongan monumental works include the Ha'amonga 'a Maui trilithon, 5 m high, made of three coral-limestone slabs each weighing over 40 tons, and the *langi* terraced royal tombs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Tonga]
- The first Europeans reached Tonga in 1616 (Willem Schouten and Jacob Le Maire, off Niuatoputapu), followed by Abel Tasman in January 1643. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuʻi_Tonga_Empire]
- Kirch's *The Evolution of the Polynesian Chiefdoms* (Cambridge University Press, 1984) is a standard work on Polynesian chiefdoms. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Vinton_Kirch]

## Rapa Nui's later history and the "ecocide" debate
- Rapa Nui was organised in lineage-based clans under an *ariki* (paramount chief) descended from the legendary founder Hotu Matu'a. In the birdman (*tangata manu*) cult at Orongo, clan representatives competed for the first sooty tern egg from Motu Nui. Katherine Routledge's 1914–15 research indicated that the competitions ran from about 1760 to 1878. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island]
- Dutch visitors in 1722 and Spanish visitors in 1770 saw only standing statues. By Cook's visit in 1774 many had been toppled. The *huri mo'ai* ("statue-toppling") continued into the 1830s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Easter_Island]
- **First European contact:** Jacob Roggeveen arrived on 5 April (Easter Sunday) 1722. When a shot was fired during the landing, ten or twelve islanders were killed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Easter_Island]
- In December 1862 Peruvian slave raiders began kidnapping islanders. Around 1,400–1,500 people were taken, about half the population. About a dozen returned, bringing smallpox. Between 1862 and 1888 about 94% of the population died or emigrated. By 1877 only 111 Rapa Nui remained on the island, and only 36 of them had descendants. Chile annexed the island in 1888, by the *Acuerdo de Voluntades* (9 September 1888) with Atamu Tekena. Rapa Nui were confined to Hanga Roa while the island was a sheep ranch, and gained Chilean citizenship only in 1966. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Easter_Island]
- **The "ecocide" thesis:** Jared Diamond, in *Collapse: How Societies Choose to Fail or Succeed* (2005), presented Rapa Nui as a case of self-inflicted environmental collapse. In this account, deforestation linked to statue-building, together with overpopulation, led to warfare, cannibalism and a population crash before Europeans arrived. Diamond drew on Heyerdahl's and William Mulloy's interpretations. Barbara A. West wrote that the population dropped "to 2,000–3,000 from a high of approximately 15,000 just a century earlier". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapse:_How_Societies_Choose_to_Fail_or_Succeed]
- **The critics:** Terry Hunt and Carl Lipo ("Revisiting Rapa Nui (Easter Island) 'Ecocide'", *Pacific Science* 63(4), 2009; *The Statues That Walked*, 2011) argue the following. Settlement was late, about 1200. The Pacific rat was a major cause of deforestation. Population grew until European contact, with the only clear decline after about 1750–1800. There is little evidence of warfare: no fortifications, obsidian *mata'a* that look like agricultural tools, and only 2.5% of pre-contact crania show healed fractures. The collapse was caused by European diseases, slave raids and colonial exploitation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_L._Hunt]
- Douglas Owsley's 1994 skeletal study found that "few fatalities were directly attributable to violence". Mulrooney et al. (2010) concluded that there was "no conclusive evidence for the proposed precontact collapse". DiNapoli and Lipo (2021) found steady population growth to 1722, with the island never holding "more than a few thousand people". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island]
- Hunt and Lipo argue that the moai were "walked" upright by rocking them with ropes (2012–13 *Journal of Archaeological Science*). A 4.35-tonne concrete replica was moved 100 m in 40 minutes by 18 people. The idea fits the Rapa Nui tradition that the statues walked. Sue Hamilton (UCL) cautioned that this shows the method was possible, not that it was used. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_L._Hunt; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island]
- The 2024 genomic study (Moreno-Mayar et al.) found no evidence for a 17th-century population bottleneck; the population grew steadily until the 1860s slave raids. [source: https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/no-evidence-for-17th-century-population-collapse-on-rapa-nui]
- A 2025 sediment-core study found a megadrought beginning about 1550 CE that coincided with cultural change but "does not support a model of abrupt pre-contact societal collapse". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island]

## European arrival (brief)
- **Magellan** entered the Pacific through the strait on 1 November 1520 and crossed the ocean in 98 days. The fleet reached the Marianas on 6 March 1521 and anchored at Guam, where Chamorro people met them in *proas* (outrigger canoes then unknown to Europeans). After a confrontation, Magellan's men burned 40–50 Chamorro houses and killed seven men. This was the first known European contact in Micronesia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magellan_expedition; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micronesia]
- Álvaro de Mendaña reached the Solomon Islands (Santa Isabel) on 7 February 1568. On his second voyage he reached the Marquesas on 21 July 1595, where, by Queirós's estimate, about 200 Marquesans were killed in two weeks. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Álvaro_de_Mendaña_de_Neira]
- Abel Tasman anchored in Golden Bay (Mohua), New Zealand, in December 1642 and left after a clash with Ngāti Tūmatakōkiri. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_New_Zealand]
- Samuel Wallis (HMS *Dolphin*) made the first recorded European visit to Tahiti in 1767. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Wallis; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cook]
- **Cook's three voyages** (1768–1771, 1772–1775, 1776–1779). On the first voyage, in *Endeavour*, he observed the 1769 transit of Venus at Tahiti (arriving 13 April 1769), reached New Zealand in October 1769 (where his men shot several Māori dead in the first two days at Tūranganui/Poverty Bay), and charted eastern Australia. On the third voyage he reached Hawai'i on 18 January 1778, the first documented European contact. He was killed at Kealakekua Bay on 14 February 1779. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cook; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Hawaii]
- At Kealakekua, Cook's ships were met by about 10,000 Hawaiians and 1,000 canoes during the Makahiki season of the god Lono. In the fatal affray Cook and four marines were killed, along with seventeen Hawaiians. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cook]
- **Debate:** did Hawaiians see Cook as the god Lono? Marshall Sahlins argued that they did. Gananath Obeyesekere (*The Apotheosis of Captain Cook: European Mythmaking in the Pacific*, 1992) called "the apotheosis of Captain Cook... a European myth of imperialism, civilization, and conquest". Sahlins replied in *How "Natives" Think: About Captain Cook, For Example* (1996). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cook; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_James_Cook]
- Cook's verdict, after his third-voyage visit to Hawai'i in 1778, was that the Polynesian "nation" extended "from New Zealand, in the South, as far as the Sandwich Islands... to the North, and... from Easter Island, to the Hebrides" (see Quotations). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]
- The Māori population fell from about 86,000 in 1769 to about 42,000 in 1896, its lowest point. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_New_Zealand]

## Tupaia
- Tupaia (c. 1725 – 20 December 1770) was a navigator and *'arioi* priest of Ra'iātea, trained in the schools of learning at the Taputapuatea marae. About 1763 Bora Bora warriors invaded Ra'iātea, and he fled to Tahiti, where he became adviser to the chiefly couple Amo and Purea. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupaia_(navigator)]
- Tupaia joined *Endeavour* in July 1769 at Joseph Banks's insistence. Cook at first refused for reasons of cost, so Banks paid for his upkeep. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupaia_(navigator)]
- From 15 August 1769 Tupaia worked with Cook, Banks and the officers on a chart of the Pacific. He is said to have known about 130 islands within about 2,000 miles (3,200 km) of Ra'iātea and named 74 on the chart, which is preserved in the British Museum. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupaia_(navigator); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]
- **Debate:** older work (summarised by Joan Druett) held that Tupaia's own voyaging was limited and that Cook undervalued him. Lars Eckstein and Anja Schwarz argue that the chart shows voyaging routes across nearly the whole Polynesian Triangle, from Rotuma to Rapa Nui and from Tahiti to Hawai'i (probably excepting New Zealand). In their reading, Tupaia invented a cartographic system that translated his island-to-island wayfinding into the logic of Cook's compass. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupaia_(navigator)]
- In New Zealand some Māori welcomed Tupaia as a *tohunga* (expert). With his help Cook became the first European to communicate with Māori (Cook article). He died at Batavia (Jakarta) of dysentery or malaria. When Cook returned to New Zealand in 1773, Māori called out "Tupaia! Tupaia!" [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupaia_(navigator)]

## Accidental drift vs deliberate voyaging: Sharp, Heyerdahl and the experimental answer
- Late 19th- and early 20th-century writers such as Abraham Fornander and Percy Smith told a romantic story of heroic fleets migrating from Asia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]
- **Andrew Sharp** (1906–1974), a New Zealand historian, wrote *Ancient Voyagers in the Pacific* (Polynesian Society, Wellington, 1956; Penguin 1957; revised as *Ancient Voyagers in Polynesia*, 1963). He accepted that Polynesia was settled from the west, but argued that islands more than about 300 miles from their nearest neighbour were settled only by accidental voyages, through drift, storms and luck, and not by deliberate navigation. His thesis provoked a 1962 symposium published as *Polynesian Navigation*, edited by Jack Golson. [source: https://www.pbs.org/wayfinders/polynesian5.html; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation] (The symposium/publication year is not settled; see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Critics answered that drift settlement needs a canoe that happens to carry a fertile couple, together with the crop plants and animals (pigs, dogs, chickens, taro, breadfruit) found on nearly all Polynesian islands. Computer simulations found settlement by drift alone extremely unlikely. [source: https://www.pbs.org/wayfinders/polynesian5.html]
- Cook himself had leaned toward the accidental explanation. After meeting Tahitians who had been blown about 1,000 miles to Atiu, he wrote that the incident "will serve to explain, better than the thousand conjectures of speculative reasoners, how the detached parts of the earth, and, in particular, how the South Seas, may have been peopled". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]
- **Thor Heyerdahl** (1914–2002), a Norwegian, sailed the balsa raft *Kon-Tiki* from Callao, Peru. He left on 28 April 1947, was first towed about 50 miles out by a Peruvian Navy tug, and drifted for 101 days and about 6,900 km before wrecking on the reef of Raroia (Tuamotus) on 7 August 1947. The 1950 documentary won an Academy Award. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kon-Tiki_expedition]
- Heyerdahl argued that Polynesia was first settled from South America by "Tiki people", white, bearded, fair-haired sun-worshippers. He held that Polynesians were too "primitive" to sail against prevailing winds. His theory has been "overwhelmingly rejected" and is described as pseudoscientific and racially controversial. Archaeological, linguistic and genetic evidence shows a western, Austronesian origin. He led an archaeological expedition to Rapa Nui in 1955–56. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kon-Tiki_expedition; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thor_Heyerdahl]
- Note for the writer: the 2020 and 2024 genetic studies do show some South American contact. They do not support Heyerdahl's thesis. The contact came after Polynesians had settled East Polynesia, was small in scale, and may well have involved Polynesians sailing east to South America. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kon-Tiki_expedition; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_transoceanic_contact_theories]
- In the 1960s David Lewis sailed his catamaran from Tahiti to New Zealand without instruments. He then sailed with and recorded traditional navigators: Tevake (Santa Cruz Islands) and Hipour of Puluwat (Caroline Islands). He published *We, the Navigators* in 1972. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]

## The 20th-century voyaging revival: Hōkūle'a, Mau Piailug, Ben Finney
- **Ben Finney** (1933–2017), an anthropologist at the University of Hawai'i at Mānoa from 1970 to 2000, set out to disprove Sharp. In the 1960s he built *Nalehia*, a 40-ft replica Hawaiian double canoe. In 1973 he co-founded the **Polynesian Voyaging Society** with the artist Herb Kawainui Kāne and the sailor Charles Tommy Holmes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Finney]
- ***Hōkūle'a***, a performance-accurate double-hulled voyaging canoe (*wa'a kaulua*) built with plywood and fibreglass, was launched on 8 March 1975. It is about 62 ft long, has crab-claw sails, and carries 12–16 crew. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hōkūleʻa]
- "In 1975, no living Hawaiian knew the ancient techniques for blue water voyaging." The Polynesian Voyaging Society recruited **Mau Piailug** (1932 – 12 July 2010) of Satawal, Caroline Islands (Federated States of Micronesia). He was a master navigator (*palu*) of the Weriyeng school, initiated in a *pwo* ceremony at about eighteen, and was the only one of the few remaining Micronesian navigators willing to share his knowledge with outsiders. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hōkūleʻa; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mau_Piailug]
- **The 1976 voyage:** Hōkūle'a sailed from Honolua Bay, Maui, on 1 May 1976 and reached Pape'ete, Tahiti, on 4 June 1976. Mau navigated without instruments, and Kawika Kapahulehua was captain. Finney and David Lewis were among the crew. On the 31st day Mau predicted land; white terns appeared and the atoll of Mataiva was sighted. About 17,000 people, "half the population of Tahiti", welcomed the canoe. The voyage aimed to test deliberate two-way voyaging as against drift or South American origins, and to serve cultural revitalisation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hōkūleʻa; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mau_Piailug]
- Crew conflict on the voyage turned physical, and Mau went home after reaching Tahiti. The return to Hawai'i was navigated with Western instruments. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hōkūleʻa]
- On 16 March 1978, a second attempt capsized off Moloka'i. The surfer and lifeguard **Eddie Aikau** paddled for help on a surfboard and was never seen again; the crew was rescued. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hōkūleʻa]
- In 1980 **Nainoa Thompson** (born 1953), trained by Mau, navigated Hōkūle'a from Hawai'i to Tahiti and back (out 15 March – 17 April 1980). He was the first Native Hawaiian in modern times to navigate such a voyage without instruments. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hōkūleʻa; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nainoa_Thompson]
- The "Voyage of Rediscovery" (1985–87) covered about 12,000 miles across Polynesia, including Rarotonga to Waitangi, New Zealand (21 November – 7 December 1985). The Tongan captain Sione Taupeamuhu, sceptical that Thompson could find Nomuka without instruments, said when it appeared: "Now I can believe the stories of my ancestors." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hōkūleʻa]
- In 1999 the Hawaiian canoe *Makali'i* sailed Mau home to Satawal. On 18 March 2007 Mau held the first *pwo* ceremony on Satawal in 56 years, initiating sixteen navigators including five Native Hawaiians (Nainoa Thompson among them). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mau_Piailug]
- Hōkūle'a's Mālama Honua worldwide voyage ran from 2014 to 2017 (47,000 nautical miles, 85 ports, 26 countries). Its Moananuiākea circumnavigation of the Pacific began on 23 June 2023 from Juneau, Alaska. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hōkūleʻa]
- The revival helped drive the second **Hawaiian Renaissance** and a revival of voyaging and canoe-building in New Zealand (for example Hector Busby), Rarotonga and Tahiti. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mau_Piailug; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation]

## Pacific Islander scholars and voices
- **Epeli Hau'ofa** (7 December 1939 – 11 January 2009), a Tongan and Fijian anthropologist and writer born in Papua to Tongan missionary parents, taught at the University of the South Pacific in Suva. In 1997 he founded its Oceania Centre for Arts and Culture. His essay **"Our Sea of Islands"** was first published in *A New Oceania: Rediscovering Our Sea of Islands* (eds. Vijay Naidu, Eric Waddell and Hau'ofa; USP, Suva, 1993) and reprinted in *The Contemporary Pacific* 6(1), Spring 1994, pp. 147–161. It argued that Islanders "were connected rather than separated by the sea" (BBC History magazine's summary). It contrasted the belittling colonial view of "islands in a far sea" with Oceania as "a sea of islands". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epeli_Hauʻofa; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/466365/]
- **Damon Salesa** (born 1972), a historian of Samoan descent, was the first Rhodes Scholar of Pacific descent. Since 2021 he has been Vice-Chancellor of Auckland University of Technology, the first Pacific person to lead a New Zealand university. His *An Indigenous Ocean: Pacific Essays* (Bridget Williams Books, 2023), 15 essays that keep Pacific peoples "at the centre of their stories", won the 2024 Ockham New Zealand Book Award for General Non-Fiction. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damon_Salesa; https://www.bwb.co.nz/books/an-indigenous-ocean]
- **Tracey Banivanua Mar**, a historian at La Trobe University, wrote *Decolonisation and the Pacific: Indigenous Globalisation and the Ends of Empire* (Cambridge University Press, 2016). It treats decolonisation in the Pacific, Australia and New Zealand as an Indigenous and international process that was "fragile, contingent and ongoing". This is relevant for the chapter's closing link to modern Pacific history. [source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/decolonisation-and-the-pacific/06DC5D6735164A6BC291E0414F8748BC]
- **Atholl Anderson** (born 1943, of Ngāi Tahu descent) is a New Zealand archaeologist (Otago, then the Australian National University) who researched Ngāi Tahu's claim to the Waitangi Tribunal. He re-dated New Zealand's moa-hunting sites, showed that Polynesians reached the sub-polar Chathams, Rakiura and Auckland Islands about 700 years ago, and co-authored the 2011 short-chronology paper. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atholl_Anderson]
- **Patrick Vinton Kirch** (born Honolulu) is an archaeologist (Berkeley, later University of Hawai'i) and the leading synthesiser of Pacific prehistory. His books include *The Lapita Peoples* (1997), *The Evolution of the Polynesian Chiefdoms* (1984) and *On the Road of the Winds*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Vinton_Kirch]
- **Lisa (Elizabeth) Matisoo-Smith** (born 1963) is a molecular anthropologist at the University of Otago. She uses DNA from people and from commensal animals such as the Pacific rat to trace Pacific migration, and won the Royal Society Te Apārangi's Mason Durie Medal in 2018. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisa_Matisoo-Smith]
- Sir **James Hēnare**, an elder of Tai Tokerau, addressed Hōkūle'a's crew during its New Zealand visit (see Quotations). Māori, Tahitian, Tongan, Samoan, Cook Islands and Marquesan sailors crewed legs of the Voyage of Rediscovery. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mau_Piailug; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hōkūleʻa]
- Tongan oral history scholarship on the Tu'i Tonga: 'Okusitino Mahina. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuʻi_Tonga_Empire]

## Debates
- **Deliberate voyaging vs accidental drift.** Romantic "heroic fleet" writers (Fornander, Percy Smith) gave way to Andrew Sharp's 1956 drift thesis: deliberate voyages only within about 300 miles. Finney, Lewis, the Polynesian Voyaging Society and Mau Piailug answered with experimental voyages, and later with computer simulations, the distribution of crops and animals, and Wairau Bar DNA showing a sizeable founding group. Today the consensus is deliberate, planned colonisation, including two-way voyaging. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation; https://www.pbs.org/wayfinders/polynesian5.html; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wairau_Bar]
- **Western (Austronesian) vs American origin.** Heyerdahl's Kon-Tiki thesis against linguistic, archaeological and genetic evidence for an origin in Taiwan and the Philippines. The thesis is rejected. Limited later American contact is accepted, but who sailed where is still open. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kon-Tiki_expedition]
- **Lapita origins.** The Triple-I model (Green, Bellwood), the majority view, against local development in the Bismarcks (Allen, White, 1980s–90s). Spriggs stresses cultural continuity between Lapita and later Melanesians. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapita_culture]
- **Long vs short chronology for East Polynesia.** Wilmshurst, Hunt, Lipo and Anderson (2011) argue for a late, rapid colonisation (Society Islands about 1025–1120, the rest about 1190–1290). Mulrooney et al. defended longer chronologies based on a broader range of dates. [source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3033261; https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/reply-to-mulrooney-et-al-accepting-lower-precision-radiocarbon-da/]
- **Why the "long pause"?** It is unexplained. Hypotheses include shifts in winds and currents. [source: https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/2896-the-long-pause]
- **The sweet potato.** Human contact (the tripartite hypothesis; Adelaar and Muysken's linguistic argument; the Ioannidis 2020 and Moreno-Mayar 2024 genetics) against natural dispersal (the 2018 study claiming divergence at least 111,500 years ago). The consensus favours pre-Columbian contact. Whether Polynesians went to America or South Americans came to Polynesia is unresolved. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweet_potato_cultivation_in_Polynesia; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweet_potato]
- **Rapa Nui "ecocide".** Jared Diamond (*Collapse*, 2005), following Heyerdahl and Mulloy, argues for self-inflicted collapse before contact. Against him: Hunt and Lipo (2009, 2011), Owsley (1994), Mulrooney et al. (2010), DiNapoli and Lipo (2021) and Moreno-Mayar et al. (2024). They argue for steady growth until European contact, with the catastrophe caused by disease, slave raids and colonial ranching, and rats as a key driver of deforestation. The weight of recent evidence is against a pre-contact collapse. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_L._Hunt]
- **How the moai moved.** Horizontal sledges and rollers (the older view, used in the ecocide argument) against upright "walking" (Lipo, Hunt and Rapu Haoa 2013, matching Rapa Nui tradition). Sue Hamilton is among the sceptics. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_L._Hunt]
- **Tupaia's chart and knowledge.** The older view of Tupaia's limited voyaging, against Eckstein and Schwarz's reading of the chart as a Polynesia-wide navigational system. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupaia_(navigator)]
- **Cook as Lono.** Sahlins against Obeyesekere; see Quotations. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cook]
- **Tonga: "empire" or "maritime chiefdom"?** The label is contested. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuʻi_Tonga_Empire]
- **From chiefdom to state in Hawai'i.** Kirch (2010) argues that archaic states with divine kings emerged before contact. Michael E. Smith's review in *Cliodynamics* (2012) raised criticisms whose substance I did not retrieve. [source: https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cliodynamics/article/38126/galley/28693/download]
- **Perspective: "islands in a far sea" vs "a sea of islands".** Hau'ofa's reframing of Oceania against colonial "belittlement", developed by Salesa's "indigenous ocean". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epeli_Hauʻofa; https://www.bwb.co.nz/books/an-indigenous-ocean]
