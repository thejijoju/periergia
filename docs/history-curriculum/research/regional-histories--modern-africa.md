## 1. The continent on the eve of conquest and the conquest itself (recap only)
- As late as the 1870s Europeans controlled approximately 10% of the continent, all near the coasts. [source: SCR] (matches *Imperial Expansion*)
- Liberia declared independence on 26 July 1847 (Americo-Liberian settlers). With Ethiopia, the only African states not under European rule by 1914. [source: LIB; *Imperial Expansion*]
- Samori Touré: reign 1878–1898; died 2 June 1900 on Missanga Island, French Congo (now near Ndjolé, Gabon). [source: SAM]
- Herero and Nama: 40,000–80,000 Herero (80% of prewar population) and 10,000 Nama (half) died; 2021 German–Namibian deal of €1.1 billion as ex gratia development aid, rejecting legal responsibility. [source: HNG] (matches published chapter)
- Maji Maji: July 1905 – August 1907; 75,000–300,000 dead, overwhelmingly from famine; began 31 July 1905 when Matumbi destroyed the cotton crop at Samanga. [source: MM]
- Congo Free State: no census before 1924 (which counted about 10 million); Hochschild and (earlier) Vansina cite ~10 million dead; Vansina later revised, criticising Hochschild for extrapolating the 50% toll in rubber provinces to the whole Congo; "more recent extensive demographic research agrees with Vansina"; other scholars argue a 20% decline over 40 years to 1924. [source: CFS] → Range 1–10 million, as in *Imperial Expansion*.
- **Rinderpest** (a theme the published chapters do not cover): introduced to Africa with infected cattle imported from Asia to feed Italian forces in the Italo-Ethiopian war of 1887–89; spread across Eastern and Southern Africa in the 1890s; about 90% of Ethiopia's cattle died; 80–90% of Fulani cattle died; famine killed an estimated third of Ethiopia's human population and two-thirds of the Maasai of Tanzania (both figures are estimates in the article). Lugard: "Never before in the memory of man… have the cattle died in such vast numbers". The epizootic "facilitated further colonial conquest by creating famine, economic dislocation, and landscape transformations" (grass to thornbush). [source: RIN]

## 2. Varieties of colonial rule
- Indirect rule is traced to Frederick Lugard, High Commissioner of the Protectorate of Northern Nigeria 1899–1906; Lugard's doctrine: because Europeans and Africans were culturally different, Africans had to be ruled through their own institutions. Also used (in forms) in French Algeria and Tunisia, Portuguese Angola and Mozambique, and Rwanda and Burundi. [source: IR] (see *British Empire* for warrant chiefs and Mamdani)
- French **Code de l'indigénat** (Native code): a "diverse and fluctuating set of arbitrary laws and regulations" creating an inferior legal status for colonial subjects from 1881 to 1944–47; decreed in Algeria (28 June 1881), Senegal 1887, Madagascar 1901, French West Africa 1904, French Equatorial Africa 1910, Togo and Cameroon mandates 1923–24; officially abolished 1946. [source: NC]
- **Chibalo**: Portuguese forced labour/debt-bondage system, notably in Angola and Mozambique; Portugal formally abolished slavery in 1869 but chibalo "outlasted slavery"; under Salazar's Estado Novo it was used in Mozambique to grow cotton, build roads and serve settlers; Portuguese colonies were legally "overseas provinces". [source: CHI]
- Rwanda under Belgium (from 1917; more direct rule from 1926): compulsory identity cards labelling each person Tutsi, Hutu, Twa or Naturalised froze formerly permeable categories into rigid ethnic groups; missionaries promoted the "Hamitic" theory of Tutsi foreign origins. [source: RWA] (Year of cards — commonly 1933 — NOT verified; see below.) Useful for the "invented ethnicity" theme; link to Ranger/Mamdani in §12.
- Union of South Africa formed 31 May 1910 from the Cape, Natal, Transvaal and Orange River colonies. [source: USA]

## 3. Colonial economies: forced labour, cash crops, migrant labour, mining
- **Congo–Ocean Railway** (French Equatorial Africa): built 1921–1934 by the Société de Construction des Batignolles with forced labour recruited from what is now southern Chad and the CAR; deaths estimated "in excess of 17,000" workers, from accidents and disease. Exposed by André Gide (*Voyage au Congo*, 1926) and Albert Londres (*Terre d'ébène*, 1929). [source: COR] (Other published estimates run higher; say "at least 17,000".)
- **Office du Niger** (French Soudan, now Mali), founded 5 January 1932: director Émile Bélime's late-1920s plans envisaged 1.85 million hectares of irrigated land and forced relocation of 1–3.5 million peasants; "tens of thousands" were forced to work on it; by 1948 irrigation reached only ~20,000 ha worked by fewer than 23,000 farmers. [source: ODN]
- **Tanganyika groundnut scheme** (1946–51): Attlee government plan to grow groundnuts on 3 million acres for British margarine; idea of Frank Samuel of the United Africa Company; cost £36 million and "failed abjectly", abandoned 1951. Example of late-colonial "development". [source: TGS]
- **Mineral revolution, South Africa**: diamonds at Kimberley 1867; gold on the Witwatersrand 1886, founding Johannesburg — "the largest gold rush ever"; a factor in the Jameson Raid (1895–96) and the Second Boer War (1899–1902). [source: MIN; WGR]
- **Migrant labour**: the Witwatersrand Native Labour Association (WNLA, "Wenela") was the gold mines' recruiting agency, eventually with depots, buses and aeroplanes across South Africa, Basutoland, Swaziland, South West Africa, Bechuanaland, both Rhodesias, Nyasaland, Angola and Mozambique, reaching into the Belgian Congo and Tanganyika; recruits were taught Fanagalo, the mining lingua franca. [source: WNLA]
- **Copperbelt**: the mining region straddling Zambia's Copperbelt Province (Ndola, Kitwe, Chingola, Luanshya, Mufulira) and Congo's Haut-Katanga/Lualaba (Lubumbashi, Kolwezi, Likasi); photographs show Rwandan migrant workers in Congolese copper mines c. 1920. [source: CU] (No production figures verified.)
- **Cash crops by African peasants**: Ghana was the world's largest cocoa producer in the early 1960s; by the early 1980s production had dwindled "almost to the point of insignificance"; most cocoa is grown by peasant farmers on plots under three hectares; Ghana is now second to Côte d'Ivoire. [source: COC] (Origins with Tetteh Quarshie — NOT verified here.)
- **Mourides and groundnuts** (Senegal): Sufi order founded 1883 by Amadou Bamba (dates given as 1850–1927 in MOU, 1853–1927 in BAM — say "c. 1853"); French exiled Bamba to Gabon (after a hearing on 5 Sept 1895) for seven years and nine months; Mouride doctrine of salvation through work and submission to the shaykh; young disciples settled marginal land in *daara* agricultural communities to grow groundnuts, "the primary cash crop of the colonial period"; the doctrine "served French economic interests"; after WWI the order cooperated with the administration (backed Blaise Diagne's 1914 election); in 1926 Bamba began the Great Mosque of Touba, where he was buried. [source: MOU; BAM] → good example of African agency inside the colonial economy.

## 4. Religion: Christianity and Islam
- Christians in Africa: about 9 million in 1900 → ~380 million in 2000 (CIA text); another sentence says ~10 million in 1900 → 734 million in 2024. In 2020 Christians were ~49% of the continent's population, Muslims ~42%. [source: CIA] → "under ten million in 1900, over 700 million today".
- Islam: Afrobarometer surveys 2016–23 put Muslims at ~33% of the population (other estimates ~45%); Nigeria has Africa's largest Muslim population. [source: IIA] → Figures conflict with CIA's 42%; give "between a third and over two-fifths".
- **Samuel Ajayi Crowther** (c. 1809–31 Dec 1891): Yoruba, enslaved and freed by the Royal Navy (his captors' ship taken by HMS *Bann*, 1822), resettled in Sierra Leone; first African Anglican bishop in West Africa (Bishop of the Niger, 1864–1891). [source: SAC]
- **Edward Wilmot Blyden**: regarded by some as the "father of Pan-Africanism"; early articulator of the "African Personality"; *Christianity, Islam and the Negro Race* (1887) argued Islam was more unifying for Africans than Christianity. [source: EWB]
- **William Wadé Harris**: Liberian prophet; from 27 July 1913 walked from Liberia towards the Gold Coast in white robe and turban; baptised over 100,000 converts in 18 months (1913–14); hundreds of "Harris" churches in Côte d'Ivoire. [source: WWH]
- **Simon Kimbangu**: founded his movement in April 1921 in the Belgian Congo; Belgian authorities saw it as a threat to the colonial labour order and imprisoned him until his death in 1951; church recognised in 1959; claims ~20 million members (self-reported, infobox). [source: KIM]
- **Aladura** ("praying people", Yoruba): from about 1918 in western Nigeria, amid influenza, smallpox and plague epidemics; branches include Cherubim and Seraphim (Moses Tunolase and Abiodun Akinsowon), Church of the Lord (Aladura) founded 1930 by Josiah Oshitelu, Christ Apostolic Church, Celestial Church of Christ; spread to Britain. [source: ALA]
- "Ethiopian" churches in southern Africa arose from the late-19th-century Ethiopian movement, which taught that Black people should control African churches. [source: AIC]
- Pentecostal/charismatic growth: a 2006 Pew study counted 147 million African "renewalist" Christians. [source: CIA]
- John Chilembwe's rising in Nyasaland began on the evening of 23 January 1915 — covered in *First World War*; link here as a case of mission-educated Christian resistance. [source: CHU]

## 5. African intellectuals, early nationalism, Pan-Africanism
- First Pan-African Conference, London (Westminster Town Hall), July 1900, called by Trinidadian barrister Henry Sylvester Williams, "to protest stealing of lands in the colonies, racial discrimination…". Marcus Garvey ("Provisional President of Africa", Harlem parade 1922). [source: PAN] (Later congresses 1919–1945 → *Interwar*, *Decolonization*.)
- South African Native National Congress founded 8 January 1912 at Bloemfontein (renamed ANC); 1914 delegation to England incl. John Dube and Sol Plaatje. [source: ANC]
- National Congress of British West Africa founded March 1920 (Accra); J. E. Casely Hayford, Gold Coast barrister, a leading figure (vice-president); one of the earliest West African nationalist organisations. [source: NCBWA]
- Négritude (Senghor, Césaire, Damas) → already in *Interwar Period*.

## 6. Independence and the new states
- 1960: 17 colonies became independent (14 French, 2 British, 1 Belgian); independent African states rose from nine (95 million people) to 26 (180 million). [source: YOA] (matches *Decolonization*)
- OAU established 25 May 1963 in Addis Ababa (text gives both 32 states and 33 signatory governments); 1964 Cairo summit (17–21 July). [source: OAU] (The Cairo resolution accepting colonial borders — NOT verified in the fetched text; see below.)
- Nkrumah: 1964 constitutional amendment made Ghana a one-party state with him president for life; overthrown 24 February 1966; OAU founding member; Lenin Peace Prize 1962. [source: NKR]
- **Nyerere and ujamaa**: Tanganyika PM 1961–62, president 1962–64, Tanzania president 1964–5 Nov 1985 (stood down, succeeded by Ali Hassan Mwinyi, who reversed many policies); built a one-party state, used detention without trial. **Arusha Declaration** 5 February 1967 → nationalisation of banks, natural resources and major industries; villagisation forced in **Operation Vijiji 1973–75**; per Nyerere (quoted by John Shao) over 7 million moved Sept 1973–June 1975 and a further 4 million by end-1976. Social gains: infant mortality 138→110 per 1,000 (1965–85); life expectancy 37 (1960)→52 (1984); primary enrolment 25%→72% (1960–85); adult literacy 17% (1960)→63% (1975). [source: NYE; UJA]
- **Mobutu**: president 24 Nov 1965 – 16 May 1997; renamed the country Zaire; "authenticity" campaign; wealth estimates $50 million–$5 billion; rule called a kleptocracy. [source: MOB]
- **Idi Amin**: 25 Jan 1971 – 11 April 1979; expelled Uganda's ~80,000 Asians in 1972 ("economic war", from 4 Aug 1972); deaths under his regime estimated 100,000–500,000 (ICJ: no fewer than 80,000, more likely ~300,000; exile groups with Amnesty: 500,000). [source: AMIN]
- **Ethiopia**: Haile Selassie emperor 1930 – deposed 12 September 1974; died 27 August 1975. The 1983–85 famine affected 7.75 million of 38–40 million people and killed ~300,000–1.2 million; causes: drought, civil war and Derg military policies. [source: HS; ETF] (Derg/Red Terror → *Global Cold War*.)
- **Thomas Sankara**: president of Burkina Faso 4 Aug 1983 – assassinated 15 Oct 1987, succeeded by Blaise Compaoré; Marxist pan-Africanist; national literacy and vaccination campaigns (meningitis, yellow fever, measles); planted over 10 million trees against desertification; banned female circumcision, forced marriage and polygamy. [source: SAN]
- **Jerry Rawlings**: ruled Ghana June–Sept 1979 and from 31 Dec 1981 to 2001; National Democratic Congress after 1992. (Ghana's 1983 Economic Recovery Programme under Rawlings → neoliberal notes.) [source: RAW]

## 7. Coups and military rule
- Powell & Thyne data via VOA (to 3 Oct 2023): Africa has had more coup attempts since 1950 than any other region — 220, of which 109 succeeded; Sudan leads with 18 attempts (6 successful); Burkina Faso 10 attempts, 9 successful. Dataset is updated; figures are a 2023 snapshot. [source: WS — https://projects.voanews.com/african-coups/]
- "Coup Belt" (2020s neologism): coups in Mali 2020 and 2021; Chad, Guinea, Sudan 2021; Burkina Faso twice in 2022; Niger and Gabon 2023; Guinea-Bissau 2025; attempts in Benin 2025, Niger 2026. Since 1990, 21 of 27 coups in sub-Saharan Africa took place in former French colonies. [source: CB]
- Alliance of Sahel States (Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger) established 16 September 2023; anti-French, anti-"neo-colonial"; all three left ECOWAS in 2025. [source: AES] (Decolonization chapter mentions their ending military agreements with France.)

## 8. Debt and structural adjustment
- **Lagos Plan of Action** (OAU, drafted Lagos, April 1980; for 1980–2000): self-sufficiency, food security, regional industrial co-operation; proclaimed 1980–90 the "Industrial Development Decade in Africa". It was supplanted as the guiding document by the World Bank's 1981 **Berg Report**, which blamed African governments while the LPA stressed external causes. [source: LPA]
- SAPs: during the 1980s IMF/World Bank loan packages for most countries of sub-Saharan Africa (and Latin America); conditions of liberalisation, privatisation, devaluation, budget balance. [source: SAP; see neoliberal-age notes for Ghana ERP 1983 and Mkandawire & Soludo 1999]
- **HIPC Initiative** launched by IMF and World Bank in 1996 after NGO lobbying; 39 countries; by 1999 only four had received relief (critics); total cost ~$71 billion (2007 dollars) for 40 eligible countries; 37 countries completed and had external debt (to participating creditors) cancelled, the last being Somalia in 2020. [source: HIPC] (Jubilee 2000 / 2005 MDRI — not checked.)

## 9. Democratisation since 1990
- **Benin**: Mathieu Kérékou, in power by coup 1972, ruled under official Marxism–Leninism, "stripped of his powers by the National Conference of 1990"; Nicéphore Soglo PM, then president 1991; Kérékou returned by election 1996–2006. [source: KER] Benin's February 1990 conference was the first national conference in francophone Africa, five months before La Baule. [source: WS — https://www.revuepolitique.fr/?p=17147]
- **La Baule**: Mitterrand's speech to the Franco-African summit, 20 June 1990, urged multiparty democracy and press freedom and was read as tying French aid to democratic progress; commentators dispute that African democratisation "began" at La Baule. [source: WS — https://www.elysee.fr/francois-mitterrand/1990/06/20/allocution-de-m-francois-mitterrand-president-de-la-republique-sur-la-situation-economique-de-lafrique-les-possibilites-daide-des-pays-les-plus-riches-et-la-position-francaise-en-matiere-de-cooperation-et-daide-financiere-la-baule-le-20-juin-1990 ; https://www.iris-france.org/ris/le-discours-de-la-baule-anatomie-dun-mythe/]
- Bratton & van de Walle, *Democratic Experiments in Africa* (1997): political reform in some 40 of 47 sub-Saharan countries in the early 1990s; 16 democratic transitions 1991–94 (as cited by others); share of countries holding multiparty polls rose from 25% (1988) to 84% (1994) (Lindberg-related data). [source: WS — https://journalofdemocracy.com/articles/book-review-democratic-experiments-in-africa-regime-transitions-in-comparative-perspective ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/institutions-and-democracy-in-africa/elections-the-power-of-elections-in-multiparty-africa/180A5E98BC4EF19AE3BFC93EEB6CCA26] → use "most" and "about forty" rather than precise counts.
- Ellen Johnson Sirleaf: won Liberia's 2005 election, took office 16 January 2006, first elected female head of state in Africa; Nobel Peace Prize 2011. [source: EJS]
- Ibrahim Prize for Achievement in African Leadership, inaugurated 2007 by the Mo Ibrahim Foundation. [source: MIF]

## 10. The wars (brief — cross-refer)
- **Nigeria/Biafra**: war 6 July 1967 – 15 January 1970; Gowon vs Ojukwu; causes included the January 1966 coup, the counter-coup, and anti-Igbo pogroms in the North in 1966; federal blockade as deliberate policy caused mass starvation; deaths: ~100,000 military; Biafran civilian deaths from starvation estimated 500,000–2 million (text) / 500,000–3 million (infobox) — "modern experts" 500,000–1 million from famine; 2–4.5 million displaced. Images of starving children in mid-1968 drove the rise of international NGOs. [source: NCW] → say "between half a million and two million, most from starvation".
- **Sudan**: Second civil war 5 June 1983 – 9 January 2005 (Comprehensive Peace Agreement); "roughly two million" died (infobox 1–2 million), mostly from starvation; 4 million southerners displaced; one of the longest civil wars on record (continuation of the first war, 1955–72). South Sudan independent 9 July 2011 after a 98.8% vote in the January 2011 referendum; civil war 2013–2018 (Kiir vs Machar). [source: SSCW; SSD] Darfur and the post-2023 war → *Modern Conflicts*.
- **Congo**: Congo crisis 5 July 1960 – 25 Nov 1965 [source: CC]; Second Congo War 2 Aug 1998 – 18 July 2003, nine African nations, ~25 armed groups, "Africa's World War" [source: SCW] — death toll debate in *Modern Conflicts*.
- **Rwanda**: see *Modern Conflicts* figures above; colonial identity cards (§2).
- Ebola, West Africa: December 2013 – June 2016; 11,310 deaths. [source: EBO]
- HIV/AIDS: Africa (about 15% of world population) accounted for more than two-thirds of infections worldwide. [source: HIV] → details belong to *Health and Healing*.

## 11. Africa today
- **Population**: more than 1.5 billion (2024; article also gives 1.59 billion in 2026), ~22% of humanity; passed 1 billion in 2009; UN projections ~2.5 billion by 2050 (26%) and 3.8 billion by 2100 (37%); DR Congo projected from 51 million (2000) to 431 million (2100). [source: DEM] Median age about 19 (UN WPP 2024 via Pew; other sources 18.8–20.2), the youngest continent; Europe ~43. [source: WS — https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/05/19/5-facts-about-africas-population-growth/]
- **Urbanisation**: ≤11% urban in 1900; 14.7% in 1957; 37.2% in 2000 (UN 2002). [source: URB] Lagos 17–21 million (Nov 2025 estimates), Africa's most populous urban area; capital moved to Abuja (seat of government 12 Dec 1991). Kinshasa ~18.5 million (2026 est.), largest nominally francophone city in the world (Lingala the street lingua franca); 2.6 million in 1984. [source: LAG; KIN]
- **Economy**: Africa's nominal GDP ~$3.56 trillion (2026 forecast). [source: ECA] "Africa Rising" (*Economist* Dec 2011) reversed its May 2000 cover "The Hopeless Continent". [source: WS — https://www.howwemadeitinafrica.com/how-the-economist-changed-its-tune-on-africa/] (growth figures → *Globalization*).
- **African Union**: Sirte Declaration 9 Sept 1999; Constitutive Act adopted at Lomé 2000; launched 9 July 2002 in Durban by first chair Thabo Mbeki; 55 member states (some suspended); Peace and Security Council 2004; member of the G20 (admitted 2023 per post-Cold War notes). [source: AU]
- **AfCFTA**: agreement signed 21 March 2018 in Kigali; in force 30 May 2019 after 22 ratifications; trading officially (largely symbolically) began 1 January 2021; 43 parties plus 11 signatories; largest free-trade area by member states after the WTO, covering 1.3 billion people. [source: AFC] (Party counts change; say "almost every African state has signed".)
- **China in Africa**: China–Africa trade $1 billion (1980), $39.7bn (2005), $55bn (2006); China Africa's largest trading partner since 2009 (overtaking the US); trade rose 700% in the 1990s; Forum on China–Africa Cooperation established 2000 (ministerial meetings Beijing 2000, Addis Ababa 2003; summit Beijing 2006). TAZARA railway (Zambia–Tanzania, 1,860 km) built with Chinese finance 1970–75, with some 50,000 Chinese engineers and workers. Debt-trap debate: Kenyan economist Anzetse Were attributes "debt-trap" narratives partly to weak fiscal transparency and bargaining position of African states. [source: ACR] Deborah Brautigam's research (incl. 2019 analysis of 3,000+ projects) finds no evidence of deliberate Chinese "debt-trap diplomacy". [source: WS — https://thechinaproject.com/2019/05/01/no-evidence-for-chinas-debt-trap-diplomacy-american-researchers/]
- **Mobile money**: M-Pesa launched 2007 by Vodafone and Safaricom in Kenya; Suri and Jack (*Science*, 2016): access "lifted 194,000 households, or 2% of Kenyan households, out of poverty" — widely cited; providers criticised for high fees for poor users. [source: MP]
- **Culture**: Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* published London (Heinemann) 17 June 1958, translated into 50+ languages. Wole Soyinka, Nobel Prize in Literature 1986, first African laureate. Nollywood: *Living in Bondage* (1992, Igbo-language, dir. Chris Obi Rapu) usually taken as starting the video-film boom (disputed as "first"). Afrobeats: umbrella term for West African pop from Nigeria, Ghana and the UK in the 2000s–2010s, distinct from Fela Kuti's 1970s Afrobeat. [source: TFA; SOY; NOL; AFB]
- Memory politics: Rhodes Must Fall (UCT) — Rhodes statue removed 9 April 2015; aims of "decolonisation of education". [source: RMF]

## Debates
- **Was colonialism an "episode" or an epoch?** J. F. Ade Ajayi (Ibadan School), "Colonialism: An Episode in African History" (in Gann & Duignan, eds, *Colonialism in Africa 1870–1960*, vol. 1, 1969), argued for continuity: colonialism as one episode in the longer flow of African history. Peter Ekeh challenged this, stressing colonialism's transforming force ("episode vs epoch"). [source: WS — https://dacb.org/stories/nigeria/ade-ajayi/ ; https://journalpub.escholarship.org/ufahamu/article/55654/galley/42042/download]
- **Recovering African history**: Hegel (1837) and Trevor-Roper (1963) denied Africa a history; the Ibadan School (Kenneth Dike, "father of modern African historiography", and J. F. Ade Ajayi) used archives plus oral tradition; the Dar es Salaam School (Terence Ranger) presented modern nations as continuations of the African past; Vansina's *Oral Tradition* (1961); Cheikh Anta Diop in Dakar; UNESCO *General History of Africa* from 1981. [source: AH]
- **Underdevelopment**: Rodney (1972) introduced dependency theory to African historiography: "Africa developed Europe at the same rate as Europe underdeveloped Africa". Rodney was killed by a car bomb in Georgetown, Guyana, 13 June 1980, aged 38. Economists such as Nathan Nunn argue a substantial part of Africa's current underdevelopment stems from the long-term effects of the slave trades. [source: HEU; ROD; AH; NUN]
- **The colonial state and ethnicity**: Mamdani — the post-colonial state cannot be understood without the colonial state's bifurcation into citizen and "native" subject (decentralised despotism). Ranger — co-editor with Hobsbawm of *The Invention of Tradition* (1983), whose Africa chapter is the root of the "invented tribes/traditions" argument; earlier *Revolt in Southern Rhodesia, 1896–97* (1967). Rwanda's identity cards illustrate how categories hardened. [source: CAS; MAM; RAN; RWA]
- **Why are African states weak?** Cooper's "gatekeeper state" (states strong at the point of contact with the world economy, weak within; *Africa since 1940*, 2002); Jeffrey Herbst, *States and Power in Africa* (2000), on the difficulty of projecting power over low population densities (Luebbert award 2001; the argument summary is from general knowledge — NOT verified beyond the title). [source: COO; HER]
- **Africa's economic story**: The *Economist*'s "Hopeless Continent" (May 2000) vs "Africa Rising" (Dec 2011); its editor later acknowledged the 2000 predictions were wrong; Bond's critique (*Globalization*). "Afro-pessimism" was rejected by many it was applied to, who preferred "Afro-realism". [source: WS — howwemadeitinafrica.com; AP]
- **Structural adjustment**: World Bank/Berg Report (domestic policy failures) vs Lagos Plan/Mkandawire & Soludo (external shocks; deindustrialisation; cuts to health and education). [source: LPA; neoliberal notes]
- **Ujamaa**: real social gains (literacy, health) vs forced villagisation, detention without trial and economic failure. [source: NYE; UJA]
- **China in Africa**: "debt-trap diplomacy" narrative vs Brautigam's evidence; African agency in negotiating loans (Were). [source: ACR; WS]
- **Democratisation**: external push (end of Cold War, La Baule, donor conditionality) vs internal drivers (Benin's national conference before La Baule, protest movements); Bratton & van de Walle. [source: WS as §9]
