## 1. France: background
- M68 places May 68 against the *Trente Glorieuses* (1945–75): rapid growth and urbanisation, but overcrowded universities, rigid academic structures, stagnant wages and poor conditions for workers; de Gaulle's Fifth Republic seen by many as outdated. [source: M68]
- February 1968: the Communist Party (PCF) and the Socialists (SFIO) agreed the "February Declaration" to try to form a joint government. [source: M68]
- De Gaulle's state held a monopoly of radio and television through the ORTF; in May 1968 every government minister was male. [source: DG]
- Nanterre, 22 March 1968: about 150 students (with far-left groups and a few poets and musicians) occupied an administration building at the Nanterre campus of the University of Paris; this gave its name to the "Movement of 22 March". [source: M68]
- Daniel Cohn-Bendit (b. 4 April 1945, Montauban), born stateless to German Jewish parents who fled Nazi Germany in 1933; German citizen from 1959, French only from 2015; known in 1968 as "Dany le Rouge" (for his politics and red hair). France 24's headline (2015) calls him the "expelled leader of May 1968". Later a Green MEP (1994–2014). Controversy over his 1970s writings on sexuality and children resurfaced in 2001 and 2013. [source: COH]
- Situationist International and the slogans: "Sous les pavés, la plage!" (Under the paving stones, the beach) — M68 attributes it to student Bernard Cousin with PR man Bernard Fritsch; other slogans: "Il est interdit d'interdire", "L'imagination au pouvoir", "Soyez réalistes, demandez l'impossible", "Élections, piège à con", "CRS = SS". [source: M68]

## 2. France: the student revolt (May)
- 2 May: Nanterre closed by the administration. 3 May: students met at the Sorbonne to protest the closure and threatened expulsions; police entered the Sorbonne. [source: M68]
- 6 May: UNEF (national student union) and the university teachers' union marched; >20,000 marched toward the sealed-off Sorbonne; police baton charges, first barricades and paving stones, tear gas, hundreds arrested. [source: M68]
- Night of 10–11 May ("night of the barricades"): crowd on the Left Bank (Latin Quarter) blocked by the CRS from crossing the river, threw up barricades; police attacked at 2:15 am; hundreds of arrests and injuries; broadcast live on radio, aftermath on TV next day. Public sympathy swung to the students. [source: M68]
- 13 May: CGT and Force Ouvrière called a one-day general strike and demonstration; "well over a million" marched in Paris (M68 lead; M68 body also has a union march figure of 200,000 — sources inconsistent; say "hundreds of thousands, by some counts over a million"). Pompidou announced release of prisoners and reopening of the Sorbonne, which students then occupied as an autonomous "people's university"; the Odéon theatre was also occupied. About 401 popular action committees formed. [source: M68]

## 3. France: the general strike
- 14 May: workers at the Sud-Aviation plant near Nantes began a sit-down strike/occupation, holding the director captive (left-leaning chronologies); Renault-Cléon followed 14 or 15 May (sources differ), then Renault-Billancourt; by c. 16 May Renault's plants were occupied. [source: WebSearch results incl. https://libcom.org/article/general-strike-france-1968-factory-factory-account and https://www.marxists.org/archive/cliff/works/1968/france/2-workers.htm — partisan sources; dates approximate]
- Size: M68 lead says "about 10 million workers, two-thirds of the labor force … the largest general strike in French history"; M68 body says "up to one-third of the country's workforce"; Kristin Ross's publisher blurb says 9 million. **Give "some 7–10 million; roughly 9–10 million at the peak is the usual figure"** and avoid the fraction. [source: M68; https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/M/bo3644914.html]
- Strikes spread to all sectors — state jobs, manufacturing, services, management, administration. Workers' demands differed from students'. [source: M68]
- Immigrant workers (North African, Portuguese, sub-Saharan African) were among the most militant strikers in some plants (e.g. Citroën), also demanding an end to segregated company housing; women workers' and immigrant women's role is under-documented in English. [source: general-audience summary found via WebSearch, https://qualifrance.pages.dev/may-1968-student-protests-france/beyond-the-stereotypes.html — treat as a lead; state cautiously]
- Deaths: 24 May (night), Lyon police inspector René Lacroix crushed by a truck sent into police lines; in Paris a 26-year-old demonstrator stabbed in a fight among demonstrators (infobox: 2 dead, 1,000+ injured, 1,000+ arrested that night). 10 June: Gilles Tautin, 17-year-old lycéen, drowned in the Seine near Meulan while supporting strikers at Renault-Flins (circumstances disputed — fleeing police). 11 June: a further death in clashes at Peugeot-Sochaux (contemporary Swiss press). [source: M68; https://www.infosyvelines.fr/articles/flins-le-drame-de-gilles-tautin-une-page-sombre-de-mai-68; https://doc.rero.ch/record/212310/files/1968-06-13.pdf]

## 4. France: Grenelle, de Gaulle's disappearance, elections
- Grenelle agreements: negotiated 25–26 May (concluded 27 May, never signed) at the Ministry of Social Affairs, rue de Grenelle, between the Pompidou government, unions and employers; negotiators included young junior minister Jacques Chirac and CGT leader Georges Séguy. Terms: minimum wage (SMIG) +35%, wages +10%, union sections recognised in firms (law of 27 Dec 1968). Rejected by the rank and file; strikes continued. [source: GRE; M68]
- Charléty stadium rally (27 May; UNEF and CFDT), about 22,000. 28 May: Mitterrand declared "there is no more state" and offered to form a government; 29 May Mendès France likewise. [source: M68]
- 29 May: de Gaulle postponed the cabinet, secretly removed his papers and vanished; for over six hours no one knew where he was (Pompidou: "He has fled the country!"). He flew to Baden-Baden to General Jacques Massu, head of French forces in Germany; Massu persuaded him to return. DG adds that in return for army support de Gaulle agreed to an amnesty for the 1961 putschists/OAS. Massu kept the loss of nerve secret until 1982. [source: M68; DG]
- To Pompidou on the morning of 29 May: "I am the past; you are the future; I embrace you." [source: M68]
- 30 May: 400,000–500,000 CGT marchers ("Adieu, de Gaulle!"). At 4:30 pm de Gaulle broadcast (radio) his refusal to resign, dissolved the National Assembly and called elections; then about 800,000 supporters marched on the Champs-Élysées (planned for days by Gaullists). Pompidou had persuaded him to dissolve by threatening to resign. [source: M68; ELE]
- De Gaulle's private phrase "La réforme oui, la chienlit non" (reform yes, chaos/"shit-in-bed" no). [source: DG]
- Police chief Maurice Grimaud credited with avoiding bloodshed by restraint. [source: M68]
- Police retook the Sorbonne 16 June; several far-left groups banned. [source: M68]
- Elections 23 and 30 June 1968: absolute majority for the Gaullist Union for the Defence of the Republic — the first single-party absolute majority in French republican history; left's worst postwar result. Seat counts vary by source: M68 "353 of 486" (PCF 34, FGDS 57); DG "352 of 487"; ELE infobox 354 (359 with allies) of 487. **Use "about 350 of 487 seats"; PCF 34, Socialist-led FGDS 57.** (GRE's "293 of 378" and "elections on 30 June" are wrong — ignore.) Pompidou resigned as PM in July, replaced by Couve de Murville. [source: ELE; M68; DG]
- Post-crisis Dogan survey: majorities found de Gaulle "too old to govern" (59%), "too authoritarian" (64%). He resigned at noon on 28 April 1969 after losing a referendum on Senate and regional reform; Pompidou elected two months later. [source: M68; DG]
- Long-term: wage gains, union rights, increased education spending; contributed to feminist, environmentalist and LGBTQ activism (M68 lead tagged "not in body" — state generally). [source: M68]

## 5. Czechoslovakia and the Eastern bloc
- Prague Spring: began 5 January 1968 when Alexander Dubček replaced Novotný as First Secretary; Ludvík Svoboda became president 22 March (after Novotný resigned). April: "Action Programme" (press, speech, travel freedoms) and "socialism with a human face". 27 June: Ludvík Vaculík's manifesto *Two Thousand Words*. Talks at Čierna nad Tisou (29 Jul–1 Aug) and Bratislava Declaration (3 Aug). [source: PRA; INV]
- Invasion night of 20–21 August 1968 ("Operation Danube"): about 250,000 troops initially, rising to about 500,000, from the USSR, Poland, Hungary and Bulgaria; Romania and Albania refused; East German participation cancelled hours before. 137 Czechoslovaks killed, ~500 seriously wounded. Dubček arrested, taken to Moscow, signed the Moscow Protocol (all but František Kriegel signed). ~70,000 fled immediately; ~300,000 emigrated before 1989. [source: INV]
- Nonviolent resistance (removing street signs, fraternisation, defying curfew). Federation into Czech and Slovak republics was the only reform that survived. April 1969 Husák replaced Dubček; "normalization"; Dubček expelled from party, made a forestry official. 1987: asked the difference between Prague Spring and Gorbachev's reforms, a Soviet spokesman said "Nineteen years." [source: PRA]
- 25 August 1968: eight demonstrators sat down at Lobnoye Mesto on Red Square with banners incl. "For your freedom and ours" (among them Natalya Gorbanevskaya, Pavel Litvinov, Larisa Bogoraz); quickly arrested. [source: RSQ]
- Ryszard Siwiec (b. 1909), Polish accountant and ex-Home Army member, set himself on fire at the harvest festival in Warsaw's 10th-Anniversary Stadium on 8 September 1968 before ~100,000 people and party leaders, protesting the invasion; died 12 September; suppressed by the press. [source: SIW]
- Jan Palach (11 Aug 1948 – 19 Jan 1969), Charles University student, self-immolated in Wenceslas Square on 16 January 1969, died three days later; demands: end censorship, stop the occupiers' paper *Zprávy*, general strike. Funeral became mass protest; Jan Zajíc followed 25 Feb 1969. "Palach Week" (15–21 Jan 1989) a prelude to the Velvet Revolution. [source: PAL]
- Poland, March 1968: ban on Mickiewicz's *Dziady* (last performance 30 January) → protest; 4 March expulsion of Adam Michnik and Henryk Szlajfer from Warsaw University; 8 March rally (500–1,000) attacked by ORMO "worker activists" and ZOMO; protests spread nationwide; repressed. An "anti-Zionist" (antisemitic) campaign led by interior minister Gen. Mieczysław Moczar with Gomułka's approval followed the Six-Day War (Gomułka's 19 June 1967 "fifth column"-type speech); at least 13,000 Poles of Jewish origin emigrated 1968–72, losing citizenship. Ivan Krastev: West's '68 was about individual sovereignty, the East's about national sovereignty. [source: POL]
- Yugoslavia: 2–9 June 1968, Belgrade University students' seven-day strike/occupation (first mass protest in postwar Yugoslavia), smaller protests in Sarajevo, Zagreb, Ljubljana; complaints about unemployment and economic reform forcing workers abroad. Tito on TV, 9 June: the "students are right", conceded some demands; later prisons and firings for leaders (e.g. Vladimir Mijanović, Lazar Stojanović). [source: BEL — thinly sourced article; keep claims minimal]

## 6. Mexico
- Movement began after police/grenadier violence against students in late July 1968; police blew open the 18th-century door of the San Ildefonso preparatory school with a bazooka. 1 Aug: UNAM rector Javier Barros Sierra lowered the flag to half-mast and led a march of perhaps 50,000. National Strike Council (CNH) formed; Silent March 13 September. Army occupied campuses in September (Santo Tomás/IPN fighting). [source: MEX; TLA]
- 2 October 1968, Plaza de las Tres Culturas, Tlatelolco, ten days before the Olympics opened: ~10,000 gathered; flares from a helicopter/foreign-ministry tower; the Olympia Battalion (white gloves/handkerchiefs) and ~5,000 soldiers. Chant: "¡No queremos olimpiadas, queremos revolución!" Documents released since 2000–01 indicate Presidential Guard snipers fired first to provoke the army. ~1,345 arrested (DFS head). [source: TLA]
- **Death toll (range):** next-day newspapers 20–28; Kate Doyle (National Security Archive) documented 44 named/identified deaths (34 identified + 10 unidentified per Crónica); common estimates 300–400 (TLA infobox 350–500); the US State Department estimated 150–200 (EBSCO summary). **Write: "officially a few dozen; documented at least 44; most estimates several hundred (c. 300–400)".** [source: TLA; https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB201/; https://www.cronica.com.mx/nacional/2026/10/01/masacre-de-tlatelolco-cuantos-muertos-hubo-el-2-de-octubre-de-1968/]
- President Díaz Ordaz; interior minister Luis Echeverría (later president) charged with genocide in 2006, charges dismissed 2009. Senate made 2 October a national day of mourning (from 2009); President Claudia Sheinbaum issued an official apology on 2 October 2024. Elena Poniatowska's *La noche de Tlatelolco* (English *Massacre in Mexico*). [source: TLA]
- Mexico City Olympics, 16 October 1968: Tommie Smith (gold, 19.83 s world record) and John Carlos (bronze) raised black-gloved fists during the US anthem; Australian Peter Norman (silver) wore an Olympic Project for Human Rights badge in solidarity; shoeless, black socks for poverty; Brundage forced their expulsion from the Games (they were not made to return medals). Smith later called it a "human rights" salute. [source: OLY]

## 7. Brazil
- 28 March 1968: military police killed 18-year-old student Edson Luís de Lima Souto at the Calabouço student restaurant in Rio. [source: BRA; AI5]
- 26 June 1968: March of the One Hundred Thousand (Passeata dos Cem Mil) in Rio — students, artists, intellectuals, clergy; banner "Down with the Dictatorship. The people in power"; speech by student leader Vladimir Palmeira. [source: BRA]
- 13 December 1968: AI-5 issued by President Costa e Silva — closed Congress, suspended habeas corpus for political crimes, prior censorship, "the coup within the coup"; ended 1 January 1979. [source: AI5]
- Caetano Veloso's "É Proibido Proibir" translated the Paris slogan. [source: M68]

## 8. Africa
- Senegal (Dakar), May–June 1968: students at the University of Dakar struck against cuts in grants (sources: monthly grant payments reduced from 12 to 10 a year) and called an unlimited strike/exam boycott (start date given as 18 or 27 May); police/army stormed the campus (~29 May), one student killed by most accounts, hundreds (one account >900) arrested and some sent to a military camp at Ouakam; unions declared a general strike 30 May; "the government was tottering" (historian Omar Gueye); Senghor blamed outside influences; movement recurred in 1969. [source: WebSearch summary of https://www.versobooks.com/blogs/news/3880-may-1968-in-senegal and https://roape.net/?p=18804 — figures vary; SEN Wikipedia article is low quality (its "28 demonstrators killed" is unsupported — do NOT use)]
- South Africa: protests and a nine-day sit-in at the University of Cape Town (from 15 Aug 1968) after the council, under apartheid-government pressure, rescinded Archie Mafeje's appointment. [source: PRO]
- Jamaica: October 1968 Rodney riots after the government banned historian Walter Rodney from returning to UWI. [source: PRO]

## 9. Asia
- Pakistan: movement from early November 1968 (Rawalpindi students' clash with police, after NSF "Demands Week" from 7 Oct 1968) to March 1969; students, workers, lawyers and peasants; PAK says 10–15 million took part. Mahbub ul Haq (21 April 1968) identified 22 families controlling 66% of industry and 87% of banking. Deaths per Mubashir Hasan: 239 (196 East, 43 West Pakistan). In East Pakistan, the uprising (Sheikh Mujibur Rahman freed 22 Feb 1969 when the Agartala Conspiracy Case was withdrawn; student Asaduzzaman killed 20 Jan; Matiur Rahman 24 Jan). Ayub Khan resigned 25 March 1969, handing power to army chief Yahya Khan. [source: PAK; EPK]
- Japan: *daigaku funsō*. University of Tokyo medical students struck in early 1968 over unpaid internships; Nihon University: 10,000 of 86,000 students demonstrated (May 1968) over ¥2 billion unaccounted funds. Zenkyōtō (All-Campus Joint Struggle Committees) formed July 1968 at Tokyo University (leader Yoshitaka Yamamoto); 21 October 1968 Shinjuku riot (International Anti-War Day). 18–19 January 1969: thousands of police retook Yasuda Hall; Tokyo University's 1969 entrance exams cancelled; occupied campuses rose from 33 to 77 by March and 111 by April 1969; Act on Temporary Measures concerning University Management (passed May–Aug 1969). Helped give rise to Tanaka Mitsu's *ūman ribu* (women's liberation). [source: JPU; ZEN]

## 10. United States
- Tet Offensive from 30–31 January 1968 (see Vietnam notes; one sentence only).
- Martin Luther King Jr. shot at the Lorraine Motel, Memphis, 4 April 1968, 6:01 pm; James Earl Ray pleaded guilty (99 years). Riots ("Holy Week Uprising") in over 100 (110) cities, 4–11 April: 43 dead, 3,000+ injured, 20,000+ arrested. Robert F. Kennedy assassinated June 1968. [source: MLK; KR]
- Columbia University: SDS and the Student Afro-American Society (SAS) protests over the university's ties to the Institute for Defense Analyses and the "Gym Crow" gymnasium in Morningside Park; occupation of Hamilton Hall from 23 April (Black students asked white SDS students to leave); NYPD cleared buildings 30 April — over 700 arrested; gym and IDA ties dropped. [source: COL]
- Chicago Democratic National Convention, 23–29 August 1968: 7,000–10,000 protesters; the Walker Report (National Commission on Violence) called it a "police riot"; chant "The whole world is watching" (28 Aug, live TV). [source: DNC]
- Miss America protest, Atlantic City, 7 September 1968: c. 200 women (New York Radical Women; Robin Morgan, Carol Hanisch …), "Freedom Trash Can" for bras, girdles, make-up etc.; "Women's Liberation" banner unfurled in the hall. The "bra-burning" trope came from reporter Lindsy Van Gelder's analogy with draft-card burning. [source: MAP]

## 11. West Germany
- 2 June 1967: student Benno Ohnesorg shot in the back of the head by policeman Karl-Heinz Kurras at a protest against the Shah's visit, West Berlin; Kurras acquitted Nov 1967; in 2009 revealed as a Stasi informer and SED member (no evidence he acted on orders; case closed 2011). [source: OHN]
- *Bild* response: "Students threaten: We shoot back." Springer press controlled ~two-thirds of West Berlin's leading media. [source: DUT]
- International Vietnam Congress, West Berlin, 17–18 Feb 1968 (3,000–4,000 attendees; 12,000–15,000 in the march). [source: GSM]
- 11 April 1968: Rudi Dutschke (b. 1940, raised in East Germany) shot by far-right Josef Bachmann; survived with brain damage; attacks on Springer offices followed. Dutschke died 24 December 1979 (seizure in the bath) after being elected a delegate to the Greens' founding congress. [source: DUT; GSM]
- Emergency Acts passed 30 May 1968; SDS dissolved 21 March 1970; Baader and Ensslin's Frankfurt department-store arson (2 April 1968) → Red Army Faction. Habermas accused Dutschke of "left fascism" (1967), later called it an overreaction. [source: GSM]

## 12. Italy
- Battle of Valle Giulia, Rome, 1 March 1968: ~4,000 students vs police; 148 police and 478 students injured, 228 arrested. In May 1968 all universities except Bocconi occupied. [source: VAL; ITA]
- "Maggio strisciante" (creeping May): term for the drawn-out spread of student and industrial conflict through 1968–69, students allying with factory workers (FIAT Turin, Pirelli Milan) — culminating in the "Hot Autumn" of 1969 (wildcat strikes, many workers migrants from the South; 40-hour week and wage gains won). [source: HOT; WebSearch results incl. https://libcom.org/library/11-breaking-ice-spring-summer-1968]
- 12 December 1969: neo-fascist bomb at the Banca Nazionale dell'Agricoltura, Piazza Fontana, Milan — 17 killed, 88 injured; beginning of the "Years of Lead". [source: PF; HOT]

## 13. Northern Ireland
- NICRA formed 29 January 1967. 5 October 1968 Derry march (organised by the Derry Housing Action Committee; banned by minister William Craig): RUC batoned marchers, trapped between police lines — images broadcast worldwide; considered the movement's birthdate. [source: NIC]
- 18 November 1968: about 1,000 workers, "mostly young women from some half dozen [shirt] factories", walked out and marched through Derry. 22 November: O'Neill's reforms (Derry Corporation dissolved, housing points system). [source: NIC]
- People's Democracy (Queen's University Belfast, formed after 9 Oct 1968), led by Bernadette Devlin and Michael Farrell, modelled its assemblies on the Sorbonne. Burntollet Bridge ambush, January 1969: 87 marchers hospitalised. [source: NIC]

## 14. Afterlives: New Left, feminism, environmentalism
- New Left: rose after 1956 among Communist dissenters (term from France's *nouvelle gauche*, Claude Bourdet); Herbert Marcuse called its "father"; prioritised social justice and lifestyle freedoms over purely economic goals. [source: NL]
- Second-wave feminism: conventionally dated from Friedan's *The Feminine Mystique* (1963); built on Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949); narratives critiqued for centring white middle-class women. [source: SWF]
- France: MLF "born" 26 August 1970 when nine women (incl. Christine Delphy, Monique Wittig) tried to lay a wreath at the Arc de Triomphe to the Unknown Soldier's wife ("There is more unknown than the unknown soldier: his wife"). [source: MLF]
- Mexico: Elaine Carey argues that women's mobilisation in 1968 set the stage for 1970s Mexican feminism. [source: https://notevenpast.org/plaza-sacrifices-gender-power-and-terror-1968-mexico-2005/]
- Environmentalism: 1968 saw the founding of the Club of Rome; first Earth Day 22 April 1970, ~20 million Americans. [source: PRO; ED]
- Cohn-Bendit and Dutschke both ended among the Greens. [source: COH; DUT]

## Debates
- **Failure or cultural victory?** Politically May 68 ended in a Gaullist landslide; culturally it is credited with feminism, environmentalism, LGBTQ activism, new social movements, and changed authority relations. M68: for some it means liberation, for others the danger of anarchy. [source: M68; ELE]
- **Kristin Ross** argues the dominant memory (sociologists, repentant ex-leaders, media) reduced May to a cultural, generational youth revolt, erasing the 9 million strikers, police violence and deaths, and the anti-imperialist influences of Algeria and Vietnam. [source: https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/M/bo3644914.html]
- **Labour reading vs revolution:** Michael Seidman sees strikers pursuing traditional bread-and-butter demands; Arthur Mendel/Dogan (in M68) stress the PCF was "moderate, nonviolent and essentially antirevolutionary". [source: M68; WebSearch ILWCH]
- **Global 1968:** Suri links the protests to the great powers' turn to détente; Krastev contrasts Western individual sovereignty with Eastern national sovereignty; the Routledge Handbook decentres the West ("between protest and nation-building"). Vinen extends the "long '68" and stresses the radicals' enemies. [source: SUR summary; POL; Routledge; Vinen reviews]
- **Conservative/hostile readings:** Sarkozy 2007 called for "liquidating" its legacy; critics (Daniel Bensaïd) said he scapegoated May 68 for the effects of liberal reform; others call Sarkozy himself "the most soixante-huitard" president. [source: https://www.politis.fr/articles/2007/07/mai-68-le-bel-heritage-1708/; https://www.slate.fr/story/45451/sarkozy-mai-68]
- **Memory in the East and South:** Tlatelolco records opened only after 2000; Palach's grave dug up by the StB (1973); Siwiec suppressed until 1989 — 1968's afterlife was contested by states. [source: TLA; PAL; SIW]
