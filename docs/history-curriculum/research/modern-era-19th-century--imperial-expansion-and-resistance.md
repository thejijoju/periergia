# Verified facts — Imperial Expansion and Resistance (researched 2026-11-11)

Scope note for the writer: this chapter is comparative (British, French, German, Belgian/Leopoldian, Italian, Dutch, US, Japanese) and centres the colonised as actors. The separate "British Empire" chapter covers the Raj, the dominions and the empire's balance sheet in depth; use the British material here only where it serves comparison (Zulu, Asante, Sudan, Māori, 1857 in brief).

Source key: WP = English Wikipedia (raw wikitext read via curl on 2026-11-11). URLs are given in full per fact.

## The scale and shape of the Scramble
- As late as the 1870s Europeans controlled about 10% of Africa, almost all on the coasts (Portuguese Angola and Mozambique, the British Cape Colony, French Algeria). By 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia were outside European control. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa]
- Hochschild (quoted on WP) puts it differently: about 80% of Africa's land area was still under Indigenous rulers when Leopold began looking for a colony (late 1870s). Treat "10%" and "80% still under African rule" as roughly the same picture, not as precise figures. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
- Between 1885 and 1914, by share of Africa's population: Britain nearly 30%, France 15%, Portugal 11%, Germany 9%, Belgium 7%, Italy 1%. By area France led, but much of its territory was the sparsely peopled Sahara. Nigeria alone had 15 million subjects, more than all of French West Africa or the whole German colonial empire. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa]
- By the end of the 19th century Europe had added almost 9 million square miles (about one-fifth of the world's land area) to its overseas possessions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa]
- Germany by 1914 held 2.6 million km² of colonies with 14 million colonial subjects, mostly in Africa (South West Africa, Togoland, Kamerun, Tanganyika/German East Africa). Bismarck at first disliked colonies but gave way to popular and elite pressure in the 1880s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa]
- Liberia, founded by the American Colonization Society (1822) and independent from 26 July 1847, kept its independence partly because European powers treated it as under US protection. It lost territory to its neighbours during the Scramble. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa]
- Anti-slavery as a justification: virtually every colonial regime claimed to be suppressing slavery and the slave trade (the Brussels Anti-Slavery Conference of 1889–90 made this its central theme). In French West Africa over one million enslaved people left their masters between 1906 and 1911, after conquest and abolition. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa]
- The Brussels Conference Act of 1890 also cut the arms trade to Africans. The British stopped selling breech-loaders to Samori Touré under it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samori_Ture]
- Human zoos: between 1877 and 1912 about thirty "ethnological exhibitions" were staged at the Jardin d'Acclimatation in Paris, and "Negro villages" were shown at the 1878 and 1900 Paris World's Fairs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa]

## The Berlin Conference 1884–85: what it did and did not do
- Fourteen countries attended, convened by Bismarck at Leopold II's request. It opened on 15 November 1884 and closed on 26 February 1885 with the General Act of Berlin. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference]
- No African state was invited. Sultan Barghash of Zanzibar asked to send a representative, the only African ruler to do so, and was refused (British anti-slavery campaigners lobbied against Zanzibar's participation). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference]
- What it DID:
  - It declared free trade for the signatories across a broadly defined "Congo Basin" and free navigation on the Congo and Niger rivers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference]
  - It confirmed the territories held by Leopold's International Association of the Congo. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference]
  - It codified a "principle of effective occupation" (Article 35) for new claims on the African coast, with a duty to notify the other signatories. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference]
  - In Article 6 the powers pledged to "watch over" Indigenous peoples and to strive to suppress slavery. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference]
- What it did NOT do:
  - It did not draw Africa's map or "carve up" the continent. A 2024 study (cited on WP) found that the only borders set at the conference were those of the Congo region, and these were later revised. Most African borders took their final form more than two decades later, through many bilateral treaties. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference]
  - Apart from what became the Congo Free State, the General Act created no new colonies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference]
  - The name "Congo Free State" does not appear in the General Act. It was announced on 1 August 1885, and Leopold's sovereignty was settled afterwards in Belgium, not at Berlin. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference]
  - "Effective occupation" applied only to coasts. Article 34 let powers claim hinterlands from a coastal base, and the British view prevailed that protectorates need not be directly governed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference]
- Six of the 14 attendees came home with no African zones of interest: Austria-Hungary, Russia, Denmark, the Netherlands, Sweden-Norway and the United States. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference]
- Historians Sybil Crowe, Matthew Craven and Simon Katzenellenbogen warn against overstating Berlin's role. One Katzenellenbogen chapter is titled "It didn't happen at Berlin" (1996). Wm. Roger Louis and Prosser Gifford conceded that "the Berlin Act did have a relevance to the course of the partition." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference]

## Technologies of conquest
- Quinine:
  - French chemists Pelletier and Caventou first isolated quinine from cinchona bark in 1820. Large-scale use as malaria prophylaxis began around 1850. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine]
  - It is credited with ending Africa's reputation as the "white man's grave". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine]
  - Its origins were Indigenous Andean. Cinchona bark was used by Quechua peoples, and the name comes from Quechua quina ("bark"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine]
  - In 1865 the Aymara/Bolivian collector Manuel Incra Mamani supplied high-quinine seeds to Charles Ledger, which ended up with the Dutch government. Mamani was arrested in 1871 and beaten so badly that he died soon after. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine]
  - By the 1930s Dutch plantations on Java produced 97% of the world's quinine. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine]
- Maxim gun:
  - Invented in 1884 by the American-born Hiram Maxim (1840–1916) in Hatton Garden, London, it was the first fully automatic machine gun. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxim_gun]
  - It fired about 550–600 rounds a minute, said to equal 60 riflemen, and was usually crewed by 4–6 men. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxim_gun]
  - Maxim became a British subject in 1899 and was knighted in 1901. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiram_Maxim]
  - First uses in Africa: Stanley's Emin Pasha Relief Expedition carried a prototype (1886–90), and Germany's Wissmann expedition used one against the Abushiri revolt (1888). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxim_gun]
  - At the Battle of the Shangani (First Matabele War, 25 October 1893), 700 soldiers with five Maxims fought off 5,000 Ndebele warriors. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxim_gun]
  - Caveat on WP itself: the Maxim's power "has often been embellished by popular myth". It was decisive in pitched battles in open ground (Matabele wars, Omdurman), and much of its effect was psychological. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxim_gun]
  - Africans and Asians also acquired the weapon. The Philippine Republic had 42 Maxims in January 1899. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxim_gun]
- Steamships, railways and the telegraph enabled expansion. Steam navies also needed coaling stations and ports, which added a strategic motive for seizing coastal bases. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa]
- Daniel Headrick's *The Tools of Empire* (1981) treats technology as one element in expansion, "not a sufficient explanation." His later *Power over Peoples* (2010) stresses that technological superiority did not guarantee success. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_R._Headrick]
- Counter-example to the technology thesis: at Adwa the Ethiopian army was mostly armed with modern rifles imported from France and Russia. Samori's blacksmiths learned to repair and even build French Gras rifles, though not in large numbers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Adwa] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samori_Ture]

## Ideologies
- On the "civilizing mission" (*mission civilisatrice*), WP calls it a hallmark of the French project. Its premise was that Europe had a duty to bring "civilisation". Japan applied the same idea in Korea. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilizing_mission]
- Jules Ferry told the Chamber of Deputies that "the superior races" have a right "because they have a duty… the duty to civilize the inferior races". See the Quotations section for the DATE CONFLICT (1885 vs 1884). Georges Clemenceau replied on 30 July 1885. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jules_Ferry] [source: https://www.herodote.net/Jules_Ferry_1832_1893_-synthese-2193.php]
- Kipling's "The White Man's Burden" was published in *McClure's Magazine* in February 1899. It was written to urge the United States to take the Philippines. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_White_Man%27s_Burden]
- Social Darwinism: historian Mohamed Adhikari lists "social Darwinist ideology that placed the racial struggle over practical considerations such as the value of indigenous labor" among the reasons for von Trotha's conduct in Namibia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide]
- Hans-Ulrich Wehler's "social imperialism": elites used imperial jingoism to win working-class support at home. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Imperialism]

## Ethiopia: Wuchale, Adwa, Menelik II and Taytu Betul
- Menelik II (born 1844, died 12 December 1913) was king of Shewa from 1866 and Emperor from 1889; he was crowned on 3 November 1889. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelik_II]
  - Before Adwa he had conquered southern lands of Oromo, Wolayta and Gurage peoples. He financed arms with ivory, coffee, gold and slave-trade revenues and took Harar in 1887. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelik_II]
  - Ethiopia was therefore itself an expanding empire. It was not only a victim of imperialism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelik_II]
- The Treaty of Wuchale (1889) was written in two versions. In the Italian text, Article 17 obliged Ethiopia to conduct its foreign relations through Italy, which amounted to a protectorate. In the Amharic text, using Italy's good offices was optional. Menelik formally rejected the treaty in 1891. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Adwa] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelik_II]
- In September 1895 Menelik issued an *awaj* (a general call to arms). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Adwa]
- Battle of Adwa, 1 March 1896: [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Adwa]
  - Ethiopian strength is estimated at 73,000 to over 100,000.
  - Italian combat strength is estimated at 14,519 (Marcus) to 17,770 (D. L. Lewis), the latter including 7,104 Eritrean askaris.
  - Italian and colonial dead were about 6,000 (Berkeley gives 6,133; Caulk about 5,900), with about 3,800 captured.
  - Ethiopian dead are estimated at 3,886 (Caulk, citing contemporary figures) to 7,000 (Berkeley), with 6,000–10,000 wounded.
  - Give casualty figures as ranges.
- Italian commander Oreste Baratieri attacked under pressure from Rome, after his brigadiers argued against retreat. The Crispi government fell as a result. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Adwa]
- Grim aftermath: about 800 captured Eritrean askaris, regarded as traitors, had their right hand and left foot amputated. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Adwa]
- The Treaty of Addis Ababa (1896) made Italy recognise Ethiopian independence. Ethiopia stayed independent until Fascist Italy's invasion of 1935–36, and the occupation lasted 1936–41. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Adwa]
- Adwa became a rallying point for African nationalists and Pan-Africanists. Adwa Victory Day is a public holiday in Ethiopia. Bahru Zewde: "few events in the modern period have brought Ethiopia to the attention of the world as has the victory at Adwa". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Adwa]
- Taytu Betul (about 1851 – 11 February 1918) was Empress from 1889 to 1913. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taytu_Betul]
  - She and Menelik founded Addis Ababa in 1886.
  - She led a hard line against Italy over Wuchale.
  - At Adwa she commanded her own force: WP's order of battle lists her contingent from Semien as 3,000 rifles, 600 horses and 4 guns, and Pankhurst gives 5,000 infantry.
  - When Menelik considered retreat during the battle, Taytu and Ras Mengesha persuaded him to commit his 25,000-man reserve.
  - She was forced from power in 1910. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taytu_Betul] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Adwa]

## Samori Touré and the Wassoulou (Samorian) state
- Samori Touré was born about 1830 and died 2 June 1900 in exile in Gabon. A Malinke (Mandinka) Muslim cleric and soldier, he founded a state spanning parts of present-day Guinea, Sierra Leone, Mali, Côte d'Ivoire and Burkina Faso. He resisted France from 1882 until his capture in 1898. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samori_Ture]
- Origins: his father was a Dyula weaver and merchant. He entered military service to free his mother, who had been captured in 1853. He proclaimed himself *faama* in 1878, made Islam the state religion and took the title *almamy* in 1884. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samori_Ture]
- Diplomacy and arms:
  - He bought breech-loaders through British Freetown, and in January 1885 offered to put his state under British protection.
  - He signed treaties with France (Kenieba Koura 1886, Bissandougou 1887, Niako 1889). He regarded the French protectorate in these as unenforceable.
  - In 1887 he could field 30,000–35,000 infantry and about 3,000 cavalry. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samori_Ture]
- Darker side: forced conversion and destruction of sacred sites in 1885 provoked rebellion, which was brutally suppressed. His 15-month siege of Sikasso (1887–88) failed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samori_Ture]
- Strategy: from 1891 he fought a war of manoeuvre and scorched earth and moved his whole state eastward. By 1893 he had about 12,000 infantry and 2,000 cavalry, with a moving retinue of about 120,000 people. This "second empire" was centred on Kong, which he destroyed in May 1897 after it revolted. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samori_Ture]
- He defeated a British column at Dokita and Wa in 1897. The French captain Gouraud captured him without a fight at Guelemou on 29 September 1898, and he was exiled to Gabon. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samori_Ture]
- He was the great-grandfather of Ahmed Sékou Touré, Guinea's first president. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samori_Ture]

## The Zulu kingdom and Isandlwana (1879)
- On 11 December 1878 High Commissioner Sir Bartle Frere, on his own initiative, sent King Cetshwayo an ultimatum he could not accept (disband the army, accept a British resident). Disraeli's government did not want the war. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Isandlwana] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Zulu_War]
- The war ran from 11 January to 4 July 1879. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Zulu_War]
- Isandlwana, 22 January 1879: [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Isandlwana]
  - About 20,000 Zulu under Ntshingwayo kaMahole defeated part of Chelmsford's column. The British force numbered about 1,800 troops plus about 350 civilians, and the Zulu used the "horns of the buffalo" envelopment.
  - More than 1,300 on the British side died, including 343 of the Natal Native Contingent. Zulu dead are estimated at 1,000–3,000.
  - WP calls it the British Army's worst defeat against an Indigenous foe with vastly inferior technology.
  - The Zulu impi covered more than 80 km in five days, against Chelmsford's roughly 16 km in ten days.
- After the war Britain captured Cetshwayo, exiled him to Cape Town, and divided Zululand into 13 chiefdoms under compliant chiefs. This led to a Zulu civil war. Zululand became a protectorate and was annexed in 1887. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Zulu_War]
- Bishop John Colenso of Natal was the Zulu kingdom's notable European defender. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Zulu_War]

## The Asante and Yaa Asantewaa (1900)
- British troops had occupied Asante in January 1896 and exiled the Asantehene Prempeh I. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Golden_Stool]
- In Kumasi on 25 March 1900 Governor Frederick Hodgson demanded the Golden Stool so that he could sit on it. Caveat: the wording of his speech survives only through an Asante translator, and WP flags its source. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Golden_Stool]
- Yaa Asantewaa (about 1840 – 17 October 1921), Queen Mother of Ejisu, led the rising. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaa_Asantewaa]
  - The British and their allies were besieged in the Kumasi fort, defended by Hausa troops with four Maxim guns.
  - A relief force under James Willcocks broke the siege.
  - In 1901 Britain annexed Asante as a Crown Colony and exiled Yaa Asantewaa and other leaders to the Seychelles, where she died in 1921.
  - The British never captured the Golden Stool. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Golden_Stool] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaa_Asantewaa]
- Cost: about 1,000 dead on the British and allied side. Asante dead are given only as "several thousand". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Golden_Stool]

## The Mahdist state in Sudan
- Muhammad Ahmad (1843/44 – 22 June 1885, of typhus) proclaimed himself the Mahdi in June 1881 against Turco-Egyptian rule, which had begun in 1821. His followers were the Ansar. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ahmad] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahdist_State]
- Late in 1883 the Ansar destroyed an 8,000-man Egyptian force under the British officer William Hicks at Shaykan. They took Khartoum on 26 January 1885, killing Gordon. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ahmad] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahdist_State]
- The state lasted from 1885 to 1899, with its capital at Omdurman. It was ruled by the Khalifa Abdallahi ibn Muhammad after the Mahdi's death. WP notes that Sudan's population declined by about half under Mahdist rule; treat this as a contested estimate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahdist_State]
- Omdurman (Karary), 2 September 1898: [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Omdurman]
  - Kitchener's Anglo-Egyptian army lost 47–48 dead.
  - The Mahdist side lost about 12,000 killed, 13,000 wounded and 5,000 captured.
  - The killing of wounded Mahdists provoked controversy (Ernest Bennett in the *Contemporary Review*). Churchill wrote *The River War* (1899).
- Afterwards the Mahdi's tomb was destroyed and his bones thrown into the Nile. Kitchener was said to have kept the skull (WP: "said to have"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ahmad]
- The Khalifa died fighting at Umm Diwaykarat in November 1899. Holdouts continued in Darfur until 1909. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahdist_State]

## Algeria and Abd al-Qadir
- The trigger was the "Fan Affair" of 29 April 1827, when the Dey Hussein struck the French consul Deval with a fly-whisk. A three-year blockade followed, then the landing at Sidi Ferruch on 14 June 1830 with 34,000 troops. Conquest ran from 1830 to 1903, with the main phase from 1830 to 1847. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_conquest_of_Algeria]
- Abd al-Qadir (born between 1806 and 1808, died 26 May 1883 in Damascus) was a Sufi (Qadiriyya) scholar's son. On 22 November 1832, aged about 25, he was chosen *amir al-mu'minin* to lead resistance. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emir_Abdelkader]
  - France recognised him in the Desmichels Treaty (26 February 1834) and the Treaty of Tafna (May 1837, with Bugeaud). He used the truce to build a state in the interior. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emir_Abdelkader] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_conquest_of_Algeria]
  - Bugeaud's response was scorched earth and fast columns. The French raided his mobile capital (the *smala*) in 1843. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_conquest_of_Algeria]
  - He surrendered in 1847 and was detained in France for almost five years. In 1860 in Damascus he intervened to protect Christians during the massacre, which won him international honours. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emir_Abdelkader]
- The 1871 Mokrani revolt in Kabylia drew in 250 tribes, nearly a third of Algeria's population. There were about 130,000 colonists in 1871 and over a million by the century's end (WP's figure; treat with care). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_conquest_of_Algeria]
- Death toll: several historians estimate 500,000 to 1 million Algerian deaths from war, famine and disease in the first three decades, out of an estimated population of about 3 million. Some call the violence genocidal; this is debated. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_conquest_of_Algeria]

## King Leopold's Congo Free State (1885–1908)
- Leopold II (9 April 1835 – 17 December 1909) was King of the Belgians from 1865 and personal sovereign of the Congo Free State from 1885 to 1908. In 1860 he gave a minister a paperweight inscribed "Belgium needs a colony". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_II_of_Belgium] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
- Front organisations and Stanley: Leopold created the International African Association (1876) as a supposedly scientific and humanitarian body, and hired Stanley in 1878. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
- Rubber boom: the inflatable tyre (about 1890) drove demand. Decrees of 1891–92 declared "vacant" land state property, about 99% of the country by one account, and handed concessions to companies such as ABIR and the Société Anversoise. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
  - The state's domain revenue rose from roughly 150,000 francs (1890) to more than 18 million francs (1901). Stengers called rubber a "miraculous deliverance" from near-bankruptcy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
- Force Publique: formed in 1888, it had more than 19,000 soldiers by about 1900 and consumed more than half the budget. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
- Terror methods: hostage-taking, the *chicotte* (hippo-hide whip), and severed hands as proof of cartridges used. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
  - The ABIR quota was 3–4 kg of dried rubber per adult man per fortnight.
  - In 1899 the African-American missionary William Sheppard was shown 81 smoked right hands.
  - Alice Seeley Harris photographed Nsala of Wala with the severed hand and foot of his five-year-old daughter (1904).
- Congolese testimony: in the 1950s missionaries Boelaert and Hulstaert, with Congolese colleague Charles Lonkama, recorded survivors' accounts. One is Tswambe on the officer Fiévez ("the Devil of the Equator"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
- Critics and campaigners: [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
  - The African-American George Washington Williams published an open letter to Leopold in 1890 and used the phrase "crimes against humanity" in a letter to the US Secretary of State.
  - The House of Commons condemned the Free State in May 1903.
  - Roger Casement, British consul at Boma, toured the upper Congo and reported in December 1903. A revised report reached the Free State in February 1904.
  - E. D. Morel led the Congo Reform Association, and his *Red Rubber* appeared in 1906. Supporters included Twain, Conrad and Conan Doyle.
  - The Congo Reform Association did not oppose colonialism as such. It sought Belgian annexation.
- Belgium annexed the Congo on 15 November 1908. Reports say the palace furnaces burned Congo papers for more than a week before the handover (Hochschild via WP, "said to"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Leopold%27s_Ghost]
- Morel (10 July 1873 – 12 November 1924) was French-born. As a clerk at the shipping firm Elder Dempster he noticed rubber coming in and guns going out. He later became a Labour MP, defeating Winston Churchill in 1922. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._D._Morel]
- Casement (1 September 1864 – 3 August 1916) was honoured in 1905 for the Congo report and knighted in 1911 for his Putumayo (Peru) rubber investigation. He was executed for treason over the 1916 Easter Rising. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Casement]
- POPULATION LOSS: give as a range, never a single figure. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
  - Modern estimates of the decline run from 1.2 million to 10 million.
  - No census existed before 1924, when the count was about 10 million.
  - Hochschild's *King Leopold's Ghost* (1998) used about 10 million, following the 1919 Belgian commission's estimate that about half the population perished. WP calls this "on the high range". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Leopold%27s_Ghost]
  - Demographer Jean-Paul Sanderson (2020) argues for 1 to 5 million, with the lower end most plausible.
  - Congolese historian Isidore Ndaywel è Nziem first estimated about 13 million, then 5–10 million, then revised further down from 2008.
  - Jan Vansina abandoned his earlier 50% estimate. He found the Kuba population was rising from 1880 to 1900, and violence killed under 5% in his local study.
  - Disease was the main direct killer: sleeping sickness (an estimated 500,000 deaths in 1901 alone), smallpox, dysentery and influenza. Social disruption from the rubber regime worsened it.
- On "genocide": most historians reject the legal term for the Congo because there was no intent to exterminate a group. Hochschild and Nzongola-Ntalaja reject it, though Nzongola-Ntalaja calls it "the Congo holocaust". Others use comparisons with genocide. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
- *King Leopold's Ghost* was rejected by nine of ten US publishers, won the 1999 Duff Cooper Prize, and had more than 600,000 copies in print by 2013. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Leopold%27s_Ghost]

## Herero and Nama genocide (German South West Africa, 1904–08)
- Background: [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide]
  - Germany claimed the territory in 1884.
  - Governor Leutwein (1894–1904) used divide and rule.
  - The 1890s rinderpest epidemic destroyed Herero cattle.
  - Settler impunity for murder and rape was a key grievance.
- On 12 January 1904 Samuel Maharero launched the uprising. Between 123 and about 160 Germans were killed, mostly farmers and traders, and the rebels generally spared women, children and missionaries. There were only 770 German soldiers in the colony. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide]
- Lothar von Trotha arrived on 11 June 1904. On 11 August the Herero were defeated at Waterberg and fled into the Omaheke desert, where Germans guarded or poisoned waterholes. About 40,000 died there (Adhikari). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide]
- Trotha's extermination order of 2 October 1904: "Within the German boundaries, every Herero, with or without firearms, with or without cattle, will be shot. I won't accommodate women and children anymore." (WP translation.) Historians debate whether he intended killing or expulsion. The Kaiser withdrew the order on 6 December 1904. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide]
- The Nama under Hendrik Witbooi had been German allies but switched sides in September 1904. Witbooi was killed on 29 October 1905. Trotha issued a second order against the Nama on 22 April 1905. Jakob Marengo and Simon Kooper fought on, and Kooper accepted a paid exile in 1908. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide]
- Concentration camps, including Shark Island: about half the prisoners died. Some skulls were sent to Germany for "racial science". The camps closed on 27 January 1908. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide]
- Deaths: about 40,000–80,000 Herero (about 80% of their pre-war population) and about 10,000 Nama (about half). Older and press figures often say "65,000 Herero". Use the range. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide] [source: https://www.npr.org/2021/05/28/1001233776/germany-officially-recognizes-it-committed-genocide-in-present-day-namibia]
- The German official history boasted that "the Herero ceased to exist as a tribe". The 1907 ordinances banned Herero from owning land or cattle and required them to wear numbered metal tags. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide]
- Memory: [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide]
  - Britain's 1918 Blue Book documented the genocide. Britain recalled and destroyed copies in 1926.
  - East German historian Horst Drechsler (1960s) was the first to call it genocide.
  - Maharero's funeral on 26 August 1923 became an annual Herero commemoration.
- Germany's acknowledgements: [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide] [source: https://www.npr.org/2021/05/28/1001233776/germany-officially-recognizes-it-committed-genocide-in-present-day-namibia] [source: https://www.dawn.com/news/396178]
  - 14 August 2004, Okakarara: Development Minister Heidemarie Wieczorek-Zeul accepted moral responsibility. Whether this was an official apology is disputed.
  - 2015: the government described the events as genocide.
  - 28 May 2021: Foreign Minister Heiko Maas announced Germany would "officially refer to these events as what they are from today's perspective: genocide", and would ask forgiveness.
  - The 2021 deal is €1.1 billion of *ex gratia* development aid over 30 years. It explicitly rejects legal responsibility and reparations.
  - Most Herero and Nama organisations rejected the deal. In 2023 the Landless People's Movement and traditional leaders sued in Namibia to nullify parliament's approval. Henning Melber calls the joint statement "a soft version of denialism". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide]

## Maji Maji (German East Africa, 1905–07)
- Causes: [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maji_Maji_Rebellion]
  - Head taxes (1898) and forced labour.
  - Governor Götzen's 1902 order that villages grow cotton for export under quotas, in land often unsuited to cotton.
  - Drought in 1905.
- The rising began in July 1905 among the Matumbi; Kinjikitile's entry dates the start to 20 July, and WP has the Samanga attack on 31 July. It spread to the Ngindo, Yao, Ngoni and others. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maji_Maji_Rebellion] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinjikitile_Ngwale]
- The spirit medium Kinjikitile Ngwale ("Bokero"), possessed by the snake spirit Hongo, gave out *maji* (water mixed with castor oil and millet) said to turn bullets to water. He was hanged on 4 August 1905. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinjikitile_Ngwale]
- At Mahenge, machine guns broke the attacks. The Ngoni threw away their medicine crying "The maji is a lie!" The last resistance ended in August 1907. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maji_Maji_Rebellion]
- Scorched-earth famine was deliberate. Captain Wangenheim wrote: "Only hunger and want can bring about a final submission." Total dead were 75,000–300,000, overwhelmingly from famine. Scholars such as A. Dirk Moses describe the tactics as genocidal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maji_Maji_Rebellion]
- Historiography: [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maji_Maji_Rebellion]
  - John Iliffe calls it a "mass movement [which] originated in peasant grievances, was then sanctified and extended by prophetic religion, and finally crumbled".
  - Götzen's 1909 history notably omitted his own cotton scheme.
  - Tanzanian nationalists later claimed it as the first stirring of Tanzanian unity. Later historians stress that it was a series of distinct revolts.

## French Indochina and Vietnamese resistance
- In 1858 France attacked Tourane (Da Nang) on the pretext of the persecution of missionaries. Spain supplied 300 Filipino soldiers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina]
- The Treaty of Saigon (5 June 1862) ceded territory and legalised Catholicism. A protectorate over Cambodia followed in 1863. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina]
- Tonkin was conquered from 1882 to 1885. The Treaty of Huế (1883) and the Sino-French War (August 1884 – April 1885) ended China's claims to suzerainty. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%E1%BA%A7n_V%C6%B0%C6%A1ng_movement]
- The Indochinese Union was formed in 1887, and Laos was added after the 1893 Franco-Siamese crisis. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina]
- The Cần Vương ("Aid the King") movement ran from 1885 to 1896. The regent Tôn Thất Thuyết rose in the name of the boy-emperor Hàm Nghi. Other leaders included Phan Đình Phùng and Hoàng Hoa Thám. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%E1%BA%A7n_V%C6%B0%C6%A1ng_movement]
- Hàm Nghi (3 August 1871 – 14 January 1944) was captured in 1888 and exiled to Algeria. He died in Algiers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%A0m_Nghi]
- Phan Bội Châu (1867–1940) founded the Duy Tân Hội in 1904. He sought Japanese help through Liang Qichao and Ōkuma Shigenobu, which bridged into 20th-century nationalism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phan_B%E1%BB%99i_Ch%C3%A2u]

## The Dutch in Aceh (1873–1904, and after)
- The 1871 Anglo-Dutch Sumatra Treaty gave the Dutch a free hand. Aceh's 1873 talks with the US consul in Singapore served as the pretext. The first expedition, under Köhler, landed in March 1873, and Köhler was killed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aceh_War]
- The war cost 15–20 million guilders a year and nearly bankrupted the colonial government. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aceh_War]
- Leadership passed to the *ulama*, such as Teungku Chik di Tiro. Resistance drew on the Hikayat Prang Sabi (holy-war literature). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aceh_War]
- Teuku Umar (1854 – 11 February 1899) took Dutch aid in 1894, then turned on them in 1896; the Dutch called this "the treason of Teukoe Oemar". He was killed at Meulaboh. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aceh_War] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teuku_Umar]
- His widow Cut Nyak Dhien (about 1848 – 6 November 1908) led the guerrillas until her capture in 1905. She died in exile in Sumedang and is a National Hero of Indonesia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cut_Nyak_Dhien]
- Counter-insurgency: the Islamic scholar Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje advised splitting the *uleebelang* aristocracy from the ulama, and Van Heutsz became governor in 1898. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aceh_War]
- In the 1904 campaign van Daalen killed at least 2,922 Acehnese, including 1,149 women and children, for 26 Dutch dead. Kuta Reh fell on 14 June 1904. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aceh_War]
- Losses: about 37,000 on the Dutch side (2,200 European, including cholera deaths) and 60,000–70,000 Acehnese. The Sultanate surrendered in 1903–04, but guerrilla resistance continued until 1942. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aceh_War]

## The United States in the Philippines (1899–1902, and Moro war to 1913)
- Filipinos proclaimed independence in June 1898. The First Philippine Republic was founded under the Malolos Constitution, promulgated 21 January 1899, with Emilio Aguinaldo as president. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War]
- Spain ceded the islands in the Treaty of Paris (10 December 1898). Andrew Carnegie offered $20 million to buy Philippine independence. McKinley proclaimed "benevolent assimilation" on 21 December 1898. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War]
- War began on 4 February 1899. Aguinaldo decreed guerrilla war on 13 November 1899. He was captured at Palanan on 23 March 1901 by Funston, with Macabebe Scouts. The US declared the war over on 4 July 1902, but the Moro war ran until 1913. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War]
- At Balangiga, Samar, on 28 September 1901, townspeople killed 54 US soldiers. General Jacob H. Smith then ordered troops to "kill everyone over the age of ten" and make Samar a "howling wilderness". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balangiga_massacre]
- Casualties: over 4,200 American dead and over 20,000 Filipino combatants (US State Department). Filipino civilian deaths were 200,000–250,000, mostly from famine and disease, including at least 150,000 in the 1902 cholera epidemic. Some estimates reach a million or more. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War]
- Domestic opposition: the American Anti-Imperialist League published soldiers' letters. The US called the conflict the "Philippine Insurrection". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War]

## The New Zealand Wars (1845–1872)
- These were fought between the colonial government with allied Māori (*kūpapa*) and the Māori who resisted. They were once called the "Māori Wars" or "Land Wars"; James Belich popularised "New Zealand Wars" in the 1980s. An earlier Māori name was *te riri Pākehā* ("the white man's anger"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Wars]
- At the peak, 18,000 British troops faced about 4,000 Māori fighters. Māori engineered *pā* with anti-artillery bunkers, which made frontal assaults costly (Ōhaeawai 1845, Rangiriri 1863, Gate Pā 1864). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Wars]
- The Kīngitanga (Māori King movement) of the 1850s resisted land sales. The Waikato invasion (1863–64) cost about 700 British and colonial dead and about 1,000 Māori. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Wars]
- The New Zealand Settlements Act 1863 led to confiscation of about 1.2–1.5 million hectares from "loyal" and "rebel" tribes alike. Total deaths: 745 on the government side and 2,154 Māori (WP infobox). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Wars]
- Later resistance leaders include Tītokowaru and Te Kooti. A 2013 Waitangi Tribunal report found East Coast Māori suffered proportionately the heaviest losses. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Wars]

## China's responses
- Self-Strengthening Movement (about 1861–1895), led by Prince Gong, Zeng Guofan, Li Hongzhang and Zuo Zongtang: [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-Strengthening_Movement]
  - The Zongli Yamen foreign office and the Tongwen Guan language school (1862) were founded.
  - Arsenals such as Jiangnan were built, followed by shipping, telegraphs and railways.
  - The phrase "self-strengthening" was used by Feng Guifen in 1861.
- Defeat by Japan in 1894–95 ended with the Treaty of Shimonoseki (17 April 1895): independence for Korea, cession of Taiwan, the Penghu Islands and the Liaodong peninsula, and an indemnity of 200 million taels. The Triple Intervention forced Liaodong's return. Taiwanese resisted, and Japan did not take control until October 1895. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Shimonoseki]
- Boxer Uprising (1899–1901): [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxer_Rebellion]
  - It arose in Shandong amid flood and drought, missionary privilege and German seizure of Jiaozhou after the 1897 Juye Incident.
  - The slogan "Support the Qing, destroy the foreigners" (扶清滅洋) was first used in October 1898.
  - Cixi issued a de facto declaration of war on 21 June 1900. Southern governors, including Li Hongzhang, refused to join (the Mutual Defence Pact of Southeast China).
  - The 55-day siege of the Beijing legations was lifted on 14 August 1900 by the Eight-Nation Alliance (Britain, US, France, Germany, Italy, Austria-Hungary, Russia, Japan).
  - Looting, executions and punitive expeditions followed.
  - About 32,000 Chinese Christians and 200 Western missionaries were killed in north China, and about 100,000 people died in total.
  - The Boxer Protocol (7 September 1901) imposed an indemnity of 450 million taels, more than a year's tax revenue, payable over 39 years, plus foreign troops in Beijing.
- Kaiser Wilhelm II's "Hun speech" to troops leaving for China was delivered on 27 July 1900. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxer_Rebellion]
- Historiography: Joseph Esherick, *The Origins of the Boxer Uprising* (1987), and Paul Cohen, *History in Three Keys* (1997). Mainland Chinese textbooks have presented the Boxers as anti-imperialist patriots; Taiwanese and Hong Kong texts present them as irrational. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxer_Rebellion]

## Japan's response: from target to imperial power
- Perry's "black ships" arrived in 1853–54, and the Ansei treaties (1858) imposed extraterritoriality. The Meiji Restoration was proclaimed on 3 January 1868. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Japanese_Treaty_of_Commerce_and_Navigation]
- The Anglo-Japanese Treaty of Commerce and Navigation (signed 16 July 1894, in force 17 July 1899) began the end of the unequal treaties and extraterritoriality in Japan. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Japanese_Treaty_of_Commerce_and_Navigation]
- Japan then became a colonial power itself: Taiwan in 1895, and Korea, where it too claimed a "civilizing mission". It was one of the eight Boxer-intervention powers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Shimonoseki] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilizing_mission] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxer_Rebellion]

## 1857 in India (brief, for comparison only; full treatment in British Empire chapter)
- The rising began on 10 May 1857 at Meerut and lasted until 1 November 1858. Rebels proclaimed the 81-year-old Mughal Bahadur Shah Zafar emperor. Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi was killed fighting. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Rebellion_of_1857]
- The name is contested: "Sepoy Mutiny", "Indian Mutiny", "Great Rebellion" or "First War of Independence". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Rebellion_of_1857]

## From resistance to nationalism (connective tissue)
- Adwa became a rallying point for African nationalists and Pan-Africanists. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Adwa]
- Tanzanian nationalists claimed Maji Maji as the first experience of national unity. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maji_Maji_Rebellion]
- Herero representatives invoked memory of the genocide against South African apartheid rule. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide]
- Phan Bội Châu reorganised Cần Vương remnants into a modernising nationalist movement. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phan_B%E1%BB%99i_Ch%C3%A2u]
- Samori's great-grandson Sékou Touré led Guinea to independence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samori_Ture]
- Pankaj Mishra's *From the Ruins of Empire* opens with Tsushima (May 1905) as a moment of Asian intellectual awakening. [source: https://www.dawn.com/news/763205]

## Motives and theories
- J. A. Hobson, *Imperialism: A Study* (1902). Hobson argued that the "taproot of imperialism" was capitalist oligarchy and maldistribution of wealth: underconsumption at home pushed capital abroad. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperialism_(Hobson_book)]
  - Caveats: the book alludes antisemitically to financiers "of a single and peculiar race", and advocates repression of "degenerate or unprogressive races".
  - D. K. Fieldhouse called his arguments superficial.
- Lenin, *Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism* (originally "the Newest Stage"), was written in 1916 and published in 1917. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperialism,_the_Highest_Stage_of_Capitalism]
  - It describes monopoly, the merger of bank and industrial capital into finance capital, and the export of capital.
  - It describes the "final partition" of the world, 1876–1900, and inter-imperial war.
  - It argues that monopoly profits "bribe" an upper layer of workers.
  - It drew heavily on Hobson but rejected his view that capitalism could be separated from imperialism.
  - In chapter VII, Lenin's briefest definition is that imperialism is "the monopoly stage of capitalism". [source: https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1916/imp-hsc/ch07.htm (located via search; page not opened, see NOT VERIFIED)]
- Critique of the accumulation theory: it fails to explain capital-poor imperialists such as Italy, and Russia and the US were net borrowers. European capital investment in tropical Africa was small. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Imperialism]
- Gallagher and Robinson, "The Imperialism of Free Trade", *Economic History Review* 6:1 (1953), pp. 1–15. They argued for continuity from the 1850s: Britain preferred informal empire through free trade and turned to formal rule only when that failed. Key wording is in Quotations. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Imperialism_of_Free_Trade]
- Robinson and Gallagher with Alice Denny, *Africa and the Victorians: The Official Mind of Imperialism* (1961). It explains the Scramble geopolitically: protecting the route to India, Egypt in 1882, and crises on the periphery. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Imperialism_of_Free_Trade] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Robinson]
- Robinson, "Non-European foundations of European imperialism: sketch for a theory of collaboration", in Owen and Sutcliffe (eds.), *Studies in the Theory of Imperialism* (Longman, 1972). Indigenous collaboration was a formative and continuing condition of empire, which puts non-Europeans at the centre of explanation. [source: https://econbiz.de/Record/non-european-foundations-of-european-imperialism-sketch-for-a-theory-of-collaboration-robinson-ronald/10002713796] [source: https://mail.berghahnbooks.com/downloads/intros/BuehrerCooperation_intro.pdf]
- Cain and Hopkins' "gentlemanly capitalism": City of London finance and services, not industry, drove British expansion. Set out in articles in 1986–87 and *British Imperialism* (1993). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentlemanly_capitalism]
- Other frames: Wallerstein's world-systems theory, and Bernard Porter's view of formal empire as a symptom of British relative decline. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Imperialism]

## Did empire pay? Profits and costs
- Leopold's Congo was hugely profitable for the king and the concessionaires: domain revenue went from about 150,000 to over 18 million francs (1890–1901). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
- Germany's wars were ruinously expensive. The Reichstag's refusal of funds for the South West Africa war triggered the "Hottentot election" of January 1907. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide]
- The Aceh War cost 15–20 million guilders a year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aceh_War]
- In 1901 Lloyd George attacked the Golden Stool war's expense in Parliament. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Golden_Stool]
- Davis and Huttenback, *Mammon and the Pursuit of Empire: The Political Economy of British Imperialism, 1860–1912* (Cambridge, 1986). Findings, from publisher and secondary summaries: [source: https://www.cambridge.org/0521236118] [source: https://heltaher.github.io/heltaher/history-analysis/the-imperial-balance-sheet/post-03/]
  - Empire investment did not dominate British capital markets.
  - Returns were not high compared with domestic and foreign investment, and there was no evidence of persistently exploitative profits.
  - Empire profits came at substantial cost to the taxpayer, especially for defence.
  - Empire worked as an income transfer from the tax-paying middle class to the elites who owned imperial enterprise.
- Patrick O'Brien, "The costs and benefits of British imperialism 1846–1914", *Past and Present* 120 (1988), 163–200, asked whether the empire benefited most Britons. He stressed the fiscal burden of defence. [source: https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/20156]
- The colonised paid many of the costs through hut and head taxes, forced labour and indemnities. Examples include the Asante war-cost indemnity Hodgson demanded and the Boxer indemnity. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Golden_Stool] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxer_Rebellion]

## Debates
- Berlin as "carve-up" versus process. The traditional view is that Berlin partitioned Africa. Crowe, Craven and Katzenellenbogen ("It didn't happen at Berlin") stress the bilateral treaties before and after it; a 2024 study finds only Congo borders were set there. Critics answer that many earlier agreements were never enforced. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference]
- Why empire? [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperialism_(Hobson_book)] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Imperialism_of_Free_Trade] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Imperialism] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentlemanly_capitalism] [source: https://econbiz.de/Record/non-european-foundations-of-european-imperialism-sketch-for-a-theory-of-collaboration-robinson-ronald/10002713796]
  - Economic: Hobson's surplus capital; Lenin's monopoly and finance capital.
  - Strategic and "official mind": Robinson and Gallagher, with crises at the periphery such as Egypt in 1882.
  - Informal empire: also Robinson and Gallagher, criticised by D. C. M. Platt (1968) and Martin Lynn, who says local economies resisted British penetration.
  - Gentlemanly capitalism: Cain and Hopkins.
  - Social imperialism: Wehler.
  - Collaboration and peripheral theory: Robinson 1972, which centres African and Asian actors.
- Did it pay? Davis and Huttenback (1986) and O'Brien (1988) see modest returns, high costs to taxpayers and gains for elites. Leopold's Congo shows extreme private profit from coerced labour. [source: https://www.cambridge.org/0521236118] [source: https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/20156]
- Congo death toll and "genocide". Estimates range from Hochschild's ~10 million and Ndaywel's first ~13 million down to Sanderson's 1–5 million. Vansina stresses disease. Most historians do not apply the legal term genocide, but many describe atrocity on a vast scale. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
- Namibia and the Holocaust ("From Windhoek to Auschwitz?"). Zimmerer and others see continuities. Critics such as Bomholt Nielsen warn this revives the *Sonderweg* thesis and hides parallels with other empires. Historians also debate whether Trotha aimed at extermination or expulsion (Häussler versus Adhikari on motives). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide]
- Was the 2021 German–Namibian deal reconciliation? Most Herero and Nama organisations rejected it as made over their heads and without legal liability. Melber calls it "a soft version of denialism". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herero_and_Nama_genocide]
- Maji Maji: one unified proto-nationalist movement (Tanzanian nationalist school) or a series of separate revolts? Iliffe's synthesis: peasant grievance, prophetic religion, then fragmentation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maji_Maji_Rebellion]
- Algeria: whether conquest-era mortality was "genocidal" is contested. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_conquest_of_Algeria]
- Technology: Headrick's tools thesis versus the Maxim "myth". Adwa, Isandlwana and the Māori pā show organisation, numbers, terrain and imported arms could beat European technology. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_R._Headrick] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxim_gun] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Wars]
- Boxers: patriots, xenophobes or event, experience and myth (Cohen)? Chinese historiography has itself shifted. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxer_Rebellion]
- Resisters were not simple victims. Menelik expanded by conquest in the south. Samori used forced conversion and enslavement. The Mahdist state was harsh and many welcomed its fall. Many Africans served colonial armies (Eritrean askaris, Hausa troops, Force Publique, Macabebe Scouts, kūpapa). The writer should present this complexity without false equivalence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelik_II] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samori_Ture] [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahdist_State]
