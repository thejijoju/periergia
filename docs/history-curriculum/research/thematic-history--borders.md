## 1. Words and concepts: frontier, border, boundary, march, borderland
- "Frontier" came into English from French in the 15th century with the meaning "borderland", the region of a country that fronts on another country. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frontier]
- "March" comes from a Proto-Indo-European root *merg- ("edge, boundary"), which also gave Latin *margo* ("margin"), Old English *mearc* and Persian/Armenian *marz* ("borderland"). A margrave was a count (*graf*) of a march. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_(territory)]
- In premodern times many borders were not clearly defined lines but zones claimed and fought over by both sides ("marchlands"). Modern survivals included the Saudi–Iraqi neutral zone (1922–1991) and the Saudi–Kuwaiti neutral zone (1922–1970). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border]
- Medieval examples of marches: the Welsh Marches (*marchia Wallia*) ruled by Norman Marcher Lords, as against *pura Wallia*; Charlemagne's marches, e.g. the Hispanic March. Charlemagne's early-9th-century *aprisio* land grants are read by historians partly as a way to lure settlers into a depopulated border region. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_(territory)]
- Adelman and Aron's distinction (paraphrase; full text not read): a *frontier* is a meeting place of peoples where no single political authority had established control over clearly demarcated territory; *borderlands* are the contested zones between colonial empires; *bordered lands* are what national borders made of them. Their thesis: in imperial borderlands intercultural relations tended to be more inclusive; as national borders replaced borderlands, relations became more exclusive and borderlanders became "ethnics", minorities set apart from the national majority. Article: Jeremy Adelman and Stephen Aron, "From Borderlands to Borders: Empires, Nation-States, and the Peoples in Between in North American History", *American Historical Review* 104, no. 3 (June 1999), pp. 814–41. [source: WebSearch results incl. https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/from-borderlands-to-borders-empires-nation-states-and-the-peoples/ and https://worldhistorycommons.org/primer-borderlands-history]
- Scholarship on borders revived from the late 1990s, partly as a counter-narrative to talk of a "borderless", deterritorialised globalised world (Newman and Paasi). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border]

## 2. Frontier historiography (Turner, Bolton, Lattimore, Hämäläinen)
- Frederick Jackson Turner presented "The Significance of the Frontier in American History" to the American Historical Association in Chicago on 12 July 1893, during the World's Columbian Exposition. The thesis: settling the frontier was decisive in forming American democracy and character. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frontier_Thesis]
- Turner began from the census: the 1890 census bulletin stated that "Up to and including 1880 the country had a frontier of settlement, but at present the unsettled area has been so broken into by isolated bodies of settlement that there can hardly be said to be a frontier line." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frontier_Thesis]
- Census maps had defined the frontier line as the line beyond which population density was under 2 persons per square mile (Wikipedia marks this sentence "citation needed": use with care). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frontier]
- Critics: the thesis says little about Indigenous Americans, African Americans and women; Bolton and others argued that colonising cultures shaped the environment, not the reverse; others point to the westward spread of slavery, the dispossession of Mexican Americans after 1848 and the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882). George Wilson Pierson published an influential rebuttal in 1942. Patricia Nelson Limerick and colleagues edited *Trails: Toward a New Western History* (1991), associated with the "New Western History". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frontier_Thesis]
- Comparative frontier studies have applied Turner's model to South Africa, Canada, Russia, Brazil, Argentina, Australia and Rome; Canadian historians (Innis, Careless) developed a centre–periphery "metropolitan" alternative. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frontier_Thesis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frontier]
- Herbert Eugene Bolton (1870–1953) studied under Turner (1896–97), taught at Berkeley from 1911, and published *The Spanish Borderlands: A Chronicle of Old Florida and the Southwest* (Yale University Press, 1921), the book that named the field. His 1932 AHA presidential address, "The Epic of Greater America", called for a hemispheric history (the "Bolton Theory"). He supervised 104 doctoral dissertations. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Eugene_Bolton]
- Owen Lattimore's *Inner Asian Frontiers of China* (American Geographical Society, 1940) recast East Asian history not as China radiating influence outward but as an interaction between two types of society, settled farming and pastoral, each changing the other (summary via William T. Rowe). Lattimore was later accused by Senator Joseph McCarthy (1950) of being a top Soviet agent; congressional hearings did not substantiate the charge. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owen_Lattimore]
- Pekka Hämäläinen (born 1967, Finnish; Rhodes Professor of American History at Oxford from 2012) published *The Comanche Empire* in 2008; it won the Bancroft Prize. His later *Lakota America* (2019) similarly argues the Lakota formed an empire. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pekka_H%C3%A4m%C3%A4l%C3%A4inen]
- Comanchería (Comanche *Nʉmʉnʉʉ Sookobitʉ*, "Comanche land") covered parts of modern New Mexico, West Texas and nearby areas before the 1860s; by the 1830s Comanche raids reached deep into Mexico. Brian DeLay's *The War of a Thousand Deserts* (Yale, 2008) treats those raids as a background to the US–Mexican War. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comancheria]

## 3. Premodern frontiers: Rome, China, the caliphate, Habsburg and Anglo-Scottish marches
- The Roman frontier ran for more than 10,000 km, from northern Britain through Europe to the Black Sea, then to the Red Sea and across North Africa to the Atlantic. Many sections were rivers (*ripa*: Rhine, Danube, Euphrates), mountains or desert rather than walls. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limes_(Roman_Empire)]
- The first use of *limes* to mean a land border is in Tacitus (*Agricola*, AD 98). The Upper Germanic–Rhaetian Limes began as a road; from about AD 162/63 it gained palisades, ditches and towers and finally ran about 550 km. The southern (African) frontier was the longest of Rome's three land frontiers, about 4,000 km as the crow flies from Rabat to Suez. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limes_(Roman_Empire)]
- Debate: Benjamin Isaac, "The Meaning of 'Limes' and 'Limitanei' in Ancient Sources" (*Journal of Roman Studies* 78, 1988), is the standard citation questioning modern readings of *limes* as a fortified line. Edward Luttwak's *The Grand Strategy of the Roman Empire from the First Century AD to the Third* (1976) argued for a planned defensive system; many ancient historians reject it and doubt Rome had any imperial grand strategy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limes_(Roman_Empire) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Luttwak]
- Hadrian's Wall was begun in AD 122; it ran 80 Roman miles (73 modern miles, 117 km) from the Tyne to the Solway; forts held about 600 men and milecastles 12–20; three legions (II Augusta, VI Victrix, XX Valeria Victrix) did most of the building in about six years. Only about three-fifths was first built in stone; the western section was turf. UNESCO World Heritage Site 1987. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadrian%27s_Wall]
- Purpose: besides slowing raiders, the wall kept people in, channelled movement through gates where it could be monitored and taxed, and was a symbolic statement; archaeologist Neil Faulkner: "the wall, like other great Roman frontier monuments was as much a propaganda statement as a functional facility". Some recent scholars question how great the northern threat was. Roman coins and pottery largely did not cross the wall. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadrian%27s_Wall]
- The *Historia Augusta* says Hadrian "was the first to build a wall, eighty miles long, to separate the Romans from the barbarians" (translation as given in Breeze and Dobson). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadrian%27s_Wall]
- The Antonine Wall (Forth–Clyde), a turf wall begun around AD 142 under Antoninus Pius, was abandoned for Hadrian's Wall around 164 (Wikipedia's Hadrian article gives 164), briefly reoccupied under Septimius Severus (208–211). Roman rule in Britain ended around 410. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadrian%27s_Wall ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonine_Wall]
- Great Wall of China: a series of fortifications, not one wall; a 2012 survey counted 21,196 km of walls and related works from all periods. Besides defence, functions included border control, duties on Silk Road goods and regulation of trade. Most surviving brick-and-stone sections are Ming, built after the defeat at Tumu (1449); up to 25,000 watchtowers are estimated. After the Qing absorbed Mongolia, work on it for border defence stopped, though the Qing built the Willow Palisade to keep Han Chinese out of Manchuria. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Wall_of_China]
- Post-Qin dynasties usually avoided the name "Long Wall" because of its association with the First Emperor's tyranny (Waldron 1983). Arthur Waldron, *The Great Wall of China: From History to Myth* (Cambridge University Press, 1990). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Wall_of_China]
- Visibility myth: the Wall cannot be seen with the naked eye from the Moon; NASA says it is barely visible from low orbit and only under near-perfect conditions. One of the earliest versions of the Moon myth is William Stukeley's 1754 letter, comparing it to Hadrian's Wall. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Wall_of_China]
- A "landscape border": in the 11th century the Song dynasty planted a defensive forest along its northern border against the Khitan Liao (Yuan Julian Chen, 2018). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_border ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border]
- Islamic frontier: *al-thughūr* (sing. *thaghr*, "cleft, opening") were the forward marches, a chain of strongholds, and *al-ʿawāṣim* the rear zone, on the Muslim side of the frontier with Byzantium in Cilicia, northern Syria and Upper Mesopotamia, from the early 8th century to the mid-10th. Harun al-Rashid made a separate province (*jund al-ʿAwāṣim*) in 786. Tarsus held 4,000–5,000 troops and hostels for volunteer warriors for the faith from across the Muslim world (Ibn Hawqal). The Byzantine side mirrored it with *kleisourai* and *akritai* (border guards); Nikephoros II Phokas took Cilicia in 964–965. Settlers included Persians, Slavs, Arab Christians and Zuṭṭ (Jats) from India. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Awasim]
- Habsburg Military Frontier (*Militärgrenze*, *Vojna krajina*), 1553–1881: a borderland facing the Ottomans, run as soldier-settler communities of *Grenzer* (Serbs, Croats, Germans, Vlachs and others) who held land in return for service; dissolved in stages, finally in 1881. It also acted as a *cordon sanitaire*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Frontier]
- Anglo-Scottish border: reivers raided both sides "without regard to their victims' nationality". The border was loosely defined by the Treaty of York (1237); March law (*Leges Marchiarum*) was first codified in 1249 and revised at least eight times to 1596; justice was negotiated at Truce Days. Disputed "threiplands" included the Debatable Land; the border was not fully demarcated until the Ordnance Survey mapped it in the mid-19th century. The word "blackmail" (protection money) is traced to Border reiver culture. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_reivers]

## 4. Westphalia and the "Westphalian myth"
- The Peace of Westphalia is the collective name for the treaties of Osnabrück and Münster, signed in October 1648 (Osnabrück treaty 24 October 1648), ending the Thirty Years' War; 109 delegations attended (16 European states, 66 Imperial States representing 140, 27 interest groups). The Spanish–Dutch Peace of Münster (30 January 1648) ended the Eighty Years' War. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_of_Westphalia]
- Debate: political scientists long treated 1648 as the birth of sovereign, non-interfering, legally equal states (e.g. Leo Gross, *American Journal of International Law*, January 1948; Henry Kissinger, *World Order*, 2014). Andreas Osiander, "Sovereignty, International Relations, and the Westphalian Myth", *International Organization* 55 (2001): "the treaties confirm neither [France's or Sweden's] 'sovereignty' nor anybody else's; least of all do they contain anything about sovereignty as a principle." Derek Croxton (*International History Review*, 1999), Stephen Krasner (*Sovereignty: Organized Hypocrisy*, 1999) and Hall Gardner are among other critics; the non-interference principle is better traced to Emer de Vattel in the mid-18th century. Westphalia in fact gave France and Sweden rights to intervene in the Empire. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westphalian_system]
- Today the staunchest defenders of "Westphalian" non-interference are often non-Western states (e.g. the 2001 Jiang Zemin–Putin statement). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westphalian_system]

## 5. Natural borders and the cartographic state
- The doctrine of natural boundaries developed in 18th-century Western thought; Arnold Toynbee criticised it in *The New Europe* (1916) as a justification for endless wars, since each "natural" frontier invites a further one. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_border]
- France's "natural frontiers" (Rhine, Alps, Pyrenees, sea): an apocryphal statement attributed to Richelieu in 1642; revived by Anacharsis Cloots in 1786; in January 1793 (Wikipedia's text gives 21 January 1793; I could not confirm the day, which other accounts give as 31 January, so write "January 1793"—see Conflicts) Danton told the Convention "the limits of France are marked by nature" (French: "Les limites de la France sont marquées par la nature"). Peter Sahlins: "most historians of France today dismiss the 'doctrine' of natural frontiers as too teleological a reading of France's history.... As a model of French identity, it formed part of a constitutive myth of the state" ("Natural Frontiers Revisited", *American Historical Review* 95:5, 1990). Ernest Renan: "The frontiers of France in 1789 had nothing natural or necessary about them." The idea stayed in French textbooks until the mid-20th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_borders_of_France]
- Rivers move: the Rio Grande's shifts caused repeated US–Mexico disputes; the Chamizal dispute (El Paso–Juárez) was settled by a 1963 treaty returning most of the land to Mexico; the Banco Convention of 1905 and the Boundary Treaty of 1970 dealt with later shifts. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_border ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border]
- Treaty of the Pyrenees, 7 November 1659, signed on Pheasant Island in the Bidasoa (a French–Spanish condominium ever since): France gained Roussillon and the northern half of Cerdanya. Because the treaty transferred "villages" north of the Pyrenees and Llívia was a town, Llívia remained a Spanish exclave. The line was only fully settled by the Treaty of Bayonne (1856) with final acts 12 years later. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_the_Pyrenees]
- Peter Sahlins (born 1957, Berkeley), *Boundaries: The Making of France and Spain in the Pyrenees* (University of California Press, 1989): combines the diplomatic history of the boundary with a historical ethnography of Catalan villagers in the divided Cerdanya valley; its conclusion is titled "Identity and Counter-Identity". (The common summary that national identities were shaped from below by locals using the state for their own ends is a fair paraphrase but I did not read the text.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Sahlins ; WebSearch: https://www.ucpress.edu/books/boundaries/epub-pdf]
- Treaty of Tordesillas, 7 June 1494: divided newly found lands outside Europe between Portugal and Castile along a meridian 370 leagues (about 2,100 km) west of Cape Verde, modifying Pope Alexander VI's bull *Inter caetera* (4 May 1493). Portugal's Brazil spread across it unresisted; it was superseded by the Treaty of Madrid (13 January 1750). A line drawn on a map of lands neither party had seen. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Tordesillas]
- The Cassini map: the first topographic, geometric map of the whole of France, based on geodesic triangulation, made by four generations of the Cassini family over more than six decades in the 18th century, at 1:86,400. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassini_map]
- Treaty of Nerchinsk, 27 August 1689, between Qing China (Songgotu, for the Kangxi Emperor) and Russia (Fyodor Golovin): the first formal treaty China signed with a Western power; negotiated in Latin through Jesuit interpreters Jean-François Gerbillon and Thomas Pereira, texts in Latin, Russian and Manchu. The border followed the Argun, Shilka and Gorbitsa rivers and the Stanovoy watershed; the area west of the Argun was left undefined; Albazin was razed; fugitives arriving after the treaty were to be returned; cross-border travel and trade were allowed "with proper documents"; both sides agreed to place boundary stones. Neither side knew the course of the Uda well. Superseded by the Treaty of Aigun (1858). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Nerchinsk]
- Thongchai Winichakul (born 1957; student leader at the 6 October 1976 Thammasat massacre; professor at Wisconsin–Madison), *Siam Mapped: A History of the Geo-Body of a Nation* (1994): modern mapping did not record a pre-existing Thai territory but created the nation's "geo-body". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thongchai_Winichakul ; already in the published Modern Southeast Asia chapter]
- Context for Siam Mapped: in the Franco-Siamese crisis (13 July–3 October 1893, beginning with the Paknam incident) Siam ceded land east of the Mekong to French Indochina, splitting the Lao-speaking world. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1893_Franco-Siamese_crisis]
- The Lapp Codicil of 1751, a 30-section addendum to the Strömstad Treaty that fixed the Norwegian–Swedish border, formalised Sámi rights to continue migratory reindeer herding across the new border, with provisions on citizenship and taxes. It lost force on the Finnish border after 1809; the Norway–Finland/Russia border was closed to herders in 1852 and the Finland–Sweden border in 1889. In 2021 the Norwegian copy was moved to the Sámi Archives in Kautokeino. Its current legal force is disputed (Sweden's Sámi groups say valid; Norway disputes). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapp_Codicil_of_1751 ; WebSearch: https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2021/09/lappkodicillen-of-1751-the-sami-magna-carta/]

## 6. The great line-drawings
### Africa: Berlin and after
- Berlin Conference: 15 November 1884 – 26 February 1885; General Act signed by 14 powers; no African state invited; set free trade in a broadly defined Congo Basin; confirmed Leopold II's International Association; Article 34–35 "effective occupation" for coastal claims. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Conference — treat with caution, article flagged AI-generated; dates agree with published chapter]
- Europeans controlled about 10% of Africa in the 1870s, all near the coasts; by 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia were outside European control. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa]
- In 1964 the Organisation of African Unity decided to keep colonial borders, from fears of civil war and instability. The Cairo resolution AHG/Res. 16(I), "Border Disputes Among African States" (July 1964), has member states pledge to respect the borders existing on their achievement of independence (the principle known in law as *uti possidetis juris*). I could not open the text, so paraphrase only. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa ; WebSearch results incl. https://archives.au.int/handle/123456789/1092]
- *Uti possidetis juris* ("as you possess under law"): new states inherit the administrative borders of the preceding dependency. Applied in Spanish America from the 1820s, in Africa, in the USSR's breakup and by the Badinter Commission for Yugoslavia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uti_possidetis_juris]
- Partitioned peoples: Michalopoulos and Papaioannou ("The Long-Run Effects of the Scramble for Africa", *AER* 2016) count 231 ethnic homelands with at least 10% of their area in more than one country (164 at a 20% threshold); working-paper versions found conflict about 50% higher, and violence against civilians about 40% higher, where partitioned groups live. Figures differ between versions: attribute and give as approximate. [source: WebSearch, VoxEU summary https://voxeu.org/article/long-run-effects-scramble-africa]
- The "arbitrary borders" debate: the published Imperial Expansion chapter already covers "It didn't happen at Berlin" (Katzenellenbogen), Crowe, Craven and the 2024 finding that the median African border reached its final form only in 1908 through bilateral treaties. Cross-refer; do not repeat.

### South and Central Asia
- Durand Line, agreement of 12 November 1893 between Mortimer Durand and Emir Abdur Rahman Khan: a single page of seven short articles, including a pledge of non-interference beyond the line. Demarcation surveys 1894–96 covered about 800 miles; the Khyber section was finalised in a treaty of 22 November 1921. Today about 2,640 km, recognised internationally as Pakistan's western border but largely unrecognised in Afghanistan. It cut through Pashtun lands; an Afghan loya jirga of 26 July 1949 repudiated it. Pakistan's fencing has been resisted by Pashtun parties. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durand_Line]
- McMahon Line: agreed in Anglo-Tibetan notes and maps at the Simla Conference (1913–14), signed by Henry McMahon and Lonchen Shatra; about 550 miles (890 km) from Bhutan to the Isu Razi Pass on the Burma border, along the Himalayan crest. China initialled but did not sign the convention and rejects the line, holding that Tibet could not make treaties; it is the de facto boundary (eastern Line of Actual Control) and was the eastern sector of the 1962 Sino-Indian War; the Tawang tract is the core dispute. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McMahon_Line]
- Radcliffe Line: Radcliffe arrived 8 July 1947, had about five weeks, had never visited India, destroyed his papers and left on Independence Day; the awards were shown to Indian and Pakistani representatives at 5 pm on 16 August and published 17 August 1947. The Chittagong Hill Tracts' Buddhist peoples had no representation. (Detail is in the Partition chapter: cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radcliffe_Line]
- Cooch Behar enclaves (*chitmahals*): 102 Indian enclaves inside Bangladesh (with 21 Bangladeshi counter-enclaves and one Indian counter-counter-enclave, Dahala Khagrabari, the world's only third-order enclave) and 71 Bangladeshi enclaves in India (3 counter-enclaves); a 2010 joint census found 51,549 residents. The Land Boundary Agreement (16 May 1974, Indira Gandhi and Sheikh Mujibur Rahman) was implemented only in 2015: exchange at midnight on 31 July 2015; about 14,000 people became Indian and about 36,000 Bangladeshi citizens. A popular legend says the enclaves were stakes in card or chess games between the Raja of Koch Bihar and the Maharaja of Rangpur (legend only). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh%E2%80%93India_enclaves]

### The Middle East
- Sykes–Picot: negotiated 23 November 1915 – 3 January 1916 (initialled 3 January), confirmed in letters of 9–16 May 1916; published by the Bolsheviks 23 November 1917. It split the Mosul vilayet at the Little Zab. In July 2014 Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi vowed at Mosul's al-Nuri mosque to hit "the last nail in the coffin of the Sykes–Picot conspiracy". See Toby Dodge, "The Danger of Analogical Myths: ... the Sykes-Picot Delusion" (*AJIL Unbound*, 2016). The Modern Middle East chapter already covers the myth debate (Laurens, Pursley): cross-refer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sykes%E2%80%93Picot_Agreement]
- Kurds: estimates of the population vary widely (Washington Kurdish Institute 30–35 million, 2024; CIA 2015 roughly 28–30 million in the region; Kurdish Institute of Paris 36.4–45.6 million, 2017). The unratified Treaty of Sèvres (1920) was replaced by the Treaty of Lausanne (24 July 1923). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurds ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Lausanne]

### Korea and Germany: Cold War lines
- Korean DMZ: created by the Armistice of 27 July 1953, each side pulling back 2,000 m from the front, making a buffer about 4 km wide and 250 km long, with the Military Demarcation Line down the middle; only two settlements are allowed inside (Daeseong-dong in the South, Kijong-dong in the North); Panmunjom's Joint Security Area; four North Korean incursion tunnels found (15 November 1974; 19 March 1975; 17 October 1978; 3 March 1990). The 38th parallel itself was picked on 10 August 1945 (see Yalta chapter). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_Demilitarized_Zone]
- Churchill at Fulton, Missouri, 5 March 1946: see Quotations. The inner German border was fortified from 1952; over 1,300 km of fortifications several kilometres deep on the East German side. After 1989 its route became the German Green Belt (planned December 1989; Bundestag protection voted December 2004), part of a European Green Belt along the old Iron Curtain. The closed zone had become a wildlife haven. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_German_border ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Curtain]
- Berlin Wall: built from 13 August 1961, officially the "Anti-Fascist Protection Rampart"; about 3.5 million East Germans had left before it was built; 155 km around West Berlin; around 5,000 people escaped; the ZZF Potsdam project confirmed at least 140 deaths (Checkpoint Charlie Museum claims over 200); opened 9 November 1989. On 15 June 1961 Ulbricht had said "Niemand hat die Absicht, eine Mauer zu errichten!" ("No one has the intention of erecting a wall!"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Wall]

## 7. Passports and papers
- Early analogues: Nehemiah 2:7–9 (c. 450 BC), a letter from Artaxerxes I requesting safe passage; England's Safe Conducts Act 1414 under Henry V is the earliest reference to what some call the first British passport. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passport]
- Railways eroded passport control: for about thirty years before 1914, passports were on the whole not required for travel within Europe. Before WWI usually only adult men held passports, with wives and children written in. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passport]
- The First World War watershed: governments introduced passport requirements for security and to keep skilled workers at home, and kept them after the war; British travellers in the 1920s complained of the photographs and descriptions as "nasty dehumanisation". The League of Nations' Paris Conference on Passports (1920) standardised the passport; ICAO set machine-readable standards from 1980. (Migration chapter covers this: cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passport]
- Nansen passports (1922–1938) for stateless refugees, prompted by the Soviet revocation (1921) of citizenship of Russians abroad, including some 800,000 civil-war refugees; extended to Armenians in 1924; holders included Marc Chagall and Vladimir Nabokov; the Nansen Office won the 1938 Nobel Peace Prize. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nansen_passport]
- John Torpey, *The Invention of the Passport: Surveillance, Citizenship, and the State* (Cambridge University Press, 2000): the state's monopolisation of the "legitimate means of movement". (Already in Migration chapter.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Torpey]
- US Border Patrol: before it, mounted "inspectors" from 1904 (never more than 75), chiefly chasing Chinese migrants evading the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882. The Patrol was created in May 1924 (date given as 26 May in the text and 28 May in the infobox; the Labor Appropriation Act of 28 May 1924 is the usual citation). 19,648 agents in FY2019, 16,731 on the southern border. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Border_Patrol]

## 8. Borderlands as worlds of their own: the US–Mexico border
- Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, signed 2 February 1848 at Villa Hidalgo: Mexico ceded more than 2.5 million km² (about 55% of its territory, counting Texas) for $15 million; Mexicans in the ceded lands could choose US citizenship within a year (over 90% did); about 80,000 Mexicans lived in the ceded lands in 1845–50. The US Senate struck out Article X guaranteeing land grants, and grants were often not honoured. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Guadalupe_Hidalgo ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border]
- Gadsden Purchase (Venta de La Mesilla): 29,640 sq mi of southern Arizona and south-western New Mexico, bought for $10 million (cut by the Senate from $15 million) for a southern railway route; signed December 1853 by James Gadsden and Santa Anna; in effect 8 June 1854. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gadsden_Purchase]
- The Gadsden Purchase split Tohono O'odham territory between the US and Mexico; unlike some groups on the Canadian border, they were not offered dual citizenship; the Nation's land now runs along about 74 miles (Mizutani) of the border; by 1910 only about 1,000 O'odham were estimated to remain in Mexico; militarisation in the 1980s–90s cut traditional crossings. In June 2026 the Nation sued DHS to stop wall construction on tribal land; in October 2026 CBP sought comment on 62 miles of barrier on the reservation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tohono_O%CA%BCodham_Nation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border_wall]
- The border runs 3,145 km (1,954 mi); from the Gulf it follows the Rio Grande for 2,020 km "along the deepest channel" (thalweg), then overland to the Pacific. It is described as the world's most frequently crossed border, with about 350 million documented crossings a year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border]
- Border economy: the Bracero Program (1942–1964) brought Mexican farmworkers north; Mexico's Border Industrialization Program (dated 1964 by Wikipedia; often given as 1965) created *maquiladoras*; NAFTA (1994) turned northern Mexico into an export-processing zone. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maquiladora]
- Gloria Anzaldúa (1942–2004), born in the Rio Grande Valley of south Texas; co-edited *This Bridge Called My Back* (1981, with Cherríe Moraga); *Borderlands/La Frontera: The New Mestiza* (Aunt Lute Books, 1987), a semi-autobiographical mix of Spanish and English, theorises "mestiza consciousness" and the borderland as a psychological, sexual and spiritual as well as physical space. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloria_Anzald%C3%BAa ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borderlands/La_Frontera:_The_New_Mestiza]
- Zomia: a term coined in 2002 by Willem van Schendel for the Southeast Asian uplands; James C. Scott's *The Art of Not Being Governed: An Anarchist History of Upland Southeast Asia* (Yale, 2009) recast it as a region of peoples evading lowland states. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asian_Massif]

## 9. Walls today and the border as industry, spectacle and crisis
- US–Mexico barrier: George H. W. Bush approved the first 14 miles of fencing at San Diego–Tijuana (1990); 75 miles of fencing in 2005; the Secure Fence Act signed 26 October 2006 authorised 700 miles; in 2008 Secretary Chertoff waived environmental laws to build it. Biden paused construction on 20 January 2021; Trump pledged in January 2025 to finish it. As of 10 November 2026 CBP's Smart Wall map reported 96.1 of 626 planned miles of new primary wall, 48.2 of 137 miles of replacement wall, 125.8 of 578 miles of new secondary wall and 79.7 of 533 miles of "waterborne barrier" completed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border_wall]
- "Prevention Through Deterrence" (Border Patrol Strategic Plan, 1994): sealing urban crossings (Operation Blockade/Hold the Line in El Paso, September 1993; Operation Gatekeeper in San Diego, 1994) pushed migrants into the Sonoran Desert. In Pima County, Arizona, 2,238 migrant bodies were found 1990–2012. Border Patrol reported more than 10,000 migrant deaths on the border 1994–2024; rights groups suggest up to 80,000. IOM recorded 686 deaths and disappearances on the US–Mexico border in 2022, making it the world's deadliest land migration route that year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prevention_Through_Deterrence ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border]
- Global toll: IOM's Missing Migrants Project has documented more than 80,000 deaths and disappearances on migration routes worldwide since 2014 (about 82,000–83,000 by its April 2026 report; nearly 8,000 in 2025; 2024 was reportedly the record year at about 9,200, single source). IOM says these are an undercount. [source: WebSearch, incl. https://reliefweb.int/report/world/iom-missing-migrants-project-2025-annual-global-report-missing-migrants-and-their-families-left-behind and https://www.dawn.com/news/1993976]
- Mediterranean: over 28,000 dead or missing since 2014 (IOM, figure on Wikipedia list as of its last update); 2014 was a turning point (3,289 dead/missing per IOM; 3,538 per UNHCR); the University of Amsterdam's "Deaths at the Borders" project counts a further 3,188 deaths in 1990–2013. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_migrant_vessel_incidents_on_the_Mediterranean_Sea]
- Ceuta (Spanish exclave in North Africa): fence built 1993 (2.5 m high, 8.4 km), raised to 3 m from 1995 and further in 2005; on 7 October 2005 hundreds of migrants stormed it and several died between Spanish rubber bullets and Moroccan gunfire. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceuta_border_fence]
- Frontex, the EU border agency, founded 3 October 2004 (relaunched 6 October 2016 as the European Border and Coast Guard Agency), Warsaw; budget about €1.12 billion (2025); a standing corps of 10,000 proposed by 2027. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frontex]
- Counting walls (attribute and give ranges): half of all border barriers built since 1945 were built after 2000 (Atlantic, Uri Friedman). Élisabeth Vallet (Université du Québec à Montréal) is the usual source of counts: fewer than a dozen to about 15 walls at the fall of the Berlin Wall (sources give 11, 15 or 16), and 74 as of early 2022 (Migration Policy Institute, 2022); a "77" figure is also quoted. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_barrier ; WebSearch incl. https://www.migrationpolicy.org/article/rapid-proliferation-number-border-walls]
- Wendy Brown, *Walled States, Waning Sovereignty* (2010): new walls answer the erosion of state sovereignty under globalised capital; their function is largely symbolic, a theatrical projection of national identity and control. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendy_Brown]
- Jason De León, *The Land of Open Graves: Living and Dying on the Migrant Trail* (University of California Press, 2015): ethnography, archaeology and forensic science on deaths in the Sonoran Desert under Prevention Through Deterrence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Land_of_Open_Graves]

## 10. Schengen against the global hardening
- Schengen Agreement signed 14 June 1985 aboard a boat on the Moselle near Schengen, Luxembourg, at the France–Germany–Luxembourg tripoint, by five of the then ten EEC states (Belgium, France, Luxembourg, Netherlands, West Germany); the Benelux states had abolished common border controls in 1970. Supplemented by the 1990 Convention; came into effect 26 March 1995; brought into EU law by the Amsterdam Treaty (signed 1997, in force 1999). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schengen_Agreement ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schengen_Area]
- Today 29 countries (all EU states except Cyprus and Ireland, plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, Switzerland), about 453 million people (2025). Bulgaria and Romania joined for air and sea on 31 March 2024 and for land borders on 1 January 2025. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schengen_Area]
- Internal controls are back: member states may reintroduce temporary internal checks and the Commission cannot veto them; as of September 2026 several states had them, including Germany (renewed continuously since 16 September 2024), Austria, Denmark, France, Italy, Norway, Poland and Sweden, citing migration and security. The biometric Entry/Exit System began on 12 October 2025 and became fully operational at all external crossings on 10 April 2026. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schengen_Area]

## Debates
- Frontier as line vs zone, and Turner vs his critics: Turner's frontier as the crucible of democracy (1893); Bolton's Spanish Borderlands and hemispheric history (1921, 1932); Lattimore's frontier as two-way interaction between steppe and sown (1940); New Western History (Limerick et al.) as conquest, not "free land"; Adelman and Aron (1999) on borderlands becoming bordered lands. [sources: sections 1–2]
- Hämäläinen's "empire" framing: praised and prize-winning, but criticised by Indigenous scholars such as Jameson Sweet for imposing a European category (already in the published Indigenous Economies chapter: cross-refer). [source: published chapter history--regional-histories--indigenous-economies]
- Roman frontiers: Luttwak's grand strategy (1976) vs Isaac (1988) and others who see no systematic grand strategy and question *limes* as a defended line. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Luttwak ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limes_(Roman_Empire)]
- The Westphalian myth: political-science orthodoxy (Gross 1948, Kissinger 2014) vs Osiander, Croxton, Krasner, Gardner. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westphalian_system]
- Natural frontiers: a French state doctrine (Danton, Michelet) vs Renan, Toynbee and Sahlins, who call it teleological myth. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_borders_of_France ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_border]
- "Arbitrary" African borders: popular claims that Berlin carved up Africa and that colonial borders cause conflict (supported in part by Michalopoulos and Papaioannou's evidence on partitioned groups) vs historians who stress bilateral treaties after Berlin, local African agency in boundary-making, and the OAU's deliberate 1964 choice to keep the borders. [sources: section 6; published Imperial Expansion chapter]
- Sykes–Picot as the origin of "artificial" Middle Eastern borders vs historians (Laurens, Pursley, Dodge) who call this a myth. [source: published Modern Middle East chapter; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sykes%E2%80%93Picot_Agreement]
- Do walls work? Governments cite deterrence; Wendy Brown reads walls as symbolic theatre of waning sovereignty; De León and the Prevention Through Deterrence record show walls redirect migrants to deadlier routes rather than stopping them. [sources: section 9]
- Is Schengen a border-free Europe or "Fortress Europe"? Internal openness paired with a hardened, militarised external frontier (Frontex, Ceuta, Mediterranean deaths) and, since 2015, repeated reintroduction of internal checks. [sources: sections 9–10]
