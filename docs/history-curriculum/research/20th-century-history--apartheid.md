## 1. Roots of segregation (before 1948) — keep short; cross-refer to British Empire chapter
- "Apartheid" is Afrikaans for "separateness" (literally "apart-hood"); first recorded use 1929. [source: APA]
- Pass controls predate apartheid by 150 years: in 1797 officials at Swellendam and Graaff-Reinet required all Khoikhoi moving about the country to carry passes; the British confirmed this in the 1809 Hottentot Proclamation. The South African Republic (Transvaal) brought in pass laws requiring Africans to carry a badge in 1896. [source: APA]
- Cape "qualified franchise" had been open to men of all races; the Franchise and Ballot Act 1892 raised the property bar and added a literacy test; Rhodes's Glen Grey Act 1894 limited African landholding; Natal took the vote from Indians in 1894. [source: APA]
- Treaty of Vereeniging (1902) peace negotiations demanded "the just predominance of the white race" as a precondition of union. [source: APA]
- Union of South Africa (1910): the South Africa Act gave whites political control and barred Black people from sitting in parliament. Then: Natives Land Act 1913; Urban Areas Act 1923 (residential segregation); "Colour Bar" Act 1926 (barred Black miners from skilled trades); 1936 Representation of Natives Act removed Black voters from the Cape common roll (they could elect three whites). [source: APA]
- **Natives Land Act 1913**: royal assent 16 June 1913, in force 19 June 1913; barred land sales between Black and white South Africans; Black areas initially about 7% of the Union's land, later expanded to 13% (Native Trust and Land Act 1936); also prohibited sharecropping; repealed 30 June 1991. [source: NLA]
- Sol Plaatje (1876–1932): founder member and first General Secretary of the South African Native National Congress (SANNC, founded 8 January 1912 in Bloemfontein; renamed ANC in 1923 — SP says "ten years later in 1922"; ANC article gives the founding date only. Say "renamed the ANC in the 1920s"); went with an SANNC deputation to England in 1914 to protest the Land Act; met Du Bois and Garvey in North America; wrote *Native Life in South Africa* (London, 1916) and *Mhudi* (written 1919, published 1930), the first novel in English by a Black South African to be written; translated Shakespeare into Setswana; kept a diary of the siege of Mafeking. [source: SP; ANC]
- **Charlotte Maxeke** (1871–1939): BSc from Wilberforce University, Ohio, 1903 — the first Black South African woman to graduate; one of the few women at the early SANNC; founded the Bantu Women's League in 1918 out of anti-pass campaigning; honoured as "Mother of Black Freedom in South Africa". [source: CM]
- **Gandhi in South Africa (1893–1914)**: arrived 1893 to act for an Indian merchant; thrown off a train at Pietermaritzburg; proposed the **Natal Indian Congress** on 22 May 1894, founded 22 August 1894 (Gandhi honorary secretary). In 1906 the Transvaal required Indians and Chinese to register with fingerprints and carry certificates; at a mass meeting in Johannesburg on 11 September 1906 Gandhi first adopted *satyagraha*. He returned to India in 1915. Caveat for the writer: historians Ashwin Desai and Goolam Vahed show he initially separated Indian from African grievances (an 1896 speech complained of whites "degrading the Indian to the level of a raw Kaffir"); in 1906 he led a stretcher-bearer unit during the suppression of the Bambatha rebellion; by 1910 *Indian Opinion* was reporting discrimination against Africans. [source: MG; NIC]
- NIC later: passive resistance 1946–48 against the "Ghetto Act" (Asiatic Land Tenure and Indian Representation Act 1946) under Monty Naicker; joined the Defiance Campaign; revived 1971; founding affiliate of the UDF. [source: NIC]
- India asked on 22 June 1946 for the treatment of Indians in South Africa to be placed on the agenda of the first UN General Assembly — the first time South Africa's racial policies reached the UN. [source: SE2]
- Under Smuts, the 1946 Asiatic Land Tenure Act banned land sales to Indians. The Fagan Commission (1946, government) and the HNP's Sauer Commission (which concluded integration would mean a "loss of personality") framed the 1948 contest. [source: APA]

## 2. 1948 and the apartheid laws
- 1948 election, 26 May 1948: the United Party won 49.18% of votes (65 seats) but Malan's Herenigde Nasionale Party (37.70%, 70 seats) with the Afrikaner Party (3.93%, 9 seats) won 79 seats to 71 — the UP got over eleven percentage points more votes; rural constituencies were over-weighted. The electorate was almost entirely white (whites roughly 20% of the population). D. F. Malan became prime minister; first all-Afrikaner cabinet since 1910. [source: E48; APA; NP]
- Factions inside the NP: "baasskap" (white domination using Black labour) versus "purists" seeking total "vertical" separation; Verwoerd sympathised with the purists but accepted Black labour. [source: APA]
- **Key laws** [source: APA unless noted]:
  - Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act 1949 (the first apartheid law); Immorality Amendment Act 1950 (sex across racial lines criminalised).
  - **Population Registration Act 1950**: everyone classified White, Black (Native), Coloured or Indian; identity cards over age 18; classification boards; the "pencil test" (a pencil pushed into the hair: if it stuck, Black; if it fell, Coloured); families split across categories.
  - **Group Areas Act** (promulgated 7 July 1950; re-enacted 1957 and 1966; repealed 30 June 1991). [source: GAA]
  - Suppression of Communism Act 1950: defined "communism" so broadly it was used against any opponent; banning orders. Mandela was convicted of "statutory communism" in 1952. [source: APA; NM]
  - Bantu Authorities Act 1951 (first homelands step); Native Laws Amendment Act 1952 (no more than 72 hours in an urban area without a permit); Reservation of Separate Amenities Act 1953 ("Whites only" beaches, buses, benches); Bantu Education Act 1953; Extension of University Education Act 1959 (separate universities; Fort Hare taken over); Promotion of Bantu Self-Government Act 1959; Black Homeland Citizenship Act 1970.
- **Pass laws**: an internal passport system to segregate, restrict movement and allocate migrant labour; the pass book was derisively called the *dompas*; women were largely excluded until the 1950s because of resistance; pass laws were effectively ended in 1986. [source: PL; APA]
- **Disenfranchising Coloured voters**: the Separate Representation of Voters Act 1951 was struck down by the Appeal Court; the government enlarged the Appeal Court (5 to 11 judges) and the Senate (49 to 89 seats) in 1955 to force it through in 1956; Coloured representation abolished from 1970. [source: APA]
- **Bantu Education**: Verwoerd (Minister of Native Affairs 1950–58): "There is no place for [the Bantu] in the European community above the level of certain forms of labour.... What is the use of teaching the Bantu child mathematics when it cannot use it in practice?" (date/venue not given in source; see NOT VERIFIED). In 1975–76 the state spent R644 per white pupil, R189 per Indian, R139 per Coloured, R42 per Black pupil; in the 1970s per-capita spending on Black education was one-tenth of white. Under Botha spending per Black child rose to one-seventh of white, from one-sixteenth in 1968. [source: BEA; APA]
- **Homelands (Bantustans)**: 10 in South Africa (plus 10 in South West Africa); 13% of land; Tomlinson Commission (1954) recommended more land, ignored. Four declared "independent": Transkei 1976, Bophuthatswana 1977, Venda 1979, Ciskei 1981 (TBVC); their people lost South African citizenship; no other country recognised them. Only about 39% of Black South Africans lived in the homelands in 1986. All reincorporated 27 April 1994. [source: APA; BAN]
- Connie Mulder, 7 February 1978: "If our policy is taken to its logical conclusion as far as the black people are concerned, there will be not one black man with South African citizenship". [source: BAN]
- **Forced removals**: 3.5 million people removed 1960–1983 (APA; BAN says "from the 1960s through the 1980s", flagged citation needed — say "an estimated 3.5 million"); nearly 600,000 Coloured, Indian and Chinese people moved under the Group Areas Act; about 40,000 whites moved when land passed to homelands. [source: APA; BAN]
- **Sophiatown**: removals began 9 February 1955 under the Western Areas Removal Scheme; 2,000 police moved the first families to Meadowlands, Soweto; residents' slogan "Ons dak nie, ons phola hier" ("we won't move"); rebuilt as white "Triomf" ("Triumph"), renamed Sophiatown in 2006. A cultural hub (Can Themba, Es'kia Mphahlele, Miriam Makeba, Father Trevor Huddleston). [source: SH]
- **District Six** (Cape Town): declared white on 11 February 1966; removals from 1968; by 1982 more than 60,000 people moved to the Cape Flats about 25 km away; houses bulldozed, only places of worship left; renamed Zonnebloem (1970); much of the land stayed empty; District Six Museum opened 1994. P. W. Botha, as Minister of Community Development and Coloured Affairs from 1961, oversaw the removals. [source: DS; PWB]
- Racial pay and tax, segregated unions (54 white-only, 38 Indian/Coloured, 19 Black), Industrial Conciliation Act 1956 against multiracial unions. [source: APA]
- About 40,000 people a year were judicially whipped (mostly, the source says, for political offences — treat cautiously). [source: APA]

## 3. Regime leaders
- **D. F. Malan** PM 1948–54; **J. G. Strijdom** (to whom the 1956 women's petition was addressed). [source: APA; WM]
- **Hendrik Verwoerd** (1901–1966), born in the Netherlands; Broederbond member; Minister of Native Affairs 1950–58, PM 1958–66; "Architect of Apartheid"; called apartheid "good neighbourliness" and "separate development"; proclaimed the Republic in 1961 (white referendum 1960, 52% yes; left the Commonwealth 31 May 1961); stabbed to death in parliament by messenger Dimitri Tsafendas, 6 September 1966. [source: HFV; APA]
- **John Vorster** (1915–1983): interned in WWII for the pro-Nazi Ossewabrandwag; as Justice Minister oversaw the Rivonia Trial; PM 1966–78 (Terrorism Act 1967, Soweto, Biko); "outward-looking" diplomacy with African states (Malawi's Banda the main partner); fell in the Muldergate scandal. [source: JV; APA]
- **P. W. Botha** (1916–2006): Defence Minister 1966–80; PM 1978–84, first executive State President 1984–89; "Die Groot Krokodil"; "Total Strategy" against a "total onslaught"; "adapt or die"; the 1985 **Rubicon speech** refused real change and the rand collapsed; quoted as saying "This uprising will bring out the beast in us"; stroke 1989; later defied a TRC subpoena, calling it a "circus". [source: PWB; APA; TRC]
- **F. W. de Klerk** (1936–2021): State President 1989–94; unbanned the ANC, PAC and SACP and announced Mandela's release on 2 February 1990; ended the nuclear weapons programme (South Africa built six weapons; disarmament essentially complete 1991, acknowledged 1993); joint Nobel Peace Prize with Mandela 1993; deputy president 1994–96; opposed the TRC and sought blanket amnesty; apologised for apartheid in 1997 but was criticised for a qualified apology and for denying knowledge of security-force abuses; said in 2020 that calling apartheid a crime against humanity was "agitprop" (then retracted — not checked); posthumous video apology 2021. [source: FWK; APA; Global Cold War notes for "six weapons"]
- De Klerk on why: the collapse of "the Marxist economic system in Eastern Europe" meant he no longer feared Marxist manipulation of the ANC; "Internationally we were teetering on the edge of the abyss." [source: FWK]
- Militarisation: conscription for white men, 9 months (1968) → 12 months (1972) → 24 months (1978); Israeli arms deals ($200 million pact 1975; over $1.4 billion in 1988 sales); Project Coast chemical/biological programme, including plans to poison opponents. [source: SE2; APA]

## 4. The ANC, the Congress Alliance and 1950s mass resistance
- ANC Youth League founded 1944 (Sisulu, Mandela, Tambo; Anton Lembede not checked); the Youth League took control in December 1949 and the ANC adopted the Programme of Action (17 December 1949): boycotts, strikes, civil disobedience. National Day of Protest, 26 June 1950. [source: DC; ANC; APA]
- **Defiance Campaign against Unjust Laws**, launched 26 June 1952 by the ANC and South African Indian Congress (preparation day 6 April 1952, the 300th anniversary of van Riebeeck's landing); about 8,500 volunteers (AL; SE2 says "over 8,000") broke apartheid laws non-violently, Gandhi-inspired; volunteers entered "whites only" facilities and refused to defend themselves in court. Police killed 14 at Kimberley on 9 November 1952. ANC membership grew from about 25,000 (1951) to 100,000 (1953). Called off April 1953; government answered with the Public Safety Act and Criminal Law Amendment Act. [source: DC; AL; SE2]
- **Albert Luthuli** (c.1898–1967): Zulu chief of Groutville from 1935; deposed as chief 1952 after refusing to leave the ANC; ANC President-General 1952–67; banned repeatedly; awarded the 1960 Nobel Peace Prize in 1961 — the first African laureate (first-African claim not in source; see NOT VERIFIED); initially opposed violence and stayed personally non-violent; forged the multiracial Congress Alliance. Died 1967 (AL lead says "assassination" — contested; see NOT VERIFIED). [source: AL]
- **Freedom Charter**: 50,000 volunteers collected "freedom demands"; adopted at the **Congress of the People, Kliptown, 26 June 1955**, about 3,000 delegates, by the Congress Alliance (ANC, SA Indian Congress, SA Congress of Democrats, Coloured People's Congress); police broke up the meeting on the second day. Opening: "We, the People of South Africa, declare for all our country and the world to know: that South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white". Clauses: "The People Shall Govern!", "The People Shall Share in the Country's Wealth!" (mines, banks and monopoly industry to be transferred to the people), "The Land Shall Be Shared Among Those Who Work It!". Africanists rejected its non-racialism and later formed the PAC. Women's contribution: FEDSAW's "What Women Demand". [source: FC; WM]
- **Women's March, 9 August 1956**: about 20,000 women of all races marched to the Union Buildings, Pretoria, organised by the Federation of South African Women (FEDSAW, founded 1954); led by Lilian Ngoyi, Helen Joseph, Rahima Moosa and Sophia Williams-De Bruyn; they delivered petitions (WM: "14,000 petitions" — say "thousands of petitions") for PM Strijdom, who was absent; stood 30 minutes in silence, sang "Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika" and "Wathint' abafazi, Strijdom!" ("you strike the women, you strike a rock"). 9 August is now National Women's Day. [source: WM; LN]
- **Lilian Ngoyi** (1911–1980): garment worker and trade unionist; joined the ANC in the Defiance Campaign; first woman elected to the ANC National Executive; FEDSAW president (not explicitly stated — "helped launch FEDSAW"); Treason Trialist (acquitted 1961 — LN says 1960); 71 days in solitary 1963; 15 years under banning orders confined to Orlando, Soweto. "Freedom does not come walking towards you - it must be won." (1961, FEDSAW conference). [source: LN]
- **Treason Trial**: 156 leaders arrested in pre-dawn raids in December 1956 (NM; AL: charges from 1 October 1952 to 13 December 1956); all acquitted by 1961. Luthuli: "made one hundred and fifty-six of us, belonging to all the races of our land, into a group of accused". Caveat: FC says the ANC was "banned" at that time — wrong; it was banned in April 1960. [source: TT; AL; ANC]

## 5. PAC, Sharpeville and the turn to armed struggle
- **PAC** founded 1959 by Africanists led by **Robert Sobukwe** (1924–1978), rejecting the Charter's claim that the land "belongs to all who live in it" and multiracial alliances; Sobukwe defined "African" as anyone who owes allegiance to Africa and accepts African majority rule. [source: PAC; RS]
- 21 March 1960: PAC anti-pass campaign — people to leave passes at home and demand arrest. Sobukwe presented himself at Orlando police station; Philip Kgosana led the march at Langa (Cape Town). [source: SM; RS]
- **Sharpeville, 21 March 1960**: crowd of about 5,000 (later said to be up to 20,000); about 130 police with Saracen armoured cars; at 13:30, without warning, police fired 1,344 rounds in about two minutes (police report: about 40 seconds); official toll 69 dead, 180 wounded; newer research: at least 91 dead and at least 238 wounded; many shot in the back. Photographer Ian Berry. TRC (1998): "a degree of deliberation in the decision to open fire". Lt-Col Pienaar: "the native mentality does not allow them to gather for a peaceful demonstration." 21 March is now Human Rights Day. [source: SM]
- Aftermath: state of emergency declared 30 March 1960; over 18,000 detained; UN Security Council Resolution 134 (1 April 1960); ANC and PAC banned (April 1960; Unlawful Organisations Act — act name not in sources read). [source: SM; ANC; APA]
- Sobukwe: three years for incitement, then held on Robben Island under the 1963 "Sobukwe Clause" allowing indefinite renewal; released after six years to house arrest in Kimberley until death (1978). [source: RS; PAC]
- **Umkhonto weSizwe (MK)**: founded 1961 (Mandela, Sisulu, Slovo among founders; Mandela chairman); first attacks 16 December 1961 (the anniversary of the Battle of Blood River) on installations; initially sabotage avoiding loss of life. Manifesto: "The time comes in the life of any nation when there remain only two choices – submit or fight. That time has now come to South Africa." The PAC's armed wing was Poqo. [source: MK; SM]
- Mandela captured near Howick, 5 August 1962. Liliesleaf Farm, Rivonia, raided 11 July 1963. **Rivonia Trial** 9 October 1963 – 12 June 1964 (state alleged 193 or 221 acts of sabotage — sources differ); Mandela, Sisulu, Govan Mbeki, Ahmed Kathrada, Denis Goldberg, Raymond Mhlaba, Elias Motsoaledi, Andrew Mlangeni sentenced to life; prosecution had sought death. Mandela's three-hour "I Am Prepared to Die" statement (20 April 1964 — date not in sources; see NOT VERIFIED). [source: NM; RT; MK]
- Luthuli's role: initially opposed to violence, later accepted it while personally non-violent. [source: AL]

## 6. Robben Island and the long prison years
- Island in Table Bay, about 6.9 km off Bloubergstrand; used for political prisoners 1961–1991 (maximum security closed 1991; criminal prison 1996); museum 1997; UNESCO World Heritage 1999. Former prisoners include Mandela, Jacob Zuma, Kgalema Motlanthe, Sobukwe. [source: RI]
- Mandela there 1964–1982 (18 years): cell 8 by 7 ft; lime quarry from January 1965 (glare damaged his eyes); Class D prisoner — one visit and one letter every six months; studied for a London LLB. Pollsmoor from April 1982; Victor Verster (Paarl) from December 1988; 27 years in prison in all. [source: NM]
- February 1985: Botha offered release if Mandela rejected violence; reply read by daughter Zindzi: "What freedom am I being offered while the organisation of the people remains banned? Only free men can negotiate. A prisoner cannot enter into contracts." [source: NM; APA]
- Secret talks: Justice Minister Kobie Coetsee met Mandela from 1985 (NEG: November 1985 in hospital; NM: "in 1987" — say "from the mid-1980s"); a government team from May 1988; tea with Botha July 1989. [source: NM; NEG]

## 7. Soweto 1976 and Black Consciousness
- **Black Consciousness**: Steve Biko (1946–1977) left white-led NUSAS and led the founding of the **South African Students' Organisation (SASO)** in 1968 (launched July 1969 — SB); "Black" included Africans, Coloureds and Indians; influenced by Fanon; Black People's Convention 1972; Black Community Programmes (clinics, crèches); slogan "Black man, you are on your own"; "black is beautiful". SASO's 1971 manifesto: Black Consciousness is "an attitude of mind, a way of life". The government at first welcomed SASO as matching its own separatism. [source: SB; BCM]
- Biko: "the most potent weapon in the hands of the oppressor is the mind of the oppressed". Banned March 1973 (confined to King William's Town). [source: SB]
- **Biko's death**: arrested at a roadblock near Grahamstown 18 August 1977; held naked and shackled in Port Elizabeth; interrogated 22 hours in room 619 of the Sanlam Building from 6 September and beaten (brain haemorrhage); driven naked and manacled in a Land Rover about 1,100–1,200 km to Pretoria; died alone in a cell 12 September 1977. Police minister Jimmy Kruger first implied a hunger strike; Kruger's remark "Dit laat my koud" ("It leaves me cold") at an NP congress (14 September 1977; reported 15 September). More than 20,000 at the funeral. All BC organisations banned October 1977. Inquest exonerated police; TRC refused amnesty to five officers (December 1998) as their accounts conflicted and the killing lacked a clear political motive; no prosecution (2003); new inquest opened 2025–26. Peter Gabriel's "Biko" (1980); Donald Woods' biography → *Cry Freedom* (1987). [source: SB; WebSearch: https://www.saha.org.za/death_of_biko.htm, https://time.com/3791503/remembering-the-death-of-steve-biko-35-years-later, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Kruger]
- **Soweto uprising**: Afrikaans Medium Decree of 1974 required Afrikaans and English equally in Black schools from Standard 5 (Afrikaans for maths, arithmetic, social studies). Orlando West Junior School strike 30 April 1976; Tsietsi Mashinini (Morris Isaacson High) and the SSRC Action Committee, supported by BC. **16 June 1976**: estimates of marchers range 3,000–20,000 (often "about 20,000"). Official toll 23 dead on the first day; figure "usually given" as 176; estimates up to 700; Cillié Commission: 575 dead by 28 February 1977 (496 Black, 75 Coloured, 2 white, 2 Indian); *The Times*: over 700 by end-1976. 16 June is now Youth Day. [source: SU; APA; BEA]
- **Hector Pieterson** (born 18 August 1963, aged 12): Sam Nzima's photograph of him carried by Mbuyisa Makhubo (Makhubu), with his sister Antoinette (Sithole), then 17, running alongside; journalist Sophie Tema drove him to a clinic, where he was declared dead. Hastings Ndlovu, 15, was actually shot first. Makhubo and Nzima were harassed and went into hiding. Hector Pieterson Museum opened 16 June 2002. Two white people killed by students, including sociologist Melville Edelstein. [source: HP; SU]
- By 19 June 1976, 123 BC leaders banned; thousands of young people left for exile and MK camps (FLS: "Thousands of South African youth traveled to these states to receive training"). [source: BCM; FLS]

## 8. Workers and unions
- **1973 Durban strikes** (from 9 January 1973): about 60,000 Black workers at over 100 firms in the first three months, illegal under the 1953 Native Labour Act; 100,000 Black and Indian workers took action by year-end; led to FOSATU (1979) and COSATU. [source: DUR]
- Black unions legalised for collective bargaining in 1979. [source: APA]
- **COSATU** founded 1 December 1985 (talks of 33 unions at the University of Natal, 30 November); May 1987 stay-away of over 2.5 million workers; COSATU House bombed 7 May 1987; adopted the Freedom Charter July 1987. [source: COS]
- 1987: about 200,000 members of the National Union of Mineworkers (NUM, general secretary Cyril Ramaphosa — not checked) struck for three weeks, the longest strike in South African history to then. [source: APA]
- Economic dependence on Black labour: Thomas Sowell's observation that whites could not fill demand, so the rules were broken "on a massive scale" — optional. [source: APA]

## 9. The 1980s: Tricameral Parliament, UDF, township revolt, emergency
- **Tricameral Parliament** (constitution 1983; 1984–94): House of Assembly (whites, 178), House of Representatives (Coloureds, 85), House of Delegates (Indians, 45); Africans excluded; white referendum 1983 approved; first elections largely boycotted. [source: APA; TRP]
- **UDF** launched 20 August 1983 at Rocklands community hall, Mitchells Plain, Cape Town — delegates from 575 organisations, rally of about 10,000; idea first floated by Rev. Allan Boesak (23 January 1983); presidents Archie Gumede, Oscar Mpetha, Albertina Sisulu; patrons included Tutu, Boesak, Helen Joseph, Mandela; slogan "UDF Unites, Apartheid Divides"; up to about 700–1,000 affiliates; women formed a Women's Congress (1987) complaining of "second-class status"; activities banned 1988; continued as the Mass Democratic Movement; dissolved 14 August 1991. [source: UDF; APA]
- **Vaal uprising** from 3 September 1984 (rent stay-away in the Vaal Triangle; about 29 dead that day); targets were Black local councillors under the Black Local Authorities Act 1982; ANC aim (1985) to make townships "ungovernable"; people's courts; army in townships from October 1984. [source: VT; APA]
- **States of emergency**: 20 July 1985 in 36 magisterial districts; nationwide 12 June 1986, renewed annually until 1990; by 1988 about 30,000 detained without trial; TV cameras banned from unrest areas. [source: APA; SE2; VT]
- **Necklacing**: first TRC-recorded victim Maki Skosana, Duduza, 20 July 1985; Kinikini killing (23 March 1985) first widely reported; the ANC executive condemned it. Winnie Mandela at Munsieville, 13 April 1986: "With our boxes of matches and our necklaces we shall liberate this country." [source: NEC; WMM]
- Churches: Desmond Tutu, Nobel Peace Prize 16 October 1984, cited for his "role as a unifying leader figure in the campaign to resolve the problem of apartheid"; Archbishop of Cape Town; coined "rainbow people of God" (1989), later "Rainbow Nation". [source: DT]
- White opposition: Helen Suzman and the Progressive Federal Party; Black Sash; Nadine Gordimer; about 20% of whites opposed apartheid (undated, treat as approximate). [source: APA]
- Dakar Conference (1987): white Afrikaner intellectuals met the ANC in Senegal. Oliver Tambo (ANC president 1967–91) gave a *Cape Times* interview in October 1985. [source: APA; NEG; OT]

## 10. Winnie Madikizela-Mandela (with the controversies)
- 1936–2018; social worker; married Mandela 1958; detained 491 days from 12 May 1969 (much in solitary, tortured — *491 Days: Prisoner Number 1323/69*); banished to Brandfort 1977–85, where she ran a crèche and clinic with Dr Abu Baker Asvat. [source: WMM]
- From 1986 the "Mandela United Football Club" bodyguards; in 1989 the Mass Democratic Movement disowned her for "violating human rights ... in the name of the struggle". 14-year-old Stompie Seipei abducted 29 December 1988 and killed; she was convicted of kidnapping in 1991 (six years, reduced on appeal — **sources conflict**: NM says to two years; WMM says to a fine. Say "reduced on appeal to a fine and suspended sentence" only if confirmed; safest: "the sentence was reduced on appeal"). TRC found her "politically and morally accountable for the gross violations of human rights committed by the Mandela United Football Club". Convicted of fraud 2003. Separated from Nelson 1992, divorced 1996; MP 1994–2003 and 2009–18. Many South Africans still revere her as "Mother of the Nation" (phrase not checked in source). [source: WMM; NM]

## 11. International campaign and sanctions
- **Boycott Movement** founded London 26 June 1959 after an appeal by Luthuli; renamed **Anti-Apartheid Movement** after Sharpeville; Julius Nyerere: "We are not asking you, the British people, for anything special. We are just asking you to withdraw your support from apartheid by not buying South African goods." Abdul Minty; Trevor Huddleston. Became ACTSA in 1994. Britain was then South Africa's largest foreign investor. [source: AAM]
- **UNGA Resolution 1761**, 6 November 1962: apartheid a threat to international peace; called for breaking diplomatic and trade relations; created the Special Committee against Apartheid, boycotted by all Western states. UNSC Res. 181 (7 August 1963) voluntary arms embargo; **Res. 418 (1977) made the arms embargo mandatory**; UNGA voluntary oil embargo 20 November 1987. [source: UNR; AAM; DSA; SE2]
- Sport: suspended from the 1964 Tokyo Olympics, expelled 1970; Basil D'Oliveira affair 1968; Gleneagles Agreement 1977; 1981 Springbok tour protests in New Zealand; Nigeria led a 32-nation boycott of the 1986 Commonwealth Games over Thatcher's stance; white South Africans in 1977 ranked lack of international sport among apartheid's three most damaging consequences. [source: APA; AAM]
- OAU and the **Lusaka Manifesto** (13 April 1969; 14 states; Malawi refused) preferred peaceful change "if it were possible" but would back armed liberation; Mogadishu Declaration (1971). [source: APA]
- Nordic support; Olof Palme, 21 February 1986: "Apartheid cannot be reformed; it has to be eliminated." [source: APA]
- Western "constructive engagement": Nixon's "Tar Baby" option; Reagan and Thatcher; both governments labelled the ANC "terrorist". [source: APA]
- **Divestment**: Sullivan Principles (1977, Rev. Leon Sullivan, a GM board member); Hampshire College first to divest (1977); University of California withdrew $3 billion (1986) — Mandela called it particularly significant; by end-1989, 26 US states, 22 counties and over 90 cities had taken binding action; Nebraska first state (Ernie Chambers). [source: DSA]
- **Comprehensive Anti-Apartheid Act** (US, 1986): introduced by Ron Dellums; Reagan vetoed (26 September, calling it "economic warfare"); overridden by House 313–83 (29 September) and Senate 78–21 (2 October) — first 20th-century override of a presidential foreign-policy veto; Lugar: "We are against tyranny, and tyranny is in South Africa!"; most sanctions lifted July 1991. [source: CAAA]
- **Financial crisis**: net capital outflow R9.2bn (1985), R6.1bn (1986), R3.1bn (1987), R5.5bn (1988); September 1985 debt standstill and exchange controls; inflation 12–15%. Commonwealth mini-summit agreed a sanctions package in 1986. [source: DSA; APA]
- Opponents of sanctions: Buthelezi ("They can only lead to more hardships, particularly for the blacks"), Suzman, Thatcher ("the way of poverty, starvation..."), Harry Schwarz ("Morality is cheap when someone else is paying."). [source: DSA]
- Mandela's 70th birthday concert at Wembley, July 1988, watched by an estimated 200 million. "Free Nelson Mandela" (The Specials). [source: NM]

## 12. Frontline states and the regional war
- **Frontline States**: recognised as an OAU committee April 1975 (Botswana, Lesotho, Tanzania, Zambia), joined by Angola and Mozambique (1975) and Zimbabwe (1980); chaired by Nyerere until 1985, then Kaunda; economically dependent on South Africa yet sheltered the ANC (HQ in Lusaka) and PAC. [source: FLS]
- South African raids: Operation Protea (1981), Askari (1983) in Angola; Gaborone raid (1985); attacks on bases in Zambia, Mozambique, Zimbabwe, Botswana; agents killed activists in London and Brussels. [source: APA]
- **South African Border War / Namibian War of Independence**: 26 August 1966 – 21 March 1990, SADF vs SWAPO's PLAN, intertwined with Angola's civil war. [source: BW]
- Portugal's 1974 revolution and Angolan/Mozambican independence (1975) encouraged resistance; SADF withdrew from Angola early 1976, which Black students celebrated. [source: APA]
- MK trained in Angola: Novo Catengue camp, over 1,000 recruits 1976–79 under Cuban advisers. 1984 mutinies in Angolan camps put down by the ANC security department (Mbokodo); executions. [source: MK]
- **Cuito Cuanavale** (14 August 1987 – 23 March 1988): FAPLA's Soviet-backed offensive on Mavinga was crushed at the Lomba River by SADF/UNITA; Cubans reinforced FAPLA in the "Tumpo Triangle", where six SADF/UNITA assaults (Operation Packer) failed; the SADF disengaged in March 1988. "Both sides claimed victory." Largest conventional battle in Africa since WWII. Castro: "the overwhelming victory at Cuito Cuanavale..."; Mandela in Cuba (1991) called it "a turning point for the liberation of our continent and my people"; Vanneman and Hampson: no decisive victory, a stalemate exploited for propaganda. Then a Cuban/FAPLA/SWAPO western front (40,000 Cubans); Cuban MiG-23s bombed Calueque dam 27 June 1988, killing 12 SA soldiers. US-mediated talks → New York Accords (22 December 1988): Cuban and South African withdrawal by 1991; Namibia independent 21 March 1990. [source: CC; BW; APA]
- Cold War framing: Pretoria saw itself as a Western bastion against "total onslaught"; Soviet support for the ANC made that claim plausible; after 1989 the fear lost force (de Klerk quote above). [source: SE2; APA; FWK]

## 13. Release, negotiations and the violence of the transition
- 2 February 1990: de Klerk unbanned ANC, PAC, SACP; lifted Separate Amenities Act. **11 February 1990**: Mandela walked out of Victor Verster hand in hand with Winnie; Cape Town City Hall speech: armed struggle continues as "a purely defensive action against the violence of apartheid". 1991: Land Acts, Group Areas Act and Population Registration Act repealed. [source: NM; FWK; GAA; NLA]
- Groote Schuur Minute (2–4 May 1990); Pretoria Minute (6 August 1990) — ANC suspended armed struggle; D. F. Malan Accord (12 February 1991); National Peace Accord (14 September 1991, 26 organisations). [source: NEG]
- **CODESA I**, 20–21 December 1991, World Trade Centre, Kempton Park: 19 parties signed a Declaration of Intent; Mandela denounced de Klerk as "head of an illegitimate, discredited minority regime". CODESA II (May 1992) deadlocked over power-sharing and federalism. [source: NEG; NM]
- White-only referendum, 17 March 1992: "Yes" 1,924,186 vs "No" 875,619 (about 69%; 68.6% of votes cast). [source: R92]
- **Boipatong**, 17 June 1992: about 45 killed by IFP supporters from KwaMadala hostel; ANC walked out of talks alleging police involvement. **Evidence conflicts**: Waddington (Goldstone Commission) and a 1993 trial found no police role; the TRC (1998) found police involvement; the TRC Amnesty Committee (2000) found none. [source: BOI]
- **Bisho**, 7 September 1992: Ciskei Defence Force shot dead 28 ANC marchers and one soldier. [source: BSH]
- Record of Understanding, 26 September 1992 (Ramaphosa–Meyer). Joe Slovo's "sunset clause" paper; ANC NEC endorsed a five-year Government of National Unity in February 1993. Multi-Party Negotiating Forum from 1 April 1993 (26 groups). [source: NEG]
- **Chris Hani** (1942–1993), SACP leader and former MK chief of staff, shot outside his Boksburg home on 10 April 1993 by Polish immigrant Janusz Waluś; Conservative MP Clive Derby-Lewis convicted too; an Afrikaner neighbour, Margareta (Retha) Harmse, took the number plate. Mandela's televised appeal: "A white man, full of prejudice and hate, came to our country and committed a deed so foul that our whole nation now teeters on the brink of disaster. A white woman, of Afrikaner origin, risked her life so that we may know, and bring to justice, this assassin." The election date was then fixed. Tambo died 24 April 1993. [source: CH; NM; OT]
- AWB armoured car through the Kempton Park WTC, 25 June 1993; APLA's St James Church attack (25 July 1993, 11 killed); Bophuthatswana crisis March 1994 (Mangope deposed; AWB intervention, 3 AWB killed on camera). Interim Constitution adopted 18 November 1993; Nobel Peace Prize to Mandela and de Klerk 1993. [source: NEG; APA]
- **Inkatha–ANC conflict**: Buthelezi (ex-ANC Youth League) founded Inkatha 21 March 1975; KwaZulu chief minister; opposed sanctions; received SADF training for militia (Operation Marion); Trust Feed massacre (3 December 1988); pact with the AWB (November 1993); joined the election at the last minute; won KwaZulu-Natal. [source: IFP]
- **"Third force"**: TRC: "while little evidence exists of a centrally directed, coherent or formally constituted 'Third Force', a network of security and ex-security force operatives, frequently acting in conjunction with right-wing elements and/or sectors of the IFP, was involved in actions that could be construed as fomenting violence". [source: TF]
- Death tolls (TRC per APA): about 21,000 political deaths 1948–94 — 7,000 in 1948–89 and 14,000 (plus 22,000 injuries) in 1990–94. Attribution 1960–94: IFP 4,500, security forces 2,700, ANC 1,300. Writer: the transition was the bloodiest phase. [source: APA; TRC]

## 14. The 1994 election
- Voting **26–29 April 1994** (27 April is Freedom Day); 19,726,579 votes counted. ANC 62.65% (252 of 400 seats) — just short of two-thirds; NP 20.39% (82); IFP 10.54% (43); PAC 5 seats. Government of National Unity; deputy presidents Thabo Mbeki and de Klerk; homelands reincorporated. Mandela inaugurated 10 May 1994 at the Union Buildings. [source: E94; APA; BAN; NM]
- Final Constitution adopted 8 May 1996; signed at Sharpeville, 10 December 1996. [source: NEG; SM]
- Mandela–Rugby World Cup 1995 as reconciliation symbol. [source: APA]

## 15. Truth and Reconciliation Commission
- Created by the Promotion of National Unity and Reconciliation Act 34 of 1995; hearings from 1996 (first televised 15 April 1996); chair Desmond Tutu, deputy Alex Boraine; seven of 17 commissioners were women. Three committees: Human Rights Violations (1960–94), Reparation and Rehabilitation, Amnesty. Amnesty required political motive, proportionality and full disclosure; all sides appeared, including the ANC. [source: TRC]
- Numbers: over 19,050 victims of gross violations, plus 2,975 from amnesty applications; 7,111 amnesty applications, 849 granted, 5,392 refused. [source: TRC]
- Women gave more than half of statements, mostly about relatives; three special women's hearings; 70% of the Khulumani Support Group were women. [source: TRC]
- Botha refused to appear; de Klerk apologised but denied a "licence to kill". Biko family challenged the amnesty provision (lost in the Constitutional Court, 1996). Over 300 unresolved cases passed to prosecutors, largely stalled; Ahmed Timol inquest re-opened 2017, police murder found 12 October 2019. Reparations late and smaller than recommended (interim payments only in 2003); Khulumani protests continue. [source: TRC; SB]
- Antjie Krog, *Country of My Skull* (1998), on covering the hearings. [source: TRC]

## 16. After 1994: inequality
- World Bank, *Inequality in Southern Africa* (2022): South Africa ranked most unequal of 164 countries; Gini about 0.63; top 10% hold over 80% of wealth; race accounts for about 41% of income inequality. [source: WebSearch: https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2022-03-13-south-africa-the-worlds-most-unequal-country-world-bank-report/]
- Official unemployment 31.9% (Q3 2023); land reform: within five years only about 1% of land changed hands against an ANC target of 30%. A Black middle class emerged, but within-race inequality grew. [source: EQ]
- Writer: also note gains — expanded housing, electricity, water and services. [source: EQ]

## Debates
- **Liberal vs radical/revisionist (capitalism and apartheid)**: 1970s neo-Marxist "revisionists" (Harold Wolpe, Martin Legassick, Frederick Johnstone) argued apartheid served capital. Wolpe's "Capitalism and cheap labour-power in South Africa: from segregation to apartheid" (*Economy and Society*, 1972): migrant workers could be paid below the cost of reproducing their families because the reserves' subsistence economy bore that cost; apartheid kept a stable urban proletariat from forming. Dan O'Meara called it "probably the most influential and widely-cited theoretical text ever written on South Africa". Liberals (Merle Lipton, *Capitalism and Apartheid*, 1985) argued apartheid's labour controls increasingly hurt capitalist interests and the two were separable; critics said Wolpe overstated the fit; Freund (1987) found Lipton's evidence of a shift away from a functional fit "very strong". Lipton revisited it in *Liberals, Marxists, and Nationalists* (2007). APA also cites Philip Bonner on apartheid's "contradictory economic effects". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Wolpe; WebSearch: https://d.lib.msu.edu/tran/37/FULL_TEXT/download; https://scielo.org.za/pdf/hist/v53n2/18.pdf; https://upjournals.up.ac.za/index.php/historia/article/download/1349/1243/5201]
- **Ideology vs economics in apartheid's origins**: Afrikaner nationalism, Calvinism and the Broederbond (Broederbond recommended mass segregation as early as 1933) versus industrial cheap-labour explanations; Dubow traces segregationist thinking to the 1919–36 period and to "scientific racism". [source: APA; DUB]
- **What ended apartheid?** Internal mass resistance (UDF, COSATU, township revolt), sanctions and capital flight (1985 debt standstill), military stalemate in Angola, the end of the Cold War, demographic change (whites 16% of population vs 20% fifty years earlier), and leadership (de Klerk, Mandela). CAAA article: sanctions' role "a matter of debate"; the US GAO said Reagan only partly enforced them; South Africa's central bank head claimed adjustment. [source: CAAA; DSA; APA; FWK]
- **Cuito Cuanavale**: Cuban/ANC narrative of decisive victory vs SADF/Western analysts' stalemate; SA documents still partly classified. [source: CC; WebSearch Gleijeses]
- **Violence of the transition**: "black-on-black" vs state-fomented ("third force") explanations; Boipatong evidence contradictory; Inkatha's SADF links (Operation Marion) established. [source: TF; BOI; IFP]
- **Armed struggle and its abuses**: TRC found ANC torture "routine" and executions in ANC camps, especially 1979–89, and called the Magoo's Bar (Durban, 1986) bombing a gross violation; Church Street bomb 1983 killed 19. [source: MK]
- **TRC**: praised as restorative alternative to Nuremberg-style trials; criticised by victims (1998 CSVR/Khulumani study: most felt it favoured perpetrators; "justice was a prerequisite for reconciliation"); Biko family ("vehicle for political expediency"); Fullard, Ramphele and Goldblatt: mandate excluded systemic crimes like forced removals and pass arrests; **Mahmood Mamdani**, "Amnesty or Impunity?" (*Diacritics* 32:3–4, 2002): focused on perpetrators and victims instead of beneficiaries and victims, individualised victims, extended impunity to most perpetrators. [source: TRC; WebSearch: https://www.africasacountry.com/2014/01/mamdani-on-the-truth-and-reconciliation-commission]
- **Gandhi's South African record**: saint vs Desai and Vahed's critique of his early views on Africans. [source: MG]
- **Winnie Madikizela-Mandela**: heroine of resistance vs TRC findings of responsibility for abuses. [source: WMM]
- **Mandela's compromise**: right called him a communist terrorist; radical left thought he conceded too much (sunset clauses, dropping nationalisation after Davos 1992). [source: NM; NEG]
