## Frameworks: civilization as an energy story
- Vaclav Smil (born 9 December 1943, Plzeň) is a Czech-Canadian scientist and policy analyst who taught at the University of Manitoba from 1972. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaclav_Smil]
- Smil's *Energy and Civilization: A History* (MIT Press, 2017) is an updated and expanded version of his *Energy in World History* (1994). He also wrote *Energy Transitions: History, Requirements, Prospects* (Praeger, 2010) and *Grand Transitions: How the Modern World Was Made* (2021). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_and_Civilization:_A_History; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaclav_Smil]
- Smil's account (the "transitions" discourse) uses each source's share of primary energy and describes a sequence from biomass to coal to oil to today's mix of mostly coal, oil and gas. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transition]
- Smil is sceptical that a rapid transition to clean energy will happen, believing it will take much longer than many predict. He noted in 2018 that coal, oil and gas still supplied 90% of the world's primary energy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaclav_Smil]
- Jean-Baptiste Fressoz's counter-discourse: "energy transition" was first a politicians' term for a future goal, not a historians' concept for past change. In absolute amounts, every main source has kept growing: more wood was burned in the 19th century even as coal grew, and cars raised both oil use and coal use (for steel). History shows "energy additions", not transitions. His book in English is *More and More and More* (Penguin/Allen Lane, 3 October 2024). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transition]
- When did fossil fuels pass traditional biomass worldwide? Secondary summaries of Smil differ: the late 1880s (a Penn State course summarising Smil's *Energy Myths and Realities*), "around 1900", or the late 1890s (as the published Environmental History chapter says). Hydrocarbons (oil plus gas) later overtook coal; summaries of Smil place oil's rise in the 1960s (oil about 23% in 1965), but no exact year was confirmed. Say "around 1900" and "by the 1960s". [source: WebSearch summaries incl. https://courses.ems.psu.edu/geog432/node/450 and https://www.schoolofpublicpolicy.sk.ca/research-ideas/publications-and-policy-insight/policy-brief/what-we-need-to-know-about-the-pace-of-decarbonization.php]

## Wrigley and Sieferle: the organic economy and the subterranean forest
- E. A. (Tony) Wrigley (born Manchester 17 August 1931; died Cambridge 24 February 2022), Cambridge historian of population and industrialisation; co-author with Roger Schofield of *The Population History of England, 1541–1871* (1981). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Wrigley]
- Wrigley's books on energy: *Continuity, Chance and Change: The Character of the Industrial Revolution in England* (Cambridge University Press, 1988; Wikipedia's bibliography lists a 1990 printing); *Energy and the English Industrial Revolution* (CUP, 2010); *The Path to Sustained Growth: England's Transition from an Organic Economy to an Industrial Revolution* (CUP, 2016). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Wrigley]
- *Continuity, Chance and Change* (1988) contains a chapter titled "The mineral-based energy economy", and a 1989 review says Wrigley distinguished two growth processes, the first of which he called the "advanced organic" economy. [source: WebSearch, https://cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/CBO9781139168045A010/type/BOOK_PART; https://era.library.ualberta.ca/files/tm70mw475/BHR_63_1989_691.pdf]
- Wrigley's argument (2010): in an organic economy, energy came almost exclusively from plant photosynthesis, a solar flow that "scarcely varied from one year to the next"; draught animals were the main source of mechanical energy in early modern England, so more mechanical energy needed more acreage for fodder; land therefore capped growth. Coal "provided an escape route from the constraints of an organic economy" but also brought new dangers. (Paraphrase; the quoted fragments come from Cambridge excerpts quoted in search results, so verify against the book before quoting.) [source: WebSearch, https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/energy-and-the-english-industrial-revolution/transition-from-an-organic-to-an-energyrich-economy/0445DF500DBC78D4EAD3D52EF059C44F; https://assets.cambridge.org/97813165/04284/excerpt/9781316504284_excerpt.pdf]
- In 1700, five-sixths of the world's coal was mined in Britain; without coal Britain would have run out of suitable watermill sites by the 1830s (Wrigley, *Continuity, Chance and Change*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal]
- By 1760 the English used two and a half times more coal than wood, and by about 1800 around ten times more (Paul Ward, Cambridge Group for the History of Population and Social Structure, 2025). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- Rolf Peter Sieferle (born Stuttgart 5 August 1949; died Heidelberg 17 September 2016), German environmental historian. His *Der unterirdische Wald* (1982) appeared in a revised English translation by Michael P. Osman as *The Subterranean Forest: Energy Systems and the Industrial Revolution* (White Horse Press, Cambridge, 2001; revised paperback 2010). It traces the shift from an agrarian "solar energy system" to fossil fuels, and explains why industrialisation began in coal-rich Britain while forested Germany came later. The phrase "subterranean forest" for coal deposits that could replace timberland goes back to the 17th-century German writer Johann Philipp Bünting (per the publisher's description). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolf_Peter_Sieferle; WebSearch, https://www.environmentandsociety.org/mml/subterranean-forest-energy-systems-and-industrial-revolution]
- Caution for the writer: Sieferle's posthumous *Finis Germania* (2017) was denounced as an "extremist tract" and called antisemitic; he had moved to the far right late in life. Cite *The Subterranean Forest* on its merits, but do not present him as an uncontroversial figure if he is profiled. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolf_Peter_Sieferle]

## Muscle, fire and biomass
- Claims for the earliest use of fire by members of *Homo* go back as far as 2.0 million years ago. The oldest definitive evidence of fire-making (striking a new fire) is about 400,000 years old, from a Neanderthal-era site in eastern England with burnt soil, fire-cracked flint handaxes and two pieces of iron pyrite (published in *Nature*, December 2025). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_of_fire_by_early_humans]
- Fire provided warmth, light, protection from predators, better tools and cooking, and so enabled geographic dispersal and changes in diet. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_of_fire_by_early_humans]
- Horse domestication: genomic studies (264 ancient genomes, *Nature*, October 2021) place the domestication of modern horses' ancestors in the Volga–Don region of the Pontic–Caspian steppe around 2200 BCE; the earlier Botai (Kazakhstan) horses were not the ancestors of modern domestic horses. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication_of_the_horse]
- Traditional biomass persists today: energy-poor households rely heavily on wood, charcoal, crop residues and dung; women and girls usually gather fuel and cook, and so bear most of the labour and the exposure to indoor smoke. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_poverty]

## Water and wind: the medieval power revolution
- The earliest evidence of a water-driven wheel is in the treatises of Philo of Byzantium (c. 280–220 BCE). Vitruvius gives the first technical description of a watermill (dated 40/10 BCE), with an undershot wheel and gearing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermill]
- One view (Helaine Selin) places the origin of the watermill in the Persian Empire before 350 BCE; the invention is "open to scholarly discussion". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wheel]
- The 2nd-century CE mill complex at Barbegal in southern France has been called "the greatest known concentration of mechanical power in the ancient world" (Kevin Greene, 2000). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermill]
- China: the waterwheel was used from about 30 CE to drive trip hammers and the bellows of iron furnaces. In 31 CE Du Shi, prefect of Nanyang, used water power to work blast-furnace bellows for cast iron. Huan Tan's *Xin Lun* (c. 20 CE) implies waterwheels already powered pounding machinery. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermill; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wheel]
- Islamic world: engineers took over watermills from former Byzantine provinces; by the 11th century every province from al-Andalus and North Africa to Central Asia had industrial watermills (grist, hulling, saw, stamp, sugar and tide mills). In 10th-century Iraq, ship mills of teak and iron on the Tigris and Euphrates could grind 10 tons of flour a day for Baghdad's granary. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wheel]
- The norias (water-raising wheels) of Hama on the Orontes in Syria are about 20 m in diameter; the largest has 120 water compartments. They survive but now serve only aesthetic purposes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noria]
- India: whether ancient texts describe water-powered wheels is disputed (Needham suggested a noria; Reynolds and Schiøler doubt it). Geared water-raising wheels were introduced from the Islamic world during the Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wheel]
- England's Domesday Book (1086) lists 5,624 mills, a low count because the survey is incomplete; Georges Duby reckoned one mill per 46 peasant households. (The Water wheel article, citing Friedel, says "over six thousand mills".) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domesday_Book; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wheel]
- Joel Mokyr: medieval Europe "became perhaps the first society in human history to build an economy on nonhuman power" (Wikipedia's paraphrase of *The Lever of Riches*, 1990). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- Windmills: there is no historical evidence of windmills before the 9th century CE. The horizontal (panemone) windmill first appeared in Persia in the 9th century; the Persian geographer Estakhri reported windmills in Khorasan, and they spread across the Middle East and Central Asia and later to China and India. A story linking windmills to the caliph Umar (r. 634–644) was recorded only in the 10th century and is doubtful. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windmill; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panemone_windmill]
- The vertical windmill appeared in north-western Europe in the 12th century; the earliest certain reference is from 1185, at Weedley in Yorkshire. Hollow-post mills drained Dutch wetlands from the early 15th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windmill]

## Coal: Song China and Britain
- China: archaeology indicates surface coal mining and household use from about 3490 BCE (Dodson et al., 2014). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_coal_mining]
- Northern Song (960–1127): in the 11th century, deforestation pushed north China's ironworks on the Henan–Hebei border to coke-burning blast furnaces; contemporary accounts estimate at least 140,000 tons of coal a year used by the iron industry there. Coal became the main household fuel in the capital Kaifeng, and twenty new coal markets opened early in the 12th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_in_China]
- The Song produced over 100,000 tonnes (100 million kg) of iron products a year (Ebrey et al.); the 11th-century switch from charcoal to coal in blast furnaces saved forests. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_dynasty]
- Marco Polo described coal burned "through the province of Cathay" and used to heat bathhouses (Yuan China, late 13th century). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_in_China; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_coal_mining]
- Why did Chinese coal not drive an industrial revolution? Pomeranz: China's largest deposits are in the north-west, near the Northern Song core, but the population and industry shifted south between the 12th and 14th centuries, far from the coal; dry northern mines needed ventilation, while Europe's wet mines needed pumping, which the Newcomen engine supplied. Clark and Jacks reply that coal could be substituted without much loss of income, and McCloskey that Britain could have imported coal and China could have moved industry. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- The Romans worked almost all the major coalfields of Britain by the late 2nd century CE. The Domesday Book (1086) records no coal mining. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_coal_mining; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_mining_in_the_United_Kingdom]
- UK coal output peaked in 1913 at 287 million tonnes; mining employment peaked at 1,191,000 in 1920 and fell to 360 by 2022. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_mining_in_the_United_Kingdom]
- Since 1890, coal miners and their unions were often leaders of the left (Britain, Germany, Poland, Japan, Chile, Canada, the US). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_coal_mining]
- Today China produces over half of the world's coal: 4.71 billion tonnes in 2023. Coal provided 59% of China's electricity in 2024 (Financial Times, 2025). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_in_China]

## Steam and fossil industrialization
- Thomas Savery patented the "Miner's Friend" steam pump in 1698; it had no moving parts and could not drain deep mines. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcomen_atmospheric_engine]
- Newcomen's atmospheric engine (1712, at the Conygree coalworks, Tipton, in the Black Country) pumped water out of coal mines. Hundreds were built in the 18th century. Watt's design roughly doubled fuel efficiency. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcomen_atmospheric_engine]
- James Watt conceived the separate condenser in 1765 and patented it in 1769; the engine went into commercial production in 1776 (the first sold to the Carron Company near Falkirk). In 1781 the sun-and-planet gear gave it rotary motion, so it could replace waterwheels. As fully developed it used about 75% less fuel than a comparable Newcomen engine. Watt also introduced the horsepower as a unit. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt_steam_engine]
- William Stanley Jevons, *The Coal Question* (1865), argued that more efficient coal use (as after Watt) increased total coal consumption rather than reducing it: the "Jevons paradox". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jevons_paradox]
- Andreas Malm (born 1977), Swedish human ecologist at Lund University. His PhD thesis (Lund, 2014) and book *Fossil Capital: The Rise of Steam Power and the Roots of Global Warming* (Verso, 2016) studied steam in the British cotton industry c. 1825–1848; the thesis was examined by Timothy Mitchell. Malm also wrote *How to Blow Up a Pipeline* (2021). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andreas_Malm]
- Railways, Newcomen numbers (1,454 by 1800), Darby's coke iron (1709), Bridgewater Canal (1761) and Allen's high-wage, cheap-energy thesis are already in the published Industrial Revolution chapter (see Conflicts section). Robert C. Allen (born 10 January 1947), *The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective* (2009), was an *Economist* and *TLS* book of the year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Allen_(economic_historian)]
- Ammonia synthesis (the Haber–Bosch process) uses about 1–2% of the world's energy supply and 3–5% of its natural gas (Smil, *Enriching the Earth*, and others): a link between fossil energy and food. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process]

## Oil's twentieth century (keep brief; "Oil and Empire" covers the politics)
- Native Americans knew the oil seeps of Oil Creek for centuries. Edwin Drake struck oil on 27 August 1859 near Titusville, Pennsylvania, at a well 69.5 ft (about 21 m) deep, setting off the Pennsylvania oil rush. The title "first commercial oil well" is also claimed for wells in Azerbaijan, Ontario (Oil Springs found oil in 1858), West Virginia, Myanmar (Yenangyaung), Persia, Arabia, Sichuan and Poland (Łukasiewicz). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drake_Well; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Drake]
- Baku: in 1803 Haji Kasimbey Mansurbekov began offshore oil extraction from two wells in Bibi-Heybat bay. Robert Nobel arrived in 1873; Branobel was founded in 1879. Between 1898 and 1901 Baku produced more oil than the United States, and by 1901 more than half the world's oil (11 million tons). On the eve of the First World War three companies (Russian General Oil, Royal Dutch Shell, Nobel) held 86% of the capital of Russia's oil industry. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Azerbaijan]
- Middle East: commercial quantities of oil were struck at Masjed Soleyman, Persia, at 4 a.m. on 26 May 1908 under William Knox D'Arcy's concession: the first oil well in the Middle East. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masjed_Soleyman]
- Kerosene for lamps was oil's first big market; James Young (Scotland) noticed a petroleum seepage in 1847 and refined paraffin. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_petroleum_industry]
- 1973: after Egypt and Syria attacked Israel on 6 October 1973, OAPEC (the Arab producers) embargoed countries that had supported Israel, initially Canada, Japan, the Netherlands, the UK and the US. The embargo was lifted in March 1974; the oil price had risen nearly 300%, from about US$3 to nearly US$12 a barrel. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis]
- 1979: after the Iranian Revolution, world oil supply fell only about 4%, but the price of crude rose from US$15.85 (April 1979) to $39.50 a barrel over 12 months. In November 1978 a strike by 37,000 workers at Iran's nationalised refineries cut output from 6 million to about 1.5 million barrels a day. Afterwards utilities worldwide switched from oil to coal, gas or nuclear power. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_oil_crisis]

## Electricity
- Edison's Pearl Street Station, New York, began generating on 4 September 1882, for 400 lamps belonging to 82 customers; by 1884 it served 508 customers with 10,164 lamps. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_Street_Station]
- Lenin's GOELRO plan (commission founded 21 February 1920) for Soviet electrification: "Communism is Soviet power plus the electrification of the whole country." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GOELRO]
- In 1934 fewer than 11% of US farms had electricity, against nearly 90% in France and Germany. Roosevelt created the Rural Electrification Administration by executive order on 11 May 1935; the Rural Electrification Act of 1936 funded rural cooperatives. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rural_Utilities_Service]
- Household electrification in Europe and North America rose rapidly to about 1930, when (per Wikipedia, figure marked "citation needed") 70% of US households had electricity; rural areas were electrified first in Europe. Treat the 70% as unverified. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrification]
- The Hoover Dam was built from 1931; China's Three Gorges Dam is the world's largest power station by installed capacity. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydropower]
- India: the Rural Electrification Corporation was set up in the 1970s to finance rural power, and the DDUGJY and Saubhagya schemes extended electricity to nearly all households. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity_sector_in_India]

## Energy and power: Mitchell's *Carbon Democracy*
- Timothy Mitchell (Cambridge BA 1977, Princeton PhD 1984), political theorist of Egypt (*Colonising Egypt*, 1991; *Rule of Experts*, 2002), wrote *Carbon Democracy: Political Power in the Age of Oil* (Verso, 2011), developed from a 2009 article in *Economy and Society*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Mitchell; WebSearch]
- Argument (from reviews and summaries; not checked against the book's text): coal's energy moved along narrow, labour-intensive channels (mines, railways, docks), so miners and railway workers could halt economies by striking, and their demands helped produce mass democracy and the welfare state. Oil, extracted by fewer workers and moved by pipeline and tanker, is far harder to disrupt, so it weakened labour's leverage. Mitchell reads the Marshall Plan's support for Middle East oil reaching Europe as partly a way to weaken coal unions. Critics call the materialist approach too narrow and dispute the Marshall Plan reading (US planners also made reviving German coal a priority). [source: WebSearch, https://truthout.org/articles/how-coal-brought-us-democracy-and-oil-ended-it-lessons-from-the-new-book-carbon-democracy; https://energyhumanities.ca/news/carbon-democracy-unfinished-business; https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/268204]
- Mitchell's opening claim, as given in summaries: modern mass politics was made possible by ways of living that used energy on a new scale. Use as paraphrase, not quotation. [source: WebSearch, https://www.sindark.com/2017/05/14/democracy-and-fossil-fuels/]

## Nuclear promise and fear
- The atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima (6 August 1945) and Nagasaki (9 August 1945). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bombings_of_Hiroshima_and_Nagasaki]
- Eisenhower's "Atoms for Peace" speech to the UN General Assembly: 8 December 1953. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atoms_for_Peace]
- Lewis Strauss, chairman of the US Atomic Energy Commission, told the National Association of Science Writers (16 September 1954) that "our children will enjoy in their homes electrical energy too cheap to meter". His son later claimed he meant fusion; the speech's context was fission. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Too_cheap_to_meter]
- Obninsk (USSR): the first nuclear power plant connected to a grid, on 27 June 1954. Calder Hall (UK), the first full-scale nuclear power station, was opened by Queen Elizabeth II on 17 October 1956. Shippingport (US) reached criticality on 2 December 1957 and produced first power on 18 December 1957. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obninsk_Nuclear_Power_Plant; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calder_Hall_nuclear_power_station; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shippingport_Atomic_Power_Station]
- World nuclear capacity reached 100 GW in the late 1970s and 300 GW by 1990. Three Mile Island (28 March 1979, US: a partial meltdown) and Chernobyl slowed growth. In 2023 nuclear supplied 2,602 TWh, about 9% of world electricity; France gets the highest share of its electricity from nuclear (65%). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Mile_Island_accident]
- Chernobyl: reactor 4 exploded at 01:23 on 26 April 1986 near Pripyat, Ukrainian SSR, during a safety test. Two workers were killed by the explosion and 28 died of acute radiation sickness within three months; estimates of eventual deaths from long-term effects range from about 4,000 to 16,000. About 49,000 people were evacuated from a 10 km zone after 36 hours, and about 68,000 more when it was widened to 30 km; permanent resettlement reached about 350,000 by 2000. More than 500,000 "liquidators" took part in the response. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernobyl_disaster]
- Fukushima Daiichi: began 11 March 2011 after the magnitude-9.0 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami knocked out power and cooling. INES Level 7. One death from radiation is suspected (lung cancer, four years later); UNSCEAR found no documented adverse health effects in residents directly attributable to radiation. At least 164,000 people were displaced, and the displacement caused at least 51 deaths; over 41,000 were still evacuees ten years later. Germany then decided to phase out its reactors by 2022. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_nuclear_accident]

## The Great Acceleration
- The Anthropocene Working Group (May 2019) proposed a mid-20th-century start, when population, industrial production and agrochemicals accelerated and bomb radionuclides entered sediments and ice. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene]
- J. R. McNeill and Peter Engelke, *The Great Acceleration: An Environmental History of the Anthropocene Since 1945* (Harvard University Press, 2016). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._R._McNeill]

## Energy poverty and inequality today
- Tracking SDG7 2025 (IEA, IRENA, UN Statistics Division, World Bank, WHO; 2023 data): almost 92% of the world had electricity; over 666 million people were without it, 85% of them in sub-Saharan Africa, mostly rural; about 2.1 billion lacked clean cooking (47% in Asia, 47% in Africa). [source: WebSearch, https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2025/06/25/energy-access-has-improved-yet-international-financial-support-still-needed-to-boost-progress-and-address-disparities]
- Tracking SDG7 2026 (published June 2026; 2024 data): 655 million people without electricity worldwide, with sub-Saharan Africa about 86% of the gap (563 million, against 565 million a year earlier). The IEA's own method gives about 730 million for 2024 (down only 11 million on 2023). Do not mix the two series; say "between about 650 and 730 million, depending on the estimate (2024)". [source: WebSearch, https://www.iea.org/news/655-million-people-still-living-without-electricity-underscore-urgent-need-to-deliver-on-universal-energy-access-target; https://iea.org/commentaries/access-to-electricity-stagnates-leaving-globally-730-million-in-the-dark; https://allafrica.com/stories/202606290024.html]
- Ninety per cent of children in sub-Saharan Africa attend primary schools without electricity (Goodwin 2013, an older figure). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_poverty]
- Historically, 5% of countries account for about 68% of total greenhouse-gas emissions, and the lowest-emitting 50% of countries for 0.74%. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_poverty]
- Energy transition minerals: the IEA lists 37 critical minerals and expects demand for them to rise by 235% by 2050; mining for lithium, cobalt, nickel and copper has created new environmental-justice conflicts. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transition]

## The renewable transition now
- Solar cell prices fell from US$76.67 per watt (1977) to $0.36 (2014), and spot prices reached $0.13 per watt in August 2018 (nearly 600 times less). Swanson's law: module prices fall about 20% for each doubling of cumulative shipments. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swanson%27s_law; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growth_of_photovoltaics]
- Early solar research from the 1860s (Augustin Mouchot) was driven by fear that coal would run out. Growth shifted from Europe to Asia, and Chinese manufacturers became the largest. In 2022 over 40% of global polysilicon capacity was in Xinjiang. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_power]
- Utility-scale solar and onshore wind have the cheapest levelised cost of electricity for new plants in most countries (Lazard 2023). Solar power density averages about 7 W/m² (lifecycle), against about 240 for nuclear and 480 for gas: a land-use constraint. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_power]
- Ember, *Global Electricity Review 2026* (21 April 2026, 2025 data): solar generated about 9% of world electricity (7% in 2024). Renewables reached 33.8% of world electricity, just above coal's 33%, for the first time; solar overtook wind; solar met roughly three-quarters of demand growth; low-carbon sources were about 42.6%. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_power; WebSearch, https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/global-electricity-review-2026/2025-in-review/]
- Energy Institute, *Statistical Review of World Energy* 2026 (75th edition, released 30 June 2026, 2025 data): total energy supply rose 1.7% to more than 600 EJ, a record; fossil fuels still supplied about 86% (about 87% in 2024 in the 2025 edition); renewables were the largest source of supply growth for the first time this century outside a recession year, yet coal, oil, gas, nuclear and hydro all reached record highs; CO2 emissions rose 1.1% to about 41 billion tonnes. Percentages depend on the accounting method (the review changed method from 2024). [source: WebSearch, https://dieselnet.com/news/2026/07energyreview.php; https://kpmg.com/ua/en/insights/2026/08/2026-statistical-review-of-world-energy.html]
- Grids: the IEA's "Electricity Grids and Secure Energy Transitions" report calls for grid investment to rise from $300 billion to over $600 billion a year by 2030 and for 80 million km of grid by 2040. Heat pumps meet only about 5% of space and water heating (IEA, 2020). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transition]
- Hydroelectricity is the largest renewable source of electricity, about 16% of the world's electricity in 2019 (IEA). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transition]

## Pace of transitions
- Benjamin Sovacool ("How long will it take? Conceptualizing the temporal dynamics of energy transitions", *Energy Research & Social Science*, 2016) gives wood-to-coal in Europe as taking 96–160 years and electricity 47–69 years to reach mainstream use, but points to fast cases such as Ontario's exit from coal (2003–2014), driven by strong government action; press coverage turned this into "phased out in a decade". Grubler, Wilson and Nemet (2016) replied that such comparisons are "apples and oranges". [source: WebSearch, https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/How_long_will_it_take_Conceptualizing_the_temporal_dynamics_of_energy_transitions/23427242; https://research-portal.uea.ac.uk/en/publications/apples-oranges-and-consistent-comparisons-of-the-temporal-dynamic/]
- Smil: transitions take decades; fossil fuels' global share has scarcely fallen. Fressoz: there has never been a true transition, only additions. Ember-style analysts read the 2025 data as a tipping point in electricity. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaclav_Smil; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transition; WebSearch, https://dieselnet.com/news/2026/07energyreview.php]

## Debates
- **Transitions or additions?** Smil (sequential shares: biomass, then coal, then oil, slow decades-long shifts) against Fressoz (absolute use of every source keeps rising; "transition" is a political term for a future goal). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transition]
- **Why Britain, why coal?** Wrigley (escape from the organic economy's land limit) and Sieferle (subterranean forest); Pomeranz (coal's location plus colonies' "land"); Allen (high wages plus cheap coal made labour-saving, coal-burning machines pay); against Clark and Jacks (coal was substitutable) and McCloskey (coal could have been imported; ideas and dignity mattered more). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Allen_(economic_historian)]
- **Why steam?** Malm: cotton masters turned from water to steam to gain control over labour and location (mills in towns), not because water ran out. Covered in the Industrial Revolution chapter. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andreas_Malm]
- **Energy and political power:** Mitchell (coal's chokepoints empowered workers and democracy; oil's flows weakened them) against critics who find the account too materialist and dispute the Marshall Plan reading. [source: WebSearch, https://truthout.org/articles/how-coal-brought-us-democracy-and-oil-ended-it-lessons-from-the-new-book-carbon-democracy]
- **Can this transition be faster?** Sovacool (fast national cases such as Ontario) against Grubler, Wilson and Nemet (inconsistent comparisons) and Smil (scepticism). The 2025 data are read both ways: record renewable growth (Ember) but record fossil use too (Energy Institute). [source: WebSearch, as above]
- **Nuclear risk:** death tolls from Chernobyl's long-term effects range from about 4,000 to 16,000; at Fukushima the evacuation itself caused more deaths (at least 51) than radiation (one suspected), and the evacuation "was accused of causing more harm than it prevented". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernobyl_disaster; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_nuclear_accident]
- **Origins of the watermill and windmill:** Greek/Hellenistic, Persian (Selin) and Chinese claims for the watermill; Persian origin of the horizontal windmill against independent European invention of the vertical post mill. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wheel; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windmill]
