# Verified facts — History of Civil Liberties (researched 2026-11-12)

## Ancient and early modern antecedents (outside Europe)

- The Cyrus Cylinder is a clay cylinder inscribed in Akkadian cuneiform in the name of Cyrus the Great. It was made as a foundation deposit after the Persian conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE (the British Museum dates it to about 539–538 BCE). Hormuzd Rassam found it at Babylon in March 1879, and it has been in the British Museum since then. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_Cylinder]
- The text praises Cyrus as chosen by Marduk, denounces the deposed king Nabonidus, and says Cyrus restored temples and returned deported peoples to their homes. It names only Mesopotamian sanctuaries and does not mention Jews, Jerusalem or Judea, although it is often read alongside the Book of Ezra's account of the return. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_Cylinder]
- Mohammad Reza Shah promoted it from the late 1960s as "the first charter of human rights". It was the emblem of the 1971 celebration of 2,500 years of Persian monarchy and was lent to Tehran from 7 to 22 October 1971. Princess Ashraf Pahlavi gave a replica to UN Secretary-General U Thant. In 2010 President Mahmoud Ahmadinejad also called it the "First Charter of Human Rights". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_Cylinder]
- Ashoka, the Mauryan emperor, ruled from about 268 to 232 BCE. His edicts are more than thirty inscriptions on pillars, boulders and cave walls across modern India, Nepal, Bangladesh, Pakistan and Afghanistan. Most are in Prakrit (in Brahmi and Kharoshthi scripts), and a few are in Greek and Aramaic. According to his own inscriptions, his turn to *dhamma* followed his remorse over the Kalinga war in the eighth year of his reign. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edicts_of_Ashoka]
- Major Rock Edict 7 says the king "wishes that all sects may dwell in all places". Edict 12 warns that whoever praises his own sect and blames others "injures his own sect very severely" and calls for "concord" (Hultzsch's translation, 1925). He built the Barabar caves for the Ajivika ascetics as well as for Buddhists. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edicts_of_Ashoka]
- An Ashokan pillar edict orders uniform judicial procedure and gives three days' respite to prisoners sentenced to death, so that relatives can plead for them. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edicts_of_Ashoka]
- Caveat on Ashoka: in the newly conquered south he used northern Prakrit and Brahmi rather than local Dravidian languages, which some scholars read as a kind of authoritarianism. The edicts are royal moral instruction, not grants of rights. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edicts_of_Ashoka]
- The Kouroukan Fouga (Manden Charter) is said in the Epic of Sundiata to have been proclaimed by an assembly after the Battle of Krina (1235), founding the Mali Empire. The epic is oral; its earliest versions were collected in the 1890s. A text of the "charter" was reconstructed from oral tradition at a workshop in Kankan, Guinea, in 1998. UNESCO inscribed "The Manden Charter, proclaimed in Kurukan Fuga" on its intangible heritage list in 2009. Its articles include "do not maltreat the foreigners". Present it as an oral tradition with a modern reconstruction, not as a thirteenth-century document. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kouroukan_Fouga]
- Akbar (Mughal emperor 1556–1605) abolished the jizya tax on non-Muslims and appointed non-Muslims to high civil and military posts. The Din-i Ilahi article dates the repeal of the jizya to 1568 (see NOT VERIFIED for other dates). In 1575 he built the Ibadat Khana ("House of Worship") at Fatehpur Sikri for debates among faiths, and he ended the debates in 1582 after they grew acrimonious. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akbar; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Din-i_Ilahi]
- *Sulh-i kul* means "universal peace". M. Athar Ali argued that Akbar adopted it as general imperial administrative policy, not only for religious purposes. His son Jahangir did not continue his tolerance. Aurangzeb reimposed the jizya in 1679, as the published Mughal chapter says. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akbar; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Din-i_Ilahi]
- The Ottoman "millet system" was not systematic before the nineteenth century. Non-Muslims (dhimmi) had wide communal autonomy in personal law, education and tax collection, without an overarching structure. Systematic use of *millet* for the Greek Orthodox, Armenian and Jewish communities dates from Mahmud II (r. 1808–1839). Communities had autonomy but were politically subordinate in an unequal hierarchy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet_(Ottoman_Empire)]
- On 3 November 1839 the Edict of Gülhane (Abdülmecid I, drafted at the urging of Mustafa Reşid Pasha) promised guarantees of rights to all Ottoman subjects regardless of religion or ethnicity. It made royal promises rather than legal changes, and they were never fully implemented. It was followed by the Reform Edict (Hatt-ı Hümayun) of 18 February 1856, issued under Western pressure after the Crimean War. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edict_of_Gülhane]

## England: Magna Carta, habeas corpus, 1689

- Magna Carta was sealed by King John at Runnymede on 15 June 1215 to make peace with rebel barons. It promised protection of church rights, protection for the barons from illegal imprisonment, swift justice and limits on feudal payments, enforced by a council of 25 barons (clause 61). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_Carta]
- Pope Innocent III annulled it in a letter dated 24 August 1215, calling it "null, and void of all validity for ever", and the First Barons' War followed. It was reissued in 1216, 1217 (when it got the name Magna Carta) and 1225, and Edward I confirmed it as statute in 1297. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_Carta]
- The myth: from the late sixteenth century, lawyers such as Sir Edward Coke presented it as restoring ancient Anglo-Saxon liberties and as the foundation of habeas corpus. That reading influenced the American colonists. Victorian historians showed it concerned the king and the barons, not ordinary subjects. Most historians now regard its interpretation as an early charter of universal rights as a myth created centuries later. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_Carta]
- In 1354 a statute of Edward III rewrote "free man" as "no man, of whatever estate or condition he may be" and introduced the phrase "due process of law". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_Carta]
- Three clauses remain law in England and Wales: the freedom of the English Church, the liberties of the City of London, and due process (1215 clauses 39–40, clause 29 of the 1297 statute). Clause 29 also remains in force in New Zealand and some Australian states. Four exemplifications of the 1215 charter survive: two at the British Library, one at Lincoln and one at Salisbury. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_Carta]
- The writ of habeas corpus is older than the 1679 Act: it existed for centuries before and is thought to derive from the Assize of Clarendon (1166). The Habeas Corpus Act 1679, passed under Charles II and promoted by the Earl of Shaftesbury as part of the Exclusion struggle, strengthened the procedure. It is often wrongly described as the origin of the writ. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habeas_Corpus_Act_1679]
- The story that the 1679 Act passed only because the Lords' tellers miscounted as a joke is "popular but likely untrue" (Helen Nutting, *American Historical Review*, 1960). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habeas_Corpus_Act_1679]
- Habeas corpus was suspended in Britain, for example by the Habeas Corpus Suspension Act 1794, and internment without trial was authorised by statute in both World Wars and during the Troubles in Northern Ireland. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habeas_corpus]
- The Bill of Rights 1689 enacted the Declaration of Right that had been read to William and Mary on 13 February 1689, and received royal assent on 16 December 1689. Among its provisions: suspending or dispensing with laws without Parliament is illegal; taxation without Parliament is illegal; subjects may petition the king; no standing army in peacetime without Parliament's consent; "Protestants may have arms for their defence"; elections ought to be free; freedom of speech and debates in Parliament may not be questioned outside Parliament; "excessive bail ought not to be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel and unusual punishments inflicted". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_of_Rights_1689]
- Caveat: the 1689 "freedom of speech" is parliamentary privilege, not a general right of the public. The Bill of Rights influenced the Virginia Declaration of Rights (1776) and the US Bill of Rights. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_of_Rights_1689]
- The Toleration Act (1689, formally dated 1688) gave freedom of worship to Protestant nonconformists who took the oaths, but deliberately excluded Roman Catholics, Jews, nontrinitarians and atheists. Dissenters stayed excluded from office and the universities. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toleration_Act_1688]
- John Milton's *Areopagitica* (published 23 November 1644) attacked Parliament's Licensing Order of 1643, which required licensing before publication. In Britain, pre-publication licensing ended with the lapse of the Licensing of the Press Act in 1695. Sweden's Freedom of the Press Act of 1766 was the first legal protection of press freedom in the world. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Areopagitica; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press]

## Enlightenment foundations

- Locke wrote *A Letter Concerning Toleration* in Latin in the winter of 1685–86, in exile in the Dutch Republic, addressed to Philipp van Limborch. William Popple published an English translation in 1689 without Locke's knowledge. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Letter_Concerning_Toleration]
- Locke's limits: no toleration for a church whose members "pass into the allegiance and service of another prince", which is traditionally read as Catholics, though Mark Goldie and others argue he did not rule them out in principle. He also excluded atheists, because oaths could not bind them (see Quotations). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Letter_Concerning_Toleration; https://www.freespeechhistory.com/timeline/1689-a-letter-concerning-toleration/]
- Montesquieu's *De l'esprit des lois* (*The Spirit of the Laws*) was published in 1748. It argued for constitutional government with separation of powers, the rule of law and civil liberties. It influenced Catherine the Great's *Nakaz* and the framers of the US Constitution. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Spirit_of_Law]
- Voltaire's *Treatise on Tolerance* followed the case of Jean Calas, a Protestant merchant tortured and executed at Toulouse on 10 March 1762 on a baseless charge of killing his son. Voltaire finished it by 2 January 1763 and it was printed in Geneva in April 1763. It was quickly banned. In January 2015, after the *Charlie Hebdo* shooting, it became a bestseller in France. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise_on_Tolerance]
- Cesare Beccaria's *Dei delitti e delle pene* (*On Crimes and Punishments*, 1764) condemned torture and the death penalty and was the founding work of penology. It came out of the Milan circle of the journal *Il Caffè*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Crimes_and_Punishments]

## The age of revolutions: 1789, 1791, 1805

- The US Constitution of 1787 had no bill of rights. George Mason and Elbridge Gerry's motion of 12 September 1787 to add one was quickly rejected. Anti-Federalists made the omission a central objection during ratification. Madison at first opposed a bill of rights but then drafted it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Bill_of_Rights]
- Congress approved twelve amendments on 25 September 1789, and ten were ratified on 15 December 1791. They bound only the federal government. Under the Fourteenth Amendment (1868), courts applied them to the states piece by piece from the early twentieth century ("incorporation"). In *Gitlow v. New York* (1925), an ACLU case, the Court accepted that First Amendment speech protection applied against the states, though it upheld Gitlow's conviction. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Bill_of_Rights; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_Liberties_Union]
- The Fourth Amendment protects "the right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects" against unreasonable searches and seizures. It is the textual root of US privacy law. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution]
- France's National Constituent Assembly adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen on 26 August 1789. Lafayette drafted early versions in consultation with Jefferson, and much of the final text came from Sieyès, edited by Mirabeau. Article XI makes free communication of thoughts and opinions "one of the most precious rights of man". It remains part of France's 1958 constitutional order. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Man_and_of_the_Citizen]
- Exclusions: the Constitution of 1791 created "passive citizens" (women, the poor, servants, the enslaved and others). About 4.3 million Frenchmen got the vote (Doyle) out of about 29 million people; the population figure is marked "citation needed". The Declaration did not abolish colonial slavery. The Convention abolished slavery on 4 February 1794, Napoleon reinstated it in 1802, and France abolished it again in 1848. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Man_and_of_the_Citizen; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism]
- Olympe de Gouges (born Marie Gouze, 7 May 1748) wrote the *Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen* on 14 September 1791 and published it on 15 September. It followed the 1789 text article by article and was dedicated to Marie Antoinette. The Revolutionary Tribunal condemned her and she was guillotined on 3 November 1793. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Woman_and_of_the_Female_Citizen; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympe_de_Gouges]
- Haiti: Dessalines declared independence on 1 January 1804, crowned himself emperor, and the imperial constitution was promulgated on 20 May 1805. The 1805 text (English translation in the *New-York Evening Post*, July 1805): Article 2, "Slavery is forever abolished"; Article 12 bars any white man "of whatever nation" from setting foot on the territory as master or proprietor or acquiring property, with exceptions (Art. 13) for naturalised white women and naturalised Germans and Poles; Article 14 ends all distinctions of colour and says Haitians will be known only "by the generic appellation of Blacks". Article 50 says "the law admits of no predominant religion" and Article 51 that freedom of worship is tolerated. Julia Gaffield notes that article numbering varies between printings. Under Dessalines, plantation labour was also enforced. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Dessalines; https://www.college.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/1805%20Haitian%20Constitution.pdf (via search summary; the PDF itself could not be fetched); https://haitidoi.com/2015/12/21/race-and-the-haitian-constitution-of-1805/]

## Abolition and women's suffrage (brief; cross-refer to the Abolition chapter)

- The British Slavery Abolition Act received royal assent on 28 August 1833 and took effect on 1 August 1834. It imposed "apprenticeship" on the freed people and paid £20 million in compensation to slaveholders. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_Abolition_Act_1833]
- The US Thirteenth Amendment was ratified on 6 December 1865 and proclaimed on 18 December 1865. It abolished slavery "except as a punishment for crime". Brazil, in 1888, was the last country in the Americas to abolish slavery. Mauritania formally abolished it by presidential decree in 1981. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism]
- New Zealand was the first self-governing country to give women the vote: royal assent on 19 September 1893, after a campaign led by Kate Sheppard. Māori women were included. The Cook Islands followed in 1893 and Finland in 1906, the first in Europe. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage_in_New_Zealand; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage]
- Britain gave women over 30 the vote in 1918 and equal terms (age 21) in 1928. The US Nineteenth Amendment took effect on 18 August 1920 and was certified on 26 August 1920, but in practice it did not enfranchise many African American, Asian American, Hispanic and Native American women. France followed in 1944 and Switzerland at the federal level in 1971. Saudi women first voted in the municipal elections of 2015. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution]

## Nineteenth-century press freedom, censorship and constitutions

- The Carlsbad Decrees were adopted by the German Confederation on 20 September 1819 at Metternich's instigation, after the student Karl Ludwig Sand murdered the writer August von Kotzebue on 23 March 1819. They dissolved the student fraternities and set up university inspectors and press censors. They were abrogated on 2 April 1848. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlsbad_Decrees]
- The Meiji Constitution of Japan was promulgated on 11 February 1889. Its chapter "Rights and Duties of Subjects" (Articles 18–32) granted freedom of movement, privacy of correspondence, property, and speech, assembly and association (Art. 29), and religion (Art. 28) "within limits not prejudicial to peace and order, and not antagonistic to their duties as subjects". The liberties were "freely subject to limitation by law" (Oda, *Japanese Law*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Constitution]
- Japan's Peace Preservation Law (22 April 1925) punished forming or knowingly joining an association aimed at "altering the kokutai" or the private-property system. More than 70,000 people were arrested under it before Allied occupation authorities repealed it on 15 October 1945. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_Preservation_Law]

## Colonial emergency law: India

- Section 124A (sedition, "exciting disaffection") was added to the Indian Penal Code in 1870 on James Fitzjames Stephen's suggestion; Macaulay's 1837 draft had a similar clause. It was used against Bal Gangadhar Tilak (convicted in 1897, sentenced to 18 months; convicted again in 1908, six years in Burma) and against Gandhi in 1922. At his trial Gandhi called it the "prince among the political sections of the Indian Penal Code designed to suppress the liberty of the citizen". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_124A_of_the_Indian_Penal_Code]
- It outlived the Raj. It became a cognisable offence (arrest without warrant) under Indira Gandhi in 1973, and the Supreme Court put it on hold on 11 May 2022. The 2023 criminal codes (in force 2024, the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita) replaced it; Wikipedia names the successor as a "waging war" provision (see NOT VERIFIED on the section number). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_124A_of_the_Indian_Penal_Code]
- The Rowlatt Act (Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act 1919) was passed by the Imperial Legislative Council on 18 March 1919 over the opposition of its Indian members and came into effect on 21 March 1919. It extended wartime Defence of India Act powers, including detention for up to two years without trial, and press controls. Jinnah called it a "Black Act" and resigned from the Council, as did Madan Mohan Malaviya and Mazharul Haque. Gandhi's satyagraha and hartals (Delhi, 30 March) followed, then the Punjab disturbances and Amritsar. It was repealed by the Special Laws Repeal Act 1922. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rowlatt_Act]
- India's Emergency lasted from 25 June 1975 to 21 March 1977, proclaimed under Article 352 for "internal disturbance". It brought rule by decree, postponed elections and press censorship. 34,988 people were arrested under MISA and 75,818 under the Defence of India rules. A coercive vasectomy campaign was led by Sanjay Gandhi. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Emergency_(India)]
- In *ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla* (1976) the Supreme Court held that detainees could not seek habeas corpus while the right to life and liberty (Article 21) was suspended. Justice H. R. Khanna was the sole dissenter of five judges and was later passed over for Chief Justice. A nine-judge bench in *Puttaswamy* (2017, the right-to-privacy case) repudiated the ruling and said Khanna "was clearly right". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ADM_Jabalpur_v._Shivkant_Shukla]

## United States: wartime and security rollbacks

- The Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798 were four statutes signed between 18 June and 14 July 1798, the Sedition Act on 14 July. They were passed under John Adams during the Quasi-War with France. They lengthened naturalisation, let the president detain and deport aliens, and criminalised "false" or "malicious" statements against the government. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien_and_Sedition_Acts]
- At least four publishers, a protester, a congressman (Matthew Lyon of Vermont, the first tried) and a drunken bystander were prosecuted under the Sedition Act between 1798 and 1801. The prosecutions helped Jefferson win the 1800 election. The Sedition Act and Alien Friends Act expired, and the Naturalization Act was repealed in 1802. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien_and_Sedition_Acts]
- The Alien Enemies Act of 1798 is still in force. It was used in the War of 1812 and both World Wars, including as authority in the internment of German, Italian and Japanese nationals. President Trump invoked it in March 2025 for deportations, and litigation followed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien_and_Sedition_Acts]
- Civil War: on 27 April 1861 Lincoln authorised General Winfield Scott to suspend habeas corpus along the military line between Philadelphia and Washington. In *Ex parte Merryman* (1861) Chief Justice Taney held that only Congress could suspend the writ, and Lincoln did not comply. Congress passed the Habeas Corpus Suspension Act, signed on 3 March 1863. In *Ex parte Milligan* (1866) the Supreme Court held that military tribunals could not try civilians where civil courts were operating. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex_parte_Merryman; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habeas_Corpus_Suspension_Act_(1863); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex_parte_Milligan]
- First World War: the Sedition Act of 1918 (an amendment to the Espionage Act of 1917) criminalised "disloyal, profane, scurrilous, or abusive language" about the government, flag or armed forces. Eugene V. Debs got ten years; Harding commuted his sentence in December 1921. The 1918 Act was repealed on 13 December 1920. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedition_Act_of_1918]
- In *Schenck v. United States* (1919) a unanimous Court, in an opinion by Holmes, upheld a conviction for anti-draft leaflets and set the "clear and present danger" test. Holmes began dissenting in *Abrams* (1919). *Brandenburg v. Ohio* (1969) largely overturned *Schenck*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schenck_v._United_States]
- The Palmer raids ran in November 1919 and January 1920 under Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer, with J. Edgar Hoover heading the new General Intelligence Division. Figures differ within the source: "6,000 people arrested across 36 cities" in the lead, "at least 3,000" in the January raids, and "Of the 10,000 arrested, 3,500 were held". All versions agree that 556 resident aliens were deported. Acting Labor Secretary Louis F. Post cancelled more than 2,000 warrants as illegal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palmer_raids]
- The ACLU grew from the National Civil Liberties Bureau, founded in 1917 by Crystal Eastman and Roger Nash Baldwin to defend anti-war speech and conscientious objectors. It was renamed the ACLU on 19 January 1920. It was the first such body not tied to one group or issue. It recruited John Scopes for the 1925 evolution trial (Clarence Darrow led the defence), and in 1977–78 defended a neo-Nazi group's right to march in Skokie, Illinois, a mostly Jewish town. Its 2024 budget was $383 million. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_Liberties_Union]
- The ACLU was internally divided over the Japanese American cases. Of about 120,000 people affected, only 12 disobeyed the orders. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_Liberties_Union]
- Executive Order 9066 was signed by Franklin D. Roosevelt on 19 February 1942. About 120,000 people of Japanese descent (sources range from 110,000 to 120,000), 62% of them US citizens, were removed and held in ten camps run by the War Relocation Authority. Canada acted similarly, and thirteen Latin American countries sent 2,264 people of Japanese descent to the US for internment. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internment_of_Japanese_Americans; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Liberties_Act_of_1988]
- *Korematsu v. United States* was decided on 18 December 1944, 6–3, opinion by Hugo Black, with Murphy, Jackson and Roberts dissenting. A federal court vacated Korematsu's conviction in 1983 because the Solicitor General had suppressed a Naval Intelligence report that found no evidence of Japanese American espionage. The 1983 report of the Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians, *Personal Justice Denied*, found little evidence of disloyalty. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korematsu_v._United_States; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internment_of_Japanese_Americans]
- In *Trump v. Hawaii* (2018) Chief Justice Roberts wrote that *Korematsu* "was gravely wrong the day it was decided". The Congressional Research Service lists it as overruled; some scholars call it a "disavowal" rather than a formal overruling. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korematsu_v._United_States]
- The Civil Liberties Act was signed by Reagan on 10 August 1988. It gave each surviving internee $20,000 and attributed the incarceration to "race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership". The first payments came on 9 October 1990 with an apology signed by George H. W. Bush. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Liberties_Act_of_1988]
- McCarthyism: Truman's Executive Order 9835 (21 March 1947) created federal loyalty screening. By 1958, an estimated one in five US employees had to pass some loyalty review. McCarthy's Wheeling speech was on 9 February 1950 ("a list of 205", as usually quoted). The Senate censured him on 2 December 1954 by 67 to 22. Targets included the Hollywood Ten and other blacklisted people, and gay federal employees in the "lavender scare" (historian David K. Johnson). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCarthyism]

## US civil rights movement (brief; cross-refer to Modern America)

- *Brown v. Board of Education* (17 May 1954) was unanimous: separate schools are "inherently unequal". It partly overruled *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896). A second ruling followed on 31 May 1955. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_v._Board_of_Education]
- The Civil Rights Act was signed on 2 July 1964 after a Senate filibuster. Kennedy proposed it in June 1963 and Johnson pushed it through after the assassination. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1964]
- The Voting Rights Act was signed on 6 August 1965. Its Section 5 "preclearance" was nullified in effect by *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act_of_1965]
- Due process and privacy cases from the same era: *Mapp v. Ohio* (1961), *Gideon v. Wainwright* (1963), *Griswold v. Connecticut* (1965, contraception and privacy) and *Miranda v. Arizona* (1966). Dates are from the article titles and short descriptions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mapp_v._Ohio; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griswold_v._Connecticut; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miranda_v._Arizona]

## International human rights law

- The UDHR was adopted as General Assembly Resolution 217 A (III) on 10 December 1948 at the Palais de Chaillot, Paris. It has 30 articles. The vote was 48 in favour, none against, and 8 abstentions: the USSR, the Byelorussian and Ukrainian SSRs, Czechoslovakia, Poland, Yugoslavia, South Africa and Saudi Arabia. Saudi Arabia objected chiefly to Article 18 (freedom to change religion). It is a declaration, not a treaty, but many international lawyers regard it as customary international law. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights]
- Drafters: Eleanor Roosevelt chaired the Commission on Human Rights (18 members). John Peters Humphrey (Canada, UN Secretariat) produced the first "blueprint". René Cassin (France) gave it its final structure, compared it to a Greek temple portico, and won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1968. P. C. Chang (Republic of China, vice-chairman) urged that it reflect more than Western ideas and quoted Mencius. Charles Malik (Lebanon, rapporteur) pressed for Article 28. Hansa Mehta (India) is credited with changing "all men are born free and equal" to "all human beings". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._C._Chang; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hansa_Mehta; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/René_Cassin]
- South Africa's delegate C. T. te Water tried to remove the word "dignity", foreseeing criticism of apartheid. The Commission approved the draft on 18 June 1948 by 12–0 with 4 abstentions. The General Assembly's Third Committee held 81 meetings and considered 168 amendments. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights]
- The European Convention on Human Rights was opened for signature in Rome on 4 November 1950 and entered into force on 3 September 1953. Sir David Maxwell-Fyfe guided the drafting. The European Court of Human Rights has sat in Strasbourg since 1959. All 46 Council of Europe states are parties (2025). Russia was a party from 1998 to 2022. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Convention_on_Human_Rights]
- The ICCPR was adopted and opened on 16 December 1966 and entered into force on 23 March 1976; it has 175 parties. Under Article 4, rights may be limited only "in time of public emergency which threatens the life of the nation", and never the rights to life, freedom from torture and slavery, freedom from retrospective law, or freedom of thought, conscience and religion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Covenant_on_Civil_and_Political_Rights]
- The African (Banjul) Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights was approved by the OAU in Nairobi in June 1981 and entered into force on 21 October 1986 (now African Human Rights Day). It includes peoples' rights and individual duties (Art. 29). The African Commission was set up on 2 November 1987, and the protocol creating the African Court came into force on 25 January 2004. 53 states had ratified as of 2019. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Charter_on_Human_and_Peoples%27_Rights]

## Surveillance and the security state

- The Stasi (East Germany, 1950–1990) had 91,015 employees and 173,081 informants when the GDR collapsed (Gieseke), about one collaborator per 63 East Germans and one full-time officer per 166 (vs one Gestapo officer per 2,000). It contributed to about 250,000 arrests. The files were opened to the people they concerned in 1992; Timothy Garton Ash's *The File* came from reading his. A popular "one informer per 6.5 citizens" figure includes estimated part-time informers (John Koehler); treat it as an upper estimate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stasi]
- The USA PATRIOT Act was signed on 26 October 2001; the House passed it 357–66 and the Senate 98–1 (Russ Feingold voting no). It drew criticism for indefinite detention of immigrants, "sneak and peek" searches and national security letters. Parts expired on 1 June 2015, the USA Freedom Act followed, and the remaining expiring provisions lapsed in March 2020. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriot_Act]
- Britain's Anti-terrorism, Crime and Security Act 2001, Part 4, allowed indefinite detention of non-citizens suspected of terrorism, under a derogation from ECHR Article 5 in force from 13 November 2001. The Law Lords held it incompatible with the Convention on 16 December 2004 (the "Belmarsh" case). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-terrorism,_Crime_and_Security_Act_2001]
- Guantánamo Bay: at least 780 people from 48 countries have been held since 2002; 15 remained as of January 2025. *Boumediene* (2008) is not checked here. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guantanamo_Bay_detention_camp]
- Edward Snowden, an NSA contractor at Booz Allen Hamilton: *The Guardian* (Glenn Greenwald, 6 June 2013) and *The Washington Post* published the first leaks in June 2013, starting with the Verizon bulk phone-records order and PRISM. He was charged under the Espionage Act on 14 June 2013 and given asylum in Russia. On 7 May 2015 the Second Circuit held (*ACLU v. Clapper*) that Section 215 did not authorise bulk collection. On 2 September 2020 the Ninth Circuit (*United States v. Moalin*) called the programme illegal and possibly unconstitutional. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowden_disclosures; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Snowden]

## Debates

- **When did human rights begin?** Lynn Hunt (*Inventing Human Rights*, 2007) places their origins in the eighteenth century: epistolary novels (Richardson's *Pamela* and *Clarissa*, Rousseau's *Julie*) and new attitudes to torture created "imagined empathy", leading to the declarations of 1776 and 1789. Samuel Moyn (*The Last Utopia*, 2010) treats Hunt as his main interlocutor. He argues that the 1940s declarations had little purchase and that human rights became a mass movement only in the later 1970s, as a "last utopia" after socialism and anticolonial nationalism lost their appeal. Steven L. B. Jensen (2016) puts the breakthrough in the 1960s and credits post-colonial states (Jamaica, Ghana, Liberia, the Philippines). Critics of Moyn stress continuity as well as rupture; critics of Hunt question whether reading novels changes behaviour, and note that her eighteenth century did not extend rights to women. [source: https://jhiblog.org/?p=10700; https://pureadmin.qub.ac.uk/ws/files/37675930/Human_Rights_Histories.pdf; https://prospect.org/2007/06/17/rights-became-human/; https://www.ethicsandinternationalaffairs.org/journal/inventing-human-rights-a-history-by-lynn-hunt; https://giwps.georgetown.edu/dei-resources/the-making-of-international-human-rights/]
- **Ancient "charters" and anachronism.** The Shah (and later Ahmadinejad) called the Cyrus Cylinder the first charter of human rights. C. B. F. Walker, Arnold and Michalowski, and Llewellyn-Jones reject this as anachronistic: it is a Babylonian building inscription. The British Museum and Shahrokh Razmjou still stress that repatriating deported peoples was "a significant reversal" of earlier Assyrian and Babylonian practice. The Kouroukan Fouga raises the same problem: an oral tradition reconstructed in 1998 and presented as a thirteenth-century constitution. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_Cylinder; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kouroukan_Fouga]
- **Magna Carta myth vs. meaning.** Seventeenth-century lawyers (Coke) built an "ancient constitution" myth; Victorian historians showed the charter was baronial. The myth itself shaped later liberties (the Petition of Right, American constitutionalism). The British Monarchy chapter already cites Butterfield's warning against "Whig" history. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_Carta]
- **Habeas corpus: liberty or sovereignty?** Paul Halliday argues that the writ grew from the king's claim to know why any subject was held, and that it moved unevenly rather than "upward" toward liberty. It was carried to the colonies and also suspended there. [source: https://kirkcenter.org/reviews/you-have-the-body/; https://harvardlawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2011/02/vol124_vladeck.pdf]
- **Ottoman and Mughal "tolerance".** Older accounts present a fixed "millet system" of tolerance. Recent work (Masters, Ursinus, Braude) shows it was informal before the nineteenth century, and communal autonomy went with political subordination. M. Athar Ali reads Akbar's *sulh-i kul* as imperial administrative policy, not a new religion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet_(Ottoman_Empire); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akbar]
- **Universal rights, partial subjects.** The declarations of 1689, 1789 and 1791 coexisted with excluding Catholics, women, the poor and the enslaved. Haiti's 1805 constitution made universal freedom from slavery the founding principle and inverted racial categories ("Blacks"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Man_and_of_the_Citizen; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toleration_Act_1688; https://haitidoi.com/2015/12/21/race-and-the-haitian-constitution-of-1805/]
- **Emergencies and the courts.** One pattern recurs: wartime or emergency measures are upheld at the time (*Korematsu* 1944, *ADM Jabalpur* 1976, *Schenck* 1919) and repudiated later (*Trump v. Hawaii* 2018, *Puttaswamy* 2017, *Brandenburg* 1969). Lincoln's question of 1861 frames the trade-off, and the ICCPR's Article 4 (1966) tries to codify which rights can never be suspended. [sources: as above]
- **Colonial law and its afterlife.** Colonial sedition and emergency laws (Section 124A of 1870, the Rowlatt Act of 1919) were used against Tilak and Gandhi, and independent India kept and even strengthened them until 2022–24. Britain drafted the ECHR while running colonial emergencies (the ECHR article refers to later cases such as Northern Ireland in 1971–72; colonial cases are not detailed in sources read). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_124A_of_the_Indian_Penal_Code; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rowlatt_Act]
- **Measuring decline.** Freedom House (20 straight years of decline to 2025) and V-Dem (average democracy back to about 1978) agree on direction. Both are indices built from expert coding, and their methods differ. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_in_the_World; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-Dem_Institute]
