## Framing: the "pristine myth" and "wilderness"
- William M. Denevan (born 16 October 1931), geographer at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, published "The Pristine Myth: The Landscape of the Americas in 1492" in *Annals of the Association of American Geographers* 82(3) (1992), pp. 369–385. He used it to name the belief that Indigenous peoples had little or no effect on the environment. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Denevan ; citation details at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_use_of_fire_in_ecosystems]
- Denevan's argument (search summary of the article): by 1492 Indigenous activity across the Americas had changed the extent and make-up of forests, created and expanded grasslands, and reshaped the ground with countless earthworks. The landscape of 1492 had largely vanished by the mid-eighteenth century, so the landscape of 1750 was more "pristine" (less humanised) than that of 1492. The cause was population collapse, not European clearing. In 2011 he published a follow-up, "The Pristine Myth Revisited", in *Geographical Review*. [source: https://www.frames.gov/catalog/43173 ; search summary]
- Denevan's edited volume *The Native Population of the Americas in 1492* (1976; 2nd edn 1992) put the hemisphere's 1492 population at 43–65 million, according to Wikipedia. The search summary of the 1992 article gives his working total there as 53.9 million. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Denevan]
- Denevan helped document pre-Hispanic raised fields, causeways and canals in the Llanos de Mojos (Bolivia), in *Scientific American* (1967) and *Science* (1970). He also wrote *Cultivated Landscapes of Native Amazonia and the Andes* (Oxford, 2001). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Denevan]
- Nineteenth-century writers such as Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, Francis Parkman and Henry David Thoreau helped spread the image of a pristine wilderness ("a world of barely perceptible human disturbance", a phrase from Shetler 1991). By the time they wrote, epidemics had depopulated the land and reduced burning, so large tracts had grown back into forest. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_use_of_fire_in_ecosystems]
- William Cronon (born 11 September 1954): *Changes in the Land: Indians, Colonists, and the Ecology of New England* (Hill and Wang, 1983; Francis Parkman Prize). It contrasts Native usufruct rights (a right to the nuts, fish, wood or hunting a place yields) with English property law, which treated land itself as a commodity. It also shows that Native people actively shaped New England ecosystems, for example by burning. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Changes_in_the_Land ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Cronon]
- Cronon's essay "The Trouble with Wilderness; or, Getting Back to the Wrong Nature" appeared in *Uncommon Ground* (1995), in *The New York Times* (13 August 1995) and in *Environmental History* (1996). It argues that the idea of untouched, pristine wilderness is a cultural invention. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Cronon]
- National parks and dispossession. Native Americans used the Yellowstone region for at least 11,000 years. Yellowstone National Park was created on 1 March 1872, and during the 1870s and 1880s tribes were effectively excluded from it. The Tukudeka ("Sheepeaters", a band of Eastern Shoshone) had been its only year-round residents. Pre-Columbian peoples burned parts of Yellowstone at low intensity, knowing that fire brought green grass that attracted bison. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellowstone_National_Park]
- The historian Mark David Spence (*Environmental History*, 1996) argued that parks such as Yellowstone, Yosemite, Grand Canyon and Glacier were "preserved" by removing the Native peoples who used them. His example is the Blackfeet and Glacier National Park (created 1910). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilderness]

## Fire as land management
- Low-intensity "cultural burning" by Indigenous peoples helped make the mosaic of grassland and forest that colonists took to be untouched wilderness. Many ecosystems first seen by Europeans had burned every one to three years, which turned forest into grassland or savanna or cleared the undergrowth. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_use_of_fire_in_ecosystems]
- Purposes of burning included: improving hunting (clearer sightlines, better forage for deer, elk and bison); raising nut yields; stimulating basketry materials (for the Karuk of northern California, beargrass and hazel are best one to three years after a burn); improving travel; reducing pests and ticks; and reducing fuel so that catastrophic fires were less likely. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_use_of_fire_in_ecosystems]
- Scale is debated. Emily Russell (1983) wrote: "There is no strong evidence that Indians purposely burned large areas.... The presence of Indians did, however, undoubtedly increase the frequency of fires above the low numbers caused by lightning." John E. Keeley (2002) argued that Native burning in California's coastal ranges significantly enlarged grassland and open shrubland. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_use_of_fire_in_ecosystems]
- Evidence comes from charcoal and pollen in sediments, fire scars in tree rings, and phytoliths. At Cliff Palace Pond in Kentucky, frequent low-severity fires from about 1000 BCE to 1800 CE coincide with more fire-adapted oak, chestnut and hickory. In the Jemez Mountains of New Mexico, low-severity fires tracked human population rather than climate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_use_of_fire_in_ecosystems]
- Prairies: eyewitness accounts of extensive prairie in the 1600s, the rapid conversion of prairie to woodland after settlement, and the efforts to outlaw Indigenous prairie burning in Canada and the US all point to widespread deliberate burning that kept prairie open. Wikipedia presents this as an argument rather than a settled consensus. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_use_of_fire_in_ecosystems]
- Suppression as colonial policy: Spanish authorities banned burning in California, and forced labour at the missions removed the people who did it. The sociologist Kari Norgaard notes: "Fire suppression was mandated by the very first session of the California Legislature in 1850 during the apex of genocide in the northern part of the state." By the early twentieth century fire suppression was official US federal policy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_use_of_fire_in_ecosystems]
- Present revival: tribal agencies are bringing cultural burning back. Examples include the Southern Sierra Miwuk Nation burning with Yosemite fire crews, and the Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde burning the Quamash Prairie in Oregon with Oregon Metro to grow camas. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_use_of_fire_in_ecosystems]
- M. Kat Anderson, *Tending the Wild: Native American Knowledge and the Management of California's Natural Resources* (University of California Press, 2005), argues from interviews with Native Californians that California was a managed landscape, not a wilderness. [source: search summary; https://search.worldcat.org/oclc/62175673]

## Northwest Coast: clam gardens, salmon, dentalium
- Clam gardens are a form of mariculture. Builders rolled boulders to the low-tide line to make a wall; sediment collected behind it and formed a flatter beach where clams thrive. The walls were maintained and raised over generations. Kwakʼwala name: lux̌ʷxiwēys. Haida name: k'yuu kudhlk'aat'iija. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clam_garden]
- More than 2,000 clam gardens have been identified on the coasts of Alaska, British Columbia, Washington and California. Dating is difficult. Some results give ages of 1,000–1,700 years and others at least 3,500–3,800 years. Kwakwaka'wakw Clan Chief Adam Dick (Kwaxsistalla) said they had existed "since the beginning of time". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clam_garden]
- Groesbeck, Rowell, Lepofsky and Salomon, *PLOS ONE* 9(3): e91235 (2014), compared 11 clam gardens with 10 unwalled beaches on northern Quadra Island, BC. The gardens held about four times as many butter clams and more than twice as many littleneck clams, and transplanted juvenile littlenecks grew about 1.7 times faster. [source: search summary; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3949788]
- Restoration: in 2014 Parks Canada and the Hul'q'umi'num and W̱SÁNEĆ Nations began restoring clam gardens in Gulf Islands National Park Reserve. In 2022 the Swinomish Tribe built a new clam garden on Kiket Island in Washington, believed to be the first built in the US in more than 200 years. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clam_garden]
- Dentalium: tusk shells, chiefly *Antalis pretiosa*, harvested from deep water mainly by the Nuu-chah-nulth. They were traded into the Plateau, Plains, Great Basin, Subarctic and Alaska. The shells reached the Canadian Plateau between 1000 and 1 BCE and were a common trade item there by the first century CE. Among northern California peoples such as the Yurok they were the most important unit of exchange and sacred wealth, valued by shell length. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dentalium_shell]
- Celilo Falls (Wyam) on the Columbia River was a fishing and trading place inhabited in various forms for about 15,000 years. It was flooded in 1957 by The Dalles Dam. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celilo_Falls]
- Before its two dams were built in the early twentieth century, an estimated 392,000 salmon and trout returned to the Elwha River each year. Salmon were a staple for the Lower Elwha Klallam. Elwha Dam was removed in 2011–12 and Glines Canyon Dam by 26 August 2014, at a cost of about $351.4 million, with strong support from the Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoration_of_the_Elwha_River]
- The four lower Klamath River dams (J.C. Boyle, Copco 1 and 2, Iron Gate) were demolished in 2023–24 after a long campaign in which the Yurok, Karuk and other tribes were central. Together the dams had produced about 1.5% of PacifiCorp's energy portfolio. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klamath_River]

## Potlatch and gift economies
- The potlatch is the gift-giving feast of Northwest Coast peoples (Heiltsuk, Haida, Nuxalk, Tlingit, Makah, Tsimshian, Nuu-chah-nulth, Kwakwaka'wakw, Coast Salish). Wikipedia describes it as their primary governmental institution and economic system. At potlatches, rights to territories and resources were stated, negotiated and affirmed, and names and titles were passed on. The word comes from Chinook Jargon ("to give away"), from Nuu-chah-nulth *paɬaˑč*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- Before colonisation, potlatch gifts included stored food (such as oolichan oil), canoes, slaves and ornamental "coppers", but never productive territories. After contact, epidemics left titles vacant and commoners competed for them, while mass-produced trade goods inflated the scale of giving. The famous competitive, destructive potlatches of the late nineteenth century were therefore partly a colonial-era development. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- Canada banned the potlatch by an amendment to the *Indian Act* signed on 19 April 1884, in force from 1885. The penalty was two to six months in prison. Missionary William Duncan had called the potlatch (1875) "by far the most formidable of all obstacles in the way of Indians becoming Christians, or even civilized". In 1883 some Coast Tsimshian and Nisga'a chiefs petitioned for a ban. Franz Boas opposed it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch_ban ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- Enforcement was weak until 1921, when Chief Dan Cranmer's potlatch at Memkumlis led to 45 people being charged and 20 imprisoned at Oakalla. The ban was dropped from the *Indian Act* in 1951 (former Section 149). Potlatching revived strongly in the 1970s–80s, and the Haida Nation roots its governance in potlatch law. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch_ban ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- Gift economy theory: Marcel Mauss's debate with Bronisław Malinowski (over the Trobriand *kula*) introduced the concepts of reciprocity and inalienable possessions used to analyse gift exchange. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gift_economy]

## Agriculture: maize, Three Sisters, milpa, Eastern Agricultural Complex, chinampas
- Maize was domesticated once, about 9,000 years ago, in southern Mexico from teosinte (Balsas teosinte, *Zea mays* subsp. *parviglumis*), most likely in the Balsas River valley (Matsuoka/Doebley et al. 2002; Doebley 2004). Stone milling tools with maize residue near Iguala, Guerrero, date to about 8,700 years ago. The oldest surviving cobs, from Guilá Naquitz cave in Oaxaca, are about 6,250 years old. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize]
- Maize reached New Mexico and Arizona about 4,100 years ago. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize]
- Three Sisters (maize, climbing beans, squash): the crops and the polyculture originated in Mesoamerica. Squash came first (about 8,000–10,000 years ago), then maize, then beans, over 5,000–6,500 years. Maize is a trellis for the beans, beans fix nitrogen, and squash leaves shade out weeds. The geographer Carl Sauer called it "a symbiotic plant complex of North and Central America without an equal elsewhere". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Sisters_(agriculture)]
- Jane Mt. Pleasant, a Haudenosaunee agronomist, found that Haudenosaunee fields were not tilled, which limited erosion. Her modern trials of Haudenosaunee Three Sisters polyculture gave more energy and more protein than any local monoculture, with maize yields of 22–76 bushels per acre. Hart and Feranec estimate lower Wendat yields of 8–22 bushels per acre. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Sisters_(agriculture)]
- Milpa (from Nahuatl *milli* + *pan*) is the Mesoamerican field system centred on maize, beans and squash, often with a dozen crops planted together. A typical cycle is two years of cultivation and eight years fallow. Maize lacks lysine and tryptophan, which beans supply. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milpa]
- The Eastern Agricultural Complex was one of about ten independent centres of plant domestication in the world. By about 1800 BCE (Riverton site, Illinois), people were cultivating goosefoot (*Chenopodium berlandieri*), sunflower, marsh elder (sumpweed) and squash, and later maygrass, little barley and erect knotweed. Maize arrived from Mexico after about 200 BCE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Agricultural_Complex]
- Until the 1970s–80s most archaeologists thought Eastern Woodlands farming had been imported from Mexico. Seed evidence then showed independent domestication. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Agricultural_Complex]
- Chinampas (Nahuatl *chināmitl*) were raised fields built in the shallow lakes of the Valley of Mexico, especially Xochimilco and Chalco. They were fertilised with dredged lake mud. The earliest securely dated chinampas are from 1150–1350 CE. Many were abandoned after the Spanish conquest destroyed the dams and sluices. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinampa]

## Cahokia's fields and environment
- Cahokia (near modern St Louis) was a Mississippian city of about 1050–1350 CE. At its peak around 1100 it covered about 6 square miles with about 120 earthworks, and the National Park Service estimate of its population is 15,000–20,000. Other archaeological estimates range from 6,000 to 40,000. Benson et al. (2007) estimate that the central 1.8 km² grew from 1,400–2,800 to 10,200–15,300 people between 1050 and 1100. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahokia]
- Maize became important in the American Bottom only around 900 CE. Before that, most crops belonged to the older Eastern Agricultural Complex. Cahokians spread their fields between lowland and upland to reduce the risk that one flood would destroy the food supply, and they built channels and levees. Its trade reached the Great Lakes (copper) and the Gulf Coast (marine shell). Mill Creek chert hoes from southern Illinois were in high demand. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahokia]
- Decline and the "ecocide" debate: explanations include deforestation, overhunting and pollution, droughts in about 1100–1250, floods (sediments from Horseshoe Lake show major floods around 1100–1260 and 1340–1460), and social unrest (a large burning in East St Louis around 1160–1170). Caitlin Rankin (*Geoarchaeology*, 2021) found no evidence of human-caused erosion or flooding at Cahokia, which challenges the ecocide narrative. The city was abandoned by about 1350–1400. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahokia]

## Amazonia: terra preta and anthropogenic forests
- Terra preta ("black earth"; Portuguese *terra preta do índio*) is a dark, fertile anthropogenic soil. People made it by adding charcoal, bone, broken pottery, compost and manure to the poor Amazonian soils. It holds up to 9% black carbon, against about 0.5% in surrounding soils. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_preta]
- Dating: Wikipedia gives formation between 450 BCE and 950 CE (citing Neves et al. 2001). Plots average about 20 ha but can reach about 360 ha. Estimates of extent range from 0.1–0.3% of lowland forested Amazonia (6,300–18,900 km², Sombroek et al.), to a model-based 3.2% (McMichael et al. 2014), to 10% or more by other estimates. Treat extent as uncertain. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_preta]
- Some terra preta formed in kitchen middens. Some may have been made deliberately on a large scale. Some recent research suggests people may have improved naturally fertile patches. The lighter *terra mulata* is thought to be farmed fields improved with charcoal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_preta]
- Francisco de Orellana, descending the Amazon in the 1540s, reported dense populations along the river. Geoglyphs (0–1250 CE) and terra preta support some of his account. Disease and *bandeirante* slave raids caused a demographic collapse in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_preta]
- Levis et al., *Science* 355 (2017): pp. 925–931 tracked 85 tree species that pre-Columbian peoples domesticated, using 1,091 forest plots. Domesticated species were about five times more likely than non-domesticated species to be "hyperdominant" (20 of the 85 are). They are more abundant and diverse near archaeological sites. The authors conclude that modern Amazonian forests are "structured to an important extent" by Indigenous plant domestication. [source: search summary; https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/65491/]

## Exchange networks: obsidian, copper, shell, wampum
- The Old Copper complex of the western Great Lakes worked nearly pure native copper from Isle Royale, the Keweenaw Peninsula and the Brule River, by heating, annealing and hammering. Dated artefacts span about 6500 to 1580 BCE. By about 1000 BCE copper was increasingly reserved for status objects. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Copper_complex]
- The Hopewell tradition (about 100 BCE–500 CE, Eastern Woodlands) was a network rather than a single society. Its burials hold copper, mica, obsidian, grizzly-bear teeth, freshwater pearls and marine shell from far away. Mound City (Ohio) held hundreds of pounds of obsidian traced to Obsidian Cliff in Yellowstone. The distance is given variously as "over 1,500 miles" (Obsidian article) and about 2,000 miles each way on foot (Hopewell article), so use "roughly 1,500–2,000 miles". Wikipedia says the Ohio Hopewell "may have" made pilgrimages there; exchange through intermediaries is the alternative. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopewell_tradition ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obsidian]
- Cody-complex people mined Obsidian Cliff from about 7300 BCE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellowstone_National_Park]
- Southeastern trade paths carried copper, flint, salt, slaves, furs and skins before European contact. The same paths later carried the deerskin trade. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deerskin_trade_in_Colonial_United_States]
- Wampum is shell beads of quahog and whelk. Its name comes from Massachusett/Narragansett *wampumpeag*, "white strings of shell beads". Strings and belts recorded treaties and histories, for example the Two Row Wampum and the Hiawatha Belt. European colonists treated it as currency. It was legal tender in New England from 1637 to 1661 and used as currency in New York until 1673. With European metal tools, production reached tens of millions of beads by the mid-seventeenth century, and Dutch workshops flooded the market. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wampum]

## Fur and deerskin trades
- The Hudson's Bay Company was chartered in 1670. In the "Beaver Wars" of the 1640s–50s the Haudenosaunee (Five Nations) sought to control the fur trade. Their 1649 raids on Wendake dispersed the Wendat. Daniel Richter argues these were "mourning wars" for captives as much as "beaver wars". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_fur_trade]
- Beaver loss had ecological effects on rivers and wetlands. Beaver and river otter populations did not recover noticeably until about the mid-twentieth century. Unions between Indigenous women and traders contributed to the ethnogenesis of the Métis. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_fur_trade]
- Southeastern deerskin trade: Charleston (founded 1670) became the hub for deerskins and Indian slaves. The trade involved the Catawba, Shawnee, Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Choctaw and Chickasaw. The trade drove a sharp decline in white-tailed deer, which were becoming scarce in Cherokee country by 1750. It also bound these nations to European goods such as guns, cloth, knives and kettles, and encouraged slave raiding. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deerskin_trade_in_Colonial_United_States]
- Export figures (attributed estimates; search summary): about 53,000–54,000 skins a year from Charleston in 1698/99–1715; an estimated 160,000 in 1748; and around 400,000 a year claimed in 1764 by John Stuart, superintendent of Indian affairs, which is an official's claim rather than a count. [source: search summary; https://blogs.lib.unc.edu/ncm/2009/09/03/]
- Kathryn E. Holland Braund (Auburn University), *Deerskins and Duffels: The Creek Indian Trade with Anglo-America, 1685–1815* (University of Nebraska Press, 1993; 2nd edn 2008), is the first extensive study of the Creek side of the trade and how commercial hunting changed Creek society. [source: search summary; https://catalog.library.tamu.edu/Record/in00002485505/Similar]
- Richard White: *The Roots of Dependency: Subsistence, Environment, and Social Change Among the Choctaws, Pawnees, and Navajos* (1983) and *The Middle Ground: Indians, Empires, and Republics in the Great Lakes Region, 1650–1815* (Cambridge University Press, 1991; Francis Parkman Prize, Pulitzer finalist). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_White_(historian)]

## The bison economy and its destruction
- Before horses, Plains peoples drove bison along drive lines into pounds or over cliffs ("buffalo jumps"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison_hunting]
- Horses spread to Plains peoples after the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, but slowly: as late as 1725 Comanches were described using large dogs to carry their bison-hide tents. Horse-mounted bison hunting was well established by the early 1700s. Some farming peoples, such as the Cheyenne, who left their cornfields at the Biesterfeldt village, moved west and became mounted bison hunters. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comanche ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison_hunting]
- The Comanche built Comanchería (northwest Texas, eastern New Mexico, southeast Colorado, southwest Kansas, western Oklahoma) on horses and bison. Pekka Hämäläinen's *The Comanche Empire* (2008) won the Bancroft Prize. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comanche ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pekka_H%C3%A4m%C3%A4l%C3%A4inen]
- By the 1830s the Comanche and their allies on the southern plains were killing about 280,000 bison a year, near the limit of sustainability. A long drought from 1845 into the 1860s caused a widespread collapse of southern herds. This is Dan Flores's "Bison Ecology and Bison Diplomacy: The Southern Plains from 1800 to 1850", *Journal of American History* 78(2) (1991). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison_hunting]
- Pemmican (Cree *pimîhkân*; Lakota *wasná*) is dried meat pounded with fat and sometimes berries. It fuelled the fur trade. Miles Macdonell's Pemmican Proclamation of 1814 at Red River led to the "Pemmican War" with the Métis. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pemmican]
- Pre-slaughter numbers are uncertain, so give ranges. Wikipedia's bison article uses 60 million, an estimate based on Col. R. I. Dodge's 1871 observation along the Arkansas River; it was extrapolated by Ernest Thompson Seton (1929). Carrying-capacity estimates are lower: about 25–30 million in the sixteenth century (M. Scott Taylor, *American Economic Review*, 2011), and 28–30 million for the whole Plains by Flores (1991). McHugh (1972) put it at about 30 million, and one reviewer (Gade) argues for 30–40 million. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison_hunting ; search summary for Flores, Seton and McHugh: https://journals.uair.arizona.edu/index.php/rangelands/article/viewFile/11258/10531]
- Causes, per Wikipedia's synthesis: habitat loss to ranching and farming; industrial-scale hunting by settlers; increased Indigenous hunting driven by settler demand for robes and meat; cattle diseases; and deliberate efforts to destroy Indigenous food sources. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison_hunting]
- Andrew C. Isenberg, *The Destruction of the Bison: An Environmental History, 1750–1920* (Cambridge University Press, 2000), argues that many causes combined: the horse transformed Plains economies, and the robe market, drought and competition from cattle all contributed. Native hunters, drawn into the market, are part of the story but not its main cause. Reviewers put his scale at roughly 30 million in 1800 to fewer than 1,000 a century later. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison_hunting ; search summary: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/29308]
- Commercial hide hunting: in the winter of 1872–73 more than 1.5 million hides were shipped east by rail. One Dodge City yard held 40,000 hides in 1878. Hides went to industrial machine belts, clothing and rugs, much of it exported to Europe. Bones were later collected for fertiliser and for bone char used in sugar refining. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison_hunting]
- Policy: President Grant pocket-vetoed a federal bison-protection bill in 1874. Secretary of the Interior Columbus Delano wrote in his annual report for 1873: "While I would not seriously regret the total disappearance of the buffalo from our western prairies, in its effect on the Indians, regarding it rather as a means of hastening their sense of dependence upon the products of the soil and their own labors..." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison_hunting]
- Low point: the bison article gives 541 animals by 1889, and another figure is 325 in 1884. William Temple Hornaday's *The Extermination of the American Bison* (report 1887, book 1889) mapped the collapse. Its census counted 256 captive bison on 1 January 1889. Totals in different sources do not agree (one map listing gives about 800 wild), so say "a few hundred wild bison, and roughly a thousand counting captive animals, by 1889". Hornaday founded the American Bison Society in 1905. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison_hunting ; search summary for the 256 figure]
- Consequences: as bison vanished, Plains nations became dependent on government rations (for example, "Ration Day at Standing Rock Agency", 1883). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison_hunting]
- Restoration: the InterTribal Buffalo Council was formed in 1991 after a February 1991 meeting of 19 tribes in the Black Hills. It now has 82 federally recognised tribes in 20 states, which manage over 20,000 bison on more than 1 million acres of tribal land. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/InterTribal_Buffalo_Council]

## Dispossession and its ecological consequences
- President Grover Cleveland signed the Dawes (General Allotment) Act on 8 February 1887. Native-held land fell from about 138 million acres in 1887 to about 48 million in 1934, a loss of about 90 million acres. Wikipedia also gives "about 100 million acres" and, in another passage, a fall from about 150 to 78 million acres by 1900. Allotments could be sold after a 25-year trust period, and "surplus" land went to settlers, railroads and other corporations. The Indian Reorganization Act of 18 June 1934 ended allotment. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dawes_Act]
- Uranium on Navajo (Diné) land: mining began in 1944 under the Manhattan Project and boomed after the Atomic Energy Commission guaranteed purchase of all US uranium ore in 1948. Thousands of Navajo miners were not told of the risks, and Navajo had no word for radiation. On 16 July 1979 the Church Rock tailings dam (United Nuclear Corporation) breached and sent more than 1,000 tons of solid waste and 93 million gallons of acidic, radioactive solution into the Puerco River, the largest radioactive release in US history. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_mining_and_the_Navajo_people]
- The Radiation Exposure Compensation Act was signed on 15 October 1990 and amended in 2000. On 29 April 2005 Navajo Nation President Joe Shirley Jr. signed the Diné Natural Resources Protection Act, banning uranium mining and processing on Navajo land. The EPA counts about 521–523 abandoned uranium mine areas there. About 15% of Navajo Nation residents lack running water. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_mining_and_the_Navajo_people]
- Dakota Access Pipeline (#NoDAPL) protests ran from April 2016 until the camps were cleared on 22–23 February 2017. The published Native American Studies chapter already covers them, so cross-refer briefly. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dakota_Access_Pipeline_protests]
- The "Great Dying" and climate: Koch, Brierley, Maslin and Lewis (*Quaternary Science Reviews*, 2019) estimate that a pre-1492 population of more than 60 million fell to about 6 million by 1600. Regrowth of forest on abandoned farmland may have contributed to a 7–10 ppm dip in atmospheric CO₂ around 1610 (the "Orbis spike"), which some propose as the start of the Anthropocene. This is debated, and drought is an alternative explanation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_history_of_the_Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbis_spike]

## Treaty rights, co-management and Indigenous-led conservation today
- The Boldt decision, *United States v. Washington*, 384 F. Supp. 312 (W.D. Wash. 1974), was issued by Judge George Hugo Boldt on 12 February 1974 (date from search summary). It interpreted the 1854–55 Stevens treaties' guarantee of "the right of taking fish at all usual and accustomed grounds and stations ... in common with all citizens of the Territory" to mean that tribes are entitled to up to half the harvestable fish. It was affirmed by the Ninth Circuit in 1975. The Supreme Court largely endorsed it on 2 July 1979 in *Washington v. Washington State Commercial Passenger Fishing Vessel Ass'n*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_v._Washington ; search summary: https://www.sos.wa.gov/legacy/the-boldt-decision.aspx]
- Context and aftermath: before Boldt, non-Indian fishers took about 95% of the catch (search summary). Washington State resisted, and Boldt had the Coast Guard and federal agencies enforce the ruling. The tribes became co-managers of the fisheries, hiring their own biologists. Billy Frank Jr. (Nisqually) was a leader of the "fish wars" (search summary: arrested more than 50 times). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_v._Washington ; search summary]
- The "culverts" subproceeding: in 2013 a federal court ordered Washington to replace salmon-blocking culverts by 2030, holding that the treaties protect habitat. The Ninth Circuit affirmed in 2016, and the Supreme Court decided the case in 2018. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_v._Washington]
- The Yakama, Warm Springs, Umatilla and Nez Perce founded the Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission in 1977 to protect treaty salmon fisheries. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_River_Inter-Tribal_Fish_Commission]
- Gwaii Haanas (Haida Gwaii): to stop logging, the Haida Nation declared the area a Haida Heritage Site in 1985. The 1993 Gwaii Haanas Agreement set up an Archipelago Management Board with equal Haida and federal representation, one of the best-known co-management models. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwaii_Haanas_National_Park_Reserve_and_Haida_Heritage_Site]
- Thaidene Nëné ("land of our ancestors", Dene/Chipewyan), east arm of Great Slave Lake, was established on 21 August 2019 as a national park reserve within the Thaidene Nëné Indigenous Protected Area, after the Łutsël K'é Dene First Nation revived the proposal in 2001. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thaidene_N%C3%ABn%C3%A9_National_Park_Reserve]
- Bears Ears National Monument (Utah) was proclaimed by Obama on 28 December 2016 at 1,351,849 acres. It is co-managed with five tribes: Navajo, Hopi, Ute Mountain Ute, Ute Indian Tribe of Uintah and Ouray, and Pueblo of Zuni. Trump cut it by 85% on 4 December 2017 and Biden restored it on 8 October 2021. Wikipedia (as of November 2026) reports that Trump again cut most of it on 13 July 2026, so the writer should re-check before publication. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bears_Ears_National_Monument]
- Global scale: Garnett et al., *Nature Sustainability* 1 (2018), found that Indigenous peoples manage or hold tenure over at least about 38 million km² in 87 countries, more than a quarter of the world's land, intersecting about 40% of terrestrial protected areas and ecologically intact landscapes. [source: search summary; https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natsus/v1y2018i7d10.1038_s41893-018-0100-6.html]
- Traditional ecological knowledge (TEK) is defined by Fikret Berkes (*Sacred Ecology*) as "a cumulative body of knowledge, practice, and belief, evolving by adaptive processes and handed down through generations by cultural transmission, about the relationship of living beings (including humans) with one another and with their environment." Debates continue over how to combine it with Western science, over intellectual property, and over critiques that the term itself appropriates Indigenous knowledge. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_ecological_knowledge]
- Robin Wall Kimmerer (born 13 September 1953) is a botanist, an enrolled citizen of the Citizen Potawatomi Nation, and former director of the Center for Native Peoples and the Environment at SUNY-ESF. She wrote *Gathering Moss* (2003; John Burroughs Medal), *Braiding Sweetgrass: Indigenous Wisdom, Scientific Knowledge, and the Teachings of Plants* (2013; more than two million copies sold within ten years) and *The Serviceberry* (2024). She received a MacArthur Fellowship in 2022. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Wall_Kimmerer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braiding_Sweetgrass]
- Kyle Powys Whyte (Citizen Potawatomi) is a philosopher at the University of Michigan's School for Environment and Sustainability (George Willis Pack Professor since 2020) and an author of the Fourth National Climate Assessment. His essay "Our Ancestors' Dystopia Now: Indigenous Conservation and the Anthropocene" (*Routledge Companion to the Environmental Humanities*, 2017) argues that many Indigenous peoples have already lived through environmental catastrophe brought by colonialism: allotment, dispossession, pollution and extraction. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyle_Powys_Whyte ; search summary: https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2770047]

## Debates
- The "ecological Indian" debate. Shepard Krech III's *The Ecological Indian: Myth and History* (1999) argued that pre-twentieth-century Native North Americans were "ecologists", deeply aware of how humans, animals and land are connected, but not "conservationists" in the modern sense. He cited bison jumps that wasted animals and Native participation in the commercial beaver and deer trades. Krech himself found the evidence for Indian responsibility for Pleistocene extinctions "very weak" (Louis Warren's review). Critics: Vine Deloria Jr. found neither Krech's evidence nor his arguments convincing and objected to a non-Indian presuming to characterise the Native past. John Mohawk said the "ecological Indian" image is likely rooted in facts the book barely hints at. Warren faulted Krech for treating white critics of Indian bison hunting as neutral witnesses. Michael Harkin and David Rich Lewis edited *Native Americans and the Environment: Perspectives on the Ecological Indian* (2007) in response, with contributors on both sides. Wikipedia's "noble savage" article says most academics now regard the "ecological noble savage" image as false, in that all peoples changed their environments, while TEK scholars stress managed, reciprocal relations. Present the debate as a contest over a stereotype rather than over whether Native peoples managed land: Krech too accepted that colonists' "wilderness" was often a managed landscape. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_savage ; search summaries: https://www.amerinda.org/newsletter/4-4/deloria.html ; https://muse.jhu.edu/journals/ethnohistory/v049/49.3warren.pdf ; https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/history_facpub/7 ; https://bcstudies.ubc.ca/book_film_review/the-ecological-indian-myth-and-history/]
- How "pristine" were the Americas? Denevan and Mann argue that Indigenous landscapes were heavily humanised. Betty Meggers argued for small, low-impact Amazonian populations ("law of environmental limitation of culture"). David Henige's *Numbers from Nowhere* (1998) criticised the high population counts. A *Geographical Review* forum on *1491* printed eight critiques and Mann's reply. Mann himself does not endorse Dobyns's high count. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1491:_New_Revelations_of_the_Americas_Before_Columbus ; search summary: https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2005/12/01/new-world-symphony/]
- Scale of burning: widespread landscape-forming fire (Keeley; Denevan; prairie evidence) versus local effects near settlements (Russell 1983). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_use_of_fire_in_ecosystems]
- Cahokia's fall: environmental degradation ("ecocide": deforestation, overhunting, pollution) versus flooding, drought and social factors. Rankin (2021) found no evidence of human-caused erosion or flooding. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahokia]
- Terra preta: deliberate soil-making versus midden by-product versus improvement of naturally fertile patches. Its extent ranges from 0.1% to more than 10% of Amazonia depending on the estimate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_preta]
- Bison collapse: policy and commercial hide hunting (Smits; Delano; Grant's veto) versus a multi-causal environmental history that includes Native market hunting, horses and drought (Flores 1991; Isenberg 2000). Do not use this to shift blame onto Native hunters. The final collapse of 1870–1885 was overwhelmingly driven by commercial hide hunting and settler expansion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison_hunting]
- Statistics about Indigenous conservation: Fernández-Llamazares et al. (*Nature*, 4 September 2024) traced the widely repeated claim that "80% of the world's biodiversity" lies in Indigenous territories to a misread 2001 encyclopedia entry and found no basis for it. They stress that Indigenous peoples and territories are nonetheless key to conservation. Use Garnett et al. (2018) instead. [source: search summary: https://news.mongabay.com/2024/09/do-indigenous-peoples-really-conserve-80-of-the-worlds-biodiversity]
- Potlatch history: some Indigenous leaders (Coast Tsimshian and Nisga'a chiefs, 1883) petitioned for the ban, and the "competitive" potlatch was partly a product of colonial-era depopulation and trade goods. This adds nuance to the story without excusing the ban. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch_ban ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- Hämäläinen's "Indigenous empire" framing (Comanche, Lakota) is praised but criticised by Indigenous scholars such as Jameson Sweet (Lakota/Dakota). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pekka_H%C3%A4m%C3%A4l%C3%A4inen]

## Conflicts with published chapters
- **Cahokia's population.** *North America before Columbus* (`history--medieval-history--north-america`) gives "10,000 to 20,000 people" around 1100. *Native American and Indigenous Studies* (`history--regional-histories--native-american-studies`) gives "perhaps 15,000–20,000". Wikipedia gives 15,000–20,000 (NPS) and a full range of archaeological estimates of 6,000–40,000 (Benson et al.: 10,200–15,300 for the central zone). Neither chapter is wrong, but they disagree with each other. Suggest "perhaps 10,000–20,000, with estimates ranging from 6,000 to 40,000".
- **Cahokia's abandonment.** *North America before Columbus* says it was "largely abandoned by 1350". Wikipedia's chronology table says "abandoned by 1400 CE with brief Oneota reoccupation", while its lead gives c. 1050–1350. These are consistent if phrased "largely abandoned by about 1350, entirely by about 1400".
- **Hemispheric population in 1492.** *Native American and Indigenous Studies* says most recent estimates, "following William Denevan, fall between 45 and 60 million". Wikipedia gives Denevan's range as 43–65 million, his 1992 working figure is 53.9 million (search summary), and Koch et al. (2019) give more than 60 million. This is not a contradiction, but the writer should attribute figures and not imply a single Denevan range.
- **Dawes Act acreage.** *Native American and Indigenous Studies* says about 100 million acres, roughly two-thirds, were lost between 1887 and 1934. Wikipedia gives both "about 100 million" and 138 → 48 million acres (about 90 million). This is consistent within the range. Use "about 90–100 million acres, nearly two-thirds".
- **Potlatch ban date.** *Native American and Indigenous Studies* says "banned the potlatch from 1885". The amendment was signed on 19 April 1884 and came into force in 1885, which is consistent; state it as "passed in 1884, in force from 1885, repealed 1951".
- No contradictions found with *The Columbian Exchange* (horses transforming Plains life; Comanche and Lakota), *Colonial North America* (Wendat dispersal of 1649; White's middle ground), *Mesoamerica* (maize from teosinte about 9,000 years ago) or *Moctezuma's Mexico* (chinampas), or with the Andes chapter (Murra's "vertical archipelago"). Cross-refer to these rather than repeat them.
