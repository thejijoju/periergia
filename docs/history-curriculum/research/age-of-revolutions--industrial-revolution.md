## Definitions, dating and the name
- Wikipedia dates the (First) Industrial Revolution c. 1760 to c. 1840: it began in Great Britain around 1760 and had spread to continental Europe and the United States by about 1840. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- It involved the shift from hand production to machines, new chemical and iron-making processes, more use of water and steam power, machine tools, and the mechanised factory system. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Before the Industrial Revolution most manufacturing took place in China and India. Afterwards most took place in North America and Western Europe. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Earliest recorded use of the phrase "Industrial Revolution": 1799, by the French envoy Louis-Guillaume Otto, announcing that France had entered the race to industrialise. Jérôme-Adolphe Blanqui wrote of "la révolution industrielle" in 1837. Engels used the phrase in The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845, German). Arnold Toynbee's 1881 lectures are credited with popularising the term. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- A recession from the late 1830s followed as adoption of the early textile innovations slowed and markets matured. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]

## Why Britain? Causes
- Institutions: Wikipedia lists the stable political situation after the Glorious Revolution of 1688, rule of law and property rights, a stable financial market built on the Bank of England's management of the national debt, joint-stock companies, and the absence of internal tariffs ("largest coherent market in Europe"). Internal tariffs survived in Russia until 1753, in France until 1789 and in Spain until 1839. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Coal: in 1700, five-sixths of the coal mined worldwide was mined in Britain. Without coal, Britain would have run out of suitable river sites for mills by the 1830s. The Netherlands had no coal and did not industrialise, despite good transport, low taxes and a well-paid, literate population. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Population and urbanisation: Britain's population grew 280% from 1550 to 1820, against 50–80% in the rest of Western Europe. 70% of European urbanisation from 1750 to 1800 happened in Britain. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Robert C. Allen (born 1947; Oxford, then NYU Abu Dhabi). In The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective (2009) he argues that high wages combined with cheap energy, especially coal, gave firms strong incentives to invent labour-saving, coal-using technology such as the steam engine, the spinning jenny and the coke-fired blast furnace. The book was a book of the year for The Economist and the TLS in 2009. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Allen_(economic_historian)]
- Criticisms of Allen: 2018 studies in The Economic History Review found that wages were not especially high in spinning or construction. A 2022 study found that industrialisation happened in areas with low wages and high mechanical skills, and that literacy, banks and nearness to coal explained little. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Joel Mokyr (born Leiden, 1946; Northwestern University) won half of the 2025 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences "for having identified the prerequisites for sustained growth through technological progress." Philippe Aghion and Peter Howitt shared the other half. His books include The Gifts of Athena (2002), The Enlightened Economy: An Economic History of Britain 1700–1850 (2009) and A Culture of Growth (2016). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joel_Mokyr]
- The Nobel committee credited Mokyr with showing that for innovations to follow one another in a self-generating process "we not only need to know that something works, but we also need to have scientific explanations for why." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joel_Mokyr]
- Mokyr on Europe: political fragmentation let heterodox thinkers move to a neighbouring state, which acted as "an insurance against economic and technological stagnation". It was combined with an "integrated market for ideas" through the Republic of Letters. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Mokyr's "Industrial Enlightenment" (search summary): cultural changes that connected practical and theoretical knowledge and made both more widely accessible. Knowledge elites and practical businessmen shared a common intellectual culture. In The Gifts of Athena he distinguishes "propositional" knowledge (understanding of nature) from "prescriptive" knowledge (techniques). [source: https://www.econlib.org/library/enc/bios/mokyr.html ; search summary]
- Networks of useful knowledge: the Lunar Society of Birmingham met from 1765 to 1809. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Dissenters: Nonconformists were barred or discouraged from public office and from Oxford and Cambridge. They became active in banking, manufacturing and education, and Dissenting Academies taught mathematics and science. Abraham Darby I was a Quaker, and Arkwright's partners Strutt and Need were nonconformist hosiers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Arkwright ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Darby_I]
- Patents could cut both ways. Watt's patent let him monopolise production, but it also stopped other inventors from introducing improved engines and so slowed the spread of steam power. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Watt's 1769 patent was extended to June 1800 by an Act of Parliament in 1775. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Watt]

## Empire, slavery and cotton (causes debate)
- Eric Williams, later the first Prime Minister of Trinidad and Tobago, published Capitalism and Slavery in the US in 1944. It grew out of his Oxford D.Phil. thesis, on a topic suggested by C. L. R. James. He argued that slavery's high profits helped finance the Industrial Revolution and that abolition was driven by economic decline rather than chiefly by humanitarianism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism_and_Slavery]
- British publishers refused Capitalism and Slavery partly because it undermined the humanitarian story of the 1833 Abolition Act. It appeared in Britain only in 1964 (André Deutsch). Kenneth Morgan in the ODNB called it "perhaps the most influential book written in the twentieth century on the history of slavery". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism_and_Slavery]
- Seymour Drescher critiqued Williams in Econocide (1977). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Profits from the colonies and the slave trade have been estimated at 7% a year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- Kenneth Pomeranz, The Great Divergence: China, Europe, and the Making of the Modern World Economy (Princeton, 2000). He argues that the most advanced regions of Europe and Asia had comparable development until about 1800 and faced similar land constraints. Only after 1800, thanks to coal and colonies, did Britain pull ahead of the Yangtze (Yangzi) Delta. The book won the John K. Fairbank Prize for 2000 and is central to the "California School". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Divergence_(book)]
- Pomeranz's land argument: New World exports of wood, cotton and wool are estimated to have saved England the need for 23 to 25 million acres of cultivated land. England's total cultivated land was only 17 million acres. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- Pomeranz's coal argument: China's largest coal deposits lay in the northwest, far from the later economic core in the south. European mines were wet, which the Newcomen engine solved, while northwestern Chinese mines were dry and harder to ventilate safely. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- Counter-arguments on coal: Gregory Clark and David Jacks argue that coal could have been substituted without much loss of income. Deirdre McCloskey says coal could have been imported. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- Prasannan Parthasarathi, reviewing Pomeranz, rejected ecological pressure as the main driver. British charcoal prices stayed fairly steady, and he put technological innovation, such as the spinning jenny and metallurgy, first. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Divergence_(book)]
- Sven Beckert (Laird Bell Professor of History, Harvard), Empire of Cotton: A Global History (2014). It won the 2015 Bancroft Prize and was a Pulitzer finalist. Peer Vries called it "better as a story on cotton than as an analysis of capitalism". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sven_Beckert]
- Beckert's "war capitalism" (search summary): armed trade in Asia, the vast expansion of slave labour in the Americas and the dispossession of land from Indigenous peoples. He sees it as the foundation from which industrial capitalism grew. Reviewers criticise the term as loosely defined. [source: https://www.wcfia.harvard.edu/publications/capitalism-woven-through-cotton ; search summary]

## Textiles: the breakthroughs
- In 1750 Britain imported 2.5 million lb of raw cotton. Raw cotton consumption was 22 million lb in 1787, 52 million lb in 1800 and 588 million lb in 1850. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Cotton's share of British value added was 2.6% in 1760, 17% in 1801 and 22% in 1831. There were about 900 cotton factories in 1797. Britain had 50,000 spindles in 1788 and 7 million about 30 years later. In 1800 less than 0.1% of world cotton cloth was made on British-invented machinery. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- John Kay (born 17 June 1704, Walmersley near Bury, Lancashire) patented the flying shuttle in 1733. It roughly doubled a weaver's output and widened the gap between spinning and weaving. It spread in Lancashire after 1760, when his son Robert added the drop-box. Colchester weavers petitioned the King against it in September 1733. Unable to enforce his patents, Kay left for France in 1747 and sold his technology to the French government for 3,000 livres plus a pension. Note: one Wikipedia article dates the invention to 1734, but the Kay and Industrial Revolution articles say patent 1733. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Kay_(flying_shuttle) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- James Hargreaves (c. 1720–1778) invented the spinning jenny in 1764–65 at Stanhill, Oswaldtwistle, near Blackburn. It began with eight spindles and grew to 120 as the technology advanced. Blackburn spinners broke into his house and smashed his machines, and he moved to Nottingham in 1768. He patented the jenny in 1770 (one article gives 12 July, another 12 June). The story that it was named after a wife or daughter called Jenny is a myth. A 40-spindle jenny cost £6 in 1792 and was used mainly by home spinners. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinning_jenny ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hargreaves ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Richard Arkwright (23 Dec 1732, Preston – 3 Aug 1792) had trained as a barber. He patented the spinning frame in 1769, working with the clockmaker John Kay (not the flying-shuttle Kay). The design was partly based on a machine built for Thomas Highs. The frame was first horse-powered in Nottingham, then water-powered, hence "water frame". It produced strong warp thread, finally making all-cotton cloth possible in Britain. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Arkwright ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_frame ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Arkwright patented a carding engine in 1775. His patents were annulled in 1785 after litigation that began in 1781. He was knighted in 1786, died worth about £500,000, and has been called the "father of the modern industrial factory system". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Arkwright]
- Cromford Mill, Derbyshire (1771), on the River Derwent, was built with Jedediah Strutt and Samuel Need. Wikipedia calls it the world's first water-powered cotton spinning mill. From 1772 it ran day and night in two 12-hour shifts. It began with 200 workers, mostly women and children, the youngest aged seven. The minimum age was later raised to ten, and children got six hours of schooling a week. Arkwright built worker housing, described as "the first factory housing development in the world". The mill is now part of the Derwent Valley Mills UNESCO World Heritage Site. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cromford_Mill]
- Cromford was one of the first places where the working day was set by the clock rather than by daylight. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_frame]
- The first continental spinning factory was built in 1783 at Ratingen by Johann Gottfried Brügelmann and also named "Cromford". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_frame]
- Samuel Crompton (3 Dec 1753 – 26 June 1827, Bolton) made the spinning mule around 1779 at Hall i' th' Wood. It combined features of the jenny and the water frame and spun fine yarn suitable for muslin. He could not afford a patent and received no royalties. A survey in 1812 counted 4–5 million mule spindles in use, and Parliament granted him £5,000 that year. The mule became fully self-acting by 1834. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Crompton]
- Edmund Cartwright (1743–1823) was a Church of England clergyman (rector) and Oxford graduate. He designed a power loom in 1784 and patented it in 1785. It worked mainly as a proof of concept, and others later made practical power looms (Horrocks 1813; Richard Roberts's loom 1822/1830). In 1790 a Manchester factory planned for 500 of his looms burned down with only 30 installed, probably arson by hand-loom weavers. Parliament granted him £10,000 in 1809. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Cartwright ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_loom]
- Weaving mechanised later than spinning. By the 1820s all cotton, wool and worsted was spun in mills, but much of the yarn still went to outworking hand-loom weavers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textile_manufacture_during_the_British_Industrial_Revolution]
- Arkwright opened the world's first steam-driven textile mill, on Miller Street in Manchester, in 1781. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cottonopolis]

## India: calicoes, the Calico Acts and deindustrialisation
- Calico is named after Calicut (Kozhikode) in south-western India. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calico]
- The East India Company brought cheap calico and chintz to Britain after the 1660s, and textiles overtook spices in value. By the 1760s cloth made up over three-quarters of the Company's exports. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calico_Acts ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Wool and linen interests petitioned Parliament against Indian cloth. A mob stormed EIC offices, and people wearing imported cloth were assaulted. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calico_Acts]
- The Calico Acts of 1700 and 1721 banned most imports of printed Indian cottons (1700) and then the sale of most cotton cloth (1721), exempting raw cotton and fustian. They were repealed in 1774. Earlier tariffs on East India goods were 10% in 1685 and 20% in 1690. Raw cotton's exemption fed a domestic industry that later mechanised. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calico_Acts]
- Indian labour costs were about one-fifth of Britain's, so British cloth could not compete. Lancashire made fustian (linen warp, cotton weft) until the water frame allowed an all-cotton warp. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Parthasarathi (quoted on the Calico Acts page): mechanisation let British producers "out-produce not just the quantity, but the quality of Indian textiles". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calico_Acts]
- Parthasarathi estimates that 28,000 tonnes of bullion, mainly from the New World, flowed into the Indian subcontinent between 1600 and 1800, about 30% of world production. Indian textiles were also bartered for enslaved Africans. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_India_under_Company_rule]
- Parthasarathi argues that post-Mughal Bengal and Mysore did not decline economically and were comparable to Britain in the late 18th century until British colonial policy deindustrialised them. Jeffrey Williamson instead argues that Mughal collapse raised Indian food prices and wages and cost India textile market share even before factory technology. Parthasarathi has suggested that wages in parts of South India (Mysore) may have matched Britain's, but called his evidence inconclusive. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- In 1813 Parliament ended the EIC's monopoly on trade with Asia. Indian handloom producers were then exposed to British machine-made goods, while British tariffs restricted Indian cloth. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De-industrialisation_of_India]
- Britain's share of Bengal's cloth imports rose from 25% in 1811 to 93% in 1840 (wording on Wikipedia is ambiguous: "British exports to Bengal increased, from 25% in 1811 to 93% in 1840"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_India_under_Company_rule]
- Machine cloth still could not match the quality of fine hand-woven Indian cloth by the mid-19th century. But coarser British cloth undersold Indian hand-made fabric and destroyed much of the Indian industry. Traditional hand-textile centres in India, the Middle East and China "left millions without work, many of whom starved" (Wikipedia wording). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Mughal India produced around 25% of global industrial output in the early 18th century. India's share of the world economy was about 4% by the 1950s. These are broad estimates. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De-industrialisation_of_India]
- India was the world's largest economy under the Mughals in 1700. By 1820 it ranked second, behind Qing China. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_India_under_Company_rule]

## Steam
- Thomas Savery patented a steam pump in 1698. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Thomas Newcomen's atmospheric engine of 1712 was the first practical device to turn steam into mechanical work. The first reliably recorded example, generally accepted as the first successful one, was built in 1712 at the Conygree (Coneygree) coalworks, Tipton, in the Black Country, to pump water from coal mines. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcomen_atmospheric_engine]
- Newcomen engines had spread to Hungary by 1722. 110 had been built by 1733 and 1,454 by 1800. They were very inefficient but cheap to run at pit-heads using unsaleable coal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- James Watt (1736–1819, born Greenock) was an instrument maker at the University of Glasgow. In 1763 he repaired a model Newcomen engine, and in 1765 he conceived the separate condenser, which kept the cylinder hot and the condenser cold. John Roebuck of the Carron ironworks backed him and his 1769 patent. When Roebuck faced insolvency in 1773, he passed his two-thirds share to Matthew Boulton. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt_steam_engine ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Watt]
- Boulton & Watt was founded in 1775 around Birmingham (Soho Manufactory) to exploit the separate-condenser patent. Cylinders came from John Wilkinson. The firm opened the Soho Foundry, Smethwick, in 1795 and built 496 engines between 1775 and 1800. The partnership passed to their sons in 1800, when the patent expired. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boulton_and_Watt ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Watt]
- The first commercial Watt engines date from 1776 and went to the Carron Company and others. In 1781 the sun-and-planet gear produced rotary motion, freeing factories from riverside sites. Watt coined "horsepower", and the SI unit, the watt, is named after him. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt_steam_engine ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Watt]
- Boulton & Watt engines used only 20–25% as much coal per horsepower-hour as Newcomen's (Industrial Revolution article). The Watt steam engine article says "half as much coal". The figures depend on comparison and date. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt_steam_engine]
- High-pressure engines, and so locomotives, followed once the Boulton & Watt patent expired in 1800. Watt himself resisted high pressure on safety grounds. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt_steam_engine]

## Iron, canals, railways
- Abraham Darby I (1677–1717) was a Quaker ironmaster. At Coalbrookdale in 1709 (furnace in blast from 10 January 1709, per Wikipedia) he smelted iron with coke instead of charcoal. Coke pig iron long suited only cast goods, until Abraham Darby II's furnaces from 1755. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Darby_I ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- UK iron: in 1720, 20,500 tons of charcoal iron and 400 tons of coke iron. In 1806, 7,800 tons of charcoal iron and 250,000 tons of coke cast iron. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- The Iron Bridge was built in 1778 using cast iron from Abraham Darby III. Henry Cort's puddling process and James Beaumont Neilson's hot blast (patented 1828) came later. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- The Bridgewater Canal, commissioned by the 3rd Duke of Bridgewater and engineered by James Brindley and John Gilbert, opened in 1761 from the Duke's Worsley mines to Manchester. Building that section cost £168,000. Within a year the price of coal in Manchester fell by about half. It is called the first great achievement of the canal age, though the Sankey Canal opened earlier. A national canal network existed by the 1820s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgewater_Canal ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- The Stockton and Darlington Railway officially opened on 27 September 1825, with Locomotion No. 1. It was the world's first public railway to use steam locomotives, but at first only coal trains were steam-hauled. Passengers rode in horse-drawn coaches until 1833. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockton_and_Darlington_Railway]
- Stephenson's Rocket won the Rainhill Trials in October 1829. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephenson%27s_Rocket]
- The Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened on 15 September 1830. It was the world's first inter-city railway and the first to rely exclusively on steam locomotives. It linked the port of Liverpool, which handled raw cotton, with Manchester's mills. George Stephenson built it, and William Huskisson MP was killed on opening day. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liverpool_and_Manchester_Railway ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]

## The factory system and clock discipline
- E. P. Thompson (1924–1993), "Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism", Past & Present no. 38 (1967), pp. 56–97. He argues that industrial capitalism imposed synchronised clock-time and work-discipline, replacing "task-orientation", where work followed natural or task rhythms rather than the clock. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._P._Thompson ; journal details from search summary: https://libcom.org/article/time-work-discipline-and-industrial-capitalism-e-p-thompson]
- Thompson's The Making of the English Working Class (1963) founded "history from below". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._P._Thompson]

## Child labour and the Factory Acts
- In 1819, 4.5% of cotton workers were under 10 and 54.5% under 19. In 1833 children made up about a third to two-thirds of textile-mill workers (a wide estimate). In 1842 children were over 25% of mining workers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_labour_in_the_British_Industrial_Revolution]
- The Health and Morals of Apprentices Act 1802 (Sir Robert Peel the elder) and the Cotton Mills and Factories Act 1819 (no children under 9; 12-hour cap for ages 9–16; cotton only) were effectively unenforced. There had been only two prosecutions under the 1819 Act by 1825. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts]
- Factory reform campaigners included Richard Oastler, Michael Sadler (Sadler Report 1832) and Lord Ashley (later 7th Earl of Shaftesbury). In 1830 Oastler wrote in the Leeds Mercury that Bradford child workers were worse off than slaves on sugar plantations. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts]
- Labour of Children, etc., in Factories Act 1833 ("Althorp's Act"):
  - No children under 9, except in silk mills; no night work under 18.
  - An 8-hour limit for ages 9–13 and 12 hours for ages 14–18.
  - Two hours of schooling a day for ages 9–13.
  - A professional Factory Inspectorate (four inspectors) under the Home Office. This was the first effective enforcement.
  - Caveat: the Factory Acts page also lists "Children (ages 9–12) are limited to 48 hours per week", which is internally inconsistent. State the 9–13 eight-hour figure, or simply "children 9–13 limited to shorter hours". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_labour_in_the_British_Industrial_Revolution]
- The Ten Hours Act (Factories Act 1847) followed long agitation by millworkers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts]

## The industrial city: Manchester and Engels
- Manchester was nicknamed "Cottonopolis" and is described as the world's first industrialised city. Its cotton mills peaked at 108 in 1853. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cottonopolis]
- Manchester's population (search summary; boundaries vary): about 22,500–23,000 in Percival's 1773–74 count, about 70,000–75,000 at the 1801 census and about 303,000 in 1851. [source: https://www.manchester.gov.uk/__data/assets/file/0025/124990/a20_1086-2016_manchester_population.pdf ; search summary]
- In 1851 about 15% of Manchester's population was Irish-born, and nearly half the men in Ancoats. The "Little Ireland" district existed from 1827 to 1847. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Manchester]
- Friedrich Engels, Die Lage der arbeitenden Klasse in England (1845), was written from his 1842–44 stay in Salford and Manchester. He was the eldest son of a German textile industrialist. The English translation, by Florence Kelley, was published in 1887 in New York. Engels argued that industrialisation made workers worse off. He reported death rates in Manchester and Liverpool well above the national average, and urban mortality from some diseases four times the rural rate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Condition_of_the_Working_Class_in_England]

## Resistance: Luddites and Peterloo
- The Luddites (1811–1816) took their name from the legendary "Ned Ludd". The movement began at Arnold, Nottinghamshire, on 11 March 1811 and spread to Yorkshire (croppers vs cropping frames) and Lancashire (power looms). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luddites]
- The Destruction of Stocking Frames, etc. Act 1812 made machine-breaking a capital crime. Lord Byron opposed it. The mass trial at York in January 1813 led to executions and transportation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luddites]
- 12,000 troops were deployed against the Luddites, more than Wellington took to Portugal in 1808 (Hobsbawm, following Darvall 1934). Hobsbawm called machine-breaking "collective bargaining by riot". Malcolm Thomis (1970) argued the attacks did not imply hostility to machinery as such. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luddites]
- Peterloo Massacre, St Peter's Field, Manchester, 16 August 1819. Yeomanry cavalry, then the 15th Hussars, charged a crowd of about 60,000 gathered to demand parliamentary reform, with Henry Hunt speaking. An estimated 18 were killed and 400–700 injured. Only about 11% of adult males then had the vote. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peterloo_Massacre]
- Women at Peterloo: female reform societies formed in the textile districts in 1819. Members of the Manchester Female Reform Society, dressed in white, accompanied Hunt. Mary Fildes carried the flag on the platform. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peterloo_Massacre]
- The name was coined by the Manchester Observer as an ironic echo of Waterloo. The aftermath brought the Six Acts and, indirectly, the founding of The Manchester Guardian. Shelley's The Masque of Anarchy, written in response, was not published until 1832. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peterloo_Massacre]

## Standard of living debate
- Allen's "Engels' pause" (term coined in a 2008 Explorations in Economic History paper): roughly 1790–1840, when working-class real wages stagnated while output per head grew. Feinstein (1990s) found working-class wages up only about 12% over 1780–1840, against per-capita output growth of about 46% (Crafts–Harley). Between 1840 and 1900 output per worker rose 90% and real wages 123%. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engels%27_pause]
- Wikipedia's Industrial Revolution article: some studies estimate British wages rose only 15% between the 1780s and 1850s, and life expectancy did not rise dramatically until the 1870s. Yet the share of Londoners dying before age five fell from 75% (1730–49) to 32% (1810–29). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- The debate (search summary): Eric Hobsbawm (pessimist) vs R. M. Hartwell (optimist) in the late 1950s–60s. Lindert and Williamson (1983) found rapid real-wage growth after 1819. Feinstein's "Pessimism Perpetuated" (1998) found much slower growth. The debate is now mostly about timing, with pessimists placing marked improvement in the 1840s–50s. [source: https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/IndustrialRevolutionandtheStandardofLiving.html ; search summary]
- Heights evidence (search summary): Floud, Wachter and Gregory, Height, Health and History (1990), used army and Royal Marines recruit records from the 1740s. Komlos (with Küchenhoff) re-estimated them and found a rapid fall in English male height, about 1.6 cm per decade, at the onset of industrialisation. Recruit-selection biases are a recognised problem. Wikipedia states that average height declined during the Industrial Revolution. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropometric_history ; search summary]

## Environmental costs
- Atmospheric CO2 was about 280 ppm for the 10,000 years before the mid-18th century. It is now over 50% higher: 422 ppm in October 2023 and 432 ppm in 2026, per Wikipedia. The IPCC uses 1750 as its "pre-industrial" baseline. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide_in_the_atmosphere_of_Earth ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene]
- Paul Crutzen proposed the Industrial Revolution as the start of the "Anthropocene". James Lovelock proposed Newcomen's 1712 engine. In March 2024 the IUGS rejected the Anthropocene as a formal geological epoch (12–4 vote, 2 abstentions), though the term remains in informal use. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene]
- Domestic UK emissions are about 3% of global cumulative CO2 since 1850. Including territories under British colonial rule raises this to about 5.1% (Carbon Brief, 2023) (search summary). [source: https://www.carbonbrief.org/revealed-colonial-rule-nearly-doubles-uks-historical-contribution-to-climate-change/ ; search summary]
- Andreas Malm, Fossil Capital: The Rise of Steam Power and the Roots of Global Warming (Verso, 2016). He argues that British cotton's switch from water to steam, centred c. 1825–1848, was driven by employers' wish to control labour, with mills sited in towns near workers, not by water scarcity (search summary). [source: https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/businessreview/?p=15140 ; search summary]
- Coal, coke, imported cotton, brick and slate replaced wood, charcoal, flax, peat and thatch, freeing land. A workhorse needed 3–5 acres of fodder. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]

## Spread: Belgium, France, the US
- Belgium was the second country to industrialise, led by coal-rich Wallonia (Liège, Charleroi) from the 1820s. John Cockerill (1790–1840, born Haslingden, Lancashire) founded the Seraing works in 1817 with King William I of the Netherlands as co-owner. It was vertically integrated and had a coke blast furnace by 1826. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cockerill_(industrialist)]
- Mechanisation of French textiles is traditionally dated to 1753, with the spread of Kay's flying shuttle. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Kay_(flying_shuttle)]
- Samuel Slater (1768–1835, born Belper) apprenticed to Jedediah Strutt. Defying British laws against emigration of skilled workers and export of designs, he memorised the machinery and sailed for New York in 1789. Belper people called him "Slater the Traitor". With Moses Brown (Almy & Brown) he built water-frame spinning at Pawtucket, Rhode Island, and the first factory opened in 1793. He employed children and whole families. Andrew Jackson called him "father of the American Industrial Revolution". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Slater]
- Francis Cabot Lowell memorised British power-loom designs on a tour in 1810 and founded the Boston Manufacturing Company at Waltham. After his death in 1817 his associates built Lowell, Massachusetts, a planned factory town (town incorporated 1826). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lowell_mill_girls]
- By 1840 the Lowell mills employed about 8,000 workers, nearly three-quarters women. Most were New England farm daughters aged 15–35, with an average age of about 24. They worked about 73 hours a week (5 am–7 pm) and lived in company boarding houses with a 10 pm curfew. They published the Lowell Offering. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lowell_mill_girls]
- The Lowell women struck ("turned out") in February 1834 over a wage cut (12.5% per NPS; 15% in other sources; about 800 walked out) and in 1836 over boarding-house rent rises. In 1845 they formed the Lowell Female Labor Reform Association, the first union of working women in the US. By 1850 Irish immigrant labour had largely replaced them. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lowell_mill_girls ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution ; https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/early-strikes-in-lowell.htm (search summary)]

## Enslaved cotton growers of the American South
- Eli Whitney's cotton gin (invented 1793; patent 1794) made short-staple upland cotton profitable and drove expansion of slavery into the Deep South. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_United_States ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution_in_the_United_States]
- About one million enslaved African Americans were forcibly moved from the Upper South to the Deep South by the domestic slave trade. Ira Berlin called this the "Second Middle Passage". The enslaved population reached 4 million by the 1860 census. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_United_States]
- US cotton production rose from 750,000 bales (1830) to 2.85 million (1850) and nearly 5 million by 1860 (the last figure is flagged "citation needed" on Wikipedia). By 1860 Southern plantations supplied 75% of the world's cotton. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_production_in_the_United_States ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Cotton]
- In 1858 Senator James Henry Hammond boasted that "Cotton is king". In the Civil War, Britain turned to India, Egypt and Brazil for cotton. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Cotton]
- Raw cotton reached Manchester through Liverpool from the West Indies, the US South and, later, India. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cottonopolis]

## Egypt: Muhammad Ali's cotton mills
- Muhammad Ali, ruler of Egypt from 1805 to 1848, created a state monopoly: producers had to sell to the state. He expanded long-staple cotton as a cash crop, using corvée labour on irrigation. He set up arms factories and a textile industry to compete with Europe. The textile industry "was not successful" but employed tens of thousands. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_of_Egypt]
- The 1838 Treaty of Balta Liman forced Egypt to dismantle import barriers and monopolies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_of_Egypt]
- A 2015 study found positive effects on Egyptian industrialisation (Panza and Williamson). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_of_Egypt]
- Search summary (MERIP 1981): about 30 cotton mills were operating by the early 1830s, with a labour force of about 30,000 recruited through the corvée. Another source gives 12,000 workers, so give a range. Egypt had no coal or iron ore, and mills were often animal-powered. [source: https://merip.org/1981/01/formation-of-the-egyptian-working-class/ ; search summary]

## China and the Great Divergence
- The term "Great Divergence" was coined by Samuel Huntington in 1996 and popularised by Pomeranz (2000). Eric Jones's alternative was "The European Miracle" (1981). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- Timing is disputed. Broadberry and Zhai date divergence to about 1700. The California School (Pomeranz) places the sharpest divergence in the 19th century, with the Yangtze Delta and Bengal comparable to advanced Europe before then. Broadberry finds English silver wages three times the Yangzi Delta's by 1600. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- Real wages, 1725–1825: labourers in Beijing and Delhi could buy only a subsistence basket, while those in London and Amsterdam could buy 4–6 times subsistence (Allen-style welfare ratios). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- Robert Brenner and Christopher Isett (2002) attribute England's divergence from the Yangzi Delta to property relations (secure tenancy in China vs competitive rental markets in England). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- Rough per capita estimates for the late 18th century (Landes): Western Europe about $1,500 PPP vs China about $450. This is contested by the California School. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]

## Was it a "revolution"?
- John Clapham and Nicholas Crafts argued that change was gradual and that "revolution" is a misnomer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- Crafts (1949–2023) and Knick Harley reinterpreted British growth in the 1980s and 1990s. Overall growth was much slower than earlier estimates suggested and concentrated in cotton and iron. Crafts himself did not say "revolution" was inappropriate; others used his figures to argue that. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Crafts]
- Crafts and Harley, "Output Growth and the British Industrial Revolution: A Restatement of the Crafts–Harley View", Economic History Review 45:4 (1992), pp. 703ff. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engels%27_pause]
- Their figures, as tabulated by later authors (search summary): national output growth of about 1.0% a year for 1760–1801 and about 1.9% for 1801–1831. Output per worker grew about 0.38% a year for 1780–1830 (Crafts 2020). Not checked against the 1992 paper. [source: https://www.jvoth.com/Factor_Prices.pdf ; https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/research/centres/cage/wp474.2020.pdf ; search summary]
- Antràs and Voth found productivity growth accelerated after 1800. Cuenca Esteban argued that Crafts–Harley understated cotton growth (search summary). [source: https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037c-66f3-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content ; search summary]

## Debates
- Why Britain: high wages and cheap coal (Allen) vs culture of useful knowledge and the Industrial Enlightenment (Mokyr) vs institutions after 1688 (property rights, finance) vs coal and colonies (Pomeranz) vs slavery profits (Williams, critiqued by Drescher). Critics of Allen include the 2018 Economic History Review wage studies. Clark, Jacks and McCloskey downplay coal. [sources: see Causes sections above]
- Great Divergence timing: California School (Pomeranz, Parthasarathi; around 1800 or later) vs Broadberry, Allen and others (Northwest Europe ahead by the 1600s–1700s). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- India's deindustrialisation: caused by British colonial and trade policy (Parthasarathi, Bairoch) vs Mughal decline and relative prices before British rule (Williamson). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- Standard of living: optimists (Hartwell; Lindert and Williamson; Griffin's people's history) vs pessimists (Engels; Hobsbawm; Thompson; Feinstein; Allen's "Engels' pause"). Heights evidence (Floud et al. vs Komlos). [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engels%27_pause ; https://www.econlib.org/library/Enc/IndustrialRevolutionandtheStandardofLiving.html]
- Revolution or evolution: Crafts–Harley gradualism vs the speed of structural change in cotton and iron, and evidence of acceleration after 1800 (Antràs and Voth). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Crafts]
- Why steam replaced water: cost and technology vs control of labour (Malm). [search summary]
- Luddites: technophobes vs rational workers using the one lever they had (Hobsbawm, Thomis, Thompson). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luddites]
- Muhammad Ali's industrialisation: a failed vanity project vs a real industrial push crushed by free-trade treaties (Balta Liman) and the lack of coal (Panza and Williamson 2015 found positive effects). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_of_Egypt]
