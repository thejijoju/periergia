## 1. Launch of reform, 1978–1984
- **Third Plenum:** reform and opening was launched by the CCP on 18 December 1978 at the third plenary session of the 11th Central Committee; Deng Xiaoping, "often credited as the 'General Architect'", replaced Hua Guofeng as paramount leader there. The "practice is the sole criterion for testing truth" discussion of 1978 defeated Hua's "Two Whatevers" ("Whatever Chairman Mao said, we will say and whatever Chairman Mao did, we will do"). [source: REF]
- **Nuance on Hua Guofeng:** REF notes Hua's break with Cultural Revolution economics was consistent with Deng's 1975 agenda; Hua "combined Soviet-style big push industrialization with an opening up to the capitalist world" and under him China opened its first SEZ and sought FDI. Use to avoid a Deng-only story. [source: REF]
- **Starting point:** 1950–73, real GDP per capita grew 2.9% a year on average, with large fluctuations; from 1970 the economy stagnated. By the late 1970s officials warned of repeating the "disaster of 1959". [source: REF]
- **Method — experiment first:** Deng described reform 1978–92 as a "large scale experiment"; major reforms "almost always began first as decentralized local experiments" before national adoption; officials faced few penalties for failed experiments. Contrast drawn with top-down perestroika. [source: REF]
- **Singapore model:** more than 22,000 Chinese officials were sent to Singapore over the years to study its methods. [source: REF]
- **Other reformers:** Hu Yaobang (general secretary) and Zhao Ziyang (premier) carried out reforms; also Xi Zhongxun (Xi Jinping's father), Wan Li. Chen Yun, "regarded by some as the second most powerful person in China after Deng", led the more conservative wing; he and Li Xiannian never visited Shenzhen. [source: REF]

## 2. The countryside: Xiaogang and the household responsibility system
- **Xiaogang (Fengyang County, Chuzhou, Anhui):** in the Great Leap famine 67 of the village's 120 people died, 1958–60; a quarter of Fengyang county's population died of starvation. In December 1978, 18 farmers (XGV names the leader "Yen Jingchang"; usually romanised Yan Jingchang — use only if checked) met in the largest house and signed a secret, illegal agreement to divide collective land into family plots, handing a quota to the state and keeping the surplus; they pledged that if anyone was caught and sentenced to death, the others would raise his children to 18. The 1979 harvest exceeded the previous five years combined; per capita income rose from 22 to 400 yuan; grain output 90,000 kg in 1979. [source: XGV; HRS] (XGV is thinly sourced; HRS tags the claim "primary source" — present as the celebrated founding story, as Chinese media do.)
- **Spread of the HRS:** first adopted 1979, officially established 1982 (the "No. 1 document"); by end-1979 in Anhui 10% of production teams had household contracting; Deng in April 1980 endorsed it for poor regions; "total victory" in 1983; communes abolished. Land stayed publicly owned. [source: HRS; REF]
- **Results:** agricultural output grew 8.2% a year vs 2.7% pre-reform; food prices fell nearly 50%; output rose 25% between 1975 and 1985 (REF gives both); by 1984 state grain stores were full. [source: REF; HRS]
- **Township and village enterprises:** "firms nominally owned by local governments but effectively private" gained market share from the state sector in the 1980s. [source: REF]

## 3. Opening: SEZs and Shenzhen
- **Origins:** Guangdong officials under party secretary Xi Zhongxun pushed to make the province a reform showcase, starting with Yuan Geng's Shekou project for the Hong Kong-based China Merchants (approved by Li Xiannian, 31 January 1979). In April 1979 Guangdong proposed special status for Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou and (in Fujian) Xiamen; Deng coined "special zones", likening them to pre-1949 revolutionary base areas. "Special economic zone" was later chosen to signal economic, not political, experiments. [source: SEZ]
- **Dates (sources differ):** SEZ says the four zones were "officially established on August 26, 1979" but also that they "were all established from 1980 to 1981"; SZ says the Central Committee designated Shenzhen the first SEZ in May 1980 and the NPC Standing Committee approved the "Regulations of the Guangdong Special Economic Zone" on 26 August (1980 — the 30th anniversary was marked 26 August 2010). **Use: established 1980 (Shenzhen's regulations approved 26 August 1980).** [source: SZ; SEZ]
- Hainan became the fifth SEZ in 1988; Shanghai's Pudong opened June 1990 (PSH/ SEZ list it as sixth SEZ, 1990); 14 coastal cities opened in May 1984. [source: SEZ; REF]
- **Location logic:** next to Hong Kong, Macau, Taiwan; by end-1990 exports to Hong Kong were two-thirds of Guangdong's industrial output. [source: SEZ]
- **Shenzhen before:** Bao'an County seat; between the 1950s and 1970s the county saw a large flow of people trying to escape to Hong Kong (100,000–560,000 refugees resided in the county). Renamed Shenzhen city 1979. SZ: "In 1980, Shenzhen had a population of 30,000" (the town); SEZ table gives 332,900 for 1980 (the whole municipality). [source: SZ; SEZ]
- **"Fishing village" myth:** Juan Du, *The Shenzhen Experiment* (Harvard UP, 2020), argues the "fishing village to megacity" story is a myth: Shenzhen was a node in older networks and had well over 300,000 people at founding by her count; its growth owed much to village collectives. **Writer: say "a border market town and farming and fishing villages", not "a fishing village".** [source: WebSearch: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shenzhen_Experiment; https://chinabooksreview.substack.com/p/a-kaleidoscopic-history]
- **Growth:** 1980–90 population ×6, GDP ×~60, industrial output ×~200 (SEZ table: 1980 pop. 332,900, GDP 270m yuan; 1990 1.677m, 17,167m yuan; 2022 17.68m, 3.24 trillion yuan). Shenzhen's GDP was 0.2% of Hong Kong's before 1980 and overtook it in 2018 (2.42 trillion yuan, US$372 bn). Population 18.2 million (2025), third-largest urban population in China. Huawei, ZTE and Konka founded there in the 1980s. [source: SEZ; SZ; REF]
- Slogans: "Time is Money, Efficiency is Life" (Shekou, under Yuan Geng); "Shenzhen speed", praised by Deng on his first inspection in January 1984. Shekou Industrial Zone founded 31 January 1979. [source: REF]
- 1983: barbed wire and checkpoints (the "second line") separated the SEZ from the rest of China; dissolved 1 July 2010. [source: SZ]

## 4. 1984–1992: prices, inflation, Tiananmen, the southern tour
- October 1984 *Decision on the Reform of the Economic System*. 1985 **dual-track price system**: plan prices inside the plan, market prices outside. In 1986 Zhao Ziyang and the leadership rejected "shock" price reform; radical price reform was tried again in 1988 and produced the first spiralling inflation since the 1940s, bank runs and panic buying; halted autumn 1988. Inflation was a problem in 1985, 1988 and 1992. [source: REF]
- **Tiananmen (brief; link out):** protests 15 April – 4 June 1989, triggered by Hu Yaobang's death; grievances included inflation, corruption, the dismantling of the "iron rice bowl" and limits on political participation; workers focused on inflation and the erosion of SOE job guarantees; protests in some 400 cities; ~1 million in the square on 17 May; martial law 20 May; crackdown 3–4 June; death toll estimates "from several hundreds to the low thousands". [source: TAM]
- **Effect on reform:** political reforms and the 1980s "New Enlightenment" ended; a conservative backlash; November 1989 resolution said reform had gone too fast; reform came to "a virtual halt"; US and EU arms embargoes, World Bank and ADB loans suspended; Jiang Zemin's leadership focused on resisting "Peaceful Evolution". Deng resigned his last post (CMC chair) in November 1989. [source: TOUR; REF]
- **Southern tour (Nanxun):** 18 January – 21 February 1992, aged 87, holding no formal post: Wuchang, Changsha, then Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Guangzhou (19–29 January), Shanghai from 31 January (spent Chinese New Year there). Told military leaders (Yang Shangkun, Liu Huaqing, Yang Baibing) that "those who do not promote reform should be brought down from their leadership positions", forcing Jiang to back reform. Hoped Guangdong would catch up with the Four Asian Tigers within 20 years. Initially ignored by Beijing media; Shanghai's *Liberation Daily* "Huangfu Ping" articles (1991) had prepared the ground. FDI reached US$25 billion in 1993. [source: TOUR]
- The 14th Party Congress (1992) followed; the "socialist market economy" became the formula; reform written into the CCP constitution 1997 and state constitution 1999. Stock exchanges: Shanghai reopened November 1990, Shenzhen founded December 1990. [source: REF; TOUR]
- The tour widened the coastal–interior gap and did not revive political reform (TOUR summarising scholars). [source: TOUR]

## 5. 1990s–2001: Zhu Rongji, SOE reform, layoffs, WTO
- **Zhu Rongji** (23 October 1928 – 12 August 2026 per ZHU; recent death — confirm before stating): mayor of Shanghai 1988–91, vice-premier 1991–98, PBoC governor 1993–95, premier 17 March 1998 – 16 March 2003. Led the 1994 tax-sharing reform; cut inflation from 24.3% (1994) to −0.8% (1998); moved bad loans of the four big state banks into asset-management companies and recapitalised them; as premier "saved the biggest SOEs and allowed thousands of other small and medium-sized firms and factories to go under"; millions lost their "iron rice bowl"; criticised for mass layoffs and for local land finance. [source: ZHU]
- **1994 fiscal reform:** government revenue had fallen from 35% to 11% of GDP by the mid-1990s (excluding SOE revenue); 1994 reform introduced a 17% VAT and raised the central share. Local tax revenue fell below 50% of the total (from ~80%) while localities kept over 70% of spending; the centre left land-transfer proceeds to local governments — the root of land finance and LGFVs. [source: REF; LGFV]
- **Xiagang ("stepping down from the post"):** more than a quarter of the SOE workforce laid off within several years; 24.4 million recorded as *xiagang* by end-1999; total layoffs mid-1990s to early 2000s estimated 27–35 million (concentrated in the Northeast and old Third Front bases); thousands of protests by laid-off workers, mostly framed as petitions for promised benefits rather than political opposition. The New Left saw xiagang as a betrayal of socialist-era guarantees. [source: XIA; REF]
- Between 2001 and 2004 the number of SOEs fell 48%. State sector's share of industrial output: 81% (1980) → 15% (2005). REF's claim that in 1997–98 "all state enterprises, except a few large monopolies, were liquidated" is overstated — do not use. [source: REF]
- **WTO:** China gained GATT observer status and from 1986 sought membership; joined the WTO on 11 December 2001; accepted harsher terms than other developing countries; designated a non-market economy for 15 years under its accession protocol (disputed after 2016). Average statutory tariff on industrial goods 8.9% by 2005 (vs 30.9% Argentina, 32.4% India); overall tariff rate fell from 56% to 15% over the reform period; trade from under 10% to 64% of GDP. [source: WTO; REF]
- **"China shock":** studies by David Autor, David Dorn, Gordon Hanson and others estimate US manufacturing jobs lost to Chinese import competition at between 550,000 and 2.0–2.4 million (estimates differ by method), with concentrated local harm. [source: SHOCK]
- Private sector: "first exceeded 50% of GDP in 2005" (REF; a figure of 70% by 2005 elsewhere in REF is unreliable). ECON today: private sector ~60% of GDP, 80% of urban employment, 90% of new jobs. [source: REF; ECON]

## 6. Growth and poverty: the numbers
- GDP growth averaged 9.91% a year 1979–2010; high 15.2% (1984), low 3.8% (1990) (HGDP). REF: about 9.5% a year 1978–2013; averaged around 10% under Hu Jintao. [source: HGDP; REF]
- GDP $150 billion (1978) → $18.74 trillion (2024) (REF). China passed Japan as the world's second-largest economy (nominal) in 2010. PPP: largest economy in 2014 (HGDP) or 2016 (REF) — **sources differ; say "by the mid-2010s"**. [source: REF; HGDP]
- **2025 official growth: 5.0%** (NBS release 19 January 2026; GDP 140.19 trillion yuan; quarterly 5.4, 5.2, 4.8, 4.5%). [source: WebSearch: https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/PressRelease/202601/t20260119_1962328.html]
- Doubts about statistics: the NBS itself has reported local data inflation; Rhodium Group estimated real 2024 growth at 2.4–2.8% vs the official figure (ECON states "4.8%"; the official 2024 figure is generally reported as 5.0% — do not quote ECON's 4.8%). [source: ECON; REF]
- Per capita: 1978–2005 Chinese GDP per capita rose from 2.7% to 15.7% of the US level; average wages rose sixfold 1978–2005; TFP accounted for 40.1% of growth after 1978 (vs −13.2% contribution 1957–78). [source: REF]
- **Poverty:** World Bank/DRC report *Four Decades of Poverty Reduction in China* (published 31 March/1 April 2022): the number living under $1.90 a day fell by close to 800 million over 40 years; by China's national line, 770 million; China contributed "close to three-quarters" of the global reduction in extreme poverty. [source: WebSearch: https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2022/04/01/lifting-800-million-people-out-of-poverty-new-report-looks-at-lessons-from-china-s-experience]
- POV: extreme poverty rate (US$1.90, 2011 PPP) fell from 88% (1981) to 0.7% (2015); table: 66.2% (1990), 11.2% (2010), 0.7% (2015), 0.1% (2019). Between 1990 and 2005 China accounted for more than three-quarters of global poverty reduction. [source: POV]
- **"Eliminated absolute poverty":** announced 23 November 2020 against China's own line (¥2,300/yr at 2010 prices, ~¥4,000 in 2020). At the $5.50/day upper-middle-income line, ~13% (2020) remained below. In 2020 Premier Li Keqiang said 600 million people lived on under 1,000 yuan a month. [source: POV]
- Pre-reform base: in 1981 China's poorest were better fed, educated and healthier than India's and Pakistan's poorest; life expectancy (World Bank estimate 64) 3 years above the middle-income average. Lin Chun stresses Mao-era human capital and collective land as factors in later success. [source: POV; REF]
- Inequality: Gini estimated above 0.45 (REF); ECON infobox gives 36.0 (2022, World Bank method) — **measures differ; say inequality rose sharply from a low base**. [source: REF; ECON]

## 7. Migrants, hukou, factories, families
- **Hukou:** 1958 Hukou Registration Regulation (signed 9 January 1958) divided people into agricultural (rural) and non-agricultural (urban) status; in the famine an urban hukou with rations "could mean the difference between life and death". After 1978 migrants (most early export-zone workers were women) lived in company dormitories or shanty towns without urban benefits; since the 1980s an estimated 200 million lived outside their registered areas. Reforms of 2014 abolished the agricultural/non-agricultural distinction in principle; the 2014–2020 National New-Type Urbanization Plan aimed to give urban hukou to 100 million by 2020, relaxing rules for small and medium cities but keeping strict limits on cities over 5 million. [source: HUK; URB]
- World Bank's Shahid Yusuf called hukou a "cornerstone of China's urbanisation strategy", helping avoid widespread slums (a counterpoint to its inequity). [source: HUK]
- **Migrant workers (NBS, 2024):** 299.73 million rural migrant workers, of whom 178.71 million worked outside their home areas; 31.6% aged over 50; average monthly income 4,961 yuan; construction's share of their jobs down to 14.3%. [source: WebSearch: https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/statistics/202504/30/content_WS6812249ac6d0868f4e8f2395.html; https://www2.yicaiglobal.com/news/chinas-migrant-worker-population-is-aging-with-almost-one-third-older-than-50-report-says]
- **Left-behind children:** UNICEF 2018 annual report: about 69 million children left behind by one or both parents due to migration, about 30% of rural children. [source: LBC]
- **Factory life:** Leslie T. Chang's *Factory Girls* (2008) follows two teenage migrant women, Min and Chunming, in Dongguan. [source: WebSearch: https://www.harvardmagazine.com/node/3836; https://www.americamagazine.org/?p=113947]
- **Foxconn suicides:** a spate of suicides at the Foxconn City industrial park in Shenzhen (Longhua) in 2010 linked to low pay and harsh conditions; FOXS's 2010 table lists 15 attempts (mostly jumping from buildings), at least 10 deaths, survivors including Tian Yu (17, paralysed). The complex employed up to 300,000 people. Foxconn raised wages, installed "suicide-prevention netting" and brought in counsellors; Apple and HP investigated. **Do not use the commonly cited "18 attempts, 14 deaths" — not confirmed.** [source: FOXS; WebSearch: https://www.counterpunch.org/2020/10/07/dying-for-an-i-phone-an-interview-with-jenny-chan/]
- **One-child policy:** near-universal limit imposed 1980 (formally 18 September 1980), in the constitution 1982; by 1984 only ~35.4% of the population subject to the strict rule; rural second child if first was a daughter; two-child policy from 1 January 2016; three children May 2021; all limits removed July 2021. 1980–2014: 324 million women received IUDs, 108 million sterilised. The CCP credits it with China's economic ascendancy and 400 million births prevented (disputed). The economic logic: fewer dependants, a "demographic dividend". Costs: coercion, son preference, "missing women"; also more female workforce participation and investment in daughters' education (Greenhalgh). Origins: missile scientist Song Jian's projections (influenced by *The Limits to Growth*); Greenhalgh's claim that Song "hijacked" policy is disputed (Liang Zhongtang). [source: OCP]
- Today: labour force 773 million (2024), shrinking with ageing; youth unemployment 16.5% (December 2025, ages 16–24). [source: ECON]

## 8. Urbanisation and new cities
- Urban share: under 10% (1949); 18% (1978); 30% (1995); 39% (2002); majority urban in 2011; 52.6% (2012); government figure 67% at end-2024. [source: URB] (URB elsewhere says the 50% mark came "in the 1990s" — wrong; ignore.)
- Mao era: almost 18 million urban youth sent to the countryside 1962–78. [source: URB]
- 1994 fiscal reform pushed local governments to raise money from land development; urban growth mostly spread outward onto farmland. [source: URB]
- **Ghost cities — Kangbashi (Ordos, Inner Mongolia):** launched 2003 as a new district for 1 million; government moved there 2006; Al Jazeera (November 2009; journalist Melissa Chan) and *Time* (April 2010) made it famous as a "ghost city" with 20,000–30,000 residents; 2014 vacancy rate of new homes 70%; population 118,796 (2020 census) / ~153,000 per Shepard (2017). Wade Shepard argued the label was premature: it had been five years old when filmed. (KANG's "1.1 trillion yuan ($161 billion)" investment figure is implausible — do not use.) [source: KANG; UOD]
- UOD: better to speak of "underoccupied developments"; national housing utilisation efficiency fell from 84% (2010) to 78% (2020). Shepard's book *Ghost Cities of China* (Zed, 2015) argues most fill up in time (reviewer's summary). [source: UOD; WebSearch: https://newstatesman.com/?p=86216]

## 9. Infrastructure
- **Three Gorges Dam** (Sandouping, Yichang, Hubei): Sun Yat-sen proposed a Yangtze dam in 1919; NPC approval 1992 with only 67.75% in favour (1,767 for, 177 against, 664 abstaining); construction from 14 December 1994; dam body (185 m high, ~2.3 km long) completed 2006; fully operational July 2012; ship lift 2015. 32 × 700 MW + 2 × 50 MW = 22,500 MW, the world's largest power station by capacity; ~95 TWh/year (112 TWh in 2020). Cost ¥203 bn (US$31.8 bn); by end-2008, 68.6 bn yuan of 148.4 bn spent went on relocating residents. [source: TGD]
- **Displacement:** estimates range from 1.13 million to about 1.4 million; 1.24 million moved by June 2008 (some as far as Gaoyang, Hebei); planners expected 13 cities, 140 towns and 1,350 villages flooded. **"About 1.3 million" is within the range; say "1.1–1.4 million".** Archaeologists excavated 723 sites. Effects: landslides (97 significant ones in the first four months of 2010), sedimentation, threats to species; the baiji (river dolphin) is extinct (some Chinese government scholars attribute it to the dam); the Chinese paddlefish declared extinct 2022. [source: TGD]
- **South–North Water Transfer:** to move 44.8 km³ a year from the Yangtze basin to the north via three routes; Mao raised the idea in 1952 ("there's plenty of water in the south, not much water in the north. If at all possible, borrowing some water would be good"); construction from 2002–03; Eastern and Central routes operational in late 2014 (Central completed 12 December 2014); Western route not built; >500 bn yuan (>$70 bn) invested; 76.7 km³ transferred in ten years (reported 2024); about 330,000 people resettled around the Danjiangkou Reservoir (dam raised from 162 m to 176.6 m). [source: SNW]
- **High-speed rail:** essentially none before 2007–08: CRH services introduced April 2007; the Beijing–Tianjin intercity line (opened 1 August 2008) was the first 350 km/h commercial line. Beijing–Shanghai HSR construction began 18 April 2008, opened June 2011. Stimulus: rail investment $49.4 bn (2008), $88 bn (2009). Network: 8,358 km (January 2011, already world's longest); 37,900 km (end-2020); 48,000 km (end-2024); passed **50,000 km at the end of 2025** (Xi'an–Yan'an line opened 26 December 2025; some reports 50,400 km); target ~60,000 km by 2030 and 70,000 km by 2035; roughly two-thirds of the world's HSR. Built on foreign technology transfer (Kawasaki, Siemens, Alstom, Bombardier) then domesticated. [source: HSR; WebSearch: https://www.dawn.com/news/1963513; https://www.business-standard.com/world-news/china-high-speed-railway-expansion-2030-five-year-plan-infrastructure-126010500716_1.html]
- HSR costs: Railway Minister Liu Zhijun removed for corruption, February 2011 (est. ¥1 bn in bribes); **Wenzhou collision, 23 July 2011: 40 dead, 172 injured** (2014 World Bank report: inadequate testing of new signalling); HSR accumulated debt $839 bn by end-2023; 2016 HSR revenue (¥140.9 bn) below interest payments (¥156.8 bn); many lines lose money, subsidised by freight and local governments. Ridership (EMU trains) 3,272 million (2024). [source: HSR]
- **Expressways:** 0 km (1988) → 11,605 km (2000) → 184,000 km (end-2023) → 190,700 km (end-2024), surpassing the US Interstate system's length in 2011; connects over 99% of cities over 200,000 people. [source: EXP]
- **Ports:** Shanghai overtook Singapore as the world's busiest container port in 2010 (29.05 m TEU), 43.3 m TEU in 2019; Yangshan deep-water port built by 2005, linked by the Donghai Bridge. Of the world's top five container ports, four are Chinese (Shanghai, Ningbo-Zhoushan, Shenzhen, Qingdao; Singapore second). [source: PSH; PORTS] (PORTS table year labels are muddled — give rankings, not latest TEU numbers.)
- **Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macau Bridge:** 55 km bridge–tunnel, longest sea crossing in the world; built 2009–18; ¥127 bn; opened 24 October 2018 (inaugurated by Xi the day before). [source: HZMB]

## 10. How it was built: state, plans, SOEs, land, debt
- **SOEs today:** about 25% of GDP (2020; other estimates 23–28% for 2017); 85 of the 135 Chinese firms in the 2023 Fortune Global 500 are state-owned; ~40% of stock-market capitalisation. SASAC created 2003 (189 central SOEs then). Under Xi the party formally took a commanding role in all SOEs (as of 2020); at least 288 firms rewrote charters by 2017 for party influence. [source: SOE; REF]
- **Local competition:** officials in fast-growing areas were more likely to be promoted, making local governments "hungry for investment". [source: REF]
- **Land finance:** urban land is state-owned; local governments sell use rights; in 2019, 38% of local government revenue came from land sales and land-backed financing. [source: LF]
- **LGFVs:** local governments were barred from borrowing (1994 Budget Law; 1996 PBoC rules), so they set up financing vehicles backed by land; the model became widespread from the Wuhu Construction Investment Company (land-collateralised from 2002) and Tianjin (¥50 bn from CDB, 2003); 2,380 LGFVs by 2007. IMF estimate: LGFV debt US$8 trillion (47% of GDP) at end-2023. [source: LGFV]
- **2008 stimulus:** 4 trillion yuan (US$586 bn), announced 9 November 2008, to be spent by end-2010; only 1.2 trillion from the central government, the rest from localities (LGFV: local governments bore 70%); largest slice 1.5 trillion (~38%) for infrastructure (rail, roads, airports); growth fell to ~6% by end-2008 and recovered to over 10% by mid-2009; blamed for the subsequent debt surge. [source: STIM; LGFV]
- **2024 debt swap:** 8 November 2024 NPC Standing Committee: 6 trillion yuan added to local special-bond quota (2 trillion a year 2024–26) plus 4 trillion over five years to swap "hidden" debt — about 10 trillion yuan in all. Officials put hidden local debt at 14.3 trillion yuan (end-2023); the IMF estimate was about 60 trillion. [source: WebSearch: https://amp.cnn.com/cnn/2024/11/08/business/china-economy-hidden-debt-intl-hnk; https://bnnbloomberg.ca/business/international/2024/11/08/china-unveils-839-billion-debt-swap-to-rescue-local-governments]
- Government debt 82.9% of GDP (2023, ECON infobox; excludes most LGFV debt). Household consumption ~39.9% of GDP; fixed investment ~39.9% (2024). [source: ECON]

## 11. Property boom and bust; Evergrande
- Housing was the first welfare sector to be marketised (1990s). After 2005 loose money produced a property bubble in which prices tripled (REF); average land values tripled by 2009. [source: REF; RE]
- **Size of real estate:** Rogoff and Yang (2021): ~29% of GDP including knock-on effects; their 2024 update ~22% (31% with infrastructure); ADB value-added method: 15.4% (2017). **Say "between about 15 and 30 per cent depending on definition".** [source: WebSearch: https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/can-chinas-outsized-real-estate-sector-amplify-delta-induced-slowdown; https://imf.org/-/media/Files/Publications/Fandd/Article/2024/12/Rogoff.ashx]
- **"Three red lines" (2020):** caps on developers' leverage, following Xi's slogan "houses are for living, not for speculation". [source: RE]
- **Evergrande:** founded 1996 by Hui Ka Yan (Xu Jiayin) in Guangzhou; world's most indebted property developer; crossed all three red lines; liquidity crisis from August 2021; offshore default December 2021 (Fitch "restricted default"); owed ~2 trillion yuan (US$310 bn) to retail investors, banks, suppliers and others; US Chapter 15 filing 18 August 2023; Hong Kong High Court winding-up order 29 January 2024; delisted from the Hong Kong exchange 24 August 2025; CSRC fined it 4.2 bn yuan for falsifying revenue. EVG: on 20 August 2026 Hui was sentenced to life imprisonment (recent — state with date and only if re-checked). Pre-sold unfinished flats left buyers waiting. [source: EVG; RE]
- Contagion: Country Garden (largest developer by sales) missed a payment October 2023; Sunac, Kaisa, others. [source: RE]

## 12. Belt and Road
- Announced by Xi in Astana (Nazarbayev University, 7 September 2013: "Silk Road Economic Belt") and Indonesia (October 2013: "21st-Century Maritime Silk Road"). Projects in more than 150 countries (146–150 per Fudan's Green Finance & Development Center, May 2025). Flagships: China–Pakistan Economic Corridor, Boten–Vientiane railway, Jakarta–Bandung HSR, ports of Piraeus, Hambantota, Gwadar. Mostly loans. AIIB proposed October 2013. Withdrawals: Italy's MoU lapsed March 2024; Panama withdrew February 2025; Philippines dropped projects 2023. Motives include exporting excess capacity, markets, resources, developing Xinjiang/the west. [source: BRI]
- Precedent: 58% of Chinese economic aid 1956–69 went to Africa. [source: BRI]
- **Hambantota (Sri Lanka):** championed by Mahinda Rajapaksa (a Hambantota native) after the US and India declined to fund it; phase one funded by a China Exim loan of US$306.7 m (85%) at 6.3%; opened 18 November 2010; phase two loan $757 m at 2% (2012); losses mounted; in July 2017 a new government sold a 70% stake and granted a 99-year lease to China Merchants Port for $1.12 bn, used for balance-of-payments needs; port now a vehicle transhipment hub but not a major commercial success (as of 2024). Brautigam and Rithmire: of $4.5 bn Sri Lankan debt service due in 2017, only ~5% was for Hambantota. [source: HAM; DTD; WebSearch: https://www.chathamhouse.org/node/23773/nojs]
- **"Debt-trap diplomacy":** term coined by Brahma Chellaney in 2017. Rebuttals: Center for Global Development (2018: China restructured or waived payments for 51 countries 2001–17 without seizing assets); Rhodium Group (2019: of 40 renegotiations "asset seizures are a very rare occurrence"); Lowy Institute (2020: Hambantota a "myth"); Brautigam (*Area Development and Policy* 2020). No Chinese asset seizures in Africa as of 2023. Still, Chinese lenders are major creditors (Laos ~40%, Kenya 21% of external debt); in 2021 six African states sent over a third of their debt payments to Chinese lenders. Kerry Brown (2023): at Hambantota China has had to commit more money and take on more risk — the opposite of a trap. [source: DTD; HAM]
- 89% of labour on Chinese-funded African projects surveyed by McKinsey (2017) was African. [source: DTD]

## 13. Environment
- **Air:** January 2013 Beijing PM2.5 near 1,000 μg/m³ at peak; US Embassy AQI over 755 on 12 January 2013; Harbin closed schools and airport, 21 October 2013. [source: POL]
- **"War on pollution":** Premier Li Keqiang, government work report to the NPC, 5 March 2014, pledged to "declare war against pollution and fight it with the same determination we battled poverty", calling pollution "nature's red-light warning against the model of inefficient and blind development"; plan to close 50,000 small coal furnaces in 2014. [source: WebSearch: https://www.scmp.com/news/china/article/1440784/china-declares-war-pollution-announces-li-keqiang; https://phys.org/news/2014-03-china-premier-declares-war-pollution.pdf]
- Results: PM2.5 in the first 74 cities fell 42% and SO₂ 68% between 2013 and 2018; particulates fell 40% 2013–20; Beijing left the top 200 most-polluted cities in 2020 (but smog returned on some days in 2026). [source: POL]
- **Soil/water:** 2014 government report: 16.1% of soils and 19.4% of farmland polluted; 2013 Huangpu dead pigs (16,000); 2014 Lanzhou benzene leak. [source: POL]
- China is the largest total emitter of CO₂, though some countries exceed it per head. [source: REF]

## 14. Xi era and the open question
- 2013 Third Plenum: market forces to play a "decisive" role; at the same time party control of firms strengthened; Made in China 2025; "dual circulation"; "common prosperity" (from 2021) used to justify crackdowns on tech and tutoring; some analysts say the reform era was "scaled down significantly". [source: REF]
- 2025: China said it would not seek new special and differential treatment at the WTO (WTO says "officially gave up its developing country status" — imprecise; use the narrower wording only if re-checked). [source: WTO]
- Growth slowing: 5.0% (2025); ECON infobox 4.5% for 2026 (estimate/forecast — do not state as final). [source: ECON]

## Debates
- **Why did China grow? Gradualism vs. market vs. state.** Naughton: reform "growing out of the plan" without a grand vision — a mix of laissez-faire and incentive experiments. Weber: China twice (1986, 1988) rejected shock therapy; the dual-track price system let markets grow at the margin of the plan, unlike Eastern Europe's big bang (where GDP fell 13–65% and inflation hit 200–1,000%). Ang: "directed improvisation" — the centre sets direction and bounded discretion, localities improvise; markets built first with weak institutions; she argues Xi has reversed Deng's limits on power. Huang: the real engine of the 1980s was rural private entrepreneurship (TVEs), and the 1990s shift to urban, state-led capitalism was a wrong turn. Lin Chun: Mao-era human capital and collective land mattered as much as markets. Mancur Olson: the Cultural Revolution had destroyed entrenched interest groups that could have blocked reform. [source: BN; IW; YYA; YH; REF; WebSearch: https://snfagora.jhu.edu/resources/how-china-escaped-the-poverty-trap]
- **Vogel's Deng:** Vogel's biography is the authoritative, broadly admiring account (Gelber Prize); critics of hagiography stress Deng's responsibility for the 1989 crackdown (TOUR: "ordered by Deng himself"). Present both. [source: EV; TOUR]
- **Poverty measurement:** World Bank "close to 800 million"; Sullivan, Moatsos and Hickel (*New Political Economy*, 2023) ask whether reform-era gains partly reflect "income deflation" (loss of free Mao-era provision) — listed in POV's references only; summarise cautiously. [source: POV]
- **Can the model last? (Pettis):** investment near 50% of GDP and depressed household consumption make growth debt-dependent; China must either deliberately cut investment (slower growth) or face a financial contraction. His 2011 prediction of ~3% growth within the decade proved too pessimistic (Arthur Kroeber disputed it). Data point: household consumption ~40% of GDP (2024). [source: MP; ECON]
- **Ghost cities:** media "ghost city" vs Shepard's "they fill up in time"; scholars prefer "underoccupied" and note falling housing utilisation (84%→78%, 2010–20). [source: UOD; KANG]
- **Debt-trap diplomacy:** Chellaney vs Brautigam/Rithmire, CGD, Rhodium, Lowy, Kerry Brown; the middle view: Chinese loans are often expensive and opaque and China is a large creditor, but evidence of a strategy to seize assets is lacking; recipient governments sought the loans. [source: DTD; HAM]
- **Statistics:** official growth vs Rhodium's lower estimates; local data inflation acknowledged by the NBS. [source: ECON; REF]
