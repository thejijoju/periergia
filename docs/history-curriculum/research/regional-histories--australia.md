# Verified facts — The 'Settler Revolution' at the Edge of Empire: Australia, 1770-1901 (researched 2026-11-11)

Node: history/regional-histories/oceania-and-the-pacific/the-settler-revolution-at-the-edge-of-empire-australia-1770-1901. Required coverage (seed summary): Cook's 1770 charting and claim of the east coast; terra nullius; the First Fleet and Sydney Cove (1788) and convict transportation; frontier violence and dispossession (epidemics, massacres, resistance leaders like Pemulwuy); squatters, pastoral expansion and wool; Belich's "settler revolution" of explosive Anglo migration and boom-bust growth; the 1850s gold rushes and Eureka (1854); colonial self-government; anti-Chinese agitation and the roots of White Australia; Federation on 1 January 1901, and the century's consequences for Indigenous Australians.

House rule: centre Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people as actors (Pemulwuy, Windradyne, Truganini, the Wybalenna petitioners, the Raukkan voters, Eddie Mabo and the Meriam plaintiffs) and Aboriginal scholars (Marcia Langton, Larissa Behrendt; Bruce Pascoe, whose identity is disputed — see Debates). Do not narrate Aboriginal people only as victims.

## 1. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander societies before 1788
- Ancestors of Aboriginal Australians arrived about 50,000–65,000 years ago; most evidence suggests "between 50,000 and 65,000 years"; Madjedbebe rock shelter in Arnhem Land is "perhaps the oldest site". Some argue oral tradition should have equal status with archaeology. [source: IA; PRE] (Published chapters say "at least 50,000" and "60,000–40,000"; use "at least 50,000 years, perhaps 65,000".)
- About 600 tribes or nations and 250 languages with dialects (Flood 2019; Broome 2019); "over 200 distinct languages" at British settlement (Heath 2017). Use "some 250 languages". [source: IA; TN]
- Pre-1788 population: estimates range from about 300,000 (ABS; the lead gives 318,000) to more than 3,000,000 (Bradshaw, Williams, Saltré, Norman and Ulm, 2021). "Recent archaeological finds suggest that a population of 500,000 to 750,000 could have been sustained", some ecologists one to two million. Distribution debated: Pardoe (most in the south-east along the Murray) vs Evans (Queensland most densely populated); Ørsted-Jensen (2011) estimates Queensland held a third to nearly 40% of the pre-contact population. Give as an attributed range. [source: IA; AFW]
- Fire-stick farming practised by certain groups. Bill Gammage, *The Biggest Estate on Earth: How Aborigines Made Australia* (2012), on fire, dams and cropping. [source: IA; DE]
- From the mid-1660s Indonesian (Makassan) trepang fishermen visited the north coast annually; Aboriginal people adopted dugout canoes and metal harpoon heads; trade is reflected in art, ceremony and oral tradition. [source: PRE; IA]
- Torres Strait Islanders are Melanesian, distinct from Aboriginal peoples. Islands first occupied about 4,000 years ago; by 2,500 years ago a distinctive maritime culture; agriculture on some islands; villages by 700 years ago. Over 100 islands, annexed by Queensland in 1879. Today ~92% of Indigenous Australians identify as Aboriginal, 4% Torres Strait Islander, 4% both (2021 census: almost one million, 3.8% of the population). [source: IA; TSI]
- Recorded Indigenous population by 1900 "approximately 93,000" (ABS; a partial count, desert peoples not counted until the 1930s); AFW gives "about 90,000 in 1901". Recovery began from the 1930s. [source: IA; AFW]

## 2. Cook, 1770, and the claim
- Cook's secret instructions: "You are also with the Consent of the Natives to take possession of Convenient Situations in the Country in the Name of the King of Great Britain; or, if you find the Country uninhabited take Possession for His Majesty..." [source: JC]
- Point Hicks sighted 19 April 1770 (first Europeans on the east coast); first landfall at Botany Bay (Kamay) 29 April 1770. Two Gweagal men opposed the landing; one was wounded with small shot. Nicholas Thomas says the E. Phillips Fox painting of Cook restraining his men is misleading: Cook was the first to shoot. Cook concluded the local people "only wanted the British to leave". Stayed a week. [source: JC]
- Endeavour struck the reef; repaired seven weeks at the Endeavour River; dispute with the Guugu Yimithirr over green turtles; an elder presented a broken-tipped spear as a peace offering, re-enacted annually in Cooktown since 1959 with Guugu Yimithirr participation. [source: JC]
- 22 August 1770, at Possession Island (Bedanug/Bedhan Lag, Kaurareg country, Torres Strait): Cook "took possession of the whole Eastern Coast... by the name New South Wales". The Kaurareg won native title over the island in 2001. [source: PI]
- Joseph Banks, not Cook, promoted Botany Bay for settlement; "Cook had no role in promoting the colonisation of Australia". [source: JC; FF]
- Cook judged the "Natives of New Holland" "far more happier than we Europeans" (see Quotations). [source: JC]

## 3. Terra nullius (and Mabo)
- The term was not used in the 18th century; Frost, Attwood and others argue the concept was widely accepted; Borch argues it "developed as a legal theory in the nineteenth century". [source: TN]
- Stuart Banner: first known Australian legal use of the concept (not term) in 1819 (Barron Field vs Macquarie tax dispute); law officers advised NSW was acquired by possession as "desert and uninhabited". [source: TN]
- Governor Bourke's proclamation, 26 August 1835: the Crown sole authority for distributing land; it voided Batman's Treaty (6 June 1835, with Wurundjeri and Boonwurrung elders), the only documented attempt by Europeans to negotiate occupation by treaty. [source: TN; BTR]
- *R v Murrell* (1836), Justice Burton: Aboriginal people "had no sovereignty". *Cooper v Stuart* (1889), Privy Council: NSW was "a tract of territory practically unoccupied, without settled inhabitants or settled law". [source: TN]
- *Mabo v Queensland (No 2)*, High Court, 3 June 1992: brought in 1982 by Eddie Mabo and other Meriam people of Mer (Murray Island), Torres Strait; the court rejected the "enlarged notion of terra nullius" and recognised native title; Native Title Act 1993 followed. Justice Brennan led. Paul Keating's Redfern speech (10 Dec 1992) said it "establishes a fundamental truth, and lays the basis for justice". Note: Mabo concerned Torres Strait Islanders, a good chance to centre them. [source: MABO; TN]

## 4. The First Fleet and Sydney Cove
- Eleven ships (two Royal Navy, three storeships, six convict transports) under Captain Arthur Phillip left Portsmouth 13 May 1787; reached Botany Bay 18–20 January 1788; Phillip rejected it for Port Jackson; arrived Sydney Cove 26 January 1788 (Australia Day). Colony formally proclaimed 7 February 1788. [source: FF; TN]
- Numbers (Mollie Gillen): 1,420 embarked, 1,373 landed, including 582 male and 193 female convicts embarked (543 and 189 landed), 247 marines, 323 crew; possibly ~1,530 embarked counting unrecorded seamen. Surgeon John White recorded 48 deaths and 28 births on the voyage. [source: FF]
- Phillip was instructed to "live in amity and kindness" with the Aboriginal people; in May 1788 five convicts were killed; up to 300 Aboriginal people sighted on the colony's outskirts in June. Phillip was speared in 1790 and forbade reprisals, but after Pemulwuy killed his huntsman (McIntyre) in December 1790 ordered ten Bidjigal killed and beheaded; Tench negotiated it down; the expedition found no one. [source: AFW; PEM]

## 5. Convict transportation
- About 162,000 convicts, 1788–1868 (CA lead says "about 162,000... between 1784 and 1868"; its cessation section says "about 164,000... on board 806 ships"). Use "about 162,000–164,000". Origin: English and Welsh 70%, Irish 24%, Scottish 5%, 1% others including Māori, Chinese from Hong Kong, enslaved people from the Caribbean. [source: CA]
- Women: about 24,000 (one in seven) 1788–1852; ~80% convicted of theft; none transported for prostitution. Thieves were 80% of all transportees. About 3,600 political prisoners (Scottish Martyrs, Irish rebels of 1798/1803/1848, Fenians, Chartists). [source: CA]
- Background: about 50,000–120,000 had been sent to America (estimates vary; 60,000 also cited) before the American Revolution closed that outlet. [source: CA]
- End dates: NSW suspended by Order-in-Council 22 May 1840 (some 150,000 sent by then); Van Diemen's Land ended 1853 (last ship *St Vincent*; jubilee 10 August 1853); Western Australia received convicts 1850–1868 (9,668 on 43 ships); last ship *Hougoumont* arrived 10 January 1868. Australasian Anti-Transportation League formed 1850; its concern was mostly the "hated stain" on free society rather than inhumanity. [source: CA]
- Assignment, tickets of leave, certificates of freedom (sentence usually seven years); secondary punishment at Port Arthur (1830), Norfolk Island, Moreton Bay (1824). By 1821 convicts, emancipists and their children owned two-thirds of cultivated land. [source: CA; H1]

## 6. The 1789 epidemic
- April 1789, ~16 months after arrival: an epidemic (usually called smallpox) devastated the Aboriginal people of the Sydney region while sparing the British. Phillip estimated perhaps half died; Bennelong also believed half the Eora of the district died. Bradley (9 May 1789) described the dying in rock shelters. [source: SP]
- Origin is contested; three explanations: (1) smallpox from Makassan visitors in the north spreading overland (Campbell, *Invisible Invaders*, 2002; Macknight 1986, 2011); (2) release from surgeon John White's variolation material, accidental or deliberate (Butlin, *Our Original Aggression*, 1983: "it is possible and, in 1789, likely, that infection of the Aboriginal people was a deliberate extermination act"; David Day 2001; Christopher Warren 2007, 2014); (3) not smallpox but chickenpox (Ford, Carmody). Fenner (1985): data "seem to me to favour smallpox, though the question of its origin is unexplained". Connor called Day's theory "unsustainable"; Keneally: "no evidence of anyone's intention"; Blainey: fresh evidence "discounted his thesis without totally discrediting it". The deliberate-release view "resonated with some Aboriginal authorities, including professors Maynard and Langton". Present as unresolved. [source: SP]
- Tench (1789): "Our surgeons brought out variolous matter in bottles... but to infer that it was produced from this cause were a supposition so wild as to be unworthy of consideration." [source: SP]

## 7. Frontier wars, resistance and massacres
- Conflict began months after January 1788 and continued to at least 1928 (Coniston), arguably 1934 (Caledon Bay crisis, 1932–34, leading to *Tuckiar v The King*, the first High Court appeal on behalf of an Indigenous Australian, Dhakiyarr Wirrpanda). [source: AFW]
- **Pemulwuy** (c.1750–1802), Bidjigal man of the Dharug nation, possibly a *carradhy* (cleverman): led a twelve-year guerrilla war from 1790 (raids around Parramatta, Toongabbie, Hawkesbury, 1792–1802), shot dead c. 2 June 1802 (possibly by Henry Hacking); head preserved in spirits and sent to Joseph Banks. Governor King: "a terrible pest to the colony, he was a brave and independent character." Skull never located; in 2010 Prince William undertook to help return it. [source: PEM]
- Hawkesbury: first frontier war from 1795 with Darug people; Appin massacre 1816 (14 killed in a dawn ambush by the 46th Regiment). [source: AFW]
- **Windradyne** (c.1800–1829), Wiradjuri: resistance around Bathurst; Governor Brisbane declared martial law on 14 August 1824; Windradyne went to Parramatta to meet Brisbane in December 1824. [source: WIN; BAT]
- Liverpool Plains 1832–38: 16 British and up to 500 Aboriginal people killed (Broome); Waterloo Creek and Myall Creek massacres 1838. [source: AFW]
- **Myall Creek**, 10 June 1838: at least 28 unarmed Wirraayaraay (Kamilaroi) people, mostly women, children and old men, killed by eleven/twelve stockmen (assigned and ex-convicts, one a Black African, John Johnstone) led by free settler John Henry Fleming, who was never tried. First trial: all acquitted after twenty minutes; second trial: seven convicted and hanged 18 December 1838 at Darlinghurst. Landowners funded the defence ("The Black Association"). [source: MYC]
- Port Phillip: Native Police Corps from 1837; Queensland Native Police from 1848; in Queensland killing was largely by civilian parties and the Native Police (Aboriginal men "recruited at gunpoint" and led by officers). Victoria's Aboriginal population fell from 60,000 to 15,000 between 1834 and 1851 (Broome); Yoorrook Justice Commission (2025) concluded British policies in Victoria constituted genocide. [source: AFW; NP; IA]
- Queensland "the single bloodiest colonial frontier": some studies give ~1,500 colonists and associates killed. Kalkadoon resistance held settlers out of western Queensland for ten years until Battle Mountain (September 1884). [source: AFW]
- Western Australia: Pinjarra massacre 28 October 1833 (Governor Stirling); Bunuba resistance under **Jandamarra** (c.1873–1 April 1897); Forrest River massacre 1926 (Royal Commission: at least eleven killed). [source: AFW; JAN]
- Central Australia 1860–1895: an estimated 650–850 of 4,500 Aboriginal people killed. NSW set up 116 reserves 1860–94; Victoria's Board for the Protection of Aborigines (1869); 1886 power to exclude "half caste" people. [source: H2]
- **Death toll estimates (all attributed):** Broome (2019): 1,700 settlers and 17,000–20,000 Aboriginal people, 1788–1928. Reynolds: earlier "up to 3,000 Europeans and at least 20,000" Aboriginal people; in 2022 "over 30,000" (infobox: about 2,500 colonists). University of Newcastle Colonial Frontier Massacres project (led by Lyndall Ryan; counts only massacres of six or more): preliminary 8,270 Aboriginal deaths (2020); at the 2022 "final" stage, more than 10,000 lives in at least 414 massacres, 1788–1930 (press reports: 11,000–14,700, against roughly 400 colonisers killed in massacres); about half the massacres involved police or soldiers; Stage 5 (20 Feb 2025) lists 438 sites. Evans and Ørsted-Jensen (2014): about 65,000–67,000 in Queensland alone and nationally "probably no less than 100,000", criticised by Richards and Finnane ("too little systematic data"). [source: H2; HR; AFW; https://www.sbs.com.au/nitv/article/the-massacre-maps-research-into-colonial-era-violence-has-revealed-its-final-grim-toll/e5enesien; https://c21ch.newcastle.edu.au/colonialmassacres/changes.php; https://www.newcastle.edu.au/hippocampus/story/2022/massacre-map]
- The frontier wars are not commemorated at the Australian War Memorial; in September 2022 its chair Brendan Nelson promised a "much broader, a much deeper" depiction after SBS's *The Australian Wars* (directed and narrated by Rachel Perkins). [source: AFW; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Australian_Wars]

## 8. Van Diemen's Land: the Black War and Truganini
- Settlement at Risdon Cove September 1803; Aboriginal population then 3,000–7,000 (Ryan). Risdon Cove massacre 3 May 1804 (witnesses: 3 to 50 killed). British population 2,000→24,000 and sheep to 680,000 between 1815 and 1830. [source: BW]
- Black War: mid-1820s to 1832, a guerrilla war; some 600–900 Aboriginal people and more than 200 colonists died (Clements 2014; Ryan 2012). Broome: "most successful Aboriginal resistance in Australia's history" (over 50 British killed 1828–30). Martial law November 1828 (extended to the whole island 1 October 1830). Arthur admitted the British were "the first aggressors". [source: BW; AFW]
- Black Line, October–November 1830: 2,200 men (about 550 soldiers, 738 convicts, 912 civilians); captured two, killed two; cost over £30,000; disbanded 26 November. [source: BW; TRU]
- Terms "Black War"/"Black Line" coined by Henry Melville (1835); Lyndall Ryan proposes "Tasmanian War". Nicholas Clements identifies settler demand for Aboriginal women (men outnumbered women six to one in 1822) as the key trigger. [source: BW]
- George Augustus Robinson's "friendly missions" (1830–34) used Aboriginal guides including Truganini, Woureddy, Kikatapula, Mannalargenna. ~220 survivors were sent to Wybalenna, Flinders Island; 46 remained when it closed in 1847. [source: BW; TRU]
- Wybalenna petition to Queen Victoria, 1846 — the first petition by Indigenous Australians to a reigning monarch — led by Walter George Arthur and Thomas Brune against superintendent Jeanneret (see Quotations). [source: WYB]
- **Truganini** (c.1812 – 8 May 1876), Nuenonne woman of Bruny Island: her mother was murdered by sailors (1816), sisters kidnapped by sealers (1826); guide on Robinson's missions; in Port Phillip in 1841–42 she joined Tunnerminnerwait and Maulboyheenner in armed raids; the two men were hanged 20 January 1842, the first legal executions in Port Phillip; she was acquitted. Died in Hobart; despite her wishes, her body was exhumed (1878), her skeleton displayed in the Tasmanian Museum (from 1904 until 1947); cremated and scattered in the D'Entrecasteaux Channel in 1976 after Tasmanian Aboriginal Centre campaigning. She was "widely, though incorrectly, described as the last surviving Aboriginal Tasmanian" — Fanny Cochrane Smith and the Cape Barren Island community survived; the "extinction myth" is rejected by scholars and the Palawa community. [source: TRU]

## 9. Squatters, pastoral expansion, wool
- Settlement in 1820 confined to ~100 km around Sydney and the Van Diemen's Land midlands (settler population 26,000 mainland, 6,000 VDL). From the 1820s sheep and cattle grazing expanded beyond the official bounds. [source: H1]
- "Limits of location" (Nineteen Counties) defined from 1826; from 1836 squatting legalised with grazing licences of £10 a year; 1847 Orders in Council gave leases of 1, 8 and 14 years. "Squattocracy" used derisively from 1841; by 1840 squatters among the colony's wealthiest men. [source: SQ]
- Elizabeth Macarthur helped establish the merino wool industry. VDL sheep rose from 170,000 to a million in the 1820s. Port Phillip by 1850: 75,000 Europeans, 2,000 Indigenous inhabitants, 5 million sheep. [source: H1]
- New colonies: Swan River 1829; South Australia 1836 (Wakefield's "systematic colonisation", privately financed); Port Phillip opened 1836, Melbourne 1837; Victoria separated 1 July 1851 (Australian Constitutions Act, assent 5 August 1850). NSW settler population 180,000 by 1850. [source: H1; ACA]
- Waste Land Act 1848 gave traditional owners limited rights to live, hunt and gather on pastoral leases; many Aboriginal men worked as shepherds and stockmen, keeping connection to country. [source: H2]

## 10. The settler revolution (Belich)
- James Belich (born Wellington 1956; NZ historian of the New Zealand Wars; Beit Professor of Imperial and Commonwealth History at Oxford 2011–2024), *Replenishing the Earth: The Settler Revolution and the Rise of the Anglo-World, 1783–1939* (Oxford University Press, 2009). [source: JB]
- Thesis (as summarised by reviewers): a "settler revolution" from about 1800 made settlement "explosive", producing cities like Chicago and Melbourne; three-phase cycle of explosive colonisation (boom), bust, and "export rescue" by staples (gold, wool, refrigerated meat), followed by "recolonisation", tightening ties to the metropole (used to explain pro-Empire sentiment in Australia, NZ and Canada). He treats the US West and the "British West" together as one "Anglo-world", and counts imports into booming settlements rather than only their exports. Critic Norman Etherington (ABR) found it "all too good to be true" and doubted the model's neglect of real export profits. [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/journals/victorian_studies/v053/53.1.cain.html; https://australianbookreview.com.au/abr-online/archive/2009/288-december-2009-january-2010-no-317/10982-norman-etherington-reviews-replenishing-the-earth-the-settler-revolution-and-the-rise-of-the-anglo-world-1783-1939-by-james-belich; https://www.progressive-economics.ca/?p=5486] (Search-summary level only; I could not open the reviews in full. Paraphrase; do not quote Belich directly.)
- Australian illustration of boom and bust: population 430,000 (1851) → 1.7 million (1871); Victoria 75,000→500,000 (1851–61); then the 1890s depression: falling wool prices and the Melbourne land-boom collapse; banks suspended; economy shrank 20% 1891–95; unemployment almost a third; "Federation Drought" 1895–1903. [source: AGR; VGR; H2]

## 11. Gold, Eureka and Chinese miners
- Hargraves and Lister found gold at Ophir near Orange, February 1851; NSW rush proclaimed 14 May 1851. Victoria: Clunes (July 1851), Ballarat (September), Castlemaine/Mount Alexander, Bendigo (November). Earlier finds (1823) suppressed. [source: AGR; VGR]
- 1852–60: 290,000 migrants to Victoria from the British Isles, 15,000 from Europe, 18,000 from the US. [source: AGR]
- Chinese: mostly men from Guangdong; ~40,000 over twenty years (WAP); "more than 19,000" on Victorian fields by end 1855 (VGR); "20,000... by 1855 and 13,000 on the New South Wales diggings" (H2); 11,493 arrivals in Melbourne in 1855 (CHA). Many formed cooperatives; some worked for European companies. Prominent merchants Lowe Kong Meng (Melbourne), Mei Quong Tart (Sydney). [source: WAP; VGR; H2; CHA]
- Anti-Chinese laws and riots: first anti-Chinese demonstration Bendigo July 1854; Victoria's entry/residence taxes from 1855; Buckland riot 1857; NSW poll taxes and tonnage limits 1861; restrictions relaxed by 1867; between 1875 and 1888 all colonies excluded further Chinese immigration; 1896 Intercolonial Conference extended restrictions to "all coloured races". [source: WAP; LF; H2]
- Lambing Flat (Burrangong), November 1860–September 1861: on 30 June 1861 2,000–3,000 miners attacked about 2,000 Chinese miners under a "Roll Up – No Chinese" banner; many beaten, no one killed; 280 troops and police sent; two ringleaders gaoled; compensation petitions probably unpaid. [source: LF]
- Eureka: monthly licence of 30 shillings from 1 September 1851; licence hunts; Bentley's Hotel burned October 1854; Ballarat Reform League (influenced by Chartism); on 29 November ~10,000 swore allegiance to the Southern Cross flag; battle at dawn 3 December 1854. Registrar recorded 27 deaths; Lalor listed 22 rebel dead; "majority of casualties being rebels". ~120 detained, 13 tried for high treason, all acquitted (including the African American John Joseph). No record of Chinese involvement. Afterwards licence replaced by £1 miner's right with the vote; Peter Lalor elected 1856. Clare Wright documents women's roles. [source: EUR; RG]

## 12. Self-government and democracy
- NSW Legislative Council 1825 (appointed); first elections 1843; partly elected councils for VDL, SA, Victoria in 1850. Constitutions for NSW, Victoria, VDL assented 1855, SA 1856; Queensland separated and self-governing 1859; Western Australia only in 1890. [source: H2; RG; WS]
- Secret ballot: Tasmania, Victoria and SA in 1856, NSW 1858, Queensland 1859, WA 1877. Credit long went to Victoria (William Nicholson); SB says Tasmania's Act may have been first (7 Feb 1856, "citation needed"). Called the "Australian ballot"; adopted by Massachusetts 1888 and most US states by 1891. [source: H2; SB]
- Male suffrage: SA 1856, Victoria 1857, NSW 1858, Queensland 1872, WA 1893, Tasmania 1900. Queensland excluded Aboriginal men from 1885; WA had a property qualification for Aboriginal, Asian and African men. 8-hour day won by Sydney and Melbourne building workers 1856. [source: H2]
- Women: SA passed the Constitutional Amendment (Adult Suffrage) Act in 1894, royal assent 1895 — vote and the right to stand (opponents added candidacy hoping to sink the bill). Aboriginal women in SA were included: at Point McLeay (Raukkan) more than 100 were on the roll and about 70% voted in 1896. Catherine Helen Spence, first female political candidate (1897). WA 1899/1900; Commonwealth Franchise Act 1902 (excluding most non-white people). NZ women voted in 1893. [source: WS; H2; https://officeforwomen.sa.gov.au/womens-policy/125th-anniversary-of-suffrage/aboriginal-women-and-the-vote; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raukkan,_South_Australia]
- The world's first Labor government was formed in Queensland in 1899 (it lasted a week); Labor parties emerged after the 1890 Maritime Strike; ~20% of male workers unionised by 1890. [source: H2]

## 13. Pacific Islander labour ("blackbirding")
- Queensland labour trade 1863–1908: 55,000–62,500 Islanders brought, mostly from the New Hebrides (Vanuatu), Solomon Islands, and around New Guinea; recruitment ranged from kidnapping at gunpoint to negotiation; mostly male, a quarter under sixteen. Began with Robert Towns' cotton at Townsvale (1863), then sugar. [source: BB; KAN]
- About 15,000 (30%) died while working in Queensland, excluding deaths in transit or recruitment; some plantations recorded up to 60% deaths over a term. Polynesian Labourers Act 1868 required licences and government agents, poorly enforced. [source: BB]
- Pacific Island Labourers Act 1901: no entry after 31 March 1904; deportation after 31 December 1906; about 9,800–10,000 Islanders in 1901, about 7,500 deported. Descendants (Australian South Sea Islanders) recognised as a distinct group in 1994. [source: PILA; WAP; KAN]

## 14. Federation and White Australia
- Parkes' 1890 conference; 1891 Sydney convention (Griffith/Inglis Clark draft: "The draft of 1891 is the Constitution of 1900" — La Nauze); referendums 1898–1900 (WA last, 31 July 1900, 69.47% yes); Commonwealth of Australia Constitution Act passed 5 July, assent 9 July 1900; proclaimed 1 January 1901 in Centennial Park, Sydney; Barton interim Prime Minister. New Zealand and Fiji stayed out. Queensland feared losing Islander labour. [source: FED]
- Constitution s.127 excluded "aboriginal natives" from population counts; s.51(xxvi) race power; both changed by the 1967 referendum (90.77% yes). [source: IA]
- Immigration Restriction Act 1901 (23 Dec 1901), drafted by Deakin: a 50-word dictation test "in a European language" chosen by the officer; Britain objected to explicit racial bars (Japanese alliance); Labor wanted explicit exclusion. Replaced by the Migration Act 1958; racial discrimination removed 1966 (Holt) and 1973 (Whitlam). [source: IRA; WAP]

## 15. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander scholars and voices to name
- Marcia Langton (born 1951), descendant of the Yiman and Bidjara peoples, anthropologist, Redmond Barry Distinguished Professor, University of Melbourne; Boyer Lectures 2012; appears in *The Australian Wars* (2022) and *The Dark Emu Story*; supported Pascoe's identity claim; criticised Windschuttle. [source: ML; HW; BP; DE]
- Larissa Behrendt (born 1969, Cooma), Eualeyai/Kamilaroi on her father's side, legal academic at UTS; *Finding Eliza: Power and Colonial Storytelling* (2016) reads the Eliza Fraser (Butchulla country, 1836) story and colonial texts for stereotypes. [source: LB; https://www.harryhartog.com.au/products/finding-eliza-power-and-colonial-storytelling-larissa-behrendt-9780702253904]
- Hannah McGlade (Noongar) called *Dark Emu* "misleading and offensive to Aboriginal people and culture"; Warren Mundine disputed Pascoe's identity. Aboriginal opinion is itself divided — say so. [source: DE; BP]
- Lyndall Ryan (1943 – 30 April 2024), non-Indigenous historian of Tasmania and leader of the massacre map (first released 2017, 150+ sites). [source: LR]

## Debates
- **Terra nullius:** whether the doctrine underlay the 1788 claim (Frost, Attwood) or was a later 19th-century legal rationalisation (Borch; Banner dates the concept's first legal use to 1819). *Mabo* rejected it as a basis of land law, not of British sovereignty. [source: TN]
- **The 1789 epidemic:** Macassan/overland (Campbell, Macknight) vs release from the First Fleet's variolation material (Butlin, Day, Warren; resonates with Maynard and Langton) vs chickenpox (Ford, Carmody). Keneally and Blainey reject deliberate release. [source: SP]
- **Frontier death toll:** Broome 17,000–20,000; Reynolds 20,000 then 30,000+; Newcastle massacre map >10,000 in massacres of six or more; Evans and Ørsted-Jensen ~100,000, criticised by Richards and Finnane. [source: H2; AFW]
- **History wars:** Blainey's "black armband" (1993 Latham lecture) and Howard (1996) vs Reynolds, Ryan, Manne. Windschuttle's *The Fabrication of Aboriginal History, Vol. 1: Van Diemen's Land 1803–1847* (2002) counted only 118 Aboriginal people killed by the British with "credible evidence", blamed disease and accused Ryan and Reynolds of fabrication; Robert Manne's anthology *Whitewash: On Keith Windschuttle's Fabrication of Aboriginal History* called his arguments "unpersuasive and unsupported"; Langton, Muecke and Goodall also criticised him. Windschuttle died 8 April 2025. [source: HW; KW]
- **Genocide:** Tasmania cited as a "textbook example" (Lemkin, Kuper, Diamond, Kiernan) vs Windschuttle and Minogue; Reynolds and Levene more circumspect ("considerably more circumspect"); Barta argues intent matters little to victims. Yoorrook (2025) found genocide in Victoria. [source: HW; AFW; BW]
- **Dark Emu:** Pascoe (2014/2018) argues pre-colonial Aboriginal society involved agriculture, aquaculture (Budj Bim/Lake Condah eel traps) and villages, drawing on explorers (Sturt, Mitchell) and Gerritsen and Gammage; 250,000 copies by mid-2021. Critics: Sutton (anthropologist) and Walshe (archaeologist), *Farmers or Hunter-Gatherers?* (2021) — selective quotation of colonial sources, and that the book devalues hunter-gatherer economy and its spiritual dimension by privileging agriculture; Ian Keen ("Aboriginal people were indeed hunters, gatherers and fishers", though "the boundary between foraging and farming is a fuzzy one"); Hiscock, Lourandos and McNiven dispute that scholars suppressed this evidence. Russell and Griffiths reject the hierarchy implied by "progress" to farming. Pascoe's Aboriginal identity is disputed (Mundine; Boonwurrung Land and Sea Council) and supported (Langton, Ken Wyatt, Uncle Max Harrison). Recommend presenting as a debate over categories and evidence, not over whether Aboriginal people shaped their land (all agree they did). [source: DE; BP; PS; https://theconversation.com/how-the-dark-emu-debate-limits-representation-of-aboriginal-people-in-australia-163006; https://insidestory.org.au/?p=67242]
- **Eureka as "birth of democracy":** some identify it with the birth of Australian democracy; others note reforms were already under way and the League made no demand for responsible government. [source: EUR; RG]
- **Belich's settler revolution** vs export-led staple accounts (Etherington). [source: section 10]

## Conflicts with published chapters
- *British Empire* says "11,000–14,000 in documented massacres". Sources here give the Newcastle project's figure as a preliminary 8,270 (2020), then "more than 10,000" in at least 414 massacres (2022 university release), with press reports of 11,000–14,700. Not a contradiction, but write "more than 10,000 (estimates up to about 14,000)" and attribute.
- *British Empire* states 1788 to "at least 1928"; AFW also notes conflicts "as late as 1934" (Caledon Bay). Compatible; mention both.
- *British Empire* research notes say transportation to NSW lasted "until 1840"; CA says it was "temporarily ended" by Order-in-Council in 1840 (some convicts later went to Moreton Bay and Port Phillip "exiles" arrived 1844–49). Say "suspended in 1840".
- *British Empire* and *Human Origins* say "at least 50,000 years"; *Pacific Voyagers* says Sahul was settled "most likely between about 60,000 and 40,000 years ago"; IA gives 50,000–65,000. Use "at least 50,000 years, possibly 65,000", which contradicts neither.
- No contradictions found on Myall Creek, the Black War, 1819, Mabo or Cook's voyage dates.
- Internal source discrepancies: convicts 162,000 vs 164,000; pre-contact population lower bound 300,000 vs 318,000; *The Other Side of the Frontier* 1981 (JCU) vs 1982 (Penguin, cited by AFW); Chinese miners in Victoria 1855: 19,000 vs 20,000.
