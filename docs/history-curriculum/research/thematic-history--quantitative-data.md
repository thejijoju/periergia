## 1. Counting before writing: tokens, tablets and the origin of writing in accounts
- A token-based accounting system came into use in parts of the ancient Near East possibly as early as the middle to late 9th millennium BCE; not every excavated token was an accounting device (many come from non-administrative contexts such as children's graves). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-cuneiform]
- Tokens were later enclosed in hollow clay envelopes (bullae) whose outer surfaces were impressed with the tokens, often with seal impressions; the oldest bullae date to the middle of the 4th millennium BCE. Purely "numerical tablets" (numbers only, no word-signs) date from Uruk V, c. 3500–3350 BCE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-cuneiform]
- Denise Schmandt-Besserat (drawing on M. Lambert and P. Amiet) linked tokens, bullae and numerical tablets in a line of descent to proto-cuneiform; her account "has been debated and criticized", but the role of these devices as precursors is accepted. Her main work: *Before Writing* (2 vols, University of Texas Press, 1992). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-cuneiform]
- Proto-cuneiform emerged c. 3350–3200 BCE in the Late Uruk period (3200 BCE used to be the conventional date; Englund proposed c. 3350 BCE). It developed into the standard cuneiform of the Early Dynastic period from c. 3000–2900 BCE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-cuneiform]
- About 5,000 proto-cuneiform tablets come from archaic Uruk (190 Uruk V, 1,776 Uruk IV, 3,094 Uruk III). The texts are "essentially administrative": records of goods moving in and out of institutions, plus lexical lists used to train scribes. Wikipedia states that "it was proven that proto-cuneiform originates in bookkeeping and administrative precursors". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-cuneiform]
- Proto-cuneiform used different number systems depending on what was counted (discrete objects, areas, volumes, time). Its underlying base was sexagesimal (base 60), like later cuneiform. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-cuneiform]
- Worked example (the "Kushim" tablet, MSVO 3, 29, from Uruk): translated as "5617 1/5 N1's, exchange barley, 37 months, Kushim's final account. ca. 135,000 liters". Whether "Kushim" is a person's name, a title or an office is uncertain. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-cuneiform]
- Babylonian mathematics used base 60, which is why we still have 60 seconds, 60 minutes and 360 degrees. The tablet YBC 7289 gives the square root of 2 as 1;24,51,10 in base 60, accurate to about six decimal places. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_mathematics]
- Babylonians kept data in tables on clay tablets as early as 1800 BCE; the term "spread sheet" for a ruled accounting grid has been in use since at least 1906. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spreadsheet]

## 2. Early states count people and property
- **Egypt**: the earliest Egyptian census was the "cattle count", which counted livestock for tax purposes and was taken every two years in the early Old Kingdom. Censuses of people appear in the late Middle Kingdom and develop in the New Kingdom. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census]
- **Hebrew Bible**: the Book of Numbers takes its name from counts of the Israelites. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census]
- **Rome**: the English word census comes from Latin *censere* ("to assess/estimate"). In the Republic the census listed adult males fit for military service and fixed each citizen's class for military and tax purposes; from the middle Republic it was usually taken every five years (a *lustrum*). Tradition (Livy) credits King Servius Tullius (c. 575–535 BCE); the office of censor existed from 443 BCE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_censor]
- In 6 CE the legate Quirinius held a census of Judaea for tax purposes, which helped provoke the Zealot movement. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census]
- **Han China**: a census of 2 CE registered 57,671,400 people in 12,366,470 households. Scholars consider it fairly accurate, but (citing Nishijima) Wikipedia says only taxable households were counted, so it measured tax and mobilisable manpower. Another census was taken in 144 CE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty]
- **Mauryan India**: the oldest recorded census in India is thought to date from about 330 BCE under Chandragupta Maurya (source: Census of India historical page; treat as traditional). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census]
- **Early Islamic caliphate**: regular censuses began soon after the caliphate was founded, starting with one ordered by the second caliph, Umar (Wadad al-Qadi, *Der Islam*, 2008, on Umayyad censuses and land surveys, 661–750). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census]
- **Chinese counting rods**: rod calculus, a positional *decimal* method of computing with counting rods, was used in China from the Warring States period until the Ming dynasty, when the abacus largely replaced it. Pupils, merchants, officials and mathematicians all learned the 45-phrase "nine-nine" multiplication table by heart. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_calculus]

## 3. The Andes: khipu
- Quipu/khipu (Quechua for "knot") are knotted cords of cotton or camelid fibre that recorded information through colour, order and number. Most of the information decoded so far is numbers in a **decimal positional** system: clusters of simple knots for tens and higher, long knots for 2–9 in the units place, a figure-eight knot for 1, and no knot for zero. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quipu]
- Leslie Leland Locke is usually credited with first demonstrating the decimal reading, in *American Anthropologist*, 1912. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quipu]
- Unambiguous khipus first appear in the 1st millennium CE, probably under the Wari Empire. Ruth Shady has argued that a possible proto-khipu from Caral is about 5,000 years old. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quipu]
- The Inca recorded census figures and other numbers on khipus. Inca administration was organised decimally: taxpaying heads of household were grouped in units headed by officials such as the *pachaka kuraka* (100) and the *hunu kuraka* (10,000). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire]
- The Inca population at its peak is uncertain: estimates range from 4 to 37 million, most between 6 and 14 million. The Inca kept "excellent census records" on khipus, but the ability to read them was lost. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire]
- In 1583 the Third Council of Lima ordered some khipus burned as idolatrous, because they recorded offerings to non-Christian gods. Other Spaniards adapted khipus for colonial administration, and some priests used them for church purposes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quipu]
- The number of surviving khipus is uncertain: a recent survey counts just under 1,400 known pre-Columbian khipus, while Gary Urton estimated "about 600" in 2003. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quipu]
- Manuel Medrano and Gary Urton, "Toward the Decipherment of a Set of Mid-Colonial Khipus from the Santa Valley, Coastal Peru", *Ethnohistory* 65:1 (2018), matched khipus with a Spanish colonial document. A potential match had already been identified in 2011. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quipu]
- Whether some khipus encoded language is unresolved. Sabine Hyland (2017) claims a partial phonetic decipherment of letter-khipus from San Juan de Collata. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quipu]

## 4. Medieval and early-modern surveys: Domesday, the defter and Akbar's zabt
- **Domesday Book**: the Great Survey of much of England and parts of Wales, traditionally dated 1086 and ordered by William the Conqueror. The *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* says planning took place in 1085 and the colophon says the survey was completed in 1086. Carol Symes has argued that the book itself may have been finished as late as 1098–1114. It names 13,418 places. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domesday_Book]
- The name "Domesday" alludes to the Last Judgement, "doom" being Old English for law or judgement. Richard FitzNeal (treasurer under Henry II) wrote that, as with the Last Judgement, from its record "there is no further appeal". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domesday_Book]
- H. C. Darby noted the data's inconsistencies and the "countless mistakes" caused by using Roman numerals. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domesday_Book]
- In 1183 the crusader Kingdom of Jerusalem took a census to estimate the men and money available against Saladin. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census]
- **Ottoman defter**: a *defter* was a tax register and land cadastre. *Tahrir defterleri* typically recorded villages, dwellings, household heads (adult males and widows), religion or ethnicity (because these affected tax) and land use. Officials in charge were *defterdars*. The word comes via Arabic *daftar* from Greek *diphthera* ("prepared skin"). A surviving example dates from 1455. The term *daftar* was also used in northern India (e.g. the Peshwa's daftar at Pune). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defter]
- **Mughal zabt**: from the late 16th century this was the main Mughal land-revenue system. Revenue was fixed **in cash** on the basis of measured land and regional per-bigha rates, not as an annual share of the crop. It was formalised in the *dahsala* (ten-year) settlement of **1580** by Akbar's revenue minister **Raja Todar Mal** and described in Abu'l Fazl's *Ain-i-Akbari*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zabt]
- Under the dahsala, each *pargana*'s revenue records for the previous ten harvests were averaged to fix a per-bigha cash rate (*dastur-ul-amal*) for each crop. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zabt]
- Akbar fixed the *ilahi gaz* (about 33 in / 84 cm) as the standard unit of length. A standard *bigha* was 3,600 square ilahi gaz (about 0.55 acre / 0.22 ha), measured with the *jarib*, a graduated rope. Sher Shah Suri (r. 1540–45) had already introduced measurement-based revenue across his empire. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zabt]
- Historiography: Irfan Habib, *The Agrarian System of Mughal India* (1963). Shireen Moosvi, *The Economy of the Mughal Empire c. 1595: A Statistical Study* (1987), reconstructed the *Ain-i-Akbari* revenue figures, including GDP estimates for 1595. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zabt]
- **Spanish empire**: on 25 May 1577 Philip II ordered a general description of the Indies. A 50-item questionnaire was sent to local officials in New Spain and Peru (the *Relaciones geográficas*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census]

## 5. Numerals and teachable arithmetic
- The Hindu–Arabic (Indian) place-value system was invented by Indian mathematicians between the 1st and 4th centuries CE. Arab mathematicians had adopted it by the 9th century and extended it to fractions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Arabic_numeral_system]
- The Bakhshali manuscript uses place value; its earliest leaves are radiocarbon-dated to 224–383 CE. The first dated, undisputed inscription with a zero symbol is at the Chaturbhuj temple, Gwalior, dated 876 CE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Arabic_numeral_system]
- Brahmagupta composed the *Brahmasphutasiddhanta* in 628 CE and is credited with formalising zero as a number, with rules for computing with it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmagupta]
- Al-Khwarizmi (c. 780–c. 850) was appointed at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad around 820. His *Al-Jabr* (compiled between 813 and 833) gave us the word "algebra". His book on Indian arithmetic, *On the Calculation with Hindu Numerals* (c. 825), was translated into Latin in the 12th century (*Algorithmo de Numero Indorum*) and introduced decimal positional numbers to the West. The Latin form of his name, *Algoritmi*, gave us "algorithm". Al-Kindi also wrote on Hindu numerals. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khwarizmi ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Arabic_numeral_system]
- In the 10th century Abu'l-Hasan al-Uqlidisi was the first to describe positional decimal fractions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Arabic_numeral_system]
- In Christian Europe the numerals 1–9 (without zero) first appear in the *Codex Vigilanus*, written in 976 by monks of San Martín de Albelda in Spain. Gerbert of Aurillac, later Pope Sylvester II (from 999), promoted an abacus using them. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Arabic_numeral_system]
- Fibonacci's *Liber Abaci* (1202; revised 1228) introduced the *modus Indorum* ("method of the Indians") to the Latin world. He learned it as a boy at the Pisan customs house in Bugia (Béjaïa, Algeria). The numerals came into common use in Europe from the 15th century, and their familiar Western shapes were fixed by printing in the late 15th to early 16th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liber_Abaci ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu%E2%80%93Arabic_numeral_system]
- **Long division**: al-Samaw'al al-Maghribi (1125–1174) made decimal calculations that amount to long division without formalising the algorithm. The earliest printed long division is in Calandri (1491; the "danda" method). The modern algorithm was introduced by Henry Briggs around 1600. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_division]

## 6. Double-entry bookkeeping and merchant capitalism
- One of the earliest known double-entry records is a ledger kept in 1211 by Florentine money-changers at a fair in Bologna. The ledger of the Farolfi firm, kept by Amatino Manucci in 1299–1300, shows full double entry. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-entry_bookkeeping]
- Benedetto Cotrugli, a merchant from Ragusa (Dubrovnik), described double entry in a treatise written in 1458 but not printed until 1573. Luca Pacioli (c. 1447–1517), a Franciscan friar and collaborator of Leonardo da Vinci, codified it in print in *Summa de arithmetica* (Venice, 1494). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-entry_bookkeeping ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luca_Pacioli]
- Double entry spread through the international fairs (Champagne, Lyon, Antwerp). In England it was used by the East India Company and the Bank of England. In the Iberian slave trade, double-entry account books were part of the valuation and depersonalisation of enslaved people, who were recorded as property. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-entry_bookkeeping]
- Mary Poovey: in early modern Europe double entry recalled "both the scales of justice and the symmetry of God's world". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-entry_bookkeeping]
- See section 18 for overlaps with the published Islamic commercial revolution chapter.

## 7. Probability
- Around 1654 the Chevalier de Méré (Antoine Gombaud) put the "problem of points" (how to divide the stakes of an unfinished game) to Blaise Pascal. Pascal's correspondence with Pierre de Fermat on it founded the mathematical theory of probability, and Pascal's solution is one of the earliest uses of expected value. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_of_points ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaise_Pascal]
- Christiaan Huygens gave the earliest known scientific treatment of probability in 1657. Jakob Bernoulli's *Ars Conjectandi* was published posthumously in 1713, and Abraham de Moivre's *The Doctrine of Chances* appeared in 1718. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_statistics]
- In 1802 Laplace estimated the population of France at 28,328,612, using the previous year's births and census data for three communities (an early ratio, or sampling, estimate). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_statistics]

## 8. Political arithmetic and *Statistik*
- John Graunt (24 April 1620 – 18 April 1674) was a London haberdasher. His *Natural and Political Observations Made upon the Bills of Mortality* is dated 1662 Old Style (1663 New Style). He is regarded as the founder of demography and perhaps the first epidemiologist, and he produced the first life table, even though the Bills did not record age at death. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Graunt]
- The London Bills of Mortality were, according to Graunt, begun in 1592 and published regularly from 1603. Deaths were reported by "Searchers" who inspected corpses and judged the cause of death. Graunt questioned their reliability, for example on plague deaths and an apparent surge in rickets in 1634. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Graunt]
- Graunt was elected to the Royal Society in 1662 with the endorsement of Charles II, which was notable because he was a tradesman. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Graunt]
- William Petty (26 May 1623 – 16 December 1687) served as physician-general to Cromwell's army in Ireland. From 1654 to 1656 he carried out the **Down Survey**, which mapped Irish land so that confiscated land could pay Cromwell's soldiers and creditors. He received about 30,000 acres at Kenmare and £9,000. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Petty]
- Petty's *Verbum Sapienti* (written 1665, printed 1691) contains the first rigorous estimates of England's national income and wealth (population 6 million; average income £6 13s 4d a year). *Political Arithmetick* was written in the 1670s and published posthumously in 1690. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Petty ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_domestic_product]
- Charles Davenant developed Petty's national-income method further in 1695. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_domestic_product]
- Gottfried Achenwall (1719–1772) of Göttingen introduced the German term *Statistik* in 1749 (*Abriß der neuen Staatswissenschaft…*). It meant the descriptive "science of state", which the English called political arithmetic. German writers called him the "father of statistics", but English writers pressed Petty's prior claim. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Achenwall ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_statistics]
- Sir John Sinclair introduced the word "statistics" into English in his *Statistical Account of Scotland* (1791–1799), compiled from returns by parish ministers. He meant to measure "the quantum of happiness" in the nation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_Accounts_of_Scotland ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_statistics]

## 9. The nineteenth-century "avalanche of numbers": Quetelet, Nightingale, graphics
- Ian Hacking named the period 1820–1840 "the avalanche of printed numbers", when states began collecting and publishing statistics. The phrase comes from his essay "Biopower and the Avalanche of Printed Numbers" (*Humanities in Society* 5, 1982) and is developed in *The Taming of Chance* (1990). [source: https://moodle.brynmawr.edu/pluginfile.php/24320/mod_resource/content/1/Hacking.%20Biopower%20and%20the%20Avalanche%20of%20Printed%20Numbers.pdf ; https://whitneyannetrettien.com/whiki/index.php/Hacking_1990]
- The Royal Statistical Society was founded in 1834. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_statistics]
- Adolphe Quetelet (22 February 1796, Ghent – 17 February 1874, Brussels) founded the Brussels Observatory (secured in 1828). His *Sur l'homme et le développement de ses facultés, ou Essai de physique sociale* (1835) set out "social physics" and the "average man" (*l'homme moyen*), defined by the mean values of measured traits that follow a normal distribution. He treated the average as an ideal and deviations from it as falling short. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolphe_Quetelet]
- Quetelet devised the Quetelet index, now the body mass index (BMI). He chaired the first International Statistical Congress in 1853. His work influenced Galton, and Wikipedia says it "played a key role in the origins of eugenics". Comte coined "sociologie" partly because Quetelet had taken over the term "social physics". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolphe_Quetelet]
- Worked example: in 1846 (*Lettres*) Quetelet fitted the chest measurements of 5,738 Scottish militiamen, published in a Scottish medical journal, to the normal curve. The average was just under 40 inches. A separate dataset of French conscripts' heights fitted less well. [source: https://rdrr.io/cran/HistData/man/ChestSizes.html ; WebSearch summary]
- William Playfair's *Statistical Breviary* (1801) contains the earliest known pie charts, one showing the shares of the Ottoman ("Turkish") Empire in Asia, Europe and Africa. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pie_chart]
- Florence Nightingale (12 May 1820 – 13 August 1910) used polar-area ("rose", "coxcomb") diagrams, e.g. "Diagram of the causes of mortality in the army in the East" (1858), to show MPs and officials that most soldiers died of preventable disease. She did **not** invent the form: André-Michel Guerry used it in 1829 and Léon Lalanne by 1830. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale]
- Nightingale became the first female member of the Royal Statistical Society in 1859 and an honorary member of the American Statistical Association in 1874. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale]
- During her first winter at Scutari, 4,077 British soldiers died there, ten times more from disease than from wounds. The Sanitary Commission arrived in March 1855, almost six months after she did. The often-repeated fall in the death rate "from 42% to 2%" comes from Stephen Paget in the *Dictionary of National Biography*; credit is shared with the Sanitary Commission. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale]
- Nightingale pressed for the Royal Commission on the sanitary state of the army in India (1858–1863). In 1873 she reported that soldiers' mortality in India had fallen from 69 to 18 per 1,000 after ten years of sanitary reform. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale]

## 10. Censuses in the modern state: the US, Hollerith and IBM
- The first US census counted the population as of 2 August 1790, as required by Article 1, Section 2 of the Constitution, under the supervision of Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson. US marshals took it. The official total is 3,929,214 (the state-by-state table on Wikipedia sums to 3,929,326). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1790_United_States_census]
- The 1790 schedule had five questions: free white males 16 and over, free white males under 16, free white females, all other free persons, and slaves. It counted about 698,000 enslaved people (table total 697,697), or 17.8 per cent, the highest proportion in any US census. Washington and Jefferson both thought the count was too low. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1790_United_States_census]
- The 1880 census took eight years to process. Herman Hollerith (29 February 1860, Buffalo – 17 November 1929), working with John Shaw Billings, recorded data as the presence or absence of holes in cards. He was partly inspired by railway conductors punching tickets. He filed his patent "Art of Compiling Statistics" on 23 September 1884 (granted 8 January 1889). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Hollerith ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabulating_machine]
- Hollerith's electric tabulators were used for the 1890 census, which punched some 100 million cards. Wikipedia says the larger population, more questions, more staff and the machines together cut processing from eight years (1880) to six (1890). The Census Bureau says the count "finished months ahead of schedule and far under budget" and that data were published 18 months sooner than after 1880. A rough count was issued in Bulletin No. 12 on 30 October 1890. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Hollerith ; https://www.census.gov/about/history/stories/monthly/2016/january-2016.html ; https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/decennial/1890/bulletins/demographics/16-population-of-the-us-by-states-and-territories-1890.pdf]
- 1890 population: 62,979,766 according to Wikipedia and Census Bureau history pages, while the 1890 Census Office bulletin gives an official count of 62,622,250. The census declared the frontier "closed". Most of the 1890 schedules were destroyed or damaged in a fire at the Commerce Department building on 10 January 1921. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1890_United_States_census ; https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/decennial/1890/bulletins/demographics/16-population-of-the-us-by-states-and-territories-1890.pdf]
- Hollerith's machines were also used for censuses in England and Wales, Italy, Germany, Russia, Austria, Canada, France, Norway and Puerto Rico. He founded the Tabulating Machine Company in 1896. In 1911 it was combined with three other firms as the Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company (CTR), which Thomas J. Watson renamed International Business Machines (IBM) in 1924. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Hollerith]
- Dehomag, IBM's German licensee, was founded in 1910 and acquired by CTR in 1923. In April 1933 the Nazi government announced a national census designed partly to identify Jews, Roma and others. Edwin Black's *IBM and the Holocaust* (2001) argues that IBM punch-card technology was central to Nazi censuses, camp administration and the Holocaust. Reviewers such as Richard Bernstein (*NYT*) accepted the documentation but said Black "does not demonstrate that IBM bears some unique or decisive responsibility". IBM criticised his methods. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_and_the_Holocaust]

## 11. Colonial counting: British India and caste
- Censuses in British India were taken from 1865 (North-Western Provinces) to 1941. The 1872 "all-India census" was actually carried out piecemeal between 1867 and 1872 and left out the princely states. The first synchronous decennial census was in 1881. A plan for decennial counts from 1861 was disrupted by the 1857 Rebellion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census_in_British_India]
- Enumerated population (millions): 1871 255; 1881 254; 1891 287; 1901 294; 1911 315; 1921 306; 1931 353; 1941 389 (coverage changed between censuses). The 1931 census counted nearly 20 per cent of the world's population. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census_in_British_India]
- The census had no formal definition of caste, race or tribe. In 1871 and 1881 officials tried to sort people by the textual *varna* scheme, which did not match social reality. The 1901 census (under H. H. Risley, whose anthropometric "race" theories are now discredited) recorded 1,646 castes, and 1931 recorded 4,147. Because people believed the census fixed social rank, many petitioned to be recorded under higher-status names (J. H. Hutton, 1931). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census_in_British_India]
- The census faced resistance: rumours that it would bring new taxes, conscription, forced labour or conversion; objections to numbering houses (Bhil, Burmese); and the 1891 Commissioner's complaint about "unwilling and obtuse" respondents. The sociologist Michael Mann called it "more telling of the administrative needs of the British than of the social reality". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census_in_British_India]
- Bernard S. Cohn, "The Census, Social Structure and Objectification in South Asia", written c. 1970 and published 1987 in *An Anthropologist among the Historians and Other Essays* (OUP). Nicholas Dirks, *Castes of Mind: Colonialism and the Making of Modern India* (2001), develops Cohn's argument that caste took its rigid modern form under British rule, a "colonial ethnographic state". [source: https://indianmedicine.eldoc.ub.rug.nl/70496 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castes_of_Mind]
- After 1947 India's census was run by temporary structures for each round until 1961. Nigeria's Women's War (1929) began over a census dispute (already in a published chapter; see section 18). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census_in_British_India]

## 12. Galton, Pearson, Fisher: statistics and eugenics
- Francis Galton (16 February 1822 – 17 January 1911) was Charles Darwin's half-cousin. *Hereditary Genius* (1869) counted eminent relatives of eminent men. He coined "eugenics" in 1883 in *Inquiries into Human Faculty and Its Development* and popularised the phrase "nature versus nurture". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Galton]
- Galton presented "regression" in his presidential address to Section H of the British Association in September 1885 (published as "Regression towards mediocrity in hereditary stature", 1886), drawing on sweet-pea experiments and his quincunx ("Galton board"). He independently rediscovered correlation in 1888 (Bravais had anticipated it in 1846), and used *r* for the coefficient. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Galton]
- Galton's Anthropometric Laboratory at the 1884 International Health Exhibition in London measured visitors for a small fee and supplied data for his regression work. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Galton]
- In 1907 Galton studied 787 guesses of an ox's weight at a country fair (*Nature*, "One Vote, One Value") and found the median close to the true weight. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_statistics]
- Karl Pearson (27 March 1857 – 27 April 1936) founded the world's first university statistics department at UCL in 1911 and was the first Galton Professor of Eugenics. With W. F. R. Weldon (and Galton) he founded *Biometrika* in 1901. His chi-squared test and correlation coefficient are still used. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Pearson ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Galton]
- Pearson saw war against "inferior races" as a consequence of evolutionary theory. In 1925, in the first issue of his *Annals of Eugenics*, he opposed Jewish immigration to Britain, calling the immigrants "somewhat inferior physically and mentally to the native population". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Pearson]
- Ronald Fisher (17 February 1890 – 29 July 1962) worked at Rothamsted Experimental Station from 1919, analysing crop data collected since 1842, and developed analysis of variance. *Statistical Methods for Research Workers* (1925) introduced the 5 per cent significance convention, and *The Design of Experiments* followed in 1935. In 1933 he succeeded Pearson as Galton Professor of Eugenics. He publicly disputed that smoking causes lung cancer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Fisher ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_statistics]
- In June 2020 UCL renamed the Galton lecture theatre and buildings named after Pearson. Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, and UCL have also removed commemorations of Fisher. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Pearson ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Galton ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Fisher]
- Eugenic sterilisation spread worldwide. Indiana passed the first US sterilisation law in 1907. In *Buck v. Bell* (2 May 1927; 8–1) Justice Holmes wrote "Three generations of imbeciles are enough"; the decision has never been expressly overturned. About 70,000 people were sterilised in the US, roughly a third of them in California (teaching source; estimates vary). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compulsory_sterilization ; https://teachingamericanhistory.org/library/document/buck-v-bell/]
- Further cases: over 400,000 people were sterilised under Nazi Germany's law, which was modelled partly on US laws. Sweden's programme (1934–1976) sterilised about 21,000 people by force and coerced about 6,000 more. Japan's Eugenic Protection Law of 1948 was ruled unconstitutional by its Supreme Court in July 2024. In 1970–76 the US Indian Health Service sterilised an estimated 25–42 per cent of the Native American women of reproductive age who came to it for care. In India, Emergency-era drives were led by Sanjay Gandhi (1976). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compulsory_sterilization]
- International Eugenics Congresses were held in London (1912) and New York (1921, 1932). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics]

## 13. Standardised testing
- The Chinese imperial examination began in earnest under the Sui (581–618), was systematised under the Tang and was abolished in **1905**. Reports by European missionaries and diplomats introduced it to the West and influenced civil-service examinations in France, Germany and the British East India Company. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_examination]
- Alfred Binet (8 July 1857 – 18 October 1911) served from 1904 on a French Ministry of Education commission on children with learning difficulties. With Théodore Simon he published a preliminary intelligence test in 1905 and the full age-normed Binet–Simon scale in 1908. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Binet]
- William Stern devised the "intelligence quotient". Lewis Terman of Stanford published the Stanford–Binet in 1916 and promoted eugenic ideas such as discouraging people with low IQ from having children. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford%E2%80%93Binet_Intelligence_Scales]
- The Army Alpha (with the Army Beta for men who were illiterate or did not speak English) was developed in 1917 by Robert Yerkes, Terman, Goddard and others. By 11 November 1918 it had been given to 1,726,966 men. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_Alpha]
- Carl Brigham (1890–1943) used the Army data in *A Study of American Intelligence* (1923) to claim "Nordic" superiority, which fed anti-immigration politics. He created the SAT for the College Board in 1926. In 1930 ("Intelligence Tests of Immigrant Groups") he recanted, calling his earlier conclusions "without foundation". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Brigham]
- The first SAT (Scholastic Aptitude Test) was taken on 23 June 1926 by 8,040 students. In 1934 James Conant and Henry Chauncey used it to award Harvard scholarships, and by 1942 the College Board had replaced its essay exams with it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAT]
- Stephen Jay Gould's *The Mismeasure of Man* (1981; 2nd edn 1996) critiqued attempts to rank intelligence as a single quantity (craniometry, IQ). Its scientific reception was "highly polarized". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mismeasure_of_Man]

## 14. GDP and national accounts
- Simon Kuznets (30 April 1901, Pinsk – 8 July 1985) took charge of the NBER's US national income work in 1931–34. He produced the first official US estimate, *National Income, 1929–1932* (Senate Document 124, 73rd Congress, 1934), commissioned to help understand the Great Depression. He won the 1971 Nobel prize in economics. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Kuznets ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_domestic_product]
- After the Bretton Woods Conference (1944) GDP/GNP became the main measure of national economies. The UN *System of National Accounts* has editions of 1953, 1968, 1993 and 2008. The US switched from GNP to GDP as its main measure in 1991. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_domestic_product]
- Critiques: GDP leaves out unpaid household work (Martha Nussbaum argues for counting it; in Australia it has been estimated at over 50 per cent of GDP), environmental depletion and income distribution. Alternatives include the Index of Sustainable Economic Welfare (Cobb and Daly, 1989), the Human Development Index, Bhutan's Gross National Happiness, the 2009 Stiglitz–Sen–Fitoussi commission and the UN's 2026 "Beyond GDP" conference. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_domestic_product]
- **Angus Maddison** (6 December 1926 – 24 April 2010) of the University of Groningen reconstructed GDP back to year 1 CE (*The World Economy: Historical Statistics*, 2004; *Contours of the World Economy, 1–2030 AD*, 2007; *Chinese Economic Performance in the Long Run*, 2nd edn 2007). His estimates show India and China as the largest economies before the 18th century. The Maddison Project (from March 2010) continues his work. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angus_Maddison ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maddison_Project]
- Critics and revisers: Maddison gave conjectural estimates for only a few benchmark years before 1800. The Bolt–van Zanden data typology ranks the weakest estimates as "guesstimates". Broadberry and co-authors have revised the series for China, India and Britain, and Peter Solar argues that revisions can lower Ming China's GDP per head by up to a third. [source: https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/64857/1/WP229.pdf ; https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/B2C63E9AF1C70B02A1DDA962D94374B5/S0022050721000309a.pdf/chinas-gdp-some-corrections-and-the-way-forward.pdf]
- **Poor Numbers**: Morten Jerven, *Poor Numbers: How We Are Misled by African Development Statistics and What to Do about It* (Cornell University Press, 2013). Worked example: on 5 November 2010 Ghana Statistical Service raised its GDP estimate by over 60 per cent (about US$13 billion of uncounted activity) when it moved the base year from 1993 to 2006. This lifted Ghana to lower-middle-income status overnight. In April 2014 Nigeria's rebasing raised its GDP by 89 per cent to $510 billion. Jerven argues that rankings of African economies by GDP are misleading. South Africa's Statistician-General Pali Lehohla disputed his claims. [source: https://africanarguments.org/2013/09/26/poor-numbers-the-politics-of-improving-gdp-statistics-in-africa-by-morten-jerven/ ; https://africanarguments.org/2012/11/poor-numbers-how-we-are-misled-by-african-development-statistics-and-what-to-do-about-it-by-morten-jerven/]
- Statistics in the early People's Republic of China: Arunabh Ghosh, *Making It Count* (2020), describes a post-1949 "crisis in counting". Soviet-style full enumeration became hard to sustain by the mid-1950s, Indian statisticians shaped Chinese interest in random sampling, and the Great Leap Forward pushed statistics toward ethnographic methods. [source: https://fairbank.fas.harvard.edu/research/publications/making-it-count-statistics-and-statecraft-in-the-early-peoples-republic-of-china]
- Quantitative history: Robert Fogel (1926–2013), a pioneer of cliometrics, shared the 1993 Nobel prize with Douglass North. His *Time on the Cross* (with Stanley Engerman, 1974) set off fierce debate over measuring slavery. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Fogel]

## 15. Machines: Jacquard, Babbage, Lovelace, ENIAC, the spreadsheet
- The Jacquard loom (introduced 1804) was controlled by punched cards. A woven silk portrait of Jacquard (1839) needed 24,000 cards, and Babbage owned a copy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacquard_machine]
- Charles Babbage (26 December 1791 – 18 October 1871) designed the Difference Engine, motivated by errors in printed mathematical tables. He described the Analytical Engine in 1837: a general-purpose design with memory and conditional branching, programmed by punched cards borrowed from the Jacquard loom. He never completed either machine. A working Difference Engine was built from his plans in 1991. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Babbage ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_engine]
- Ada Lovelace (10 December 1815 – 27 November 1852) translated and annotated Menabrea's 1842 description of the engine in 1843. Her Note G set out an algorithm for Bernoulli numbers, often called the first published computer program, and she saw that the machine could manipulate symbols such as music. The "first programmer" title is disputed: Bromley (1990) says all but one of the programs in the notes were prepared by Babbage. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_Lovelace]
- ENIAC was built by Mauchly and Eckert at the University of Pennsylvania for Army artillery tables, completed in 1945 and dedicated on 15 February 1946 at a cost of about $487,000. Its six primary programmers were women: Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Fran Bilas and Ruth Lichterman. At least 200 women worked as human "computers" at the Moore School. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ENIAC] (detail already in the Digital Age chapter; cross-refer)
- Spreadsheets: LANPAR (Remy Landau and Rene Pardo, 1969) was the first electronic spreadsheet on mainframes. Dan Bricklin conceived **VisiCalc** while watching a Harvard Business School professor recompute a blackboard financial model, and wrote it with Bob Frankston (Software Arts). It was released for the Apple II on 17 October 1979 and became the personal computer's "killer application". Lotus 1-2-3 (1983) overtook it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VisiCalc ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spreadsheet]

## 16. Databases, big data, algorithms, surveillance
- "Big data" has been used as a term since the 1990s (John Mashey is often credited). It was originally defined by volume, variety and velocity, with "veracity" added later. Google Flu Trends overstated flu outbreaks by a factor of about two, a warning about the hype. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_data]
- India's Aadhaar (launched 28 January 2009) links a 12-digit number to a photograph, ten fingerprints and two iris scans. There were about 1.38 billion holders by October 2024. In *Puttaswamy* (24 August 2017) the Supreme Court held privacy to be a fundamental right. In September 2018 it upheld the Aadhaar Act but barred making Aadhaar mandatory for bank accounts, mobile connections and school admissions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aadhaar]
- **China's social credit system, described precisely**: it is a set of national credit-rating and blacklist ("redlist") mechanisms covering businesses, government bodies and individuals, run chiefly by the NDRC, the People's Bank of China and the Supreme People's Court. It is **not** a single unified personal score. Media reports of such a score are a "widespread misconception". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_credit_system]
- Its timeline: roots in 1990s financial credit-rating efforts (inspired by FICO, Equifax and TransUnion). The SPC began a judgment-defaulter blacklist in 2013 (about 32,000 names at first). The State Council issued its *Planning Outline (2014–2020)* in 2014. In 2015 eight firms were licensed for trials, including Alibaba's Sesame Credit, but none was authorised as an official system by 2017. In 2019 the central government ruled that citizens could not be punished for low pilot-city scores, only for legally defined offences. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_credit_system]
- Its effects: by June 2019, 26.82 million air tickets and 5.96 million high-speed rail tickets had been denied to blacklisted people, mainly court-judgment defaulters. A February 2022 MERICS report said: "In reality, the SoCS is not the techno-dystopian nightmare we fear: it is lowly digitalized, highly fragmented, and primarily focuses on businesses. Most importantly, such a score simply does not exist." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_credit_system]
- Algorithmic bias, worked example: COMPAS (Northpointe, 1998) scores recidivism risk for US courts. ProPublica ("Machine Bias", 23 May 2016) found it biased against Black defendants, while Northpointe showed it was equally accurate across races. Both claims are true under two mutually incompatible definitions of fairness. In July 2016 the Wisconsin Supreme Court (*Loomis*) allowed its use, with warnings attached. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COMPAS_(software)]
- Cathy O'Neil, *Weapons of Math Destruction* (2016), argues that big-data algorithms often reinforce inequality. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapons_of_Math_Destruction]
- Census confidentiality: agencies deliberately add small errors or suppress cells so that individuals in small groups cannot be identified. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census]

## 17. Theorists of quantification
- Ian Hacking (18 February 1936 – 10 May 2023): *The Emergence of Probability* (1975), *The Taming of Chance* (1990). "Making Up People" (lecture at Stanford, 1983; published 1986 in Heller and Sosna, eds, *Reconstructing Individualism*, Stanford UP, pp. 222–36; reprinted in *Historical Ontology*, 2002) argues that new classifications can bring new kinds of person into being. The "looping effect" is the feedback by which classified people change the categories. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Hacking ; https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-981-16-7255-2_71]
- Theodore M. Porter (b. 1953, UCLA): *The Rise of Statistical Thinking, 1820–1900* (1986); *Trust in Numbers: The Pursuit of Objectivity in Science and Public Life* (Princeton, 1995). Porter argues that quantification spreads because weak or exposed officials and professionals need impersonal "mechanical objectivity" ("technologies of distance") to win legitimacy, not because it copies the success of physics. His cases include US Army Corps of Engineers cost-benefit analysis, French state engineers and British actuaries. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trust_in_Numbers ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Porter]
- James C. Scott, *Seeing Like a State: How Certain Schemes to Improve the Human Condition Have Failed* (Yale, 1998). States impose "legibility" through permanent surnames, cadastral surveys, censuses and standard measures. "High modernist" schemes such as Soviet collectivisation, Brasília and Tanzanian villagisation (*ujamaa*, 1970s) failed because they ignored local knowledge. David Laitin called it "magisterial" but methodologically selective. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seeing_Like_a_State]
- Alain Desrosières (1940–2013, INSEE): *The Politics of Large Numbers: A History of Statistical Reasoning* (French 1993; English 1998). He treated public statistics as a constructed reality, between the views that they are objective facts and that they are political artefacts. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alain_Desrosi%C3%A8res]
- Michel Foucault's "biopower": the means by which modern states manage populations, with statistics (census, birth and death rates, epidemiology) as a central tool. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biopower]

## Debates
- **Tokens and writing**: Schmandt-Besserat's token-to-tablet theory is influential but criticised. Many think it explains number signs better than word-signs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-cuneiform]
- **Khipu as writing?** Were khipus purely numerical, or did some record language (Hyland 2017; Urton)? Unresolved. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quipu]
- **Did the census "make" caste?** Cohn and Dirks argue that colonial classification gave caste its rigid modern form. Dipankar Gupta rejects this as implying Indians had "no identity worth the name" before colonialism, though he accepts the Raj's large role. Timothy Alborn warns against overstating officials' power to control people by counting them. The geneticist David Reich (2018) cites genetic evidence of long-term endogamy against the strong version of Dirks's thesis. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census_in_British_India ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castes_of_Mind]
- **Islamic origins of Italian commercial techniques** (double entry, bills of exchange): see the Islamic commercial revolution chapter.
- **Nightingale's statistics**: her diagrams were innovative in persuasion rather than invention (Guerry 1829). The size of her own effect on Scutari mortality versus the Sanitary Commission's is debated. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale]
- **Lovelace as "first programmer"**: Fuegi and Francis emphasise her vision. Bromley, Collier, and Kim and Toole say the programs were mostly Babbage's. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_Lovelace]
- **IBM and the Holocaust**: Black argues IBM's technology was crucial. Reviewers accept the business ties but question "unique or decisive responsibility", and IBM disputes his methods. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_and_the_Holocaust]
- **Statistics and eugenics**: are the founders' methods separable from their eugenic aims? Institutions (UCL in 2020, Caius) removed honours, and Fisher's views on race are themselves debated. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Fisher ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Pearson]
- **IQ and testing**: Gould's *Mismeasure of Man* versus its scientific critics. The SAT's predictive value versus test-optional movements (over 1,230 colleges dropped requirements in the 2010s per FairTest, while Brown, Yale and Dartmouth found scores helped identify disadvantaged applicants). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mismeasure_of_Man ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAT]
- **GDP**: Kuznets himself warned against equating it with welfare. Feminist and ecological critics, and Sen and Nussbaum's capability approach, offer alternatives. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_domestic_product]
- **Maddison's long-run numbers**: they are widely used (Our World in Data, the Great Divergence debate: Pomeranz's *The Great Divergence*, 2000, versus Maddison's earlier European lead), but pre-1800 figures are conjectural benchmarks and are being revised by Broadberry and others.
