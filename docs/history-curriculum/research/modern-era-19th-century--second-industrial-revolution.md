## Dating, the name and the big picture
- Wikipedia dates the Second Industrial Revolution (also called the "Technological Revolution") from about 1870 to 1914, when the First World War began. The first revolution, which lasted until the mid-19th century, was followed by a slowdown in important inventions before the second began around 1870. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Earlier roots include the machine-tool industry, interchangeable parts, the Bessemer process and the open-hearth furnace. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Main centres: the UK, Germany and the US, and also France, Japan and the Low Countries. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- The name: Patrick Geddes introduced the concept of a "second industrial revolution" in Cities in Evolution (1910). Erich Zimmermann used it in 1951. David Landes's 1966 essay and The Unbound Prometheus standardised the scholarly definition, and Alfred Chandler (1918–2007) promoted the term most intensely. Some scholars still have reservations about it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Vaclav Smil called the period 1867–1914, when most of the great innovations emerged, "The Age of Synergy", because the inventions were engineering- and science-based. His book is Creating the Twentieth Century: Technical Innovations of 1867–1914 and Their Lasting Impact (Oxford, 2005). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaclav_Smil]
- Railway, steel and coal reinforced one another: the US laid 75,000 miles of track in the 1880s, which Wikipedia calls the largest amount anywhere in world history. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- By 1870 the work done by steam engines exceeded that done by animal and human power. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- By 1900 the leaders in industrial production were Britain (24% of the world total), the US (19%), Germany (13%), Russia (9%) and France (7%). Europe together made up 62%. (A different measure gives other figures; see "US and Germany overtake Britain".) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Prices of almost all goods fell, which caused business failures and periods called "depressions" even as the world economy grew. The Long Depression began in 1873. The US NBER dates its American contraction from October 1873 to March 1879. Contemporary British opinion held that Britain was in continuous depression from 1873 to as late as 1896. Britain was hardest hit and lost some of its industrial lead over continental Europe. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Depression ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Contemporary verdict (David A. Wells, cited as Wells 1891): "The economic changes that have occurred during the last quarter of a century -or during the present generation of living men- have unquestionably been more important and more varied than during any period of the world's history". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]

## Steel: Bessemer, Siemens–Martin, Gilchrist–Thomas
- Henry Bessemer patented his process in 1856. Air blown through molten pig iron burns off excess carbon and impurities, and the oxidation keeps the mass molten. The American William Kelly is said to have discovered it independently around 1851, but the claim is controversial. Some suggest Kelly was inspired by techniques of Chinese ironworkers he hired in 1854. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessemer_process]
- Bessemer said his invention was prompted by a conversation with Napoleon III in 1854 about steel for better artillery during the Crimean War. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessemer_process]
- Early failure: the first licensees could not produce good steel ("rotten hot and rotten cold"), and Bessemer bought the licences back. Robert Forester Mushet solved the problem by adding spiegeleisen, a manganese-bearing alloy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessemer_process]
- Cost: the process cut the price of steel from £40 per long ton to £6–7. Before it, steel was too expensive for bridges or building frames, so wrought iron had been used throughout the Industrial Revolution. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessemer_process]
- Steel rails lasted over ten times longer than iron. Mushet's 1857 steel rail at Derby station was still sound in 1863 after about 700 trains a day. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Siemens–Martin (open hearth): Charles William Siemens developed his regenerative furnace in the 1850s and claimed in 1857 that it saved 70–80% of fuel. The French engineer Pierre-Émile Martin first applied it to steelmaking in 1865. It could melt large amounts of scrap, was easier to control and did not make the steel brittle with nitrogen. It became the leading process by the early 20th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Gilchrist–Thomas ("basic") process: Sidney Gilchrist Thomas and his cousin Percy Gilchrist, a chemist at Blaenavon Ironworks in Wales, lined the converter with dolomite or limestone to remove phosphorus. That let Europe's abundant phosphoric ores (Lorraine, Belgium, Germany) be used. The by-product slag ("Thomas meal") was sold as phosphate fertiliser. The patent date differs by source: 1877 on the process page, May 1878 on the Bessemer and Second Industrial Revolution pages. Say "1877–78" or "patented in 1878". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilchrist%E2%80%93Thomas_process ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessemer_process]
- Wikipedia states that the inventor's name became better known in Belgium and Germany than in Britain. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Carnegie: Andrew Carnegie visited Bessemer in 1872. His Edgar Thomson Steel Works, built by Alexander Holley, opened in 1875. Carnegie Steel cut steel rail prices from $100 to $50 a ton between 1873 and 1875, and to $18 a ton by the 1890s. US steel output was about 157,000 tons a year before the Thomson Works and 26 million tons a year by 1910. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessemer_process]
- By 1889 US steel output exceeded that of the UK. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Carnegie]
- Henry Clifton Sorby began metallography in 1863, using acid etching. He was the first to understand that a small, precise amount of carbon gave steel its strength. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]

## Chemicals and dyes: Perkin, BASF, Bayer, Hoechst, Haber–Bosch
- William Henry Perkin discovered mauveine by accident in 1856, aged 18 (age not separately verified, so omit it), while trying to synthesise quinine against malaria. It was the first synthetic dye to be commercialised. (BASF's page says 1857; use 1856.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauveine ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Perkin was taught by August Wilhelm von Hofmann at the Royal College of Chemistry in London. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- BASF (Badische Anilin- und Soda-Fabrik) was founded by Friedrich Engelhorn on 6 April 1865 in Mannheim. The plant was built across the Rhine at Ludwigshafen because Mannheim's council feared air pollution. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BASF]
- BASF's first head of research, Heinrich Caro, had dye-industry experience in England. He filed a British patent for synthetic alizarin (a red dye) on 25 June 1869, and Perkin filed a nearly identical one the next day. Synthetic indigo research began in 1880 and the product reached the market in 1897. In 1901 dyestuffs were some 80% of BASF production. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BASF]
- Bayer was founded in 1863 in Barmen by the dye salesman Friedrich Bayer and the dyer Johann Friedrich Weskott. It launched Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) in 1899 and marketed heroin from 1898 to 1910 as a cough suppressant, presented as a non-addictive morphine substitute. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayer]
- German dominance: by 1900 German firms dominated the world market for synthetic dyes. In 1913 eight German firms, led by BASF, Bayer and Hoechst, produced almost 90% of world dyestuffs and sold about 80% of their output abroad. Chandler called the German dye companies "the world's first truly managerial industrial enterprises". British firms' R&D was increasingly eclipsed by Germany. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- The German dye firms spun off pharmaceuticals, photographic film, agricultural chemicals and electrochemicals. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Haber–Bosch: Fritz Haber (with his assistant Robert Le Rossignol) showed ammonia synthesis from atmospheric nitrogen in the laboratory in summer 1909, at about 125 mL an hour. BASF bought the process and Carl Bosch scaled it up. Alwin Mittasch found a cheap iron catalyst in 1909. The first industrial plant opened in 1913 at Oppau and reached 20 tonnes a day in 1914. Haber won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1918 and Bosch in 1931. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process]
- The process ended dependence on mined nitrates (Chile saltpetre) and guano. During the First World War the Allies had Chilean nitrate, controlled by British companies, while Germany did not. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process]
- Long-run effect: nearly 50% of the nitrogen in human tissues comes from the Haber–Bosch process. Vaclav Smil writes that without it, feeding the world at 1900 yields "would have required nearly four times more land". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process]
- The dark side: Haber is called the "father of chemical warfare" for weaponising chlorine at the Second Battle of Ypres. His wife Clara Immerwahr, the first woman to earn a chemistry PhD at the University of Breslau, killed herself on 2 May 1915. Haber was born into a Jewish family in Breslau and was later driven out by Nazi law in 1933. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Haber]
- The Norwegian Birkeland–Eyde process (1903) preceded Haber–Bosch but was less efficient. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]

## Electricity: Edison, Swan, Tesla, Westinghouse and the "current war"
- Joseph Swan's lamps lit Mosley Street in Newcastle in 1879, the first electric street lighting. In 1881 his lamps (about 1,200) made the Savoy Theatre in London the first public building lit entirely by electricity. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Menlo Park: Edison built his laboratory in 1876 at Menlo Park, New Jersey, funded by the sale of his quadruplex telegraph. It is "now considered the first industrial research laboratory". He wanted "a stock of almost every conceivable material". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Edison]
- Edison holds 1,093 US patents, and he was credited with inventions made largely by his staff. He moved to a lab in West Orange more than ten times the size in 1887. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Edison]
- Light bulb: Edison filed US patent 223,898 (a carbon-filament lamp) on 4 November 1879, and it was granted on 27 January 1880. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Edison]
- Edison opened the Holborn Viaduct station in London in January 1882. Pearl Street Station in New York began generating on 4 September 1882 with six 100 kW dynamos and 110 V DC, serving an initial 400 lamps for 82 customers. By 1884 it served 508 customers with 10,164 lamps. (Edison's own page says "946 customers". Prefer the Pearl Street figures.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_Street_Station ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Edison]
- Nikola Tesla was born 10 July 1856 in Smiljan (then in the Austrian Empire, now Croatia) to an ethnic Serb family; his father was an Orthodox priest. He emigrated to the US in June 1884, worked at Edison Machine Works for about six months and quit. He developed a polyphase AC induction motor in 1887. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikola_Tesla]
- In July 1888 Westinghouse licensed Tesla's motor and transformer designs for $60,000 in cash and stock plus a royalty of $2.50 per AC horsepower. In 1891, under financial pressure after the Barings crisis (the Panic of 1890), Tesla agreed to release Westinghouse from the royalty clause. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikola_Tesla]
- Current war: in June 1888 the engineer Harold P. Brown began a campaign against AC. He electrocuted dogs publicly at Columbia College on 30 July 1888 and a horse at Edison's West Orange lab on 5 December 1888. Historians note collusion between Brown and the Edison company; Edison Electric's treasurer Francis S. Hastings apparently originated the idea. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_currents]
- The first electric-chair execution was of William Kemmler on 6 August 1890. It was botched, and Westinghouse said: "They would have done better using an axe." Westinghouse had financed Kemmler's appeal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_currents]
- Resolution: Edison Electric merged with Thomson-Houston to form General Electric in 1892. In May 1892 Westinghouse underbid GE to light the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago with AC, and then won the Niagara Falls power station contract. In 1896 J. P. Morgan brokered an 11-year patent-sharing deal between GE and Westinghouse. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_currents]
- Europe led in AC too. In 1891 Mikhail Dolivo-Dobrovolsky transmitted three-phase power 176 km at 75% efficiency at the Frankfurt exhibition. Sebastian de Ferranti's Deptford station (completed 1891) used 10,000 V AC and is called the first modern power station. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_currents ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Charles Parsons invented the steam turbine in 1884, which made cheap, plentiful electricity possible. Frank Sprague built the first successful DC motor in 1886, and by 1889, 110 electric street railways were using or planning to use his equipment. Household electrification became common only in the 1920s, and then only in cities. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Werner von Siemens built the first electric passenger train in 1879 and the first electric elevator in 1880. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_von_Siemens]
- Electric light in factories removed the heat, pollution and fire risk of gas lighting, often enough to offset the cost of the electricity through lower insurance premiums. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]

## Internal combustion and the automobile
- Nicolaus Otto's four-stroke engine of 1876 was the first widely used internal combustion engine. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach (whom Daimler met as a 15-year-old orphan) worked at Deutz, then set up in Cannstatt in 1882. They designed a high-speed petrol engine in 1883, and in 1885 the vertical "grandfather clock" engine, which they fitted to the Reitwagen motorcycle. They founded the Daimler-Motoren-Gesellschaft (DMG) in 1890 and sold their first car in 1892. DMG and Benz & Cie merged in 1926. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottlieb_Daimler]
- Karl Benz applied for his patent for an "automobile fueled by gas" on 29 January 1886 (Wikipedia's text gives the patent number as "37345", while it is often cited elsewhere as 37435; omit the number). He drove the car publicly in Mannheim on 3 July 1886. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Benz]
- Bertha Benz had used her dowry to buy out his failing partner. On 5 August 1888, supposedly without telling her husband, she drove the Motorwagen with her sons Eugen and Richard about 104 km from Mannheim to Pforzheim. She bought fuel at pharmacies and had a shoemaker nail leather onto the brake blocks, inventing brake lining. She sent word of her arrival by telegram. Benz then added a third gear for hills. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Benz]
- Benz began selling the Patent-Motorwagen in late summer 1888, the first commercially available automobile. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Rudolf Diesel and Herbert Akroyd Stuart independently designed the diesel engine in the 1890s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- John Boyd Dunlop developed the first practical pneumatic tyre in 1887 in Belfast. The safety bicycle (J. K. Starley) caused the bike boom of the 1890s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]

## Oil: Rockefeller, Standard Oil, Baku and the Nobels
- Edwin Drake's 1859 well near Titusville, Pennsylvania, is considered the first "modern oil well". Wikipedia says Drake learned of cable-tool drilling (developed in ancient China for brine wells) from Chinese labourers in the US. The main early product was kerosene for lamps. Gasoline was an unwanted by-product until mass car production. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- John D. Rockefeller incorporated Standard Oil in Ohio in 1870. The Standard Oil Trust was created on 2 January 1882 by a secret agreement. It was the model "trust". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Oil]
- Methods: secret railroad rebates, the 1872 South Improvement Company scheme and buying out Cleveland refiners. Kerosene prices fell from 58 to 26 cents between 1865 and 1870. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Oil]
- Market share: 88% of refined oil flows by 1890. In 1904 it had 91% of refining (or "production") and 85% of final sales, and 55% of its kerosene was exported. The share fell to 70% by 1906 and 64% by 1911. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Oil]
- Ida Tarbell's father was an oil producer whose business failed because of Rockefeller's dealings. Her History of the Standard Oil Company appeared in McClure's from 1902 to 1904 and as a book in 1904. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ida_Tarbell ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Oil]
- On 15 May 1911 the US Supreme Court (Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States) held Standard an "unreasonable" monopoly under the Sherman Antitrust Act (1890) and ordered it broken up. Wikipedia's Standard Oil page says into 39 companies; other sources commonly say 34. Avoid the number or give the range. The case created the "rule of reason". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Oil ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Oil_Co._of_New_Jersey_v._United_States]
- Baku: Robert and Ludvig Nobel (brothers of Alfred) began with a refinery in Baku in 1876. It became Branobel, a share company headquartered in St Petersburg, in 1879, with Ludvig Nobel holding 53.7% and Alfred 3.8%. Between 1877 and 1901 the company drilled over 500 wells and employed 12,000 workers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branobel]
- Ludvig Nobel's Zoroaster was the world's first successful oil tanker. Its build contract was signed in January 1878 and it made its first run Baku–Astrakhan that year. (Another page says built 1877.) Nobel refused to patent the design. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludvig_Nobel ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Azerbaijan]
- Between 1898 and 1901 Baku produced more oil than the US. By 1901 it produced more than half the world's oil (11 million tons). A kerosene pipeline from Baku to Batum on the Black Sea was built between 1897 and 1907. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Azerbaijan]
- Baku was not only a Nobel story. Armenians reportedly ran almost a third of the region's oil business. Leading local magnates included the Azerbaijanis Zeynalabdin Taghiyev and Musa Nagiyev, and the Armenian Alexander Mantashev. The Rothschilds traded and shipped Baku oil. On the eve of 1914 three foreign-linked companies (Russian General Oil, Royal Dutch Shell, Nobel) held 86% of the capital. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Azerbaijan]
- Strikes, violence and ethnic strife around the 1905 revolution cut Baku output from its peak of 212,000 barrels a day. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Azerbaijan]

## Telegraph, undersea cables and wireless
- The first transatlantic cable (Cyrus Field's Atlantic Telegraph Company) carried its first messages on 16 August 1858, including Queen Victoria's to President Buchanan. It failed after three weeks when Wildman Whitehouse applied excessive voltage. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transatlantic_telegraph_cable]
- The SS Great Eastern laid a lasting cable in July 1866 (completed 18 July 1866 per the main article) from Valentia Island, Ireland, to Heart's Content, Newfoundland. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Red_Line ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Cables were insulated with gutta-percha, a latex from Palaquium trees of Southeast Asia. The word comes from Malay (getah, "sticky gum"). It served for the first transatlantic cable, and 30,000 hundredweight was imported into Britain in 1851. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gutta-percha]
- Empire by cable: by 1870 Suez was linked to Bombay, Madras, Penang and Singapore, and Australia was linked in 1871 via Java to Port Darwin. The "All Red Line" with its trans-Pacific section was inaugurated on 31 October 1902. In 1911 the Committee of Imperial Defence reported that 49 cuts would be needed to isolate the UK. British cable systems dominated the world from the 1850s until 1911. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Red_Line ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- By 1890 the telegraph let merchants in England or the US order goods from India and China for steamship delivery. With the Suez Canal, this led to the decline of London's great warehousing districts and cut out middlemen. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Marconi founded his company in Britain in 1897 and made the first transatlantic wireless transmission in 1901, from Poldhu, Cornwall, to Signal Hill, Newfoundland. John Ambrose Fleming's vacuum tube (1904) followed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]

## Telephone
- On 14 February 1876 Bell's lawyer filed his telephone patent application on the same day that Elisha Gray's lawyer filed a caveat. Bell's patent 174,465 was granted on 7 March 1876. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invention_of_the_telephone]
- On 10 March 1876 Bell said, "Mr. Watson, come here, I want to see you." (Wording varies slightly between sources; one has a dash: "Mr. Watson, come here – I want to see you".) He used a liquid transmitter similar to Gray's design. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invention_of_the_telephone]
- Antonio Meucci's telettrofono (c.1849–1856) is credited by several sources as the first telephone. He filed a caveat in 1871 but let it lapse after 1874. A 2002 US House resolution said that "if Meucci had been able to pay the $10 fee to maintain the caveat after 1874, no patent could have been issued to Bell". The Canadian Parliament replied by declaring Bell the inventor. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invention_of_the_telephone]
- Women's work: Boston Telephone Dispatch first hired boys as operators in January 1878, then switched to women. Emma Nutt, hired on 1 September 1878, was the first woman operator. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_switchboard]
- Like the early telegraph, the telephone was used mainly to speed business transactions. US transcontinental calls became possible only in 1915. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_the_United_States]

## Steamships, refrigeration and canned food
- Triple-expansion engines let ships carry far more freight than coal. Engine efficiency increased total coal use (the Jevons paradox). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Refrigerated meat from New Zealand: the sailing ship Dunedin, refitted with a Bell-Coleman refrigeration machine, left Port Chalmers on 15 February 1882. It carried 4,331 mutton, 598 lamb and 22 pig carcasses, 250 kegs of butter and other produce. It reached London after 98 days, and only one carcass was condemned. The voyage effectively began the refrigerated meat trade and New Zealand's dominance in it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunedin_(1874_ship)]
- In 1878 Gustavus Swift, with the engineer Andrew Chase, developed the first practical ice-cooled railcar, ushering in the "era of cheap beef" from Chicago's Union Stock Yards. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refrigerator_car ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustavus_Franklin_Swift]
- Canning grew from Nicolas Appert's work (prompted by a French government prize of 12,000 francs) and Peter Durand's 1810 tin-can patent. By the 1860s machine-made steel cans had cut cooking time in the can from about six hours to thirty minutes. The Crimean, American Civil and Franco-Prussian Wars spread canned food to working-class men. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canning]
- Tin replaced silver as Bolivia's main export in the early 20th century because canning used tin solder. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin]

## Science-based industry and the research lab
- Menlo Park (1876) is considered the first industrial research laboratory (see Electricity). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Edison]
- BASF had a main laboratory at Ludwigshafen by 1887 and a head of research (Caro) from the 1860s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BASF]
- Germany invested more heavily than Britain in research, especially in chemistry, motors and electricity. Its chemical lead rested on universities and industrial laboratories. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_Germany]
- Maxwell's electromagnetic theory predicted radio waves, which Hertz and Hughes demonstrated, a case of science preceding technology. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]

## Big business, trusts and finance capital
- Chandler argued that railroads created the modern business enterprise. Their scale required departments, clear lines of authority, telegraph control and "railroad accounting", which steel and other industries later adopted. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- By 1900 a few large corporations, some organised as trusts, dominated US steel, oil, sugar, meatpacking and farm machinery. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- In 1901 J. P. Morgan merged Carnegie Steel, Federal Steel and National Steel into U.S. Steel for $492 million. It was capitalised at $1.4 billion, the world's first billion-dollar corporation, and produced about two-thirds of US steel. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Steel]
- In December 1912 Morgan testified before the Pujo Committee, which concluded that a small number of financial leaders held concentrated control. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._P._Morgan]
- The Sherman Antitrust Act was passed in 1890, introduced by Senator John Sherman (R–Ohio). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherman_Antitrust_Act]
- German cartels were legal and enforceable in German courts, unlike in Britain and the US. There were 275 in 1900 and over 500 by 1908. Banks played central roles in financing industry. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_Germany]
- Carnegie's "The Gospel of Wealth" (1889) called on the rich to use their wealth for society. In an 1868 memo to himself he wrote that "the man who dies thus rich dies disgraced". He gave away about $350 million, almost 90% of his fortune. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Carnegie]
- Krupp of Essen: by the late 1880s armaments were around 50% of Krupp's production. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krupp]

## Mass production, Taylorism and Ford
- Precursors: Chicago meatpacking used "disassembly lines" from 1867. Ransom Olds used an assembly line for the Curved Dash Oldsmobile from 1901. William "Pa" Klann brought the idea to Ford after visiting Swift's slaughterhouse. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assembly_line]
- Frederick Winslow Taylor started as a clerk at Midvale Steel in 1877 and became foreman in 1880. Louis Brandeis popularised the term "scientific management" in the Eastern Rate Case before the Interstate Commerce Commission in 1910. Taylor's The Principles of Scientific Management appeared in 1911. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_management ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Winslow_Taylor]
- Taylor's four principles: replace rule-of-thumb with scientific study of tasks; select and train each worker; give detailed instruction and supervision; and divide the work, with managers planning and workers executing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Winslow_Taylor]
- Opposition: in 1911 the moulders at Watertown Arsenal walked out against the Taylor system. Samuel Gompers of the AFL opposed it. A 1912 House committee opposed stopwatch time study without the workers' consent. Historians (Wrege and Perroni, 1974) question Taylor's account of the pig-iron handler "Schmidt". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_management ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Winslow_Taylor]
- In 1913 Lenin called Taylorism a "'scientific' system of sweating". In 1918, in power, he urged Russians to learn from it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_management]
- The Model T was produced from 1 October 1908 to 26 May 1927, with 15 million built. It was designed by Childe Harold Wills and the Hungarian immigrants Joseph Galamb and Eugene Farkas. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Model_T]
- The moving assembly line began on 7 October 1913 at Highland Park, Michigan. Final assembly time fell from 12.5 man-hours to 1 hour 33 minutes (93 minutes), with 45 steps. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assembly_line ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highland_Park_Ford_Plant]
- Ford said mass production would not have been possible without electricity, which let machine tools be placed in the order of the work flow. Model T production used 32,000 machine tools. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Price: sources differ. One gives about $825 in 1908 and about $575 in 1912; another gives $780 in 1910 and $360 in 1916. Give both pairs with their years, or say "roughly halved". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assembly_line ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- The $5 day was announced on 5 January 1914, raising minimum pay from $2.34 to $5 for qualifying male workers. It was partly a response to turnover: in 1913, to add 100 men Ford had to hire 963. Eligibility depended on approval by Ford's "Social Department", which had about 50 investigators. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Ford ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assembly_line]
- Repetitive strain and the ban on talking were common complaints of line work. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assembly_line]

## The US and Germany overtake Britain
- By 1895 the US had overtaken Britain in manufacturing output. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_the_United_States]
- Germany's share of world trade rose from 17.2% (1880) to 26.6% (1913), while Britain's fell from 38.2% to 30.2%. German exports tripled between 1890 and 1913. By 1913 Germany had 14.8% of world manufacturing against Britain's 13.6%, and Britain was third in the world steel market behind the US and Germany. In 1914 German steel output (17.6 million tons) exceeded that of Britain, France and Russia combined. German coal output was 277 million tons against Britain's 292. (These manufacturing shares differ from the main article's 1900 figures above, which use another measure, so name the source and year.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_Germany]
- Ruhr coal output was 2 million short tons in 1850, 22 million in 1880, 60 million in 1900 and 114 million in 1913. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_Germany]
- Reasons given for Germany's rise: a late start allowed copying of best practice, more research spending, the concentrated Konzerne system, no costly worldwide empire to defend, and Lorraine's industry after the 1871 annexation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- "Made in Germany" was imposed by Britain's Merchandise Marks Act of 1887 to mark foreign goods, many of them German imports falsely bearing British marks. Germany turned it into a mark of quality. Bismarck's "rye and iron" tariff of 1879 preceded it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Made_in_Germany]
- Britain alone kept free trade, while most governments protected their economies with tariffs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Bismarck introduced old-age pensions, accident and health insurance in the 1880s, partly to win workers away from socialism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_Germany]
- Industrial growth was not only Anglo-German-American. Łódź ("the Polish Manchester") grew from under 1,000 people in 1823 to over 400,000 by 1914, supplying the Russian market. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]

## Everyday life: department stores, cheap paper, light
- Aristide Boucicaut became a partner in Au Bon Marché in Paris in 1852 and introduced fixed prices and exchanges and refunds. Store income rose from 20 million francs (1870) to 72 million (1877). Staff grew from 12 in 1838 to 1,788 in 1879. Zola's Au Bonheur des Dames (1883) was inspired by it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Bon_March%C3%A9 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_store]
- John Wanamaker's Philadelphia store (1877) marked prices on every article and introduced electric light (1878), the telephone (1879) and pneumatic tubes (1880). Selfridges opened in London in 1909, promoting shopping for pleasure. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_store]
- Japan: Mitsukoshi, rooted in the Echigoya kimono store of 1673, became the first "modern-style" department store in 1904. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_store]
- Department stores became spaces where middle-class women could shop "safely and decorously, away from home". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_store]
- Cheap wood-pulp paper made schoolbooks, newspapers and fiction widely available by 1900. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Public health: London's sewers (1860s) and the Metropolis Water Act 1852 greatly cut disease. Crop failures no longer caused starvation in areas linked to large markets by transport. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]

## The first globalisation: trade, migration, gold
- O'Rourke and Williamson, Globalization and History: The Evolution of a Nineteenth-Century Atlantic Economy (MIT Press, 1999). They treat commodity-price convergence as the core evidence of integration, finding little before 1800 and substantial convergence after. They argue that mass migration drove real-wage convergence in the Atlantic economy. Later chapters cover a political backlash: a return to protection (ch. 6) and increasingly restrictive immigration policy (ch. 10), measured with an index of restrictiveness. [search summary; source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/business-history-review/article/abs/globalization-and-history-the-evolution-of-a-nineteenthcentury-atlantic-economy-bykevin-h-orourke-and-jeffrey-g-williamson-cambridge-mass-mit-press-1999-xii-343-pp-figures-graphs-notes-references-and-index-cloth-4500-isbn-0262150492/397D850CBE9DB73A0D5C9D5974CEE03C ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_G._Williamson]
- Kevin Hjortshøj O'Rourke (born 1963, Irish) is now at CNRS and Sciences Po. Jeffrey G. Williamson (Harvard Laird Bell Professor) died on 29 August 2026, aged 90, according to Wikipedia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_O%27Rourke_(economist) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_G._Williamson]
- Gold standard: the international classical gold standard is dated 1873–1914, starting when the German Empire switched from silver to the gold mark (law of 4 December 1871, phased from 1873). The US followed with the Coinage Act of 1873, Russia in 1897, Japan in 1897 (using gold reserves from the Sino-Japanese War indemnity, to gain access to Western capital), India with a gold-exchange standard in 1893, the Philippines in 1903 and Mexico in 1905. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard]
- Barry Eichengreen: "only then did countries settle on gold as the basis for their money supplies." The switch drove down the price of silver after 1873. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard]
- Migration: over 30 million Europeans migrated to the US between 1836 and 1914. The peak year was 1907 (1,285,349 entrants), and by 1910, 13.5 million immigrants lived in the US. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_to_the_United_States]
- Restriction: the Page Act of 1875 banned Chinese women. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 banned almost all Chinese immigration until 1943. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_to_the_United_States]
- Asian labour migration: more than 1.6 million Indians went overseas under indenture, beginning in 1834 with the ship Atlas to Mauritius. Over 150,000 Chinese "coolies" were sold to Cuba before that trade ended in 1875. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coolie]
- Keynes, looking back from 1919 (The Economic Consequences of the Peace, ch. 2): "The inhabitant of London ... regarded this state of affairs as normal, certain, and permanent, except in the direction of further improvement, and any deviation from it as aberrant, scandalous, and avoidable." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Economic_Consequences_of_the_Peace]

## Labour: unions, May Day, Haymarket, socialism
- Eight-hour movement: Australian stonemasons in Victoria held a mass stoppage on 21 April 1856. In October 1884 the Federation of Organized Trades and Labor Unions set 1 May 1886 as the date for the eight-hour day. On 1 May 1886, 300,000 to half a million US workers struck. Their song: "Eight Hours for work. Eight hours for rest. Eight hours for what we will." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Day ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haymarket_affair]
- The Knights of Labor grew from 70,000 members in 1884 to over 700,000 by 1886. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haymarket_affair]
- Haymarket: on 4 May 1886 in Chicago, a bomb was thrown at police at a rally the day after police fired on strikers at McCormick Harvesting. Seven policemen and at least four workers died. Paul Avrich said most police deaths were from police gunfire; Timothy Messer-Kruse thinks several were shot by protesters. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haymarket_affair]
- Trial: eight anarchists were convicted of conspiracy, though only two were present and none was shown to have thrown the bomb. Seven were sentenced to death. Two had their sentences commuted, and Louis Lingg killed himself in his cell. Four (including August Spies and the Alabama-born Albert Parsons) were hanged on 11 November 1887. Governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned the survivors on 26 June 1893 and criticised the trial. The bomber was never identified. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haymarket_affair]
- May Day: the Second International's founding congress in Paris (1889), on a proposal by Raymond Lavigne, called for demonstrations on 1 May 1890. These took place in the US and most of Europe. The 1891 Brussels congress made May Day annual. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Day ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_International]
- The Second International was founded in Paris on 14 July 1889, the centenary of the Bastille. It excluded anarchists in 1891 and had a bureau in Brussels from 1900. Its 1907 Stuttgart congress narrowly condemned colonialism in principle, 127 to 108, against a minority favouring a "socialist colonial policy". The Japanese socialist Sen Katayama shook hands with Plekhanov at the 1904 Amsterdam congress during the Russo-Japanese War. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_International]
- Collapse: anti-war resolutions at Stuttgart (1907), Copenhagen (1910) and Basel (1912) failed in July 1914. After Jean Jaurès's assassination, the major parties backed their nations' war efforts, and the International collapsed on 4 August 1914. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_International]
- The German SPD was formed in 1875 and grew after the Anti-Socialist Laws lapsed in 1890. It adopted the Erfurt Program in 1891. In 1912 it won 34.8% of the vote and 110 of 397 Reichstag seats, the largest party. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Democratic_Party_of_Germany]
- Homestead (Carnegie Steel), 1 July to 20 November 1892: strikers defeated Pinkerton agents on 6 July. Seven strikers and three to eight Pinkertons died, and the National Guard broke the strike. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestead_strike]
- Pullman strike, 11 May to 20 July 1894: President Cleveland sent the army, and Eugene V. Debs was jailed for six months. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pullman_Strike]
- Japan: 1907 saw the most labour disputes in a decade, with troops suppressing riots at the Ashio and Besshi copper mines. Unions were small, and the Public Order and Police Law of 1900 hindered them. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_Japan]
- India: in July 1908 Bombay mill workers struck for six days after Tilak was sentenced to six years for sedition (served in Mandalay). Lenin hailed it in "Inflammable Material in World Politics". The strike's size is uncertain: sources give from about 20,000 to over 100,000, and 65,000 in 60-odd mills is common. [search summary; source: https://publications.cpiml.net/book/indias-march-to-freedom-the-other-dimension-contents-of-indias-march-to-freedom-the-other-dimension/lenin-hails-the-political-awakening-of ; Tilak sentence and Mandalay: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bal_Gangadhar_Tilak]

## Women and child workers
- Matchgirls' strike, July 1888: about 1,400 women and girls at Bryant & May in Bow, London, walked out after a worker was dismissed. Annie Besant had published an exposé in The Link on 23 June 1888. They won the abolition of fines and deductions and founded the Union of Women Matchmakers, the largest union of women and girls in the country. The workforce was mostly Irish or of Irish descent. White phosphorus caused "phossy jaw" (necrosis of the jaw). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matchgirls%27_strike]
- 1870 US census: women were 15% of the workforce (1.8 million of 12.5 million), a third of factory operatives and two-thirds of teachers. From 1890 to 1930 women workers were typically young and unmarried, often in clerical work, teaching, textiles or domestic service, and left the workforce on marriage. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_the_workforce]
- Claudia Goldin (2023 Nobel) describes women's labour-force participation as a U-shaped curve over development. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_the_workforce]
- Japan: textile mills employed mainly women, half of them under 20. Their fathers sent them, and the women handed their wages to their fathers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_Japan]
- Child labour: an estimated 1.7 million children under 15 worked in American industry by 1900, and over 2 million by 1910. Lewis Hine's photographs from the 1910s documented them. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_labour]
- Of the 4,152 workers in Britain's 25 match factories in 1897, 1,492 were aged 14–18. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matchgirls%27_strike]

## Japan's industrialisation
- The state built model factories with foreign technology and experts from the late 1860s to the early 1890s, then sold many to well-connected entrepreneurs. After the late 1880s most growth came from private enterprise, especially the zaibatsu (the "Big Four": Sumitomo, Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Yasuda). The slogan was fukoku kyōhei, "Enrich the country, strengthen the military". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_Japan ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaibatsu]
- The Tomioka Silk Mill was founded by the government with the Frenchman Paul Brunat and opened on 4 November 1872. Its 300 reels made it one of the largest in the world (the biggest French and Italian plants had about 150). It began with about 210 women, short of workers partly because rumours spread that the wine-drinking foreigners drank workers' blood. It was sold to Mitsui in 1893 and became a UNESCO site in 2014. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomioka_Silk_Mill]
- Raw silk was Japan's most important export and overtook China's. Silk revenue funded imports of industrial technology. Average annual exports rose from 646 tons (1868–72) to 9,462 tons (1909–14). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration]
- By the 1890s Japanese textiles dominated the home market and competed with British goods in China and India. Japan largely skipped water power and went straight to steam. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_Japan]
- Japanese coal output was 0.6 million tonnes in 1875, 1.2 million in 1885 and 5 million in 1895. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration]
- The Imperial Yawata (Yahata) Steel Works was established in 1896, and its first blast furnace, designed by the German firm Gute Hoffnungshütte, was lit on 5 February 1901. It faltered at first. By 1912 it made 80% of Japan's pig iron and 80–90% of its steel. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahata_Steel_Works]
- Japanese National Railways was formed in 1907 by nationalising 17 private railways. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_Japan]

## India: Bombay mills and Tata steel
- The first Bombay cotton mill, the Bombay Spinning and Weaving Company of Cowasji Nanabhoy Davar, was founded on 7 July 1854 at Tardeo and began production on 7 February 1856. There were 10 mills with over 6,500 workers by 1865 and 136 mills by 1900 in "Girangaon" ("mill village"). Owners included the Tatas, Petits, Wadias and Sassoons. Workers lived in crowded chawls. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombay_Spinning_and_Weaving_Company ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girangaon]
- Jamsetji Tata (1839–1904), a Parsi from Navsari, floated the Central India Spinning, Weaving and Manufacturing Company at Nagpur in 1874. Its mill was named Empress Mill when Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India on 1 January 1877. He imported long-staple Egyptian cotton and American ring spindles to compete with British imports. (One Wikipedia page wrongly places the company "in Bombay".) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tata_Textiles ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_India_under_the_British_Raj ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamsetji_Tata]
- Tata named his Bombay mill "Svadeshi" (bought in 1887) before the Swadeshi movement began in 1905. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tata_Textiles ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamsetji_Tata]
- Tata Iron and Steel Company (TISCO) was established on 26 August 1907 at Jamshedpur (then Bihar), raised from Indian funding after the British had refused permission since 1883. It produced pig iron from 1911 and steel from 1912, and introduced an eight-hour day in 1912. The Raj promised to buy surplus steel, but its British-standard specification hampered Indian steel. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tata_Steel ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_India_under_the_British_Raj]
- Wider context: India's share of world industrial output fell from 25% (1750) to 2% (1900). Handloom yarn output fell from 419 million lb in 1850 to 240 million lb in 1900. The rail network grew from 1,349 km in 1860 to 25,495 km in 1890. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_India_under_the_British_Raj]

## Russia: Witte's industrialisation
- Sergei Witte was Minister of Finance from August 1892 to August 1903. He accelerated the Trans-Siberian Railway (begun on 9 March 1891; 9,289 km to Vladivostok), created a state vodka monopoly in 1895, and obtained the Chinese Eastern Railway concession through the 1896 Li–Lobanov Treaty. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Witte ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Siberian_Railway]
- He used high protective tariffs (the 1891 customs law), foreign loans, subsidies and the gold standard. Russian industrial output grew by an average of 8% a year in the 1890s, the fastest in the world. Still, peasants were 80% of the population around 1900. (The gold standard date is 1897 on the gold standard and Tsarist economy pages and 1896 on Witte's page, when the reform began. Use 1897.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Tsarist_Russia ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Witte]
- Witte argued that the regime could be saved only by turning Russia into a "modern industrial society". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Witte]
- The Welshman John Hughes took a Russian concession in 1869 for ironworks north of the Sea of Azov. His settlement, Yuzovka ("Hughesovka"), became Donetsk. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hughes_(businessman)]
- Alexander Gerschenkron's "Economic Backwardness in Historical Perspective" (essay 1951; book 1962, not separately verified) argued that latecomers like Russia skip "stages". It predicts faster industrial growth, more stress on capital goods and sharper spurts, with bigger roles for the state and banks. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Gerschenkron]

## Raw materials from the colonies and commodity frontiers
- Congo rubber: the Berlin Conference of 1884–85 recognised King Leopold II's Congo Free State, more than 85 times the size of Belgium. The inflatable tyre around 1890 created a rubber boom, which Jean Stengers called Leopold's "miraculous deliverance" from near-bankruptcy. Free State revenue rose from about 150,000 francs (1890) to over 18 million (1901). Leopold made 70 million francs profit from 1896 to 1905. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
- The "red rubber" system: forced labour as tax, quotas (in the ABIR concession, 3–4 kg of dried rubber per adult man per fortnight), hostage-taking, the chicotte whip and the severing of hands. The Force Publique (formed 1888) had 19,000 men by 1900. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
- Exposure: missionaries, E. D. Morel and Roger Casement (consul's report, 1904) publicised the abuses, and Belgium took over the colony in 1908. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casement_Report ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
- Deaths are disputed. Estimates of the population decline range from 1.2 million to 10 million. Hochschild (King Leopold's Ghost, 1998) uses about 10 million, which is generally seen as the high end. Ndaywel è Nziem gives 5–10 million. The demographer Jean-Paul Sanderson (2020) gives 1–5 million, with the lower end most plausible. Disease (sleeping sickness, smallpox and others) was the main direct cause, made worse by the violence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Leopold%27s_Ghost]
- Amazon rubber, 1879–1912: Manaus and Belém boomed, and Indigenous peoples of the Putumayo were enslaved by Julio César Arana's company (Casement investigated in 1910–11). In 1876 Henry Wickham smuggled 70,000 rubber seeds out of Brazil, and the British planted them in Malaya, Ceylon and tropical Africa, ending Brazil's monopoly. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_rubber_cycle]
- Malayan tin: about 40,000 Hakka and Cantonese miners worked in Larut, Perak, by about 1870. Chinese secret societies (Ghee Hin and Hai San) fought the Larut Wars (1861–74), which ended with the Pangkor Treaty of 1874 that brought British intervention. Malaya was the world's largest tin producer by 1883. The first railway, Taiping to Port Weld, was built in 1885. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larut_Wars ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining_in_Malaysia]
- Chilean nitrates and the War of the Pacific (14 February 1879 to 4 April 1884): the trigger was Bolivia's 1878 ten-centavo tax on the Chilean Antofagasta Nitrate and Railway Company (CSFA), in breach of the 1874 treaty. The company had British capital from Antony Gibbs & Sons. Chile won Peru's Tarapacá (Treaty of Ancón, 20 October 1883) and Bolivia's coast, leaving Bolivia landlocked. The war is also called the "Saltpeter War" and the "Ten Cents War". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Pacific]
- The historian Victor Kiernan said the British Foreign Office "never at any time contemplated any kind of active intervention". Some historians stress Chilean elite interest in nitrate wealth amid the 1870s crisis; William Sater stresses geopolitics. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Pacific]
- Nitrate taxes then made up roughly half of Chilean government revenue. Estimates range from 45% to over 50% for 1880–1914, rising over time, with nitrates about 70% of exports. [search summary; source: https://countrystudies.us/chile/57.htm ; https://repositorio.uc.cl/dspace/items/862b10be-0ade-4692-a4f2-f603fe7e1155]
- Commodity frontiers: Jason W. Moore coined the concept. Beckert and Bosma (Journal of Global History forum, 2021) describe commodity frontiers as places and processes at the edge of capitalism's expansion where raw materials are commodified, organised into successive "regimes". Maxine Berg traces the idea to world-systems theory and commodity-chain analysis. [search summary; source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/10F18899007067E841C51983C7F845DE ; https://epicenter.wcfia.harvard.edu/podcast/ep17-exploring-commodity-frontiers]

## Debates
- Was there a "second" industrial revolution? Landes and Chandler made the label standard, while some scholars still have reservations about it. Smil's "Age of Synergy" stresses clustered, science-based innovation from 1867 to 1914. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Industrial_Revolution]
- Mokyr, "The Second Industrial Revolution, 1870–1914" (1998 essay): the period accelerated the feedback between "propositional" knowledge (science) and "prescriptive" knowledge (technique). Mokyr calls the difference from the first revolution "one of degree", and notes that many techniques still emerged with little scientific base. [search summary; source: https://www.econlib.org/library/enc/bios/mokyr.html ; essay excerpt via search results]
- Britain's relative decline: was it entrepreneurial failure? The older view, and Martin Wiener's cultural explanation (1981), blamed British business. Deirdre McCloskey ("Did Victorian Britain Fail?", Economic History Review 1970; Economic Maturity and Entrepreneurial Decline: British Iron and Steel, 1870–1913) found that British steelmakers performed about as well as German and American ones. William Lazonick says the debate is far from settled. [search summary; source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/ehsrev/v23y1970i3p446-459.html ; https://www.deirdremccloskey.com/articles/rev_iron1.php]
- Gordon v. techno-optimists: Gordon argues the 1870–1970 "Great Inventions" (electricity, the internal combustion engine, clean water, chemicals, communications) were a one-time wave, that growth has flatlined since 1970, and that future growth faces "headwinds". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rise_and_Fall_of_American_Growth ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_J._Gordon]
- Chandler's managerial thesis has critics who want to extend it and some who would replace it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_D._Chandler_Jr.]
- Globalisation's origins: O'Rourke and Williamson date "true" globalisation to 19th-century price convergence. Flynn and Giráldez dispute this, arguing for earlier (16th-century silver-trade) origins. [search summary; source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w7632 (title "When Did Globalization Begin?")]
- Late development: Gerschenkron's backwardness model (state and banks substitute for missing prerequisites) applied to Russia and, by extension, Japan. Wikipedia notes that Japan's success owed something to its relative lack of resources, which made it unattractive to Western imperialism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Gerschenkron ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration]
- India: did colonial rule cause deindustrialisation (the Digby and nationalist view, and the decline in handloom output) or bring investment (Niall Ferguson cites £270 million by the 1880s and £400 million by 1914)? [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_India_under_the_British_Raj]
- Congo death toll (see above): "maximalists" such as Hochschild and Vansina's local studies, against demographers such as Sanderson and historians such as Stengers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocities_in_the_Congo_Free_State]
- Haymarket: who killed the police? Avrich (mostly police friendly fire) against Messer-Kruse (armed protesters). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haymarket_affair]
- Ford's $5 day: generosity, or a cure for turnover plus social control through the Social Department? [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assembly_line ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Ford]

- Topsy the elephant (1903) as an Edison anti-AC stunt. This is false: Wikipedia states that Edison was never at Luna Park and that the killing was not part of the war of currents, though an Edison company film crew shot it. Use only as a debunked myth. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topsy_(elephant)]
