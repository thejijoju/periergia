## 1. Timeline and scale
- The UN had 51 original (founding) members in 1945; it now has 193. [source: MEM]
- Admissions to the UN by decade, counted by me from the admission dates in MEM's member table (admission is not the same as independence: e.g. some states joined years after independence, and the 1990s figure is mostly post-Soviet and post-Yugoslav states, not colonies): 1940s 57 (51 founders + 6), 1950s 24, 1960s 42, 1970s 25, 1980s 7, 1990s 32, 2000s 5, 2010s 1 (South Sudan, 2011). Writer: present as "UN membership rose from 51 in 1945 to about 100 by 1960 and almost 150 by 1980" only as an approximation derived from these counts (51+6+24 = 81 by end-1959; +42 = 123 by end-1969; +25 = 148 by end-1979), or simply "UN membership nearly tripled between 1945 and 1980". [source: MEM (my count)]
- Waves: immediately after 1945 a wave in Asia; then the Middle East; then sub-Saharan Africa in the 1960s; most remaining colonies were small islands. "Decolonization was often not extensively planned, instead occurring as a response to politics in the colony, politics at home, and increasing international pressure." [source: DEC]
- 1960, the "Year of Africa": 17 African countries became independent (14 from France, 2 from Britain [Nigeria; Somalia, formed from British Somaliland and the Italian-administered Trust Territory], 1 from Belgium [Congo]); the number of independent African countries rose from 9 (95 million people) to 26 (180 million). Ralph Bunche used the phrase "year of Africa" on 16 February 1960; O. H. Morris of the British colonial ministry predicted it in January. [source: YOA] (Matches the 20th-century overview's "seventeen African independences of 1960".)
- French Africa's 1960 independences: Cameroon, Togo, Mali Federation, Madagascar, Dahomey, Niger, Upper Volta, Ivory Coast, Chad, Central African Republic, Congo-Brazzaville, Gabon, Mauritania; British: Sudan 1956, Ghana 1957, Nigeria 1960, Sierra Leone and Tanganyika 1961, Uganda 1962, Kenya and Zanzibar 1963, Malawi and Zambia 1964, Gambia 1965, Botswana and Lesotho 1966, Mauritius and Swaziland 1968, Seychelles 1976; Belgium: Congo 1960, Rwanda and Burundi 1962. [source: DEC]
- Asia: Philippines 1946; India and Pakistan 1947; Burma and Ceylon 1948; Indonesia 1949; Cambodia and Laos fully independent late 1953, Vietnam 4 June 1954 (Geneva Accords 21 July 1954 divided it); Malaya 1957; Singapore expelled from Malaysia 1965; Brunei 1984; Hong Kong to China 1997; Macau 1999; Timor-Leste 2002 (after Indonesian occupation from 1975). [source: DAS; DEC]
- UN General Assembly Resolution 1514 (XV), the Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples, adopted 14 December 1960: 89 in favour, 0 against, 9 abstentions (Australia, Belgium, Dominican Republic, France, Portugal, Spain, South Africa, United Kingdom, United States). It characterised foreign rule as a violation of human rights, affirmed self-determination and called for an end to colonial rule. [source: R1514]
- Resolution 1514 context: the Soviet Union first proposed a declaration; 43 Asian and African states brought forward the compromise text calling for "immediate steps to be taken". The US abstained under British pressure (decision escalated to Eisenhower); US delegate Zelma George, an African American, nonetheless led an ovation after the vote. [source: R1514]
- In 1961 the UN created a committee on decolonisation (Resolution 1654 (XVI) is listed in R1514's "see also"; details NOT VERIFIED here — say only that the UN followed up with a monitoring committee if needed, or omit). 1990–2000 and 2001–2010 were declared International Decades for the Eradication of Colonialism. [source: R1514]
- Seventeen territories remain on the UN list of non-self-governing territories (as of 2020 per DEC). [source: DEC]
- Atlantic Charter (August 1941): Britain and the US said they would "respect the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live; and they wish to see sovereign rights and self-government restored to those who have been forcibly deprived of them". Colonised nationalists took it as a promise; Churchill did not intend it to apply to the British Empire (this last point NOT VERIFIED in sources read — omit or verify). [source: DAF]
- The Second World War's role: the Fifth Pan-African Congress met in Manchester in October 1945 (dates: 15–19 October per NKR; 15–21 October per PAC — say "October 1945"); Nkrumah and George Padmore were principal organisers; it adopted a strategy for supplanting colonialism and a federal United States of Africa; earlier Congresses met 1919 Paris, 1921, 1923, 1927; the sixth in Dar es Salaam in 1974. [source: NKR; PAC]
- France's Brazzaville conference (January–February 1944): de Gaulle promised the end of forced labour, elected territorial assemblies and representation in Paris, but explicitly rejected independence. [source: DAF]
- The First Indochina War's French Union forces included 296,000 Europeans and 183,000 Africans (of 496,560 main troops in total, per FIW infobox) — useful for the point that colonial soldiers fought colonial wars. [source: FIW]

## 2. Asia
### Indonesia (1945–49)
- Sukarno and Hatta proclaimed independence on 17 August 1945, pressed by radical *pemuda* (youth) groups; the next day the Preparatory Committee elected Sukarno president and Hatta vice-president. [source: INR]
- British and British-Indian troops landed from late September 1945 to accept the Japanese surrender; the Dutch returned under their cover. The Battle of Surabaya (late October–November 1945), after Brigadier Mallaby was killed on 30 October 1945, was the bloodiest single battle of the revolution and a national symbol; estimates of Indonesian fighters killed there 6,300–15,000; British forces lost about 660 killed, mostly British Indians. 10 November is commemorated in Indonesia (Heroes' Day — NOT VERIFIED in text read; omit the name). [source: INR]
- *Bersiap* (1945–46): pemuda violence against Dutch internees, Eurasians, Ambonese, Chinese and others; death toll estimates 3,500–30,000; NIOD concluded about 5,500 Dutch dead, not above 10,000. [source: INR]
- Linggadjati Agreement (November 1946): the Netherlands recognised the Republic as de facto authority over Java, Madura and Sumatra, within a planned federal United States of Indonesia under the Dutch crown. [source: INR]
- First "police action" (*politionele actie*), Operation Product: launched at midnight 20/21 July 1947 (INR says midnight 20 July; PROD gives 21 July–4 August 1947), to seize economically valuable areas of Java and Sumatra and pay for a ~120,000-strong army; the Dutch called it a "police action" to restore order. UN Security Council and US pressure produced a ceasefire. Indonesian history calls it the First Dutch Military Aggression (PROD; wording NOT fully verified — say "Indonesians call these attacks 'aggressions'" only if checked). [source: INR; PROD]
- Renville Agreement (January 1948, UN-brokered): ceasefire along the "Van Mook line". Madiun uprising (September 1948) by the PKI crushed by Republican forces; Darul Islam revolt from May 1948. [source: INR]
- Second police action, Operation Kraai (Crow), 19 December 1948: Yogyakarta, the Republican capital, taken; Sukarno, Hatta and most ministers captured and exiled to Bangka; guerrilla war under General Sudirman followed. The offensive was "a diplomatic disaster"; US Secretary of State Dean Acheson pushed the Dutch to negotiate. [source: KRAAI; INR]
- Round Table Conference in The Hague, 23 August–2 November 1949; sovereignty transferred on 27 December 1949 to a federal "United States of Indonesia" (RUSI), excluding West New Guinea; Indonesia took on the Netherlands East Indies debt of 4.5 billion guilders — in effect paying for the "police actions". Sukarno proclaimed a unitary republic on 17 August 1950. [source: INR]
- Dutch violence: Westerling's South Sulawesi campaign (10 December 1946–21 February 1947) used summary executions (the "Westerling method"); Dutch historian Willem IJzereef estimates about 1,500 killed by Westerling's unit and another ~1,500 by other units (infobox: 3,100–3,500 civilians killed by Dutch troops); the Republic claimed 40,000 (Makassar monument "Korban 40,000 Jiwa"), which Dutch historians call inflated. Give as "several thousand, by Dutch scholarly estimates; Indonesia commemorates 40,000". [source: SULA]
- Rawagede, 9 December 1947: Dutch troops killed almost all the men of the village, 431 by most estimates, for refusing to reveal a guerrilla's whereabouts. A Dutch court ruled on 14 September 2011 that the state was liable; widows received €20,000 each; the Dutch ambassador apologised on 9 December 2011. [source: RAWA]
- Death toll: Indonesian deaths (fighters and civilians) estimated 97,421–100,000 (some estimates up to 250,000); 4,585–5,281 Dutch dead in older figures (INR text: "more than 4,000 Dutch soldiers"); 980 British soldiers killed or missing, mostly Indian; 7 million displaced on Java and Sumatra. [source: INR]
- 2022 Dutch review (KITLV, NIMH and NIOD), *Independence, Decolonization, Violence and War in Indonesia, 1945–1950*, released 17 February 2022: "The use of extreme violence by the Dutch armed forces was not only widespread, but often deliberate, too" and "was condoned at every level: political, military and legal." Prime Minister Mark Rutte apologised the same day. Earlier apologies: 2013 (government), 2016 (Koenders, Rawagede), March 2020 (King Willem-Alexander, for "excessive violence"). [source: INR; https://thediplomat.com/2022/02/dutch-pm-apologizes-for-atrocities-committed-during-indonesian-independence-war]
- Rutte, 17 February 2022: "I make a deep apology to the people of Indonesia today. For the systematic and widespread extreme violence by the Dutch side in those years and the consistent looking away by previous cabinets." (wording via a news report; treat as reported translation) [source: https://thediplomat.com/2022/02/dutch-pm-apologizes-for-atrocities-committed-during-indonesian-independence-war]
- Cold War angle: when the Republic crushed the Madiun communist revolt, Washington saw it as an anti-communist ally; US pressure forced Dutch withdrawal. [source: DEC; INR]

### Indochina (brief — Vietnam chapter exists)
- First Indochina War: 19 December 1946 – 11 August 1954 (FIW infobox); Viet Minh had declared independence in September 1945 (DEC; the exact date 2 September 1945 is standard but NOT confirmed in text read — say "September 1945"). Fighting in Haiphong (November 1946) preceded war. From 1950 China and the USSR recognised Ho's DRV and the US the State of Vietnam: the colonial war became a Cold War war. [source: FIW; DEC]
- Total deaths: 400,000–842,707 soldiers and 125,000–400,000 civilians (FIW). [source: FIW]
- Dien Bien Phu: siege 13 March–7 May 1954; Võ Nguyên Giáp's Viet Minh hauled artillery up the surrounding heights; on 8 May the Viet Minh counted 11,721 prisoners (4,436 wounded); only 3,290 were repatriated four months later; thousands of porters (including Hmong) carried supplies. The Geneva Conference opened 8 May 1954; the accords (July 1954) divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel pending elections in 1956 that were never held. [source: DBP; FIW]
- Dien Bien Phu "brought a profound psychological shock to France" and its significance for colonised peoples is often stressed (Algeria's FLN rose six months later, 1 November 1954 — juxtaposition is my inference; dates verified). [source: DBP; ALG]

### Malaya (the Emergency, 1948–60)
- Emergency declared after three European plantation managers were killed at Sungai Siput (MAL text: 17 June 1948; infobox start date 16 June 1948 — say "June 1948"); it lasted until 31 July 1960. The insurgents were the Malayan Communist Party's Malayan National Liberation Army (formed 1 February 1949), led by Chin Peng, largely veterans of the wartime anti-Japanese MPAJA that Britain had helped arm; almost 90% of MNLA fighters were ethnic Chinese. Communists call it the "Anti-British National Liberation War". [source: MAL]
- Malaya's rubber and tin earned dollars: Malayan rubber exports to the US were worth more than all of Britain's domestic exports to America. [source: MAL]
- Briggs Plan (from April 1950): about one million rural people (roughly 10% of Malaya's population, MAL) forcibly resettled into about 600 guarded camps called "New Villages"; collective punishments (e.g. Tanjong Malim, March 1952, a 22-hour curfew and reduced rice rations for 20,000 people under General Templer); herbicide spraying of crops. The "one million" and "600" figures are MAL's; other works give lower figures (around half a million) — NOT VERIFIED here; write "hundreds of thousands, by some counts a million". [source: MAL]
- Casualties: security forces 1,346 Malayan and 1,443 British killed (plus Australians, New Zealanders); 6,710 guerrillas killed, 226 executed; 2,478 civilians killed and 810 missing. [source: MAL]
- Batang Kali, 12 December 1948: Scots Guards shot 24 unarmed men at a rubber estate in Selangor; one survivor, Chong Hong. Retrospective Regulation 27A was introduced; British courts (High Court 4 September 2012, later appeals) declined to order a public inquiry while accepting the killings occurred. [source: BKM]
- Malaya became independent on 31 August 1957 (MAL: "declaration of Malaya's independence in August 1957"; Tunku Abdul Rahman). The Emergency outlived independence by three years. [source: MAL]

### The Philippines
- The US recognised Philippine independence on 4 July 1946 (Treaty of Manila, Proclamation 2695), as scheduled by the Tydings–McDuffie Act of 1934 (Commonwealth from 1935, President Manuel Quezon). [source: TOM; DEC]
- Independence was qualified: the US kept dozens of military bases (Military Bases Agreement); the Bell Trade Act, a condition of war-rehabilitation aid, pegged the peso to the dollar, gave preferential tariffs and "parity rights" giving Americans equal rights to Philippine natural resources, requiring a constitutional amendment approved by plebiscite in 1947; critics called parity "an inexcusable surrender of national sovereignty". Revised by the Laurel–Langley Agreement, 1955. [source: TOM; BELL]
- Hukbalahap rebellion, 4 July 1946 – 17 May 1954: the communist-led peasant army that had fought Japan rose after its elected members were denied seats in Congress; rooted in Central Luzon tenant grievances; defeated under Ramon Magsaysay with US adviser Edward Lansdale, by concessions and force; ~9,700 Huk killed, ~1,600 government troops (Philippine army figures). [source: HUK]

### India, Burma, Ceylon, Palestine (brief — Partition chapter)
- India and Pakistan independent 14–15 August 1947; Partition deaths "range from 200,000 to 2 million" (Talbot and Singh); 12–20 million displaced. [source: PART]
- Burma and Ceylon independent 1948; British Mandate in Palestine ended May 1948. [source: DAS]
- Operation Legacy precedent: overt burning of documents in New Delhi in 1946 embarrassed Britain. [source: LEG]

## 3. Africa
### The Gold Coast and Ghana (1957)
- Background grievances, 1948: postwar inflation and a boycott of shops from January 1948; cocoa farmers angry that colonial officials destroyed trees with swollen-shoot virus; about 63,000 Second World War veterans, many jobless. On 28 February 1948 police shot dead three veterans on a petition march, setting off the Accra riots across the colony. Nkrumah's biographer David Birmingham: West Africa's "model colony" saw riot and looting — "The African Revolution had begun." [source: NKR]
- The UGCC (founded August 1947, a party of professionals) hired Nkrumah as secretary; after the riots the British arrested the "Big Six", including Nkrumah and J. B. Danquah. Nkrumah founded the Convention People's Party on 12 June 1949, a mass party of the young, ex-servicemen and market women ("Self-Government Now"; slogan "Forward Ever, Backward Never"). [source: NKR]
- "Positive Action" general strike from 8 January 1950; Nkrumah jailed 22 January 1950 (three-year sentence). In the February 1951 election — the first under universal franchise in colonial Africa — the CPP won 34 of 38 party-contested seats; Nkrumah, elected from prison, was released 12 February 1951 and asked by Governor Arden-Clarke to form a government (Leader of Government Business; prime minister from 1952). [source: NKR]
- Opposition (the National Liberation Movement, with Ashanti chiefs and the north) wanted a federal state; independence came as a unitary state with five regions. [source: NKR]
- Independence: 6 March 1957, as the Dominion of Ghana — "the first of Britain's African colonies to gain majority-rule independence"; Nixon, Ralph Bunche and the Duchess of Kent attended. Republic from 1960 (Nkrumah elected president 27 April 1960). [source: NKR; YOA] (Writer: Sudan, independent 1956, came earlier in sub-Saharan terms; say "the first sub-Saharan colony to win independence from Britain under majority rule" or "the first in British tropical Africa" — NKR's own wording is "first of Britain's African colonies to gain majority-rule independence".)
- Nkrumah then: a one-party state from 1964 (constitutional amendment), "President for life"; repression of opposition. Volta River Project (Akosombo Dam), built with Kaiser Aluminum, which restricted use of the power; opened 22 January 1966. Cocoa fell from £250 a ton (1960) to £91 (August 1965). Overthrown by the military and police (National Liberation Council, led by Joseph Ankrah) in February 1966 (term ended 24 February 1966) while abroad on the way to Hanoi. Died 27 April 1972 in Bucharest. [source: NKR]
- Pan-African role: founding member of the Non-Aligned Movement (Belgrade 1961), Casablanca Group, OAU (chair 1965–66); hosted Cabral's training camps in Ghana. [source: NKR; CABRAL]

### Guinea's "No" (1958) and French Africa
- In de Gaulle's referendum of 28 September 1958, all French African territories voted "Yes" to the French Community except Guinea, led by Ahmed Sékou Touré; Guinea became independent on 2 October 1958. France cut aid, expelled Guinea from the franc zone, and destabilised it (Opération Persil, flooding it with counterfeit notes). [source: FRA; TOURE]
- The rest of French sub-Saharan Africa became independent in 1960 while keeping close ties: Gabon's president Léon M'ba: "Gabon is independent, but between Gabon and France nothing has changed; everything goes on as before" (as quoted by Fanon). [source: WRE; YOA]
- Madagascar: the Malagasy Uprising, 29 March 1947 – February 1949, crushed; Malagasy dead estimated 11,342–89,000 (WP infobox "average estimate of 30,000 to 40,000"; DEC says 11,000–40,000); 590 French soldiers and 240 French civilians killed. [source: MAD; DEC]
- Cameroon's UPC insurgency is NOT covered by sources read (omit or verify).

### Algeria (1954–62)
- Legal status: Algeria was treated as an integral part of France (départements), with about a million European settlers (*pieds-noirs*): 1,025,000 in 1959, 10.4% of the population (85% of European Christian descent, 15% Jewish). [source: ALG]
- Prelude: Sétif and Guelma, from 8 May 1945 (VE Day): after police fired on a demonstration, Algerians killed 102 settlers; French forces and settler militias then killed thousands. Estimates: 1,020 (official Tubert report); 3,000–8,000 (French historians Faivre, Cochet, Pervillé, Vétillard); 5,000–6,000 (Ageron; Peyroulou); 45,000 (Radio Cairo at the time; the figure is widely cited in Algeria — that last point NOT VERIFIED). ALG gives 6,000–30,000 elsewhere. In February 2005 France's ambassador called it an "inexcusable tragedy". [source: SETIF; ALG]
- War began with the FLN's coordinated attacks of 1 November 1954 (*Toussaint Rouge*, "Red All Saints' Day") and the FLN's Cairo-broadcast declaration calling for the "restoration of the Algerian state – sovereign, democratic and social". The FLN eliminated the rival MNA of veteran nationalist Messali Hadj. [source: ALG]
- Philippeville (Skikda), August 1955: FLN massacre of pied-noir civilians and savage reprisals marked escalation. [source: ALG]
- By 1956 more than 400,000 French troops were in Algeria (peak strength 470,000; some estimates 700,000 counting auxiliaries); 1.5 million French men mobilised in all. [source: ALG]
- Battle of Algiers, from 30 September 1956: women including Djamila Bouhired and Zohra Drif planted bombs (e.g., the Milk Bar café); General Jacques Massu's paratroopers broke the general strike and the FLN network by systematic torture. [source: ALG]
- Torture: historian Pierre Vidal-Naquet estimated "hundreds of thousands of instances of torture"; methods included the *gégène* (electric generator); Henri Alleg's *La Question* (1958, sold 60,000 copies in a day) and Maurice Audin's death under torture (June 1957) made it public. Audin, a mathematician and communist, disappeared in army custody. [source: TORT; AUDIN]
- Regroupement: over 2 million Algerians removed from their villages into army-controlled camps (1957–60); over 8,000 villages destroyed (ALG lead). [source: ALG]
- Crisis of 13 May 1958 in Algiers brought de Gaulle back and created the Fifth Republic; the FLN formed a government-in-exile (GPRA) in Tunis, 1958. De Gaulle accepted the principle of self-determination on 16 September 1959. Settler "week of barricades" January 1960; first self-determination referendum 8 January 1961 (75% yes; 69.51% in the Algerian départements). OAS settler-army terrorism from 1961. [source: ALG; EVIAN]
- Évian Accords signed 18 March 1962 (ceasefire 19 March); French referendum 8 April 1962 approved (~91%; 17,866,423 to 1,809,074); Algerian referendum 1 July 1962: 5,975,581 (EVIAN) or 5,992,115 (ALG) for independence against 16,534 — figures differ slightly; independence declared 5 July 1962 (de Gaulle pronounced it on 3 July). France kept Mers-el-Kébir for 15 years (left 1967) and Saharan facilities. [source: EVIAN; ALG]
- Deaths (give as ranges; all contested): Algerian deaths between about 400,000 and 1.5 million — French historians ~350,000–400,000 (some up to 700,000), Alistair Horne ~1 million, Algerian official figure 1.5 million (the "million and a half martyrs"); 12,000+ in internal FLN purges; French sources estimate 70,000 Muslim civilians killed or abducted by the FLN. French military dead ~23,000–25,600 (ALG gives 23,196 in the infobox, 25,600 in the lead, and "nearly 17,456" as French military authorities' figure elsewhere — say "about 25,000"); ~6,000 European civilians. [source: ALG] (Consistent with the 20th-century overview's "hundreds of thousands to over a million".)
- Exodus: about 900,000 pieds-noirs fled in a few months of 1962 (ALG); "within a year, 1.4 million refugees, including almost the entire Jewish community" (ALG, a broader figure) — say "close to a million Europeans left within months". The French government had expected only 250,000–300,000. [source: ALG]
- Harkis (Muslim auxiliaries): by 1960 about 150,000 Muslim Algerians served France, including 95,000 harkis; about 90,000 harkis and family members reached France, against government orders, and were kept in internment camps such as Rivesaltes; in Algeria the FLN or lynch mobs killed "at least 30,000 and possibly as many as 150,000" harkis and dependants (French historians' estimates; ALG elsewhere 50,000–150,000). [source: HARKI; ALG]
- Memory: France officially called it "operations to maintain order" until a law of (10 August) 1999 recognised it as a war; amnesties in 1962 (decree), 1968 and 1982 covered war crimes. In 2000 Louisette Ighilahriz's testimony in *Le Monde* (20 June 2000) and General Aussaresses's 2001 book, admitting torture and 24 executions on government orders, reopened the issue. Massu told *Le Monde* in 2000 that "torture was not necessary and that we could have decided not to use it" (paraphrase in quotation marks in TORT; treat as reported). [source: TORT]
- Macron, 13 September 2018: recognised the French state's responsibility for Audin's death, saying he died under torture "stemming from the system instigated while Algeria was part of France" (Élysée statement as reported in English by AP/Al Jazeera). Benjamin Stora's report on "memories of colonization and the Algerian War" delivered January 2021; in September 2021 Macron asked for "forgiveness" for France's abandonment of the harkis. [source: https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2018/9/13/france-admits-torture-during-algerias-war-of-independence; HARKI]
- Fanon: born Fort-de-France, Martinique, 20 July 1925; served in the Free French army and was disillusioned by its racism; *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952); head of service at the Blida-Joinville psychiatric hospital in Algeria from 1953 until expelled in January 1957; joined the FLN (contact from 1955); dictated *The Wretched of the Earth* while dying of leukaemia; died 6 December 1961 at the NIH in Bethesda, Maryland. [source: FANON]
- *The Wretched of the Earth* (*Les Damnés de la Terre*), François Maspero, 1961, with a preface by Jean-Paul Sartre (removed from later editions by Fanon's widow Josie after 1967); English translations by Constance Farrington (1963) and Richard Philcox (2004). It argues decolonisation is necessarily violent because colonial rule rests on force; it warns of the "national bourgeoisie" and neo-colonialism after independence; its chapter "On National Culture" ties culture to resistance. Scholars argue mistranslation has made Fanon look like an advocate of violence; "a dialectical opponent of nonviolence" is one characterisation. [source: WRE; FANON]

### Kenya (brief; align with British Empire chapter)
- State of Emergency declared 20 October 1952 (Operation Jock Scott arrested Kenyatta and ~180 others); main conflict to the capture of Dedan Kimathi on 21 October 1956; Kimathi executed. Kenyatta's trial (1953) was flawed; British intelligence itself found no evidence he directed Mau Mau. Independence 12 December 1963 (date: DEC lists 1963; 12 December is the standard date — NOT VERIFIED in text read; use "December 1963"). [source: KEN; DEC]
- Figures (identical to the British Empire chapter): officially 80,000 detained, Elkins 160,000–320,000; 1,090 executed ("at no other time or place in the British Empire was capital punishment dispensed so aggressively"); Mau Mau dead 12,000–20,000+ (Anderson); total deaths ~25,000 (Anderson) or 50,000 (demographer John Blacker, half of them children aged ten or under); "as many Kikuyu fought against Mau Mau ... as joined them" — Kenyatta called it a civil war. [source: KEN]
- Hola camp, 3 March 1959: 11 detainees clubbed to death by guards; Enoch Powell condemned it in the Commons. [source: HOLA; KEN]
- 6 June 2013: Foreign Secretary William Hague announced a settlement for 5,228 claimants worth £19.9 million; a memorial in Uhuru Park, Nairobi, unveiled 12 September 2015. [source: KEN]

### Belgian Congo (brief; Global Cold War chapter has Lumumba)
- Léopoldville riots, 4 January 1959: at least 49 killed, perhaps as many as 500 casualties. Round Table Conference in Brussels, January 1960; independence 30 June 1960 "for a country bitterly and deeply divided"; "no serious planning for independence, and exceedingly little training or education provided". [source: CONGO; DEC]
- Lt-Gen. Émile Janssens told Congolese NCOs on 5 July 1960 that nothing would change, writing "Before Independence = After Independence"; the Force Publique mutinied. Congo Crisis 1960–65: Katanga and South Kasai secessions backed by Belgium (and others); UN operation (ONUC) with troops from Ghana, Tunisia, Morocco, Ethiopia, India, Indonesia and others; Hammarskjöld killed in the 1961 Ndola crash; c. 100,000 killed; ended with Mobutu. [source: CONGO]

### Portuguese Africa (1961–75)
- Portugal under Salazar's Estado Novo (1933–74) treated its colonies as "overseas provinces" and was determined to keep them; Salazar's regime cited the old maxim that any African could in theory become a Portuguese citizen ("Lusotropicalism" — term NOT VERIFIED in text read). Before 1974 Portugal sent 300,000 more European settlers to Angola and Mozambique (DEC). [source: PCW; DEC]
- The Portuguese Colonial War (*Guerra do Ultramar* in Portugal; *Guerra de Libertação* in the former colonies) ran from 4 February 1961 (Luanda attack on the São Paulo prison by MPLA militants) to 25 April 1974. Preceded by the Baixa de Cassanje revolt of Cotonang cotton workers (January 1961); on 15 March 1961 Holden Roberto's UPA (later FNLA) attacked in the north with mass killings of settlers and Africans; Portugal's counteroffensive drove ~150,000 Bakongo refugees into Congo. [source: PCW]
- Scale: 1.4 million Portuguese men mobilised in total; African soldiers rose from 18% of Portuguese forces in Africa (1961) to over 50% (1974); by the 1970s Portugal spent 40% of its annual budget on the wars. Portuguese military 8,831 killed; guerrillas 41,000–46,000 killed; civilians 70,000–110,000 killed (infobox). [source: PCW]
- Guinea-Bissau: PAIGC founded 1956; war from 23 January 1963. Amílcar Cabral (born Bafatá 12 September 1924 to Cape Verdean parents; trained as an agronomist in Lisbon; conducted a colonial agricultural census from 1953, travelling some 60,000 km) built "liberated zones" with a roving hospital, a mobile barter-bazaar selling staples below colonial prices, and people's courts; insisted on women's participation (women's union UDEMU 1961; at least two of five village-council members women). He was assassinated in Conakry on 20 January 1973 (PIDE involvement suspected; US found "Lisbon's complicity" in some sense but no direct involvement). [source: CABRAL; GBW]
- PAIGC's People's National Assembly unilaterally declared the Republic of Guinea-Bissau in September 1973, recognised by several states (Romania first, per PCW); Portugal recognised independence in September 1974 (war end 10 September 1974), Cape Verde a year later. PAIGC was "the first sub-Saharan African liberation movement to achieve independence ... through armed struggle". After the war 7,447 African former Portuguese soldiers were executed by the PAIGC. [source: GBW; PCW]
- Mozambique: FRELIMO founded in Dar es Salaam 25 June 1962 under Eduardo Mondlane; war from 25 September 1964; Mondlane killed by a parcel bomb in Dar es Salaam, 3 February 1969 (PIDE widely blamed); Samora Machel succeeded. Wiriyamu massacre, 16 December 1972 (Operation Marosca, Tete): estimates 150 (Red Cross) to 300 (Portuguese *Expresso* investigation from soldiers' testimony); the priest Adrian Hastings reported about 400 (WIR doubts this figure). In September 2022 Prime Minister António Costa called it "an unforgivable act that dishonours Portuguese history". Ceasefire 8 September 1974 (Lusaka Accord); independence 25 June 1975. [source: MOZ; WIR]
- Angola: MPLA (1956, Agostinho Neto), FNLA (UPA 1954, Holden Roberto), UNITA (Jonas Savimbi) fought Portugal and each other; Alvor Agreement 15 January 1975; independence 11 November 1975, straight into civil war (Cold War chapter). [source: ANG; PCW]
- The Carnation Revolution, 25 April 1974: a military coup by officers radicalised by the wars (after Caetano dismissed General Spínola for advocating negotiation) overthrew the Estado Novo; independence followed for all the African colonies by 1975; hundreds of thousands of *retornados* returned to Portugal. India had annexed Goa in 1961; East Timor was invaded by Indonesia on 7 December 1975 and occupied until 1999 (independent 2002). [source: PCW; DEC; DAS]
- Portugal's empire formally ended with Macau's handover in 1999. [source: DEC]

## 4. Southern Africa
- Southern Rhodesia had been a self-governing British colony since 1923; its white minority was about 5% of the population. On 11 November 1965 Ian Smith's Rhodesian Front cabinet declared UDI, rejecting the British condition that independence terms be acceptable "to the people of the country as a whole". [source: UDI]
- Rhodesian Bush War (Second *Chimurenga*), July 1964 – December 1979: ZANU's ZANLA (Mugabe) and ZAPU's ZIPRA (Nkomo) against Rhodesian forces (with Portuguese help until 1974); about 20,000 killed overall (Rhodesian forces 2,616; guerrillas c. 10,000). Smith–Muzorewa Internal Settlement 1978; "Zimbabwe Rhodesia" 1979. Lancaster House Agreement signed 21 December 1979; elections February 1980; independent Zimbabwe 1980 (18 April 1980 standard date — NOT VERIFIED in text read; British Empire chapter says 1980). The land question was effectively deferred; Mugabe's forced land acquisitions began 1998–2000. [source: RBW; LHA]
- Macmillan's "Wind of Change" speech to South Africa's parliament, Cape Town, 3 February 1960 (first given in Accra, 10 January 1960); it signalled that Britain would no longer impede independence and distanced Britain from apartheid. [source: WOC]
- Namibia (South West Africa) under South African rule became independent on 21 March 1990. [source: DAF]
- Apartheid: brief only; South Africa abstained on Resolution 1514; cross-refer to the apartheid chapter.

## 5. Settler colonies and why they resisted
- Pattern (synthesis for the writer, built from the facts above): the longest, bloodiest wars of decolonisation in Africa came where European settlers held land and political power — Algeria (~1 million pieds-noirs, legally part of France), Kenya (the White Highlands; British Empire chapter), Rhodesia (5% white minority, self-governing since 1923, UDI 1965), Angola and Mozambique (Portugal sent 300,000 more settlers before 1974), and South Africa and Namibia. Settlers had votes, lobbies and armed militias (OAS in Algeria; Rhodesian Front), and colonial states feared their revolt as much as nationalist revolt (Algiers May 1958 and January 1960; OAS terror 1961–62). [source: ALG; UDI; DEC; PCW]
- Compare Fanon's description of the colony as two "reciprocally exclusive" zones of settler and native. [source: WRE]
- Settler exits: ~900,000 pieds-noirs (1962); hundreds of thousands of Portuguese *retornados* (1974–76); 300,000+ ethnic Dutch expelled from Indonesia in the later 1950s under Sukarno (DEC). [source: ALG; PCW; DEC]
- The Atlantic Charter rhetoric of 1941 and the UN's 1960 declaration clashed with settler claims of "kith and kin" — the phrase is NOT VERIFIED in sources read; do not use.

## 6. Suez (1956), Bandung and non-alignment (brief; Cold War chapter has more)
- Nasser announced nationalisation of the Suez Canal Company in a speech at Alexandria on 26 July 1956. Britain, France and Israel planned the attack secretly at Sèvres (22–24 October 1956): Israel invaded on 29 October; Britain and France joined on 31 October. France's motive included Egyptian support for the FLN. US financial pressure (threatened sale of sterling bonds; Britain's reserves could not sustain the pound) and UN action forced withdrawal; a UN Emergency Force was created (Lester Pearson won the Nobel Peace Prize). Egyptian military dead 1,650–3,000; ~1,000 civilians. "The crisis demonstrated that the United Kingdom and France could no longer implement independent foreign policies without U.S. consent" (SUEZ, paraphrasing historians). In Arabic it is the "Tripartite Aggression". [source: SUEZ]
- Bandung, 18–24 April 1955 (details in Cold War chapter; BAND = https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandung_Conference confirms the dates and that Sukarno and Nehru were key organisers, and that delegates debated whether to condemn Soviet policies in Eastern Europe and Central Asia alongside Western colonialism). [source: BAND]
- Non-Aligned Movement founded at Belgrade, 1 September 1961; now 120 member states. [source: NAM]
- OAU founded 25 May 1963 in Addis Ababa by 32 independent states (OAU article also says 33 signatory governments); aimed to eradicate colonialism and white minority rule; succeeded by the African Union in 2002. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organisation_of_African_Unity]
- Kenya detail: MI5's Director General, Sir Percy Sillitoe, wrote to Governor Evelyn Baring on 9 January 1953: "Our sources have produced nothing to indicate that Kenyatta, or his associates in the UK, are directly involved in Mau Mau activities, or that Kenyatta is essential to Mau Mau as a leader, or that he is in a position to direct its activities." [source: KEN]

## 7. The Caribbean and the Pacific
- West Indies Federation, 3 January 1958 – 31 May 1962 (capital planned at Chaguaramas, Trinidad), meant to become independent as one state; Jamaica voted 54% to leave in a referendum in September 1961; Trinidad's Eric Williams then chose separate independence. The territories became nine states; Anguilla, Montserrat, Cayman and Turks and Caicos stayed British overseas territories. [source: WIF]
- Jamaica independent 6 August 1962; Trinidad and Tobago 31 August 1962 (Eric Williams, historian of *Capitalism and Slavery*, 1944, first prime minister; he had led it to majority rule in 1956); Barbados independent 30 November 1966, a republic from 30 November 2021; Suriname independent from the Netherlands 25 November 1975 (constituent country of the Kingdom from 15 December 1954). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaica; EW; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbados; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suriname]
- Caribbean thinkers shaped anticolonialism worldwide: Fanon (Martinique); Aimé Césaire's *Discourse on Colonialism* (1950; revised 1955 in *Présence Africaine*) argued that Nazism was the "boomerang effect" of colonial violence returning to Europe ("And then one fine day the bourgeoisie is awakened by a terrific boomerang effect"); George Padmore (Trinidad) co-organised Manchester 1945; Walter Rodney (Guyana), *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* (1972, Bogle-L'Ouverture). [source: CES; NKR; RODNEY]
- Getachew treats the West Indian Federation (with the Union of African States) as a "worldmaking" federal project to secure non-domination for small states. [source: https://ethicsandinternationalaffairs.org/2019/worldmaking-after-empire-the-rise-and-fall-of-self-determination (search summary)]
- Pacific: Western Samoa independent from New Zealand on 1 January 1962 (as the Independent State of Western Samoa); Fiji 10 October 1970 (Indo-Fijians, descendants largely of indentured labourers, 37.5% today; military coups from 1987); Papua New Guinea from Australia 16 September 1975; Vanuatu (the Anglo-French condominium of the New Hebrides since 1906) 30 July 1980. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samoa; FIJI; PNG; VAN]
- New Caledonia: the Nouméa Accord (signed 5 May 1998; approved by referendum 8 November 1998) promised the Kanaks a 20-year transition; referendums in 2018 and 2020 chose to remain French; the third, 12 December 2021, was boycotted by pro-independence parties after France refused a postponement (Kanak communities hit hard by COVID deaths): 96% against independence on about 44% turnout (86% in 2020). [source: NOU; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_New_Caledonian_independence_referendum]
- The 17 remaining UN non-self-governing territories include Western Sahara (no referendum held despite a UN mission since 1991), New Caledonia, French Polynesia, Guam, American Samoa, Tokelau (referendums 2006 and 2007 narrowly short of the two-thirds required), the Falklands and Gibraltar. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_list_of_non-self-governing_territories]

## 8. Economic decolonisation and neocolonialism
- Kwame Nkrumah, *Neo-Colonialism, the Last Stage of Imperialism* (1965; London: Thomas Nelson; US edition International Publishers 1966). Its introduction: "The essence of neo-colonialism is that the State which is subject to it is, in theory, independent and has all the outward trappings of international sovereignty. In reality its economic system and thus its political policy is directed from outside." The title echoes Lenin's *Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism* (connection NOT VERIFIED in sources read — do not assert). [source: NKR; https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/node/1324783 (search summary); https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/26th-november-1965/29/redeemers-folly]
- Term: NEO says the word was coined by Jean-Paul Sartre in 1956 and first applied to decolonising Africa by Nkrumah, and also that Nkrumah "is credited with coining" it; it appears in the 1963 preamble of the OAU Charter. Use "popularised". [source: NEO]
- Contemporary critic: Brian Crozier in *The Spectator* (26 November 1965) argued that "neo-colonialism", insofar as it exists, "is not a system but a state of affairs" (search summary of the review; treat as paraphrase-plus-quotation). [source: https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/26th-november-1965/29/redeemers-folly]
- New International Economic Order: UN General Assembly Declaration and Programme of Action adopted 1 May 1974 (the Sixth Special Session — session name NOT VERIFIED in text read); the existing order "was established at a time when most of the developing countries did not even exist as independent states and which perpetuates inequality"; "trade not aid"; it claimed the prevailing order gave only 30% of world income to the 70% of humanity in the developing world. Principles: sovereign equality; full permanent sovereignty over natural resources and regulation of multinationals; fair terms of trade between raw materials and manufactures; aid and technology transfer. A Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States followed "a few months later" (December 1974). UNCTAD (1964) had tried to promote it; OPEC's 1973–74 price rises gave it leverage; the 1980s debt crisis and the Washington Consensus sank most of it, though the Common Fund for Commodities came into force in 1989 and the "Right to Development" (1986) drew on it. [source: NIEO; DEC]
- Ghana's own case: cocoa price collapse (£250 a ton in 1960 to £91 in August 1965) and the Volta dam built on terms set by Kaiser Aluminum. [source: NKR]
- CFA franc: created 26 December 1945 ("Colonies françaises d'Afrique"); now two currencies used in 14 countries (8 West, 6 Central African); pegged to the euro at 655.957 with a French Treasury guarantee; members had to deposit half their foreign reserves with the French Treasury (requirement later dropped for West Africa); devalued 50% overnight on 12 January 1994; on 22 December 2019 a reform to an "Eco" was announced and in May 2020 the French National Assembly voted to end French involvement in the West African CFA franc's reserve arrangements. [source: CFA]
- Françafrique: France's sphere of influence in its former sub-Saharan colonies, run through the Élysée's "African cell", Jacques Foccart (Secretary-General for African and Malagasy Affairs under de Gaulle and Pompidou), business networks (Elf) and defence agreements with secret clauses allowing intervention. First known use: a 1945 editorial by Jean Piot in *L'Aurore* — often wrongly attributed to Félix Houphouët-Boigny (1955); François-Xavier Verschave's book *La Françafrique: le plus long scandale de la République* (1998) made it a term of criticism (pun: "France à fric"). In the 2020s the juntas of Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger ended military agreements with France and dropped French as an official language. [source: FRA]
- Commonwealth: London Declaration 1949 let republics (India) stay; 56 members today, including Mozambique, Rwanda, Gabon and Togo, which Britain never ruled. [source: COMM; British Empire chapter]
- Rodney (1972) argued that Europe and Africa were bound in one system in which Africa "developed Europe at the same rate that Europe underdeveloped Africa" (RODNEY summary — paraphrase, not a verified quotation). [source: RODNEY]

## 9. Archives, memory and apology
- Operation Legacy: from the 1950s to the 1970s the Colonial Office (later Foreign Office) had files that could "embarrass" Britain destroyed or removed from at least 23 territories before independence; some 8,800 files were secretly stored in Britain (Hanslope Park); Iain Macleod's telegram of 3 May 1961 gave instructions; "destruction certificates" were sent to London. After admitting in 2011 that it held Mau Mau papers, the UK declassified some 20,000 files (the "migrated archives", FCO 141) by November 2013. [source: LEG; BKM]
- Apologies and acknowledgements: Netherlands 2013, 2016, 2020, 2022 (Indonesia); France 2005 (Sétif, ambassador), 2018 (Audin), 2021 (harkis); Britain 2013 (Kenya settlement, "sincerely regrets"); Belgium 2002 (Lumumba, Cold War chapter); Portugal 2022 (Wiriyamu, Costa). [sources as above]

## 10. Is decolonisation complete? Chagos and beyond
- Chagos: Britain detached the Chagos Archipelago from Mauritius in 1965 (British Indian Ocean Territory created 8 November 1965) as a condition of Mauritian independence; Harold Wilson told Premier Seewoosagur Ramgoolam on 23 September 1965 that he "could return to Mauritius either with Independence or without it". UNGA Resolution 2066 (XX) (16 December 1965) said detachment would contravene Resolution 1514. Mauritius independent 12 March 1968. The 1,500–1,750 Chagossians were expelled 1967–73 so the US could build the Diego Garcia base. [source: CHAG]
- ICJ advisory opinion, February 2019, by 13 votes to 1: the detachment "was not based on the free and genuine expression of the will of the people concerned", so Mauritius's decolonisation "had therefore not been lawfully completed", and Britain must end its administration "as rapidly as possible"; UNGA Resolution 73/295 adopted 116 to 6. [source: CHAG]
- UK–Mauritius agreement signed 22 May 2025: Mauritius sovereign over the whole archipelago; UK to exercise sovereign rights over Diego Garcia for an initial 99 years in return for annual payments. As of November 2026 the treaty had not entered into force: ratification stalled after the US position changed, the implementing bill lapsed in April 2026, and the British government said it was seeking to "recast" the arrangements. **Writer: do not say the Chagos have been returned.** (DAF's statement that Diego Garcia was "immediately returned" is garbled; prefer CHAG.) [source: CHAG]
- Other open cases: 17 UN non-self-governing territories; Western Sahara; New Caledonia (2021 boycott); Françafrique's contested end; CFA reform. [source: DEC; NSGT; FRA; CFA]
- Cultural decolonisation: Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, *Decolonising the Mind: The Politics of Language in African Literature* (1986), argued for writing in African languages (he founded the Gikuyu-language journal *Mũtĩri* in 1992; he had been detained without trial from December 1977 after his Gikuyu play *Ngaahika Ndeenda*). [source: NGUGI]

## Debates
- **"Transfer of power" versus struggle.** The official British framing is visible in the India Office document series edited by Nicholas Mansergh, *Constitutional Relations between Britain and India: The Transfer of Power, 1942–7* (12 vols, 1967–82), commissioned by Harold Wilson. Defenders of the record (e.g., a former British colonial official/diplomat on the Barder blog) call Britain's decolonisation "peaceful, orderly and consensual", with violence rarely caused by British refusal of independence. Historians from the colonised side, and Elkins, Anderson and Linstrum, stress emergencies, detention and torture (Malaya, Kenya; Cyprus is also cited) and that nationalists forced the pace (Accra 1948, Positive Action, Mau Mau). A *Journal of British Studies* review (Zaib un Nisa Aziz, 2024) calls the "peaceful" view a Western conception dominant c. 1970s–2000s that postcolonial societies never accepted. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Mansergh; https://barder.com/?p=3181; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-british-studies/article/erik-linstrum-the-age-of-emergency-living-with-violence-at-the-end-of-the-british-empire-oxford-oxford-university-press-2023-pp-328-3500-hard-cover/64E95283ED6301985B85826844D0262E (search summary)]
- **The British "end of empire" myth.** Linstrum (2023) argues the British public knew a good deal about colonial violence — the myth was not simple ignorance; officials managed information rather than wholly suppressing it (LRB review by Christienna Fryar; Irish Times review calls the peaceful-exit story "self-serving myth"). Operation Legacy's destruction of files (see §9) shows the record was curated. Philip Murphy (Institute of Commonwealth Studies) argues that a counter-myth exaggerates historians' past neglect of British atrocities in Kenya. Sarah Stockwell (2018) argues for a specifically "British end of empire": British institutions (Bank of England, Royal Mint, Sandhurst, universities) kept imperial roles after independence. [source: https://www.irishtimes.com/culture/books/review/2023/05/13/age-of-emergency-living-with-violence-at-the-end-of-the-british-empire-a-meticulous-innovative-damning-work; https://lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v46/n15/christienna-fryar/love-me-or-i-shoot-you; https://qz.com/africa/813445/british-brutality-against-kenyas-mau-mau-has-been-exaggerated-by-historians; https://newbooksnetwork.com/sarah-stockwell-the-british-end-of-the-british-empire-cambridge-up-2018]
- **Getachew's "worldmaking".** Decolonisation was not just nation-building but an attempt to remake the international order: a universal right to self-determination at the UN, regional federations (the Union of African States, the West Indies Federation) and a "welfare world" (the NIEO). Empire meant "unequal integration" into international society, not just exclusion; the projects ended up reinforcing the nation-state. Critics doubt her claim that nationalism and internationalism were fully compatible in practice. [source: https://ethicsandinternationalaffairs.org/2019/worldmaking-after-empire-the-rise-and-fall-of-self-determination; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adom_Getachew; https://politicsslashletters.org/commentary/what-was-decolonization-a-review-of-adom-getachews-worldmaking-after-empire/]
- **Cooper's "Africa since 1940" and the gatekeeper state.** Cooper treats 1940–present as one era: late-colonial "development" states and their successors were "gatekeeper states", strong at the interface with the world economy (customs, aid, export revenues) but weak inside — "Africa was systematically conquered but not so systematically ruled"; control of the gate became an "either/or" prize, which explains coups and one-party rule better than "tribalism". He said the phrase was "intended more to provoke discussion than to be learned". Critics note thin statistical evidence on revenue. Cooper's other work (*Citizenship between Empire and Nation*, 2014) shows French African leaders pursuing federal and citizenship-based alternatives to the nation-state — decolonisation's outcome was not inevitable. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gatekeeper_state; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Cooper_(historian); https://upjournals.up.ac.za/index.php/historia/article/download/1718/1608/6672 (search summary)]
- **Shepard's "invention of decolonization".** France presented losing Algeria as an inevitable step of history ("decolonisation"), avoiding responsibility and abandoning Algerians' French citizenship; the illegal, unexplained "amputation" shapes France's later racial and religious tensions. [source: https://history.jhu.edu/faculty-books/the-invention-of-decolonization-the-algerian-war-and-the-remaking-of-france]
- **Is decolonisation complete?** Arguments for "no": 17 UN non-self-governing territories; Western Sahara; the unratified Chagos treaty after the ICJ found Mauritius's decolonisation "not lawfully completed"; New Caledonia's boycotted 2021 vote; Françafrique and the CFA franc; Nkrumah's neocolonialism and Getachew's "unequal integration"; Ngũgĩ's call to decolonise language and minds; Indigenous peoples in settler states (DEC). Arguments for "largely yes": formal empire has almost vanished (UN from 51 to 193 states); Françafrique has been "progressively weakened" since the Cold War (FRA). [sources as cited in §§7–10]
- **Neocolonialism as analysis.** Nkrumah's thesis versus critics such as Brian Crozier (1965: "not a system but a state of affairs"); a later Ghanaian scholarly article calls the "last stage of imperialism" claim Nkrumah's error. [source: https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/26th-november-1965/29/redeemers-folly; https://journals.ug.edu.gh/index.php/cjas/article/download/1364/912 (search summary)]
- **Counting the dead in Indonesia and Algeria.** Republican/Algerian national figures (40,000 for South Sulawesi; 1.5 million for Algeria; 45,000 for Sétif) versus Dutch and French historians' lower estimates; the 2022 Dutch review and Westerling's own 1969 TV admission versus Bauke Geersing's "myth" thesis. [source: SULA; ALG; SETIF]
- **Fanon and violence.** Sartre's preface celebrated violence; scholars argue English mistranslations made Fanon look like an apostle of violence and that his warnings about the "national bourgeoisie" were the prophetic part. [source: WRE; FANON]
- **Kenya as anticolonial revolt or civil war.** Kenyatta called it a civil war; as many Kikuyu fought against Mau Mau as with it; Kenyan governments long suppressed Mau Mau memory; the ban on Mau Mau was lifted later (2003, per British Empire research — NOT re-verified here). [source: KEN]
- **The balance sheet of empire** (Ferguson vs Elkins): already in the British Empire chapter — cross-refer.
