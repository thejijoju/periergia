# Verified facts — Declarations of Independence: the American Revolution (researched 2026-11-11)

Source note: all facts below were checked against English Wikipedia raw wikitext fetched on 2026-11-11, plus a few WebSearch results for quotations and book details. W: = https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/

## Background: the Seven Years' War and its debts (1763–1764)
- Britain defeated France in the French and Indian War (1754–1763) and took New France east of the Mississippi. Britain fielded 45,000 soldiers in North America, half regulars and half colonial volunteers. Britain reimbursed about two-fifths of colonial war spending. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- After the war, Britain's national debt was £133 million. Annual debt payments were £5 million, out of an £8 million annual budget. Keeping troops permanently in North America would cost a further £360,000 a year. Grenville claimed Americans paid 1 shilling a year per head in taxes to the empire, against 26 shillings for the English. (An editors' footnote in the same article gives Lord North's version: 25 shillings against sixpence.) Treat both as contemporary political claims, not measured statistics. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- Pontiac's War (1763–1766) followed Native anger at General Jeffery Amherst's policies. George III issued the Royal Proclamation on 7 October 1763. It barred new settlement west of a line along the Appalachians and reserved that land for Native peoples. Settlers, fur traders and land speculators resented it, and it did not stop westward migration. [source: W:Royal_Proclamation_of_1763; W:American_Revolution]
- Sugar Act: Grenville proposed it on 9 March 1764 and Parliament passed it in April. It cut the duty on foreign molasses from 6d to 3d a gallon but tightened enforcement against smuggling. Violators could be tried in vice-admiralty courts, which had Crown-appointed judges and no juries. By the winter of 1764, nine colonial legislatures had petitioned for repeal. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- The New England molasses trade was tied to slavery. Molasses from the French West Indies was made into rum, and the rum was traded for enslaved Africans sold to the southern colonies and the West Indies. [source: W:American_Revolution]

## Parliamentary sovereignty vs colonial rights
- Since the Glorious Revolution of 1688, the British orthodoxy held that Parliament was the supreme authority throughout the empire. Colonists argued that the British constitution protected fundamental rights that no government could violate, and that they were not represented in Parliament. [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- Parliament argued that colonists had "virtual representation", like most Britons, since only a small minority of Britons could vote. James Otis and others replied that no member of Parliament answered to any colonial constituency. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- By 1774, Samuel Adams, James Wilson and Thomas Jefferson argued that Parliament was Britain's legislature only. In their view the colonies were tied to the empire only through allegiance to the Crown. The texts were Jefferson's *A Summary View of the Rights of British America* (1774), Wilson's *Considerations on the Authority of Parliament* (1774) and Adams's 1768 Circular Letter. [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- The colonists objected to lack of representation, not to the size of the taxes, which were low. This is often summed up in the slogan "No taxation without representation". [source: W:American_Revolution]

## Stamp Act crisis (1765–1766)
- The Stamp Act 1765 (5 Geo. 3 c. 12) received royal assent on 22 March 1765 and took effect on 1 November 1765. It was a direct tax: legal documents, newspapers, almanacs, pamphlets and even playing cards had to carry stamps, paid for in British currency. [source: W:Stamp_Act_1765; W:American_Revolution]
- Sons of Liberty groups formed in each colony. In Boston, crowds looted the home of Thomas Hutchinson and burned vice-admiralty court records. Nine colonies sent delegates to the Stamp Act Congress in New York in October 1765. Moderates led by John Dickinson wrote its Declaration of Rights and Grievances. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- The Commons voted for repeal on 21 February 1766, after Benjamin Franklin testified. Repeal took effect on 18 March 1766. The Declaratory Act, passed the same day, asserted Parliament's power to legislate for the colonies "in all cases whatsoever". British merchants hurt by colonial boycotts had pressed for repeal. CAVEAT: the lead of W:American_Revolution wrongly says the Stamp Act was "repealed in 1767". Use 1766. [source: W:Stamp_Act_1765; W:American_Revolution]

## Townshend duties, Boston Massacre, Tea Party, Coercive Acts (1767–1774)
- The Townshend Acts of 1767–68 are named after Chancellor of the Exchequer Charles Townshend. The Revenue Act (29 June 1767) put import duties on glass, lead, paints, paper and tea. Other acts set up a Board of Customs Commissioners in Boston. [source: W:Townshend_Acts]
- John Dickinson's *Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania* (1767–68) argued that the duties were unconstitutional because their purpose was to raise revenue, not regulate trade. Colonists answered with non-importation boycotts. [source: W:American_Revolution; W:Townshend_Acts]
- British troops were stationed in Boston from 1768. [source: W:Boston_Massacre]
- Boston Massacre, 5 March 1770: soldiers under Captain Thomas Preston fired into a crowd estimated at 300–400 that was throwing clubs, stones and snowballs. Three people died at once and two more died of their wounds, five deaths in all. John Adams defended the soldiers. Six soldiers were acquitted. Two (Montgomery and Kilroy) were convicted of manslaughter and branded on the thumb. CAVEAT: W:American_Revolution says the soldiers were "acquitted". The fuller account gives two manslaughter convictions. [source: W:Boston_Massacre]
- Crispus Attucks (c. 1723–1770) was a sailor of African and (most historians agree) Wampanoag descent. He is traditionally counted as the first person killed in the Massacre. Historians disagree on whether he was free or a fugitive from slavery. Eleven-year-old Christopher Seider had been shot by a customs employee on 22 February 1770. [source: W:Crispus_Attucks; W:Boston_Massacre]
- In 1770 Lord North's ministry repealed most of the Townshend duties but kept the duty on tea to assert Parliament's right to tax. [source: W:Townshend_Acts]
- The Tea Act (royal assent 10 May 1773) let the East India Company sell tea directly to the colonies through appointed consignees. This undercut both smugglers of Dutch tea and colonial merchants. The Company was in serious financial crisis in 1772. [source: W:Boston_Tea_Party]
- Boston Tea Party, 16 December 1773: about 5,000–7,000 people (Boston's population was about 16,000) gathered at Old South Meeting House. Men disguised as Native Americans then dumped 340 or 342 chests of tea into the harbour (sources differ on the count). The tea was worth about £10,000. The name "Boston Tea Party" came only decades later. [source: W:Boston_Tea_Party; W:American_Revolution]
- Coercive Acts, 1774. The Boston Port Act (31 March) closed the port until the tea was paid for. The Massachusetts Government Act (20 May) altered the charter and limited town meetings to one a year. The other two were the Administration of Justice Act and a Quartering Act. The Quebec Act, passed in the same session, was counted by colonists among them. The label "Intolerable Acts" dates only from the 19th century. [source: W:Intolerable_Acts]

## Continental Congresses, Lexington and Concord, Bunker Hill (1774–1775)
- The First Continental Congress met at Carpenters' Hall, Philadelphia, from 5 September to 26 October 1774. It had 56 delegates from 12 colonies; Georgia did not attend. It shelved Joseph Galloway's plan for a colonial parliament by a vote of 6 to 5. It also adopted a boycott of British goods from 1 December 1774 (the Continental Association), enforced by local committees. [source: W:First_Continental_Congress; W:American_Revolution]
- The Committees of Correspondence linked all 13 colonies. About 7,000–8,000 Patriots served on them. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- Lexington and Concord, 19 April 1775: at sunrise at Lexington, eight militiamen were killed and ten wounded, with one British soldier wounded. At about 11 a.m., about 400 militiamen engaged about 100 regulars at Concord's North Bridge. Casualties for the day: British 73 killed, 174 wounded, 53 missing; Patriots 49 killed, 39 wounded, 5 missing. [source: W:Battles_of_Lexington_and_Concord]
- Bunker Hill, 17 June 1775: most of the fighting was on Breed's Hill. The British took the redoubt on the third assault. British losses were 226 killed and 828 wounded; American losses were 138 killed, 305 wounded and 30 captured. Joseph Warren was killed. [source: W:Battle_of_Bunker_Hill]
- The Second Continental Congress first met on 10 May 1775. On 14 June 1775 it created the Continental Army, and the next day it unanimously named George Washington commander. On 6 July it approved the Declaration of the Causes and Necessity of Taking Up Arms, and on 8 July delegates signed the Olive Branch Petition. George III declared the colonies in rebellion on 23 August 1775. [source: W:Second_Continental_Congress]

## Common Sense and the road to independence (1776)
- *Common Sense* was a 47-page pamphlet published anonymously in Philadelphia by Robert Bell on 10 January 1776. Paine was 39 and had lived in America for 14 months. His name was not publicly linked to it until 30 March 1776. [source: W:Common_Sense]
- Sales figures are disputed. Paine claimed 120,000 copies in the first three months. Some historians say almost 100,000 in 1776. One biographer says 500,000 in the first year in America and Europe. Others call these figures implausible. It was often read aloud in taverns. [source: W:Common_Sense; W:American_Revolution]
- Paine wrote that Britain "hath stirred up the Indians and the Negroes to destroy us." [source: W:Common_Sense]
- John Adams attacked the "absurd democratical notions" in *Common Sense*. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- Pauline Maier identified more than 90 local and state "declarations of independence" issued from April to July 1776. One was North Carolina's Halifax Resolves (12 April 1776). [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- On 10 May 1776, Congress recommended that colonies form new governments; John Adams's more radical preamble followed on 15 May. New Hampshire adopted the first state constitution on 5 January 1776. [source: W:Second_Continental_Congress; W:American_Revolution]

## The Declaration of Independence
- Richard Henry Lee proposed the independence resolution on 7 June 1776, on instructions from the Virginia Convention of 15 May. Congress adopted it on 2 July 1776, with 12 colonies voting yes and New York abstaining. It declared the colonies "free and independent States". [source: W:Lee_Resolution]
- On 11 June 1776, Congress appointed the Committee of Five: John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, Thomas Jefferson, Robert R. Livingston and Roger Sherman. Jefferson drafted the text, largely between 11 and 28 June. It was presented to Congress on 28 June. Congress cut it by about a fourth, and Jefferson later complained that Congress had "mangled" his draft. [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- Franklin may have changed "sacred and undeniable" to "self-evident". The wording "may have" is the source's own hedge. [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- Congress approved the final text on 4 July 1776, and John Hancock signed it that day as President of Congress. John Dunlap printed about 200 broadsides that night, of which 26 survive. Most delegates signed the engrossed parchment on 2 August 1776. There were 56 signers in all; the youngest was Edward Rutledge (26) and the oldest Franklin (70). Jefferson, Franklin and Adams later remembered a 4 July signing, but historians follow Thomas McKean's correction. [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- Washington had it read to troops in New York on 9 July 1776. A crowd pulled down the equestrian statue of George III, and its lead was used for musket balls. [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- The Declaration lists 27 grievances against George III. One reads: "He has excited domestic insurrections amongst us, and has endeavoured to bring on the inhabitants of our frontiers, the merciless Indian Savages..." [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- Jefferson's most immediate sources were his own draft preamble for the Virginia constitution and George Mason's draft of the Virginia Declaration of Rights (June 1776). Mason drew in turn on the English Bill of Rights of 1689. Most scholars no longer accept the Declaration of Arbroath (1320) or the Dutch Act of Abjuration (1581) as models. [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- Locke is usually cited as a primary influence; Jefferson called him one of "the three greatest men that have ever lived". Other influences scholars have proposed: Burlamaqui (Ray Forrest Harvey, 1937), Francis Hutcheson and the Scottish Enlightenment (Garry Wills, *Inventing America*, 1978, a reading Ronald Hamowy and others strongly criticized), and Vattel's *Law of Nations* (David Armitage). [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- In Armitage's reading, the Declaration's primary purpose was "to express the international legal sovereignty of the United States". Advocates of independence argued that foreign powers would not aid a merely internal British rebellion. [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- Contemporary critics: John Lind's *Answer to the Declaration* (secretly commissioned by the North ministry) included an attack on natural rights by Jeremy Bentham. Lind and Thomas Hutchinson both asked how slaveholders could proclaim that "all men are created equal". [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]

## The Declaration and slavery
- Jefferson's draft included a passage condemning George III for the slave trade, which Congress deleted. Jefferson said it was struck to appease South Carolina and Georgia. He added that northerners were also uneasy, "for though their people had very few slaves themselves, yet they had been pretty considerable carriers of them to others." [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- The deleted passage also accused the king of "exciting those very people to rise in arms among us". This refers to Dunmore's Proclamation. [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- Jefferson was a major Virginia slaveholder. About 600 enslaved people lived at Monticello over the decades. CAVEAT: this is a lifetime total, not the number held at one time. W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence's phrasing "owning six hundred" is loose. [source: W:Thomas_Jefferson_and_slavery]
- Lemuel Haynes wrote in "Liberty Further Extended" that "Liberty is Equally as pre[c]ious to a Black man, as it is to a white one". [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- After the war, the share of free people among Black people in the Upper South rose from under 1% to 8.3% (1790–1810). Northern states had all abolished slavery (mostly gradually) by 1804. William Whipple, a signer, freed Prince Whipple. [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- Simon Schama argued that for southern Patriots, "Theirs was a revolution, first and foremost, mobilized to protect slavery." Dunmore's Proclamation pushed southern opinion toward independence. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- Later uses: the Seneca Falls Declaration of Sentiments (1848) said "all men and women are created equal". Frederick Douglass asked "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?" on 5 July 1852. Lincoln used the Declaration in the Peoria speech (1854) and the Gettysburg Address (1863). [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]

## The war
- The Continental Army never had more than 48,000 men in total, or more than 13,000 in one place. Enlistments ran one to three years. [source: W:Continental_Army]
- Washington lost New York in 1776, then won at Trenton and Princeton at the end of the 1776 campaign (Trenton came after the 25–26 December crossing of the Delaware). [source: W:George_Washington_in_the_American_Revolution]
- Saratoga: the battles were fought on 19 September and 7 October 1777, and Burgoyne surrendered on 17 October 1777. British and German losses included 6,222 captured. [source: W:Battles_of_Saratoga]
- Valley Forge: the encampment lasted from 19 December 1777 to 19 June 1778. About 12,000 people arrived, including soldiers, artificers, women and children. Roughly 1,700–2,000 soldiers died of disease, made worse by malnutrition and cold. The Prussian officer Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben drilled the army there. [source: W:Valley_Forge; W:American_Revolution]
- France had secretly aided the Americans from 1776, through Beaumarchais's dummy company; France and Spain each put up one million livres for munitions. The Treaty of Amity and Commerce and the Treaty of Alliance were signed on 6 February 1778. Under the commercial treaty France became the first nation to formally recognize the US. Under the alliance neither party could make a separate peace. [source: W:Treaty_of_Alliance_1778; W:American_Revolution]
- Spain declared war on Britain on 21 June 1779 as France's ally. It never formally allied with or recognized the US, and fought as a co-belligerent. Bernardo de Gálvez, governor of Louisiana, captured West Florida (siege of Pensacola, 1781). Spain also supplied money and munitions. [source: W:American_Revolution; W:Spain_and_the_American_Revolutionary_War]
- The Dutch Republic: the island of St. Eustatius had been a major arms channel for the rebels. Britain declared war on the Dutch in December 1780 (the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War, 1780–84), and Rodney captured St. Eustatius on 3 February 1781. In April 1782 the Dutch accepted John Adams as envoy, the second European state to recognize the US. A treaty of amity and commerce followed in October 1782. [source: W:Fourth_Anglo-Dutch_War]
- The war went global. The Chasseurs-Volontaires de Saint-Domingue were a regiment of free men of colour and enslaved men promised freedom, raised in Saint-Domingue (Haiti) on 12 March 1779. About 545 of them took part in the failed Franco-American siege of Savannah (Sept.–9 Oct. 1779). The Second Anglo-Mysore War in India (1780–1784) is counted as part of the American Revolutionary War and the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War. Spain besieged Gibraltar. [source: W:Chasseurs-Volontaires_de_Saint-Domingue; W:Second_Anglo-Mysore_War; W:Treaty_of_Paris_1783]
- Yorktown, 28 September–19 October 1781. Washington, Lafayette and Rochambeau's French army besieged Cornwallis. De Grasse's French fleet had beaten a British fleet in the Battle of the Chesapeake in early September. Allied strength was about 19,800: 8,000–9,000 Americans and 10,800 French with 29 warships. The British side had about 8,000–9,000. About 7,400–7,700 were captured. [source: W:Siege_of_Yorktown]
- Lord North's government fell on 20 March 1782, and peace talks began in Paris in April 1782. The American negotiators were Franklin, Jay, Adams and Laurens. Jay bypassed France and Spain to deal directly with Shelburne's government. [source: W:Treaty_of_Paris_1783]
- Treaty of Paris, signed 3 September 1783 at the Hôtel d'York (56 rue Jacob). Britain recognized independence and the US got land east of the Mississippi. The US also got fishing rights off Nova Scotia, and agreed that British creditors and Loyalists could try to recover their property. Congress ratified it on 14 January 1784 at Annapolis. Britain ratified on 9 April 1784, and ratifications were exchanged on 12 May 1784. Vergennes remarked: "The English buy peace rather than make it." [source: W:Treaty_of_Paris_1783]
- In separate treaties, Spain received East and West Florida and Menorca, and France gained Tobago and Senegal. [source: W:Treaty_of_Paris_1783]

## Society: Patriots, Loyalists, neutrals, immigrants
- Robert Calhoon estimates that 40–45% of white colonists supported the Patriots and 15–20% the Loyalists, with the rest neutral or keeping a low profile. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- Loyalists tended to be older, Anglican, merchants with imperial ties, and royal officials. Recent immigrants who had not been "Americanized" also tended to back the king. William Franklin, royal governor of New Jersey, stayed loyal, and he and his father Benjamin never spoke again. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- Loyalist exile figures differ by source. W:American_Revolution: of about half a million Loyalists, most stayed; about 46,000 went to Canada, 7,000 to Britain and 9,000 to Florida or the West Indies, about 2% of the population. Maya Jasanoff, *Liberty's Exiles*: about 60,000 Loyalists left, including about 8,000 free Black people, and they took about 15,000 enslaved people with them. [source: W:American_Revolution; W:Maya_Jasanoff]
- The Loyalist influx into Nova Scotia (about 34,000 refugees) raised its population by almost 60% and led to New Brunswick being made a separate colony in 1784. [source: W:Black_Loyalist]
- Immigrants and enlisted men: enlisted soldiers came largely from the working class and from minority groups (English, Ulster Protestant, African). Up to a fourth of Washington's army may have been Scots-Irish of Ulster origin, many of them recent arrivals. Bounties, pay and promised land drew many to enlist. [source: W:Continental_Army]
- Britain hired German auxiliaries, notably from Hesse-Kassel. Other Germans, already colonists, fought for the Patriots. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- Most Quakers tried to stay neutral. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- Elites and voting: on 26 May 1776, John Adams warned against widening the franchise ("Women will demand a vote... every man, who has not a farthing, will demand an equal voice"). Pennsylvania's radical constitution of 1776 had near-universal male suffrage and lasted 14 years before conservatives rewrote it in 1790. New Jersey enfranchised some property-owning women (widows), then took the vote back about 25 years later. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- Gary Nash stressed the role of seamen, tenant farmers, artisans, the enslaved, Native Americans and women. Edmund Morgan countered that these movements were "disparate, local, and mostly unsuccessful" and that elites in Congress drove independence. [source: W:Gary_B._Nash]

## Women
- Women enforced boycotts of household goods such as tea and cloth through spinning bees and the homespun movement (the "Daughters of Liberty"). In 1769 Boston women produced 40,000 skeins of yarn. Women drank "liberty tea" made from raspberry leaves or other substitutes. [source: W:American_Revolution; W:Daughters_of_Liberty; W:Women_in_the_American_Revolution]
- Edenton Tea Party: on 25 October 1774, 51 women of Edenton, North Carolina, led by Penelope Barker, signed a statement backing the boycott. It is one of the earliest known political protests organized by women in the colonies. [source: W:Edenton_Tea_Party]
- Deborah Sampson (or Samson) served for over a year in the Continental Army as "Robert Shurtleff". She was honourably discharged and later received a Massachusetts pension. "Molly Pitcher" is probably a composite legend, possibly based on Mary Ludwig Hays at Monmouth (28 June 1778) and Margaret Corbin. [source: W:Women_in_the_American_Revolution]
- Women also worked as camp followers (cooks, laundresses, nurses), spies and couriers. Mercy Otis Warren wrote plays and histories. Legal divorce, usually rare, was granted to some Patriot women whose husbands were Loyalists. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- Abigail Adams's "Remember the Ladies" letter to John Adams is dated 31 March 1776 (the Adams Papers dates it 31 March–5 April). John replied on 14 April 1776, laughing at her "extraordinary Code of Laws". In the same letter she doubted that Virginians had such a "passion for Liberty" when they enslaved "their fellow Creatures". [source: W:Abigail_Adams; WebSearch results citing https://www.masshist.org/publications/adams-papers/index.php/view/ADMS-04-01-02-0241]

## African Americans, enslaved and free
- Dunmore's Proclamation was signed on 7 November 1775 and made public on 14–15 November 1775. It declared martial law in Virginia and promised freedom to "indented servants, Negroes, or others" belonging to rebels who were able and willing to bear arms. Between 800 and 2,000 people escaped to Dunmore and formed the "Ethiopian Regiment". Smallpox and typhoid devastated them. The regiment lost at Great Bridge (December 1775). When Dunmore left Virginia in 1776 he took about 300 survivors. [source: W:Dunmore's_Proclamation]
- Sir Henry Clinton's Philipsburg Proclamation (30 June 1779) offered freedom to anyone enslaved by rebels who fled to British lines, whether or not they fought. [source: W:Philipsburg_Proclamation; W:Thomas_Peters]
- South Carolina lost an estimated 25,000 enslaved people (about a third) to flight, migration or death. Karen Cook Bell finds that roughly a third of wartime escapees were women. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- Black Patriots: estimates differ. W:Black_Patriot gives "5,000 or more" in the Continental Army and militias. Gary Nash counts about 9,000, including the navy, privateers, wagoners, servants and spies. W:Continental_Army gives about 6,600 men of colour (Black, Native and multiracial) serving with American forces. [source: W:Black_Patriot; W:American_Revolution; W:Continental_Army]
- Black men were officially barred from the Continental Army from 12 November 1775 to 23 February 1778. Even so, slaveholders often sent enslaved men to serve as substitutes. [source: W:1st_Rhode_Island_Regiment]
- The 1st Rhode Island Regiment (the "Black Regiment", 1778–80) totalled about 225 men, up to 140 of them Black. Eighty-eight enslaved men enlisted in 1778 on a promise of freedom. It fought at the Battle of Rhode Island in August 1778. [source: W:1st_Rhode_Island_Regiment]
- In January 1781, Virginia's General Assembly offered volunteers a "healthy sound negro" as an enlistment bounty. [source: W:Continental_Army]
- James Armistead spied for Lafayette during the Yorktown campaign. Virginia's 1783 law freed only enslaved soldiers who had been armed. Armistead had been a spy, not a soldier, so he had to petition separately and was freed only on 9 January 1787. He took the surname Lafayette. [source: W:James_Armistead_Lafayette]
- Phillis Wheatley (c. 1753–1784) was born in West Africa and enslaved in Boston. Her *Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral* (London, 1 September 1773) was the first published book of poetry by an African American. Washington praised her work. [source: W:Phillis_Wheatley]
- At the evacuation, British commander Sir Guy Carleton refused to hand back Black Loyalists. The *Book of Negroes*, compiled under Samuel Birch, records about 3,000 people evacuated from New York, mostly to Nova Scotia. The British also took about 10,000 formerly enslaved people from Savannah and Charleston. People enslaved by Loyalists and carried to the Caribbean generally stayed enslaved until British abolition in 1833–38. [source: W:Book_of_Negroes; W:American_Revolution]
- In Nova Scotia (Birchtown, Shelburne, Digby, Preston, Halifax), Black Loyalists got worse land or none, and white settlers attacked them in the Shelburne riots of July 1784, the first recorded race riots in Canada. Birchtown was for a few years the largest free Black community in North America. [source: W:Black_Loyalist]
- Thomas Peters (1738–1792) was enslaved in Wilmington, North Carolina, escaped in 1776, and rose to sergeant in the Black Pioneers. In 1791 he went to London with a petition from Black settlers in Nova Scotia and New Brunswick. His mission helped bring about the Sierra Leone resettlement. A later account of his Yoruba/Egba birth is flagged as self-published and doubtful. [source: W:Thomas_Peters]
- Sierra Leone: London's "Black Poor" made an earlier attempt in 1787, sending 411 people including about 300 Black Londoners to found Granville Town. In 1792, John Clarkson led about 1,100–1,200 Black Loyalists from Halifax. They founded Freetown on 11 March 1792 under the Sierra Leone Company (Act of 1791). Their descendants became part of the Krio (Sierra Leone Creole) people. CAVEAT: W:Black_Loyalist gives inconsistent numbers ("1,192", "nearly 1200", "nearly 2,000") and wrongly says "4,000 resettled by the Sierra Leone Company in 1787". Use about 1,100–1,200 in 1792. [source: W:Sierra_Leone_Company; W:Black_Loyalist]

## Native nations
- About 200,000 Native people lived east of the Mississippi. Most who took sides backed Britain, hoping to stop settler expansion. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- The Haudenosaunee split. The Grand Council chose neutrality at Albany in 1775. In July 1777, four nations (Mohawk, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca) sided with Britain, while most Oneida and Tuscarora sided with the Americans. At Oriskany (6 August 1777), Oneida fought alongside Patriot militia against other Haudenosaunee. This is described as the start of a Haudenosaunee civil war, and oral histories call the site "A Place of Great Sadness". [source: W:Joseph_Brant; W:Battle_of_Oriskany]
- Joseph Brant (Thayendanegea, b. 1743), a Mohawk war leader, led raids on frontier settlements in New York and Pennsylvania in 1778 and 1780. The Seneca war chiefs Sayenqueraghta and Cornplanter also led raids. [source: W:Joseph_Brant; W:Sullivan_Expedition]
- The Sullivan Expedition (June–October 1779) was led by John Sullivan and James Clinton and involved more than a third of the Continental Army (4,469 men assigned). It defeated about 600 Haudenosaunee and Loyalists at Newtown and razed 40 villages, destroying crops and food stores. Just over 5,000 refugees fled to Fort Niagara, and several hundred died of starvation, exposure and disease that winter. Some scholars call the campaign genocide; others dispute the term. [source: W:Sullivan_Expedition]
- Gnadenhutten, 8 March 1782: Pennsylvania militia under David Williamson killed 96 pacifist Moravian Christian Indians, mostly Lenape and Mohican. [source: W:Gnadenhutten_massacre]
- The Cherokee attacked frontier settlements in 1776. The Chickamauga Cherokee under Dragging Canoe (Tsiyu Gansini) kept fighting for about a decade after 1783. [source: W:American_Revolution]
- Native views of the peace: the Treaty of Paris did not mention Britain's Native allies and handed their lands to the US. In May 1783 Brant wrote that "England had sold the Indians to Congress." Some Haudenosaunee warriors openly threatened to attack the British over the betrayal. [source: W:Joseph_Brant; W:Treaty_of_Fort_Stanwix_1784]
- The Haldimand Proclamation (1784) granted Brant's followers about 2 million acres along the Grand River in present-day Ontario, now the Six Nations Reserve. [source: W:Joseph_Brant]
- The Treaty of Fort Stanwix (22 October 1784) was the first US treaty with the Haudenosaunee after the war. The Six Nations council at Buffalo Creek refused to ratify it, and the Ohio nations (Shawnee, Lenape and others) rejected it. [source: W:Treaty_of_Fort_Stanwix_1784]
- The Haudenosaunee named Washington "Town Destroyer". In 1790 Cornplanter told him: "to this day, when that name is heard, our women look behind them and turn pale, and our children cling close to the neck of their mothers." [source: W:Sullivan_Expedition]

## Articles of Confederation, Shays's Rebellion, the Constitution (1777–1789)
- Congress finalized the Articles of Confederation on 15 November 1777. Virginia ratified first, on 16 December 1777. Maryland held out until 2 February 1781, waiting for states to cede their western land claims. The Articles took effect on 1 March 1781. Under them Congress was a single chamber voting by state, and it could not levy taxes, only request money from the states. [source: W:Articles_of_Confederation]
- Between 1781 and 1784, less than $1.5 million reached the treasury, although the states had been asked for $2 million in 1783 alone. Rhode Island vetoed a federal import tax in 1782, and New York blocked a second one in 1785. [source: W:Articles_of_Confederation; W:Constitutional_Convention_United_States]
- Shays's Rebellion (29 August 1786 – February 1787) was an uprising of about 4,000 people in western and central Massachusetts against debt and tax collection. Rebels tried and failed to seize the Springfield Armory (January 1787). State militia under William Shepard and a privately funded force under Benjamin Lincoln put it down. Recent scholarship (early 2020s) suggests Daniel Shays's leadership has been exaggerated, and historians still debate how much the rising shaped the Constitution. [source: W:Shays's_Rebellion]
- Five states met at the Annapolis Convention in September 1786. [source: W:Constitutional_Convention_United_States]
- The Constitutional Convention met in Philadelphia from 25 May to 17 September 1787. Fifty-five delegates came from 12 states, since Rhode Island boycotted. Washington presided, and Madison's Virginia Plan was taken as the basis. The Connecticut Compromise settled representation in mid-July. Thirty-nine of the 55 delegates signed on 17 September 1787. Madison's notes were published only after his death in 1836. The new government replaced the Articles on 4 March 1789. Rhode Island ratified last, in May 1790. [source: W:Constitutional_Convention_United_States; W:Articles_of_Confederation]
- Three-fifths compromise (Art. I, §2, cl. 3): House seats and direct taxes were apportioned by counting free persons plus "three fifths of all other Persons", excluding "Indians not taxed". The ratio came from a failed 1783 amendment to the Articles, proposed by Madison. At the Convention, James Wilson proposed it and Charles Pinckney seconded it on 11 June 1787, and it was finally agreed 8–2 after debate on 9–13 July. The Fourteenth Amendment (1868) replaced it. [source: W:Three-fifths_Compromise]
- Congress could not ban the slave trade until 1808. In exchange, navigation acts needed only simple majorities, not two-thirds. The fugitive slave clause was strengthened. [source: W:Constitutional_Convention_United_States]

## Global afterlife of the Declaration
- David Armitage (*The Declaration of Independence: A Global History*, Harvard UP, 2007) argues that the Declaration was influential abroad mainly as a declaration of statehood, not of human rights. It began a new genre of declarations of independence, and he collects over 100 of them written since 1776. He sees their spread as a transition from a world of empires to a world of sovereign states. [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence; WebSearch summary of publisher/review pages, e.g. https://www.wcfia.harvard.edu/publications/declaration-independence-global-history]
- The first foreign derivation was the Manifesto of the Province of Flanders (1790). Others include Venezuela (1811), Liberia (1847), the Confederate secession declarations (1860–61) and Vietnam (1945). Haiti's declaration of 1 January 1804 drew inspiration from it, as did those of many Spanish American states in 1811–1830 and of Texas (1836), Hungary (1849) and Czechoslovakia (1918). Rhodesia's Unilateral Declaration of Independence (November 1965) copied the model but left out "all men are created equal" and "the consent of the governed". [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- Ho Chi Minh read the Vietnamese declaration at Ba Đình Square, Hanoi, on 2 September 1945. It referred to the US Declaration and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man. OSS officers were consulted. Historian Pierre Asselin calls the references a strategic move to win Allied support. [source: W:Declaration_of_independence_of_the_Democratic_Republic_of_Vietnam]
- Lafayette drafted the French Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789) in consultation with Jefferson. It also drew on Mason's Virginia Declaration of Rights. Spanish American authorities banned the US Declaration, but Manuel García de Sena and others translated and circulated it. [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]

## Debates
- Ideas vs interests. Progressive historians (Charles Beard, Vernon Parrington, Arthur Schlesinger Sr.) treated revolutionary rhetoric as cover for economic motives. In the 1950s Louis Hartz made Locke's property-centred liberalism central. [source: W:Republicanism_in_the_United_States]
- Republican synthesis. From the 1960s, Bernard Bailyn and Gordon Wood (the "Cambridge School") and J. G. A. Pocock (*The Machiavellian Moment*, 1975) stressed classical republicanism: civic virtue, fear of corruption, standing armies and conspiracy. Bailyn argued that the colonists' fear of a "ministerial conspiracy against liberty" was sincere, not propaganda. [source: W:Republicanism_in_the_United_States; W:The_Ideological_Origins_of_the_American_Revolution; W:The_Machiavellian_Moment]
- Liberal rebuttal. Isaac Kramnick and Joyce Appleby (*Liberalism and Republicanism in the Historical Imagination*, 1992) kept Locke and individualism central. The question remains strongly debated. [source: W:Republicanism_in_the_United_States]
- Sources of the Declaration's philosophy. Carl Becker (1922) argued for Locke. Garry Wills (1978) argued for Hutcheson and the Scottish Enlightenment, and was criticized by Hamowy and others. Armitage stresses Vattel and international law. [source: W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- Radicalism and social history. Wood's *Radicalism of the American Revolution* (1992) argues the Revolution was socially radical. Nash's bottom-up account (2005) faced Edmund Morgan's objection that popular movements were "disparate, local, and mostly unsuccessful". [source: W:Gordon_S._Wood; W:Gary_B._Nash]
- Slavery and motive. Simon Schama argues that southern Patriots' revolution was "first and foremost, mobilized to protect slavery". The Declaration's slavery contradiction was attacked from 1776 (Lind, Hutchinson, Day, Johnson). Lincoln later read the founders as tolerating slavery in the expectation that it would wither away. [source: W:American_Revolution; W:United_States_Declaration_of_Independence]
- The Sullivan campaign as genocide: argued by some scholars, disputed by others. [source: W:Sullivan_Expedition]
- How much Shays's Rebellion caused the Constitution, and how far Shays himself led it, are both debated. [source: W:Shays's_Rebellion]
- Common Sense sales (100,000 vs 500,000) are disputed as implausible given literacy rates. [source: W:Common_Sense]
