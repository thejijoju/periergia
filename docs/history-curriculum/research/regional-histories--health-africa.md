## 1. Historiography of African healing: Janzen, Feierman, pluralism
- John M. Janzen (born 28 October 1937) is an American anthropologist, now professor emeritus at the University of Kansas. He did fieldwork among the Kongo of the lower Congo in 1964–66 and again from 1969. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_M._Janzen]
- Janzen, *The Quest for Therapy in Lower Zaire* (University of California Press, 1978), was written with William Arkinstall. It was reissued in paperback in 1982 as *The Quest for Therapy: Medical Pluralism in Lower Zaire* and won the Royal Anthropological Institute's Wellcome Medal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_M._Janzen]
- The publisher's description says the book studies how Lower Zaire clients diagnose illness, choose therapies and assess treatments, a process the authors call "therapy management". It also says the book clarifies something central African clients had long known: that "medical systems are used in combination", i.e. medical pluralism. [source: https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780520046337]
- The "therapy managing group" is the set of non-professionals, namely the patient, kin and community network, who manage a sufferer's care. A later project carried the term forward with this meaning. I did not find Janzen's own 1978 definition, so the writer should describe the idea, that kin rather than the patient alone decide on and combine therapies, and should not quote it. [source: https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/TS3*940326]
- Janzen's *Lemba (1650–1930): A Drum of Affliction in Africa and the New World* (1982) studied a Kongo healing cult. In his reading, Lemba's sickness came from subordinates' envy of the mercantile elite's wealth during the Atlantic-trade era, and the cult combined healing with alliance-building through marriage and trade. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_M._Janzen]
- "Drum of affliction" is Janzen's translation of the pan-Bantu word *ngoma*. He developed the idea in *Ngoma: Discourses of Healing in Central and Southern Africa* (University of California Press, 1992). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_M._Janzen]
- Steven Feierman, "Struggles for Control: The Social Roots of Health and Healing in Modern Africa", *African Studies Review* 28, nos. 2/3 (1985), begins on p. 73. It surveys about a century of the social determinants of health in Africa and argues that health change cannot be separated from wider social change. It also argues that healers of any kind, biomedical or African, mattered less to population health than is usually assumed. [source: https://cambridge.org/core/journals/african-studies-review/article/struggles-for-control-the-social-roots-of-health-and-healing-in-modern-africa/C3ECEBFFEF1456A236687B776FE03F38]
- Feierman and Janzen co-edited a special issue, *The Social History of Disease and Medicine in Africa* (*Social Science and Medicine* 13B, 1979), and then *The Social Basis of Health and Healing in Africa* (University of California Press, 1992). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_M._Janzen]
- In a published letter answering Feierman's article, Pascal James Imperato argued that African practitioners are creators of medical knowledge, not just carriers of a tradition, and called for a history of therapeutics written "in the active voice". Feierman replied in the same issue. Exactly who said what in this exchange should be checked before it is quoted. [source: https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/5F81F4A8B808B2B68DAAA4661E27720E/S0002020600011835a.pdf/letter-from-pascal-james-imperato.pdf]

## 2. African therapeutic systems: healers, herbalists, divination, possession
- African traditional medicine typically involves diviners, midwives and herbalists. On this view illness does not arise by chance but from spiritual or social imbalance, so diagnosis is often spiritual and treatment, usually herbal, has symbolic as well as physical meaning. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_medicine]
- More than 4,000 plants are used medicinally in tropical Africa. Commonly cited estimates say 60–80% of Africans use traditional remedies. Both figures are rough and should be attributed as estimates. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_medicine]
- Under colonial rule some healing practices were outlawed and attempts were made to control the sale of herbal medicines. After independence in 1975, Mozambique went so far as to send diviner-healers to re-education camps. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_medicine]
- Among the Nguni, Sotho and Tsonga of southern Africa there are two main kinds of healer: the diviner (*sangoma*, a Zulu term) and the herbalist (*inyanga*). Colonialism, urbanisation and apartheid blurred the distinction, and many healers now practise both. Illness is attributed to witchcraft, to pollution, or to neglect of the ancestors (Zulu *amadlozi*, Sesotho *badimo*, Xhosa *izinyanya*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangoma]
- A sangoma is "called" through an initiation illness and trains as an *ithwasane* in *ukuthwasa* (Xhosa: to "come out" or "be reborn"). Training lasts from months to years and ends in song and dance. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangoma]
- One estimate puts South Africa's traditional healers at up to 200,000, against about 25,000 biomedical doctors. About 60% of South Africans consult healers, usually alongside biomedicine. These are estimates. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangoma]
- South Africa's Traditional Health Practitioners Act of 2007 (Act 22 of 2007) recognises diviners, herbalists, traditional birth attendants and traditional surgeons. An earlier 2004 Act was struck down by the Constitutional Court for insufficient public participation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangoma]
- Some healers are paid only after treatment has worked. The source gives this as one reason patients may prefer them to doctors who require payment in advance. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_medicine]
- Indigenous healers in Kahura, Uganda, were seen performing a successful caesarean section by R. W. Felkin in 1879. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_medicine]
- *Zār* is a spirit believed to possess people, mostly women, in the Horn of Africa and neighbouring regions of the Middle East. Zār rites reconcile spirit and host; the source calls them "adorcism" rather than exorcism, because possession is often lifelong. The word first appears for a possessing spirit in a 16th-century Ge'ez manuscript from Ethiopia. Its origin is debated: Ethiopian, Persian, Arabic and other derivations have all been proposed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C4%81r]
- In Sudan practitioners distinguish *zār bori*, practised by free-born women in the north, from *zār tumbura*, practised by descendants of enslaved people from the south and west. Zār is thought to have reached Egypt with enslaved Ethiopian, especially Oromo, women in the 19th century. The Egyptian government tried to ban it in the 19th century, but by the 1860s it was common among women of all classes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C4%81r]
- Janice Boddy's *Wombs and Alien Spirits: Women, Men and the Zar Cult in Northern Sudan* (University of Wisconsin Press, 1989) is the standard ethnography. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C4%81r]
- Bori is the comparable possession cult among the Hausa. Leo Frobenius suggested that zār and bori shared a Persian origin, a view now treated as speculative. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C4%81r]
- Variolation, inoculation against smallpox with matter from a smallpox pustule, was practised in parts of Africa before it reached England and North America in the 1720s. Two long-established methods are recorded in Sudan in the late 18th and early 19th centuries. In one, *tishteree el jidderi* ("buying the smallpox"), women of Sennar bargained with an infected child's mother over the price of each pustule. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variolation]
- Onesimus, an enslaved West African man in Boston (Cotton Mather called him "Guaramantee", possibly Coromantee/Akan), was given to Mather in December 1706. In or before 1716 he described to Mather the inoculation performed on him in Africa. Mather drew on this knowledge to advocate inoculation in the Boston outbreak of 1721. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onesimus_(Bostonian)]

## 3. Islamic and Ethiopian medicine and manuscripts
- The Timbuktu manuscripts are a large body of mainly Arabic manuscripts, some in African languages written in Arabic script (ajami), kept for centuries in private households in Timbuktu. The collections include works on medicine as well as art, philosophy, science, astronomy and the Qur'an. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbuktu_manuscripts]
- "Prophetic medicine" (*al-tibb al-nabawi*) is health guidance drawn from hadith: diet, cupping (*hijama*), cautery, simple drugs (especially honey), black seed (*Nigella sativa*) and prayers, with no surgery. A hadith attributes to Muhammad the saying: "Healing is in three things: cupping, a gulp of honey or cauterization ... but I forbid my followers to use cauterization." The source gives no specific evidence for its use in West or East Africa, so the writer should present it as part of the Islamic medical tradition shared across Muslim Africa, not as documented local practice. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophetic_medicine]
- Debtera (*dabtara*) are lay clergy of the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo churches, trained as scribes in Ge'ez and as cantors. Outside the liturgy many act as healers: herbalists, astrologers, makers of amulets. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debtera]
- Debtera make protective amulets against the evil eye, *buda* and zār spirits. Some take the form of small scrolls kept in pouches, made from the skin of a sacrificed goat or lamb whose blood ritually purifies the owner (the "Ethiopian healing/magic scrolls"). Priests attribute illness to sin and prescribe prayer, holy water, fasting and penance; debtera remedies centre on evil spirits. Some debtera supply contraceptive herbs against the church's official position. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debtera]

## 4. Witchcraft, causation and misfortune
- E. E. Evans-Pritchard (1902–1973) began fieldwork among the Azande of the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan (upper Nile, Bahr el-Ghazal) in 1926. *Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic Among the Azande* was published by the Clarendon Press, Oxford, in 1937. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witchcraft,_Oracles_and_Magic_Among_the_Azande ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._E._Evans-Pritchard]
- Chapter II of the book is titled "The notion of witchcraft explains unfortunate events", and Chapter III "Sufferers from misfortune seek for witches among their enemies". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witchcraft,_Oracles_and_Magic_Among_the_Azande]
- The book's two main arguments are that witchcraft acts as a "safety valve" for social conflict, and that it is a consistent, rational way of explaining misfortune in a society's own terms. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witchcraft,_Oracles_and_Magic_Among_the_Azande]
- The famous granary example: when a granary collapses on people sheltering beneath it, the Azande know that termites ate the supports. They ask instead why it fell at that moment on those particular people, and the answer is witchcraft. The wording circulating on Wikipedia is a later paraphrase (see Quotations). Witchcraft does not excuse human error, incompetence or moral failings such as lying. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azande_witchcraft]
- The most powerful Zande oracle is *benge*, the poison oracle, used only by men. A chicken is fed poison and a question is asked, and the answer depends on whether the bird dies. A second chicken must give the opposite result to confirm the first. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azande_witchcraft]
- The book has been criticised: Peter Winch (1958) attacked Evans-Pritchard's reduction of Zande belief to its social functions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witchcraft,_Oracles_and_Magic_Among_the_Azande]
- Witchcraft and modernity: Peter Geschiere's *The Modernity of Witchcraft: Politics and the Occult in Postcolonial Africa* (University Press of Virginia, 1997; French original *Sorcellerie et politique en Afrique*, 1995) is based on the Maka and eastern and southern Cameroon. Jean and John Comaroff edited *Modernity and Its Malcontents: Ritual and Power in Postcolonial Africa* (University of Chicago Press, 1993). Since 1993 there has been "a torrent of studies" treating witchcraft as modern rather than traditional. [source: https://search.ub.tum.de/vufind/Record/DE-604.BV011890174 ; https://www.chicagoreference.com/ucp/books/book/chicago/M/bo3625348.html ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/932DF318C8726BE3ACCA264D41D4B4FA]
- Ghana's "witch camps" are settlements where women accused of witchcraft flee, often to escape lynching. Many are widows, sometimes accused by relatives who want their late husbands' property, and many have mental illnesses. In 2012 there were at least six camps in northern Ghana (Bonyasi, Gambaga, Gnani, Kpatinga, Kukuo, Naabuli) housing about 1,000 women; some are thought to be over 100 years old. The government announced in 2012 that it would close them and disbanded Bonyasi in December 2014. By 2020 four camps housed over 500 people. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witch_camp]
- In the 21st century accusations against children have risen. UNICEF links them to "urbanization, poverty, conflict and fragmenting communities"; other factors include HIV/AIDS orphanhood and Pentecostal-charismatic preachers such as Helen Ukpabio. Accused children have suffered violent exorcisms, expulsion onto the streets and killing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witchcraft_accusations_against_children_in_Africa]
- Kinshasa: about 25,000 children are estimated to live on the streets, and 60% of them are reported to have been expelled from home after witchcraft accusations. These are unsourced-quality estimates and must be attributed and hedged. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witchcraft_accusations_against_children_in_Africa]
- South Africa's Witchcraft Suppression Act of 1957 remains a matter of contention. The Traditional Healers Organisation asked the Law Reform Commission to review its constitutionality. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangoma]

## 5. Disease ecology: malaria, sickle cell, tsetse, the "white man's grave"
- The sickle-cell trait (one copy of the HbS gene) gives carriers a survival advantage against malaria. This heterozygote advantage keeps the gene common where malaria is endemic, even though two copies cause sickle-cell disease, which is often fatal in childhood. It is a textbook case of natural selection. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sickle_cell_disease]
- Malaria, especially *P. falciparum*, has exerted "the greatest selective pressure on the human genome in recent history". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria]
- Carrier frequency of the sickle-cell gene is 10–40% across equatorial Africa, falling to 1–2% on the North African coast and below 1% in South Africa. About 80% of sickle-cell disease cases are in sub-Saharan Africa. A 2006 WHO report estimated about 2% of Nigerian newborns are affected, some 150,000 a year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sickle_cell_disease]
- In 1949 James V. Neel and E. A. Beet worked out the genetics, and Linus Pauling identified the abnormal haemoglobin molecule. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sickle_cell_disease]
- WHO's *World Malaria Report 2025* estimates 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths worldwide in 2024. The WHO African Region accounted for about 94% of cases and 95% of deaths, and children under five made up 75% of malaria deaths in Africa. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria]
- Quinine was isolated from cinchona bark in 1820. Its availability "had been said to be the prime reason Africa ceased to be known as the 'white man's grave'", which opened West Africa to colonists. Chloroquine replaced it in the 1940s, but resistance spread from the 1950s and became global by the 1980s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria]
- The "white man's grave": in Philip D. Curtin's data (*Death by Migration: Europe's Encounter with the Tropical World in the Nineteenth Century*, Cambridge University Press, 1989), secondary sources report that fevers in Sierra Leone killed white troops at about 410 per 1,000 per year in 1819–36, against about 2.5 per 1,000 for African troops. A summary of Curtin puts Britain's average losses of white troops in West Africa at 300–700 per 1,000 mean strength per year. Both figures come from secondary citations and should be checked against the book's tables before exact numbers are printed; a phrase such as "between a third and two-thirds of European soldiers a year" is safer. [source: https://search.ub.tum.de/vufind/Record/DE-604.BV043928181]
- Sleeping sickness (human African trypanosomiasis) is caused by *Trypanosoma brucei* and spread by tsetse flies (*Glossina*). *T. b. gambiense* in West and Central Africa causes over 92% of cases and is chronic. *T. b. rhodesiense* in East and Southern Africa is acute and zoonotic, carried by cattle and game. In animals the disease is *nagana*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_trypanosomiasis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsetse_fly]
- A 14th-century Arab writer, describing a sultan of Mali, recorded death from "the sleeping sickness (*illat an-nawm*)". In 1734 the British naval surgeon John Atkins described the "Sleepy Distemper" among West Africans. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_trypanosomiasis]
- Tsetse flies infest nearly 10 million km² of sub-Saharan Africa in 38 countries. They have left much fertile land uncultivated (a "green desert") and largely closed to cattle and draught animals. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsetse_fly]
- Marcella Alsan, "The Effect of the TseTse Fly on African Development", *American Economic Review* 105, no. 1 (2015), pp. 382–410, finds that ethnic groups in tsetse-suitable areas were less likely to use domesticated animals and the plough, less likely to be politically centralised, and had lower population densities. Sceptics reply that the fly was not the sole cause of African technological choices. [source: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:105:y:2015:i:1:p:382-410 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsetse_fly]
- Ecology after rinderpest, for cross-reference only (the Modern Africa chapter covers rinderpest): with grazing collapsed in the 1890s, grassland turned to bush, game and tsetse expanded, and sleeping sickness reached previously fly-free East African highlands. Conservationists later read the emptied land as primeval wilderness and turned much of it into reserves such as the Serengeti. Some of this passage on Wikipedia is tagged "citation needed", so keep it brief and hedged; John Ford's argument along these lines is not verified here. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsetse_fly]

## 6. Colonial medicine: tropical medicine, sleeping-sickness campaigns, mission medicine
- In 1901 a sleeping-sickness epidemic erupted in Uganda and killed more than 250,000 people, including about two-thirds of the population of the affected lakeshore areas. Wikipedia dates the wider outbreak 1896–1906 (Uganda and the Congo Basin), with later major outbreaks around 1920 and 1970. The *Cambridge History of Africa* estimates that up to half the people on both banks of the lower Congo died of sleeping sickness and smallpox. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_trypanosomiasis]
- David Bruce identified the tsetse fly as the vector in 1903, having earlier shown that *T. brucei* caused nagana in cattle and horses. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_trypanosomiasis]
- Atoxyl, an arsenic compound, was the first effective drug, introduced around 1910, but it caused blindness. During Robert Koch's 1906–07 German sleeping-sickness expedition, which set up a treatment camp on the Sese Islands (Lake Victoria) in August 1906, Koch himself reported 22 cases of blindness from atoxyl. One account says he did not openly reveal the drug's poor efficacy and severe side effects, and that atoxyl became the basis of a large campaign in the German colonies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_trypanosomiasis ; https://edoc.rki.de/bitstream/176904/5218/1/534-546.pdf ; https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/koch-robert]
- In British Uganda in 1907 the sick were to be placed in "segregation camps" for atoxyl treatment. [source: https://dynamic-rsnz.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/DOM19071106.2.59]
- Suramin was introduced in 1920. Tryparsamide was tested in the Belgian Congo by Louise Pearce of the Rockefeller Institute in 1920 and became the mainstay of mass treatment until the 1960s. Melarsoprol, introduced in the 1940s, causes fatal encephalopathy in some patients. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_trypanosomiasis]
- Eugène Jamot (1879–1937), a French colonial military doctor, used mobile teams that went village to village in Cameroon to screen and treat the population. In 1926 he became head of the Permanent Mission for the Prophylaxis of Sleeping Sickness, and he proclaimed "I will awaken the black race." Wikipedia's claim that Jamot "discovered" the tsetse vector is wrong (see NOT VERIFIED). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Jamot]
- Mass "Lomidinization": pentamidine (brand name Lomidine) has been used since 1937. In the 1950s single preventive injections were given en masse across French and other colonies in West Africa, and eradication seemed close. In *The Lomidine Files: The Untold Story of a Medical Disaster in Colonial Africa* (French original 2014; English translation by Noémi Tousignant, Johns Hopkins University Press, 2017), Guillaume Lachenal documents disasters. In Gribi, Cameroon, in 1954, 32 people died soon after injection; Lachenal attributes the deaths to poorly filtered water used to prepare the drug. Deaths were also reported at Abong-Mbang, Bafia and among the Eton in Cameroon, and 14 at Nkoltang, Gabon. The campaign was abandoned in the late 1950s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_trypanosomiasis ; https://www.press.jhu.edu/books/title/11534/lomidine-files ; https://hekint.org/2018/03/20/hidden-history-lomidine/]
- Sleeping sickness today: about 70 million people in 36 countries are at risk. Cases have fallen dramatically, with 583 confirmed in 2024 and deaths falling from 34,000 in 1990 to about 3,500 in 2015. More than 80% of cases are in the DR Congo. Oral fexinidazole is now first-line treatment. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_trypanosomiasis]
- Megan Vaughan, *Curing Their Ills: Colonial Power and African Illness* (Polity, Cambridge, and Stanford University Press, 1991), has chapters on psychiatry in Africa, venereal disease, European "jungle doctors'" memoirs and mission medicine. It explores the tension between biomedicine's claims to scientific neutrality and its political and cultural purposes. Vaughan was Smuts Professor of Commonwealth History at Cambridge from 2002 to 2016 and is a historian of Malawi and Zambia. [source: https://www.sup.org/books/title?id=3171 ; https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BA13160987?l=en ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megan_Vaughan]
- Mission medicine: Albert Ruskin Cook (1870–1951) of the Church Missionary Society reached Uganda in 1896 and founded Mengo Hospital in Kampala in 1897, described as the oldest hospital in East Africa. With his wife, the nurse Katharine Cook, he opened a school for midwives and wrote a midwifery manual in Luganda. He began training African medical assistants at Mulago during the First World War. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Ruskin_Cook]

## 7. African practitioners and nurses
- James Africanus Beale Horton (c. 1835–c. 1883) was born in Gloucester, Sierra Leone, to Igbo parents liberated from a slave ship by the Royal Navy. He studied at King's College London and took an MD at Edinburgh. He was commissioned in 1859 as a Staff-Assistant Surgeon in the British Army, one of the first Black officers, and retired as Surgeon-Major. He served in Lagos, the Gambia, Sierra Leone and the Gold Coast. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Africanus_Horton]
- Horton published *The Medical Topography of the West Coast of Africa* (1859), *West African Countries and Peoples* (1868), defending Africans against racist theories and urging self-government, and *The Diseases of Tropical Climates and their Treatment* (1874). In 1861 he wrote to the War Office urging a medical school in West Africa, ideally headed by an African. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Africanus_Horton]
- Cecilia Makiwane (1880–1919) trained at Victoria Hospital, Lovedale (Alice, Eastern Cape), and passed the Cape Colonial Medical Council examination on 19 December 1907. In January 1908 she became the first African woman registered by the state as a professional nurse in what became South Africa; the source says "in Africa". She was also an early women's-rights activist. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecilia_Makiwane]
- Ameyo Adadevoh (1956–2014), a Lagos endocrinologist, diagnosed Nigeria's first Ebola case, the Liberian traveller Patrick Sawyer, in July 2014. She kept him in isolation despite pressure, including from Liberian officials, to release him, saying she would not "for the greater public good". She died of Ebola on 19 August 2014. WHO declared Nigeria Ebola-free on 20 October 2014. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ameyo_Adadevoh]
- Sheik Umar Khan, a Sierra Leonean doctor, died of Ebola on 29 July 2014. By 1 July 2015 WHO counted 874 health workers infected and 509 dead. *Time* named the Ebola health workers its Person of the Year for 2014. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_African_Ebola_virus_epidemic]

## 8. Colonial psychiatry, the "African mind", asylums, Fanon, Lambo
- Mathari Hospital in Nairobi began in 1910 when the authorities of British Kenya converted a smallpox isolation centre into the Nairobi Lunatic Asylum. Africans were 95% of patients and were kept in the worst, most overcrowded conditions. It remains Kenya's only public psychiatric hospital. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathari_Hospital]
- J. C. Carothers (1903–1989), born in South Africa, joined the East African Medical Service in Kenya in 1929. He later headed Mathari Mental Hospital for 12 years and left Africa in 1951. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Colin_Carothers]
- In "Frontal Lobe Function and the African" (*Journal of Mental Science*, 1951), Carothers argued that "the African was developmentally child-like owing to underdeveloped frontal lobes that result in an effective leucotomy". (The quotation marks are Wikipedia's paraphrase-quote of his argument and may not be his exact words.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Colin_Carothers]
- The WHO published Carothers's *The African Mind in Health and Disease: A Study in Ethnopsychiatry* in 1953 (WHO Monograph Series no. 17). Psychiatrists and anthropologists reviewed it scathingly for its biological determinism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Colin_Carothers]
- In 1954 the Kenya government invited Carothers back during the Mau Mau rebellion. His *The Psychology of Mau Mau* (1955) helped guide British psychological warfare, which painted Mau Mau as irrational and bestial. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Colin_Carothers]
- Frantz Fanon (20 July 1925 – 6 December 1961), born in Martinique, was a psychiatrist. He trained under François Tosquelles at Saint-Alban and published *Black Skin, White Masks* in 1952. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frantz_Fanon]
- From 1953 until his expulsion in January 1957, Fanon was *chef de service* at the Blida-Joinville Psychiatric Hospital in Algeria. He introduced sociotherapy adapted to patients' cultures and trained nurses. After the war began he treated both French soldiers who tortured and Algerian victims of torture, and he joined the FLN. *The Wretched of the Earth* appeared in 1961. He died of double pneumonia while being treated for leukaemia at the NIH in Bethesda, Maryland. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frantz_Fanon]
- Fanon attacked the "Algiers School" of Antoine Porot, who described the Algerian Muslim as hysterical, predisposed to criminality and intellectually inferior. His essays "The North African Syndrome" (1952) and "Ethnopsychiatric considerations" (1955) criticised its biologism and racism. [source: https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/3280/1/F%20Menozzi%20Fanon.pdf ; https://africasacountry.com/2024/06/the-psychology-of-oppression-and-liberation]
- Thomas Adeoye Lambo (1923–2004) was born in Abeokuta and studied medicine at Birmingham. Heaton calls him the first indigenous Nigerian to earn a specialty degree in psychiatry in the UK, in 1954. From 1954 he headed the new Aro Neuro-Psychiatric Hospital at Abeokuta, where he pioneered the Aro Village System: patients lived with relatives in nearby villages and were treated with modern psychiatry combined with the help of traditional healers. [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/604713 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Adeoye_Lambo]
- Lambo was vice-chancellor of the University of Ibadan from 1967 to 1971 and WHO Deputy Director-General; Wikipedia gives his WHO years as 1971–1988. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Adeoye_Lambo]
- In colonial Nigeria, asylums (for example at Yaba, Lagos) confined people seen as a public nuisance. Many Nigerians viewed them with suspicion and kept using healers. See Jonathan Sadowsky, *Imperial Bedlam: Institutions of Madness in Colonial Southwest Nigeria* (University of California Press, 1999). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Adeoye_Lambo]
- Matthew M. Heaton, *Black Skin, White Coats: Nigerian Psychiatrists, Decolonization, and the Globalization of Psychiatry* (Ohio University Press, 2013), argues that Nigerian psychiatrists actively decolonised psychiatric knowledge through a "humanistic universalism". It covers "brain fag" debates, Nigerian migrants in Britain and encounters with healers. [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/604713 ; https://newbooksnetwork.com/matthew-m-heaton-black-skin-white-coats-ohio-up-2013]
- *Amafufunyana*, a Xhosa idiom of distress, is described as an extreme form of depression with psychotic features. Healers treat it with herbal mixtures. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_medicine]

## 9. Substances: khat, cannabis, alcohol
- Khat (*Catha edulis*) cultivation originated in the Harar region of Ethiopia and spread to Somalia, Yemen and East Africa. It is called *chat* in Amharic and Harari, *qaat* or *jaad* in Somali, and *miraa* in Swahili. Sufis used it to intensify devotion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khat]
- Khat is legal today in Ethiopia, Somalia and Djibouti. Kenya permits it but classes its alkaloids cathinone and cathine as Class C, and Uganda had banned it by 2023. The UK banned it on 24 June 2014. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khat]
- Colonial khat controls began in British Somaliland (1921–57). In Kenya prohibition came in the 1940s, dated 1945–56 in one reference work, after senior officials campaigned for it; the target was mainly Somali pastoralists rather than Meru growers. All these controls were eventually suspended as unworkable. In Kenya the ban collapsed under widespread smuggling, which Anderson and Carrier read as African agency. [source: https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/abs/khat-in-colonial-kenya-a-history-of-prohibition-and-control/1BF04C2A84891EDD811A85B6D5137AD8 ; https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/drugpolicy/chpt/khat]
- After a ban in the British Aden Protectorate, the Qāt Commission of Inquiry concluded that qāt "does not create an addiction, like opium or hashish". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khat]
- Cannabis (*dagga*, from the Khoikhoi *dacha*) was in wide use among Khoisan and Bantu-speaking peoples of South Africa before 1652; Basotho used it in childbirth. In 1870 Natal's Coolie Law Consolidation banned its use by Indian indentured workers. In 1922 it was criminalised nationally under customs regulations. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_in_South_Africa]
- In 1923, at the League of Nations opium committee, South Africa asked for dagga to be added to the list of prohibited narcotics, and cannabis was outlawed internationally in 1925. South Africa criminalised it fully in 1928. The Constitutional Court decriminalised private adult use and cultivation on 18 September 2018. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_in_South_Africa]
- The Brussels Conference Act of 2 July 1890 (in force 31 August 1891) was formally the "Convention Relative to the Slave Trade and Importation into Africa of Firearms, Ammunition, and Spiritous Liquors". Its liquor clauses make it the first international treaty to control a psychoactive substance, earlier than the 1909 opium commission. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels_Conference_Act_of_1890]
- In Ghana, according to Emmanuel Akyeampong (*Drink, Power, and Cultural Change: A Social History of Alcohol in Ghana, c. 1800 to Recent Times*, Heinemann/James Currey, 1996), liquor revenue was critical to British colonial finances. Authorities banned local distilling and raised duties, the illicit spirit *akpeteshie* became linked to the independence struggle, and bars became centres of nationalist politics. [source: https://eakyeampong.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/drink-power-and-cultural-change-social-history-alcohol-ghana-c-1800-recent- ; https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2015/6/17/ghanas-anti-colonial-beverage-gets-rehabilitated]

## 10. The 1918–19 influenza in Africa
- The deadly second wave reached Freetown, Sierra Leone, in late August 1918 by ship from Brest. It spread along the West African coast, rivers and railways. South Africa received it in September on ships bringing home members of the South African Native Labour Corps from France, and it reached Ethiopia in November. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu]
- South Africa: Howard Phillips, the historian of "Black October" (his 1990 study is *Black October: The Impact of the Spanish Influenza Epidemic of 1918 on South Africa*), gives about 300,000 deaths. Different versions put this at 4.4% or about 6% of the population, and some give up to 350,000 (about 5%), mostly within six weeks. Wikipedia says about 300,000, or 6%. Give "about 300,000 (some estimates up to 350,000)". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu ; https://www.litnet.co.za/in-a-time-of-plague-memories-of-the-spanish-flu-epidemic-of-1918-in-south-africa-by-howard-phillips-a-review/ ; https://open.uct.ac.za/handle/11427/7852]
- Almost a quarter of Kimberley's working population, the diamond-mine workers, died. In British Somaliland one official estimated that 7% of the native population died. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu]
- In the Gold Coast (Ghana) influenza killed at least 100,000 people. In Addis Ababa, estimates range from 5,000 to 10,000 dead or more; Tafari Makonnen (later Haile Selassie) caught it and survived. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu]
- Death rates in sub-Saharan Africa were two to three times those in North Africa. A 2019 study estimated mortality in coastal Kenya at about 25.3 per 1,000. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu ; https://wicri-demo.istex.fr/Wicri/Sante/explor/PandemieGrippaleV1/Site/fr/Pmc/Corpus/bibRecord.php?hk=000E26]
- Names and responses: some Africans called it a "white man's sickness", while white South Africans used a racist name for it. The *Sierra Leone Weekly News* (July 1918) wrote that "the doctors are at present flabbergasted". The Modern Africa chapter already notes that the Aladura churches of western Nigeria arose from about 1918 amid epidemics of influenza, smallpox and plague. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu]

## 11. Smallpox eradication in Africa
- Smallpox caused epidemics in Ethiopia and Sudan in 1811–13, 1838–39, 1865–66, 1878–79, 1885–87 and 1889–90. The slave trades spread it inland. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_smallpox]
- In 1967 WHO estimated 15 million cases and 2 million deaths worldwide. WHO's global campaign ran from 1958 (intensified from 1967) to 1977. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_smallpox ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallpox]
- The Horn of Africa was the last endemic focus. Nomads in the Ogaden kept a mild form of the disease (variola minor) in circulation. From 1975 WHO concentrated its efforts there and treated Ethiopia, Somalia, Kenya and Djibouti as one epidemiological unit. [source: https://www.cdc.gov/Mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00049694.htm]
- Ethiopia's last case was in August 1976; the CDC gives 9 August 1976. Sources disagree on the place (El Kere, Bale, or Dimo per the WHO "Red Book"). Somalia reported its first cases in 14 years in late September 1976, though one account dates a Mogadishu outbreak to August 1976. Kenya's last case was on 5 February 1977 in Mandera District. [source: https://www.cdc.gov/Mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00049694.htm ; https://biotech.law.lsu.edu/blaw/bt/smallpox/who/red-book/9241561106_chp22.pdf]
- Ali Maow Maalin (1954 – 22 July 2013) was a hospital cook and occasional vaccinator from Merca (Marka), Somalia. He was infected with variola minor on 12 October 1977 while guiding a vehicle carrying two infected children to an isolation camp, a journey of 5–15 minutes. Onset or diagnosis is often dated 26 October 1977. He was the last person in the world to catch naturally occurring smallpox. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Maow_Maalin]
- Maalin was first misdiagnosed with chickenpox. Fearing isolation, he did not report himself; a colleague reported him on 30 October. He later said he had not been vaccinated: "I was scared of being vaccinated then. It looked like the shot hurt." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Maow_Maalin]
- Containment traced 161 contacts (41 unvaccinated), with surveillance for six weeks and a search across all of Somalia completed in December 1977. On 17 April 1978 WHO Nairobi cabled: "Search complete. No cases discovered. Ali Maow Maalin is the world's last known smallpox case." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Maow_Maalin]
- Maalin later worked in Somalia's polio eradication campaign. He died of malaria in 2013 while carrying out polio vaccinations. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Maow_Maalin]
- The only later cases came from a laboratory accident in Birmingham, UK, in 1978; Janet Parker died on 11 September 1978. A WHO commission certified eradication on 9 December 1979, and WHO declared it in May 1980. Rinderpest, the only other eradicated disease, was declared eradicated in 2011. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_smallpox ; https://www.cdc.gov/Mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00049694.htm]

## 12. HIV/AIDS
- AIDS was identified in the US and France in 1981. Heterosexual Africans were diagnosed in 1982–83. In south-western Uganda in the early 1980s the disease was known locally as "Slim", and many sufferers first consulted healers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_in_Africa ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_medicine]
- Current figures, from UNAIDS data for 2024 as summarised by amfAR: 40.8 million people (37.0–45.6 million) were living with HIV worldwide, including 21.1 million in eastern and southern Africa and 5.2 million in western and central Africa. That makes about 26 million, roughly 64%, in sub-Saharan Africa. AIDS-related deaths were about 630,000 worldwide (490,000–820,000), with about 260,000 in eastern and southern Africa and 120,000 in western and central Africa. The regional figures come from a secondary summary, because the UNAIDS host was unreachable from this session. [source: https://www.unaids.org/sites/default/files/2025-07/2025_Global_HIV_Factsheet_en.pdf ; https://www.amfar.org/about-hiv-and-aids/facts-and-stats/statistics--worldwide]
- At the epidemic's height, life expectancy fell sharply in southern Africa. In Eswatini (Swaziland) it was 59 (men) and 62 (women) in 1990 but 49 and 51 in 2009. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_in_Africa]
- Ethiopia's adult prevalence fell from 3.6% in 2001 to 1.4% in 2011. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_in_Africa]
- Africans on antiretroviral treatment rose from 1 million in 2005 to 7.1 million in 2012. Between 2000 and 2018 new infections fell 37% and HIV-related deaths 45%. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_in_Africa]
- Older people, especially grandmothers, often care for sick adult children and then for orphaned grandchildren; children take on income-earning and caring roles. Orphanhood also contributes to child-witchcraft accusations (section 4). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_in_Africa ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witchcraft_accusations_against_children_in_Africa]
- South African denialism: HIV/AIDS denialism shaped policy from 1999 to 2008 under President Thabo Mbeki. In 2000 he convened a Presidential AIDS Advisory Panel of 33 scientists, about half of them denialists such as Peter Duesberg. On 20 April 2000 he wrote to Bill Clinton calling AIDS a "uniquely African catastrophe". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_denialism_in_South_Africa]
- Health Minister Manto Tshabalala-Msimang promoted garlic, beetroot, lemon juice and the herbal remedy *ubhejane* over antiretrovirals, earning the nickname "Dr. Beetroot". The vitamin seller Matthias Rath ran illegal "trials" in 2005. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_denialism_in_South_Africa]
- On 6 July 2000, before the Durban International AIDS Conference, more than 5,000 scientists signed the Durban Declaration that HIV causes AIDS. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_denialism_in_South_Africa]
- Estimated cost of the policies: Chigwedere et al., "Estimating the Lost Benefits of Antiretroviral Drug Use in South Africa", *Journal of Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndromes* 49, no. 4 (December 2008), estimated that "more than 330,000" lives (about 2.2 million person-years) were lost in 2000–05 because a feasible antiretroviral programme was not implemented. They also estimated that about 35,000 babies were born with HIV because a nevirapine mother-to-child prevention programme was not run. Nicoli Nattrass (February 2008) estimated about 343,000 deaths. Wikipedia summarises the range as 343,000–365,000. Present these as attributed model estimates. [source: https://www.hsph.harvard.edu/news/press-releases/researchers-estimate-lives-lost-delay-arv-drug-use-hivaids-south-africa/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_denialism_in_South_Africa]
- The government announced it would provide antiretrovirals through public clinics in October 2006; the Cabinet had voted for a roll-out in 2003. On 25 September 2008 the new president, Kgalema Motlanthe, replaced Tshabalala-Msimang with Barbara Hogan, who declared: "The era of denialism is over completely in South Africa." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_denialism_in_South_Africa ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatment_Action_Campaign]
- The Treatment Action Campaign (TAC) was launched on 10 December 1998 (International Human Rights Day) by Zackie Achmat (born 1962) and about ten others. They acted after the death from AIDS of the gay anti-apartheid activist Simon Nkoli, at a time when combination therapy existed but was unaffordable. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatment_Action_Campaign]
- Achmat, who is HIV-positive, publicly refused antiretrovirals until all South Africans could get them. He began taking them in August 2003 after a TAC congress voted to urge him to. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zackie_Achmat]
- TAC sued the government and won, on the constitutional right to health care: the government was ordered to provide mother-to-child transmission prevention, including nevirapine, in public clinics. This was the Constitutional Court case of 2002. TAC also backed the government as *amicus curiae* when pharmaceutical companies sued over the Medicines Act, a case the companies withdrew. TAC marched on Parliament in February 2003 and began civil disobedience in March 2003. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatment_Action_Campaign]
- The *New Yorker* called Achmat "the most important dissident in the country since Nelson Mandela". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatment_Action_Campaign]
- Herbal remedies used by some patients with HIV (hypoxis, sutherlandia) may interfere with antiretroviral treatment. There have been programmes to train healers in HIV prevention. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_medicine]

## 13. War, trauma, child soldiers, PTSD and global mental health
- PTSD entered the American Psychiatric Association's DSM-III in 1980, shaped largely by Vietnam War veterans. Cultural and medical anthropologists question applying its criteria across cultures. They argue that local idioms of distress (for example "soul loss" or affliction of the spirit) are forced into a category built for post-Vietnam America, which they call a "category fallacy". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-traumatic_stress_disorder]
- Alcinda Honwana, a Mozambican anthropologist, wrote *Child Soldiers in Africa* (University of Pennsylvania Press, 2006), drawing on Mozambique and Angola. It documents cleansing and purification rites that free former fighters from a tainted past, protect them from the spirits of the dead, and settle the spirits of relatives killed without proper funerals. A 1999 World Bank note by Honwana and Edward Green found that healing for war-affected children in both countries consisted principally of such cleansing rituals. In Mozambique, child soldiers under 15 were excluded from demobilisation benefits because they did not fit the official definition of "soldier". [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/book/6235 ; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/221085/pdf ; https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/612131468767662523/text/23422-Replacement-file-IKNT10.txt]
- Ishmael Beah (born 23 November 1980, Sierra Leone) is a former child soldier and author of the memoir *A Long Way Gone* (2007). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishmael_Beah]
- Child soldiers: Liberian rebel forces under Charles Taylor had "Small Boy Units", and about 21,000 Liberian child soldiers still needed demobilising in early 2004. The Lord's Resistance Army drove a rise in child recruitment, and the UN estimated up to 6,000 children in the Central African Republic conflict in 2014. FRELIMO and RENAMO both used child soldiers in Mozambique's civil war. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_soldiers_in_Africa]
- Global mental health: the "treatment gap" for mental disorders is estimated at 76–85% in low- and middle-income countries, against 35–50% in high-income countries. One in eight people worldwide lived with a mental disorder in 2019. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_mental_health]
- Critics call the Movement for Global Mental Health a neo-colonial or "missionary" project that medicalises distress and ignores local concepts and healing. Prominent critics include China Mills (*Decolonizing Global Mental Health: The Psychiatrization of the Majority World*) and Ethan Watters (*Crazy Like Us*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_mental_health]
- Lambo's Aro model (section 8) and Fanon's community psychiatry are early African precedents for community-based, culturally adapted care. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Adeoye_Lambo ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frantz_Fanon]

## 14. Ebola 2013–16 and recent developments
- The West African Ebola epidemic of December 2013 to June 2016 (Zaire ebolavirus) centred on Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone, with smaller outbreaks in Nigeria and Mali. It was the largest Ebola outbreak in history. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_African_Ebola_virus_epidemic]
- By the end of the epidemic 28,616 people had been infected and 11,310 had died, a case-fatality rate of about 40%. The final WHO report (as of 8 May 2016) gave 28,646 suspected cases and 11,323 deaths. WHO believes the true toll was substantially higher. Use 11,310 to match the published Modern Africa chapter. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_African_Ebola_virus_epidemic]
- The index case is generally believed to be a one- or two-year-old boy, Emile Ouamouno, who died in December 2013 in Méliandou, Guinea. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_African_Ebola_virus_epidemic]
- WHO declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern on 8 August 2014 and ended it on 29 March 2016. The epidemic was declared over on 9 June 2016. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_African_Ebola_virus_epidemic]
- Contributing factors included extreme poverty, weak health systems, distrust of government after years of war, a months-long delay in the response, burial customs of washing the body, and spread to dense cities. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_African_Ebola_virus_epidemic]
- The epidemic left about 17,000 survivors, many with post-Ebola syndrome. The UN said 22,000 children had lost one or both parents. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_African_Ebola_virus_epidemic]
- A ring-vaccination trial in Guinea (results December 2016) found the rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine protective, and it was approved in 2019. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_African_Ebola_virus_epidemic]
- Africa CDC: Ethiopia proposed it in 2013, the African Union established it in 2016, and it was launched in January 2017 with its seat in Addis Ababa. Its first director was John Nkengasong. In August 2024 it declared mpox a Public Health Emergency of Continental Security, its first such declaration. It aims for 60% of vaccines used in Africa to be made in Africa by 2040, against under 1% in 2021. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa_Centres_for_Disease_Control_and_Prevention]
- COVID-19 was first confirmed in Africa in Egypt on 14 February 2020, and in sub-Saharan Africa in Nigeria at the end of February. African scientists, including Tulio de Oliveira's team in South Africa, helped identify the Omicron variant in November 2021. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Africa]
- Malaria vaccines: WHO approved RTS,S (Mosquirix) in 2021, after pilots in Ghana, Kenya and Malawi from 2019, and endorsed R21/Matrix-M in 2023. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria]
- A 2026 Ebola epidemic is ongoing, caused by Bundibugyo virus, against which existing Zaire-ebolavirus vaccines and treatments are not designed. It was first reported on 14 May 2026 in Ituri, eastern DR Congo, and WHO declared a PHEIC on 16 May 2026. By early November 2026 Wikipedia reported about 11,000 confirmed cases and about 5,390 deaths, mostly in the DRC, with cases in Uganda and Kenya; it had become the second-largest Ebola outbreak on record. The figures are changing rapidly, so if mentioned, say "in 2026 a new Ebola epidemic ... more than 5,000 deaths by November" and date the figure. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Ebola_epidemic]

## Debates
- **Healing as social and political, not just technical.** Janzen's "quest for therapy" showed Kongo patients and their kin ("therapy management") combining biomedicine and African therapies, so pluralism was the norm, not a transitional stage. Feierman (1985) argued that health depends on social and political struggles over land, labour and power more than on healers of any kind. Imperato's reply insisted on African practitioners as creators of knowledge. [source: https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780520046337 ; https://cambridge.org/core/journals/african-studies-review/article/struggles-for-control-the-social-roots-of-health-and-healing-in-modern-africa/C3ECEBFFEF1456A236687B776FE03F38 ; https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/5F81F4A8B808B2B68DAAA4661E27720E/S0002020600011835a.pdf/letter-from-pascal-james-imperato.pdf]
- **Witchcraft: rationality, function, modernity.** Evans-Pritchard presented Zande witchcraft as logically coherent and socially stabilising. Winch (1958) attacked the functionalist reduction. Geschiere (1997) and the Comaroffs (1993) argued that witchcraft is thoroughly modern, tied to postcolonial politics, inequality and the market, which helps explain present-day accusations, witch camps and child "witches". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witchcraft,_Oracles_and_Magic_Among_the_Azande ; https://search.ub.tum.de/vufind/Record/DE-604.BV011890174 ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/932DF318C8726BE3ACCA264D41D4B4FA]
- **Zār and possession: origins and meaning.** Origins are disputed (Ethiopian, Persian, Arabic, Hebrew or West African). Interpretations include women's refuge and resistance (Natvig on the Sahel; Boddy on northern Sudan), therapy, and entertainment. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C4%81r]
- **Colonial medicine: benevolence or control?** Vaughan (1991) showed biomedicine claiming scientific neutrality while serving colonial power. The sleeping-sickness campaigns (Koch's camps and atoxyl blindings, Jamot's mobile teams, Lomidine deaths documented by Lachenal) illustrate coercive mass medicine. Jamot is still honoured in Cameroon, which shows that the legacy is contested. [source: https://www.sup.org/books/title?id=3171 ; https://www.press.jhu.edu/books/title/11534/lomidine-files ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Jamot]
- **The "African mind".** Carothers's WHO monograph (1953) and frontal-lobe theory, and Porot's Algiers School, versus Fanon's critique of colonial psychiatry's racism and his focus on colonial violence as a cause of disorder. Lambo, Heaton's "humanistic universalism" and Nigerian psychiatrists offered an African-led alternative. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Colin_Carothers ; https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/3280/1/F%20Menozzi%20Fanon.pdf ; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/604713]
- **Disease and African development.** Alsan (2015) found that tsetse suitability is associated with less plough use, less political centralisation and lower density; others caution against single-cause ecological explanations. On the "white man's grave", quinine is conventionally credited with opening Africa to Europeans; Curtin's mortality data underpin this. [source: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:105:y:2015:i:1:p:382-410 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsetse_fly ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine]
- **Universal categories or local idioms of distress?** Anthropologists question PTSD's cross-cultural validity and the "category fallacy". The Movement for Global Mental Health stresses the treatment gap, while critics (Mills, Watters) see psychiatrisation of the majority world. Honwana's work on cleansing rituals for child soldiers offers African models of healing collective as well as individual trauma. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-traumatic_stress_disorder ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_mental_health ; https://muse.jhu.edu/book/6235]
- **Denialism: how many died, and why did Mbeki embrace it?** The 330,000+ (Chigwedere et al.) and 343,000 (Nattrass) estimates are model-based. Denialists disputed them in a 2009 paper in *Medical Hypotheses*, which was later withdrawn. Mbeki's motives remain unclear. Some link them to his suspicion of Western medicine and racial stereotyping of African sexuality, shown in his letter's comparison of the treatment of dissidents to apartheid. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_denialism_in_South_Africa]
- **Colonial drug control.** Anderson and Carrier read colonial khat prohibition in Kenya as an expression of colonial attitudes to African culture, defeated by African smuggling. South Africa's racialised dagga laws (Natal 1870 aimed at Indian workers; the 1922 criminalisation) and its push for international control in 1923–25 show colonial and settler states exporting drug policy. On alcohol, Akyeampong shows British colonial finance depending on liquor duties even as officials worried about order. [source: https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/abs/khat-in-colonial-kenya-a-history-of-prohibition-and-control/1BF04C2A84891EDD811A85B6D5137AD8 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_in_South_Africa ; https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2002/10/the-importance-of-the-quotidian]

## Conflicts with published chapters
- **Modern Africa chapter** (history--regional-histories--modern-africa.sql): "Sub-Saharan Africa has carried over two-thirds of the world's HIV infections." By current UNAIDS data for 2024 (via amfAR), sub-Saharan Africa has about 26.3 million of 40.8 million people living with HIV, about 64%, slightly under two-thirds. Wikipedia still says "more than two-thirds", which was true historically. The writer should say "about two-thirds" or "nearly two-thirds of people living with HIV today", which is consistent rather than contradictory. A minor point, flagged for consistency.
- **Modern Africa chapter:** "the West African Ebola epidemic of 2013–16 killed 11,310 people". This is consistent with Wikipedia's end-of-epidemic figure. WHO's last situation count was 11,323 (to 8 May 2016), so use 11,310 for consistency.
- **Modern Africa chapter, rinderpest:** the Italian route of 1887–89, about nine in ten of Ethiopia's cattle, and about two-thirds of the Maasai of Tanzania dead. This is consistent with the Tsetse fly article (arrival in Eritrea 1887, Ethiopia 1888, South Africa 1897, over 90% of Maasai cattle, "two-thirds of the Masai died in 1891"). Cross-refer and do not repeat.
- **Imperial Expansion chapter:** "Quinine ... is credited with ending Africa's reputation as the 'white man's grave'." This is consistent; the Quinine article phrases it as "had been said to be the prime reason". Keep the attribution.
- **Decolonization chapter**, on Fanon "headed a service at a psychiatric hospital in Algeria": consistent (*chef de service*, Blida-Joinville, 1953–January 1957).
- **First World War / 20th-century overview** (the influenza pandemic killed more than the war; 17–50 million): consistent. No Africa-wide total was verified, so do not add one.
- **20th-century overview:** "smallpox ... was certified eradicated in 1980". Consistent: the WHO commission certified it on 9 December 1979 and the World Health Assembly declared it in May 1980. Say "certified in 1979–80" or "declared in 1980".
- No contradictions were found on malaria, sleeping sickness or Leopold's Congo. The Imperial Expansion and Modern Africa chapters say disease, "above all sleeping sickness", was the main direct killer in the Congo Free State. This chapter's figures on the lower Congo, where up to half the people may have died of sleeping sickness and smallpox (*Cambridge History of Africa*), are compatible.

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use
- A continental total for 1918–19 influenza deaths in sub-Saharan Africa (figures of about 1.5–2.4 million circulate, attributed to K. David Patterson). I could not find it in a source I read.
- Fanon's chapter title "Colonial War and Mental Disorders" in *The Wretched of the Earth* and its case histories. The Wikipedia summary does not list it, and my search did not confirm its contents.
- Janzen's own wording defining the "therapy managing group" in the 1978 book.
- Wikipedia's claim that Lambo was "the first western-trained psychiatrist in Africa". It is contradicted by the narrower verified claim (first indigenous Nigerian with a UK psychiatry specialty degree, 1954); use the narrower one.
- Wikipedia's claim that Jamot "discovered that the tsetse fly was the vector". This is wrong: David Bruce did so in 1903.
- The 1927–31 Bafia (Cameroon) tryparsamide accident (often said to have blinded about 700 people under Jamot's mission). Not found in a source I read.
- Koch's camps in German East Africa: their conditions, numbers and coercion are not documented in the sources I read beyond the Sese Islands camp and the 22 blindings.
- Tsetse fly article: "Millions of people died of [sleeping sickness] in the early 20th century" (tagged citation needed), and "the best game warden in Africa" (tagged "never attributed").
- John Ford's thesis (*The Role of the Trypanosomiases in African Ecology*, 1971) that colonial disruption caused the epidemics. I did not verify it here.
- Kenya's 1945 khat ban date as exact. Sources give "the 1940s", 1945–56, and 1940 import restrictions.
- The date of the Aden Qāt Commission.
- That the Timbuktu manuscript *Kashf al-Ghummah fi Nafa al-Ummah* (1733) is a medical text.
- That Ibn Khaldun wrote the 14th-century sleeping-sickness description of a Mali sultan.
- Curtin's exact West African mortality figures. Only secondary citations were seen: about 410 per 1,000 for Sierra Leone 1819–36, and 300–700 per 1,000. Use them hedged or check the book.
- Wikipedia's claim that "approximately 35 million" Africans were infected, "of whom around 1 million have already died". This is internally garbled; do not use it.
- Chigwedere's figure as "330,000 per year". It is a total for 2000–05, not an annual figure (Wikipedia's Duesberg passage misstates it).
- The specific Constitutional Court case name and date (*Minister of Health v Treatment Action Campaign*, July 2002). The Wikipedia page was not available. The court victory and its substance are verified; the exact date is not.
- The Friendship Bench (Dixon Chibanda, Zimbabwe), a likely African example for global mental health. No page could be fetched.
