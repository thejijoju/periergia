## The steppe, the horse and the first pastoralists

- The **Botai culture** (c. 3700–3100 BCE) of northern Kazakhstan is named after the settlement of Botai on the Imanburlyq, a tributary of the Ishim; the type site has 153+ (some counts "over 160") pit-houses, and the culture is associated with enormous numbers of horse bones, corral-like enclosures and layers of horse dung. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botai_culture]
- Evidence once read as Botai domestication: bit-wear facets of 3 mm or more on seven horse premolars from Botai and Kozhai 1, dated c. 3700–3500 BCE, and lipid residues in pottery interpreted as mare's milk (Outram et al., *Science*, 2009, "The Earliest Horse Harnessing and Milking"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication_of_the_horse ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botai_culture]
- Counter-evidence: other researchers argue Botai horses were wild and hunted on foot (no skeletal changes of domestication); later dental-calculus studies found no evidence of dairy consumption among Botai individuals, weakening the milking claim. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botai_culture ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication_of_the_horse]
- Gaunitz et al. (*Science*, 2018), comparing 42 ancient horse genomes (20 from Botai) with 46 published genomes, found that modern domestic horses are **not** descended from Botai horses; Przewalski's horses turned out to be feral descendants of horses herded at Botai. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication_of_the_horse]
- Librado et al. (*Nature*, October 2021; 264 ancient genomes; 150+ scientists) located the origin of the modern domestic horse lineage ("DOM2") in the **Volga–Don region of the Pontic–Caspian steppe**, around **2200 BCE**, after which it spread rapidly across Eurasia from c. 2000 BCE, replacing other populations; DOM2 is associated with horses buried in Sintashta kurgans with early spoke-wheeled chariots. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication_of_the_horse]
- Librado et al., "Widespread horse-based mobility arose around 2200 BCE in Eurasia" (*Nature* vol. 631, 2024; 475 ancient horse genomes) dated reproductive control of the modern lineage to c. 2200 BCE, after a bottleneck beginning no earlier than c. 2700 BCE, and argued against the view that large herds of domestic horses accompanied the Yamnaya migrations of c. 3000 BCE. [source: WebSearch summary of the Nature paper, https://escholarship.org/content/qt7ww163tk/qt7ww163tk.pdf ; https://www.sci.news/genetics/modern-horse-domestication-13000.html]
- The most persuasive evidence of domestication is chariot burials: pairs of horses buried with spoke-wheeled chariots in at least 16 graves of the Sintashta and Petrovka cultures, southeast of the Urals, dated c. 2100–1700 BCE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication_of_the_horse]
- The **Yamnaya** (Pit Grave) culture, c. 3300–2600 BCE, on the Pontic–Caspian steppe: herders who buried elites under kurgans (mounds), some with complete wooden wagons; carts and wagons were probably ox-drawn. Under the widely accepted Kurgan hypothesis they spoke a late stage of Proto-Indo-European (David Anthony). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamnaya_culture]
- Yamnaya horse-riding is contested: Trautmann et al. (*Science Advances*, 2023) identified skeletal traits in several Yamnaya individuals they attribute to riding; the archaeozoologist William T. Taylor disputes this, arguing that horse domestication long postdates the Yamnaya. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamnaya_culture]
- Dairying: protein analysis indicates Early Bronze Age Yamnaya groups consumed milk products (from goats, cattle, sheep); in Mongolia, dental-plaque evidence shows horse milk consumption by c. 1200 BCE alongside the rise of mobile pastoralism and horse burials at khirigsuurs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication_of_the_horse]
- Chariots: the clearest early military impact of horses was in pulling chariots, introduced around 2000 BCE; mounted archery became decisive in the Iron Age. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication_of_the_horse]

## Scythians and Saka

- The **Scythians** were Eastern Iranian-speaking equestrian nomads who migrated in the 9th–8th centuries BCE from Central Asia to the Pontic steppe (modern Ukraine and southern Russia), where they remained until the 3rd century BCE; their name derives from an endonym *Skuδa*, "archers". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythians]
- Ancient Greek usage applied "Scythian" to any northern steppe people; Persians called northern nomads **Saka**. Darius I's inscriptions distinguish several Saka groups after his campaign of 520–518 BCE, including the "Saka beyond Sogdia". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saka]
- Archaeology suggests "Scythian" culture (kurgans and the "animal style") originated in the east: eastern kurgans such as **Arzhan 1** in Tuva (9th–7th centuries BCE) are older than western ones. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saka]
- The **Pazyryk** burials of the Altai (Ukok plateau), 4th–3rd centuries BCE, are thought to be those of Saka chieftains; the "Golden Man" parade armour from the **Issyk kurgan** near Almaty dates c. 400–200 BCE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saka]
- Herodotus records that Cyrus the Great died (530 BCE) campaigning against the Massagetae, whose queen **Tomyris** defeated the Persians and, in his account, put Cyrus's severed head in a sack of blood (a story, not established fact). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saka]

## Xiongnu and Huns

- The first known Xiongnu ruler was **Touman** (r. 220–209 BCE); in 215 BCE the Qin general Meng Tian drove the Xiongnu out of the Ordos; in **209 BCE** Touman's son **Modu** became *chanyu* and united a powerful confederation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiongnu]
- In 200 BCE Modu surrounded the Han founder Gaozu at Baideng for seven days; the resulting *heqin* treaty (marriage alliance plus gifts) set the pattern for about sixty years and was renewed nine times up to 135 BCE, with growing "gifts". Modu died in 174 BCE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiongnu]
- Han–Xiongnu wars: 133 BCE–89 CE. In **48 CE** eight southern tribes seceded and acclaimed Bi as chanyu, splitting the Xiongnu into northern and southern groups. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiongnu]
- Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian* (c. 100 BCE) is the earliest detailed account of the Xiongnu. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiongnu]
- A link between Xiongnu and the European Huns is possible but unproven ("possibly their relation to the Huns", supported by some archaeogenetics). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiongnu]
- The **Huns** appear in Europe in the 370s with the conquest of the Alans and Goths; Attila's court flourished 434–453. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huns]

## Kushans and Sogdians

- The **Kushan Empire** was formed by the Yuezhi in the early 1st century CE; its founder Kujula Kadphises united Yuezhi tribes. The Bactrian **Rabatak inscription** (found 1993, deciphered 2000) records that **Kanishka** (c. 127 CE) replaced Greek with Bactrian as the language of administration. Kushan inscriptions reach Sarnath near Varanasi. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kushan_Empire]
- **Sogdian**, an Eastern Iranian language now extinct, was a lingua franca of Central Asia and one of the court languages of the First Turkic Khaganate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sogdia]
- Arab conquest: Samarkand's ruler Gurak submitted to the Umayyad governor al-Harashi; Divashtich (r. 706–722), ruler of Panjakent, resisted in the Zarafshan range (documents from **Mount Mugh**); Sogdians who fled to Khujand were massacred. From 722 many Sogdians, including Nestorian Christians, moved east to Semirechye. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sogdia]
- Panjakent murals (c. 740) include a scene from *Kalila wa-Dimna* (the Panchatantra fable of the lion and the hare), now in the Hermitage. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sogdia]
- Sogdian conversion to Islam was virtually complete by the end of the Samanid state (999), as Sogdian gave way to New Persian. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sogdia]

## Turkic khaganates and the Uyghurs

- The **First Turkic Khaganate** (552–603) was founded by **Bumin Qaghan** of the Ashina clan, who in 552 defeated his Rouran overlord Anagui. Chinese chronicles record Anagui's earlier rebuke: "You are my blacksmith slave. How dare you utter these words?" ("blacksmith slave" may denote vassalage, i.e. supplying iron weapons). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Turkic_Khaganate]
- Bumin's brother **Istämi** (552–575) ruled the west and in 557 allied with the Sasanian Khosrow I to destroy the Hephthalites; the Gokturks were the first people to call themselves "Turk"; the Old Turkic script was devised in the 6th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Turkic_Khaganate]
- The **Orkhon inscriptions** — bilingual Old Turkic/Chinese steles to the princes Kul Tigin and his brother Bilge Qaghan — were erected in the early 8th century in Mongolia's Orkhon valley, found by Nikolai Yadrintsev's expedition in 1889 and published by Vasily Radlov; Vilhelm Thomsen's Danish rendering was translated into English in 1930. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orkhon_inscriptions]
- The **Uyghur Khaganate** (744–840) succeeded the Turks; its third khagan **Bögü Qaghan** converted to Manichaeism in 762, which became official; its successor state was the Yenisei Kyrgyz. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uyghur_Khaganate]
- **Battle of Talas** (May–September 751): an Abbasid army under Ziyad ibn Salih with Karluk forces defeated the Tang under Gao Xianzhi; modern estimates c. 30,000 per side versus much larger figures in Chinese and Arab sources. The story that Chinese prisoners introduced papermaking is "disputed by several findings"; the An Lushan rebellion (755) did more than Talas to end Tang presence in the west. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Talas ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sogdia]

## Islamisation, the Samanids and the Karakhanids

- The **Samanids** (819–999) were a Persianate Sunni dynasty of Iranian *dehqan* origin; in 819 four sons of Asad ibn Saman were made governors under the Abbasids; **Ismail Samani** united the family's lands in 892; capitals Samarkand (819–892) then Bukhara (892–999). By 945 Turkic military slaves held real power. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samanid_Empire]
- The Samanids patronised **Rudaki**, **Ferdowsi** and **Avicenna**; Bukhara was said to rival Baghdad; scholars credit them with doing more than the Buyids or Saffarids to revive Persian language and culture, while Arabic remained the language of science and religion. The state fell to the Ghaznavids and Karakhanids (999). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samanid_Empire]
- **Ibn Sina (Avicenna)**, c. 980–1037, born at Afshana near Bukhara, died at Hamadan. **Al-Khwarizmi** (c. 780–c. 850), from Khwarazm, wrote treatises on algebra and Hindu–Arabic numerals. **Al-Biruni** (973–1048) was born at Kath in Khwarazm. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khwarizmi ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Biruni]
- **Satuq Bughra Khan** (r. c. 920–955) was the first Muslim khan of the **Kara-Khanids**, whose official religion is listed as Islam from 934. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kara-Khanid_Khanate]
- **Mahmud al-Kashgari** compiled the first comprehensive dictionary of Turkic languages, the *Dīwān Lughāt al-Turk*, in 1072–74. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahmud_al-Kashgari]
- Wikipedia's Central Asia article notes the 8th-century Islamic expansion had "no significant demographic impact"; the region remained predominantly Iranian-speaking at that time. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia]

## Timur and the Timurids

- **Timur** (Tamerlane) was born into the Turkicised Mongol **Barlas** near Kesh (Shahrisabz), about 80 km south of Samarkand. **Birth date: the 1320s.** Later Timurid histories give 8 April 1336, but most sources from his lifetime imply a late-1320s birth; Beatrice Manz (*Encyclopaedia of Islam*) calls the 1336 date "probably an invention" of Shah Rukh's time. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timur]
- Timur took power in the western Chagatai Khanate by **1370** (enthroned at Balkh, 9 April 1370); not a Chinggisid, he took the title *güregen* ("son-in-law") through marriage to a Chinggisid. He died **17/18 February 1405** at Farab (Otrar) at the start of a winter campaign against Ming China begun in December 1404; buried in the Gur-e-Amir, Samarkand. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timur]
- Campaigns: Isfahan (1387), Baghdad (1393, 1401), war with Tokhtamysh of the Golden Horde, sack of Delhi (1398), defeat and capture of the Ottoman Bayezid I at **Ankara, 28 July 1402** (some sources 20 July). "Scholars estimate that his military campaigns caused the deaths of millions of people" (no precise figure verified). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timur ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ankara]
- Contemporaries — Ibn Khaldun (who met him at Damascus in 1401), Ibn Arabshah, the Castilian envoy Clavijo (1404) — identified Timur as a Mongol; Ibn Khaldun called him "sultan of the Mongols and Tatars". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timur]
- **Ulugh Beg** (1394–1449), Timur's grandson, built the Ulugh Beg madrasa in Samarkand (1417–1420) and an observatory; he was overthrown and killed by his son Abd al-Latif in 1449. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulugh_Beg]

## Uzbek khanates

- The **Shaybanids** (Abu'l-Khayrid Shibanids) were an Uzbek dynasty; the **Khanate of Bukhara** existed 1501–1785 (Shaybanids, then the Janids/Ashtarkhanids in the 17th–18th centuries). In 1785 Shah Murad formalised **Manghit** rule as the **Emirate of Bukhara** (1785–1920); the Manghits were **non-Chinggisid** and therefore took the title emir rather than khan (Soucek, *A History of Inner Asia*, 2000). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanate_of_Bukhara ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaybanids]
- **Khiva**: khanate 1511–1920 (capital Khiva from 1599), Russian protectorate 1873–1920. **Kokand**: 1709–1876. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanate_of_Khiva ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanate_of_Kokand]
- Babur (founder of the Mughals) lost Ferghana and Samarkand to the Uzbeks — already told in the published Mughal chapter.

## Kazakhs and Dzungars

- The **Kazakh Khanate** emerged c. 1465 from the break-up of the Golden Horde under **Kerei** and **Janibek** (1465 is the date "commonly accepted" in Kazakh historiography; some date it to 1470–71). Fifteenth-century Central Asian authors called Kazakhs, Shaybanids and Nogais alike "Uzbeks". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakh_Khanate]
- In the 17th century three **zhuz** (hordes) emerged: Senior (Great), Middle and Junior (Little); after Khan **Tauke** died (1715 or 1718) they became effectively separate khanates. Tauke's law code was the *Jeti Jarghy* ("Seven Charters"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakh_Khanate]
- Dzungar invasion of 1723, remembered as the "Great Disaster" (*Aqtaban Shubyryndy*, name not separately verified); Kazakh forces under Abul Khair won battles 1727–1730. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakhs]
- On **10 October 1731** Abu'l Khayr, khan of the Junior Zhuz, swore fealty to Empress Anna, seeking Russian help against rivals; the Middle Zhuz khan Semeke followed; Kazakh oaths of allegiance continued 1731–1740 (Bregel, *Encyclopaedia Iranica*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakh_Khanate]
- **Ablai Khan** reunited the Kazakhs and balanced between Russia and Qing China; after his death in 1781 the khanate split again. Russia abolished the khans by the Statutes of 1822–1824; Kenesary Qasymuly's revolt (from 1837) ended with his death in 1847. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakh_Khanate ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_conquest_of_Central_Asia]
- The **Dzungar Khanate** (1634–1758), of Oirat Mongol origin, has been called the last nomadic empire (Barthold). Rulers included Erdeni Batur, Galdan Boshugtu, Tsewang Rabtan and Galdan Tseren. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dzungar_Khanate]
- **Dzungar genocide**: after Amursana's 1755 rebellion the Qianlong Emperor ordered the Dzungars' eradication. Estimates: about 70–80% of the Dzungars died from war and disease, c. **420,000–480,000** people (Perdue; Clarke), other estimates 600,000 to as high as 800,000. Wei Yuan (1794–1857) put the pre-conquest population at about 600,000 in 200,000 households and wrote that 40% of households died of smallpox, 20% fled to Russia or the Kazakhs, and 30% were killed by bannermen. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dzungar_genocide]
- The Qing then resettled Han, Hui, Uyghurs and others in Dzungaria. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dzungar_genocide]

## Russian conquest

- Early failures: Bekovich-Cherkassky's expedition of 1717 to Khiva (about 3,500 men) was destroyed; Perovsky's winter Khivan campaign of 1839 failed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_conquest_of_Central_Asia]
- Chernyaev took **Tashkent** in 1865; Samarkand surrendered in May 1868 and Bukhara's army was defeated at Zerabulak on 2 June 1868 (Bukharan losses 3,500–10,000; Russian under 100); peace on 5 July 1868 made Bukhara a dependent state. **Khiva** fell in 1873; **Kokand** was abolished in 1876 (Skobelev). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_conquest_of_Central_Asia]
- At **Geok Tepe** (January 1881) Skobelev's forces killed **at least 14,000 Tekke** Turkmen; Transcaspia was annexed in May 1881; Merv in 1884; the clash at Panjdeh (1885) brought Britain close to war; the conquest is conventionally dated 1717–1895. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_conquest_of_Central_Asia]
- Alexander Morrison, *The Russian Conquest of Central Asia: A Study in Imperial Expansion, 1814–1914* (Cambridge University Press, 2020; some citations 2021); his 2014 article is titled "Killing the Cotton Canard and getting rid of the Great Game". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asian_revolt_of_1916 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Game]
- **1916 revolt** (*Ürkün*, "exodus", in Kyrgyz memory): triggered by the decree of 25 June 1916 conscripting Central Asian men aged 19–43 into labour battalions; suppression and flight to China caused an estimated **100,000–500,000 deaths**, mostly Kyrgyz and Kazakhs; perhaps up to 40% of ethnic Kyrgyz died during or after it (figure in Kyrgyz historiography; Morrison cited for the overall range). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asian_revolt_of_1916]

## The "Great Game"

- The phrase was coined in 1840 by Captain **Arthur Conolly**, who wrote to Henry Rawlinson: "You've a great game, a noble game, before you"; Kipling's *Kim* (1901) popularised it. Two British representatives (Conolly among them) were executed in Bukhara in 1842, after which Britain discouraged officers from travelling in Turkestan (Yapp). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Game]
- Conventional dates: from 12 January 1830 (Ellenborough's instruction to open a trade route to Bukhara) to the Anglo-Russian Convention of August 1907. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Game]
- Malcolm Yapp, "The Legend of the Great Game" (*Proceedings of the British Academy* 111, lecture of 16 May 2000): the term became common only after the Second World War; British India's main concern was controlling its own population, not a Russian invasion; archives show no British intelligence network in Central Asia. Gerald Morgan argued similarly. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Game]

## Soviet Central Asia

- Bolsheviks stormed Kokand (February 1918), seat of the autonomous Turkestan government; the **Basmachi** resistance lasted 1916–1934 by Wikipedia's dating (published Soviet chapter: crushed by mid-1920s, fighting into early 1930s). The Turkestan ASSR existed 1918–1924; Bukhara and Khiva became People's Soviet Republics in 1920. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basmachi_movement ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_delimitation_in_the_Soviet_Union]
- **National-territorial delimitation**: the Politburo announced it on 25 February 1924; Bukhara and Khorezm republics were partitioned (Khorezm's party was "strong-armed" into voting for dissolution in July 1924); the Uzbek and Turkmen SSRs were created in 1924; the Tajik ASSR within Uzbekistan became the Tajik SSR on 5 December 1929; the Kyrgyz and Kazakh SSRs were created on 5 December 1936. Bukhara and Samarkand, with large Tajik populations, stayed in Uzbekistan. Kazakhs were then officially called "Kyrgyz" and the Kyrgyz "Kara-Kyrgyz". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_delimitation_in_the_Soviet_Union]
- "Divide and rule" reading versus nuanced reading: critics see the borders as deliberate divide-and-rule; archive-based work stresses competing ethnic, economic and local-elite claims; the borders were never meant to be international frontiers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_delimitation_in_the_Soviet_Union]
- **Korenizatsiia** ("indigenisation") was formalised in April 1923 (12th Party Congress): teaching and administration in local languages and promotion of non-Russians. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korenizatsiia]

## The Kazakh famine (Asharshylyq), 1930–1933

- Death toll: an estimated **1.3–2.3 million** died, most of them Kazakhs; other estimates about **1.75 million**, some above 2 million; **38–42%** of all Kazakhs died, the highest proportion of any ethnic group in the Soviet famines. Cameron's publisher gives "more than 1.5 million … a quarter of Kazakhstan's population"; Kindler's publisher gives "nearly one-third of the Kazakh population". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakh_famine_of_1930%E2%80%931933 ; WebSearch summaries of publishers' descriptions]
- It began in late 1930, a year before the worst of the Ukrainian famine, and lasted through 1933; Kazakhstan's population fell by more than a third; Kazakhs fell from about 60% to 38% of the republic's people. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakh_famine_of_1930%E2%80%931933]
- Causes: a *zhut* (livestock-killing freeze) in 1927–28; the 1928 "Small October" campaign under party chief **Filipp Goloshchyokin** confiscating herds from wealthier Kazakhs (*bai*); one-third of livestock confiscated 1930–31; over 1 million tons of grain taken in 1930; districts "blacklisted" from trade; forced sedentarisation. Two-thirds of survivors were sedentarised after herds fell by 80%. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakh_famine_of_1930%E2%80%931933]
- Refugees: **665,000–1.1 million** fled to China, Mongolia, Afghanistan, Iran, Turkey and other Soviet republics; thousands were shot at the Chinese border (e.g. the Karatal affair). Goloshchyokin was replaced in 1933 by Levon Mirzoyan. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakh_famine_of_1930%E2%80%931933]
- An earlier famine of 1919–1922 killed an estimated 400,000–2 million in the Kazakh region. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakh_famine_of_1930%E2%80%931933]
- Memory: 31 May is a day of remembrance; a monument was built in 2017. Soviet schooling blamed natural *zhut*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakh_famine_of_1930%E2%80%931933]

## Later Soviet period

- The Aral Sea: formerly the world's fourth-largest lake (68,000 km² in 1960), it shrank from the 1960s after Soviet irrigation diverted the Amu Darya and Syr Darya, and by 2007 was 10% of its original size, in four lakes; in August 2014 its eastern basin dried completely. Kazakhstan's **Kokaral dam** (2005) has partly restored the North Aral. In 2010 at Moynaq, Ban Ki-moon called it "one of the planet's worst environmental disasters". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aral_Sea]
- Semipalatinsk: 468 Soviet nuclear tests, 1949–1989; Kazakh experts estimate 1.5 million people were exposed to fallout; the site was closed on 29 August 1991. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semipalatinsk_Test_Site]

## Qing and Chinese Xinjiang

- The First East Turkestan Republic was proclaimed in 1933 and crushed in 1934 by Sheng Shicai with Soviet help; the Second East Turkestan Republic (from the Ili rebellion, 1944) was absorbed into the PRC in 1949. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Uyghurs_in_China]
- July 2009 Ürümqi riots left over one hundred dead; attacks attributed to Uyghur militants followed from 2009 to 2016, including Kunming (2014) and Ürümqi (May 2014); the "Strike Hard Campaign Against Violent Terrorism" was announced in 2014. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Uyghurs_in_China ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang_re-education_camps]
- From 2017 under party secretary **Chen Quanguo** (in Xinjiang from August 2016), the authorities detained, by the researcher **Adrian Zenz's 2020 estimate, up to 1.8 million** people (other reporting: "over an estimated one million"), mainly Uyghurs but also Kazakhs, Kyrgyz and others, in camps China officially called "vocational education and training centers". China denied the camps until 2018, claimed in 2019 that the centres had closed, and denies human-rights abuses. Zenz says mass internment peaked in 2018 and shifted toward forced-labour programmes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang_re-education_camps ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Uyghurs_in_China]
- Chinese government statistics show birth rates in mainly Uyghur Hotan and Kashgar fell by more than 60% from 2015 to 2018 (the national birth rate fell 9.7% in the same period). Leaked documents: "Xinjiang Papers" (New York Times, 16 November 2019) and "China Cables" (ICIJ, 24 November 2019). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Uyghurs_in_China ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang_re-education_camps]
- Assessments: the **US State Department declared genocide in 2021**; several legislatures passed non-binding motions; the **UN Human Rights Office (OHCHR, 2022)** said China's actions "may constitute" crimes against humanity but did not use the word genocide. ## Independent Central Asia

## Debates
- **Horse domestication:** Botai (c. 3700–3500 BCE) as the first horse herders/riders (Olsen, Outram) versus wild-horse hunting; genomics (Gaunitz 2018; Librado 2021, 2024) puts the ancestor of modern horses on the Volga–Don c. 2200 BCE and rules out Botai as its source; Yamnaya riding asserted by Trautmann et al. (2023), denied by William T. Taylor. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication_of_the_horse ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamnaya_culture ; Nature 2024 via WebSearch]
- **Nomads and sedentary states:** Owen Lattimore suggested nomads could be self-sufficient; later scholars rejected this. **Anatoly Khazanov**, *Nomads and the Outside World* (Cambridge, 1984; 2nd ed. 1994) — a Moscow-trained Soviet anthropologist who emigrated in 1985 — argued that nomads were "never autarkic" and depended economically, culturally and politically on the sedentary world, a view "shared now by the majority of experts". **Thomas Barfield**, *The Perilous Frontier: Nomadic Empires and China, 221 BC to AD 1757* (1989), argued that steppe empires formed in tandem with unified Chinese empires and lived off them through an "outer frontier strategy" of raiding and extortion ("shadow empires" in his later work). **Nicola Di Cosmo** ("Ancient Inner Asian Nomads", *Journal of Asian Studies* 53:4, 1994; *Ancient China and Its Enemies*, 2002) engages and departs from Barfield's dependence model, reconstructing the frontier from archaeology and a critical reading of Sima Qian. **Christopher Beckwith** (*Empires of the Silk Road*, 2009) argues that Central Eurasia was the core of civilization, not a land of barbarians, and reportedly repeats the critique of Barfield; reviewers (e.g. Doug Hitch) found "glaring flaws", and his claim that Central Eurasia is "our homeland" has been called hyperdiffusionist. Caveat: Di Cosmo's precise counter-argument (that Xiongnu state formation arose from internal crisis and militarisation) could not be checked against an accessible text — keep the wording general. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Khazanov ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_nomads ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_I._Beckwith ; WebSearch summaries incl. https://asianreviewofbooks.com/shadow-empires-an-alternative-imperial-history-by-thomas-j-barfield/]
- **"Inner Eurasia":** David Christian, "Inner Eurasia as a Unit of World History", *Journal of World History* 5:2 (1994), pp. 173–211, argues the region is a coherent unit because its societies faced distinctive ecological and military problems (animal-based pastoralism versus plant-based farming in "Outer Eurasia"); the Mongol and Russian empires both arose there. [source: WebSearch, https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/inner-eurasia-as-a-unit-of-world-history/]
- **The "Great Game":** Yapp ("The Legend of the Great Game", 2000) and Gerald Morgan call it largely a legend; Robert Middleton calls it a figment of jingoist imaginations; Evgeny Sergeev treats it as real great-power competition; Alexander Morrison argues it explains little about Russian motives (published Imperial Russia chapter already uses Morrison). Critics also say the "New Great Game" label treats Central Asian states as mere pawns (Marlene Laruelle cited). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Game]
- **Soviet nationality policy and delimitation:** Terry Martin, *The Affirmative Action Empire: Nations and Nationalism in the Soviet Union, 1923–1939* (Cornell, 2001): the Soviet state systematically promoted non-Russian elites and cultures (korenizatsiia) — "affirmative action empire" is Martin's own term. Francine Hirsch, *Empire of Nations: Ethnographic Knowledge and the Making of the Soviet Union* (Cornell, 2005; AHA Herbert Baxter Adams Prize, 2007): former imperial ethnographers and local elites supplied the knowledge (censuses, border commissions) that shaped the USSR's national units; their results were political. Yuri Slezkine's "The USSR as a Communal Apartment" (*Slavic Review*, 1994) is the classic precursor. Adeeb Khalid, *Making Uzbekistan* (2015), stresses Central Asian (Jadid) agency. Popular "divide and rule" readings of the 1924 borders are contested by archival studies. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korenizatsiia ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francine_Hirsch ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adeeb_Khalid ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_delimitation_in_the_Soviet_Union ; WebSearch on Martin and Hirsch]
- **Was the Kazakh famine genocide?** Kazakh historians Zhulduzbek Abylkhozhin and Makash Tatimov introduced the phrase "Goloshchyokin's genocide" after independence; the Kozybayev parliamentary commission (1991–92) called it genocide; later Kazakh state historiography shifted to "humanitarian catastrophe". **Sarah Cameron** (*The Hungry Steppe*, 2018) ties the famine to Moscow's project of turning nomads into a modern Soviet Kazakh nation and argues it "probably would be considered a genocide" under Lemkin's broader definition (reviewer's quotation, p. 178). **Niccolò Pianciola** (*Stalinismo di frontiera*, 2009; "Sacrificing the Qazaqs", 2022) shows Kazakh livestock was requisitioned for Moscow, Leningrad and the army — a "hierarchy of consumption" — and argues that in Lemkin's sense all Soviet nomads were victims. **Robert Kindler** (*Stalin's Nomads*, German 2014 / English 2018) studies sedentarisation and the famine's violence. **Stephen Wheatcroft** calls the policies "criminal acts of negligence", not intentional genocide. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakh_famine_of_1930%E2%80%931933 ; WebSearch, https://www.ciuspress.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Kulchytsky-Journal-of-Modern-History-2021.pdf ; https://scholars.ln.edu.hk/en/publications/nomads-and-the-state-in-soviet-kazakhstan/]
- **Xinjiang:** "persecution", "cultural genocide/ethnocide" or "genocide" (US State Department 2021; some parliaments) versus "may constitute crimes against humanity" (OHCHR 2022) versus China's position (counter-terrorism and vocational training; denial). Explanations offered in scholarship: rising contention from 2009, a shift to assimilationist minority policy, and Chen Quanguo's leadership. Barry Sautman (2026) denies genocide claims; Magnus Fiskesjö calls his book "genocide-denialist". Frame with attribution throughout. [sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Uyghurs_in_China ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang_re-education_camps]
- **The Dzungar genocide** is also named as such in recent literature; Perdue's translation of Qianlong's orders is the key evidence (published Chinese History chapter already covers this). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dzungar_genocide]

## Conflicts with published chapters
- **Timur's birth date.** `history--medieval-history--mongol-empire.sql` gives "Timur (Tamerlane, 1336–1405)". Wikipedia (citing Manz, *Encyclopaedia of Islam*) says he was born in the **1320s**; 8 April 1336 is a later Timurid date, "probably an invention". Recommend "c. 1320s–1405" here, and flag the Mongol Empire chapter for correction.
- **"Last Chinggisid ruler deposed in Bukhara in 1920."** `history--medieval-history--mongol-empire.sql` says: "the last ruler of Chinggisid descent was deposed in Bukhara in 1920". Wikipedia (Khanate of Bukhara, citing Soucek 2000) states the Manghit emirs of Bukhara (1785–1920) were **non-Chinggisid** and therefore called themselves emirs. Chinggisid rulers lasted longer elsewhere: Maqsud Shah, khan of Kumul (Xinjiang), ruled 1908–1930; Mongolia's Navaanneren Setsen Khan was executed in 1937 (Borjigin article). Do not repeat the Bukhara claim; flag that chapter.
- **Semipalatinsk test count.** `history--20th-century-history--global-cold-war.sql` says "over 450 nuclear explosions ... from 1949 to 1989"; Wikipedia gives **468** tests. Not a contradiction (468 is "over 450"), but use 468 or "over 450" consistently.
- **Basmachi end date.** The Soviet Union chapter says the Basmachi were "largely crushed by the mid-1920s, though fighting continued into the early 1930s"; Wikipedia dates the movement 1916–1934. Compatible; say "1916 to the early 1930s".
- **Bukhara's status.** Imperial Russia chapter: "Bukhara became a vassal in 1868". Wikipedia's Emirate of Bukhara infobox lists the Russian protectorate as 1873–1920, with 1868 as the defeat and treaty. Compatible if phrased "defeated in 1868 and a Russian protectorate by 1873".
