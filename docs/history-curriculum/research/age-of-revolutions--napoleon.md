## Background: Corsica and early career
- Born Napoleone di Buonaparte, 15 August 1769, in Ajaccio, Corsica; died 5 May 1821. Family of Italian origin: paternal Buonapartes descended from minor Tuscan nobility who emigrated to Corsica in the 16th century; maternal Ramolinos from Lombard nobility. Parents Carlo Maria Buonaparte and Maria Letizia Ramolino. [source: NAP]
- Born one year after the Republic of Genoa ceded Corsica to France (Treaty of Versailles, 1768). His father had supported Pasquale Paoli in the Corsican war of independence; after the Corsican defeat at Ponte Novu (1769), Carlo became a client of the French governor Marbeuf, who obtained Napoleon a royal bursary to a mainland military academy. [source: NAP]
- His mother tongues were Corsican and Italian. Moved to France January 1779 (aged 9), school at Autun, then Brienne military academy (May 1779), where he was bullied for his accent, birthplace and poor French; became a Corsican nationalist and admirer of Paoli. [source: NAP]
- École militaire, Paris, from September 1784; father died February 1785; completed the two-year course in one; examined by Laplace; first Corsican to graduate; commissioned second lieutenant in the La Fère artillery regiment, September 1785. [source: NAP]
- Fought on the Jacobin/pro-French side against Paoli in Corsica; in June 1793 the Corsican assembly condemned the family and the Buonapartes fled to Toulon. [source: NAP]
- Siege of Toulon 1793: appointed artillery commander (September, with help of fellow Corsican Saliceti); his plan to take a hill fort dominating the harbour succeeded in the assault of 16–17 December; promoted brigadier general. [source: NAP; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Toulon_(1793)]
- Briefly arrested (9 August 1794) after Robespierre's fall for his Jacobin links. [source: NAP]
- 13 Vendémiaire (5 October 1795): defended the Convention against a royalist rising with canister fire ("the whiff of grapeshot", Thomas Carlyle's phrase); about 300 to 1,400 rebels died. Made commander of the Army of the Interior (26 October), head of the Army of Italy (January 1796). [source: NAP]
- Married Joséphine de Beauharnais (born in the French Lesser Antilles; her family owned slaves on sugar plantations) in a civil ceremony on 9 March 1796; around then began styling himself "Napoleon Bonaparte". [source: NAP]
- Italian campaign 1796–97: Montenotte campaign knocked Piedmont-Sardinia out in two weeks; victories at Castiglione, Bassano, Arcole, Rivoli (January 1797); preliminary Peace of Leoben (18 April 1797); ended Venice's 1,100-year independence; looted e.g. the Horses of Saint Mark. Army captured 150,000 prisoners; Bonaparte extracted an estimated 45 million French pounds plus 12 million in precious metals and jewels and more than 300 paintings and sculptures. Treaty of Campo Formio with Austria; returned to Paris a hero, 5 December 1797. [source: NAP]

## The Egyptian expedition, 1798–1801
- Aims: undermine Britain's route to India; he hoped to link up with Tipu Sultan of Mysore. Expedition sailed from Toulon May 1798 with around 36,000 troops; took Hospitaller Malta 9 June 1798. Landing at Alexandria: EGY gives 28 June (landing)/1–2 July (march on and capture of Alexandria); NAP says landed 1 July — dates differ by source. [source: NAP; EGY]
- Savants: the Commission des Sciences et des Arts (est. 16 March 1798) had 167 members, of whom all but 16 went to Egypt (hence the "151" sometimes given; NAP and EGY say 167). More than half were engineers and technicians. Included Monge, Berthollet, Fourier, Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, Vivant Denon, Conté, Malus, Dolomieu. They founded the Institut d'Égypte. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commission_des_Sciences_et_des_Arts; EGY; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosetta_Stone]
- Battle of the Pyramids, 21 July 1798, near the village of Embabeh (Imbaba), about 24 km from the pyramids; French ~25,000 in divisional squares vs Murad Bey's Mamluk-led forces (estimates 21,000–50,000). French losses: 29 killed, 260 wounded (the battle article also says "about 300"). Egyptian/Mamluk losses vary widely by source: ~1,500 dead and wounded (EGY), ~2,000 killed (NAP), "several thousand to as many as 10,000" (battle article); hundreds drowned in the Nile. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Pyramids; NAP; EGY]
- Bonaparte's proclamations cast him as liberator of Egyptians from Mamluk "slavery", promising to respect religion and property and to establish dīwāns of local notables. [source: EGY]
- Battle of the Nile, 1 August 1798: Nelson captured or destroyed all but two French ships of the line, cutting the army off. [source: NAP]
- Revolt of Cairo, 21–22 October 1798: centred on al-Azhar; Bonaparte's aide Sulkowski and General Dupuy killed; French shelled the city from the Citadel and fired on al-Azhar Mosque, then stormed it; 5,000–6,000 Cairenes dead or wounded. [source: EGY]
- Syrian campaign 1799 (~13,000 men): took el-Arish, Gaza, Jaffa, Haifa. At Jaffa he ordered some 1,500–5,000 prisoners executed; failed to take Acre (defended by Ahmed al-Jazzar with British support); plague; alleged poisoning of plague-stricken soldiers with opium. Defeated an Ottoman landing at Abukir, 25 July 1799. [source: NAP]
- Left Egypt secretly 23–24 August 1799 without orders, leaving Kléber in command. Kléber was assassinated in Cairo on 14 June 1800 by the Syrian student Suleiman al-Halabi (who was impaled after his right hand was burned). Menou took command; after British-Ottoman victories in 1801, Menou capitulated at Alexandria on 2 September 1801 and signed Egyptian antiquities, including the Rosetta Stone, over to Britain. Treaty of Paris (25 June 1802) returned Egypt to Ottoman rule. [source: EGY]
- Cost: EGY states 15,000 French killed in action and 15,000 by disease; its infobox gives French casualties 38,500 (incl. 23,500 captured) and Ottoman 65,000 — treat as rough estimates. [source: EGY]
- Brought French, Arabic and Greek printing presses to Egypt. Long-term effects as framed by EGY: demonstrated European military-technological superiority, introduced the press and ideas such as liberalism and incipient nationalism, and helped open the way to Muhammad Ali's modernising rule and later the Nahda; "to modernist historians, the French arrival marks the start of the modern Middle East" (citing Cleveland). Treat the "start of the modern Middle East" framing as a historiographical position, not fact. [source: EGY]
- Rosetta Stone: granodiorite stele bearing a decree of 196 BC (dated 27 March 196 BC) for Ptolemy V, in hieroglyphic, Demotic and Ancient Greek; found mid-July 1799 by Lieutenant Pierre-François Bouchard while strengthening Fort Julien near Rashid (Rosetta); ~760 kg. Reported in the Courrier de l'Égypte (September 1799). Surrendered to the British 1801, landed at Portsmouth February 1802, in the British Museum since 1802 (exhibited almost continuously since June 1802). Edges inscribed "Captured in Egypt by the British Army in 1801" and "Presented by King George III". Champollion announced his decipherment in Paris in 1822. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosetta_Stone]
- Repatriation: in July 2003 Zahi Hawass (Egypt's Supreme Council of Antiquities) called for its return, calling it the "icon of our Egyptian identity"; British Museum gave Egypt a full-size replica in 2005 (Rashid National Museum). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosetta_Stone]
- Description de l'Égypte published on Napoleon's orders 1809–c.1821/29 (sources give end-dates 1821 or c.1829); foundational for Egyptology. [source: EGY; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commission_des_Sciences_et_des_Arts]

## Al-Jabarti and Egyptian responses
- 'Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti (1753–1825), Cairo 'alim trained at al-Azhar, family of Somali (Zeila/Jabarta) origin; kept a monthly chronicle. Three works: Tarikh Muddat al-Faransis bi-Misr (on the first seven months of occupation, completed late 1798), Mazhar al-Taqdis bi-Zawal Dawlat al-Faransis (December 1801), and the long 'Aja'ib al-Athar fi'l-Tarajim wa'l-Akhbar. The French-occupation sections are published in English as Napoleon in Egypt (tr. Shmuel Moreh). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Rahman_al-Jabarti]
- He corrected the bad Arabic grammar of Bonaparte's proclamation, rejected republican ideas (laicism, liberty, equality) and insisted on revelation over European rationalism; yet he was astonished by French science and technology and admired what he saw as the fairness of French trials (e.g. Kléber's assassin), and judged Ottoman rule more tyrannical. His disciple Hasan al-Attar shared this ambivalence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Rahman_al-Jabarti; EGY citing Moreh 2004]
- Shaykh Abdullah al-Sharqawi, who headed Napoleon's Cairo dīwān, later called the French "materialist, libertine philosophers ... they deny the Resurrection, and the afterlife, and ... [the] prophets". [source: EGY; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Pyramids]

## 18 Brumaire, Consulate and coronation
- Coup of 18 Brumaire: 9 November 1799; next day (19 Brumaire, 10 November) at Saint-Cloud, the Five Hundred were dispersed by grenadiers after Lucien Bonaparte (president of the Council) rallied the troops; a rump appointed Bonaparte, Sieyès and Ducos provisional consuls. Allies included Talleyrand, Sieyès, Ducos, Fouché. [source: NAP; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coup_of_18_Brumaire]
- Constitution of the Year VIII (13 December 1799): three consuls for 10 years, real power with the First Consul (Cambacérès and Lebrun as Second/Third). Plebiscite approved 7 February 1800: official count over 3 million yes, 1,562 no; the vote was falsified by Lucien — NAP says he "doubled" the yes count; the Brumaire article says some 900,000 votes forged. [source: NAP; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coup_of_18_Brumaire]
- Historians' labels for the regime: "dictatorship by plebiscite", "soft despotism"; prefects created 1800; censorship and closure of most opposition newspapers. [source: NAP]
- Marengo, 14 June 1800 (rescued by Desaix's arrival); Treaty of Lunéville February 1801; Peace of Amiens with Britain, March 1802. [source: NAP]
- Consul for life by plebiscite, 1802: ~3.6 million yes, 8,374 no. [source: NAP]
- Legion of Honour created May 1802. Louisiana sold to the US in 1803 for $15 million. Execution of the Duke of Enghien (1804) after kidnapping from Baden. [source: NAP]
- Senate proclaimed him Emperor of the French 18 May 1804; plebiscite ~3.5 million yes, 2,569 no, with the yes count inflated by 300,000–500,000; turnout ~35%. [source: NAP]
- Coronation at Notre-Dame, 2 December 1804, with Pope Pius VII present; after the papal anointing Napoleon crowned himself (with a crown made to look medieval, called the "Crown of Charlemagne") and then crowned Joséphine. David painted the scene. [source: NAP; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_of_Napoleon]
- Kingdom of Italy proclaimed 17 March 1805, with stepson Eugène de Beauharnais as viceroy; crowned with the Iron Crown of Lombardy in Milan Cathedral on 23 May 1805. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Italy_(Napoleonic)]

## The Civil Code (1804), its spread and influence
- Officially Code civil des Français; promulgated/in force 21 March 1804; called "Code Napoléon" from 1807; still in force in France, much amended. Drafting commission appointed 12 August 1800: Portalis, Tronchet, Bigot de Préameneu, Maleville; Cambacérès and Napoleon chaired plenary sessions. Sources differ on Napoleon's role: CODE says he "was not directly involved in the drafting" but his support was crucial; NAP says he "participated actively" in Council of State sessions revising drafts. The Legislative Body rejected the preliminary bill 142–139 (December 1801) before Napoleon purged/cowed the assemblies. [source: CODE; NAP]
- Content: equality before the law, religious toleration, secure property, equal inheritance for legitimate children, end of feudal vestiges; but established the husband's supremacy over wife and children ("women had even fewer rights than children"), and abolished divorce by mutual consent. [source: CODE; NAP]
- Article 213 (1804): "Le mari doit protection à sa femme, la femme obéissance à son mari" (the husband owes protection to his wife, the wife obedience to her husband); basis of married women's legal incapacity until the reform of 18 February 1938. [source: https://criminocorpus.org/fr/legislation/droit/versions/718/213/pdf/ (via WebSearch); https://www.laculturegenerale.com/code-civil-femme-inferiorite/]
- Further codes: civil procedure 1806, commercial 1807, criminal procedure 1808, penal code 1810; seven codes in all. Alfred Cobban called the Code "the most effective agency for the propagation of the basic principles of the French Revolution." Robert Holtman regarded it as one of the few documents that have influenced the whole world. [source: NAP; CODE]
- Not the first civil code (Bavaria 1756, Prussia's Allgemeines Landrecht 1794, West Galicia 1797) but the first widely adopted in Europe. Remained in force in the Rhineland, Berg and Baden until the German BGB of 1900. Romania's 1864 civil code drew on it (in force until 2011). [source: CODE]
- Outside Europe: extended to Mauritius as the Code Napoléon by decree of Governor Decaen, 21 April 1808 (now "Code Civil Mauricien"); Louisiana's civil code retains French/Spanish civil-law elements; the Civil Code of Lower Canada (Quebec) is often grouped with it though mainly derived from the Coutume de Paris. [source: CODE]
- Egypt: the Mixed Courts, founded October 1875 under Khedive Isma'il, used codes "inspired by the French Civil Code and British common law but with significant Islamic and local principles"; proceedings in French; Egypt's 1883 national codes were based on them. (A popular source dates the courts to after Isma'il's fall in 1879 — Wikipedia's October 1875 is the better-supported date.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_Courts_of_Egypt]
- Chile: Andrés Bello's Civil Code, passed 14 December 1855; "traditionally" the Napoleonic Code is considered its main source of inspiration, especially in the law of obligations, but not a transcription. NOTE: CODE separately claims most Latin American law is "not heavily influenced" by the Napoleonic Code, citing the Chilean and Mexican codes — the Wikipedia articles contradict each other; write it as "drew significantly on" with a caveat. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilean_Civil_Code; CODE]
- Japan: Gustave Boissonade's French-model draft civil code (1890) was superseded by a German-model code. (Searched only; secondary.) [source: https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-00833595v1/file/Sylvain_Soleil.pdf (via WebSearch); https://www.britannica.com/topic/Napoleonic-Code]
- Other reforms: Council of State (December 1799); metric system officially introduced 1801; revolutionary calendar abolished December 1805; lycées, Imperial University (1808), baccalaureate (1809); hereditary imperial nobility from 1806 (over 3,000 nobles, 30,000+ Legion of Honour members). [source: NAP]

## The Concordat
- Signed 15 July 1801 in Paris between the French Republic and Pius VII; published 8 April 1802 together with the unilateral Organic Articles increasing state control. Declared Catholicism "the religion of the great majority of the French" but not the state religion; the Church renounced claims to lands confiscated after 1790; the state paid clergy salaries; the government nominated bishops and clergy swore loyalty. In force until 1905 (still in Alsace–Moselle). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concordat_of_1801; NAP]
- Napoleon on religion (1800): "it was by making myself a Catholic that I won the war in the Vendée, by making myself a Moslem that I established myself in Egypt, by making myself an ultramontane that I turned men's hearts towards me in Italy. If I were to govern a nation of Jews I would rebuild the Temple of Solomon." [source: NAP]
- Annexed Rome May 1809; excommunicated (bull Quum memoranda, 1809); pope arrested July 1809, held at Savona then Fontainebleau, not released until 1814. [source: NAP]

## Restoration of slavery and the war in Haiti
- The Convention abolished slavery in all colonies on 4 February 1794 (implemented only in Saint-Domingue, Guadeloupe and Cayenne/Guiana). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_20_May_1802]
- Law of 20 May 1802 (30 Floréal an X): maintained/reinstated slavery and the slave trade in colonies returned by the Treaty of Amiens (Martinique, Tobago, Saint Lucia) and where abolition had never been applied (Réunion, Isle de France/Mauritius). Slavery was restored in Guadeloupe by consular decree of 16 July 1802 and in Cayenne by decree of 7 December 1802. Historians debate "maintenance" vs "reinstatement" (Frédéric Régent: legally it re-established slavery). France was the only country to reinstate slavery after abolishing it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_20_May_1802; RS]
- Accompanying measures: decree of 2 July 1802 banning Black people and people of colour from entering mainland France; circular of 8 January 1803 prohibiting mixed marriages; return to pre-1789 discrimination against free people of colour. [source: RS; NAP]
- Debate on motive: Yves Benot (1992) saw it as deliberate; others (e.g. Philippe Girard summarising) stress two years of hesitation and opportunism; Branda and Lentz (Fondation Napoléon) speak of a "gradual advance" to which Napoleon "was no stranger"; influence of a "Creole party" including Joséphine is argued by Brégeon and disputed by others. In 1800 Napoleon had argued before the Council of State that Saint-Domingue's freed Black population would serve France "if bound to us by the interest of their freedom". [source: RS]
- Guadeloupe: Richepance's expedition; Louis Delgrès and about 1,000 followers blew themselves up rather than surrender at Matouba, 28 May 1802; slavery restored (formally 16 July 1802). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Delgrès; NAP]
- Saint-Domingue expedition under Napoleon's brother-in-law Leclerc: fleet left Brest 14 December 1801; landed February 1802; total ~31,000 French troops sent. Toussaint Louverture surrendered, then was seized in June 1802 and deported; imprisoned at Fort de Joux (Doubs) from 25 August 1802; died there 7 April 1803. Yellow fever killed ~15,000 French in two months; Leclerc died of it 1 November 1802; Rochambeau used slave-hunting dogs from Cuba. French defeated at Vertières, 18 November 1803, by Dessalines. Only 7,000–8,000 of 31,000 French soldiers survived; over 20 generals died (expedition infobox: French losses 35,000–40,000; Haitian ~80,000 — estimates). Haiti's independence proclaimed 1 January 1804. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Domingue_expedition; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toussaint_Louverture; NAP]
- Defeat in Saint-Domingue contributed to the sale of Louisiana (April 1803). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution; NAP]
- During the Hundred Days, under British pressure, Napoleon banned the slave trade (not slavery) by decree of 29 March 1815. France finally abolished slavery by decree of 27 April 1848 (Victor Schœlcher). France imposed a 150-million-franc indemnity on Haiti in 1825 (reduced to 90 million in 1838). [source: RS]

## Campaigns 1805–1809 and Trafalgar
- Ulm (October 1805): Mack surrendered; ~60,000 Austrians captured for ~2,000 French casualties. [source: NAP]
- Trafalgar, 21 October 1805, off Cádiz: Nelson's 27 ships of the line defeated Villeneuve's Franco-Spanish 33; British 458 killed, 1,208 wounded; Franco-Spanish 4,395 killed, 2,541 wounded, 7,000–8,000 captured, 17 ships captured, 1 destroyed. Nelson shot by a French marksman, died ~3 hours later. Royal Navy never again seriously challenged. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Trafalgar; NAP]
- Austerlitz, 2 December 1805: French 65,000–75,000 vs Russo-Austrian 73,000–89,000; French losses ~8,500–9,300; allied 27,000–36,000. Feigned weakness on the right, seizure of the Pratzen Heights. Peace of Pressburg 26 December 1805 (40 million franc indemnity). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Austerlitz; NAP]
- Jena–Auerstedt, 14 October 1806: Prussian army shattered (Prussian losses ~41,000; French ~12,600). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Jena–Auerstedt]
- Eylau (February 1807, bloody stalemate), Friedland (14 June 1807); Tilsit (raft on the Niemen, from 25 June): Russia joined the Continental System; Prussia lost about half its territory and population; Kingdom of Westphalia (Jérôme) and Duchy of Warsaw created. [source: NAP]
- Aspern-Essling (21–22 May 1809): first serious check, ~23,000 casualties each side. Wagram, 5–6 July 1809: French ~172,000 vs Austrian ~136,000; casualties ~35,000 French and ~39,000 Austrian (NAP: 37,000–39,000 each). Treaty of Schönbrunn (October 1809): Austria lost over 3 million subjects, Illyrian lands, 200 million francs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Wagram; NAP]
- Married Marie Louise of Austria April 1810 after annulment from Joséphine; son (King of Rome) born 20 March 1811. At its height Napoleon ruled about 40% of Europe's population directly or through satellites. [source: NAP]
- Franco-Ottoman and Franco-Persian alliances: Selim III recognised Napoleon as emperor (Feb 1806); Franco-Persian alliance with Fath-Ali Shah collapsed with Tilsit (1807). [source: NAP]

## End of the Holy Roman Empire; Germany and Italy remade
- Final Recess (Reichsdeputationshauptschluss), 25 February 1803: secularised church states and absorbed free imperial cities; 112 imperial estates disappeared. Over the whole mediatisation (1802–1814/15), German states fell from almost 300 to 39. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_mediatisation]
- Confederation of the Rhine: treaty signed 12 July 1806 by 16 German states, Napoleon as "Protector"; lasted 1806–1813, dissolving after Leipzig. Bavaria and Württemberg raised to kingdoms; Baden, Hesse-Darmstadt and Berg to grand duchies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederation_of_the_Rhine]
- On 1 August 1806 a French envoy told the Reichstag Napoleon no longer recognised the Empire; Francis II abdicated and dissolved the Holy Roman Empire on 6 August 1806 (he had already proclaimed himself Emperor of Austria on 11 August 1804). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Holy_Roman_Empire]
- Kingdom of Westphalia (1807–1813): constitution promulgated 15 November 1807, often called the first modern constitution in a German monarchy; 1808 laws granting Jews equal rights, Germany's first. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Westphalia]
- Brothers as kings: Joseph in Naples (1806), Louis in Holland (1806), Jérôme in Westphalia; Joseph moved to Spain 1808. Napoleonic rule abolished feudal remnants, guilds, ghettos and (in Spain) the Inquisition. NAP links the streamlining of Germany and anti-French reaction to German nationalism and unification, and similarly to Italian unification — a contested long-term causal claim. [source: NAP]
- Poland: Napoleon called 1812 the "Second Polish War"; his reputation remains favourable in Poland, whose anthem mentions him. [source: NAP]

## Jewish emancipation
- Jews in France had full civil rights from September 1791. Revolutionary/Napoleonic armies abolished ghettos in conquered lands (e.g. Ancona, Venice, Rome). [source: NAP; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_and_the_Jews]
- Assembly of Jewish Notables convened 1806; Grand Sanhedrin of 71 members (two-thirds rabbis, one-third laymen), sat 9 February–9 March 1807 under Rabbi David Sintzheim, declaring Jews' loyalty to the French state. Consistories organised by decree, March 1808. Russia's Holy Synod denounced the Sanhedrin. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Sanhedrin; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_and_the_Jews; NAP]
- "Infamous Decree" (décret infâme), 17 March 1808: annulled, reduced or postponed debts owed to Jews, imposed a ten-year restriction on Jewish money-lending and limits on migration; July 1808 decree required fixed family names. Emancipation thus came with discrimination. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_and_the_Jews; NAP]

## Spain, Portugal and the Latin American chain reaction
- Portugal refused to enforce the blockade; Junot's 24,000 troops crossed the Pyrenees 17 October 1807 with Spanish consent; Lisbon occupied late November–1 December 1807. [source: NAP; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfer_of_the_Portuguese_court_to_Brazil]
- Portuguese court flight: Queen Maria I, Prince Regent John and nearly 10,000 people embarked 27 November 1807, sailed 29 November with a British naval escort; reached Salvador 22 January 1808, then Rio de Janeiro. Ports of Brazil opened to foreign shipping; Rio was capital of the Portuguese empire for 13 years ("metropolitan reversal"); Brazil raised to co-kingdom (United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil and the Algarves) 1815; John returned 26 April 1821; Brazil independent 7 September 1822 under Pedro I. Rio's enslaved population rose by an estimated 200% 1808–1822. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfer_of_the_Portuguese_court_to_Brazil]
- Spain: Tumult of Aranjuez (17–19 March 1808) forced Charles IV's abdication for Ferdinand VII; abdications of Bayonne, 5–7 May 1808 (castle of Marracq), both ceded the throne to Napoleon, who gave it to Joseph. Ferdinand interned at Valençay (Talleyrand's château) until the Treaty of Valençay, 11 December 1813. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdications_of_Bayonne]
- Dos de Mayo, 2 May 1808: Madrid rising sparked by Murat's attempt to remove remaining royals to Bayonne; artillery officers Daoíz and Velarde killed defending the Monteleón barracks; mass executions on 3 May (French military commission under Grouchy); the Móstoles mayors' "declaration of war" the same day. Casualty figures vary (Spanish e.g. ~200 dead + ~300 executed; French 31–150 dead). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dos_de_Mayo_Uprising]
- Goya: The Second of May 1808 and The Third of May 1808, both painted in 1814 for the restored government at Goya's own suggestion, to commemorate "our glorious insurrection against the Tyrant of Europe"; now in the Prado; influenced Manet and Picasso (Guernica, Massacre in Korea). The Disasters of War prints made mostly 1810–14, completed 1820. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Third_of_May_1808]
- Peninsular War 1808–1814: "guerrilla" ("little war") entered the vocabulary; the war tied up some 300,000 French-led troops 1808–12; French casualties over 150,000; total military and civilian dead estimated 1,000,000+ (Clodfelter). Bailén (July 1808) was the first major French defeat; Wellington's Anglo-Portuguese army; atrocities on both sides. Napoleon: "the unlucky war [that] ruined me". Cortes of Cádiz produced the liberal Spanish Constitution of 1812. Some Spanish publicists exaggerated guerrilla achievements. [source: NAP; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peninsular_War]
- Latin America: the abdications were rejected by "patriots" in Spain and Spanish America; juntas formed in Spain to rule in Ferdinand's name were replicated in America (e.g. Junta Suprema de Caracas, 19 April 1810; new juntas in 1810 when the Central Junta fell). Claiming to act for the captive king, they gave independence advocates a public platform. Hidalgo's rising in Mexico (1810). Wars of independence dated 25 September 1808–1833 in the infobox. Joseph Bonaparte never recognised any Spanish American independence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_American_wars_of_independence; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdications_of_Bayonne; NAP]

## Continental System
- Berlin Decree, 21 November 1806 (answering Britain's blockade order of May 1806), banned trade with Britain; Milan Decree (1807) declared neutral ships calling at British ports lawful prize. Widely evaded through smuggling (Heligoland, Malta, the Baltic) often with connivance of Napoleon's own satellite rulers; Britain found replacement markets in the Americas. British economy suffered 1810–12. Russia's withdrawal (allowing neutral shipping, December 1810) motivated the 1812 invasion. Portugal's refusal led to the 1807 invasion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_System; NAP]

## The invasion of Russia, 1812
- Crossed the Niemen 24 June 1812. Size: NAP gives "around 450,000 frontline troops, about a third native French speakers"; the campaign article gives first wave 440,000–449,000 and 600,000–685,000 total over the campaign, "only half of whom were from France" (incl. ~90,000 Poles, ~117,000 Germans). Russian forces ~180,000–220,000 at the outset. [source: NAP; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_invasion_of_Russia]
- Borodino, 7 September 1812: NAP gives 44,000 Russian and 35,000 French dead/wounded/captured; the battle article gives French 30,000–42,000 and Russian 40,000–53,000. Napoleon entered Moscow 14 September; the city was fired on Governor Rostopchin's orders; left after about six weeks. [source: NAP; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Borodino]
- Retreat: Berezina crossing late November; Napoleon left the army 5 December; about 75,000 remnants recrossed the Niemen (NAP). Losses (estimates): Grande Armée 580,000–630,000 total losses incl. 300,000–350,000 dead, plus prisoners and deserters; Russian military losses ~210,000 dead (campaign infobox) or "up to 300,000" (NAP); total military and civilian deaths c. 1,000,000 (Zamoyski). Most losses came in summer and autumn from logistics failure, disease (typhus) and desertion, not only the winter — Minard's chart. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_invasion_of_Russia; NAP]
- Russians call it the "Patriotic War" of 1812. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_invasion_of_Russia]

## Leipzig, Elba, the Hundred Days, Waterloo
- Leipzig ("Battle of the Nations"), 16–19 October 1813: largest battle of the Napoleonic wars; coalition ~257,000 vs French ~177,000 (16–17 Oct); coalition casualties 54,000–80,000; French 60,000–79,000 (NAP: 38,000 killed/wounded + 15,000 prisoners, up to 50,000 more lost in retreat). Saxon units defected; Poniatowski (only foreign Marshal of the Empire) drowned in the Elster. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Leipzig; NAP]
- Allies entered Paris 31 March 1814; Senate declared him deposed 2 April; unconditional abdication 6 April; Treaty of Fontainebleau signed 11 April (ratified 13 April), giving him sovereignty of Elba with 400 guardsmen. Attempted suicide by poison the following night. Arrived Portoferraio 4 May 1814. [source: NAP; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Fontainebleau_(1814)]
- Escaped Elba 26 February 1815 with ~1,000 men; landed Golfe-Juan 1 March; at Laffrey before Grenoble he reportedly told royalist troops "If any of you will shoot his Emperor, here I am" (anecdote); Ney, who had promised to bring him back "in an iron cage", joined him 14 March; entered Paris 20 March; Louis XVIII had fled. Vienna powers declared him an outlaw 13 March. "Hundred Days" = 20 March–8 July 1815 (110 days). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_Days; NAP]
- Waterloo, 18 June 1815: French 72,000–73,000 vs a coalition total of 118,000–120,000 engaged (Wellington's Anglo-allied army of British, Dutch, Belgian and German troops, plus Blücher's Prussians arriving in the afternoon; NAP gives the campaign armies as Wellington 112,000 and Blücher 130,000); French casualties ~25,000–27,000 killed/wounded plus prisoners; coalition ~24,000 (Wellington ~17,000). Wellington: "the nearest-run thing you ever saw in your life". Ney executed 7 December 1815. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Waterloo; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_Days]
- Abdicated 22 June 1815; surrendered to Captain Maitland of HMS Bellerophon 15 July 1815. [source: NAP]

## Saint Helena and death
- Saint Helena, South Atlantic, ~1,870 km off West Africa; arrived Jamestown October 1815 on HMS Northumberland with 27 followers; guarded by 2,100 soldiers and 10 ships; Briars pavilion from 17 October, then damp, rat-infested Longwood House. Governor Hudson Lowe (met 17 April 1816). [source: NAP; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon%27s_exile_to_St._Helena]
- Las Cases' Memorial of Saint Helena created the legend of a liberal, European-unifying Napoleon (NAP dates it 1822; the exile article says 1823). [source: NAP; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon%27s_exile_to_St._Helena]
- Died 5 May 1821, aged 51; will blamed the "English oligarchy". Both autopsies: stomach cancer (as his father); the arsenic-poisoning theory is undercut by similar arsenic levels in childhood and family hair samples; a 2021 study again concluded stomach cancer. Remains returned 15 December 1840 (700,000–1,000,000 lined the route to Les Invalides); sarcophagus under the dome since 1861. [source: NAP]
- Height ~168 cm (5 ft 6 in), average for his time; the "short" image came largely from British caricature. [source: NAP]

## Congress of Vienna and the conservative order
- Congress of Vienna, September 1814–June 1815, chaired by Metternich (with Gentz); Final Act signed 9 June 1815, nine days before Waterloo. Main actors: Metternich (Austria), Castlereagh (Britain), Hardenberg (Prussia), Tsar Alexander I/Nesselrode (Russia), Talleyrand (France). Ottoman Empire invited but sent no delegation; Spain did not sign. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Vienna]
- Settlement: German Confederation (39 states) under Austrian leadership; Kingdom of the Netherlands as buffer; "Congress Poland" under the tsar; Prussia took 60% of Saxony, Rhineland territory and Swedish Pomerania; Kraków a free city. Aim: balance of power rather than simple restoration. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Vienna; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_mediatisation]
- Slave trade: Declaration of the Eight Courts Relative to the Universal Abolition of the Slave Trade, 8 February 1815 (Austria, France, Britain, Portugal, Prussia, Russia, Spain, Sweden), annexed to the Final Act (Annex XV); condemned the trade but set no date for ending it and had no enforcement. Castlereagh drove it under British public pressure. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_for_the_Suppression_of_the_African_Slave_Trade; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decree_of_29_March_1815; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_slave_trade; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Vienna]
- Historians have linked Vienna to the "second slavery" of the 19th-century Americas (Lane, Santos & El Youssef, Journal of Global Slavery 2019 — cited in the Vienna article bibliography; argument not read). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Vienna]
- Concert of Europe: Quadruple Alliance (1815), France admitted at Aix-la-Chapelle (1818); Holy Alliance of Russia, Austria and Prussia (Castlereagh: "a piece of sublime mysticism and nonsense"); Troppau Protocol (1820) on intervention against revolutions. Its first phase is called the "Age of Metternich". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concert_of_Europe]
- Metternich (1773–1859): Austrian foreign minister from 1809, chancellor 1821–1848; arranged the Marie Louise marriage; Carlsbad Decrees (1819); forced out by the Revolutions of 1848. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klemens_von_Metternich]
- Henry Kissinger's dissertation A World Restored (1954 dissertation; the Vienna article cites a 1964 edition) is an influential admiring study of the settlement. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Vienna]

## Cost and legacy (facts)
- NAP: the Napoleonic wars "cost six million lives"; a Standpoint review of Roberts gives "around three million military and one million civilian deaths" for the Revolutionary and Napoleonic wars together — give a range (roughly 3.5–6 million). [source: NAP; https://www.standpointmag.co.uk/?p=1803 (via WebSearch)]
- Napoleonic looting of art (e.g. to the Louvre); censorship; property-qualified indirect elections; reduced rights of women, children and people of colour. [source: NAP]
- Contemporary reactions: Beethoven had titled the Third Symphony for Bonaparte; on hearing (via his secretary Ferdinand Ries) that Napoleon had made himself emperor (May 1804), he reportedly tore the title page; published as Sinfonia eroica (1806). Hegel, at Jena on 13 October 1806, wrote of seeing "the Emperor – this world-soul" on horseback. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._3_(Beethoven); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Wilhelm_Friedrich_Hegel]
- The Napoleonic legend fuelled opposition to the Bourbon Restoration (1815–30); the 1840 return of the remains propped up Louis-Philippe; Napoleon III's regime relied on the legend. [source: NAP]

(English renderings vary by outlet; original French at elysee.fr not read.) ## Debates
- Hero or tyrant: historians ask whether he was "an enlightened despot who laid the foundations of modern Europe" or "a megalomaniac who wrought greater misery than any man before the coming of Hitler" (phrasing from Max Hastings's WSJ review of Roberts). Pieter Geyl's Napoleon: For and Against (1947) surveyed French historians' shifting verdicts. Chandler rejected the Hitler comparison as degrading to Napoleon. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legacy_of_Napoleon; NAP]
- Andrew Roberts defends the epithet "Great": a revisionist portrait based on ~33,000 newly published letters, stressing the reforms; critics (Spectator review; Andrew Adonis in the New Statesman) say the "immense collateral damage" gets too little weight; reviewers note he does treat 1802 slavery and Saint-Domingue, which Napoleon later called (per Roberts' quotation) "the greatest error that in all my government I ever committed". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Roberts,_Baron_Roberts_of_Belgravia; https://m.csmonitor.com/Books/Book-Reviews/2015/0313/Napoleon-argues-for-the-humanity-of-the-infamous-French-emperor ; https://www.newstatesman.com/?p=114823 (via WebSearch)]
- Michael Broers: applies concepts from the study of overseas "cultural imperialism" to Napoleonic Europe; distinguishes an "inner empire" (a core region, already predisposed to the reforms, which he likens to a "middle kingdom" at Europe's crossroads) where reforms took root from an "outer empire" where rule was military and reforms shallow — reception describes it as creative but controversial. [source: https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/historical-journal/article/monstre-sacre-the-question-of-cultural-imperialism-and-the-napoleonic-empire/D91E4B23F20EA586FD13E1B7407941A1 ; https://ehne.fr/en/encyclopedia/themes/political-europe/national-construction-and-european-issues/napoleonic-europe (via WebSearch); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Broers]
- Sudhir Hazareesingh: the Napoleonic legend was a popular, liberal-patriotic memory used by veterans and ordinary people to defy the Restoration; his Black Spartacus recentres the period on Toussaint and the struggle against slavery. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legacy_of_Napoleon; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudhir_Hazareesingh]
- Slavery: deliberate policy (Benot; Ribbe's polemic) vs hesitation and opportunism under colonial-lobby pressure (Lentz, Branda, Brégeon, Girard); "maintenance" vs "reinstatement" terminology (Régent). [source: RS; BIC]
- Egypt: Enlightenment science vs "a masterstroke of propaganda" masking conquest; Juan Cole on "liberal imperialism"; the "start of the modern Middle East" thesis (Cleveland) vs the view that internal Egyptian/Ottoman change mattered more (not fully researched). [source: EGY; https://zenon.dainst.org/Record/000739492/Description]
- Latin American independence: Napoleon "indirectly began" the process by creating a vacancy on the Spanish throne (NAP); historians (Guerra) stress loyalty to Ferdinand was initially near-universal and juntas acted in his name. [source: NAP; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdications_of_Bayonne]
- Vienna: Kissinger's admiration for a stable balance of power vs critics who stress repression of liberalism and nationalism and the slavery declaration's lack of teeth. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Vienna]
