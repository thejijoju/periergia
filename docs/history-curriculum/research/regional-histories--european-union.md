## 1. Ideas of European unity before 1950
- Richard von Coudenhove-Kalergi's manifesto *Paneuropa* (1923) launched the Paneuropean Union, the oldest European unification movement; it was banned by Nazi Germany in 1933 and refounded after the war. [source: PAN]
- French foreign minister Aristide Briand proposed a European federal union in a speech to the League of Nations on 5 September 1929, followed in 1930 by his "Memorandum on the Organization of a Regime of European Federal Union"; his efforts were overtaken by the Depression and the rise of Nazism. [source: BRI]
- The Ventotene Manifesto (official title *Per un'Europa libera e unita. Progetto d'un manifesto*, "For a Free and United Europe. A Draft Manifesto") was written by Altiero Spinelli and Ernesto Rossi (with Eugenio Colorni) while they were confined by Mussolini's regime on the island of Ventotene; Spinelli and Rossi completed it in June 1941. It called for a federal European republic after the defeat of fascism, called the League of Nations useless and "even harmful", and argued that the decisive political divide was no longer more or less democracy or socialism but between those seeking national power and those seeking "a solid international State". It declared "the European revolution must be socialist". [source: VEN; SPIN]
- Spinelli (1907–1986) joined the Communist Party at 17, spent ten years in prison and six in confinement under fascism, broke with the party in 1937, founded the European Federalist Movement, became a European Commissioner (1970) and MEP; his Draft Treaty Establishing the European Union was adopted by the European Parliament on 14 February 1984 (237 to 31, 43 abstentions) and fed into the Single European Act and Maastricht. The Parliament's main Brussels building is named after him. [source: SPIN]
- Churchill's Zurich speech of 19 September 1946 called for "a kind of United States of Europe" (without Britain as a member) and ended "let Europe arise!" [source: WebSearch summary of Churchill Centre / CVCE transcripts, https://winstonchurchill.org/resources/speeches/1946-1963-elder-statesman/united-states-of-europe/ — exact sentence wording not seen directly; quote only the phrase "a kind of United States of Europe"]
- The Hague Congress (Congress of Europe), 7–11 May 1948, brought together about 750 delegates (Adenauer, Churchill, Macmillan, Mitterrand, Spinelli among them) and led to the Council of Europe (1949) and, via Teitgen and Maxwell-Fyfe, the European Convention on Human Rights. [source: HAG]
- The Marshall Plan's only condition was that aid be used in a coordinated fashion (citing Milward, *The Reconstruction of Western Europe*). [source: SCH]

## 2. Schuman, the ECSC and the failed defence community (1950–54)
- The Schuman Declaration was made by French foreign minister Robert Schuman on 9 May 1950 (now Europe Day) at the Quai d'Orsay; it was drafted by Jean Monnet with Paul Reuter and Étienne Hirsch, and Schuman had secured Adenauer's support that morning. It proposed placing "Franco-German production of coal and steel as a whole … under a common High Authority, within the framework of an organization open to the participation of the other countries of Europe". [source: SCH]
- Adenauer reportedly called it "our breakthrough" (citing Judt). [source: SCH]
- Context: the Monnet Plan for French modernisation depended on German coal; French policy shifted away from Gaullist aims of permanent control of the Ruhr and Saar. [source: SCH; ECSC]
- UK Prime Minister Clement Attlee refused to join, saying he "would not accept the [UK] economy being handed over to an authority that is utterly undemocratic and is responsible to nobody". [source: ECSC]
- Treaty of Paris signed 18 April 1951 by the "Inner Six" (France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg); the ECSC came into being on 23 July 1952 and the treaty expired on 23 July 2002, fifty years later. Monnet was the first President of the High Authority (1952–55). [source: ECSC; SCH]
- The ECSC's four institutions — High Authority, Common Assembly, Special Council of ministers, Court of Justice — were the blueprint for today's Commission, Parliament, Council and Court. [source: ECSC]
- The Pleven Plan (24 October 1950), drafted mainly by Monnet, proposed a supranational European army to avoid separate German divisions; the European Defence Community treaty was signed 27 May 1952 by the Six. Four ratified, but on 30 August 1954 the French National Assembly voted 319 to 264 (31 abstentions) for indefinite postponement, killing it. [source: EDC]
- After the EDC's failure, Monnet founded the Action Committee for the United States of Europe (1955). [source: MON]

## 3. The Treaties of Rome and the 1960s
- The Messina-launched Spaak Committee report was accepted at the Venice Conference (29–30 May 1956); the Treaties of Rome — the EEC Treaty and the Euratom Treaty — were signed on 25 March 1957 on the Capitoline Hill and came into force on 1 January 1958. The EEC Treaty provided for a customs union, a common market in goods, labour, services and capital, a Common Agricultural Policy, a common transport policy and a European Social Fund. France insisted the new Commissions have fewer powers than the ECSC High Authority. [source: ROME]
- The preamble: "DETERMINED to lay the foundations of an ever closer union among the peoples of Europe" (official consolidated text; some translations read "establish the foundations"). [source: WebSearch, https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eut/teec/introduction/data.pdf; https://ofce.sciences-po.fr/blog/?p=8378]
- Merger Treaty signed in Brussels 8 April 1965, in force 1 July 1967, merging the executives of the three Communities. [source: MRG]
- The CAP was introduced in 1962; its share of the budget was about three-quarters in the mid-1980s (WP gives both "75% in 1984" and "73% in 1985") falling to 37% in 2017. Critics coined "butter mountains" and "wine lakes" for surpluses; dumped surpluses and subsidies have been criticised for disadvantaging developing-country farmers, though the EU is also the largest importer of farm products from developing countries. [source: CAP]
- The Hallstein Commission's 1965 CAP-financing proposals (own resources, more power for Parliament, majority voting) provoked de Gaulle's "empty chair crisis": from 30 June 1965 France boycotted the Council for six months; the Luxembourg compromise (January 1966) let a state veto matters of "very important" national interest, entrenching unanimity in practice. [source: HALL]
- De Gaulle opposed a supranational Europe, preferring a Europe of sovereign nations acting as a third pole between the US and USSR; he signed the Élysée Treaty with West Germany in January 1963 and twice vetoed British entry (1963, 1967). He reportedly saw Britain as a "trojan horse" for the United States. [source: DG; E73]
- Britain applied (with Ireland, Denmark and Norway) in 1961 (E73 says 31 July 1961; the ENL table gives 10 August 1961 for the formal UK application); after de Gaulle resigned in 1969, Pompidou lifted the veto. [source: E73; ENL; UK75]
- The Court of Justice: *Van Gend en Loos* (decided 5 February 1963) established direct effect — "The Community constitutes a new legal order of international law for the benefit of which the states have limited their sovereign rights…"; *Costa v ENEL* (1964, Case 6/64) established the primacy of Community law over national law. Neither doctrine is written in the treaty. [source: VGL; COSTA]

## 4. Enlargements and the 1970s–80s
- First enlargement 1 January 1973: UK, Ireland, Denmark (Ireland and Denmark held approving referendums in 1972). Norway's voters rejected membership in 1972 and again in 1994. Greenland, which joined with Denmark, gained home rule in 1979, voted 53% to leave in 1982 (after a fishing dispute) and left in 1985. [source: E73; ENL; GRL]
- UK referendum of 5 June 1975 (Wilson government, after "renegotiation"): 67.2% Yes, 32.8% No, turnout about 64%; only Shetland and the Western Isles voted No. Thatcher, newly Conservative leader, backed Yes; Tony Benn led the No side. Labour's 1983 manifesto pledged withdrawal. [source: UK75]
- Greece joined 1 January 1981; Spain and Portugal 1 January 1986 — none by referendum. The southern enlargement was primarily political: newly post-dictatorship states sought to secure democracy, and the EEC sought stable, NATO-aligned southern neighbours. Mitterrand had opposed it, fearing the former dictatorships were unready. [source: ENL]
- Morocco applied on 20 July 1987 and was rejected as not European; Turkey applied on 14 April 1987 (see §11). [source: ENL]
- European Monetary System agreed at Brussels on 5 December 1978 (championed by Giscard d'Estaing and Helmut Schmidt), in force 13 March 1979 with eight states. [source: EMS]
- First direct elections to the European Parliament in 1979; the elected Parliament first sat on 17 July 1979 under President Simone Veil. Turnout fell at every election until 2014 (low of about 42%) and was below 50% from 1999 to 2014; it rose to just over 50% in 2019. The Parliament has 720 members after the 2024 elections. [source: EP; DD]
- The term "democratic deficit" was used in 1979 by David Marquand (in *Parliament for Europe*) about the EEC. [source: DD]
- Erasmus student exchange adopted 15 June 1987, launched 1 July 1987: 3,244 students in 1987; over 13 million participants by 2021. [source: ERAS]
- Schengen Agreement signed 14 June 1985 by France, West Germany and the Benelux states; implementing convention 1990; the Schengen Area began 26 March 1995; incorporated into EU law by the Treaty of Amsterdam. [source: SCHG]

## 5. Delors, the Single European Act and the single market
- Jacques Delors (20 July 1925 – 27 December 2023), former French finance minister under Mitterrand, was President of the Commission from January 1985 to 1995. [source: DEL]
- Thatcher sent Lord Cockfield to the Delors Commission; his 1985 White Paper listed about 300 measures to complete the single market; the Commission relied on the Court's *Cassis de Dijon* mutual-recognition principle. [source: SM]
- The Single European Act (first major revision of Rome) was signed in Luxembourg on 17 February 1986 by nine states and in The Hague on 28 February 1986 by Denmark, Italy and Greece; in force 1 July 1987 (delayed by Ireland's *Crotty* case and a referendum of 26 May 1987, 69.9% Yes). It set the goal of a single market by 31 December 1992, extended qualified majority voting and introduced the cooperation procedure giving the Parliament a real legislative say. [source: SEA]
- The single market formally began on 1 January 1993; its "four freedoms" are goods, services, capital and people; Norway, Iceland and Liechtenstein participate via the EEA, Switzerland via sectoral treaties. [source: SM]
- Thatcher's Bruges speech, 20 September 1988 (College of Europe), answered Delors's 8 September speech to the TUC and became a founding text of British Euroscepticism (wording disputed — see Quotations). [source: BRU]

## 6. Maastricht and the euro
- Background: end of the Cold War and German reunification. The Delors Committee (asked at the Hanover summit, June 1988) reported in 1989 with a three-stage plan for EMU; an earlier Werner plan (October 1970) had proposed EMU in three stages. [source: EMU; MAA]
- Treaty on European Union signed at Maastricht on 7 February 1992 by the twelve; in force 1 November 1993. It created the EU on three "pillars", introduced EU citizenship, the principle of subsidiarity, and the path to monetary union with convergence ("Maastricht") criteria; the UK and later Denmark had opt-outs from the single currency. [source: MAA]
- Denmark rejected it on 2 June 1992 by 50.7% to 49.3% (turnout 83.1%); after the Edinburgh Agreement gave four opt-outs (EMU, defence, justice and home affairs, citizenship), Danes approved it on 18 May 1993 with 56.7%. [source: DK92; MAA]
- France approved it on 20 September 1992: 13,162,992 Yes to 12,623,582 No, i.e. about 51.0% (the *petit oui*). (MAA gives 50.8% — see conflicts.) Delors commented: "Europe began as an elitist project in which it was believed that all that was required was to convince the decision-makers. That phase of benign despotism is over." [source: FR92; MAA]
- UK ratification split the Conservatives ("Maastricht Rebels"); John Major tied it to a confidence vote. The German Bundestag approved it on 2 December 1992 by 543 of 562. [source: MAA]
- High Bundesbank interest rates after reunification strained the ERM from 1990. [source: MAA]
- Stability and Growth Pact (June/July 1997): deficits capped at 3% of GDP and debt at 60%; France and Germany themselves escaped the excessive-deficit procedure in the early 2000s. [source: SGP]
- The name "euro" was adopted in Madrid on 16 December 1995. The euro launched as an accounting currency on 1 January 1999 in eleven states (Austria, Belgium, Finland, France, Germany, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Portugal, Spain); Greece joined 1 January 2001; notes and coins circulated from 1 January 2002. [source: EURO; EZ]
- Croatia adopted the euro on 1 January 2023 (20th member); Bulgaria on 1 January 2026 (21st). The eurozone now has 21 members; six EU states keep their own currencies (Czech Republic, Denmark, Hungary, Poland, Romania, Sweden), all but Denmark legally committed to adopt the euro eventually. Used by about 358 million people; second-largest reserve currency. [source: EZ; EURO; CRO; BUL]
- "Flawed architecture": economists (IMF authors cited in WP) argued the eurozone was not an optimum currency area; Martin Feldstein called the euro "an experiment that failed"; Olivier Blanchard argued fiscal union would mitigate but not cure the periphery's competitiveness problem; Stiglitz and Krugman argued the crisis was a banking crisis more than a sovereign-debt one. [source: EZ; EURO; EZC]
- Debate: Der Spiegel (2010) reported that Mitterrand demanded the euro as the price for accepting German reunification (cited in DEL/EURO); but the Delors Report and Hanover mandate (1988–89) predate the fall of the Wall, so the "price of unity" story is at most a partial explanation. [source: DEL; EMU]

## 7. Amsterdam, Nice, eastern enlargement
- Copenhagen criteria (June 1993): stable democratic institutions, rule of law, human rights and minority protection, a functioning market economy, and ability to take on membership obligations (the *acquis*). [source: ENL]
- Austria, Finland and Sweden joined 1 January 1995. [source: ENL]
- Treaty of Amsterdam signed 2 October 1997, in force 1 May 1999; Treaty of Nice signed 26 February 2001, in force 1 February 2003 (after an initial Irish rejection on a 34% turnout). [source: AMS; NICE]
- 1 May 2004: Cyprus, Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovakia, Slovenia joined — the largest enlargement by states and population. Accession treaty signed 16 April 2003 at the Stoa of Attalus, Athens. The *acquis* then ran to about 3,000 directives and some 100,000 pages. [source: E04]
- Cyprus: Greek Cypriots rejected the Annan Plan in a referendum on 24 April 2004, so the divided island joined a week later with the north outside EU law in practice. [source: E04]
- Free movement for the 2004 entrants was subject to transitional restrictions in most old states; only Austria and Germany kept them to the 2011 limit. The "Polish plumber" became a French campaign cliché (Poland's tourist board turned it into an advert). [source: E04; ENL]
- Bulgaria and Romania joined 1 January 2007; Croatia 1 July 2013 (28 members until Brexit; 27 since). [source: ENL]
- After 2004 public opinion in Europe turned against further expansion. [source: ENL]

## 8. Constitution and Lisbon
- The Convention on the Future of Europe (from the Laeken European Council, December 2001) was chaired by Valéry Giscard d'Estaing. The Treaty establishing a Constitution for Europe was signed in Rome on 29 October 2004 by the then 25 states; 18 ratified it (Spain by referendum on 20 February 2005, 76% Yes; Luxembourg 10 July 2005, 57%). [source: TCE]
- France rejected it on 29 May 2005 (15,449,508 No to 12,808,270 Yes ≈ 54.7% No; turnout 69%); the Netherlands on 1 June 2005 (4,705,685 No to 2,940,730 Yes ≈ 61.5% No; turnout about 62%) — the first Dutch national referendum in over 200 years. [source: FR05; NL05; TCE]
- Article 50 (exit clause) was drafted by Lord Kerr of Kinlochard, secretary-general of the Convention. [source: A50]
- Treaty of Lisbon ("Reform Treaty") signed 13 December 2007 in the Jerónimos Monastery, in force 1 December 2009 (Czech ratification last, 13 November 2009). It amended rather than replaced the treaties, dropping constitutional symbols; created a full-time President of the European Council and strengthened the High Representative; extended qualified majority voting to at least 45 more areas; made the Charter of Fundamental Rights binding; renamed the EEC Treaty the Treaty on the Functioning of the EU. [source: LIS]
- Irish voters rejected Lisbon on 12 June 2008 (53.4% to 46.6%, turnout 53.1%) and approved it in a second referendum in October 2009 after guarantees (including keeping one commissioner per state). [source: IRL08; LIS]

## 9. How the institutions work (verified elements)
- Commission: the executive with the (near-)monopoly right of legislative initiative; Parliament can only ask the Commission to propose laws. [source: EP]
- Parliament: directly elected every five years since 1979; co-legislator with the Council under the ordinary procedure; the *Spitzenkandidaten* process (2014: Juncker for the EPP; 2024: von der Leyen re-nominated). [source: EP]
- Council of the EU (national ministers) and the European Council (heads of state/government, with a full-time President since Lisbon). [source: LIS]
- Court of Justice: doctrines of direct effect (1963) and primacy (1964). [source: VGL; COSTA]
- EU population about 452 million (2026 estimate); 24 official languages. [source: EUR]

## 10. The crisis decade: eurozone and Greece
- Greece: in late 2009 it emerged that deficits had been underreported; the 2009 deficit was finally revised to 15.4% of GDP. Bailouts: €110 billion (agreed 3 May 2010, eurozone and IMF), a second of €130 billion (2012) with private bondholders accepting a 53.5% cut in face value, and a third ESM programme of up to €86 billion (agreed July/August 2015). Greece exited the programmes in August 2018. GDP fell by more than 25% between 2009 and 2014; unemployment rose from below 10% to about 25% (2014–15); debt/GDP rose from 127% (2009) to 179% (2017). Twelve rounds of austerity 2010–16 brought riots and nationwide protests. On 30 June 2015 Greece became the first developed country to miss an IMF repayment on time. [source: GRC; GR3]
- Other programmes: Ireland (November 2010, €85 billion incl. €17.5 billion of its own reserves; exited December 2013), Portugal (May 2011, €78 billion), Spain (2012, bank recapitalisation only), Cyprus (2013). [source: EZC]
- Firewalls: EFSF (2010), ESM; the Fiscal Compact (Cameron vetoed treaty change, December 2011). [source: EZC]
- Draghi, ECB President, London, 26 July 2012: "whatever it takes" (full wording in Quotations); on 6 September 2012 the ECB announced Outright Monetary Transactions — unlimited bond purchases for countries in an EFSF/ESM programme — which calmed markets though never activated at the time. [source: DRA; EZC; WebSearch]
- Greek referendum, 5 July 2015 (called by Tsipras on 27 June, with banks closed and capital controls): 3,558,450 No to 2,245,537 Yes, i.e. 61.3% No; turnout about 62.5%; No won in every region. On 13 July Tsipras accepted a third bailout with harsher terms; Finance Minister Yanis Varoufakis resigned. [source: GR15]

## 11. Turkey's long candidacy
- Turkey joined the Council of Europe in 1950, applied for EEC association in 1959 and signed the Ankara Agreement on 12 September 1963 (in force 12 December 1964); customs union signed 1995; applied for membership 14 April 1987; the Commission deferred it in December 1989; candidate at the Helsinki European Council, 11 December 1999; negotiations opened 3 October 2005. [source: TUR]
- Austria and Germany wanted a "privileged partnership" short of membership; France and Austria promised referendums. Cyprus dispute: in December 2006 eight chapters were frozen because Turkey would not extend the customs union to Cyprus; Cyprus blocked six more in 2009. By May 2016 only 16 of 35 chapters were opened, one closed. Talks effectively frozen since 2016 (Parliament voted to suspend 24 November 2016) over rule-of-law backsliding. [source: TUR; ENL]
- Eurobarometer (Sept–Oct 2006): 59% of EU citizens opposed Turkish membership, 28% in favour. [source: TUR]

## 12. Migration and the Mediterranean
- 3 October 2013: a boat from Misrata, Libya, sank off Lampedusa; more than 360 died (confirmed toll 359 by 12 October); many were from Eritrea, Somalia and Ghana. [source: LAMP]
- Italy's Operation Mare Nostrum (18 October 2013 – 31 October 2014) rescued at least 150,000 people; other states declined to share its cost; it was replaced by Frontex's smaller Operation Triton focused on border protection. The IOM claimed deaths at sea rose ninefold afterwards. [source: MN; MIG]
- 2015: an estimated 1.3 million people requested asylum in Europe, the most in a year since the Second World War; over 1 million crossed the Mediterranean (about 85% to Greece via Turkey); mostly Syrians, also Afghans, Iraqis, Eritreans. At least 3,771 drowned in the Mediterranean in 2015; a single April shipwreck near Lampedusa killed about 800. [source: MIG]
- Germany suspended the Dublin rule for Syrians (August 2015); Merkel said on 31 August 2015 "wir schaffen das" ("we can manage this"); some 550,000 entered Germany in the following three months. Hungary closed its border with Serbia by fence, then with Croatia; Sweden took over 160,000 in 2015. The body of two-year-old Alan Kurdi (drowned 2 September 2015) became a symbol. [source: MIG; WSD]
- EU–Turkey deal (in force 20 March 2016): Turkey to take back irregular entrants to Greece; the EU to pay €6 billion, resettle one Syrian from Turkey for each returned (up to 72,000) and promise visa liberalisation; arrivals in Greece fell about 90%. UNHCR refused to take part; NGOs criticised it. [source: MIG]
- Italy funded and trained the Libyan coast guard from 2017. A 2021 Guardian investigation attributed almost 40,000 pushbacks and 2,000 deaths to member states with Frontex complicity; Frontex denies it (OLAF investigated). Frontex was founded 2004 (Warsaw), budget €1.12 billion (2025). [source: MIG; FRX]
- The Temporary Protection Directive (2001, after the Yugoslav wars) was activated for the first time on 4 March 2022 for Ukrainians. [source: TPD]
- New Pact on Migration and Asylum approved by the Council 14 May 2024 (Hungary and Poland against), with a "mandatory solidarity mechanism"; it took effect June 2026. [source: PACT]

## 13. Brexit
- Referendum 23 June 2016: Leave 17,410,742 (51.89%), Remain 16,141,241 (48.11%), turnout 72.2%. Scotland 62.0% Remain, Northern Ireland 55.8% Remain, Wales 52.5% Leave. [source: UK16]
- Article 50 triggered 29 March 2017; the original deadline of 29 March 2019 was extended; revised withdrawal agreement with the Northern Ireland Protocol agreed 17 October 2019; after Johnson's December 2019 election majority (80 seats), the UK left on 31 January 2020; transition to the end of 2020; Trade and Cooperation Agreement signed 30 December 2020. [source: BREX; A50]
- UK is the only state ever to withdraw; earlier territorial exits: French Algeria (1962), Greenland (1985), Saint Pierre and Miquelon (1985), Saint Barthélemy (2012). [source: A50]

## 14. COVID-19 and NextGenerationEU
- Commission proposal 27 May 2020 (between €1,000 bn sought by Italy/Spain and €500 bn proposed by France and Germany); agreed in principle by the European Council on 21 July 2020, adopted 14 December 2020: €750 billion (2018 prices), €390 billion grants and €360 billion loans, financed by common EU borrowing on the markets, operating 2021–2026, repaid by 2058. [source: NGEU; WebSearch https://ip-quarterly.com/en/eu-close-another-hamiltonian-moment]

## 16. Global perspective: Eurafrica, association, and the former colonies
- "Eurafrica" was an interwar idea: Coudenhove-Kalergi's Paneuropa saw African colonies as Europe's "dowry", with racial undertones; Atlantropa and Nazi-era geopoliticians (Haushofer, Obst) had versions; Oswald Mosley's postwar "Europe a Nation" included Africa. [source: EAF]
- The Schuman Declaration named "the development of the African continent" as one of Europe's "essential tasks" ("With increased resources Europe will be able to pursue the achievement of one of its essential tasks, namely, the development of the African continent"). [source: SCH; EAF; WebSearch https://www.robert-schuman.eu/en/european-issues/433-declaration-of-9-may]
- At the Rome negotiations France (and Belgium) made association of their overseas territories, with European funds for them, a condition; Gaston Defferre, French minister for overseas territories, made it a prerequisite; West Germany, sceptical, agreed to contribute substantially to the European Development Fund. The associated territories were chiefly the Belgian Congo, French West Africa and French Equatorial Africa. Algeria, legally three French departments, was inside the Community and gained exemption clauses; it left with independence in 1962. [source: EAF; WebSearch https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/europpblog/2023/10/31/the-colonial-origins-of-european-integration]
- Léopold Sédar Senghor developed his own Eurafrique linked to négritude, treating African and European cultures as equals in one continuum. [source: EAF]
- Yaoundé I signed 20 July 1963 (in force 1 June 1964) between the EEC and 18 newly independent African states (AASM); Yaoundé II signed 29 July 1969 (in force 1 January 1971), with Mauritius; the Arusha Agreement with Kenya, Uganda and Tanzania ran alongside. Association was viewed with suspicion as neo-colonialism by some African Commonwealth states; a 1966 Lagos association agreement with Nigeria never came into force. [source: YAO; WebSearch https://journals.codesria.org/index.php/ad/article/download/3507/3279/4631 summary]
- Lomé Convention, signed February 1975 in Lomé (Togo) with 46 African, Caribbean and Pacific (ACP) states, in force April 1976 — including former British colonies after UK entry: duty-free entry for most ACP exports, quotas for sugar and beef, EUA 3 billion aid, and the Stabex and Sysmin compensation schemes. Renewed as Lomé II (1981), III (1985), IV (signed December 1989, about 70 ACP states). After US complaints the WTO ruled against the banana preferences (1996 onward). [source: LOME]
- Cotonou Agreement signed June 2000 in Benin by 78 ACP states and the EU-15 (in force 2003): replaced non-reciprocal preferences with reciprocal Economic Partnership Agreements (from 2008) and added political conditionality. Superseded by the Samoa Agreement, signed by 44 ACP states in Samoa in November 2023, which includes provisions easing return of undocumented migrants. Everything But Arms (in force 5 March 2001) gives least developed countries duty- and quota-free access for all goods except arms. [source: COT; EBA]
- Barcelona Process (1995) and Sarkozy's Union for the Mediterranean (Paris summit, 2008, 43 states); Sarkozy in 2007: "While Europe's future is in the South, Africa's is in the North … the linchpin of Eurafrica". [source: EAF; UFM]

## Debates
- **Why integration happened.** Neofunctionalism: Ernst B. Haas (1924–2003; Frankfurt-born Jewish refugee, Berkeley), *The Uniting of Europe* (1958), studying the ECSC, argued integration in one sector creates "spillover" pressure into others, with interest groups and supranational bureaucrats shifting loyalties; Monnet's sector-by-sector method fits it. After de Gaulle's empty chair, Haas declared the theory obsolete (later revoking that). [source: HAAS; NEO]
- Liberal intergovernmentalism: Andrew Moravcsik (*The Choice for Europe*, 1998) explains integration from Messina to Maastricht as "a series of rational choices made by national leaders" responding to domestic economic interests, relative power and the need for credible commitments; critics say it explains grand bargains not everyday politics, and his use of sources has been criticised. [source: LI; MOR]
- Alan Milward (*The European Rescue of the Nation-State*, 1992): integration did not erode the nation-state but rescued it, letting governments deliver welfare, farm incomes and employment that underpinned their postwar legitimacy; a polemic against federalist "saints" narratives (Perry Anderson, LRB); influenced Moravcsik. [source: MIL; WebSearch https://www.lrb.co.uk/v18/n01/perry-anderson/under-the-sign-of-the-interim]
- **Democratic deficit.** Term used by David Marquand (1979); Follesdal and Hix argue a structural deficit (no real contest over EU executive power and policy); Giandomenico Majone answers that the EU is a regulatory body to be judged by output, not majoritarian standards; Moravcsik (2002) argues it is legitimate through elected national governments in the Council. [source: DD]
- **Peace project vs neoliberal machine.** Nobel Committee 2012 (peace and reconciliation) vs critics (Tutu, Maguire, Pérez Esquivel opposed the prize); Slobodian's "encasement" thesis (see *Neoliberal Age*); Greek austerity and the overridden 2015 referendum as evidence for critics; NGEU as "Hamiltonian moment" and break from austerity. [source: NOB; GR15; NGEU]
- **Eurafrica as late colonialism.** Hansen and Jonsson (2014) argue Africa was central to the Rome negotiations and that Eurafrica was constitutive of integration — a way to pool and prolong colonial rule — and that EU memory suffers "historical amnesia" about it; reviewers praise the corrective but call it EU-centric and say the broad use of "Eurafrica" blunts the concept. Megan Brown (2022) shows French anxieties over Algeria shaped the early Communities. [source: WebSearch summaries of reviews, https://library.oapen.org/handle/20.500.12657/58764; Brown as above]
- **Neo-colonialism of association.** Yaoundé was criticised as neo-colonial, especially by non-associated (Commonwealth) African states; Lomé was presented as a fairer, non-reciprocal partnership, Cotonou's reciprocity as a retreat. [source: YAO; WebSearch codesria/Commonwealth summary; COT]
- **Euro as the price of German unity?** Spiegel (2010) reported Mitterrand demanded it; the 1988–89 Delors process predates the fall of the Wall. [source: DEL; EMU]
- **Was the euro's architecture flawed?** Optimum currency area critics (Feldstein, IMF authors), Blanchard on fiscal union, Stiglitz/Krugman on banking crisis and austerity; Draghi's OMT seen as the turning point. [source: EZ; EURO; EZC]
- **Non-Western views.** Jaishankar's Eurocentrism critique (2022); many Global South states' neutrality on Ukraine (see *Post-Cold War* chapter); EU-China "systemic rival" framing; African critiques of EPAs and of migration externalisation (Samoa Agreement's return clauses; Libya coast guard funding). [source: WebSearch; CHN; COT; MIG]
