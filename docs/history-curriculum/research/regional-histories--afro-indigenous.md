# Verified facts — Afro-Indigenous Intersections in Early America (researched 2026-11-11)

Node: `history/regional-histories/americas/afro-indigenous-intersections-in-early-america`

## 1. The Indian slave trade and Native slavery alongside African slavery

- Before European contact, many southeastern Native societies took war captives; captives were often adopted to replace dead kin or held as a temporary status. Colonial demand turned captive-taking into a commercial trade in which Native groups armed with European guns raided other Native peoples and sold captives to colonists. [source: W:IST; W:SNA; W:CF]
- Carolina (founded 1670) made trade with Native peoples, in deerskins and Indian captives, its core business from its founding to the early 1700s; Charles Town (Charleston) became the main port exporting enslaved Indians, often to the Caribbean (where escape was harder) and to other mainland colonies. [source: W:IST]
- Successive Native slave-raiding partners of Carolina: the Westo (probably Iroquoian, likely Erie in origin; slave raiders on the Savannah River c.1660–1680), then the Savannah (Shawnee), then the Yamasee and Creek. [source: W:IST; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westo]
- Estimate (attributed): Alan Gallay estimates that between 1670 and 1715, 24,000 to 51,000 captive Native Americans were exported through Carolina ports, of whom 15,000–30,000 came from then-Spanish Florida — more than the number of Africans imported into Carolina in the same period. (A figure of "30,000 to 51,000", attributed to Krauthamer 2013, also circulates; the Yamasee War article says "up to 50,000". Give as a range and attribute.) [source: W:SNA; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamasee_War]
- Slave raids by Creek and Yamasee with Carolina backing destroyed the Apalachee missions of Spanish Florida (1704) and devastated the Timucua. [source: W:SNA]
- Peter H. Wood found South Carolina's 1708 population was 9,580, including 4,100 enslaved Africans and 1,400 enslaved Native Americans; Native women outnumbered Native men among the enslaved and African men outnumbered African women, which encouraged unions between the two groups. By 1715 the enslaved Native population of Carolina was estimated at 1,850. [source: W:IST]
- Christina Snyder's figures (attributed): the southern Native population fell from about 199,400 in 1685 to about 90,100 in 1715, from slaving, disease and war. [source: W:SNA]
- The Yamasee War (1715–1717): a coalition led by the Yamasee and including Muscogee (Creek), Catawba, Apalachee and many others attacked South Carolina; causes included trader abuses, debts and the Indian slave trade. About 7% of South Carolina's settlers were killed. The tide turned in early 1716 when the Cherokee sided with the colonists. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamasee_War]
- In August 1715 South Carolina's new paid army contained about 600 colonists, 400 enslaved Black men, 170 allied Indians and 300 troops from North Carolina and Virginia — enslaved Africans were armed against Native enemies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamasee_War]
- After the Yamasee War Carolina stopped buying Native captives on a large scale and relied increasingly on enslaved Africans; the Native slave trade in the region had essentially ended by about 1750 (some sources say its dominance lasted to c.1730). [source: W:IST; W:SNA]
- Georgia (founded 1732) banned African slavery by a 1735 law, but the ban did not cover Native Americans; purchases of Indian slaves are recorded in 1738 and Indian slaves still worked Georgia plantations in 1772 (Rodney Baine). [source: W:IST]
- New England: the Pequot War (1636–37) produced enslaved war captives; John Winthrop recorded sending fifteen Pequot boys and two women to be sold. The ship *Desire* carried 17 Pequot prisoners, who were sold on the Puritan colony of Providence Island; it returned to Boston on 26 February 1638 with cotton, tobacco, salt and an unspecified number of enslaved Africans bought there — an early direct link between Native and African enslavement. [source: https://legacyofslaveryreport.harvard.edu/report/indigenous-slavery-and-african-slavery; https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/beginnings-of-ma-slave-trade.htm]
- Hemispheric scale (attributed estimate): Andrés Reséndez estimates that between 2.5 and 5 million Indigenous people were enslaved in the Americas between 1492 and 1900 (a range built from fragmentary records); for North America excluding Mexico he estimates 147,000–340,000. Unlike the African trade, which targeted mostly adult men, most Indian slaves were women and children. [source: https://historynewsnetwork.org/article/164562; https://commonreader.wustl.edu/hidden-servitude/; W:SNA]
- Spain formally outlawed Indian slavery (New Laws, 1542) but, Reséndez argues, enslavement continued through loopholes and later through debt peonage, vagrancy laws and convict leasing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Other_Slavery]
- Africans and Native people were enslaved together: they "worked together, lived together in communal quarters, produced collective recipes for food, shared herbal remedies, myths and legends, and in the end they intermarried" (quoted in Wikipedia from a secondary source; use as paraphrase). Records often did not distinguish Native from African slaves; a Rhode Island woman, Sarah Chauqum, listed as "mulatto" in a bill of sale, won her freedom by asserting her Narragansett identity. [source: W:SNA]

## 2. Alliance, kin and divide-and-rule in the colonial borderlands

- South Carolina passed a law in 1725 forbidding taking enslaved people to the frontier (fine £200) and in 1751 a law against keeping Africans near Native Americans, fearing alliances. Under Governor James Glen (in office 1743–1756) the colony pursued a policy of creating an "aversion" in Native people toward Africans. [source: W:BI]
- In the 1730s the Cherokee signed a treaty with Britain to return runaway slaves to colonists. [source: W:CF]
- 1686: Yamasee fought alongside Africans in an attack on a South Carolina garrison (per Wikipedia; details thin). [source: W:SNA]
- Intermarriage in New England: a 1792 census of the Wampanoag at Gay Head (Aquinnah) showed 28% of the tribe had African ancestry; Black ancestry is common among the Mashantucket Pequot, Narragansett, Shinnecock, Aquinnah and Mashpee Wampanoag. [source: W:BI]
- An 1835 census of the Cherokee showed about 10% were of African descent. [source: W:BI]

### Spanish and French borderlands
- Esteban (Estevanico) was described by Cabeza de Vaca as "negro alárabe, natural de Azamor" (an Arabic-speaking Black man from Azemmour, Morocco); enslaved, he was sold in 1522 to Andrés Dorantes. He survived the Narváez expedition (landed in Florida 1528), crossed the continent with three Spanish survivors (reaching Spanish settlement in Sinaloa in July 1536), and was killed at Cíbola (Zuni) in 1539 while guiding Fray Marcos de Niza's expedition. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esteban_de_Dorantes]
- Spanish Florida offered asylum to escaped slaves from the British colonies from as early as 1689; a 1693 royal decree of Charles II granted freedom to runaways who converted to Catholicism and served (four years) in the militia. [source: W:FM]
- Fort Mose (Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose), about 2 miles north of St Augustine, was ordered built in 1738 by Governor Manuel de Montiano. It was the first free Black settlement of formerly enslaved people legally sanctioned in what became the United States, with a population of about 100. Its militia captain was Francisco Menéndez, a Mandinka born in the Gambia region, enslaved in Carolina, who had escaped to Florida. [source: W:FM]
- The Mose militia fought with Spanish troops and Indian auxiliaries against James Oglethorpe's 1740 siege of St Augustine. Fort Mose was rebuilt in 1752; in 1759 it had 22 huts housing 37 men, 15 women, 7 boys and 8 girls; most married other refugees, but some married Indian women or enslaved people of St Augustine (Jane Landers's archival research). When Spain ceded Florida in 1763, about 400 Black inhabitants (slave and free) left with the Spanish for Cuba. [source: W:FM]
- Fort Mose is believed to have helped inspire the Stono Rebellion (September 1739), whose rebels marched south towards Spanish Florida. [source: W:FM]
- Natchez revolt: on 28 November 1729 the Natchez attacked Fort Rosalie and nearby French concessions (present-day Natchez, Mississippi); a year later the dead were tallied at 138 men, 35 women and 56 children (about 230). Women, children and enslaved Africans were mostly spared; some enslaved Africans joined the Natchez, others escaped; when the Natchez fled in February 1730 they took enslaved Africans with them. Some scholars argue the Natchez spared Africans out of a sense of affinity. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natchez_revolt]
- Governor Étienne Perier then ordered enslaved Africans and French troops to massacre the Chaouacha, a small people with no part in the revolt — an act that may have been meant to prevent alliance between enslaved Africans and Native people. In January 1731 Perier captured about 45 men and 450 women and children of the Natchez and sold them as slaves in Saint-Domingue. Survivors took refuge among the Chickasaw, later the Creek and Cherokee; the Natchez remain a constituent town of the Muscogee (Creek) Nation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natchez_revolt]
- Spain abolished Indian slavery in Louisiana in 1769 — the legal basis of the Scypion freedom suits (below). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marguerite_Scypion]

## 3. Maroons and the Black Seminoles

- Great Dismal Swamp (Virginia–North Carolina): research suggests thousands lived there between about 1700 and the 1860s; Herbert Aptheker (1939) estimated "about two thousand" fugitives or descendants. Native peoples (Chesapeake, Nansemond, later Tuscarora in the early 1700s) used or lived in the swamp; maroons reused Native tool remnants. Militias raided in 1823; North Carolina passed an 1847 law aimed at capturing the maroons; most avoided capture. Archaeology by Daniel Sayers (American University) began in 2002. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Dismal_Swamp_maroons]
- Harriet Beecher Stowe's novel *Dred: A Tale of the Great Dismal Swamp* (1856) has a swamp maroon as its hero. [source: same]
- The word *Seminole* (via Muskogee) and the English *maroon* probably both derive from Spanish *cimarrón* ("wild", used of runaways) — William Sturtevant's derivation; linguists differ on the route. [source: W:BS; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Dismal_Swamp_maroons]
- The Seminole formed in Florida in the 18th century by ethnogenesis from Creek (Muscogee) migrants and remnant Florida peoples; William Bartram called them a distinct people in 1773. [source: W:BS]
- Black Seminoles (Seminole Maroons): runaways, many of them Gullah people from the Carolina and Georgia rice coast, built their own towns near Seminole villages, growing rice and corn; they lived in separate, culturally and politically autonomous communities, in a relationship Wikipedia describes as nominal vassalage/tribute to Seminole leaders, with far more freedom than slaves held by whites, including bearing arms. They developed the Afro-Seminole Creole language. [source: W:BS]
- Negro Fort (Prospect Bluff, on the Apalachicola River), built by the British in 1814 and left to Black and Native allies in 1815, was destroyed on 27 July 1816 when a heated shot from a US gunboat hit its magazine (on Andrew Jackson's orders via Gaines and Clinch); about 270 died (the infobox gives 334 killed, wounded and captured). [source: W:BS; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negro_Fort]
- First Seminole War (1817–18): breaking up the Florida maroon communities was one of Jackson's main aims; on 16 April 1818 his troops burned about 400 Seminole and maroon homes at Bowlegs Town on the Suwannee. Spain ceded Florida by the Adams–Onís Treaty (1819), effective 1821. [source: W:BS; W:SW]
- Hundreds of Black Seminoles and other fugitives fled from Cape Florida to the Bahamas in the early 1820s (one group of about 300 in 1823); their descendants live at Red Bay, Andros Island. [source: W:BS]
- Second Seminole War (1835–1842), "by far the longest" of the three; began with the Dade battle on 28 December 1835. Black Seminole leaders included John Caesar, Abraham and John Horse; Black Seminoles and enslaved plantation workers joined in destroying 21 sugar plantations from 25 December 1835 into 1836 (historians disagree whether this was a separate slave rebellion). [source: W:BS; W:SW]
- General Thomas Jesup (in command from late 1836) offered freedom to Black Seminoles who surrendered and agreed to removal; John Horse surrendered on these terms; the promise was only partly honoured. More than 500 Black Seminoles went west with the Seminole after 1838. [source: W:BS; W:SW]
- By 1842, 3,612 Seminoles had been removed to Indian Territory; about 350–500 stayed in Florida. The Second Seminole War may have cost up to $40 million and the lives of about 1,500 US soldiers (mostly from disease). Third Seminole War 1855–1858. [source: W:SW]
- John Horse (c.1812–1882, also Juan Caballo, "Gopher John") of African and Seminole descent; Abraham (born enslaved in Georgia, 1790s–1870s), interpreter for Micanopy, called "the principal negro chief" of the Seminoles in July 1837. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Horse; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_(Seminole)]
- In Indian Territory, Black Seminoles were initially placed under the slaveholding Creek and threatened by slave raiders; in 1849 John Horse led about 180 Black Seminoles, with the Seminole leader Coacochee (Wild Cat), to Mexico (crossing in July 1850), where slavery had been abolished (1829); they settled at El Nacimiento, Coahuila, as border guards and became known as *Mascogos*. [source: W:BS]
- From 1870 the US Army recruited them as the Black Seminole Scouts at Fort Clark, Texas; four were awarded the Medal of Honor (three for an 1875 action against the Comanche); the unit was disbanded in 1914; descendants centre on Brackettville, Texas. Mascogos still celebrate Juneteenth in Coahuila. [source: W:BS; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Seminole_Scouts]
- John Horse founded Wewoka (1849) as a Black settlement; it is now the capital of the Seminole Nation of Oklahoma. In 1900 about 1,000 Freedmen made up roughly a third of the Seminole Nation's population. [source: W:BS]

## 4. Freedom suits that turned on Indigenous ancestry

- Virginia's 1662 statute adopted *partus sequitur ventrem* (a child takes the mother's status). Virginia prohibited Indian slavery from 1705 (or 1691, depending on judicial interpretation), so descent from a free Native woman in the maternal line could ground a claim to freedom. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_suit; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudgins_v._Wright]
- *Hudgins v. Wright* (1806): Jackey Wright sued for her and her two children's freedom on the basis of descent from Indian women (her great-grandmother Frances Mingo of Prince George County; grandmother Hannah). Chancellor George Wythe ruled for her on a presumption of freedom under the 1776 Virginia Declaration of Rights; the Court of Appeals (St George Tucker participating) upheld the result on narrower grounds — Hudgins had not proved maternal African descent, and Indians had been free since 1705. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudgins_v._Wright]
- Legal historian Ariela Gross (2008) on *Hudgins*: the ruling confirmed that "Indians were by default citizens of a free nation; Africans were by default members of an enslaved race." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudgins_v._Wright]
- Marguerite Scypion, of African and Natchez descent (her maternal grandmother was Natchez, captured in war; her mother Marie Jean Scypion), filed the first freedom suit in St Louis in 1805. After losses and refilings (1825–26, against Jean Pierre Chouteau and others), a unanimous jury freed her and her family in November 1836; the decision withstood appeal to the US Supreme Court in 1838 and is considered to have ended Indian slavery in Missouri. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marguerite_Scypion; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_suit]
- A Library of Virginia blog titled "'Hundreds of the descendants of Indians have obtained their freedom': Freedom Suits in 18th and 19th Century Virginia" (2012) documents the wider pattern (title verified as a link only). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_suit]

## 5. New England: Afro-Indigenous lives
- Crispus Attucks (c.1723–5 March 1770), sailor and whaler from Framingham, is traditionally counted as the first person killed in the Boston Massacre. Most historians agree he was of Wampanoag and African descent; whether he was free or a runaway is debated. A 1750 *Boston Gazette* advertisement by William Brown of Framingham sought a runaway "Molatto Fellow, about 27 Years of Age, named Crispas". Eyewitness sources of 1770 called him a mulatto and an Indian rather than "Negro"; a memoir described him as "a Nantucket Indian". His biographer Mitch Kachun noted a possible link to a Natick (Praying Indian) woman, Nanny Peterattucks. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crispus_Attucks]
- John Adams, defending the soldiers, called the crowd "a motley rabble of saucy boys, negros and molattoes, Irish teagues and outlandish jack tarrs." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crispus_Attucks]
- Paul Cuffe (17 January 1759 – 7 September 1817), merchant, sea captain and abolitionist, was the son of Kofi (Coffe) Slocum, an Ashanti captured as a child and enslaved in Newport, and Ruth Moses, a Wampanoag from Cape Cod. In 1780 he and his brother John refused to pay taxes because free Black men could not vote and petitioned Bristol County against taxation without representation. On 25 February 1783 he married Alice Abel Pequit, a Wampanoag widow. He sailed to Sierra Leone (1811) and in 1815–16 carried nine free Black families there. (Wikipedia gives his parents' marriage as both 1746 and 1747; avoid the year.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Cuffe]
- Mashpee Wampanoag: intermarriage with Black people dates to the 1700s and many Mashpee have African ancestry. Massachusetts revoked Mashpee self-government in 1788; in the 1830s the Pequot Methodist preacher William Apess helped lead a peaceful Mashpee protest against the white overseers (Wikipedia's Mashpee article dates it 1837; the commonly cited date of 1833 was not verified — say "the 1830s"); in 1834 the state restored partial self-government; the state incorporated Mashpee as a town in 1870 against a tribal vote. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mashpee_Wampanoag_Tribe]
- In the land-claim trial *Mashpee Tribe v. Town of Mashpee* (began 17 October 1977; jury answers January 1978), an all-white jury found the Mashpee were not a "tribe" at several key dates, accepting that tribal identity had been "abandoned" between 1842 and 1869; commentators (Carrillo, Indiana Law Review) argue the defence racialised the claim; James Clifford's essay "Identity in Mashpee" (*The Predicament of Culture*, 1988) is the classic analysis. The Mashpee won federal recognition on 23 May 2007. [source: https://journals.indianapolis.iu.edu/index.php/inlawrev/article/download/3133/3061; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mashpee_Wampanoag_Tribe]

## 6. How settler racial regimes divided the two: one-drop vs blood quantum
- Virginia's 1705 "Indian blood" law limited the civil rights of people of one-half or more Native ancestry. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_quantum_laws]
- Blood quantum was not widely applied by the federal government until the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934; earlier, tribal belonging rested on kinship, lineage and family ties. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_quantum_laws; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-drop_rule]
- The one-drop rule was codified in state law from Tennessee (1910) to Virginia (1930), part of Jim Crow. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-drop_rule]
- Patrick Wolfe (2006; *Traces of History*, 2016) argued the two regimes ran in opposite directions: for Black people the one-drop rule meant any African ancestry made a person Black (expanding the enslavable/exploitable population), while for Indians non-Native ancestry compromised indigeneity, producing "half-breeds" and blood quantum rules that tended to define Indians out of existence (shrinking claims to land). He tied this to expropriation of labour vs of land. [source: https://vuir.vu.edu.au/1831/; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/663322]
- Virginia's Racial Integrity Act (1924), pushed by Walter Plecker, registrar of the Bureau of Vital Statistics (1912–1946; 1861–1947), defined a "white person" as one with "no trace whatsoever of any blood other than Caucasian", required birth and marriage certificates to record "white" or "colored", and classed Native Americans as "colored". The "Pocahontas exception" let people with one-sixteenth or less American Indian ancestry and no other non-white ancestry count as white. [source: W:RIA; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Plecker]
- Plecker directed the reclassification of nearly all Virginia Indians as "colored"; journalist Warren Fiske reported that this made it impossible for six of Virginia's eight state-recognised tribes to document continuity for federal recognition. Census: 779 Indians in Virginia in 1930, 198 in 1940 (Wikipedia cites with a dead link — use cautiously). *Loving v. Virginia* (12 June 1967) struck down the marriage ban; Virginia repealed the rest in 1975. [source: W:RIA]
- Earlier, in 1812 Virginia's General Assembly declared the Gingaskin of the Eastern Shore no longer Indian but "free Negroes", dispossessing them of their reservation, on the claim that it had become "an asylum for free negroes and other disorderly persons". [source: W:RIA]
- Under this pressure some Virginia tribes distanced themselves from Blackness: Pamunkey bylaws of 1887 forbade members to marry anyone "except White or Indian". The Pamunkey became Virginia's first federally recognised tribe in January 2016. [source: W:RIA; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pamunkey]
- Lumbee (Robeson County, North Carolina): recognised by the state in 1885 (as "Croatan Indians"); a 1956 Act of Congress recognised them but denied federal services; free people of colour in the area lost the vote under North Carolina's 1835 constitutional amendments; the Lowry War of the 1860s–1872 under Henry Berry Lowry/Lowrie. The Lumbee Fairness Act, attached to the defence authorisation bill, was signed on 18 December 2025, granting full federal recognition. Their origins are contested (some genealogists, e.g. Virginia DeMarce and Paul Heinegg, trace founding families to free Black families of Virginia; Lumbee scholars dispute this framing) — handle as a debate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumbee; https://governor.nc.gov/news/press-releases/2025/12/18/governor-stein-celebrates-decision-extends-federal-recognition-lumbee-tribe]
- Chitimacha (Louisiana): between 1904 and 1919 members with any African ancestry were disenrolled. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-drop_rule]
- DNA and family lore: the PBS series *African American Lives* (Henry Louis Gates Jr., 2006) reported that only about 5% of African Americans have at least one-eighth Native American ancestry (one great-grandparent). Kim TallBear (Sisseton Wahpeton Oyate): "People think there is a DNA test to prove you are Native American. There isn't." [source: W:BI; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_quantum_laws]

## 7. Slavery in the Five Tribes, removal and the Civil War
- From the late 1700s some Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Creek (Muscogee) and Seminole, encouraged by the US "civilization" policy (Washington, Knox, agents such as Benjamin Hawkins), became planters holding enslaved African Americans. "Five Civilized Tribes" is a colonial-era label historians now criticise. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Civilized_Tribes; W:SNA]
- Cherokee: Return J. Meigs's 1809 census counted 583 enslaved Black people held by Cherokee; by 1835, 1,592, with about 7.4% of Cherokee families holding slaves (vs about 5% of families across the South, per Wikipedia). Prominent Cherokee slaveholders included Joseph Vann, Major Ridge, Stand Watie, Elias Boudinot and Principal Chief John Ross. [source: W:CF]
- Pre-removal enslaved populations (US removal-era figures): Choctaw ~500; Creek ~900; Chickasaw 1,156; Cherokee 1,592; Seminole "fugitive slaves" uncounted. Doran (1978): an estimated 4,500–5,000 enslaved Black people in Indian Territory by 1839, most held by people of mixed European–Native ancestry. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_removal; W:SNA]
- Cherokee slave law: an 1824 law banned marriage between Cherokee and slaves or free Black people; Cherokee laws barred people of African descent from voting, office and bearing arms and banned teaching Black people to read; the 1839 constitution barred "negro and mulatto" people from office. [source: W:CF]
- Shoe Boots (Tarsekayahke), a Cherokee warrior, had children with Doll, a woman he enslaved; on 20 October 1824 he petitioned the Cherokee National Council to free his three children by her and make them citizens; granted 6 November 1824. Twin sons born later were enslaved by his heirs after his death in 1829. This family is the subject of Tiya Miles's *Ties That Bind*. [source: W:CF; https://mixedracestudies.org/?p=7106]
- Cherokee Slave Revolt of 1842: enslaved people, including 25 held by Joseph Vann, fled from near Webbers Falls toward Mexico and were recaptured by a Cherokee militia; on 2 December 1842 the Council expelled free Black people (other than those freed by Cherokee). In 1846 about 130–150 more escaped. [source: W:CF]
- By 1861 the Cherokee held about 4,000 enslaved people. On 7 October 1861 John Ross signed a treaty with the Confederacy (Albert Pike); after his capture and parole in 1862 he sided with the Union. Stand Watie, slaveholder and Confederate brigadier general, was the last Confederate general to surrender (23 June 1865). [source: W:CF]
- The Union-aligned Cherokee National Council passed acts abolishing slavery on 18 and 20 February 1863 (effective 25 June 1863) — the only one of the Five Tribes to abolish slavery during the war, though few were actually freed. [source: W:CF]
- Treaties of 1866: the US required the Five Tribes to abolish slavery and (variously) adopt their Freedmen. Cherokee treaty signed 19 July 1866; Article 9 gave Freedmen "all the rights of native Cherokees". Creek and Seminole treaties gave Freedmen citizenship; the Choctaw–Chickasaw treaty let the two nations choose to adopt Freedmen within two years or have the US remove them. The Choctaw adopted their Freedmen by an act of 1883 (some sources say 1885); the Chickasaw never did, leaving Chickasaw Freedmen stateless until Oklahoma statehood (1907). [source: W:CF; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choctaw_Freedmen; https://www.okhistory.org/publications/enc/entry.php?entry=FR016; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chickasaw_Freedmen]

## 8. Dawes Rolls and the Freedmen's citizenship struggles
- The Dawes Commission (formed 1893) enrolled citizens of the Five Tribes 1898–1907 in separate categories: "by blood", intermarried whites, and Freedmen. Registrars often classified people by appearance, so Freedmen with Native ancestry were listed without blood quantum. The 1902 rolls listed 41,798 Cherokee citizens and 4,924 Cherokee Freedmen; nearly 20,000 Freedmen were listed across the Five Tribes (genealogist Angela Walton-Raji). [source: W:CF]
- Allotment: Choctaw Freedmen typically received under 40 acres per household vs about 320 for Choctaw by blood (per the 1897 Choctaw agreement as summarised on Wikipedia). Seminole Freedmen could sell allotments without federal approval, making them targets for land loss. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choctaw_Freedmen; W:BS]
- Cherokee Nation: the 1975/76 constitution tied citizenship to the Dawes Rolls; in 1983 Principal Chief Ross Swimmer required BIA-issued CDIB ("by blood") cards to vote, excluding Freedmen descendants. Lucy Allen's case: on 7 March 2006 the Cherokee Judicial Appeals Tribunal ruled Freedmen descendants were citizens. On 3 March 2007 a referendum restricted citizenship to "by blood" descendants, 76% (6,702) to 24% (2,041). [source: W:CF]
- *Cherokee Nation v. Nash* / *Vann v. Zinke*: on 30 August 2017 Senior US District Judge Thomas F. Hogan (D.D.C.) ruled that under Article 9 of the 1866 treaty Freedmen descendants have citizenship rights coextensive with native Cherokees; the Cherokee Nation did not appeal. In 2021 the Cherokee Nation Supreme Court ordered "by blood" removed from the constitution and laws. Marilyn Vann became the first Freedmen descendant to hold a Cherokee government post (confirmed 13 September 2021). [source: W:CF]
- Seminole Nation of Oklahoma: Freedmen bands (Caesar Bruner and Dosar Barkus) sit on the General Council (in the constitution since 1954). A 1990 award of about $56 million for Florida lands taken in 1823 excluded Freedmen; in 2000 the Nation restricted membership to "by blood" descent, excluding about 1,200 Freedmen; Wikipedia reports a 2022 D.C. District Court ruling that Freedmen retained membership and voting rights (verify before stating). [source: W:BS]
- Muscogee (Creek) Nation: a 1979 constitution required "by blood" descent. On 23 July 2025 the Muscogee Nation Supreme Court unanimously held the "by blood" requirement void under the 1866 treaty (case of Rhonda Grayson and Jeff Kennedy); rehearing was denied; implementation was disputed in federal court as of late 2025. [source: https://www.kgou.org/indigenous-news/2025-07-24/muscogee-supreme-court-freedmen-are-entitled-to-citizenship; https://nondoc.com/2025/08/20/muscogee-nation-supreme-court-denies-rehearing-of-freedmen-citizenship-case/]
- Chickasaw and Choctaw constitutions (Choctaw 1983) define citizenship "by blood"; Chickasaw Freedmen descendants are not eligible. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chickasaw_Freedmen; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choctaw_Freedmen]
- Arguments on both sides (Cherokee case): opponents, including Principal Chief Chad Smith, argued Freedmen were imposed by the US in 1866 and that tribal sovereignty includes the right to define citizenship; supporters, including Cherokee activist David Cornsilk, argued the nation must honour the treaty and not define itself racially. [source: W:CF]

## Debates
- Why did the Five Tribes adopt chattel slavery? (a) Strategic adaptation: elites adopted plantation slavery to meet US "civilization" demands and defend their land and sovereignty; (b) assimilation of Euro-American racial ideology: Krauthamer shows Choctaw and Chickasaw elites came to equate Blackness with hereditary inferiority and property; Miles argues Cherokee leaders linked "progress" with slaveholding and, through legislation, equated Blackness with slavery; (c) older Native captivity practices were transformed rather than continued (Perdue; Snyder). Most historians reject a simple "milder slavery" story — Wikipedia notes missionaries called Native slaveholders brutal while some Freedmen remembered better treatment. [source: W:SNA; https://mixedracestudies.org/?p=7106; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_Krauthamer]
- Sovereignty vs treaty rights/civil rights in the Freedmen disputes: tribal governments argue nations decide their own citizenship and that 1866 adoption was imposed; Freedmen descendants and courts (2017, 2025) hold the 1866 treaties bind; Congressional Black Caucus members (2007) proposed cutting federal funding; the United Keetoowah Band's chief warned that Cherokee non-compliance threatened all tribes' treaty relationships. Sturm (2014) frames it as race vs sovereignty vs civil rights. [source: W:CF; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circe_Sturm]
- Terms: "Black Indians" (Katz's popular term), "Afro-Native", "Afro-Indigenous" (Mays), "Red-Black peoples" (Forbes), "Freedmen" (legal/treaty term, which descendants use while some reject its implication that they lack Native ancestry). Mays explicitly rejects claims that people of African descent are the First Peoples of North America as an erasure of Native peoples. [source: W:BI; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_D._Forbes; https://afam.ucla.edu/2022/11/15/an-afro-indigenous-history-of-the-united-states/]
- Wolfe's "opposite logics" (one-drop vs blood quantum) is influential but criticised as too binary (e.g. Sai Englert argues settler colonies used both elimination and exploitation). [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/663322; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Settler_colonialism]
- Scale of Indian slavery: Gallay's 24,000–51,000 for Carolina; Reséndez's 2.5–5 million hemispheric — reviewers call it the most comprehensive estimate but it is extrapolated from fragmentary records; it builds on Brett Rushforth's work. [source: https://commonreader.wustl.edu/hidden-servitude/]
- Black Seminole status: "slaves", "vassals", "allies"? Sources describe nominal ownership by Seminole leaders with near-autonomous towns; historians debate whether the 1835–36 plantation attacks were a separate slave rebellion. [source: W:BS]
- Crispus Attucks: free or runaway; African, Wampanoag, Natick or Nantucket Indian connections — evidence is thin. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crispus_Attucks]
- Lumbee origins: Native continuity vs descent from free families of colour; federal recognition finally came in 2025. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumbee]
- Genetic ancestry vs kinship: Gates's DNA findings vs family stories; TallBear and others insist tribal belonging is political and relational, not genetic. [source: W:BI]
