## Wartime origins and the first computers
- Alan Turing (23 June 1912 – 7 June 1954) delivered his 36-page paper "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem" for publication on 28 May 1936. [source: WP: Alan_Turing]
- Turing's work at Bletchley Park included the functional specification of the bombe, "an improvement on the Polish Bomba"; at a July 1939 meeting near Warsaw the Polish Cipher Bureau had given the British and French details of the Enigma rotor wiring and their decryption methods. [source: WP: Alan_Turing]
- Turing was prosecuted for gross indecency in 1952 after acknowledging a relationship with a man; he received probation conditional on chemical castration and lost his security clearance. He died of cyanide poisoning on 7 June 1954, aged 41; the inquest ruled suicide, a verdict his family and biographers have variously accepted or disputed. Gordon Brown apologised in 2009 for the "appalling way [Turing] was treated"; Queen Elizabeth II pardoned him in 2013. [source: WP: Alan_Turing]
- Colossus: designed by Post Office engineer Tommy Flowers (based on plans by Max Newman) to break the German Lorenz cipher; Mark 1 working by December 1943 and in use at Bletchley by early 1944; Mark 2 first ran on 1 June 1944, just before D-Day; ten were in use by the war's end; their existence was kept secret until the mid-1970s. Wikipedia calls it "the world's first programmable, electronic, digital computer" (but it was special-purpose, not general-purpose). [source: WP: Colossus_computer]
- Bletchley Park: in January 1945, 8,995 personnel worked at Bletchley and its outstations; about three-quarters were women (many Wrens of the Women's Royal Naval Service); Colossus was operated by Wrens. [source: WP: Bletchley_Park; WP: Colossus_computer]
- ENIAC: designed by John Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert at the University of Pennsylvania to compute artillery firing tables for the US Army's Ballistic Research Laboratory; Wikipedia calls it "the first programmable, electronic, general-purpose digital computer", completed 1945; first put to practical use 10 December 1945, and its first program was a feasibility study for the hydrogen bomb; formally dedicated 15 February 1946, having cost about $487,000; the press called it a "Giant Brain". [source: WP: ENIAC]
- By 1955 ENIAC contained about 18,000 vacuum tubes, 7,200 crystal diodes, 6,000 relays (other sources give lower counts — safest: "thousands of relays"), 70,000 resistors, 10,000 capacitors and about 5 million hand-soldered joints. [source: WP: ENIAC] (Note: another WP article says "more than 17,000" tubes in 1946 — give "about 17,000–18,000".) [source: WP: Invention_of_the_integrated_circuit]
- ENIAC's six primary programmers: Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings (Jean Bartik), Betty Snyder (Holberton), Marlyn Wescoff (Meltzer), Fran Bilas (Spence) and Ruth Lichterman (Teitelbaum). At least 200 women had been hired by the Moore School as human "computers". The women were generally not publicly recognised in their lifetimes; they were inducted into the Women in Technology International Hall of Fame in 1997. [source: WP: ENIAC]
- The ENIAC patent (filed 1947, issued 1964) was invalidated on 19 October 1973 in Honeywell v. Sperry Rand. [source: WP: ENIAC]
- Manchester Baby (Small-Scale Experimental Machine), built by Frederic Williams, Tom Kilburn and Geoff Tootill, was the first electronic stored-program computer; it ran its first program on 21 June 1948. [source: WP: Manchester_Baby]
- LEO I (Lyons Electronic Office), built by the British catering firm J. Lyons and Co., was the first computer used for commercial business applications; its first business job, "Bakery Valuations", ran successfully on 5 September 1951. Mary Coombs, hired 1952, is recognised as the first female commercial programmer. [source: WP: LEO_(computer)]
- Soviet computing: MESM, built by a team under Sergei Lebedev at the Kiev Institute of Electrotechnology at Feofaniya near Kyiv (Ukrainian SSR), was the first universally programmable electronic computer in the USSR; operational 1950, first useful calculations 4 January 1951. [source: WP: MESM]
- Grace Hopper (9 December 1906 – 1 January 1992), US Navy rear admiral: joined the Harvard Mark I team in 1944; joined Eckert–Mauchly in 1949 (UNIVAC I); by 1952 finished her program for the A-0 System (which she called a compiler; in modern terms closer to a linker); developed FLOW-MATIC, a major influence on COBOL (1959, CODASYL). Posthumous Presidential Medal of Freedom, 22 November 2016. [source: WP: Grace_Hopper]
- The "bug": in 1947 associates of Hopper found a moth in a relay of the Harvard Mark II; it is taped into the log book (now at the Smithsonian). Hopper did not coin "debugging" but is credited with popularising it. [source: WP: Grace_Hopper]
- Katherine Johnson (26 August 1918 – 24 February 2020), Black mathematician from West Virginia, joined NACA's West Area Computing section in 1953 (hired after a 1952 tip); calculated trajectories for Alan Shepard's flight and checked the electronic computer's orbital figures for John Glenn's Friendship 7 flight at Glenn's request; later worked on Apollo rendezvous paths. Presidential Medal of Freedom 2015. Portrayed by Taraji P. Henson in the film Hidden Figures (2016), based on Margot Lee Shetterly's book (William Morrow, 6 September 2016). [source: WP: Katherine_Johnson]
- Mar Hicks: "women were the largest trained technical workforce of the computing industry during the second world war and through to the mid-sixties" (Programmed Inequality). Britain's Stephanie "Steve" Shirley founded Freelance Programmers, employing women working from home, and signed letters as "Steve" to win clients. [source: WP: Women_in_computing]

## The transistor, the chip and Moore's Law
- The point-contact transistor was invented at Bell Labs (Murray Hill, New Jersey) in November–December 1947 by John Bardeen and Walter Brattain; William Shockley invented the bipolar junction transistor in 1948; the three shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics. [source: WP: Transistor]
- Mohamed Atalla and Dawon Kahng proposed the silicon MOS transistor in 1959 and demonstrated it in 1960. MOSFETs are "the most numerously produced artificial objects in history", more than 13 sextillion by 2018. [source: WP: Transistor]
- Sony (founded 7 May 1946 as Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo by Masaru Ibuka and Akio Morita; renamed Sony 1958) launched its first Sony-branded product, the TR-55 transistor radio, in 1955; the Sony TR-63 (1957) was the first mass-produced transistor radio; the Walkman followed in 1979. [source: WP: Sony; WP: Transistor]
- Integrated circuit: Jack Kilby of Texas Instruments assembled a first prototype on 28 August 1958 — a hybrid IC; Robert Noyce of Fairchild Semiconductor invented the monolithic IC using Jean Hoerni's planar process; the first operational semiconductor IC on Noyce's design was made at Fairchild on 27 September 1960. Modern chips descend from Noyce's monolithic design. Kilby received the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics; Noyce had died in 1990 (death date NOT VERIFIED here). Historians debate credit: the 1960s press named four inventors (Kilby, Lehovec, Noyce, Hoerni), later shortened to two. [source: WP: Invention_of_the_integrated_circuit]
- Moore's Law: Gordon Moore (Fairchild, later Intel co-founder), in a 1965 Electronics article "Cramming more components onto integrated circuits", observed components per chip doubling every year; in 1975 he revised it to every two years. Moore in 2015: "I just did a wild extrapolation". Industry-wide advancement has slowed since around 2010; in September 2022 Nvidia's Jensen Huang called Moore's law dead while Intel's Pat Gelsinger disagreed; Dennard scaling ended in the 2000s. [source: WP: Moore's_law]
- Intel 4004, released 15 November 1971, about 2,300 transistors, priced $60; originated in a 1969 commission from the Japanese calculator firm Busicom; design led by Federico Faggin with Busicom's Masatoshi Shima, from Ted Hoff's architecture. [source: WP: Intel_4004]

## East Asia and the global chip and electronics economy
- Taiwan: the semiconductor industry began in 1974; ITRI founded 1973; in 1976 the government persuaded RCA to transfer technology; UMC and Hsinchu Science Park both 1980; development directed under Chiang Ching-kuo. [source: WP: Semiconductor_industry_in_Taiwan]
- TSMC founded 21 February 1987 at ITRI in Hsinchu by Morris Chang (born Ningbo, China, 1931; formerly at Texas Instruments and General Instrument) as a joint venture of the Taiwanese government, ITRI and private investors, with Philips taking about 27.5% and transferring technology; the world's first dedicated semiconductor foundry. It holds about 70% of the global foundry market and is the main supplier to Nvidia, Apple, Broadcom and Qualcomm; WP's Taiwan article says TSMC produces "over half the world's advanced chips". [source: WP: TSMC; WP: Morris_Chang; WP: Semiconductor_industry_in_Taiwan]
- Samsung Electronics, founded 13 January 1969 in Suwon, South Korea (the Samsung group dates from 1938, in Japanese-ruled Korea), flagship of the Samsung chaebol; developed the world's first 64 MB DRAM in 1992; the world's largest memory-chip maker since 1993; with Apple one of the two largest smartphone vendors. [source: WP: Samsung_Electronics]
- US export controls effective 7 October 2022 (Commerce Department, Bureau of Industry and Security) targeted China's access to advanced computing and chip-making items. [source: WP: United_States_New_Export_Controls_on_Advanced_Computing_and_Semiconductors_to_China]

## Networks: ARPANET, the internet, the Web
- Paul Baran (RAND) in the early 1960s researched networks that could survive partial destruction such as nuclear war; Donald Davies (UK National Physical Laboratory) independently developed packet switching. Bob Taylor initiated ARPANET in 1966; Larry Roberts incorporated Davies's packet-switching design and consulted Baran. [source: WP: ARPANET]
- Myth correction: sources quoted in WP state "The ARPANET was not started to create a Command and Control System that would survive a nuclear attack, as many now claim"; its purpose was resource sharing between remote computers. [source: WP: ARPANET]
- First host-to-host connection: UCLA (student Charley Kline) to SRI, 10:30 pm PST, 29 October 1969; the system crashed after two letters, so the first characters transmitted were "lo" (from "login"). [source: WP: ARPANET]
- France's CYCLADES network (from 1972, Louis Pouzin) pioneered the end-to-end approach that influenced TCP/IP; Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn published their internetworking research in 1974; ARPANET switched to TCP/IP on "flag day", 1 January 1983; ARPANET was decommissioned in 1990; NSFNET (1986) linked US research centres; the NSFNET backbone was decommissioned in 1995, removing the last restrictions on commercial traffic. [source: WP: History_of_the_internet; WP: ARPANET]
- Soviet OGAS: Viktor Glushkov proposed in 1962 a national computer network for economic planning (a Moscow centre, up to 200 regional centres, up to 20,000 terminals); it was denied funding in 1970. An earlier Anatoly Kitov proposal was rejected in 1959. [source: WP: OGAS]
- Chile's Project Cybersyn (1971–73) under Salvador Allende: architect British cybernetician Stafford Beer, invited in July 1971 by Fernando Flores of CORFO; four modules (economic simulator, factory-monitoring software, an operations room, and "Cybernet", a telex network — the only part regularly used); used during the October 1972 employers' truck strike to coordinate transport with about 200 trucks (per CORFO's Gustavo Silva); by May 1973, per historian Eden Medina, 26.7% of nationalised industries (50% of sector revenue) were incorporated to some degree; abandoned after the coup of 11 September 1973, when the military destroyed the operations room. [source: WP: Project_Cybersyn]
- World Wide Web: Tim Berners-Lee (born London, 8 June 1955) submitted a proposal at CERN in March 1989; the Web was released to other research institutions from January 1991 and to the whole internet on 23 August 1991; CERN made the code royalty-free on 30 April 1993. NCSA Mosaic (University of Illinois; Marc Andreessen, Eric Bina), released January 1993, made the Web graphical and popular; Netscape overtook it in late 1994. Berners-Lee was knighted in 2004, received the 2016 Turing Award, and at the 2012 London Olympics tweeted "This is for everyone" — also the title of his 2025 book. [source: WP: World_Wide_Web; WP: Tim_Berners-Lee; WP: NCSA_Mosaic]
- Dot-com bubble: Nasdaq rose about 600% from 1995 to its peak on 10 March 2000, then fell 78% by October 2002. Over 40 million people used the internet by 1995. [source: WP: Dot-com_bubble]
- Internet users: about 2.095 billion (30% of world population) in March 2011 [source: WP: Internet]. ITU Facts and Figures 2025 (17 November 2025): about 6 billion people (74%) online; 2.2 billion offline, 96% of them in low- and middle-income countries; 94% use in high-income vs 23% in low-income countries; 77% of men vs 71% of women; 85% urban vs 58% rural. (ITU revised its 2024 offline estimate from 2.6 to 2.3 billion, so older figures differ.) [source: WebSearch: https://www.itu.int/en/mediacentre/Pages/PR-2025-11-17-facts-and-figures.aspx; https://social.desa.un.org/ar/node/8848]

## Personal computers and mobile phones
- Xerox Alto (PARC, 1973) had a graphical user interface that later inspired Apple's Macintosh and Microsoft Windows. The Altair 8800 (MITS, introduced 1974–75) is widely seen as the first commercially successful personal computer; Microsoft's founding product was Altair BASIC. The "1977 Trinity": Commodore PET (revealed January 1977), Apple II (first units shipped 10 June 1977), TRS-80 (August 1977). [source: WP: Personal_computer]
- IBM PC released 12 August 1981 (Intel 8088, open architecture, Microsoft's MS-DOS), price about $1,565. [source: WP: IBM_Personal_Computer]
- Martin Cooper of Motorola made the first public call from a handheld cellular phone prototype on 3 April 1973, on a Manhattan sidewalk, to his counterpart at rival Bell Labs. [source: WP: Martin_Cooper_(inventor)]
- Japan's NTT launched the first commercial automated cellular (1G) network in 1979 (WP marks this sentence "citation needed" in one place; repeated elsewhere in the article). Worldwide mobile subscriptions exceeded 9.1 billion by 2024. [source: WP: Mobile_phone]
- iPhone announced by Steve Jobs on 9 January 2007, launched 29 June 2007 at $499; more than 3 billion sold by July 2025; assembled by contract manufacturers including Foxconn, Pegatron, Wistron and Tata Group. Android has been the best-selling OS on all devices since 2013; 1.54 billion smartphones were shipped in 2019. [source: WP: IPhone; WP: Smartphone]
- Kenya's M-Pesa: piloted October 2005, launched commercially March 2007 by Safaricom and Vodafone, with seed funds from the UK's Department for International Development; since expanded to Tanzania, Mozambique, DRC, Lesotho, Ghana, Egypt and Ethiopia. [source: WP: M-Pesa]

## Platforms, China's internet and the attention economy
- Amazon founded 5 July 1994 (Bellevue, Washington, Jeff Bezos); Google founded 4 September 1998 by Larry Page and Sergey Brin, reorganised under Alphabet in August 2015; in 2025, 73% of Google's revenue came from advertising. Facebook founded 4 February 2004 by Mark Zuckerberg with Harvard roommates; reported about 3.07 billion monthly active users (December 2023). [source: WP: Amazon_(company); WP: Google; WP: Facebook]
- "Big Tech" (Big Five): Microsoft, Apple, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, together about a quarter of the S&P 500; Baidu, Alibaba, Tencent and Xiaomi ("BATX") are their Chinese counterparts. [source: WP: Big_Tech]
- Antitrust: in August 2024 US District Judge Amit Mehta ruled Google held an illegal monopoly in online search and text advertising; in September 2025 he declined to order divestiture of Chrome or Android but barred exclusive contracts and required sharing search data with competitors. [source: WP: Google]
- Social media scale: SixDegrees (1997) is often regarded as the first social media site; approx. 5.66 billion social media user identities in October 2025 (68.7% of world population) per the Digital 2026 report (identities ≠ unique people). [source: WP: Social_media]
- Herbert Simon, 1971: "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention" — basis of the "attention economy" idea, later developed by Michael Goldhaber and others. [source: WP: Attention_economy]
- China: the phrase "Great Firewall of China" was first used in print by Australian sinologist Geremie Barmé in 1997; the Ministry of Public Security issued comprehensive internet regulations in 1997; the Golden Shield Project (surveillance and censorship) began in 2003; Chinese-language Wikipedia blocked May 2015; since 2013 the Cyberspace Administration of China operates the firewall. [source: WP: Great_Firewall]
- Alibaba founded 28 June 1999 in Hangzhou by Jack Ma; its 19 September 2014 New York IPO raised US$25 billion, then the largest in history; in November 2020 Xi Jinping blocked the Ant Group IPO and regulators opened an antitrust probe. [source: WP: Alibaba_Group]
- WeChat (Tencent), released 21 January 2011; over 1 billion monthly users by 2018; a "super-app" for messaging, social media and payments; censors politically sensitive topics. [source: WP: WeChat]
- TikTok/Douyin (ByteDance): Douyin launched 20 September 2016; TikTok is blocked in mainland China, where Douyin operates separately; 2 billion downloads by April 2020; banned in India among others; a US joint venture (TikTok USDS Joint Venture LLC) was formed 22 January 2026, with Oracle, MGX and Silver Lake 15% each and ByteDance 19.9%. [source: WP: TikTok]
- India's IT industry: born in Mumbai in 1967 with Tata Consultancy Services, which began software exports via a 1977 partnership with Burroughs; SEEPZ export zone (Mumbai, 1973) handled over 80% of software exports in the 1980s; in 1991 the government created Software Technology Parks of India to provide satellite links; Infosys founded 2 July 1981 in Pune by seven engineers (incl. N. R. Narayana Murthy, Nandan Nilekani), now headquartered in Bengaluru. Sector share of GDP rose from 1.2% (1998) to 7% (2019); NASSCOM estimates FY2026 revenue $315 billion, exports $246 billion, 5.95 million direct employees. [source: WP: Information_technology_in_India; WP: Infosys]

## The hidden labour and material costs
- Content moderation is largely outsourced to low-wage areas such as India and the Philippines; Meta's outsourced moderators in Kenya and Ghana reported mental illness, self-harm and retaliation; 150 moderators working for Meta, ByteDance and OpenAI contractors launched the first African Content Moderators Union in Nairobi on 1 May 2023, four years after Daniel Motaung was fired for organising at Sama. [source: WP: Content_moderation]
- To build ChatGPT's safety filters, OpenAI used outsourced Kenyan workers via Sama, earning $1.32–$2 per hour, to label sexual-abuse and violent content. [source: WP: ChatGPT]
- Cobalt: the DRC supplied an estimated 67–76% of world cobalt production in 2024 (IEA, USGS, Cobalt Institute) and holds ~55% of reserves; cobalt is key to lithium-ion batteries in phones and electric vehicles; artisanal mining is dangerous and child labour is "rampant"; residents near Kolwezi artisanal sites showed high cobalt exposure; a US lawsuit against Tesla, Google, Apple and others over child labour in cobalt supply chains was dismissed; Chinese companies have expanded their footprint. [source: WP: Mining_industry_of_the_Democratic_Republic_of_the_Congo]
- E-waste, Agbogbloshie (Accra, Ghana): called a "digital dumping ground" by the Basel Action Network; BAN's widely cited claims (80% waste in 2002; up to 75% in 2008) lacked peer-reviewed documentation, and a US International Trade Commission study found little evidence of unprocessed US e-waste shipped to Africa; imports were partly second-hand electronics in demand after the Akosombo Dam brought electricity; burning for metals remains a serious health hazard; the government attempted demolition in July 2021, but scrap workers returned in 2025. The Basel Convention allows export for reuse and repair. [source: WP: Agbogbloshie]

## Biotechnology: from the double helix to mRNA
- Photo 51, an X-ray diffraction image of B-form DNA, was taken on 2 May 1952 by Raymond Gosling, Rosalind Franklin's PhD student at King's College London; Maurice Wilkins showed it to James Watson without Franklin's or Gosling's permission. An MRC report with Franklin's and Gosling's data also reached Crick via Max Perutz. Watson and Crick's model and papers by Wilkins's group and by Franklin and Gosling appeared together in Nature in 1953. [source: WP: Photo_51; WP: Rosalind_Franklin]
- Rosalind Franklin (25 July 1920 – 16 April 1958) died of ovarian cancer at 37; Crick, Watson and Wilkins shared the 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (Nobels are not awarded posthumously); her colleague Aaron Klug won the 1982 Chemistry Nobel. [source: WP: Rosalind_Franklin]
- Human Genome Project: launched 1990 (US DOE and NIH, $3 billion budget; final cost about $2.7 billion); twenty centres in the US, UK, Japan, France, Germany and China; rival private effort by Celera from 1998; working draft announced 2000, published February 2001; declared complete 14 April 2003 at ~92%; gapless sequence January 2022. [source: WP: Human_Genome_Project]
- CRISPR: in 2012 Jennifer Doudna (born 1964, UC Berkeley) and Emmanuelle Charpentier (born 1968, French; later Max Planck, Berlin) proposed CRISPR-Cas9 for programmable genome editing; they shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry "for the development of a method for genome editing". Lithuanian Virginijus Šikšnys, who shared the Kavli Prize for the same discovery, did not receive the Nobel. The Broad Institute (Feng Zhang) won US patents for use in eukaryotic cells (2014; interference ruling February 2017). [source: WP: Jennifer_Doudna; WP: Emmanuelle_Charpentier; WP: CRISPR_gene_editing]
- First CRISPR therapy: Casgevy (exagamglogene autotemcel) for sickle-cell disease and beta thalassaemia, approved in the UK in 2023 (first), Bahrain 2 December 2023 (second), US FDA 8 December 2023. [source: WP: CRISPR_gene_editing]
- He Jiankui (SUSTech, Shenzhen) announced on 26 November 2018 the birth of gene-edited twin girls "Lulu" and "Nana" (born October 2018; a third child born 2019), edited to disable CCR5 for HIV resistance; story broken by MIT Technology Review on 25 November; he presented at the Hong Kong summit on 28 November; on 30 December 2019 a Shenzhen court sentenced him to three years and a 3 million yuan fine; released April 2022; China's 2020 Civil Code and Criminal Law amendments now criminalise illegal human gene editing (up to seven years). [source: WP: He_Jiankui_affair]
- Katalin Karikó (born 17 January 1955, Szolnok, Hungary; PhD Szeged) left Hungary in 1985 with her husband and two-year-old daughter, carrying £900 hidden in a teddy bear; demoted by the University of Pennsylvania in 1995 and never tenured; from 2005 she and Drew Weissman (born 1959) published studies showing nucleoside-modified mRNA avoids triggering immune reactions; joined BioNTech 2013; they shared the 2023 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for their discoveries concerning nucleoside base modifications that enabled the development of effective mRNA vaccines against COVID-19". From 1978 to 1985 she was listed as an informant by Hungarian secret police, which she says she was blackmailed into. [source: WP: Katalin_Karikó; WP: Drew_Weissman]
- Pfizer–BioNTech (BioNTech founded by Uğur Şahin and Özlem Türeci, Mainz): first COVID-19 vaccine authorised by a stringent regulator; UK emergency authorisation December 2020; full FDA approval 23 August 2021. [source: WP: Pfizer–BioNTech_COVID-19_vaccine]
- Vaccine inequity: COVAX shortages were blamed on "vaccine nationalism" by richer nations and on India's diversion of 400 million doses made by the Serum Institute of India; COVAX closed on 31 December 2023 after delivering 2 billion doses. [source: WP: COVAX]

## Artificial intelligence
- Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence, summer 1956 (about six to eight weeks), organised by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon; proposed 2 September 1955; the proposal is credited with introducing the term "artificial intelligence". [source: WP: Dartmouth_workshop]
- Early symbolic AI: the Logic Theorist (Newell, Simon, Shaw, 1955–56) proved 38 theorems from Principia Mathematica. IBM's Deep Blue beat Garry Kasparov in a game in 1996 (the 1997 match victory is NOT VERIFIED in fetched text). [source: WP: Symbolic_artificial_intelligence]
- AI winters: roughly 1974–1980 and 1987–2000; triggers included the 1966 ALPAC report on machine translation, the 1969 critique of perceptrons, the UK Lighthill report (1973), DARPA cutbacks (1973–74), the collapse of the LISP-machine market (1987) and the unmet goals of Japan's Fifth Generation project (MITI set aside $850 million in 1981). [source: WP: AI_winter]
- Deep learning's long, international roots: Alexey Ivakhnenko and Lapa (USSR/Ukraine) published a method to train deep networks in 1965; Shun'ichi Amari (Japan) a deep multilayer perceptron trained by stochastic gradient descent in 1967; Kunihiko Fukushima the ReLU (1969) and the Neocognitron (1979); Seppo Linnainmaa (Finland) the modern form of backpropagation (1970); Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams's 1986 paper popularised backpropagation (Hinton: "David Rumelhart came up with the basic idea"). [source: WP: Deep_learning; WP: Geoffrey_Hinton]
- AlexNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Hinton) won the ImageNet competition in October 2012 by a wide margin. Computation used in the largest projects grew 300,000-fold from AlexNet (2012) to AlphaZero (2017) (OpenAI estimate). [source: WP: Deep_learning]
- Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton and Yann LeCun won the 2018 Turing Award "for conceptual and engineering breakthroughs that have made deep neural networks a critical component of computing". [source: WP: Deep_learning]
- Hinton (born 6 December 1947) worked for Google 2013–2023 and left in May 2023 citing AI risks; he and John Hopfield (born 1933) shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics "for foundational discoveries and inventions that enable machine learning with artificial neural networks". [source: WP: Geoffrey_Hinton; WP: John_Hopfield]
- DeepMind (co-founded 2010; Demis Hassabis born 1976) was bought by Google in 2014 for £400 million; AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol 4–1 in Seoul in March 2016. AlphaFold 2 led CASP14 in November 2020; its paper and open database (with EMBL-EBI, launched 22 July 2021) followed; some researchers noted it did not reveal the underlying rules of folding. Hassabis and John Jumper shared half of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry "for protein structure prediction"; David Baker got the other half "for computational protein design". [source: WP: Demis_Hassabis; WP: AlphaFold]
- "Attention Is All You Need" (2017), by eight Google researchers (Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez, Kaiser, Polosukhin), introduced the transformer architecture; cited more than 150,000 times as of 2026; all eight later left Google. [source: WP: Attention_Is_All_You_Need]
- GPT-2 (2019) and GPT-3 (2020) from OpenAI; BERT (Google, 2018). ChatGPT released 30 November 2022; reportedly reached 100 million monthly users in two months and 900 million weekly active users in February 2026 (company/press figures). In January 2025 China's DeepSeek released R1, an open-weight model reported to perform comparably to leading models at much lower price. [source: WP: Large_language_model; WP: ChatGPT]
- LLMs "hallucinate": they confidently assert unjustified claims. The term "stochastic parrot" comes from the 2021 paper "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots" by Emily Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major and Margaret Mitchell; Google had asked for its retraction, leading to Gebru's contested departure in December 2020. [source: WP: Large_language_model; WP: Stochastic_parrot]
- IEA, Energy and AI (April 2025): data centres used about 415 TWh, ~1.5% of world electricity, in 2024; projected ~945 TWh by 2030 (roughly Japan's current consumption), with large uncertainty. [source: WebSearch: https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary]

## Privacy, surveillance and regulation
- Edward Snowden (born 21 June 1983), NSA contractor (Booz Allen Hamilton, 2013), gave documents to journalists in Hong Kong; publication began June 2013 in The Guardian and The Washington Post (programmes included PRISM); charged under the Espionage Act on 21 June 2013; granted Russian asylum in late July 2013; Russian citizen since 2022. On 2 September 2020 the US Ninth Circuit ruled the bulk phone-records programme he exposed was illegal and possibly unconstitutional. [source: WP: Edward_Snowden; WP: Snowden_disclosures]
- Cambridge Analytica: the app "This Is Your Digital Life" (Aleksandr Kogan, 2013) harvested data from up to 87 million Facebook profiles; used for the 2016 Cruz and Trump campaigns; exposed March 2018 by whistleblower Christopher Wylie in The Guardian and The New York Times; FTC fined Facebook $5 billion (July 2019); UK ICO fine £500,000 (agreed October 2019). The UK investigation found the firm was not involved in the Brexit referendum "beyond some initial enquiries". [source: WP: Facebook–Cambridge_Analytica_data_scandal]
- GDPR adopted 14 April 2016, effective 25 May 2018; fines up to €20 million or 4% of worldwide turnover; became a model ("Brussels effect") for laws in Brazil, Japan, South Africa, South Korea, Thailand and others; Meta was fined €1.2 billion for unlawful EU–US data transfers. [source: WP: General_Data_Protection_Regulation] (WP dates that fine to January 2025 — NOT VERIFIED; other reporting places it in May 2023. Say "a €1.2 billion fine" without a date unless checked.)
- EU Digital Services Act in force 16 November 2022; strictest duties for platforms with over 45 million EU users; fines up to 6% of global turnover. [source: WP: Digital_Services_Act]
- EU AI Act: proposed 21 April 2021; Parliament vote 13 March 2024; Council 21 May 2024; in force 1 August 2024; risk tiers (unacceptable/banned — e.g. social scoring, manipulation, real-time public biometric ID with exceptions — high, limited, minimal) plus general-purpose AI; bans applied 2 February 2025; general-purpose AI rules 2 August 2025; Code of Practice published 10 July 2025. The Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026) postponed high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 (Annex III) and 2 August 2028 (Annex I). [source: WP: Artificial_Intelligence_Act]

## Automation and work
- Keynes popularised "technological unemployment" in the 1930s, calling it "only a temporary phase of maladjustment"; debates peaked in the 1930s and 1960s. [source: WP: Technological_unemployment]
- Carl Benedikt Frey and Michael Osborne (Oxford Martin School, 2013, "The Future of Employment") estimated 47% of US jobs at high risk of automation; Bruegel (2014) applied the method to the EU (54%). [source: WP: Technological_unemployment]
- Arntz, Gregory and Zierahn (OECD, 2016) assessed tasks rather than whole occupations and found 9% of jobs automatable across 21 OECD countries (6% Korea to 12% Austria), warning that low-skilled workers would bear the adjustment costs. [source: WebSearch: https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/the-risk-of-automation-for-jobs-in-oecd-countries_5jlz9h56dvq7-en.html]
- Daron Acemoglu shared the 2024 economics Nobel with Simon Johnson and James Robinson (for comparative studies of prosperity). [source: WP: Daron_Acemoglu]

## Debates
- **Who built "the first computer"?** Colossus (1943–44) was the first programmable electronic digital computer but special-purpose and secret until the 1970s; ENIAC (1945) the first general-purpose one; the Manchester Baby (1948) the first stored-program machine; the ENIAC patent was voided in 1973 partly because of earlier work (Atanasoff–Berry). Wartime secrecy shaped the American-centred story. [source: WP: Colossus_computer; WP: ENIAC; WP: Manchester_Baby]
- **Invisible women:** historians (Hicks; Abbate) argue that women dominated early programming and were written out as computing gained prestige — ENIAC's programmers were recognised only in the 1990s, and Bletchley's workforce was three-quarters female. [source: WP: Women_in_computing; WP: ENIAC; WP: Bletchley_Park]
- **Rosalind Franklin, victim or partner?** The traditional "wronged heroine" narrative (rooted in Watson's 1968 *The Double Helix* — book date NOT VERIFIED) vs Cobb and Comfort (Nature, 2023), who use a 1953 letter and an unpublished news article to argue she was an equal contributor, not a victim; Nature's editorial acknowledged the credit question remains contested. [source: WebSearch: https://phys.org/news/2023-04-rosalind-franklin-contributed-discovery-dna.amp; WP: Rosalind_Franklin]
- **Credit in invention:** IC (Kilby vs Noyce vs Lehovec and Hoerni); deep learning (Soviet, Japanese and Finnish precursors vs the 2018 Turing laureates); CRISPR (Doudna–Charpentier vs Broad/Zhang patents; Šikšnys left out of the Nobel). [source: WP: Invention_of_the_integrated_circuit; WP: Deep_learning; WP: CRISPR_gene_editing]
- **Surveillance capitalism vs monopoly:** Zuboff (2019) argues Google and Facebook invented a "new logic of accumulation" that turns behavioural data into prediction products; Cory Doctorow, *How to Destroy Surveillance Capitalism* (OneZero, August 2020), counters that this credits Big Tech with "mind control" it lacks and that the real problem is monopoly; Evgeny Morozov ("Capitalism's New Clothes", The Baffler, 4 February 2019) also critiqued her. Note the doubts about Cambridge Analytica's real effectiveness: the UK investigation found no significant Brexit role. [source: WP: Surveillance_capitalism; WebSearch: https://boingboing.net/2020/08/26/cory-doctorow-describes-what.html; WP: Facebook–Cambridge_Analytica_data_scandal]
- **Platforms and atrocity:** passive-platform view vs Amnesty's argument (2022) that engagement-driven algorithms actively amplified hate in Myanmar; UN's "determining role". [source: WebSearch: Amnesty URL above; WP: Facebook_content_management_controversies]
- **Automation and jobs:** Frey and Osborne's 47% vs OECD's 9% (occupation vs task method); critics (Bruce Chapman) note such studies ignore new jobs. Acemoglu and Johnson (2023) argue gains depend on whether technology creates new tasks or merely automates, and on workers' countervailing power; critics (Sarewitz; Noah Smith) question their mechanism and historical examples. [source: WP: Technological_unemployment; WebSearch: OECD URL; https://sloanreview.mit.edu/?p=108664; https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/book-review-power-and-progress]
- **Mobile money and poverty:** Suri and Jack (Science, 2016) estimated M-Pesa lifted 194,000 households (2% of Kenyan households) out of poverty, a figure widely cited by development institutions; Milford Bateman et al. (2019) called the work so flawed that it helped create "a largely false narrative" about fintech and poverty reduction. [source: WP: M-Pesa]
- **E-waste "dumping" narrative:** BAN's 75–80% waste claims vs USITC findings and the reuse/repair trade; real health harms remain. [source: WP: Agbogbloshie]
- **LLMs:** "stochastic parrots" critics vs those who see understanding; hallucination; copyright of training data; energy use (IEA). [source: WP: Large_language_model; WP: Stochastic_parrot; WP: ChatGPT]
- **Who governs?** EU rule-making (GDPR, DSA, AI Act — partly postponed by the 2026 Digital Omnibus) vs US antitrust litigation vs China's state control (Great Firewall, 2020 Ant Group crackdown) vs techno-national chip rivalry (US export controls 2022; TSMC's concentration in Taiwan). [sources as cited above]
