# Verified facts — Modern Southeast Asia (researched 2026-11-11)

Node: `history/regional-histories/asia/modern-southeast-asia`. Scope: about 1800 to the present; Indonesia, the Philippines, Malaysia and Singapore, Thailand (Siam), Myanmar (Burma), Cambodia, Laos, Vietnam (brief: the sibling chapter "Indochina and Modern Vietnam, 1880s–1980s" covers it in depth), Brunei and Timor-Leste.

Sources are English Wikipedia raw wikitext (fetched 2026-11-11) unless stated; URLs give the article. Where Wikipedia attributes a figure to a historian, the attribution is given.

## Colonial expansion: the Dutch East Indies
- The Java War (1825–1830), led by Prince Diponegoro against the Dutch, killed at least 200,000 Javanese civilians through violence, disease and starvation (Carey); about 15,000 Dutch-side soldiers and about 20,000 rebels died. Diponegoro was seized while negotiating with General de Kock at Magelang and exiled to Sulawesi, where he died in 1855. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_War]
- The Anglo-Dutch Treaty was signed in London on 17 March 1824 (for Britain, George Canning and Charles Williams-Wynn), dividing British and Dutch spheres in the archipelago. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Dutch_Treaty_of_1824]
- The Cultivation System (Dutch *cultuurstelsel*; Indonesian historians call it *tanam paksa*, "enforced planting") was Dutch government policy from 1830 to 1870, introduced by Governor-General Johannes van den Bosch after the costly Java War and the Belgian Revolution left Dutch finances near bankruptcy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultivation_System]
- Under it, 20% of village land was meant to be given to government export crops (sugar, indigo, coffee and others), or peasants worked 66 days a year on government plantations; in practice more than 20% of land was often taken. Up to about 1 million farmers worked under the system at its peak. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultivation_System]
- At its peak the system supplied about 50% of Dutch state revenue (figure cited from de Volkskrant); as early as 1831 it balanced the Indies budget. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultivation_System]
- It is linked to famines and epidemics in the 1840s, first in Cirebon and then Central Java, as indigo and sugar replaced rice; recent research (de Zwart and Gallardo-Albarrán, 2021) finds mortality rose by as much as 30%, and "several tens of thousands" died. The Sugar Law and Agrarian Law of 1870 ended it and opened the "Liberal Period" of private enterprise; forced labour persisted in other forms. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultivation_System]
- The Dutch "Ethical Policy" ran from 1901 (announced by Queen Wilhelmina under Abraham Kuyper's cabinet) until the Japanese occupation of 1942; it followed C. Th. van Deventer's 1899 essay in *De Gids* arguing the Netherlands owed the Indies a "debt of honour". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Ethical_Policy]
- (Cross-reference) The Aceh War from 1873 and Cut Nyak Dhien's guerrilla war are told in the published chapter on Imperial Expansion and Resistance (50,000–70,000 Acehnese dead; resistance until 1942).

## Colonial expansion: British Burma, Malaya, Singapore and Borneo
- Three Anglo-Burmese wars (1824–26, 1852, 1885) brought all of Burma under British rule. By the Treaty of Yandabo (1826) Burma lost Assam, Manipur and Arakan; the second war (1852), provoked by Commodore Lambert "in extremely questionable circumstances" under Lord Dalhousie, annexed Pegu (Lower Burma); King Mindon then built a new capital at Mandalay; the third war (November 1885) ended with King Thibaw a prisoner and the total annexation of Burma, followed by a decade of resistance (1885–1895). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Burmese_Wars]
- Stamford Raffles negotiated a treaty in 1819 letting Britain place a trading port on Singapore island. The Straits Settlements (Penang, Malacca, Singapore) were a division of the Bengal Presidency from 1826 and a Crown colony from 1867 to 1946. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Singapore; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straits_Settlements]
- The Pangkor Treaty of 20 January 1874, signed aboard the steamer *Pluto* off Perak by Straits governor Sir Andrew Clarke, required the Sultan of Perak to accept a British Resident "whose advice must be asked and acted upon on all questions other than those touching Malay religion and custom". The Federated Malay States followed in 1895 (in effect 1 July 1896). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pangkor_Treaty_of_1874; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federated_Malay_States]
- By the Anglo-Siamese Treaty, signed in Bangkok on 10 March 1909, Siam gave up claims to Kedah, Kelantan, Perlis and Terengganu, which became British protectorates; Britain in return gave up extraterritorial jurisdiction granted in 1855. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Siamese_Treaty_of_1909]
- Brunei: oil was struck at Seria on 5 April 1929. A 1962 armed rebellion (from 8 December 1962) was put down with British help; the British protectorate ended and Brunei became fully sovereign on 1 January 1984, joining ASEAN on 7 January 1984. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunei; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASEAN]

## Colonial expansion: the Philippines, Spanish then American
- José Rizal (19 June 1861 – 30 December 1896) wrote the novels *Noli Me Tángere* (1887) and *El filibusterismo* (1891), together taken as a national epic; he founded La Liga Filipina in July 1892, was arrested on 6 July 1892 and banished to Dapitan, Mindanao, and was executed by firing squad at Bagumbayan, Manila, in December 1896 for "rebellion". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Rizal]
- The Katipunan secret society was founded in Manila on 7 July 1892; the revolution's first phase ran from 26 August 1896 to the Pact of Biak-na-Bato (December 1897). Andrés Bonifacio was sentenced to death by Aguinaldo's War Council on 10 May 1897. Independence was proclaimed on 12 June 1898. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine_Revolution]
- The United States annexed the Philippines from Spain under the Treaty of Paris (December 1898). Fighting began on 4 February 1899 (Battle of Manila); Aguinaldo was captured on 23 March 1901; the US declared the war over on 4 July 1902, though the Moro Rebellion in the south continued until 1913. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War]
- Death tolls, attributed: the US State Department says the war killed over 4,200 American and over 20,000 Filipino combatants and that "as many as 200,000 Filipino civilians died from violence, famine, and disease"; modern historians generally put civilian deaths at 200,000–250,000; some estimates reach 1,000,000. Luzviminda Francisco cited General James M. Bell's reported claim of "over 600,000" dead in Luzon alone; John M. Gates calls 600,000 unfounded and puts the maximum possible war-related deaths 1898–1903 at 321,000 from census data. A cholera epidemic at the war's end killed 150,000–200,000. Andrea Pitzer counts over 11,000 Filipino deaths in concentration camps. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War]
- After the Balangiga attack, General Jacob H. Smith's order during the pacification of Samar, rendered "KILL EVERY ONE OVER TEN", became the caption of a *New York Journal* cartoon of 5 May 1902. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War]
- The Tydings–McDuffie Act (Philippine Independence Act, 24 March 1934) set a ten-year transition; the 1935 Constitution created the Commonwealth of the Philippines with its first directly elected president. (Independence came on 4 July 1946, as the published Decolonization chapter states.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tydings%E2%80%93McDuffie_Act]

## Colonial expansion: French Indochina (brief; sibling chapter covers in depth)
- The Indochinese Union (French Indochina) was created by decree of 17 October 1887 (Cochinchina, Annam, Tonkin, Cambodia); Laos was added after the 1893 Franco-Siamese crisis (French protectorate of Laos from 1899 in the union's chronology). The federation lasted 1887–1954, with Japanese occupation 1941–45. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina]
- In the 1893 crisis (13 July – 3 October 1893) French gunboats forced their way up the Chao Phraya at Paknam to blockade Bangkok; Siam ceded the land east of the Mekong. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1893_Franco-Siamese_crisis]

## Siam's survival and Chulalongkorn
- The Bowring Treaty with Britain, signed 18 April 1855, fixed import duties at 3%, allowed British opium imports through government opium tax farmers, and granted extraterritoriality (ended for Britain in 1909). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowring_Treaty]
- Chulalongkorn (Rama V; 20 September 1853 – 23 October 1910) reigned from 1 October 1868 to 1910, succeeding Mongkut; his reign combined modernisation with territorial concessions to Britain and France. He set up a Council of State in 1874 and ministries in 1892, and abolished slavery gradually, citing the bloodshed of the American Civil War as a reason to avoid an abrupt break; the Slave Abolition Act of 1905 ended slavery in all forms. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chulalongkorn]
- The Siamese revolution of 24 June 1932, by the Khana Ratsadon (People's Party: Pridi Banomyong, Plaek Phibunsongkhram and others), ended absolute monarchy under King Prajadhipok. The country was renamed Thailand on 24 June 1939. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siamese_revolution_of_1932; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thailand]
- Wikipedia states Thailand "remains the only Southeast Asian state never colonised by a Western power". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thailand]
- Thongchai Winichakul's *Siam Mapped: A History of the Geo-Body of a Nation* (1994) critiqued existing Thai historiography (see Debates). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thongchai_Winichakul]

## Plantation and migrant economies
- Sunil Amrith estimates that "somewhere around 28 million people crossed the Bay of Bengal in both directions between 1840 and 1940", mainly between India and Burma and Malaya; reviewers stress it is an estimate because statistics are unreliable. [source: https://reviews.history.ac.uk/print/review/2042 (via WebSearch); https://www.deccanherald.com/features/crosscurrents-2287983]
- The kangani system recruited labour (mostly Tamil, from South India) for British Ceylon, Burma, Malaya and Singapore from the early nineteenth to the early twentieth century; a *kangani* (Tamil, "one who observes", i.e. foreman) recruited through family and village networks and then supervised the workers. It began in Ceylon's coffee estates in the 1820s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kangani_system]
- Indian migrants cleared land, established rubber plantations and built roads, railways and ports in Malaya; about 80% of Malaysian Indians trace roots to the Madras Presidency. There were about 120,000 Indians in the Straits Settlements and FMS in 1901 and 640,000 in Malaya and Singapore by 1931, outnumbering Malays in Selangor that year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysian_Indians]
- (Cross-reference) Chinese tin miners in Larut (about 40,000 by about 1870) and Malaya as the world's largest tin producer by 1883; rubber seeds from Brazil planted in Malaya: see the published Second Industrial Revolution chapter.
- The Japanese-era and colonial "plural society" fed later ethnic politics: Malaysia's 13 May 1969 riots in Kuala Lumpur after the general election killed 196 by official count; other sources say from nearly 600 to over 1,000, mostly Chinese. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/13_May_incident]
- Malaysia's New Economic Policy (1971–1990, succeeded by the National Development Policy in 1991) aimed to raise Bumiputera ownership of corporate equity from 2.4% to 30%. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysian_New_Economic_Policy]
- Under Suharto's New Order (1967–1998) the government dismantled pillars of ethnic Chinese identity in favour of assimilation; the May 1998 riots targeted ethnic Chinese communities in Jakarta, Medan and Surakarta. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Indonesians; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Suharto]

## Nationalism
- Budi Utomo held its first congress on 20 May 1908, founded by students of the STOVIA medical school in Batavia on the initiative of retired government doctor Wahidin Soedirohoesodo; its founding is treated as the start of the Indonesian National Awakening. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budi_Utomo]
- Sarekat Islam began in 1911 as Sarekat Dagang Islam, a cooperative of Muslim Javanese batik traders competing with Chinese traders (leader Samanhudi), renamed in 1912 under Oemar Said Tjokroaminoto (leader 1912–1934). By 1916 it claimed 80 branches and around a million members; in 1921 communist members split off as "Red" Sarekat Islam, absorbed into the PKI. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarekat_Islam]
- (Cross-reference) Sukarno's party (1927) and the Youth Pledge of 28 October 1928 are in the published Interwar chapter.
- Saya San, a Burmese physician and former monk, led the Galon rebellion (1930–1932), proclaiming himself king; he was captured in August 1931 and executed on 28 November 1931. (Wikipedia's lead gives 1937 as his death year in one place, an evident typo against its infobox and body; use 1931.) Maitrii Aung-Thwin's *The Return of the Galon King* (2011) re-examines how colonial law framed the rebellion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saya_San]
- Aung San (13 February 1915 – 19 July 1947) was expelled from Rangoon University with U Nu in February 1936 over a student-paper article; joined the Thakin Society (Dobama Asiayone) in 1938 as general secretary; trained with the "Thirty Comrades" under Japanese officers on Hainan; inaugurated the Burma Independence Army in Bangkok on 28 December 1941; turned his army against Japan on 27 March 1945. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aung_San]
- He negotiated independence in the Aung San–Attlee agreement (January 1947) and the Panglong agreement with ethnic minority leaders on 12 February 1947 (celebrated as Union Day); led the AFPFL to victory in the 1947 election; and was assassinated with most of his cabinet on 19 July 1947. Former prime minister U Saw was arrested, tried and hanged, though other claims of responsibility persist. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aung_San; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Myanmar]

## Japanese occupation, 1941–45
- Japan invaded Burma in 1942 and on 1 August 1943 declared it nominally independent as the "State of Burma" under Ba Maw; 170,000 to 250,000 civilians died in Burma under occupation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_occupation_of_Burma]
- Rōmusha (forced labourers) were mobilised in Java, Sumatra and eastern Indonesia; about 10% were women. M. C. Ricklefs estimates 200,000–500,000 Javanese were sent off Java (to outer islands, Burma, Thailand), of whom only about 70,000 survived; Shigeru Satō estimates about 270,000 sent outside Java, with about 15% dying or stranded. They built the Burma–Thailand Railway (1942–43) and the Sumatra Railway (1943–45); their death rate was far higher than that of Allied POWs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C5%8Dmusha]
- "A later United Nations report" stated that 4 million people died in Indonesia from famine and forced labour during the occupation (including about 30,000 European internees); around 2.4 million are said to have died of famine in Java in 1944–45. Treat both as high, single-source estimates. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_occupation_of_the_Dutch_East_Indies; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C5%8Dmusha]
- The PETA (Defenders of the Homeland) volunteer force was created by Japan; Supriyadi led a PETA mutiny against the Japanese at Blitar in February 1945. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_occupation_of_the_Dutch_East_Indies]
- Vietnamese famine of 1944–45 in the north: estimates range from 400,000 to 2 million deaths. A French military report of October 1945 estimated half a million; Governor-General Jean Decoux wrote of about a million; Ho Chi Minh's independence proclamation of 2 September 1945 cited 2 million; the Viet Minh said 1–2 million. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_famine_of_1944%E2%80%931945]
- Philippines: about 76,000 American and Filipino defenders surrendered on Bataan on 9 April 1942; 7,000–10,000 died on the Bataan Death March. Around 500,000 Filipinos died during the occupation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_occupation_of_the_Philippines]
- The Battle of Manila (3 February – 3 March 1945) killed at least 100,000 civilians, through Japanese massacres and American firepower. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Manila_(1945)]
- (Cross-reference) Sook Ching, the Burma Railway, "comfort women" and the Greater East Asia Conference are told in the published chapter on Japan's empire; do not repeat its figures differently.

## Decolonisation
- Indonesia: independence proclaimed 17 August 1945. Bersiap-period deaths are estimated at 3,500–30,000. The Linggadjati Agreement (November 1946) recognised the Republic as *de facto* authority in Java, Madura and Sumatra; the UN-brokered Renville Agreement followed its collapse. The Round Table Conference in The Hague ran from 23 August to 2 November 1949 and sovereignty passed on 27 December 1949. Indonesian dead: 97,421 confirmed killed, about 100,000 estimated. Indonesia paid the inherited debt in instalments until 2002. King Willem-Alexander apologised for "excessive violence" in March 2020 and PM Mark Rutte apologised in 2022. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_National_Revolution]
- Malaya: the Malayan Union (from 1 April 1946) provoked Malay opposition and was replaced in 1948 by the Federation of Malaya. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malayan_Union]
- Malayan Emergency, 16 June 1948 – 31 July 1960 (began with the Sungai Siput killings). Under the Briggs Plan about 1,000,000 rural people, mostly Chinese, were moved into more than 600 "New Villages", which some historians call concentration camps. At Batang Kali in Selangor in December 1948 Scots Guards executed 24 unarmed villagers. Infobox totals: 6,710 guerrillas killed; 1,346 Malayan and 1,443 British security forces killed; 2,478 civilians killed and 810 missing. Gerald Templer was a leading commander. Malaya became independent on 31 August 1957. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malayan_Emergency]
- Malaysia was formed on 16 September 1963 from Malaya, Singapore, North Borneo (Sabah) and Sarawak; Brunei stayed out. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia_Agreement; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_Singapore_(Malaysia)]
- Indonesia's *Konfrontasi* against Malaysia ran from 20 January 1963 (announced by Foreign Minister Subandrio) to a peace agreement on 11 August 1966. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konfrontasi]
- After 1964 race riots and political disputes, Tunku Abdul Rahman chose to separate Singapore; the Separation Agreement was signed on 7 August 1965 and Singapore became sovereign on 9 August 1965. Lee Kuan Yew (16 September 1923 – 23 March 2015) was prime minister from 5 June 1959 to 28 November 1990. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_Singapore_(Malaysia); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Kuan_Yew]
- Burma became independent under the Burma Independence Act 1947 on 4 January 1948, with U Nu as prime minister. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Myanmar]

## The Cold War
- Indonesia under Sukarno: Guided Democracy from 9 July 1959 (Sukarno acting as his own prime minister until 1966). Sukarno lived 6 June 1901 – 21 June 1970. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sukarno]
- After the 30 September Movement killed six generals, Suharto took command of the army on 1 October 1965; the army's propaganda campaign blaming the PKI began on 5 October, the day of the generals' funeral. On 11 March 1966 Sukarno transferred key powers to Suharto by the letter called *Supersemar*; Suharto was president from 12 March 1967 to 21 May 1998. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suharto; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_mass_killings_of_1965%E2%80%9366]
- Killings of 1965–66: "the most widely published estimates" are 500,000 to 1 million; some higher estimates reach 2 to 3 million (Robinson, *The Killing Season*, is cited for the 500,000–1,000,000+ range; Robinson calls 500,000 conservative). As many as 1.5 million were imprisoned at some stage; perhaps 100,000 were still held without trial in the mid-1970s. Victims included PKI members, Gerwani women, trade unionists and ethnic Chinese. The worst killings were in Central and East Java, Bali, Aceh and northern Sumatra; army units, paramilitaries and in places Nahdlatul Ulama's Ansor youth wing took part. Novelist Pramoedya Ananta Toer was among those arrested. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_mass_killings_of_1965%E2%80%9366]
- A 1968 CIA report said the massacres "rank as one of the worst mass murders of the 20th century"; historians and journalists have established that US officials supplied lists of PKI officials to the army; declassified US files were released in October 2017. Britain and other allies also aided the army. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_mass_killings_of_1965%E2%80%9366]
- Acknowledgement: Abdurrahman Wahid apologised in 2000; President Joko Widodo acknowledged the violations in January 2023 (Wikipedia's text says January 2024, but its own cited news report is dated 12 January 2023). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_mass_killings_of_1965%E2%80%9366]
- Suharto's New Order: real GDP growth of about 5% a year from 1966 to 1997 (around 7% in some accounts), poverty falling from about 45% (1970) to about 11% (1996); Transparency International listed him among the most corrupt leaders in modern history, with an alleged US$15–35 billion embezzled. He died on 27 January 2008. On 10 November 2025 President Prabowo Subianto, his former son-in-law, named him a National Hero, reviving debate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suharto]
- Benedict Anderson's work on the "Cornell Paper", which disputed the official account of the 30 September Movement, led to his expulsion from Indonesia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedict_Anderson]
- Khmer Rouge rule, 17 April 1975 – 7 January 1979 (country renamed Democratic Kampuchea in 1976). Death tolls, attributed: about 1.5 to 2 million (20–25% of the 1975 population) in widely cited ranges; Ben Kiernan: "between 1.671 and 1.871 million people, 21 to 24 percent of Cambodia's 1975 population"; Patrick Heuveline: "over two million Cambodians died during the 1970s because of the political events of the decade, the vast majority" under the Khmer Rouge. Victims included 200,000–300,000 Chinese Cambodians, 90,000–500,000 Cham (estimates vary sharply), about 20,000 Vietnamese. Documentation Center of Cambodia had mapped 23,745 mass graves by 2009 with about 1.3 million suspected execution victims. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambodian_genocide]
- The Khmer Rouge Tribunal (ECCC) convicted Kang Kek Iew (Duch, head of the Tuol Sleng/S-21 prison) on 26 July 2010; on 16 November 2018 it convicted Nuon Chea and Khieu Samphan, finding genocide (including of the Cham, for Nuon Chea, under superior responsibility, and of ethnic Vietnamese). Vietnam's invasion (December 1978) ended the regime. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambodian_genocide]
- Laos: the civil war ran from 23 May 1959 to 2 December 1975, when the Pathet Lao proclaimed the Lao People's Democratic Republic (President Souphanouvong). Hmong irregulars under Vang Pao fought for the royal government with CIA support. The US dropped over 2 million tons of ordnance on Laos in about 580,000 bombing missions (1964–73), making it the most heavily bombed country per capita (Legacies of War; some sources say 2.5 million tons); up to 80 million of 270 million cluster bomblets did not detonate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laotian_Civil_War; https://www.legaciesofwar.org/ (via WebSearch)]
- Ferdinand Marcos (11 September 1917 – 28 September 1989; president 1965–1986) signed Proclamation 1081 dated 21 September 1972 and announced martial law on the evening of 23 September 1972; martial law was formally lifted on 17 January 1981 (Proclamation 2045) without restoring habeas corpus. He expanded the military from 65,000 to 270,000 between 1972 and 1976. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Marcos; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclamation_No._1081]
- Human-rights toll, attributed: Wikipedia summarises about 1,500 to 4,000 documented extrajudicial killings or disappearances, plus thousands killed in the Moro conflict. Alfred McCoy's widely quoted figures are 3,257 killed (1975–85), 35,000 tortured and 70,000 incarcerated; Amnesty International (2018) gives "some 70,000 people were imprisoned and 34,000 were tortured; over 3,200 people were killed" for 1972–1981. The family allegedly took as much as US$10 billion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Marcos; https://www.manilatimes.net/3257-fact-checking-the-marcos-killings-1975-1985/255735/ (via WebSearch)]
- Benigno "Ninoy" Aquino Jr. was assassinated at Manila International Airport on 21 August 1983 on returning from exile. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ninoy_Aquino]
- People Power (EDSA) Revolution, 22–25 February 1986: after a disputed snap election, hundreds of thousands filled EDSA highway; Marcos fled into exile on 25 February 1986 and Corazon Aquino became president. His son Bongbong Marcos has been president since 2022. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_Power_Revolution; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Marcos]
- Thailand in the Cold War: the 14 October 1973 uprising (9–15 October; 77 killed) drove out the dictator Thanom Kittikachorn; on 6 October 1976 police and right-wing paramilitaries attacked students at Thammasat University (official count 46 killed on both sides, unofficial reports over 100). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_Thai_popular_uprising; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/6_October_1976_massacre]
- Burma: Ne Win's coup of 2 March 1962 set up the Union Revolutionary Council and a socialist state; military rule in various guises lasted 1962–2010. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Myanmar]
- (Cross-reference) Bandung (18–24 April 1955) and the Non-Aligned Movement: see published Global Cold War chapter.

## ASEAN
- ASEAN was founded by the ASEAN (Bangkok) Declaration of 8 August 1967, signed by Adam Malik (Indonesia), Tun Abdul Razak (Malaysia), S. Rajaratnam (Singapore), Narciso Ramos (Philippines) and Thanat Khoman (Thailand). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASEAN_Declaration]
- First summit in Bali, 1976: Treaty of Amity and Cooperation. Members joined: Brunei 7 January 1984; Vietnam 28 July 1995; Laos and Myanmar 23 July 1997; Cambodia 30 April 1999 (delayed by the 1997 coup); Timor-Leste on 26 October 2025 at the 47th Summit as the 11th member. ASEAN works by consensus on the principle of non-interference. Southeast Asian Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone Treaty signed 15 December 1995. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASEAN]

## Developmental states and the 1997 crisis
- A 1993 World Bank report, *The East Asian Miracle*, credited export-oriented and market-friendly policies for the boom; Southeast Asia's fast-growing economies were dubbed "Tiger Cub Economies". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Asian_Tigers]
- The crisis began in Thailand on 2 July 1997 (in Thai, the "Tom Yum Kung crisis") when the government had to float the baht after speculative attacks (14–15 May 1997). Mahathir Mohamad blamed George Soros and currency speculators and imposed strict capital controls; Indonesia lost about 13% of GDP in 1998. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Asian_financial_crisis]
- Mahathir was prime minister of Malaysia 1981–2003 and 2018–2020. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahathir_Mohamad]
- Suharto resigned on 21 May 1998 after protests (including the Trisakti shootings) and riots; B. J. Habibie succeeded him and *Reformasi* began. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Suharto]

## Democratisation and its reversals
- Thailand: the 19 September 2006 coup (Sonthi Boonyaratglin) ousted Thaksin Shinawatra; the 22 May 2014 coup by General Prayut Chan-o-cha against Yingluck Shinawatra's caretaker government was "the twelfth since the country's first coup in 1932". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Thai_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Thai_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat]
- Myanmar: the 8888 Uprising began with students in Rangoon on 8 August 1988 and was crushed in a coup on 18 September 1988 (SLORC); deaths 350 officially, 3,000–10,000 by estimates. The junta changed the English name to Myanmar in 1989. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8888_Uprising; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Myanmar]
- Aung San Suu Kyi (born 19 June 1945): NLD won 81% of seats in 1990 but the result was nullified; she spent almost 15 of the 21 years 1989–2010 under house arrest; Nobel Peace Prize 1991; NLD won 86% of seats in 2015; State Counsellor from 6 April 2016 (barred from the presidency because her late husband and children are foreign citizens). In 2019 she defended the military at the International Court of Justice. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aung_San_Suu_Kyi]
- 2021 coup: on 1 February 2021 the Tatmadaw deposed the NLD government after its November 2020 victory and handed power to Senior General Min Aung Hlaing; mass protests and a multi-sided civil war followed, ongoing as of 2026. Suu Kyi's sentences totalled 33 years, cut to 27, and in 2026 the government commuted her sentence to house arrest. The NLD was declared dissolved in 2023; junta-run elections were held in three phases from 28 December 2025 to 25 January 2026, with the military-backed USDP winning an overwhelming majority, widely described as a sham. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Myanmar_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aung_San_Suu_Kyi; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025%E2%80%9326_Myanmar_general_election]
- Rohingya: denied citizenship since the 1982 nationality law. After Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (ARSA) attacks on police posts on 25 August 2017, the military's campaign killed at least 6,700 Rohingya in the first month (to 24 September 2017); over 700,000 fled to Bangladesh by September 2018; total death estimates run to about 24,000–30,000. The UN called it "a textbook example of ethnic cleansing". ARSA also killed Hindus (up to 99, per Amnesty International). The Gambia, backed by the 57-member OIC, filed a genocide case at the ICJ on 11 November 2019; on 23 January 2020 the court ordered Myanmar to prevent genocidal acts and preserve evidence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rohingya_genocide]

## Timor-Leste
- After Portugal's 1974 Carnation Revolution and a short civil war, Fretilin declared independence on 28 November 1975; Indonesia invaded on 7 December 1975. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_occupation_of_East_Timor; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timor-Leste]
- The Commission for Reception, Truth and Reconciliation (CAVR) estimated deaths from famine and violence during the occupation at 90,800–202,600, including 17,600–19,600 violent deaths or disappearances, out of a 1999 population of about 823,000. The 1991 Santa Cruz massacre caused worldwide outrage; Bishop Carlos Ximenes Belo and José Ramos-Horta shared the 1996 Nobel Peace Prize. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_occupation_of_East_Timor]
- In the UN-run referendum of 30 August 1999, 344,580 voted to reject autonomy within Indonesia and 94,388 to accept (78.5% for independence); militia violence followed (about 1,000 reported murdered in Oecusse alone); INTERFET, mainly Australian under Peter Cosgrove, was authorised on 15 September 1999. Independence was recognised on 20 May 2002; Timor-Leste joined ASEAN on 26 October 2025. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_East_Timorese_independence_referendum; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASEAN]

## Debates
- **Autonomous history (Smail).** John R. W. Smail, "On the Possibility of an Autonomous History of Modern Southeast Asia," *Journal of Southeast Asian History* 2, no. 2 (July 1961): 72–102. Building on J. C. van Leur, he criticised the Eurocentric "angle of vision", argued for writing from internal, local dynamics rather than foreign relations, and held that colonial rule should be one part of the region's history rather than its definition. It is treated as foundational for Southeast Asian studies. [source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-southeast-asian-history/article/on-the-possibility-of-an-autonomous-history-of-modern-southeast-asia/1ADF8C54F109273EAD46089E04063865; https://seasia.wisc.edu/publications/autonomous-histories-particular-truths/ (via WebSearch)]
- **Anderson and nationalism.** Benedict Anderson (1936–2015), a specialist on Indonesia, argued that nations are "imagined communities" made possible by print capitalism; the Chinese edition's translator says the 1978–79 wars among China, Vietnam and Cambodia were the immediate impetus. Partha Chatterjee objected that Anderson left colonised peoples only "modular" forms borrowed from Europe: "Even our imaginations must remain, forever, colonized." Anderson later wrote on Rizal and global anarchism in *Under Three Flags* (2005). Thongchai Winichakul (*Siam Mapped*, 1994) and Reynaldo Ileto (*Pasyon and Revolution*, 1979) show Southeast Asian scholars rewriting national histories from within: the map as maker of the Thai "geo-body", and the Passion narrative as the language through which Filipino peasants understood revolution. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imagined_Communities; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedict_Anderson; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Rizal; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reynaldo_Ileto]
- **James C. Scott (1936–2024).** *The Moral Economy of the Peasant: Rebellion and Subsistence in Southeast Asia* (1976): peasants put subsistence security first and rebel when colonial states and markets break elites' moral obligations; his examples are Burma's Saya San rebellion and Vietnam's Nghệ-Tĩnh soviets (1930s). *The Art of Not Being Governed: An Anarchist History of Upland Southeast Asia* (2009): the hill peoples of "Zomia" (a term coined by Willem van Schendel in 2002; lands above about 300 m from Vietnam's Central Highlands to northeast India, about 100 million people, e.g. Akha, Hmong, Karen, Lahu, Mien, Wa) deliberately practised "state avoidance"; Scott himself calls these "bold claims". He also worked on Malaysian agrarian politics (1968–85). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Moral_Economy_of_the_Peasant; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Art_of_Not_Being_Governed; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_C._Scott]
- **Reid's "Age of Commerce".** Anthony Reid (1939–2025, New Zealand-born; taught at the University of Malaya 1965–70, ANU, and founding director of NUS's Asia Research Institute), *Southeast Asia in the Age of Commerce, 1450–1680* (vol. 1 *The Lands Below the Winds*, 1988; vol. 2 *Expansion and Crisis*, 1993). Following Braudel, he treated maritime Southeast Asia as a coherent region; a trade boom fed by New World and Japanese silver and spice demand gave way to a seventeenth-century crisis partly caused by Dutch monopolies. Victor Lieberman countered that the pattern fits island, not mainland, Southeast Asia; others said it downplayed eighteenth-century dynamism. The debate matters for this chapter because it frames colonial rule as following, not preceding, a dynamic indigenous commercial age. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia_in_the_Age_of_Commerce; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Reid_(academic)]
- **"Asian values".** Lee Kuan Yew, in "Culture Is Destiny: A Conversation with Lee Kuan Yew" (Fareed Zakaria, *Foreign Affairs*, March/April 1994), and Mahathir argued that communitarian values suited Asia better than Western liberal democracy; the 1993 Bangkok Declaration stressed sovereignty and non-interference. Kim Dae-jung replied in "Is Culture Destiny? The Myth of Asia's Anti-Democratic Values" (*Foreign Affairs*, November/December 1994) that democratic values are universal; Amartya Sen's 1997 Morgenthau Lecture "Human Rights and Asian Values" found long Asian traditions of tolerance and dissent (including Confucius) and denied any general West/non-West divide. Mark R. Thompson (2001) argued the idea's popularity waned after the 1997 crisis. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_values; https://seasia.yale.edu/node/1147; https://carnegiecouncil.org/publications/archive/morgenthau/254 (via WebSearch)]
- **Japanese occupation: liberation or exploitation?** Japan trained the armies (PETA, the BIA) that led independence movements, yet Supriyadi's PETA and Aung San's army turned on Japan in 1945 (see published Japan's Empire chapter for its framing). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_occupation_of_the_Dutch_East_Indies; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aung_San]
- **Was 1965–66 genocide, and who was responsible?** Usually termed politicide, occasionally genocide (Cribb); Robinson argues it was an army-led campaign with US, British and allied help (lists of PKI cadres); the CIA denied active involvement. A 2016 people's tribunal in The Hague found crimes against humanity and Western complicity. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_mass_killings_of_1965%E2%80%9366]
- **Khmer Rouge as genocide.** Michael Vickery and Stephen Heder argued the killings were not racially motivated genocide; the ECCC in 2018 convicted for genocide against the Cham and Vietnamese. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambodian_genocide]
