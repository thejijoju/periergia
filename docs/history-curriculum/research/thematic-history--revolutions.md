## The word "revolution"
- Latin *revolutio*, "a turn around". The French noun *revolucion* is attested from the 13th century and English "revolution" from the late 14th century, at first only for the revolving motion of heavenly bodies; the sense of abrupt change in the social order is first recorded in the mid-15th century but became common only in the 17th century (OED, as cited by Wikipedia). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution]
- Copernicus's *De revolutionibus orbium coelestium* ("On the revolutions of the heavenly spheres") was first printed in 1543 at Nuremberg. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_revolutionibus_orbium_coelestium]
- The "Glorious Revolution" of 1688: its conservative reading fits "the original meaning of 'revolution' as a circular process under which an old system of values is restored to its original position", England's supposed "ancient constitution" being reasserted rather than formed anew (Mitchell 2009, cited by Wikipedia). Engels judged it "a relatively puny event". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glorious_Revolution]
- Hannah Arendt (*On Revolution*, 1963) argued that the word long kept its astronomical sense of a cyclical return; in an Arendt essay published by Literary Hub, the word's first political use is placed in 1660, for the restoration of the monarchy after the Rump Parliament, i.e. it originally meant restoration. A Cambridge review instead summarises Arendt as dating the "bringing things back" usage to 1688. Safe wording: "In seventeenth-century England 'revolution' still meant a turning back, a restoration — in 1660 and again in 1688." [source: WebSearch, https://lithub.com/never-before-published-hannah-arendt-on-what-freedom-and-revolution-really-mean ; https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/product/EDCE633ACDB90E7750152CC5A5C8B7B7]
- The modern sense is traditionally illustrated by the exchange on the night of 14–15 July 1789, when the duc de La Rochefoucauld-Liancourt brought Louis XVI news of the Bastille: "C'est une révolte?" — "Non, Sire, c'est une révolution." Wording varies between sources (e.g. "C'est donc une révolte" / "Sire, c'est une révolution") and its authenticity is not established; present it as a famous anecdote, not a transcript. [source: WebSearch, https://www.herodote.net/Louis_XVI-citations-TG91aXMgWFZJ.php ; https://www.dicocitations.com/citations/citation-13119.php]
- Common definitions today: "a rapid, fundamental transformation of a society's class, state, ethnic, or religious structures" (after Skocpol). Jack Goldstone: revolution is "an effort to transform the political institutions and the justifications for political authority in society, accompanied by formal or informal mass mobilization and noninstitutionalized actions that undermine authorities" — broad enough to include the relatively peaceful 1989 revolutions, but excluding coups, revolts, civil wars and rebellions that do not try to transform institutions, and excluding peaceful transitions by election (Spain after Franco, Argentina and Chile after their juntas). Source: Goldstone, "Toward a Fourth Generation of Revolutionary Theory", *Annual Review of Political Science* 4 (2001), 139–187. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution]
- Charles Tilly distinguished the coup d'état, civil war, revolt and "great revolution" (one transforming economic and social structures as well as political institutions: France 1789, Russia 1917, Iran 1979) (*European Revolutions, 1492–1992*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution]
- Mark Katz's typology (1997): rural revolution, urban revolution, coup d'état, revolution from above, revolution from without (e.g. the Allied invasions of Italy and Germany), revolution by osmosis; not mutually exclusive (Russia 1917 began as an urban revolution, then rural revolution, then the Bolshevik coup). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution]
- A "revolutionary wave" is a series of revolutions in different places within a short time; the 1989 revolutions are sometimes called the "Autumn of Nations" in reference to 1848's "Spring of Nations". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutionary_wave ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1989]

## The Atlantic age of revolutions (compare, don't retell)
- The "Age of Revolution" label was popularised by Eric Hobsbawm's *The Age of Revolution: Europe 1789–1848* (1962), first of his trilogy on the "long 19th century" (*The Age of Capital: 1848–1875*, *The Age of Empire: 1875–1914*); *The Age of Extremes* covers the "short twentieth century". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Age_of_Revolution:_Europe_1789%E2%80%931848 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Hobsbawm]
- Hobsbawm's "dual revolution" (also "twin revolution"): the political revolution in France and the Industrial Revolution in Britain together as the midwives of modern European and, through colonialism and imperialism, world history, 1789–1848. Hobsbawm (9 June 1917, Alexandria – 1 October 2012) was a lifelong Marxist. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Age_of_Revolution:_Europe_1789%E2%80%931848 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Hobsbawm]
- Hobsbawm on France's legacy (quotable, from *The Age of Revolution*): "France made its revolutions and gave them their ideas, to the point where a tricolour of some kind became the emblem of virtually every emerging nation, and European (or indeed world) politics between 1789 and 1917 were largely the struggle for and against the principles of 1789, or the even more incendiary ones of 1793." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Revolution]
- R. R. Palmer (11 January 1909 – 11 June 2002), *The Age of the Democratic Revolution: A Political History of Europe and America, 1760–1800*: vol. 1 *The Challenge* (1959, Bancroft Prize), vol. 2 *The Struggle* (1964); one-volume edition with an introduction by David Armitage, 2014. It traced the collision of democratic and egalitarian ideas with growing aristocratic power, and foreshadowed comparative "Atlantic history". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Roswell_Palmer]
- Palmer and the French historian Jacques Godechot presented the "Atlantic revolution" thesis jointly at the Tenth International Congress of Historical Sciences (1955); the debate opened in the Cold War, and the thesis was received with hostility by much of French historiography. (Detail of Soviet/NATO-era criticism not verified; keep to "Cold War context".) Godechot: *France and the Atlantic Revolution of the Eighteenth Century, 1770–1799* (English 1965). [source: WebSearch, https://journals.openedition.org/ahrf/12988?lang=en ; https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/itinerario/article/idea-of-atlantic-history/A327C7756232BD4F0EC8E47BF70C352D ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Revolution]
- The Atlantic wave included the United States (1775–1783), the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth (1788–1792), France and French-controlled Europe (1789–1814), Haiti (1791–1804), Ireland (1798) and Spanish America (1810–1825), plus the Dutch Patriot movement (from 1781, suppressed 1787; Batavian Republic 1795) and smaller upheavals in Switzerland (Geneva), Russia and Brazil; the revolutionaries knew of and partly emulated one another. Precursors in Spanish America include the rebellion of Túpac Amaru II. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Revolution]
- Globalising the frame: David Armitage and Sanjay Subrahmanyam (eds), *The Age of Revolutions in Global Context, c. 1760–1840* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2010), with chapters on the Caribbean (Geggus), Bonaparte's army in Egypt (Juan Cole), Java (Peter Carey), Qing China (Kenneth Pomeranz), and an afterword by C. A. Bayly. [source: WebSearch, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-global-history/article/age-of-revolutions-in-global-context-c-17601840-edited-by-david-armitage-and-sanjay-subrahmanyam-basingstoke-palgrave-macmillan-2010-pp-xi-301-hardback-5000-isbn-9780230580466-paperback-1699-isbn-9780230580473/7E8B4E11D5D8D977CD73C38F37BC528B ; https://library.bracu.ac.bd/vufind/Record/14006/TOC]
- Other recent syntheses listed by Wikipedia: Janet Polasky, *Revolutions without Borders* (Yale, 2015); David A. Bell, "The Atlantic Revolutions", in David Motadel (ed.), *Revolutionary World: Global Upheaval in the Modern Age* (CUP, 2021); Nathan Perl-Rosenthal, *The Age of Revolutions: And the Generations Who Made It* (Basic Books, 2024); Dan Edelstein, *The Revolution to Come: A History of an Idea from Thucydides to Lenin* (Princeton, 2025). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution]
- Edmund Burke supported the American colonists ("On American Taxation", 1774) but deplored the French Revolution in *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790), seeing property, customary and religious rights removed by revolutionaries rather than by the crown. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Revolution]

### Haiti: the most radical Atlantic revolution
- The Haitian Revolution (slave revolt from 21 August 1791; independence 1 January 1804) is one of the only known slave rebellions to found a state both free of slavery (though not of forced labour) and ruled by former captives; Wikipedia calls it the largest slave uprising since Spartacus. France recognised Haiti only in 1825, against an indemnity of 150 million francs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- Casualty figures in Wikipedia's infobox (estimates, attribute them): about 200,000 Afro-Haitian dead; French 57,000–75,000 dead (37,000 killed in action); white colonists 25,000; British 100,000 dead or wounded. Treat as rough; KNOWN_ISSUES already flags disputes over French losses. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- C. L. R. James, *The Black Jacobins: Toussaint L'Ouverture and the San Domingo Revolution* (London: Secker & Warburg, 1938); James, a Trinidadian living in England since 1932, wrote it amid "the booming of Franco's heavy artillery, the rattle of Stalin's firing squads". The 1963 Vintage edition added the appendix "From Toussaint L'Ouverture to Fidel Castro" — an explicit comparative bridge from Haiti to Cuba. James argued the ideals of liberty and equality "meant far more to them than to any Frenchman". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Black_Jacobins]
- Edward Said on the book: "events in France and in Haiti criss-cross and answer each other like voices in a fugue." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Black_Jacobins]
- Michel-Rolph Trouillot (Haitian-American anthropologist, 26 November 1949 – 5 July 2012), *Silencing the Past: Power and the Production of History* (1995), with a chapter "An Unthinkable History": the Haitian Revolution was "silenced" in Western memory compared with the French. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silencing_the_Past ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel-Rolph_Trouillot ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Revolution]
- Brinton, by contrast, wrote that "'Untouchables' very rarely revolt" and that successful slave revolutions like Haiti's are few — Haiti sat outside his four-case model. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution]

### Spanish American independence: Bolívar and San Martín
- The wars began after Napoleon's invasion of Spain (1808): with the throne vacant, sovereignty "reverted" to juntas and town councils (*cabildos*); from 1810 attempts at reconstruction turned into wars. Military campaigns ran from Chacaltaya (1809, Bolivia) to Tampico (1829, Mexico). They were "mixed wars" — civil wars, wars between states and between empires. Indigenous allies fought on both sides. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_American_wars_of_independence]
- Results: the new republics abolished the Inquisition and noble titles, but slavery was not immediately abolished — total abolition came only in the 1850s in most countries; Criollos stayed at the top of the social order. Infobox death estimate about 1,000,000 (600,000 in New Spain, 400,000 in New Granada) — a rough Wikipedia figure; attribute or omit. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_American_wars_of_independence]
- Simón Bolívar (24 July 1783, Caracas – 17 December 1830), a wealthy criollo: Decree of War to the Death (1813, Admirable Campaign); exile in Jamaica (Jamaica Letter, September 1815); met Haiti's president Alexandre Pétion on 2 January 1816 and received military support after pledging to free slaves; Boyacá (7 August 1819); independence of New Granada 1819, Venezuela and Panama 1821, Ecuador 1822, Peru 1824, Bolivia 1825; president of Gran Colombia; died of tuberculosis, disillusioned with the republics. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sim%C3%B3n_Bol%C3%ADvar]
- José de San Martín (25 February 1778, Yapeyú – 17 August 1850, Boulogne-sur-Mer): served in the Peninsular War, returned to Buenos Aires 1812; organised the Army of the Andes in Cuyo, crossed the Andes and won Chacabuco and Maipú (1818), freeing Chile; appointed Protector of Peru on 12 July 1821, independence declared 28 July 1821; met Bolívar at Guayaquil on 26 July 1822, then left Peru, resigned his command and moved to France in 1824 — the content of the Guayaquil meeting is still debated. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_de_San_Mart%C3%ADn]
- Sucre's victory at Ayacucho, 9 December 1824, ended royalist resistance in Peru (Callao held out). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sim%C3%B3n_Bol%C3%ADvar]

## 1848: the springtime of peoples and its failure
- The revolutions of 1848 ("springtime of the peoples", from German *Völkerfrühling*) ran from January 1848 to October 1849 and remain "the most widespread revolutionary wave in European history" (Sperber). They began in Sicily in January and spread after the February revolution in France; over fifty countries were affected, with no significant coordination among revolutionaries. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848]
- Causes: industrialisation and population growth, repression after 1815, liberalism, radicalism and nationalism, and an acute crisis in 1845–47 (potato blight from 1845, then an industrial recession in 1847). Economic historians Helge Berger and Mark Spoerer argue "it was economic misery and the fear thereof that triggered them". In France before 1848 only about 241,000 of 26 million people (0.5%) could vote. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848]
- Social conflict: Jonathan Sperber finds land rights the most common rural conflict; convictions for wood theft in the Bavarian Palatinate rose from 37,500 (1821–22) to 185,000 (1846–47). Lyon's silk weavers' slogan (1831, 1834): "Live working or die fighting!" [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848]
- The coalitions of workers and middle-class reformers did not hold. In France the national workshops were closed on 21 June 1848, provoking the June Days, crushed by General Cavaignac with dictatorial powers; Louis Napoléon Bonaparte was elected president on 10 December 1848 with 75% of the vote. Tocqueville, an elected member of the 1848 Constituent Assembly, sided with the Party of Order and supported the repression. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexis_de_Tocqueville]
- Counter-revolution: by the end of 1848 conservative governments were back (Prussian royal coup; Ferdinand I abdicated for Franz Joseph); Rome's republic fell in July 1849 to French intervention, Venice surrendered in August, Hungary surrendered to Austria and Russia on 13 August 1849. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848]
- Lasting changes despite defeat: abolition of serfdom in Austria and Hungary, the end of absolute monarchy in Denmark, representative democracy in the Netherlands; France kept universal male suffrage. Priscilla Robertson thought many goals were achieved by the 1870s, "largely by the enemies of the revolutions". Christopher Clark sees a "revolution in government" afterwards: states learned to manage the press and public sphere. Habsburg arrests rose from 70,000 in 1850 to one million by 1854. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848]
- 1848 beyond Europe: in New Granada (Colombia) José Hilario López took power in 1849 and abolished slavery and the death penalty; Brazil's Praieira revolt in Pernambuco (November 1848–1852); in Wallachia (from June 1848) revolutionaries sought abolition of serfdom and of boyar privilege; in September 1848 Russia, by agreement with the Ottomans, invaded and put the revolution down. About 4,000 exiles ("Forty-Eighters") went to the United States, largely anti-slavery. Rürup sees 1848 as a turning point in modern antisemitism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848]
- Marx's 1850 "Address of the Central Committee to the Communist League" set out a theory of "permanent revolution". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848]
- Proudhon's verdict (quote box on Wikipedia): "We have been beaten and humiliated ... scattered, imprisoned, disarmed, and gagged. The fate of European democracy has slipped from our hands." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848]
- Recent book: Christopher Clark, *Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World 1848–1849* (2023; US title *Revolutionary Spring: Europe Aflame and the Fight for a New World, 1848–1849*, Crown). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutionary_Spring ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848]

## Twentieth-century revolutions (compare; details are in published chapters)
- Russia 1917: Nicholas II abdicated 15 March (2 March Old Style) 1917; a provisional government and soviets formed "dual power"; the Bolshevik insurrection in Petrograd began 7 November (25 October OS). Slogan "peace, land, and bread". Followed by the Cheka, Red Terror, civil war, Kronstadt rising (1921). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution]
- China: the Chinese Communist Revolution is usually dated 1927–1949, ending with the proclamation of the People's Republic on 1 October 1949; Mao rose during the Long March and built a peasant-based strategy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Revolution]
- Mao's Hunan Report (March 1927): "Revolution is not a dinner party ..." and "A revolution is an insurrection, an act of violence by which one class overthrows another." English translations of the middle of the passage differ (Wikipedia: "nor an essay, nor a painting, nor a piece of embroidery"; the Selected Works version: "or writing an essay, or painting a picture, or doing embroidery"); quote only the opening clause and the "insurrection" sentence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Report_on_an_Investigation_of_the_Peasant_Movement_in_Hunan ; WebSearch, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution_is_not_a_dinner_party]
- Cuba: Moncada attack 26 July 1953 (Castro's "History Will Absolve Me" trial speech); *Granma* landing 1956; Batista toppled at the end of December 1958 and fled (published chapter: 1 January 1959); the 26th of July Movement became the Communist Party of Cuba in October 1965. Hal Brands calls Cuba the ideological and strategic heart of "Latin America's Cold War". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Revolution]
- Iran: the Shah's top-down "White Revolution" (1963); Khomeini exiled 1964; protests from October 1977; Cinema Rex fire (19 August 1978, about 400 dead); Tasu'a and Ashura marches of 10–11 December 1978 drew 6–9 million — Kurzman: "even discounting for exaggeration, these figures may represent the largest protest event in history"; Shah left 16 January 1979; Khomeini returned 1 February; monarchy fell 11 February; 98% approved an Islamic republic in the March 1979 referendum. Revolutionary deaths 532–2,781 (Tucker's consensus range) vs Khomeini's claim of 60,000; Abrahamian: 8,000 opponents executed June 1981–June 1985. Women were mobilised in large numbers; the regime's rhetoric centred on women's place. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution]
- Iran broke the theorists' expectations: it came without defeat in war, financial crisis, peasant rebellion or a disaffected military, in a relatively prosperous country. Charles Kurzman, *The Unthinkable Revolution in Iran* (Harvard UP, 2004). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution]
- Mexico: armed conflicts from 20 November 1910 to 1 December 1920; around one million deaths, mostly non-combatants (Wikipedia); "the defining event of modern Mexican history". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Revolution]
- Ethiopia: began with an army mutiny at Negele Borana on 12 January 1974; the Derg formed 27 June 1974 (109 privates, NCOs and junior officers); Haile Selassie deposed by coup on 12 September 1974. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Revolution]
- Nicaragua: overthrow of the Somoza dictatorship 1978–79 by the Sandinistas (FSLN), then the US-backed Contra war (1981–1990); Sandinistas lost the 1990 election. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicaraguan_Revolution]
- Taiping (1850–1864) — rebellion or revolution? Leader Hong Xiuquan sought religious conversion, overthrow of the Qing and "a general transformation of the mechanisms of state"; death estimates 20–30 million (5–10% of population) up to 73–100 million (contested). Use as a test case for definitions (it failed, so it is a "rebellion" in Skocpol's terms despite revolutionary aims). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiping_Rebellion]
- Durability: Steven Levitsky and Lucan Way (*Revolution and Dictatorship*, 2022) find autocracies born of revolution (China, Cuba, Iran, Vietnam) last on average about three times as long as non-revolutionary ones and collapse at one-fifth the rate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution]

## Revolution from above
- Ellen Kay Trimberger, *Revolution from Above: Military Bureaucrats and Development in Japan, Turkey, Egypt, and Peru* (Transaction Books, 1978): mostly on the Meiji Restoration and the Turkish War of Independence, extended to Egypt 1952 and Peru 1968 (Velasco). Her criteria: an extralegal takeover led by high military and civil bureaucrats of the old regime; little or no mass participation; very little violence, execution, emigration or counter-revolution; pragmatic, step-by-step change with little radical ideology; and — unlike a coup — destruction of the economic and political base of the aristocracy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution_from_Above ; WebSearch, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/revolution-from-above-military-bureaucrats-and-development-in-japan-turkey-egypt-and-peru-by-trimbergerellen-kay-new-brunswick-nj-transaction-books-1978-pp-viii-196-1495/9529B6CAC0E7C0CD8959ADE45DE2099F]
- Meiji Restoration: on 3 January 1868 Emperor Meiji declared power restored to the imperial house; Boshin War followed; the new regime abolished the domains and eventually the samurai class's position. Japanese *Meiji Ishin* is also translated "Meiji Renewal" or "Meiji Revolution". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration]
- Barrington Moore called Japan's route a "revolution from above" that cemented a reactionary alliance of weak bourgeoisie and powerful landowners leading to fascism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Origins_of_Dictatorship_and_Democracy]
- Brinton himself concluded that "what is loosely called the Industrial Revolution", Atatürk's reforms, the Meiji Restoration and the MacArthur occupation changed societies more lastingly than his four political revolutions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution]

## Unarmed revolutions: 1989, people power, colour revolutions, Arab Spring
- Philippines, People Power (EDSA) Revolution, 22–25 February 1986: over two million civilians plus military and religious groups (Cardinal Jaime Sin) ended Marcos's 20-year dictatorship. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_Power_Revolution]
- 1989: roots in Poland's August 1980 strikes and Solidarity (peak 10 million members); Poland's elections of 4 June 1989 (same day as the Tiananmen crackdown); Baltic Way human chain (August 1989, 675 km, over a quarter of the Baltic population); Berlin Wall opened November 1989; Velvet Revolution in Czechoslovakia 17–28 November 1989. Romania was the only country where violence overthrew the communist regime (Ceaușescus executed on Christmas Day 1989). China's protests ended in the 4 June crackdown. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1989 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velvet_Revolution]
- Colour revolutions: Serbia's Bulldozer Revolution (5 October 2000), Georgia's Rose (2003), Ukraine's Orange (2004), Kyrgyzstan's Tulip (2005), Armenia's Velvet (2018); mostly triggered by elections widely seen as rigged; student movements such as Serbia's Otpor! (founded October 1998); outcomes included failure to reduce corruption. Russia, China and Iran accuse the West of orchestrating them. Pavel Baev's definition stresses they did not aim to reorganise society or redistribute property. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colour_revolution]
- Arab Spring: began with Mohamed Bouazizi's self-immolation in Sidi Bouzid on 17 December 2010 (he died 4 January 2011); Ben Ali fled 14 January 2011; Mubarak resigned 11 February 2011 after 18 days; Gaddafi overthrown 23 August and killed 20 October 2011; Saleh out in 2012. Slogan: "the people want the fall of the regime". About 61,000 deaths in total (international estimate). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Spring ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohamed_Bouazizi]
- Outcomes: civil wars in Syria, Libya and Yemen; Bahrain's uprising crushed with Saudi-led intervention; in Egypt the army under Abdel Fattah el-Sisi removed the elected President Morsi on 3 July 2013. Tunisia was the success story — the National Dialogue Quartet won the 2015 Nobel Peace Prize — until President Kais Saied's self-coup of 25 July 2021 and a 2022 constitution expanding presidential powers. Tawakkol Karman of Yemen shared the 2011 Nobel Peace Prize. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Spring ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_Egyptian_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunisian_revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Tunisian_self-coup]
- Erica Chenoweth (born 1980; Harvard Kennedy School) and Maria J. Stephan, *Why Civil Resistance Works: The Strategic Logic of Nonviolent Conflict* (Columbia University Press, 2011): 323 violent and nonviolent campaigns, 1900–2006; 53% of nonviolent campaigns succeeded vs 26% of violent ones; nonviolent campaigns drew on average four times as many participants; 20 of the 25 largest were nonviolent; nonviolence was followed more often by democracy. Won the APSA Woodrow Wilson Foundation Award (2012) and the Grawemeyer Award (2013). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3.5%25_rule ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erica_Chenoweth]
- The "3.5% rule" (Chenoweth, 2013, building on Mark Lichbach's 1995 *The Rebel's Dilemma*, which proposed 5%): in the NAVCO 1.1 data nearly every campaign with active participation of at least 3.5% of the population succeeded, and all those were nonviolent. Caveats: Chenoweth calls it a "rule of thumb", descriptive not prescriptive; some movements won with less; Bahrain and Brunei exceeded 3.5% and failed (Chenoweth: small monarchies with powerful outside backers); critics (Alexei Anisin) say NAVCO omits failed nonviolent uprisings and miscodes "unarmed" violence (stones, burning cars); others say the research applies to toppling autocracies, not to democracies (Kyle Matthews on Extinction Rebellion). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3.5%25_rule]
- Chenoweth's later revision: in "The Future of Nonviolent Resistance" (*Journal of Democracy* 31:3, July 2020, pp. 69–84) they found that civil resistance became more common in the 2010s but less effective, even before COVID-19, because of savvier state responses and movements relying on mass protest without prior planning, organisation, coalition-building and discipline; both nonviolent and armed resistance have lost efficacy since 2010. (No exact 2010s success percentage verified.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erica_Chenoweth ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3.5%25_rule ; WebSearch, https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/the-future-of-nonviolent-resistance-2/]

## How revolutions run: radicalization, terror, Thermidor, restoration
- Crane Brinton (2 February 1898 – 7 September 1968; Harvard; AHA president 1963), *The Anatomy of Revolution* (1938; revised 1952 and 1965), compared England (1640s), America (1775), France (1789) and Russia (1917) to find "uniformities"; he likened revolutions to the progress of a fever. Sequence: old regime → moderate regime → radical regime → Thermidor. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crane_Brinton ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution]
- Brinton's symptoms of the old regime: government deficits and tax complaints, a ruling class that loses self-confidence, intellectuals deserting the government; "three of our four revolutions started among people who objected to certain taxes". Revolutions "are born of hope" rather than misery; revolutionaries "feel restraint, cramp" rather than crushing oppression. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution]
- Radicalization: after a "honeymoon", power goes "from Right to Center to Left"; moderates face "dual sovereignty" from an illegal radical government (Jacobin network, soviets, New Model Army); radicals win because they are "better organized, better staffed, better obeyed". The radical phase is a reign of "Terror and Virtue", ruled by committees (Committee of Public Safety). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution]
- Thermidor as Brinton used it: "convalescence" from the "fever"; Russia's NEP of 1921 "can be called Russia's Thermidor"; England's is best dated to Cromwell's dissolution of the Rump. Marks: a "tyrant" (Cromwell, Bonaparte, Stalin), return of old habits, reaction against puritanism, "aggressive nationalism". Lasting results disappoint: "The blood of the martyrs seems hardly necessary to establish decimal coinage." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution]
- Brinton quoted Vergniaud: "the revolution, like Saturn, devours its children". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution]
- Brinton's influence and limits: his model shaped advice from Zbigniew Brzezinski to President Carter during the Iranian Revolution; he treated the US as stable and declared "the Great Russian Revolution is quite over, finished" (1965 edition) — wrong about the USSR's fate; the American case "does not quite follow this pattern". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution]
- The original Thermidor: from the fall of Robespierre on 9 Thermidor Year II (27 July 1794) to the inauguration of the Directory on 2 November 1795; end of the Terror, Jacobin Club closed, *sans-culottes* dispersed, a "White Terror" against the left. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermidorian_Reaction]
- Trotsky applied the term to Stalin's USSR in *The Revolution Betrayed* (1936): the "Soviet Thermidor", the triumph of a bureaucratic stratum. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Revolution_Betrayed]
- Restorations: the Stuart Restoration of 1660; the Bourbon Restoration after Napoleon's fall in 1814 and 1815, ended by the July Revolution of 1830. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glorious_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourbon_Restoration_in_France]

## The theorists: Tocqueville to Arendt to Skocpol and after
- Alexis de Tocqueville (29 July 1805 – 16 April 1859), *L'Ancien Régime et la Révolution* (1856): the "theory of continuity" — the Revolution, trying to break with the past, eventually reverted to a powerful central government. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Old_Regime_and_the_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexis_de_Tocqueville]
- The "Tocqueville paradox/effect": revolutions come after improvement, not immiseration (contrast Marx). In one English translation of *The Old Regime*: "The regime that a revolution destroys is almost always better than the one that immediately preceded it, and experience teaches that the most dangerous time for a bad government is usually when it begins to reform." Successors: Harlan Cleveland's "revolution of rising expectations" (c. 1950), James C. Davies's J-curve, Ted Robert Gurr's relative deprivation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tocqueville_effect]
- Marx: "Revolutions are the locomotives of history" — *The Class Struggles in France, 1848 to 1850* (articles written January–October 1850 for the *Neue Rheinische Zeitung*; title by Engels, booklet 1895). [source: WebSearch, https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1850/class-struggles-france/index.htm ; https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/les-luttes-de-classes-en-france/]
- Hannah Arendt, *On Revolution* (Viking, 1963): compares the American and French revolutions; prefers the American, which aimed at public freedom and a lasting constitution, whereas the French was swamped by the "social question" of mass poverty, which in her view unleashed violence; she admired council systems that arose spontaneously in revolutions and were suppressed by parties. Critics: Hobsbawm (1973) attacked her exclusion of non-Western revolutions such as 1911 China and her "old-fashioned philosophical idealism"; others note she sidelined slavery and inequality in America. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Revolution]
- Goldstone's "generations" of theory: first generation descriptive (Le Bon, Ellwood, Sorokin); second generation (1960s) psychological (Gurr), structural-functionalist (Chalmers Johnson, Smelser) and political (Tilly, Huntington); third generation structural (Skocpol, Moore, Jeffrey Paige); fourth generation (from the late 1980s, after Iran, Nicaragua, the Philippines and 1989) stresses agency, contingency, ideology, coalitions and links to social-movement research, and uses large-N statistics. Goldstone, "Theories of Revolutions: The Third Generation", *World Politics* 32 (1980); John Foran, "Theories of Revolution Revisited: Toward a Fourth Generation", *Sociological Theory* (1993). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution]
- Barrington Moore Jr., *Social Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy: Lord and Peasant in the Making of the Modern World* (1966): three routes to the modern world — liberal democratic (England, France, US: via "bourgeois revolution", incl. the Civil War as a revolution), fascist (Japan, Germany: "revolution from above"), communist (China, Russia: peasant revolution). Dictum: "No bourgeois, no democracy". He stressed the violence that preceded democracy. James Mahoney: Moore's hypotheses get "only limited and highly conditional support". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Origins_of_Dictatorship_and_Democracy]
- Theda Skocpol (born 4 May 1947, Detroit; Harvard), *States and Social Revolutions: A Comparative Analysis of France, Russia, and China* (Cambridge UP, 1979). Social revolutions are "rapid, basic transformations of society's state and class structures ... accompanied and in part carried through by class-based revolts from below"; they arise from "a conjunction of multiple conflicts involving state, elites and the lower classes": agrarian states under international military pressure break down, when landed elites resist resource extraction, and peasant revolts then sweep the countryside. Revolutions "come" rather than being "made"; she used England, Prussia and Japan as negative cases. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_and_Social_Revolutions ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theda_Skocpol]
- Critiques of Skocpol: neglect of agency and ideology (Pfaff, others); Barbara Geddes found the theory failed when nine Latin American cases were added (Mahoney and Goertz dispute her case selection); Lewis Coser predicted it would be "a landmark". Skocpol was denied tenure at Harvard in 1980; a review found the charge of sex discrimination justified, and in 1984 she became Harvard's first tenured woman sociologist. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_and_Social_Revolutions ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theda_Skocpol]
- Skocpol's rethinking for Iran: "Rentier State and Shi'a Islam in the Iranian Revolution", *Theory and Society* 11:3 (1982), 265–283 — the oil-rentier state's social isolation and Shi'a Islam's organisational and cultural resources allowed a revolution that was, to a degree, deliberately "made". (Paraphrase; her exact wording not verified.) [source: WebSearch, https://civilresistance.info/biblio-item/1982/rentier-state-and-shia-islam-iranian-revolution ; https://undsoc.org/2014/02/11/skocpol-on-the-1979-revolution-in-iran/]
- Charles Tilly (27 May 1929 – 29 April 2008), *From Mobilization to Revolution* (Addison-Wesley, 1978): a revolutionary situation "entails multiple sovereignty" — more than one bloc effectively controlling a significant part of the state apparatus (a concept drawing on Trotsky's "dual power"); a revolutionary outcome is a transfer of state power; situations can occur without outcomes. Also *European Revolutions, 1492–1992* (1993) and *Dynamics of Contention* (2001, with McAdam and Tarrow). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Tilly ; WebSearch, https://colorado.pressbooks.pub/revolution/chapter/charles-tillys-revolutionary-process-theory/]
- Jack A. Goldstone (born 30 September 1953; George Mason University), *Revolution and Rebellion in the Early Modern World: Population Change and State Breakdown in England, France, Turkey, and China, 1600–1850* (University of California Press, 1991; 25th-anniversary edition, Routledge 2016): population growth on limited resources strained rigid institutions, producing state fiscal crisis, intra-elite conflict and mass-mobilisation potential — the same causes behind the English and French revolutions and the great Ottoman and Chinese rebellions (demographic-structural theory). Also *Revolutions: A Very Short Introduction* (2014). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Goldstone ; WebSearch, https://www.routledge.com/products/9781912282654 ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/revolution-and-rebellion-in-the-early-modern-world-by-jack-a-goldstone-berkeley-university-of-california-press-1991-608p-3495/C317A7F8261821F48924E1AF394C272B]
- George Lawson, *Anatomies of Revolution* (Cambridge UP, 2019): a "global historical sociology" treating revolutions as processes that are as international as they are national; argues theory has lagged behind empirical work since the 2011 uprisings. [source: WebSearch, https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/102731/ ; https://bellschool.anu.edu.au/ir/content-centre/research/anatomies-revolution]
- Mark R. Beissinger (born 28 November 1954), *The Revolutionary City: Urbanization and the Global Transformation of Rebellion* (Princeton UP, 2022): revolutions since 1900 have shifted from rural "social" revolutions to "urban civic" revolutions — large, short, broad-coalition, mostly nonviolent mobilisations in city centres, more often successful and less lethal; but the regimes they produce are less durable and, even when they widen political freedom, rarely deliver growth or reduce inequality. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Beissinger ; WebSearch, https://press.princeton.edu/node/45760 ; https://democracyparadox.com/2022/04/12/mark-beissinger-on-contemporary-urban-civic-revolutions/]

## The word's wider reach
- "Industrial revolution": first recorded in 1799 (French envoy Louis-Guillaume Otto); Jérôme-Adolphe Blanqui, *la révolution industrielle*, 1837; Engels in *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (written 1844, published 1845) spoke of "an industrial revolution, a revolution which...changed the whole of civil society"; popularised by Arnold Toynbee's lectures of 1881. Conventional span about 1760 to 1820–40. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution]
- "Neolithic revolution": term invented by V. Gordon Childe in *Man Makes Himself* (1936). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Revolution]
- "Scientific revolution": Alexandre Koyré used the term for a "mutation" of the intellect; popularised by Herbert Butterfield's *The Origins of Modern Science*, which said it "outshines everything since the rise of Christianity". Steven Shapin, *The Scientific Revolution* (University of Chicago Press, 1996), opens: "There was no such thing as the Scientific Revolution, and this is a book about it." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Revolution ; WebSearch, https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780226750200]
- Thomas S. Kuhn, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962), launched "paradigm" and "paradigm shift" into wide use; a 1969 postscript answered critics. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Structure_of_Scientific_Revolutions]
- "Cultural revolution": the Great Proletarian Cultural Revolution, launched by Mao in 1966 and lasting until his death in 1976 — revolution as permanent purge from above ("to rebel is justified"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_Revolution]

## Debates
- **What counts as a revolution?** Skocpol's narrow "social revolution" (only France, Russia, China) vs Goldstone's broad definition including peaceful 1989-type revolutions vs Tilly's situation/outcome split. Cases that test definitions: Taiping (failed "rebellion" with revolutionary aims), Meiji (restoration or revolution from above), colour revolutions (no redistribution). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_and_Social_Revolutions]
- **Made or come?** Structuralists (Skocpol 1979: revolutions "come") vs agency/ideology (fourth generation; Skocpol's own 1982 concession for Iran; Kurzman's "unthinkable" Iran). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution ; https://undsoc.org/2014/02/11/skocpol-on-the-1979-revolution-in-iran/]
- **Misery or hope?** Marx's immiseration vs Tocqueville's paradox, Brinton's "born of hope", Davies's J-curve and Gurr's relative deprivation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tocqueville_effect ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution]
- **Is there a natural course (Brinton's fever)?** Brinton's sequence fits England, France, Russia but not America; critics say his four Western cases cannot generalise and he misjudged the USSR's stability. Arendt's American-vs-French contrast is criticised (Hobsbawm) as Eurocentric and idealist. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Revolution]
- **Atlantic vs global vs "silenced" Haiti.** Palmer/Godechot's Atlantic democratic revolution (Cold War context; criticised for neglecting privilege and inequality) vs James and Trouillot putting Haiti at the centre vs Armitage and Subrahmanyam's global frame. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Roswell_Palmer ; https://journals.openedition.org/ahrf/12988?lang=en ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silencing_the_Past]
- **Did 1848 fail?** Traditional verdict of failure vs Robertson (goals achieved by the 1870s, by the revolutions' enemies) and Clark ("revolution in government"); Berger and Spoerer stress economic triggers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848]
- **Does nonviolence work?** Chenoweth and Stephan's 53% vs 26% and the 3.5% rule vs Anisin's data critique, Bahrain/Brunei counterexamples, declining success since 2010 (Chenoweth 2020), and Beissinger's finding that urban civic revolutions win more often but build weaker regimes. Meanwhile Levitsky and Way find violent revolutionary regimes unusually durable. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3.5%25_rule ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution]
- **Moore's routes**: Moore's bourgeois-democracy link challenged by Rueschemeyer, Stephens and Stephens (1992) and Valenzuela (Chile's landed elite supported democratisation). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Origins_of_Dictatorship_and_Democracy]
- **Industrial and scientific "revolutions"**: economic historians question whether slow British growth merits "revolution" (see published Industrial Revolution chapter); Shapin vs Butterfield on whether there was a Scientific Revolution. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Revolution]
