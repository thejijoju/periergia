## 1. The "unipolar moment" and American primacy
- Charles Krauthammer wrote several articles titled "The Unipolar Moment" in 1990 (a *Washington Post* column of 20 July 1990), and the essay "The Unipolar Moment" in *Foreign Affairs* vol. 70 no. 1 ("America and the World 1990/91"), pp. 23–33. It was adapted from his Henry M. Jackson Memorial Lecture, Washington, 18 September 1990. [source: KRA; WebSearch: https://www.historynewsnetwork.org/article/32924, https://researchcentre.army.gov.au/library/australian-army-journal-aaj/volume-5-number-1/primacy-and-unipolar-moment-debate-over-american-power-asymmetrical-world]
- Krauthammer argued the bipolar Cold War world would give way not to multipolarity but to a unipolar world with power residing in a "serenely dominant" US-led West, with a gap between first and second power greater than any in history. KRA (uncited sentence) adds that he expected the "moment" to last at most three or four decades; a secondary summary says he later spoke of twenty to thirty years — treat duration as approximate. In *The National Interest* (Winter 2002/03) he wrote that "the unipolar moment has become the unipolar era". [source: KRA; WebSearch as above]
- He treated the 1990–91 Gulf crisis as the event that revealed US unipolarity. [source: WebSearch summary of the essay, as above]
- **Gulf War** as the showcase of the new order: Iraq invaded Kuwait (2 August 1990); the UN Security Council condemned it unanimously; a coalition of 42 countries formed, "the broadest military alliance since World War II", largest contributors the US, Saudi Arabia, the UK and Egypt. UNSC ultimatum 29 November 1990 ("all necessary means") with a 15 January 1991 deadline; air campaign from 17 January 1991; ground offensive 24 February 1991, ceasefire within 100 hours. Iraq then crushed internal uprisings (to 5 April 1991). [source: GULF]
- **Somalia, 1993**: the UN intervened over the 1992 famine; on 3–4 October 1993 in Mogadishu ("Black Hawk Down") 18 American soldiers were killed and 73 wounded; Somali dead "most estimates between 133 and 700" (other estimates much higher). [source: SOM] Fear of "another Somalia" shaped US non-intervention in Rwanda (see §3). [source: RWA]
- Political scientists' definition: unipolarity = one state with a preponderance of power and no competitor; William Wohlforth: "a unipolar system is one in which a counterbalance is impossible." [source: UNI]
- Monteiro (2011–12): unipolarity is conflict-prone — "the United States has been at war for thirteen of the twenty-two years since the end of the Cold War". Kenneth Waltz (2000): unipolarity is "the least durable of international configurations". [source: UNI]
- Present-day status contested: Brooks and Wohlforth (*Foreign Affairs*, 2023) — the US "remains at the top of the global power hierarchy—safely above China and far, far above every other country"; Posen (2011) and Mearsheimer (2019) — shift to multipolarity; China has surpassed the US in GDP at purchasing-power parity. [source: UNI]
- Liberal-order institutions of the 1990s–2000s: the **WTO** was established 1 January 1995 (Marrakesh Agreement 1994), succeeding GATT (1948). [source: WTO] The **Rome Statute** of the International Criminal Court was adopted 17 July 1998 by 120 votes to 7 (against: China, Iraq, Israel, Libya, Qatar, the US, Yemen; 21 abstained); it entered into force 1 July 2002; 125 states parties as of October 2024; China, India, Russia and the US are not parties. The court "has also been accused of bias and of disproportionately targeting African leaders"; Burundi, the Gambia and South Africa threatened to withdraw (c. 2016). [source: ICC]
- **Responsibility to protect (R2P)**: first articulated by the International Commission on Intervention and State Sovereignty (report December 2001); unanimously endorsed at the UN 2005 World Summit (paragraphs 138–139), covering genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity; contested in application (Libya, Syria, etc.). [source: R2P]

## 2. Fukuyama versus Huntington
- Francis Fukuyama, "The End of History?", *The National Interest* no. 16 (Summer 1989); expanded into *The End of History and the Last Man* (Free Press, 1992). He drew on Hegel and Marx, and on Alexandre Kojève. [source: EOH; FUK]
- Fukuyama's claim was not that events would stop, but that liberal democracy is the final form of government with no superior alternative; critics read it as "American-style" triumphalism, which he denies — he has said the EU more closely reflects a "post-historical" world than the US. [source: EOH]
- Samuel P. Huntington (Fukuyama's former teacher) answered in a 1992 American Enterprise Institute lecture, then "The Clash of Civilizations?", *Foreign Affairs* vol. 72 no. 3 (Summer 1993), pp. 22–49, then *The Clash of Civilizations and the Remaking of World Order* (1996). [source: COC; WebSearch: https://grip.org/wp-content/uploads/2003/05/THE-CLASH-OF-CIVILIZATIONS_1993_Huntington.pdf]
- Thesis: after ideology, culture and religion would be the main source of conflict; he divided the world into civilizations (Western, Latin American, Orthodox, Islamic, Sinic, Hindu, Japanese, possibly African, Buddhist), named Sinic and Islamic as "challenger" civilizations, wrote that "Islam has bloody borders", called Turkey, Mexico, Russia and Australia "torn countries", and Ukraine a "cleft" country — he explicitly rejected the possibility of a Russia–Ukraine war. [source: COC]
- Huntington's earlier book *The Third Wave: Democratization in the Late Twentieth Century* (1991) described more than 60 countries in Europe, Latin America, Asia and Africa making democratic transitions since 1974 (starting with Portugal's Carnation Revolution). [source: WAV]
- The phrase "clash of civilizations" predates Huntington: Basil Mathews (1926), Albert Camus (1946), Girilal Jain (1988, on Ayodhya), Bernard Lewis ("The Roots of Muslim Rage", *Atlantic*, September 1990), Mahdi El Mandjra (1992). [source: COC]
- After 9/11 Fareed Zakaria called the events "the end of the end of history"; George Will wrote history had "returned from vacation". Fukuyama (WSJ, October 2001): "I believe that in the end I remain right." [source: EOH]
- Fukuyama's later revisions: 2014 WSJ column (25th anniversary) — less idealistic than "during the heady days of 1989", warned of "political decay" incl. in the US; after Brexit and Trump (2016): "twenty five years ago, I didn't have a sense or a theory about how democracies can go backward. And I think they clearly can." March 2022: his "ultimate nightmare" would be China backing Russia's war in Ukraine and Russia backing a Chinese invasion of Taiwan — "then that really is the end of the end of history." [source: EOH]
- Influence: the clash thesis has been cited as an influence on both Islamist strategies and the war on terror, on Trump's 2016 campaign, on Netanyahu, and on Orbán's rejection of multiculturalism. [source: COC]
- Counter-initiative from the Muslim world: Iranian president Mohammad Khatami promoted "Dialogue Among Civilizations"; the UN proclaimed 2001 the "United Nations Year of Dialogue among Civilizations". Spain's Zapatero proposed the Alliance of Civilizations at the UN in 2005, co-sponsored by Turkey's Erdoğan. [source: COC]

## 3. The dark 1990s: Yugoslavia (cross-refer) and Rwanda
- Yugoslavia: one paragraph only, citing the Collapse chapter's figures (see overlap note above).
- **Rwanda**: genocide 7 April – 19 July 1994, about 100 days, during the Rwandan Civil War; triggered by the shooting down of President Juvénal Habyarimana's plane on 6 April 1994. Scholarly estimates 500,000–662,000 Tutsi killed; ~10,000 Twa; 10,000–30,000 opposition and moderate Hutu; the Rwandan Constitution states over 1 million; 250,000–500,000 rapes. Victims were often killed by neighbours. [source: RWA]
- Background: RPF invasion from Uganda in 1990; Arusha Accords August 1993; the UN peacekeeping mission UNAMIR. Propaganda: the magazine *Kangura* (incl. the "Hutu Ten Commandments") and Radio Télévision Libre des Mille Collines (RTLM), which called Tutsi *inyenzi* ("cockroaches"). [source: RWA]
- 11 January 1994: UNAMIR commander Roméo Dallaire's "genocide fax" reported an informant ordered to register Tutsi in Kigali; his request to raid weapons caches was refused by UN peacekeeping headquarters. [source: RWA]
- Ten Belgian peacekeepers guarding Prime Minister Agathe Uwilingiyimana were killed (7 April); she was killed; Senegalese UNAMIR officer Mbaye Diagne rescued her children. UNAMIR became largely a bystander; Dallaire called it a "failure". [source: RWA]
- US policy shaped by Somalia; Clinton later: "I don't think we could have ended the violence, but I think we could have cut it down. And I regret it." France's Opération Turquoise (from 23 June 1994, ~2,500 troops) saved few lives, and genocidal authorities welcomed it. [source: RWA]
- The RPF ended the genocide by military victory; génocidaires and many Hutu refugees fled to Zaire, contributing to the First Congo War (1996). RPF reprisal killings: one researcher estimated ~30,000. [source: RWA]
- Justice: the UN created the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda (1994); Rwanda's *gacaca* community courts from 2001 amendments. [source: RWA]

## 4. The democratic wave and its reversal (data with dates)
- Freedom House electoral democracies: **69 of 167 countries (~41%) in the 1989–90 survey → 123 of 192 (~64%) in the survey covering 2005** (the December 2005 release reported 122, and called 64% the highest share in the survey's history); 117 by 2012. [source: WebSearch: https://www.freedomhouse.org/sites/default/files/Electoral%20Democracy%20Numbers%2C%20FIW%201989-2012.pdf; https://newswatch.in/news/2005/12/19/freedom-in-the-world-survey-global-gains-in-freedom; https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/10/Moller-24-4.pdf]
- *Freedom in the World 2026: The Growing Shadow of Autocracy* (March 2026, covering 2025): global freedom declined for the **20th consecutive year**; 54 countries declined, 35 improved; the US was the sharpest decliner among Free countries (−3 to 81/100); summaries say about 21% of the world's population lives in "Free" countries. (The 20-year run implies the decline began in 2006.) [source: WebSearch: https://freedomhouse.org/article/new-report-global-freedom-declined-20th-consecutive-year-2025; https://humanrightsdefenders.blog/2026/03/21/freedom-house-report-freedom-in-the-world-2026-the-growing-shadow-of-autocracy]
- V-Dem: the number of democracies fell modestly from 100 (2011) to 97 (2017); liberal democracies fell from 44 to 39 (2008–2018); V-Dem's 2019 report spoke of a "third wave of autocratization" affecting 24 countries incl. Brazil, Bangladesh and the US; V-Dem's Democracy Report 2026: nearly a quarter of countries were autocratizing in 2025. Since the Cold War, backsliding has come mostly through elected leaders dismantling institutions rather than coups. [source: DB]
- Welzel's counter-view: the third wave overshot popular demand for democracy; today's autocratization is regression to the mean that may reverse. [source: DB]

## 5. Europe: the EU deepens and widens; Brexit
- **Maastricht Treaty** (Treaty on European Union) signed 7 February 1992 by the twelve EC members; in force 1 November 1993. It created EU citizenship, set the path to a single currency, and common foreign and security policy. Denmark rejected it in its first referendum (2 June 1992, 50.7%–49.3%); France approved it with 50.8% (September 1992), the "*petit oui*". [source: MAA]
- **Euro**: name adopted 16 December 1995 (Madrid); launched as an accounting currency 1 January 1999; notes and coins from 1 January 2002. As the article stands (2026), the euro is the currency of 21 EU states, Bulgaria among them; 358 million people use it. [source: EUR]
- **2004 enlargement**: 1 May 2004, ten states (Cyprus, Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovakia, Slovenia) — the largest ever; Bulgaria and Romania 2007. (Already in Collapse chapter.) [source: EU04]
- **Brexit**: referendum 23 June 2016, Leave 51.9%; England and Wales (except London) voted Leave, Scotland, Northern Ireland and Gibraltar Remain; Cameron resigned; UK left at 23:00 GMT on 31 January 2020; the UK is the only member state ever to withdraw. [source: BRX]

## 6. NATO enlargement and Russia's response
- NATO now has 32 members. Post-Cold War accessions: Poland, Hungary, Czech Republic (1999); Bulgaria, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia (2004); Albania and Croatia (1 April 2009); Montenegro (5 June 2017); North Macedonia (27 March 2020, after the 2018 Prespa Agreement); Finland (April 2023); Sweden (March 2024) — the last two applied in May 2022 after Russia's invasion. [source: NATOE]
- At the April 2008 Bucharest summit NATO promised Georgia (and Ukraine) eventual membership; Putin called Ukrainian membership "a direct threat". In 2002 he had said Ukraine–NATO ties were a matter for Ukraine and NATO. [source: NATOE]
- George Kennan called enlargement "a strategic blunder of potentially epic proportions"; in a 1998 *New York Times* interview after the Senate ratified the first round he said "there was no reason for this whatsoever" and warned it would "inflame the nationalistic, anti-Western and militaristic" opinions in Russia. [source: KEN]
- Putin's Munich speech (10 February 2007) attacked US "monopolistic dominance": "One single center of power. One single center of force. One single center of decision-making. This is the world of one master, one sovereign." [source: PUT, quoting NYT 11 Feb 2007]
- Cross-refer to Collapse chapter (Sarotte) for the 1990 "not one inch" debate.

## 7. Russia under Putin (brief; wars in *Modern Conflicts*)
- Putin (b. 7 October 1952), ex-KGB; prime minister August 1999; acting president on Yeltsin's resignation; elected March 2000; re-elected 2004; prime minister 2008–12 under Medvedev; president again from 2012 (re-elected 2018, 2024). Economy grew ~7% a year in his first terms, helped by a fivefold rise in oil and gas prices. Second Chechen War restored federal control. [source: PUT]
- 2008 Russo-Georgian War (August 2008, over South Ossetia and Abkhazia). [source: GEO] Crimea: Russian special forces without insignia seized sites from 27 February 2014; annexed 18 March 2014 — the first annexation of part of one European country by another since 1945. [source: CRI]
- Constitutional amendments (signed April 2021) could keep him in office to 2036. Full-scale invasion of Ukraine 24 February 2022; ICC arrest warrant March 2023; WP describes the regime as an authoritarian dictatorship with a personality cult. [source: PUT; RUS]
- UN General Assembly resolution ES-11/1 (2 March 2022): 141 in favour, 5 against, 35 abstentions; many Global South states stayed neutral. As of August 2026 Russia occupied roughly 20% of Ukraine; ~8 million internally displaced and 6–7 million refugees from a population of 41 million. (Details belong to *Modern Conflicts*.) [source: RUS]

## 8. The rise of China and India; the Global South (brief: *China Today* and *Globalization* chapters)
- China: joined APEC 1991 and the WTO in 2001; extreme poverty reduced by 800 million (1978–2018); overtook Japan as second-largest economy by nominal GDP in 2010 and the US by PPP in 2016; growth ~9.5% a year 1978–2013. [source: CHN]
- **India's 1991 reforms**: a balance-of-payments crisis left reserves at about three weeks of imports (triggered by the Gulf War oil spike, lost Gulf remittances, collapse of Soviet trade). PM P. V. Narasimha Rao and finance minister Manmohan Singh dismantled most industrial licensing (the "Licence Raj", except 18 industries), cut tariffs, floated the rupee and opened sectors to foreign investment. Singh's budget speech, 24 July 1991: "Let the whole world hear it loud and clear. India is now wide awake." How far IMF/World Bank conditions drove the reforms is disputed. Growth reached 9.6% in 2006. [source: LIB]
- **Nuclear tests**: India's Pokhran-II (Operation Shakti), five tests on 11 and 13 May 1998 under PM Atal Bihari Vajpayee; Pakistan answered with Chagai-I (28 May) and Chagai-II (30 May 1998); UNSC Resolution 1172 (6 June 1998) condemned both; the US and Japan imposed sanctions. [source: POK]
- **BRICS**: "BRIC" coined by Jim O'Neill of Goldman Sachs in 2001; first summit 2009; South Africa joined 2010; Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran and UAE joined (first summit as members 2024); Indonesia early 2025; Saudi status unconfirmed; more than a quarter of the world economy and nearly half its population. [source: BRICS]
- Anti-globalization: the "Battle of Seattle" against the WTO ministerial meeting, 1999. [source: GLOB]

## 9. Latin America's "pink tide"
- A turn to left governments from the late 1990s: Hugo Chávez elected in Venezuela 1998; Lula in Brazil (2002); Evo Morales in Bolivia (2005); Rafael Correa in Ecuador (2006); Daniel Ortega's return in Nicaragua (2006). Roots: disappointment with 1990s neoliberal policy (privatisation, spending cuts), protest movements such as Argentina's *piqueteros* and Bolivia's Indigenous and *cocalero* movements. Two styles: Chávez's confrontational "socialism of the 21st century" vs Lula's accommodation with private capital. [source: PINK]
- Funded by the 2000s commodity boom (partly Chinese demand); inequality initially fell; as prices fell in the 2010s, a "conservative wave" followed; Nicaragua and Venezuela turned authoritarian. Scholars call the rise and fall "a byproduct of the commodity cycle". [source: PINK]
- Second pink tide: Mexico 2018, Argentina 2019, Bolivia 2020, Peru/Honduras/Chile 2021, Colombia and Brazil 2022; Sheinbaum (Mexico) 2024. By the mid-2020s many states turned right again. **2026 (flag as recent):** WP reports US strikes on Venezuela and the capture of President Nicolás Maduro in early January 2026, and Lula's defeat of Flavio Bolsonaro in the 2026 Brazilian election. [source: PINK; TRU]

## 10. Africa after the Cold War
- **African Union**: called for in the Sirte Declaration (9 September 1999); launched 9 July 2002 in Durban, replacing the OAU (founded 25 May 1963); 55 members; seat Addis Ababa; admitted to the G20 in 2023; Morocco rejoined 30 January 2017. [source: AU]
- Coups and suspensions: as of 2026, six members suspended after coups — Mali (2021), Sudan (2021), Burkina Faso (2022), Niger (2023), Madagascar (2025), Guinea-Bissau (2025); Guinea readmitted January 2026, Gabon April 2025. [source: AU]
- "Africa Rising": coined by *The Economist* in December 2011; Africa's economy grew 50% in 2005–15 vs 23% worldwide. Critics: only ~18 million "middle class" (3.3%); Ebola (2013–16) and conflict; Patrick Bond called it a neoliberal "chimera" coined just as the 2002–11 commodity-driven growth ended. [source: AR]

## 11. 9/11 and the "war on terror" (brief: wars in *Modern Conflicts*)
- 11 September 2001: 19 al-Qaeda hijackers seized four airliners; the North Tower hit 8:46 a.m., South Tower 9:03, the Pentagon 9:37; Flight 93 crashed near Shanksville, Pennsylvania, at 10:03 after a passenger revolt. 2,977 victims (plus 19 hijackers); 9,000+ later deaths from related illness. NATO invoked Article 5 for the only time. [source: SEP]
- Bush first used "war on terrorism" on 16 September 2001; the "axis of evil" (Iran, Iraq, North Korea) in the January 2002 State of the Union. [source: WOT; IRQ]
- Iraq 2003: justified by false claims about WMD and Saddam–al-Qaeda links; no stockpiles were found; Kofi Annan called the invasion illegal; the 2016 Chilcot Report found it unnecessary. Death estimates diverge widely: Iraq Family Health Survey 151,000 (2003–06), Lancet survey 654,965 (2003–06). [source: IRQ]
- Costs of War project (Brown University): 4.5–4.7 million direct and indirect deaths in post-9/11 war zones (2023 report; 3.6–3.8 million indirect); ~38 million displaced; over $8 trillion in US costs; 363,939–387,072 civilians killed directly (2021 report). [source: WOT]
- **2026 (flag):** WP reports that in 2026 the US and Israel launched a war on Iran. Do not elaborate. [source: WOT; TRU]

## 12. The 2008 financial crisis (brief: *Neoliberal Age*)
- Subprime mortgage crisis from early 2007; Bear Stearns sold March 2008; Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac seized 7 September 2008; Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy 15 September 2008 (largest in US history); $700 billion TARP (3 October 2008). In the US ~8.7 million jobs lost, unemployment 5% → 10% (October 2009); household wealth fell $11 trillion. It deepened the Great Recession and contributed to crises in Iceland and the eurozone. [source: GFC]
- Orbán (2014) framed the 2008 crisis as a paradigm shift comparable to the world wars and the Soviet collapse; V-Dem dates the "third wave of autocratization" from c. 2010 after the Great Recession. [source: ORB; DB]

## 13. The Arab Spring (brief)
- Mohamed Bouazizi set himself on fire on 17 December 2010 in Tunisia (died 4 January 2011). Ben Ali fled 14 January 2011; Mubarak resigned 11 February 2011 after 18 days of protest; Gaddafi overthrown (August 2011) and killed; Yemen's Saleh handed over power (2012). Slogan: *ash-shaʻb yurīd isqāṭ an-niẓām* ("the people want to bring down the regime"). Aftermath: civil wars in Syria, Libya and Yemen; the rise of ISIS; Egypt's 2013 coup against Morsi; Bahrain's uprising crushed with Saudi-led help. Assad fell in December 2024. [source: ARS]

## 14. Populism and illiberalism
- Cas Mudde, "The Populist Zeitgeist", *Government and Opposition* (2004): populism is a "thin-centred ideology" that considers society "ultimately separated into two homogeneous and antagonistic groups, 'the pure people' versus 'the corrupt elite'", and argues politics should express the *volonté générale* (general will). Mudde and Rovira Kaltwasser warn against dismissing populists as "irrational" or "immoral". [source: POP; WebSearch: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/government-and-opposition/article/populist-zeitgeist/2CD34F8B25C4FFF4F322316833DB94B7]
- Fareed Zakaria popularised "illiberal democracy" in *Foreign Affairs* (1997). Viktor Orbán, at Băile Tușnad (Tusnádfürdő), Romania, July 2014: "the new state we are constructing in Hungary is an illiberal state, a non-liberal state." Orbán lost the parliamentary election of 12 April 2026 in a landslide to Péter Magyar's Tisza party; his term ended 9 May 2026. [source: ILL; ORB]
- Narendra Modi: PM since 26 May 2014; first single-party majority since 1984; revoked Jammu and Kashmir's special status (2019); Citizenship Amendment Act (2019) and 2020 Delhi riots; BJP lost its majority in 2024 and governs in coalition; WP lists "democratic backsliding" under his tenure. [source: MOD]
- Jair Bolsonaro: president of Brazil 2019–23; lost to Lula in 2022; supporters stormed federal buildings 8 January 2023; barred from office until 2030; convicted by the Supreme Federal Court on 11 September 2025 over a coup plot, sentenced to 27 years 3 months. [source: BOL]
- Donald Trump: won 2016 (vs Clinton); refused to concede 2020, January 6, 2021 Capitol attack; impeached twice and acquitted; won 2024 (vs Harris); 47th president; second-term tariffs "at the highest level since the Great Depression"; researchers describe his actions as contributing to democratic backsliding. [source: TRU]

## 15. The COVID-19 pandemic
- Outbreak in Wuhan, December 2019. WHO declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern on **30 January 2020** and called it a pandemic on **11 March 2020**; the PHEIC ended **5 May 2023**. Vaccines rolled out from December 2020 (COVAX for equity). The largest global recession since the Great Depression. [source: COV]
- WHO (5 May 2022): **about 14.9 million excess deaths in 2020–21 (range 13.3–16.6 million)**, vs 5.42 million reported COVID deaths for those years; 84% in South-East Asia, Europe and the Americas; 81% in middle-income countries. (A December 2022 WHO update gives ≈14.8 million.) [source: WebSearch: https://www.who.int/news/item/05-05-2022-14.9-million-excess-deaths-were-associated-with-the-COVID-19-pandemic-in-2020-and-2021; COV]

## 16. Climate
- UNFCCC 1992; **Kyoto Protocol** adopted 11 December 1997, in force 16 February 2005; binding cuts for developed countries only ("common but differentiated responsibilities"); Canada withdrew effective December 2012. [source: KYO]
- **Paris Agreement**: negotiated December 2015 (COP21, Le Bourget); opened for signature 22 April 2016; in force 4 November 2016; aim "well below 2 °C", preferably 1.5 °C, measured as 20-year averages; developing countries also submit plans. US withdrawal: effective 4 November 2020; rejoined 2021; Trump signed a new withdrawal 20 January 2025, effective 27 January 2026. [source: PAR]
- IPCC SR15 (8 October 2018, Incheon): limiting warming to 1.5 °C requires CO₂ to fall about 45% from 2010 levels by 2030 and reach net zero around 2050. AR6: WGI 9 August 2021, WGII February 2022, WGIII April 2022, Synthesis March 2023. [source: SR15; AR6]
- **2024**: WMO consolidated six datasets: 1.55 °C ± 0.13 °C above 1850–1900, "likely… the first calendar year" above 1.5 °C; one or more years above 1.5 °C does not mean the Paris goal has been missed. Copernicus (C3S): about 1.6 °C, first calendar year above 1.5 °C; UK Met Office ~1.53 °C. The 2016–2025 decade averaged 1.26 °C (1.13–1.36). IPCC expects the 20-year average to pass 1.5 °C in the early 2030s. ## Debates
- **End of history vs clash of civilizations:** Fukuyama's defenders cite democratic peace theory and post-Cold War declines in wars and refugees; critics (Barber, *Jihad vs. McWorld*, 1992/1995) stress ethnic and religious loyalties; Derrida (*Specters of Marx*, 1993) called it "Christian eschatology"; Žižek: capitalism in China and Singapore shows capitalism and democracy can be uncoupled; Azar Gat (2007) and Robert Kagan (*The Return of History and the End of Dreams*, 2008): authoritarian great powers. Against Huntington: Edward Said, "The Clash of Ignorance" (2001); Amartya Sen (*Identity and Violence*; 1999) — people have plural identities and "diversity is a feature of most cultures"; Paul Berman (2003); Chomsky (2001) — a pretext for Cold War-style policy; Garton Ash — "crude to the point of parody"; Paul Musgrave — few IR specialists rely on it; the biggest post-1991 wars (Syria, Yemen, Russia–Ukraine) were *within* his civilizations. Some scholars also criticise both as Western-centric (e.g. alternatives rooted in Islamic political thought). [source: EOH; COC]
- **Is the unipolar moment over?** Wohlforth (1999) and Ikenberry: unipolarity stable; Waltz, Monteiro: unstable and war-prone; Finnemore (2009): unipolarity frustrates the unipole; Brooks & Wohlforth (2023): still unipolar; Posen, Mearsheimer: multipolarity; Acharya: a "multiplex" world, end of the "American world order" though not American decline per se (vs Ikenberry). [source: UNI; ACH]
- **NATO enlargement and Russia:** Kennan and realist critics vs. those who stress Central European states' own choices; Putin's narrative of broken promises (Munich 2007) — cross-refer to Sarotte in the Collapse chapter. [source: NATOE; KEN]
- **Rwanda:** whether the genocide was planned before 6 April (the ICTR could not prove a conspiracy; Bagosora acquitted of conspiracy in 2008 but convicted of genocide); RPF reprisal killings and the government's restriction of investigations; the death toll (see overlap note). [source: RWA]
- **India's reforms:** home-grown programme vs IMF/World Bank conditionality; how much poverty fall is due to reform; critics point to inequality, slow formal job growth, agrarian distress. [source: LIB]
- **Pink tide:** genuine democratic redistribution vs commodity-cycle populism; authoritarian drift in Venezuela and Nicaragua. [source: PINK]
- **Africa Rising vs Afro-pessimism:** see Bond above; also critique of ICC "bias" against Africa. [source: AR; ICC]
- **Populism:** ideational (Mudde) vs other approaches; whether populism is a corrective or a threat to democracy; "illiberal democracy" as a regime type vs a euphemism for hybrid authoritarianism. [source: POP; ILL]
- **Democratic recession:** Freedom House/V-Dem decline vs Welzel's regression-to-the-mean and expected rebound. [source: DB]
- **Iraq War legality** (Annan; Chilcot) and the war on terror's toll (Costs of War). [source: IRQ; WOT]
- **COVID origins:** zoonosis (consensus) vs lab-leak hypothesis. [source: COV]
