## "War every year": what the data can and cannot say
- The seed's claim that there was a war or other violent conflict somewhere in every year of the 19th and 20th centuries depends on definitions. Our World in Data (summarised in search results) says there has been no year without *armed conflict* for centuries; but under a stricter definition of "war" (especially deadly conflicts) there have been between 0 and 12 active wars per year, with "very few years without any". Counting only *interstate* wars, many years had none. Recommend phrasing: "almost every year, and every year if colonial, civil and frontier conflicts are counted" — and explain that it depends on definitions. [source: WebSearch summary of https://ourworldindata.org/war-and-peace-long-run and https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/11601/2/FullText.pdf — OWID itself could not be opened from this session]
- The Correlates of War (COW) project was started in 1963 at the University of Michigan by political scientist J. David Singer; its data start in 1816 and include lists of interstate and civil wars since 1816; it moved to Penn State in March 2001. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlates_of_War]
- COW counts a conflict as a war only when at least 1,000 deaths are recorded in one calendar year; the Uppsala Conflict Data Program (UCDP) counts an "armed conflict" from 25 battle-related deaths in a year where at least one party is a state government; UCDP/PRIO data run from 1946. UCDP also records "non-state conflict" and "one-sided violence" (organized violence against civilians causing at least 25 deaths in a year). This difference in thresholds is a good worked example of why counts of "wars" differ. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uppsala_Conflict_Data_Program]

## Casualty statistics and their uncertainty (general)
- Wikipedia's list of wars by death toll warns that tolls are "often uncertain" because of incomplete records, unreliable censuses, differing counting methods and destroyed evidence, and that figures include deaths from war-induced epidemics, famines and genocides. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_wars_by_death_toll]
- Worked example (Napoleonic Wars): "With the partial exception of the United Kingdom, all of the states at the time did not keep especially accurate records, so calculating losses is to a certain extent a matter of conjecture" (Clodfelter 2017). Military deaths are put at 2.5–3.5 million and civilian deaths at 750,000–3 million (this range is tagged "citation needed"); Charles Esdaile gives 5–7 million total deaths including civilians; the list of wars by death toll gives 4–7 million for the Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars together. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars_casualties ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_wars_by_death_toll]
- How a figure gets revised: in 1832 the head of France's conscription division estimated 1.7 million French soldiers dead in 1803–1815; Gaston Bodart (1916) found this an overestimate, and he and Meynier (1930) put French military dead at 800,000–1,000,000; Jacques Houdaille, sampling nominal rolls at 1:500 and civil registers, concluded 900,000–1 million French soldiers died 1800–1815, roughly two non-combat deaths for each combat death, and about half of them in 1812–1814. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars_casualties]
- In 19th-century wars disease usually killed more soldiers than combat. Crimean War: in every army "death by disease exceeded the sum of 'killed in action' or 'died of wounds'"; e.g. Britain 22,182 dead, of whom 4,602 combat deaths and 17,580 non-combat; France 95,615 dead (20,240 combat). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War]

## Napoleonic Wars and the Concert of Europe
- Napoleonic Wars dated 1803–1815 in the casualties article; about 3,000,000 military personnel served on the French side in total (Clodfelter). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars_casualties ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars]
- The wars introduced or spread new methods of warfare including mass conscription and guerrilla warfare. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars]
- Spain: 300,000+ military dead and more than 586,000 killed including civilians (Canales 2004); Portugal up to 250,000 dead or missing including civilians. Britain: 311,806 military dead or missing 1803–1815 (92,386 Royal Navy + 219,420 Army). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars_casualties]
- The Concert of Europe: a general agreement among the great powers to maintain the balance of power, boundaries and spheres of influence; the Congress (Vienna) System after the Congress of Vienna (1814–1815) was dominated by Austria, France, Prussia, Russia and the United Kingdom. Scholars dispute its duration: some say it fell apart in the 1820s, others that it lasted until 1914. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concert_of_Europe]
- The Concert did not stop the wars of Italian unification (completed 1861) and German unification (1871); in its second phase (1870s–1914) it "facilitated the growth of European colonial and imperial control in Africa and Asia without wars between the great powers" — i.e. great-power peace in Europe coexisted with colonial war elsewhere. It ended with the outbreak of the First World War in 1914. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concert_of_Europe]
- The Crimean War is described as "the first war between Great Powers since Napoleon". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concert_of_Europe]

## The century of "small wars" (colonial and frontier war)
- C. E. Callwell, a British Army officer, published *Small Wars: Their Principles and Practice* in 1896, revised 1899 and 1906; it was read by the IRA during the Irish War of Independence; reprinted by University of Nebraska Press 1996. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Edward_Callwell]
- Callwell defined small wars as "all campaigns other than those where both the opposing sides consist of regular troops" (cited to p. 21 of the 1996 reprint in a secondary source); the term had nothing to do with scale; one Australian Army analysis notes he largely meant campaigns against non-professional, non-Western armies, guerrillas or "bandits". [source: WebSearch results incl. https://researchcentre.army.gov.au/library/australian-army-journal-aaj/volume-7-number-3-summer/big-books-small-wars-milestones-or-millstones-counterinsurgency-thinking — wording seen only in a search summary; treat as a paraphrase unless checked]
- By 1914 universal male conscription on broadly Prussian lines was the norm in European armies; colonial powers such as France kept conscripts at home while using professional units overseas. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscription]
- Second Boer War (1899–1902): 45 tented camps for Boer internees and 64 camps for Black Africans; between 18,000 and 26,000 Boers (mostly women and children) died in the camps. (The British Empire chapter already gives these and the Black African deaths — cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Boer_War_concentration_camps]
- Women in African armies: the Agojie ("Dahomey Amazons"/Mino), an all-female Fon regiment of the Kingdom of Dahomey, existed from the 17th century until disbanded in 1894, with 1,000–6,000 at their peak; they fought the French in the Franco-Dahomean Wars from 1890 under King Béhanzin. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahomey_Amazons]
- Rani Lakshmi Bai of Jhansi made Jhansi a centre of the 1857 rebellion against Company rule. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Rebellion_of_1857]

## Latin America: independence and interstate wars
- The Spanish American wars of independence are dated 25 September 1808 – 29 September 1833 in Wikipedia's infobox; casualty figures are poorly known (infobox "Unknown" for the patriots). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_American_wars_of_independence]
- Paraguayan War (War of the Triple Alliance), 13 November 1864 – 1870, Paraguay against Argentina, Brazil and Uruguay: "the deadliest inter-state war in Latin American history" (Schenoni 2024). Francisco Solano López was killed at Cerro Corá on 1 March 1870. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraguayan_War]
- Paraguayan losses are "hotly disputed": 14 estimates of pre-war population range from 300,000 to 1,337,000; Vera Blinn Reber (1988) argued losses were "enormously exaggerated" (low estimate 7%); Whigham and Potthast (1999, 2002) estimated a 1864 population of 420,000–450,000 and 150,000–160,000 survivors, a loss of roughly 60–70%; claims of 90% losses rest on an 1857 census that modern scholarship shows was a government invention. An 1871 census counted 106,254 women, 28,746 men and 86,079 children. Pinker wrote that, if over 60% died, it was proportionally one of the most destructive wars in modern times "for any nation state". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraguayan_War]
- War of the Pacific, 14 February 1879 – 4 April 1884 (Chile against Bolivia and Peru). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Pacific]
- Mexican–American War: 25 April 1846 – 2 February 1848. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican%E2%80%93American_War]
- War and the state in Latin America: Miguel A. Centeno, *Blood and Debt: War and the Nation-State in Latin America* (2002), argued that the region's relatively weak states stemmed from the absence of large-scale "total" wars; governments financed limited wars through customs revenues and foreign loans rather than building strong fiscal bureaucracies (a counterpoint to Tilly). Luis Schenoni, *Bringing War Back In* (Cambridge, 2024), revisits this. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_A._Centeno]

## The Taiping war
- Taiping Rebellion, December 1850 – August 1864 (last loyalists defeated January 1866), between the Qing and the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom led by Hong Xiuquan. Wikipedia calls it "the deadliest civil war in history" and the second-deadliest conflict overall. Death-toll estimates range from 20–30 million (about 5–10% of China's population) to 73–100 million; the 20–30 million range is the usual conservative figure. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiping_Rebellion ; WebSearch on Platt, e.g. https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780307271730]
- It was defeated mainly by decentralised provincial armies such as Zeng Guofan's Xiang Army; the Western-officered "Ever Victorious Army" (3,500–5,000 in 1862) was later commanded by Charles George Gordon; Nanjing fell in July 1864. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiping_Rebellion]

## Rehearsals of industrial war: Crimea, unification wars, US Civil War
- Crimean War, 16 October 1853 – 30 March 1856 (Treaty of Paris). One of the first conflicts to use explosive naval shells, railways and telegraphs, and one of the first documented extensively in written reports and photographs. Allied (Ottoman, French, British, Sardinian) dead 165,363; Russian dead 450,015 (Clodfelter/Figes). Nearly 200,000 Crimean Tatars displaced. Florence Nightingale and the Jamaican nurse Mary Seacole cared for the wounded. Defeat pushed Russia toward reforms including the 1861 emancipation of the serfs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War]
- Second Italian War of Independence (Franco-Austrian War), 26 April – 12 July 1859: the French moved into Piedmont "in the first massive military use of railways"; the war ended after Solferino (24 June 1859). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Italian_War_of_Independence]
- Austro-Prussian War, 14 June – 22 July 1866 (Königgrätz/Sadowa): Prussia's denser railway network and the breech-loading Dreyse needle gun (faster-firing than Austria's muzzle-loading Lorenz rifles) were advantages; Prussian universal three-year conscription gave a large reserve; Moltke commanded. Austrian losses 106,796 (24,431 battle deaths, 19,134 disease deaths). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Prussian_War]
- Franco-Prussian War, 19 July 1870 – 28 January 1871: French Chassepot rifle outranged the Dreyse; Napoleon III captured at Sedan; German Empire proclaimed 18 January 1871; French losses 756,285 including 138,871 dead and 474,414 captured; German dead 44,781; about 450,000 French civilian excess deaths. It led to the Paris Commune. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco-Prussian_War]
- Michael Howard's *The Franco-Prussian War* (1961) examined how each army reflected its society. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Howard_(historian)]
- Industrial warfare brought mass-conscripted armies, railways, telegraph and wireless, rifled breech-loaders, and machine guns (Gatling's hand-cranked, then Maxim's fully automatic). Wikipedia's Industrial warfare article calls the American Civil War "the earliest modern example of total war" (contested, see Debates). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_warfare]
- Russo-Japanese War, 8 February 1904 – 5 September 1905; some scholars argue it set in motion a change in world order with Japan emerging as the main Asian power. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Japanese_War]

## Conscription and the nation in arms
- The *levée en masse* was decreed by the National Convention on 23 August 1793 (drafted by Barère with Lazare Carnot); unmarried able-bodied men aged 18–25 were requisitioned. The army reached about 1,500,000 in September 1794, though actual fighting strength probably peaked at no more than 800,000. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lev%C3%A9e_en_masse]
- The Jourdan Act of 5 September 1798 made conscription permanent; its first article: "Any Frenchman is a soldier and owes himself to the defense of the nation." More than 2.6 million men were inducted 1800–1813. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscription]
- Russia cut serfs' service from 25 years to 20 in 1834 and introduced universal male conscription in 1874, made possible by the abolition of serfdom in 1861. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscription]
- Japan's Conscription Ordinance of January 1873 (already covered in the published Meiji chapter, including the "blood tax" riots — cross-refer). [source: published chapter history--modern-era-19th-century--meiji-restoration]
- The *levée en masse* is often said to have heralded the age of total war, replacing the "cabinet wars" of 1715–1792. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lev%C3%A9e_en_masse]

## Logistics and war economies
- Martin van Creveld, *Supplying War: Logistics from Wallenstein to Patton* (Cambridge University Press, 1977; 2nd ed. 2004). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_van_Creveld]
- In the Napoleonic Wars Britain (population about 16 million against France's 30 million) offset France's numbers by mobilising industrial and financial resources; it doubled its frigates and added 50% more ships of the line. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars]
- The Crimean War "quickly symbolised logistical, medical and tactical failures"; a supply tramway had to be built from Balaclava to the siege lines in 1855. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War]
- Paraguay's yerba mate harvest fell by as much as 95% between 1865 and 1867 (Reber). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraguayan_War]

## The world wars and total war
- The phrase "total war" seems to have originated among French writers in the First World War; Léon Daudet published *La Guerre totale* in 1918; Erich Ludendorff's *Der totale Krieg* appeared in 1935. Stig Förster identifies four dimensions: total purposes, total methods, total mobilisation and total control. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_war]
- David A. Bell, *The First Total War: Napoleon's Europe and the Birth of Warfare as We Know It* (2007) traces total war to the French Revolutionary Wars. (Wikipedia's Military history article cites it as 2008; the Total war article and its bibliography give 2007; use 2007.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_war]
- Förster and Nagler (eds), *On the Road to Total War: The American Civil War and the German Wars of Unification, 1861–1871* (Cambridge, 2002); Mark E. Neely Jr., "Was the Civil War a Total War?" (1991). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_war]
- The Second World War is called "the quintessential total war of modernity". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_war]
- World-war death tolls: use the published chapters' figures — First World War 15–22 million dead; Second World War about 60–75 million (some above 80 million), military deaths 21–25 million. [source: published chapters history--20th-century-history--first-world-war and --second-world-war]
- More than 800,000 women served in the Soviet armed forces in the Second World War, mostly as medics and nurses; 89 became Heroes of the Soviet Union, serving as pilots, snipers, machine gunners, tank crew and partisans. Russia's Provisional Government raised Women's Battalions in 1917 under Maria Bochkareva. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_the_military]

## Post-1945: proxy wars, civil wars, genocides and the "long peace"
- A proxy war is an armed conflict in which at least one belligerent is directed or supported by an external third-party power. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxy_war]
- Since 1945 interstate wars have declined in number and casualties and most fighting has been in civil wars and insurgencies; major exceptions include the Indo-Pakistani War of 1971, the Iran–Iraq War 1980–88, the Gulf War 1990–91 and the Russo-Ukrainian War. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_history]
- Korean War (armistice 27 July 1953): about one million military deaths and an estimated 1.5–3 million civilian deaths. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_War]
- Nigerian Civil War (Biafra), 6 July 1967 – 15 January 1970: about 100,000 military casualties and between 500,000 and 2 million Biafran civilians died of starvation under a deliberate blockade; other experts give 500,000 to one million total. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigerian_Civil_War]
- Bangladesh 1971: Pakistani soldiers and allied militias killed between 300,000 and 3,000,000 Bengalis and raped 200,000–400,000 women; about 10 million refugees fled to India; the "3 million" figure is embedded in Bangladeshi culture but contested. Ended 16 December 1971. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_genocide]
- Cambodia: by January 1979, 1.5–2 million people (20–25% of the 1975 population) had died under the Khmer Rouge; ended by Vietnam's invasion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambodian_genocide]
- Rwanda: genocide against the Tutsi, 7 April – 19 July 1994, about 100 days; 500,000–662,000 Tutsi killed (lower bound 491,000). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rwandan_genocide]
- The term "Long Peace" was introduced by John Lewis Gaddis in 1986 ("The Long Peace: Elements of Stability in the Postwar International System", *International Security*), book 1989; it denotes the absence of great-power war after 1945. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Peace]
- Pinker, *The Better Angels of Our Nature: Why Violence Has Declined* (Viking, 2011), treats the Long Peace as part of a longer decline. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Peace]

## The laws of war
- Henry Dunant witnessed the aftermath of Solferino (1859) and published *Un Souvenir de Solferino* (*A Memory of Solferino*) in 1862 at his own expense (1,600 copies). In October 1863, 14 states met in Geneva; on 22 August 1864 the First Geneva Convention was signed by 12 states. Dunant shared the first Nobel Peace Prize in 1901. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Dunant]
- The Ottoman government ratified the 1864 Convention on 5 July 1865. Later conventions: 1906, 1929, the four 1949 Conventions (after the Second World War), Additional Protocols I and II (1977; II covers non-international armed conflicts) and Protocol III (2005, the Red Crystal emblem). The 1949 Conventions have been ratified by 196 states. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva_Conventions]
- The Lieber Code (General Orders No. 100, 24 April 1863), drafted by Francis Lieber for the Union army, was the first official comprehensive codification of the laws of war and influenced the 1874 Brussels Declaration and the Hague Conventions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieber_Code ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hague_Conventions_of_1899_and_1907]
- Saint Petersburg Declaration (11 December 1868): 17 states renounced explosive projectiles under 400 grams; it affirmed that the only legitimate object of war is to weaken the enemy's military forces. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Petersburg_Declaration_of_1868]
- Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907: among the first formal statements of the laws of war in secular international law; the 1899 conference created the Permanent Court of Arbitration; declarations banned projectiles from balloons and asphyxiating-gas projectiles; China's delegation was led by Lu Zhengxiang; a third conference planned for 1914/15 never met. Many rules were violated in the First World War. The 1925 Geneva Protocol banned chemical and biological weapons in war. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hague_Conventions_of_1899_and_1907]

## Memory and commemoration
- Britain and France held services for their unknown soldiers on Armistice Day 1920 (Westminster Abbey; beneath the Arc de Triomphe, burial January 1921); the United States and Portugal followed in 1921. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_the_Unknown_Soldier]
- Remembrance Day (Poppy Day) grew out of Armistice Day; the first commemoration was in 1919 at George V's request; New Zealand's main day is Anzac Day, 25 April. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remembrance_Day]
- Yasukuni Shrine (Tokyo), founded 1869 after the Boshin War as Tōkyō Shōkonsha, lists 2,466,532 people, including 1,068 convicted war criminals, 14 of them Class A; it includes Koreans and Taiwanese who served Japan; it is highly controversial in East Asia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasukuni_Shrine]
- Jay Winter, *Sites of Memory, Sites of Mourning: The Great War in European Cultural History* (Cambridge University Press, 1995). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jay_Winter]

## The field: what military history is and how it developed
- Ancient roots: Herodotus and Thucydides; in India the *Arthashastra*; in China *The Art of War*, *The Book of Lord Shang* and the *Guanzi*; Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian* describes the Warring States. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_history]
- William H. McNeill (1980) wrote that military history "flourishes in an intellectual ghetto" and that disdain for it was "one of the strangest prejudices of the profession". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_history]
- The "new military history" emerged in the 1960s, expanding the field from battles, campaigns and commanders to ordinary soldiers, military institutions and the social and cultural contexts of war. The Society for Military History has published *The Journal of Military History* since 1937. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_history]
- Sir Michael Howard (1922–2019) helped found the Department of War Studies at King's College London; known for widening military history to the sociological significance of war; *War in European History* (1976); co-translated Clausewitz's *On War* with Peter Paret. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Howard_(historian)]
- John Keegan (1934–2012) lectured at Sandhurst for 26 years from 1960. *The Face of Battle* (Jonathan Cape, 1976) examined Agincourt, Waterloo and the Somme from the mechanics of combat and soldiers' experience rather than generals' viewpoint. In *A History of Warfare* (1993) he argued war is inherently cultural; Howard and Christopher Bassford criticised his treatment of Clausewitz. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Keegan ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Face_of_Battle]
- Jeremy Black lists the field's inherited problems: Eurocentricity, technological bias, focus on leading powers, separation of land, sea and air, focus on state-to-state war, and neglect of political "tasking". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_history]
- Gender: Cynthia Enloe (born 1938, Clark University), *Does Khaki Become You? The Militarization of Women's Lives* (Pandora edition 1988), *Bananas, Beaches and Bases* (1989), *Maneuvers* (2000), studies how militarisation depends on women's labour and gender roles. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynthia_Enloe]

## Classic theorists
- Carl von Clausewitz (1780–1831), *Vom Kriege* (*On War*), written mostly 1816–1830, unfinished, published posthumously by his wife Marie von Brühl from 1832. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_War]
- *The Art of War*, attributed to Sunzi (Sun Tzu), late Spring and Autumn period (roughly 5th century BC), 13 chapters; first European translation by the Jesuit Jean Joseph Marie Amiot in 1772; first annotated English translation by Lionel Giles, 1910; the Yinqueshan Han slips found in 1972 (tombs sealed 134–118 BC) contain an early version. Mao Zedong, Võ Nguyên Giáp and Takeda Shingen are among those said to have drawn on it. Sunzi's historicity has been questioned since about the 12th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Art_of_War]

## Military revolutions, gunpowder and global comparisons
- Michael Roberts introduced the "military revolution" (1560–1660, Sweden and the Dutch) in a 1955 lecture at Queen's University Belfast, published 1956 as "The Military Revolution, 1560–1660". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Roberts_(historian)]
- Geoffrey Parker challenged Roberts in "The Military Revolution, 1560–1660 – A Myth?" (1976), pointing to the *trace italienne* fortress; his *The Military Revolution: Military Innovation and the Rise of the West, 1500–1800* (Cambridge, 1988; rev. 1996) argued the revolution helped small European powers conquer much of the world. Critics: Jeremy Black (key period 1660–1710; state growth caused army growth, not vice versa; "technological determinism"), Michael Duffy, Clifford Rogers (series of revolutions from the 14th century), Jason Sharman (*Empires of the Weak*, 2019). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Revolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Parker_(historian)]
- "Gunpowder empires": term coined by Marshall Hodgson (*The Venture of Islam*, 1974) and taken up by William H. McNeill for the Ottoman, Safavid and Mughal empires; Douglas Streusand (2011) calls the hypothesis neither "adequate [n]or accurate". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpowder_empires]
- Kenneth Chase, *Firearms: A Global History to 1700* (Cambridge, 2003): early guns were of little use against mounted steppe nomads, so China and others had less incentive to improve them than Europe and Japan; criticised as geographical determinism. [source: WebSearch, https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780521822749 ; https://deremilitari.org/REVIEWS/Chase_Firearms.htm]
- Tonio Andrade, *The Gunpowder Age: China, Military Innovation, and the Rise of the West in World History* (Princeton, 2016): China held its own with Europe except 1839–1945; a "Great Military Divergence" began in the mid-18th century, partly because China enjoyed relative peace after about 1760. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonio_Andrade ; WebSearch reviews e.g. https://imperialglobalexeter.com/2016/01/27/the-gunpowder-age-china-military-innovation-and-the-rise-of-the-west-in-world-history/]
- John Thornton, *Warfare in Atlantic Africa, 1500–1800* (UCL Press, 1999), written at Jeremy Black's urging. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Thornton_(historian)]
- The Ottoman case: a 2013 study argues the military revolution "failed" there because of civil-military relations and timing (a strong army early in state formation). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Revolution]

## Debates
- **Old vs new military history**: "drum and trumpet" battle history and staff-college "lessons" vs war-and-society history (Howard, 1960s onward) vs the experience of combat (Keegan 1976) vs cultural history and memory (Winter 1995). Howard stressed that the past is unique and cannot give easy lessons. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_history ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Howard_(historian)]
- **Military revolution**: Roberts (1955/56) vs Parker (1976, 1988) vs Black, Duffy, Rogers, Sharman; extensions and critiques for Asia (Andrade, Chase, Peter Lorge *The Asian Military Revolution*, 2008) and the Ottomans (Ágoston). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Revolution]
- **"Western way of war"**: Victor Davis Hanson, *The Western Way of War* (1989) and *Carnage and Culture* (2001; UK title *Why the West Has Won*) argue Western military dominance comes from Greek-derived culture (citizen soldiers, decisive battle, consensual government); John A. Lynn's *Battle* (2003) rejects a continuous Western way of war and Hanson's "Oriental way of war", saying its features have parallels in European history and "the West" is never defined. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Davis_Hanson ; WebSearch on Lynn, e.g. https://www.bookandsword.com/2015/08/15/some-thoughts-on-john-lynns-battle/]
- **War and the state**: Charles Tilly's dictum (*The Formation of National States in Western Europe*, 1975, p. 42) and *Coercion, Capital, and European States, AD 990–1990* (1990) make war the driver of European state formation; critics call this Eurocentric; Centeno (2002) argues Latin America's limited wars produced weak states. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Tilly ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_A._Centeno ; WebSearch]
- **Total war**: when did it begin — 1793 (Bell), the US Civil War (Industrial warfare article; contested by Neely 1991), the unification wars (Förster and Nagler), or only the world wars? [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_war]
- **Decline of war**: Pinker (2011) and Joshua Goldstein argue violence and war have declined; Braumoeller (2019) finds no decline in international war initiation over two centuries and stresses chance and the clashes of rival international orders; the published Modern Conflicts chapter also cites Taleb and Cirillo and Clauset (2018). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Peace ; WebSearch on Braumoeller]
- **Was the Cold War a "long peace"?** Gaddis (1986) vs historians such as Heonik Kwon who stress Cold War civil wars and massacres in the global South (published Global Cold War chapter covers this). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Peace ; published chapter]
- **Paraguayan losses**: Reber (low) vs Whigham and Potthast (60–70%). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraguayan_War]
- **Technology vs organisation/culture**: Dreyse/railways at Königgrätz; Headrick's *Tools of Empire* (covered in the published Imperial Expansion chapter); Hodgson–McNeill "gunpowder empires" vs Streusand. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Prussian_War ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpowder_empires]

## Conflicts with published chapters
- **Taiping**: the published Taiping chapter calls it "the deadliest civil war of the nineteenth century" with "around 20 to 30 million" dead; the seed calls it "history's bloodiest civil war", and Wikipedia says "the deadliest civil war in history" with estimates up to 73–100 million. No real contradiction: use 20–30 million (conservative) and "often called the deadliest civil war in history"; do not use the 70–100 million upper figures without caveat. Cross-refer to history--modern-era-19th-century--taiping-late-qing.
- **Paraguayan War**: the published Latin America after Independence chapter gives the same figures (14 estimates 300,000–1,337,000; Reber 7–18.5%; Whigham and Potthast 60–69%; 1871 census). Note the seed says "proportionally the deadliest" — sources say "deadliest inter-state war in Latin American history" and (Pinker) "proportionally one of the most destructive in modern times"; soften to "perhaps proportionally the deadliest".
- **US Civil War deaths**: the published American Civil War chapter gives about 700,000–750,000 (Hacker 2011 c. 752,000; 2024 study c. 698,000). Wikipedia's Geneva Conventions article says 750,000–900,000; follow the chapter.
- **World wars**: use the published figures (WWI 15–22 million; WWII 60–75 million, some estimates above 80 million).
- **Long peace / Pinker / Braumoeller**: already discussed in history--contemporary-history--modern-conflicts and history--20th-century-history--global-cold-war; summarise briefly and cross-refer.
- **Military revolution**: the published Absolutism chapter dates Roberts's argument to 1955 (lecture) and cites Parker 1988; consistent with these notes (article published 1956).
- **Napoleon**: the Napoleon chapter gives Russian-campaign losses (Grande Armée 580,000–630,000 lost, 300,000–350,000 dead) and over a million dead in the Peninsular War; no conflict.
- **Boer War camps, Maxim gun, Omdurman, Adwa, Herero**: covered in the British Empire and Imperial Expansion chapters; cross-refer for "small wars".
- **Meiji conscription (1873)**: covered in the Meiji chapter.
- **Bell's *The First Total War***: Wikipedia's Military history article dates it 2008; Total war article 2007 — use 2007.
- **Howard–Paret *On War***: Wikipedia's Howard article dates the translation 1977; search results and common citation give Princeton 1976. Say "1976" only with care, or omit the year.
- No published chapter mentions Keegan, Clausewitz, Sunzi (as theorist), Hanson, Lynn, Enloe, Callwell, Dunant or the Lieber Code, so the chapter can introduce them.
