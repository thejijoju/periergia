# Verified facts — Oil and Empire (researched 2026-11-12)

## Producing societies before the majors: Baku and the Caspian
- In 1871 Ivan Mirzoev, an ethnic Armenian holding the tax-farm monopoly, built Baku's first wooden derrick. A law of 1873 replaced the monopoly with long-term leases. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Azerbaijan]
- Robert Nobel arrived in Baku in March 1873; Nobel Brothers Petroleum Production Company was founded in 1877, Branobel in 1879; they built Russia's first pipeline (1877), rail tank cars and the first successful oil tanker, *Zoroaster* (built 1877 in Sweden per this article; the Second Industrial Revolution chapter says 1878). The Nobels' company town was Villa Petrolea. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Azerbaijan]
- The Rothschilds (De Rothschild Frères) traded and shipped Baku oil in association with Shell and had the second-largest Caspian tanker fleet; their Mazut company (1898) had 13 tankers on the Caspian. In 1912 Shell obtained 80% of the Rothschilds' Caspian–Black Sea company "Mazut". A Baku–Batum kerosene pipeline was built 1897–1907. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Azerbaijan]
- Local capitalists: Armenians reportedly ran almost a third of the region's oil industry by 1900; Alexander Mantashev (Armenian) owned the third-largest Baku company by 1904; the Azerbaijanis Zeynalabdin Taghiyev and Musa Nagiyev were leading producers (Nagiyev the largest native producer). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Azerbaijan]
- Between 1898 and 1901 Baku produced more oil than the United States; by 1901 half the world's oil came from about 1,900 wells within about six square miles. On the eve of WWI three companies (Russian General Oil, Royal Dutch Shell, Nobel) held 86% of the equity capital and 60% of production of Russia's oil industry. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Azerbaijan]
- Labour: in Batumi (the Black Sea export terminal), the young Stalin worked at the Rothschild refinery storehouse and called a strike after a fire on 4 January 1902; when strike leaders were arrested, a crowd tried to free them and Cossack troops killed 13 and wounded 54 (the "Batumi massacre"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_life_of_Joseph_Stalin]
- The Baku general strike of December 1904 (begun 13 December in the Balakhany and Bibi-Heybat districts, per a Russian encyclopedia summary) ended with what is commonly called the first collective labour agreement in the Russian oil industry (nicknamed the "mazut constitution"), signed at the end of December 1904; Stalin's own 1909 anniversary article says a nine-hour day was set and an eight-hour day gradually introduced for well workers. Who called the strike is disputed (a Cambridge history says the Shendrikov brothers, joined by the RSDLP and Armenian groups; Soviet accounts credit the Baku RSDLP committee). [source: WebSearch — https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/stalin/works/1909/12/13.htm ; https://www.branobelhistory.com/employees/working-conditions-and-strikes/ ; https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/books/russian-azerbaijan-19051920/1905-revolution-and-azerbaijani-political-awakening/15AB7E3537E676F0D5CE6ADC9BCA7241]
- Strikes, violence and ethnic strife around the 1905 revolution cut Baku output from its peak of about 212,000 barrels a day; Stalin lived in Baku 1907–1909, editing Bolshevik papers and calling for strikes, while the city saw repeated Armenian–Azeri violence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Azerbaijan ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_life_of_Joseph_Stalin]
- Post-Soviet Azerbaijan: the "Contract of the Century" (Azeri–Chirag–Gunashli production-sharing agreement) was signed by President Heydar Aliyev and international companies on 20 September 1994; the Baku–Tbilisi–Ceyhan pipeline (about 1,760 km) officially opened on 13 July 2006. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Azerbaijan]

## Standard Oil and its breakup
- Rockefeller incorporated Standard Oil (Ohio) in 1870 with $1 million capital; the Standard Oil Trust was formed on 2 January 1882, its stockholders conveying their shares to nine trustees. In 1872 Rockefeller joined the South Improvement Company rebate scheme. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Oil]
- In 1904 Standard Oil controlled 91% of US oil refining and 85% of final sales. Ida Tarbell's *The History of the Standard Oil Company* appeared in *McClure's* 1902–1904 and as a book in 1904. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Oil ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ida_Tarbell]
- *Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States*, 221 U.S. 1, decided 15 May 1911 (Chief Justice White), found illegal monopolisation and ordered the break-up, adopting the "rule of reason". The number of successor companies is given variously: 34 is common in textbooks; Wikipedia's Standard Oil article says 39, the case article says 43 — write "more than 30" or "dozens". Successors include Jersey Standard (Exxon from 1973, ExxonMobil 1999), Standard of New York (Mobil) and Standard of California (Chevron). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Oil_Co._of_New_Jersey_v._United_States ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Oil]
- Global reach: from the 1890s Standard marketed kerosene in China under the name *Mei Foo* and gave away or sold cheap tin "Mei Foo" lamps to encourage farmers to switch from vegetable oil; China became its largest Asian market. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Oil]
- Rival: Royal Dutch Petroleum was founded in 1890 to develop a field at Pangkalan Brandan, North Sumatra (Dutch East Indies); "Shell" Transport and Trading was founded in 1897 by Marcus Samuel; they merged in April 1907 (60% Royal Dutch, 40% Shell), a deal arranged by Calouste Gulbenkian, explicitly to compete with Standard Oil. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shell_plc]

## Persia: the D'Arcy concession and Anglo-Persian
- On 28 May 1901 Mozaffar ad-Din Shah Qajar signed an 18-article concession giving William Knox D'Arcy (a London-based millionaire who had made his fortune at Australia's Mount Morgan mine) exclusive rights for 60 years over about three-quarters of Persia (the five northern provinces near Russia excluded), in return for £20,000 cash, £20,000 in shares and 16% of annual net profits. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%27Arcy_Concession ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Persian_Oil_Company]
- Drilling began at the end of 1902; Burmah Oil took over most of D'Arcy's interest (1905 agreement, majority sale 1907) with Admiralty encouragement. Geologist George Reynolds struck oil at Masjed Soleyman at 4 a.m. on 26 May 1908. Reynolds paid local Bakhtiari tribes fees and a share of profits to protect the works. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%27Arcy_Concession]
- Burmah created the Anglo-Persian Oil Company (APOC) on 14 April 1909. The Abadan refinery began volume production in 1913 and was for its first 50 years the largest refinery in the world. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Persian_Oil_Company]
- Workforce hierarchy (Stephanie Cronin, reviewing Bamberg): before 1933 almost all APOC's workmen were Iranian, there were almost as many Indian foremen as Iranian, and only a few senior posts went to Iranians. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%27Arcy_Concession]
- Reza Shah cancelled the concession on 27 November 1932; the new agreement (ratified by the Majlis 28 May 1933) cut the concession area by three-quarters, set a royalty of four shillings a ton plus 20% of distributed profits above a minimum, a minimum of £750,000 a year, faster "Persianisation", and extended the term from 1961 to 1993. Historians debate why: Bamberg (BP's official historian) blames the Depression; Cronin blames company accounting that excluded subsidiaries' profits and sold cheaply to the Admiralty. In 1947 Hassan Taqizadeh, who signed the 1933 agreement, said it was signed under duress. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%27Arcy_Concession ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Persian_Oil_Company]
- APOC became the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company (AIOC) in 1935 and British Petroleum in 1954. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Persian_Oil_Company]

## Churchill's oil navy (1911–14)
- As First Lord of the Admiralty (from 1911) Churchill moved the Royal Navy from coal to oil. The Admiralty settled the *Queen Elizabeth*-class battleship design on 15–16 June 1912 as oil-fired, the first British battleships to burn oil alone (24 knots in service vs the 21-knot battle line). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth-class_battleship]
- Rationale given: oil's greater energy density (longer range per bunker), and freeing Britain from dependence on Standard Oil and Shell; but Britain had abundant coal and almost no oil, so supply security required control at source. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Persian_Oil_Company]
- The government signed an agreement with APOC on 20 May 1914 and became majority (51%) shareholder; the investment is given as £2 million (BP's own history) or £2.2 million (other accounts) — say "about £2 million". The Commons approved it in June 1914 and the Anglo-Persian Oil Convention received royal assent on 10 August 1914. The supply contract ran 20 years. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Persian_Oil_Company ; WebSearch — https://www.bp.com/en/global/corporate/who-we-are/our-history.html]
- In November 1914 British Indian troops landed in Mesopotamia to protect Persian oil (already in the First World War chapter). [source: published chapter history--20th-century-history--first-world-war]

## Iraq, the Red Line and the "Seven Sisters"
- The Turkish Petroleum Company (TPC) was created by Calouste Gulbenkian, a British-Armenian financier; APOC took 50% under the 1914 arrangement, whose clause 10 already contained the "self-denial" principle. Post-war shares were fought over at the San Remo conference (1920). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_Petroleum_Company ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Line_Agreement]
- The mandate government of Iraq granted TPC a concession in 1925 (the American group was admitted under the "Open Door"). Oil was struck at Baba Gurgur near Kirkuk on 14 October 1927. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_Petroleum_Company]
- The Red Line Agreement was signed at Ostend on 31 July 1928 by APOC, Royal Dutch Shell, Compagnie Française des Pétroles, the Near East Development Corporation (American companies including Exxon's predecessors) and Gulbenkian ("Mr Five Percent"). Each of the four companies got 23.75%, Gulbenkian 5%; all bound themselves not to seek oil independently within the former Ottoman lands marked by a red line (the story that Gulbenkian drew it himself is reported, but some accounts deny it). TPC became the Iraq Petroleum Company (IPC) in 1929. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Line_Agreement]
- Within the line IPC monopolised exploration for about 20 years, except in Saudi Arabia and Bahrain. The agreement lapsed in practice after Jersey Standard and Socony, IPC partners, joined Aramco in 1947–48 following a legal fight with CFP and Gulbenkian settled out of court. Jersey's Walter Teagle later called it "a damn bad move". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Line_Agreement ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_Petroleum_Company]
- IPC's pipelines from Kirkuk reached Tripoli (Lebanon) on 14 July 1934 and Haifa on 14 October 1934. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_Petroleum_Company]
- Iraq reclaimed oil in stages: Qasim's Law No. 80 of 12 December 1961 took back 99.5% of IPC's concession area without compensation; the Ba'thist government nationalised IPC in June 1972. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_Petroleum_Company]
- "Seven Sisters": APOC (BP), Gulf Oil, Royal Dutch Shell, Standard Oil of California (Chevron), Standard Oil of New Jersey (Exxon), Standard Oil of New York (Mobil) and Texaco. The term was popularised by Enrico Mattei, head of Italy's state oil company Eni. By the 1930s they dominated world production and owned nearly all rights in Iran, Iraq, Saudi Arabia and the Gulf; Jeff Colgan (*Partial Hegemony*, 2021) argues they coordinated to restrain supply and minimise payments to host governments, backed by British and US pressure. Before 1973 they controlled about 85% of world reserves (a single newspaper source; treat as approximate). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Oil]
- In February 1959 the companies unilaterally cut posted prices for Venezuelan and Middle Eastern crude by 10%, angering producer governments and prompting the first steps towards OPEC. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OPEC ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Oil]

## Saudi Arabia, Aramco and the Gulf
- The Bahrain Petroleum Company (a SoCal subsidiary) found the first Gulf oil outside Iran in 1932. Saudi Arabia granted SoCal a concession in 1933 (company founded 29 May 1933 as California-Arabian Standard Oil, CASOC), preferring it to an IPC bid. Texaco bought 50% in 1936. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Aramco]
- Dammam No. 7 at Dhahran struck oil in March 1938 (the well, led by geologist Max Steineke with the Bedouin guide Khamis bin Rimthan, produced over 32 million barrels by 1982). Oil was also found in Kuwait (Burgan) in 1938, by the Kuwait Oil Company (APOC and Gulf, founded 23 December 1934). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dammam_No._7 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuwait_Oil_Company]
- CASOC became the Arabian American Oil Company (Aramco) on 31 January 1944; Jersey Standard (30%) and Socony (10%) joined in 1947. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Aramco]
- Article 23 of the original concession: the enterprise "shall be directed and supervised by Americans who shall employ Saudi nationals as far as practicable". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Aramco]
- In 1950 King Abdulaziz (Ibn Saud), threatening nationalisation, won a 50/50 profit split; the US government let the Aramco partners deduct the payments from US taxes (the "golden gimmick", a foreign tax credit of November 1950). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Aramco ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_gimmick]
- Labour: in October 1953 (17–26 October) about 13,000 Saudi Aramco workers struck at Dhahran — the kingdom's first major strike — demanding release of arrested representatives, better pay, fewer foreign workers and living conditions like those of American employees; the government ended it by threatening strikers with forced return to their villages. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1953_Aramco_Saudi_Arabia_strike]
- Ownership: the Saudi state took 25% in 1973 and 60% in 1974; Aramco's own history says the state's interest reached 100% in 1980 (one study: retroactive to 1976); Saudi Aramco was created by royal decree in November 1988. IPO on 11 December 2019 raised $25.6 billion, then the world's largest. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Aramco ; WebSearch — https://www.aramco.com/en/who-we-are/overview/our-history]
- Kuwait nationalised its oil industry in 1975. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuwait_Oil_Company]
- 14 September 2019: drones struck Aramco's Abqaiq and Khurais plants, disrupting about 5.7 million barrels a day; the Houthis claimed it, Saudi and US officials blamed Iran. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abqaiq%E2%80%93Khurais_attack]

## Race, camps and company towns
- Robert Vitalis, *America's Kingdom: Mythmaking on the Saudi Oil Frontier* (Stanford University Press, October 2006; Verso paperback later), argues that Aramco's Dhahran camps from the 1930s were run as a Jim Crow system modelled on oil camps in the US and Latin America, with housing worsening down a ladder from white Americans to Arab workers, and that "American exceptionalism" and company-sponsored histories hid this racial colonialism behind a myth of Aramco's benevolent "miracle" in the desert. Former Aramco employees disputed his account (*New York Review of Books* letters, 2013). [source: WebSearch — https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/university-pennsylvania-professor-re-writes-history-us-saudi-relations ; https://daily.jstor.org/the-jim-crow-roots-of-the-u-s-saudi-arabia-relationship ; https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2013/03/21/did-aramco-exploit-saudis/]
- Abadan: in 1947 AIOC reported after-tax profits of £40 million while Iran received £7 million. Workers lived in a shanty town called Kaghazabad ("Paper City") without running water or electricity (Manucher Farmanfarmaian, quoted by Kinzer). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Persian_Oil_Company]
- The Khuzestan general strike began on 14 July 1946 across AIOC's area, led by local Tudeh unionists; martial law was declared; clashes killed 17 (British embassy communiqué) or about 24 (British consular/Gulf reports), mostly Arabs; scholars describe Iranian, Arab and Indian workers united across ethnic lines, and early calls for nationalisation. [source: WebSearch — https://www.qdl.qa/archive/81055/vdc_100023246323.0x0000c1 ; https://associationforiranianstudies.org/content/cosmopolitan-nationalism-and-tudeh-party-during-1946-khuzestan-general-strike ; https://tribunemag.co.uk/2021/11/attlees-labour-against-iranian-workers]
- Mexico's Huasteca: Myrna Santiago argues oil created a new "ecology of oil", overturning Teenek (Huastec) communal lands; companies imported migrant labour, housed near wells and refineries; the lower in the hierarchy, the greater the environmental risk; oil workers became among the most radical in Mexico's labour movement and helped make the 1938 nationalisation possible. [source: WebSearch — https://clas.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/publications/brlas_spring_2007_05-24-07a_5.pdf ; https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/63247/excerpt/9780521863247_excerpt.pdf]

## Gender: oil towns and petro-masculinity
- Baku in WWII: as oil workers left for the front, by summer 1942 more than 25,000 women (33% of workers) worked 18-hour shifts in the oil industry; by 1944 women were about 60%. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Azerbaijan]
- Michael Ross argues oil reduces women's presence in the labour force (Dutch-disease effects crowd out export manufacturing that employs women), with consequences for fertility, girls' education and women's political influence; he contrasts oil-rich Algeria with Morocco. Critics (Groh and Rothschild, 2012) say the data fit an Islam explanation as well. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resource_curse ; WebSearch — https://www.brookings.edu/articles/book-review-the-oil-curse-how-petroleum-wealth-shapes-the-development-of-nations/ ; https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:now:jlqjps:100.00011036]
- Cara Daggett (Virginia Tech), "Petro-masculinity: Fossil Fuels and Authoritarian Desire", *Millennium: Journal of International Studies* 47(1), 2018, pp. 25–44: links Western authoritarian movements to a combination of climate denial, racism and misogyny, arguing fossil-fuel use can work as a "compensatory" assertion of masculine identity and that fossil fuels secure cultural meaning and political subjectivities, not just profits. Paraphrase; the paper drew mockery from conservative commentators. [source: WebSearch — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petro-masculinity ; https://genderandsecurity.org/projects-resources/research/petro-masculinity-fossil-fuels-and-authoritarian-desire]

## Mexico: from Doheny and Pearson to Pemex
- By 1901 commercial production had begun; the American Edward Doheny (Huasteca Petroleum) and the British Weetman Pearson (El Águila, struck oil July 1908) dominated. By 1922 Huasteca owned or leased 1,223,780 acres and El Águila 1,890,286 acres (Santiago). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Mexico]
- Article 27 of the 1917 Constitution vested subsoil rights in the nation. From 1918 into the 1920s Mexico was second only to the US in output and led the world in exports: 193 million barrels produced and 172 million exported in 1921. Output then fell to about 20% of the 1921 level by the 1930s as companies shifted to Venezuela. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Mexico]
- The oil workers' union (STPRM) was formed in 1935; after strikes, an arbitration award of 18 December 1937 ordered the companies to pay 26 million pesos; the Supreme Court upheld it on 1 March 1938; the companies refused. On 18 March 1938 Cárdenas announced the expropriation by radio. A crowd of 200,000 (press estimate) rallied in the Zócalo five days later; on 12 April crowds donated everything from chickens to jewellery towards compensation. Pemex was created by decree on 7 June 1938 (operating from 20 June). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_oil_expropriation]
- Retaliation: Jersey Standard and Shell organised a boycott; Britain withdrew its ambassador (May 1938); the US cut silver purchases. Roosevelt's Good Neighbor policy and the coming war ended the quarrel. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_oil_expropriation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Mexico]
- Compensation: the US–Mexico agreement of 18 April 1942 set compensation for US companies at about $24 million (contemporary press; the State Department's history site says about $29 million, perhaps including interest); the British held out until 1947 and received $130 million. [source: WebSearch — https://history.state.gov/milestones/1937-1945/mexican-oil ; https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/DOM19420421.2.88]
- Critics note wages were cut in the short run after nationalisation; Pemex later suffered corruption and political use; Peña Nieto opened the sector in 2013. Mexico's 1938 anniversary is a civic holiday. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_oil_expropriation]

## Venezuela: 50/50 and Pérez Alfonzo
- Venezuelan oil took off after the 1922 Maracaibo strike under dictator Juan Vicente Gómez (ruled to 1935), who let American companies draft the petroleum law and granted concessions to cronies. A strike paralysed the Zulia oil fields in López Contreras's first year (December 1936; led by communists Rodolfo Quintero and Jesús Faría) and was declared illegal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Venezuela ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Venezuela_(1908%E2%80%931958)]
- In 1943 Jersey Standard accepted a new agreement based on the 50–50 principle (Yergin: "a landmark event"); Juan Pablo Pérez Alfonzo (1903–1979), minister of development 1945–48, formalised the 50/50 tax under Rómulo Gallegos (1948), which became the global norm until about 1970 (Saudi Arabia 1950, Iran's 1954 consortium). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Venezuela ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Pablo_P%C3%A9rez_Alfonzo]
- After the November 1948 coup he was jailed nine months and exiled (US, then Mexico), studying the Texas Railroad Commission's prorationing; minister of mines and hydrocarbons 1959–63 under Betancourt; called the "father of OPEC". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Pablo_P%C3%A9rez_Alfonzo]
- Venezuela nationalised oil on 1 January 1976 (Carlos Andrés Pérez), creating PDVSA. In the 2002–03 strike Chávez dismissed nearly 19,000 PDVSA employees. Venezuela claims the world's largest proven reserves (about 300 billion barrels, OPEC figures, mostly Orinoco extra-heavy crude). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDVSA ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Venezuela]

## Mossadegh, nationalisation and the 1953 coup (keep brief; Global Cold War chapter tells it)
- 15 March 1951: the Majlis passed oil nationalisation; Mosaddegh became prime minister on 28 April 1951 (Majlis vote 79–12); NIOC's directorate entered the AIOC building at Abadan on 20 June 1951. Britain imposed a naval embargo, froze Iranian accounts and considered seizing Abadan ("Buccaneer" plan); the ICJ declined jurisdiction on 22 July 1952. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalization_of_the_Iranian_oil_industry ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammad_Mosaddegh ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Persian_Oil_Company]
- Mosaddegh's earlier compromise proposal was a 50/50 split modelled on Venezuela's 1948 deal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1953_Iranian_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat]
- Coup: 15–19 August 1953 (MI6 "Operation Boot", CIA "TPAJAX"); 200–300 killed (Wilford). The US formally acknowledged its role in documents released in August 2013. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1953_Iranian_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat]
- Aftermath: the 1954 consortium (Shah signed 29 October 1954): BP 40%, Shell 14%, the four Aramco partners 8% each, Gulf 8%, CFP 6%; profits split 50–50 with Iran, but no Iranian auditors or board members. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Persian_Oil_Company]

## Oil in the world wars
- 1914–18: Lord Curzon is reported to have said at an Inter-Allied Petroleum Conference (23 November 1918, per one account) that the Allies "floated to victory on (upon) a wave of oil"; exact wording varies between sources (see Quotations). [source: WebSearch — https://encyclopedia.1914-1918-online.net/article/petroleum]
- 1939–40: while the USSR supplied oil to Nazi Germany, Britain and France planned "Operation Pike" to bomb Baku. In 1942 Hitler's Case Blue and Directive No. 45 (23 July 1942, "Edelweiss") aimed at Maikop, Grozny and Baku (Baku to be taken by 25 September 1942); the drive failed after Stalingrad. Azerbaijan produced a record 25.4 million tons in 1941. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Azerbaijan ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Caucasus]
- Operation Tidal Wave, 1 August 1943: 177 USAAF B-24s attacked the Ploiești refineries in Romania; 53 were lost and 310 aircrew killed or missing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Tidal_Wave]
- Japan: the US/British/Dutch oil embargo of July 1941 and Japan's attack on the Dutch East Indies are already in the Second World War and Japan's Empire chapters — cross-refer. In 1941 Britain and the USSR jointly invaded Iran (Indian troops entered Abadan, 25 August 1941). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Persian_Oil_Company ; published chapters]

## OPEC, 1973 and petrodollars
- At Cairo's first Arab Petroleum Congress (1959), journalist Wanda Jablonski introduced Saudi Arabia's Abdullah Tariki ("the Red Sheikh", 1919–1997, Texas-trained geologist, first Saudi on Aramco's board, May 1959) to Pérez Alfonzo. The Baghdad Conference of 10–14 September 1960 (initiative of Tariki, Pérez Alfonzo and Iraq's Qasim) founded OPEC on 14 September 1960: Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, Venezuela. HQ Geneva, then Vienna from 1 September 1965. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OPEC ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdullah_Tariki]
- Later members: Qatar 1961, Indonesia 1962 (left 2008, rejoined 2016, suspended again end-2016), Libya 1962, Abu Dhabi/UAE 1967, Algeria 1969, Nigeria 1971, Ecuador 1973, Gabon 1975, Angola 2007, Equatorial Guinea 2017. Qatar left 1 January 2019. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OPEC]
- 1973: after Egypt and Syria attacked Israel (6 October 1973), OAPEC (the Arab producers), led by King Faisal, cut output and embargoed Canada, Japan, the Netherlands, the UK and the US (later Portugal, Rhodesia, South Africa). The embargo was lifted in March 1974, by which time prices had risen from about $3 to nearly $12 a barrel. Consistent with Energy and Neoliberal Age chapters: say OAPEC imposed the embargo. Consumers founded the IEA in response. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OPEC]
- Petrodollars: the term dates from 1973 (credited to Ibrahim Oweiss or Peter G. Peterson). OPEC's current-account surplus 1974–81 totalled about $450 billion, 90% to the Gulf Arab states and Libya; much went into US Treasuries and Western banks, which lent onward; the IMF estimated debts of 100 oil-importing developing countries rose 150% between 1973 and 1977 — prelude to the 1980s debt crisis. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrodollar_recycling]
- The "petrodollar warfare" thesis (William R. Clark, 2005) — that the dollar's oil role is enforced by wars — is contested; critics say dollar oil-pricing adds little to dollar demand. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrodollar_recycling]
- Backlash: OPEC's market share fell from about 50% (1979) to under 30% (1985); Saudi revenues fell from $119 billion (1981) to $26 billion (1985); prices more than halved in 1986. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OPEC]
- Indonesia: Shell's Indonesian assets were nationalised in 1957 (Permina, under Lt-Gen. Ibnu Sutowo); Pertamina formed 1968. 1974 oil revenue was $4.2 billion, about one-sixth of GDP; in February 1975 Pertamina defaulted, revealing over $10 billion of debt; the bail-out nearly doubled Indonesia's foreign debt. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pertamina]

## Carter Doctrine and the Gulf wars
- Carter Doctrine: State of the Union, 23 January 1980, responding to the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan; key sentence written by Brzezinski (see Quotations). It led to the Rapid Deployment Force, later CENTCOM. Reagan's 1981 "corollary" extended protection to Saudi internal security. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carter_Doctrine]
- Tanker war (1981–88): Iraq made 283 attacks on shipping, Iran 168; Kuwaiti tankers were reflagged under US protection (Operation Earnest Will, 24 July 1987 – 26 September 1988). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanker_war]
- 1990: Iraq accused Kuwait and the UAE of exceeding OPEC quotas (prices fell as low as $10, costing Iraq $7 billion a year) and of slant-drilling into the Rumaila field; at Jeddah Iraq demanded $10 billion, Kuwait offered $500 million; invasion 2 August 1990. War narrative is in Modern Conflicts — cross-refer. Saudi Aramco raised output by about 4.8 million barrels a day to replace lost supply. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Aramco]
- 2003 and oil motives: General John Abizaid (CENTCOM 2003–07) said "of course it's about oil, it's very much about oil and we can't really deny that"; Alan Greenspan wrote in *The Age of Turbulence* (2007) that "the Iraq war is largely about oil", then said that was not the administration's motive. Against: Tony Blair called it a "conspiracy theory"; John Duffield (2012) found "no compelling evidence" oil was prominent in decision-making; Jeff Colgan (2013) notes no consensus and that contracts went to non-US firms including Russian and Chinese ones. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationale_for_the_Iraq_War ; WebSearch — https://www.irishtimes.com/news/greenspan-says-invasion-and-war-largely-about-oil-1.962612 ; https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna20817260]

## Nigeria and the Niger Delta
- A 1914 ordinance vested minerals in the Crown; by 1938 Shell D'Arcy (later Shell-BP) had a monopoly over exploration; commercially viable oil was found at Oloibiri (now Bayelsa State) in 1956. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Nigeria]
- Oil and Biafra: output fell from 420,000 barrels/day (1966) to 140,000 (1968); control of Delta oil was entangled with the secession (some evidence of company tax disputes stoking tensions). Nigeria created the Nigerian National Oil Corporation in May 1971 and joined OPEC that year; NNPC formed 1979 by merging the NNOC with the petroleum ministry. Around 2000, oil and gas were over 98% of export earnings and about 83% of federal revenue. Cocoa output fell as oil rose (Dutch-disease pattern). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Nigeria]
- Ken Saro-Wiwa (10 October 1941 – 10 November 1995), Ogoni writer (*Sozaboy*, 1985; TV series *Basi & Company*), led MOSOP; in January 1993 about 300,000 Ogoni — more than half the population — marched. After four Ogoni chiefs were murdered on 21 May 1994, he and eight others were convicted by a special military tribunal (witnesses later said they were bribed) and hanged at Port Harcourt on 10 November 1995 under Sani Abacha; Nigeria was suspended from the Commonwealth. In 2009 Shell paid $15.5 million to settle the Wiwa family lawsuits, denying wrongdoing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Saro-Wiwa ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shell_Nigeria]
- Court documents released in 2009 show Shell in the 1990s worked with Nigeria's military and police against Ogoni activists. Shell attributed up to 98% of spills to sabotage, revising to 70% after Amnesty/Friends of the Earth challenges. In March 2025 Shell sold its onshore Niger Delta subsidiary to Renaissance Africa Energy Holdings for $1.3 billion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shell_Nigeria]

## The resource curse and Dutch disease
- "Dutch disease" was coined by *The Economist* in 1977 for the decline of Dutch manufacturing after the Groningen gas discovery (1959); the classic model is Corden and Neary (1982). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_disease]
- Richard Auty coined "resource curse" in 1993. Terry Lynn Karl, *The Paradox of Plenty: Oil Booms and Petro-States* (University of California Press, 1997). Michael L. Ross, *The Oil Curse: How Petroleum Wealth Shapes the Development of Nations* (Princeton, 2012): oil hinders democracy, makes civil war likelier and perpetuates patriarchy, partly through revenue secrecy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resource_curse ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_Karl ; WebSearch — https://washingtonmonthly.com/2012/04/17/the-oil-curse/]
- Critics: Haber and Menaldo (2011) find resource reliance does not induce authoritarianism and may aid democratisation; other studies find effects conditional on institutions or only for easily extracted oil; Norway is the standard counter-example. A *Nature* review faulted Ross for dating the curse to the 1970s nationalisations and downplaying earlier corporate and colonial exploitation. Mitchell also rejects "curse" as a property of oil itself. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resource_curse ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_politics ; WebSearch — https://www.nature.com/articles/482306a.pdf]
- Rentier-state theory (Mahdavy 1970; Beblawi and Luciani 1987) is already in Modern Middle East — cross-refer, don't repeat.

## Knowledge: The Prize, Carbon Democracy and the revisionists
- Daniel Yergin (b. 1947), *The Prize: The Epic Quest for Oil, Money, and Power* (Simon & Schuster, published December 1990, four months after the invasion of Kuwait; often cited as 1991); 912 pages; Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction 1992; often called the "definitive" history. Yergin founded Cambridge Energy Research Associates (1982), an industry consultancy, and is vice chairman of S&P Global — relevant to the "insider" critique. Sequels: *The Quest* (2011), *The New Map* (2020). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prize:_The_Epic_Quest_for_Oil,_Money,_and_Power ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Yergin]
- Company histories: BP's official multi-volume *History of the British Petroleum Company* (Ferrier vol. 1, 1982; Bamberg vols 2–3) — Cronin argues Bamberg's account deflects responsibility from company practices (example of court/company history shaping knowledge). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%27Arcy_Concession ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Persian_Oil_Company]
- Timothy Mitchell, *Carbon Democracy: Political Power in the Age of Oil* (Verso, 2011): coal's labour-intensive channels empowered workers and mass democracy; oil, moved by pipeline and tanker with fewer workers, weakened that leverage; reviewers say he challenges the "oil curse" as an oversimplification, arguing Western democracies were active partners in building oil's hegemony and became dependent on an undemocratic Middle East. Paraphrase only; consistent with the Energy chapter. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Mitchell ; WebSearch — https://www.thecairoreview.com/book-reviews/carbon-democracy-political-power-in-the-age-of-oil/ ; https://istishraf.dohainstitute.org/en/issue03/Pages/art17.aspx]

[source: WebSearch — https://aro-isig.fbk.eu/issues/2021/1/the-rise-and-fall-of-opec-in-the-twentieth-century-rudiger-graf/ ; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/762999/pdf]

## Oil's built environment
- Spindletop, near Beaumont, Texas: gusher on 10 January 1901, flowing an estimated 100,000 barrels a day for nine days; Gulf Oil and Texaco were formed to develop it. Mellon money (Guffey Petroleum) financed Lucas. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spindletop]
- Houston: oil companies set up offices within a few years of 1901; by the 1930s petroleum, not cotton, dominated the Ship Channel; WWII petrochemicals (Shell Deer Park, Humble); Gulf Freeway (begun 1944, completed 1952) was the first US freeway finished after WWII and spurred suburban growth; voters rejected zoning in 1962; the 1970s oil boom drew over 1,000 newcomers a week from the Rust Belt; the 1986 price collapse brought recession. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Houston]
- Sprawl: defined by low density, single-use zoning and reliance on the private car. The Interstate system (1956, petrol-tax funded) and the racial politics of US suburbs are in Infrastructure and Urban History — cross-refer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_sprawl ; published chapters]

## Climate and the oil majors' knowledge
- July 1977: Exxon senior scientist James Black told executives there was general scientific agreement that burning fossil fuels was the most likely way humans were influencing climate. 1979–82 Exxon fitted its supertanker *Esso Atlantic* with CO2 sensors; a 1982 internal report warned consequences could be catastrophic and might not be reversible once measurable. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ExxonMobil_climate_change_denial]
- From 1989, after a board presentation warning of "irreversible and costly Draconian steps", the company publicly questioned climate science; it was a founding board member of the Global Climate Coalition, funded groups opposing Kyoto, and ran newspaper advertorials (1989–2010) calling the science unsettled. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ExxonMobil_climate_change_denial]
- Supran and Oreskes (*Environmental Research Letters*, 23 August 2017): 83% of Exxon's peer-reviewed papers and 80% of internal documents acknowledged human-caused climate change, but only 12% of its advertorials did, 81% expressing doubt. Their 2023 *Science* paper (13 January 2023): Exxon's own projections, 1977–2003, "accurately" and "skillfully" forecast warming. ExxonMobil says the allegations are cherry-picked. *InsideClimate News* broke the story in 2015. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ExxonMobil_climate_change_denial]
- Saudi Aramco has emitted 59.26 billion tonnes CO2e since 1965, 4.38% of the world total (analysis reported by the Guardian, 9 October 2019). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Aramco]
- The Energy chapter already gives the 2026 Statistical Review figure (fossil fuels about 86% of energy in 2025, record oil use) — cross-refer for "the contested end of oil".

## Recent developments (dated; treat as provisional)
- OPEC+ was formed in late 2016 (OPEC plus Russia and about ten others). In October 2022, amid the energy crisis after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, OPEC+ cut targets by 2 million barrels a day; the US accused Saudi Arabia of helping Russia; Riyadh called it "purely economic". IEA members released 62.7 million barrels (1 March 2022) and then 120 million (April 2022), the largest release in IEA history; the G7 set a price cap on Russian oil. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OPEC ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020s_energy_crisis]
- 2025: OPEC+ began reversing voluntary cuts (about 2.5 million barrels a day reversed by September 2025). Angola left OPEC (listed as former member). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OPEC]
- January 2026: the US captured President Maduro in Venezuela (3 January); the Trump administration said access to Venezuelan oil was a core reason; a 29 January law gave private companies control of oil production and sale and US sanctions on Venezuelan oil were lifted. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_States_intervention_in_Venezuela]
- Since 28 February 2026 the US and Israel have been at war with Iran; tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz (normally about 20 million barrels a day, roughly 20% of seaborne oil trade) fell close to zero; crude passed $100 on 8 March 2026 for the first time since 2022, with reported peaks of about $119–126 (sources differ); Brent fell to about $72 by 1 July, then rose again. Described as the largest energy disruption since the 1970s. As of mid-October, Hormuz flows were about 82% of pre-war levels (Kpler). Houthis struck Aramco's Dhahran headquarters on 13 October 2026. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Strait_of_Hormuz_crisis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_of_oil ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Aramco]
- The UAE, then OPEC's third-largest producer, left OPEC and OPEC+ effective 1 May 2026 to produce outside quotas; in June 2026 Iraq reportedly considered leaving. OPEC now has 11 members. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OPEC]

## Debates
- **Why Britain and the US overthrew Mosaddegh:** Abrahamian — oil was central and the communist threat a "smokescreen"; Gasiorowski — fear of communism mattered most, and US majors (with a world glut) were reluctant to enter Iran; his 2019 study adds that Eisenhower read the same evidence more alarmingly than Truman. Others stress Iranian actors (Kashani, the clergy). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1953_Iranian_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat]
- **Why Reza Shah cancelled D'Arcy (1932):** Bamberg (Depression) vs Cronin (company accounting and labour practices). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%27Arcy_Concession]
- **Was the 2003 Iraq war "for oil"?** Abizaid, Greenspan, critics of Cheney/Halliburton links vs Blair, Bush, Duffield, Colgan; Gary Becker argued an oil-driven policy would have avoided war. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationale_for_the_Iraq_War]
- **Resource curse:** Karl, Ross (oil hurts democracy, peace, women) vs Haber and Menaldo (no causal curse), conditional-effects studies, Norway; Mitchell and the *Nature* reviewer locate the problem in how companies, empires and networks were built rather than in oil itself. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resource_curse]
- **OPEC — cartel or counter-cartel?** Economists call it a textbook cartel; OPEC presents itself as a stabiliser founded against the Seven Sisters' cartel; widespread quota cheating limits its power. Garavini writes sympathetically from the producers' side. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OPEC]
- **Whose history of oil?** Yergin's insider narrative and company-commissioned histories vs Mitchell's materialist account and revisionists (Vitalis on race and myth; Santiago on labour and ecology; Cronin on company accounts). Vitalis's account was disputed by former Aramco staff. [sources as above]
- **Petro-masculinity:** Daggett's psycho-political thesis vs critics who see it as overreach. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petro-masculinity]
