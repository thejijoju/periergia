## 1. Framing and the 1910 outbreak (brief; see overlap)
- MR defines the revolution as "an extended sequence of armed regional conflicts in Mexico from 20 November 1910 to 1 December 1920" and quotes it being called "the defining event of modern Mexican history". It was "primarily a civil war", but foreign powers with economic and strategic interests figured in the outcome, US involvement "particularly high". [source: MR]
- "There is consensus as to when the revolution began, that is in 1910, but there is no consensus when it ended": candidates are 1917 (constitution), 1920 (last successful military rebellion; Álvaro Matute: "By the time Obregón was sworn in as president on December 1, 1920, the armed stage of the Mexican Revolution was effectively over"), 1940 (Cárdenas hands over to Ávila Camacho) or 1946. A 1966 scholarly anthology was titled *Is the Mexican Revolution Dead?* [source: MR]
- Katz (cited in MR): in 1910 "there was no foreboding" that a revolution was about to break out; the aging Díaz failed to manage the presidential succession amid labour unrest (Cananea and Río Blanco strikes). [source: MR]
- Madero: born 30 October 1873, Parras, Coahuila, into one of Mexico's wealthiest families; educated in France (HEC Paris) and at UC Berkeley; a spiritist who believed his book *La sucesión presidencial en 1910* (published January 1909) was written at the direction of spirits, including Benito Juárez's; called "the Apostle of Democracy". (MAD's lead calls it a "1908 book"; it was written in 1908 and published January 1909.) [source: MAD]
- Plan of San Luis Potosí called for rising on Sunday 20 November 1910 at 6 pm; "few initially heeded the call". 20 November is Revolution Day. [source: SLP]
- Rebellions broke out first in Morelos and then, "to a much greater extent", in northern Mexico; the Federal Army could not suppress them. The first phase "was relatively bloodless and short-lived". Spontaneous risings involved farm labourers, miners, "along with much of the country's population of Indigenous peoples"; Madero's call also triggered the Magonista rebellion of 1911 in Baja California. [source: MR]
- Orozco and Villa took Ciudad Juárez against Madero's wishes; Treaty of Ciudad Juárez, 21 May 1911; interim president Francisco León de la Barra (May–November 1911). The Federal Army and the old regime's congress stayed in place; Madero demobilised the revolutionary forces. [source: MR]
- De la Barra's government sent General Victoriano Huerta against the Zapatistas in Morelos, "burning villages and wreaking havoc", driving a wedge between Zapata and Madero. [source: MR]
- Anti-Chinese violence: at Torreón, 13–15 May 1911, Maderista forces and a local mob killed 308 Chinese, about half the city's Chinese community; China demanded 30 million pesos in reparations; an investigation called it an unprovoked act of racism. (MR mentions the massacre without a number.) [source: TOR; MR]

## 2. Madero's presidency (November 1911 – February 1913)
- Madero won the October 1911 election and was inaugurated in November 1911. He allowed a free press and the right of labour to organise and strike; the anarcho-syndicalist Casa del Obrero Mundial was founded in September 1912; a National Catholic Party did well in the June 1912 congressional elections. [source: MR]
- Madero told Zapata the agrarian question "needed careful study"; MR: as a member of a rich northern hacendado family he "was not about to implement comprehensive agrarian reform". [source: MR]
- Villa's account of a 1911 banquet in Ciudad Juárez: "You [Madero], sir, have destroyed the revolution ... It's simple: this bunch of dandies have made a fool of you, and this will eventually cost us our necks, yours included." (Villa's own story, so attribute "according to Villa".) [source: MR]
- Pascual Orozco rebelled (Plan Orozquista, 25 March 1912; his men called *Colorados*); Huerta put him down in 1912 and became a rising figure. Huerta had Villa sentenced to death for insubordination in June 1912; Raúl Madero intervened; Villa escaped prison to the United States. [source: MR]
- Friedrich Katz (quoted in MR): Madero's retention of the Federal Army "was the basic cause of his fall"; his failure was also "the failure of the social class to which he belonged ... the liberal hacendados". [source: MR]

## 3. Zapata and the Plan of Ayala
- Emiliano Zapata, born 8 August 1879, Anenecuilco, Morelos; killed 10 April 1919 at the Hacienda de San Juan, Chinameca. "Contrary to popular legend, the Zapatas were a well-known local family and reasonably well-off"; Zapata traded in mules and horses. MR: "Zapata was not a peasant himself but led peasants". [source: ZAP; MR]
- Anenecuilco c. 1900 was a mixed Spanish-speaking mestizo and Nahuatl-speaking town whose leaders kept colonial-era land titles (some in Nahuatl) for lawsuits against haciendas. In 1909 the village elders chose the 30-year-old Zapata as head of the village council. An eyewitness, Luz Jiménez of Milpa Alta, said Zapata spoke Nahuatl fluently (recorded in Horcasitas, *De Porfirio Díaz a Zapata: memoria náhuatl de Milpa Alta*, 1968). [source: ZAP]
- In 1892 an Anenecuilco delegation petitioned Díaz; he had them arrested and Zapata was conscripted into the army (conscription was a common punishment). [source: ZAP] (Caveat: Zapata would have been about 13 in 1892; ZAP's chronology is loose—say only that Zapata was at one point forcibly conscripted.)
- Zapatistas took Cuautla after a six-day battle on 19 May 1911. [source: ZAP]
- Plan of Ayala: first proclaimed 28 November 1911 (AYA: "in the town of Ayala, Morelos"; ZAP says Zapata and the schoolteacher Otilio Montaño drafted it in the mountains of southwest Puebla; other sources date publication to December 1911). Co-author Otilio Montaño Sánchez, a schoolteacher. It declared Madero a traitor and named Pascual Orozco chief of the revolution (Zapata himself if Orozco declined); amended 19 June 1914 to remove Orozco after he joined Huerta. [source: AYA; ZAP; WebSearch summary of SCJN digital library and other sources]
- Content (AYA summary): lands usurped by "landlords, científicos, or bosses" to be returned to villages holding titles, possession kept "at all costs, arms in hand", with tribunals after victory; one-third of monopolised estates expropriated for landless villages and individuals; owners who resisted to lose the other two-thirds; invoked Juárez's expropriation of Church lands as precedent. [source: AYA; ZAP]
- Plan of Ayala's cry: "¡Reforma, Libertad, Justicia y Ley!" ("Reform, Freedom, Justice and Law!"), later, after Zapata's death, shortened to "¡Tierra y Libertad!" ("Land and Liberty"), a phrase first used by Ricardo Flores Magón. [source: AYA]
- MR: of all factions the Zapatistas were "the most homogeneous", mostly free village peasants rather than hacienda peons; no industrial workers; fighters joined for battles and returned to their villages; geographically cut off from the US arms trade. "Morelos was the only region where land reform was enacted during the years of fighting." [source: MR]
- 1915 in Morelos: Zapata redistributed hacienda lands and let village councils run local affairs; peasants grew subsistence crops (maize, beans, vegetables) rather than cash crops, so "as the capital was starving ... the peasants had more to eat than they had had in 1910". [source: ZAP]
- 1918: cold and the Spanish flu killed "a quarter of the total population" of Morelos (ZAP's wording; treat as approximate). [source: ZAP]
- Assassination: General Pablo González had Colonel Jesús Guajardo feign defection; on 10 April 1919 at Chinameca Guajardo's men "riddled him with bullets". Photographs of the corpse were published as proof; "Carranza was tainted by the deed". Zapata's body was displayed in Cuautla. [source: ZAP; MR; AYA]
- Zapatista generals later made terms with Obregón (1920); Zapatistas held posts under De la Huerta and Obregón and carried out land reform in Morelos. "The agrarian reforms that peasants themselves enacted in Morelos were impossible to reverse." [source: ZAP; MR; AYA]
- Zapata's forces fought under the emblem of the Virgin of Guadalupe; the movement was considered friendly to the Church, and many Cristeros later admired Zapata. [source: ZAP]
- Hostile contemporary framing: Zapata's revolt "was soon construed as a 'caste war'", "members of an 'inferior race' ... captained by a 'modern Attila'"; pejorative nickname "El Atila del Sur". [source: ZAP]

## 4. The Ten Tragic Days and Huerta (1913–14)
- *La Decena Trágica*, 9–19 February 1913 (HUE: "actually fifteen days" counting to the murders): Félix Díaz (Porfirio's nephew) and Bernardo Reyes broke out of prison; Reyes was killed on the first day; Díaz held the Ciudadela armoury; Huerta, put in charge of the defence, changed sides. Civilians in central Mexico City were caught in the shelling; TTD's infobox gives "5,500 dead" (cite as a contemporary-scale estimate only). [source: TTD; HUE; MR]
- US Ambassador Henry Lane Wilson brokered the Pact of the Embassy between Félix Díaz and Huerta. After the resignations, Pedro Lascuráin was president for about 45 minutes, long enough to appoint Huerta interior minister and resign. Asked whether to exile Madero or put him in an asylum, Wilson reportedly replied: "General, do what you think is best for the welfare of Mexico." [source: TTD; MR]
- Madero and Vice-President José María Pino Suárez were shot on the night of 22 February 1913 behind Lecumberri prison by officers Francisco Cárdenas and Rafael Pimienta; the official story was crossfire. Katz: whether they acted on Huerta's orders "is hotly debated", "but there is strong evidence that Huerta gave the order and that Wilson knew". [source: TTD; MR]
- MR: Madero's "martyrdom accomplished what he was unable to do while alive: unite all the revolutionists under one banner". [source: MR]
- Huerta: born 23 December 1850 (disputed; other dates 1845, 1853, 1854) in Colotlán, Jalisco; he identified himself as Indigenous, and both parents are reported to have been Huichol (his father possibly mestizo). Mexicans call him *El Chacal* ("the Jackal") or *El Usurpador*; in Mexican historiography he is the "demon" of the revolution. [source: HUE]
- The Huerta regime was recognised by most governments, including Britain and Germany, but not by the United States under Woodrow Wilson; the Catholic hierarchy and business initially supported it. Huerta dissolved Congress on 26 October 1913. [source: MR; HUE]
- Huerta tried to raise the army from about 50,000 on paper to 150,000, 200,000 and (spring 1914) 250,000 through the *leva* (forced conscription); conscripts deserted and mutinied. [source: MR; HUE]
- Huerta fled in mid-July 1914 (VIL: left the country 14 July 1914) on a German ship; arrested in the US in 1915 while plotting a return with German backing; died in US custody at El Paso, 13 January 1916, probably of cirrhosis. [source: MR; HUE; VIL]

## 5. Carranza, Villa, Obregón and the war of the winners
- Venustiano Carranza (29 December 1859 – 21 May 1920), landowner and Porfirian senator, governor of Coahuila; Plan of Guadalupe, 26 March 1913: strictly political, rejecting Huerta and naming Carranza *Primer Jefe* ("First Chief") of the Constitutionalist Army; it made no promises of reform. [source: CAR; GUA]
- Pancho Villa, born José Doroteo Arango Arámbula, 5 June 1878 (Durango); sharecropper's son, then bandit; brought into Madero's movement by Abraham González. Commander of the División del Norte; provisional governor of Chihuahua 1913–14, printed his own currency, confiscated hacienda wealth and promised post-war land redistribution, but "did not implement it when he had power". [source: VIL]
- Villa's army used Porfirian railways, had hospital trains, and paid soldiers one peso a day; Villa won at Torreón (April 1914) and Zacatecas (June 1914), which "broke the back of the Huerta regime". [source: VIL; MR]
- John Reed spent four months with Villa's army; *Insurgent Mexico* (1914). Wilson reportedly described Villa as "a sort of Robin Hood". [source: VIL]
- Álvaro Obregón (19 February 1880 – 17 July 1928), Sonoran chickpea (garbanzo) farmer who had not joined Madero in 1910; learned the Mayo language working beside Mayo labourers and drew Mayo and Yaqui fighters to his army. [source: OBR]
- Convention of Aguascalientes, from 5 October 1914; Carranza spurned it, "Villa effectively hijacked it"; Eulalio Gutiérrez chosen president; the Convention declared Carranza in rebellion. Obregón sided with Carranza; Villa and Zapata formed a loose alliance and took Mexico City in December 1914 (Carranza withdrew to Veracruz). The photo of Villa in the presidential chair beside Zapata is "a classic image"; Villa reportedly said the chair "is too big for us". [source: MR; OBR]
- Carranza's "Additions to the Plan of Guadalupe", 12 December 1914, promised reform; agrarian law of 1915 (drafted by Luis Cabrera) restored illegally seized village lands. In February 1915 the Constitutionalists signed a pact with the Casa del Obrero Mundial, forming six "Red Battalions" of workers to fight Villa and Zapata; Carranza dissolved them in January 1916. [source: CAR; MR]
- Battle(s) of Celaya, 6–7 and 13–15 April 1915: Villa's frontal cavalry charges met Obregón's entrenched machine guns and wire; OBR counts four battles including Trinidad/Santa Ana del Conde (29 April – 5 June) and calls the series the largest military confrontation in Latin American history before the Falklands War (1982). CEL's infobox (poorly sourced, tagged "more citations needed") gives Villa about 6,000 killed and 6,500 captured; do not use exact numbers. [source: MR; OBR; CEL]
- Obregón lost his right arm in the 1915 fighting against Villa (OBR places it in the Trinidad/Santa Ana del Conde phase; MR says "the 1915 Battle of Celaya"; say "in the 1915 campaign against Villa"). Nicknamed *El Manco de Celaya*. The embalmed arm was displayed in a monument (1935) at La Bombilla, where he was assassinated; it was cremated in 1989. [source: OBR; MR]
- The US recognised Carranza's government de facto in October 1915. Zapata remained in arms; Villa retreated north. [source: MR]
- Carranza returned confiscated estates to owners and did not carry out land reform; land often went to Constitutionalist generals. In Yucatán Salvador Alvarado (from 1915) carried out extensive land reform. [source: MR]
- Carranza's fall: he tried to impose a civilian, Ignacio Bonillas; the Sonorans Obregón, Calles and Adolfo de la Huerta rose under the Plan of Agua Prieta (1920), "the last successful coup of the revolution". Carranza fled and was killed on 21 May 1920 at Tlaxcalantongo, Puebla, in an attack by Rodolfo Herrero; MR: "perhaps an assassination, but also possibly by suicide". [source: MR; CAR]
- De la Huerta negotiated Villa's surrender (1920) for a hacienda (Canutillo, Durango). Villa was ambushed and killed in Parral on 20 July 1923, most likely on the orders of Obregón and Calles; Jesús Salas Barraza claimed responsibility and served only months. Villa's reputed last words ("Don't let it end like this. Tell them I said something") have no contemporary evidence; Katz says he died instantly. [source: VIL; MR]
- "All of the major leaders of the Revolution were later assassinated: Madero in 1913, Zapata in 1919, Carranza in 1920, Villa in 1923, and Obregón in 1928." [source: MR]

## 6. The United States: Veracruz 1914 and the Punitive Expedition
- Tampico Affair, 9 April 1914: nine US sailors briefly detained at Tampico; Admiral Henry T. Mayo demanded an apology and a 21-gun salute; the apology was given, not the salute. [source: VER; TAM]
- Occupation of Veracruz, 21 April – 23 November 1914, under Rear Admiral Frank F. Fletcher, then General Frederick Funston; orders: "Seize custom house. Do not permit war supplies to be delivered to Huerta government or any other party." The aim was to stop the German ship *Ypiranga* landing arms; the ship later landed its cargo (largely American-made rifles) at another port. Veracruz Naval Academy cadets resisted. [source: VER; MR]
- Casualties: MR: 193 Mexican soldiers, 19 Americans "and an unknown number of civilians"; VER infobox: US 21 killed (text: nineteen sailors and Marines), Mexico 152–172 killed, 195–250 wounded. Give ranges. [source: MR; VER]
- It provoked anti-US riots in Mexico and several Latin American countries; even Huerta's opponents backed his stand on sovereignty. The ABC powers (Argentina, Brazil, Chile) mediated at the Niagara Falls conference; the US left the city to the Carrancistas with war matériel. [source: VER; HUE; MR; CAR]
- Columbus raid, 9 March 1916: Villistas attacked Columbus, New Mexico and a 13th Cavalry camp. COL infobox: 8 US soldiers and 10 civilians killed; at least 67 Villistas killed. VIL text: "Eighteen Americans and about 80 Villistas were killed." Motives debated (supplies; anger at US recognition of Carranza and at US help to Carranza at Agua Prieta). [source: COL; VIL; PVE]
- Punitive (Pershing) Expedition, 14 March 1916 – 7 February 1917: about 10,000 troops (PVE infobox; MR and VIL say "around 5,000", likely the initial force). Under Funston's overall command, led by John J. Pershing. First US use of truck convoys; the 1st Aero Squadron flew reconnaissance; 2nd Lt George S. Patton led the first US motorised raid (14 May 1916). Villa was never caught; the expedition killed 169 Villistas who had taken part in the Columbus raid, and several of his commanders. [source: PVE; VIL]
- Carranza's forces resisted the incursion: Parral (12 April 1916) and Carrizal (21 June 1916, a defeat for the 10th Cavalry, the Black "Buffalo Soldiers" regiment) brought the two countries close to war; the US put 100,000 troops on the border. Withdrawal 28 January – 5 February 1917. It strengthened Carranza and "permanently escalat[ed] anti-American sentiment in Mexico". [source: PVE; MR]
- Zimmermann telegram, 17 January 1917: Germany offered Mexico an alliance and help to recover Texas, New Mexico and Arizona; Carranza did not take it up; its publication helped bring the US into the war. Mexico stayed neutral. [source: ZIM; MR; VER]
- Wilson's remark about teaching Latin Americans "to elect good men" (to a British visitor, November 1913) is widely reported but the wording varies between sources; quote only as "reportedly". [source: WebSearch results incl. https://www.wilsoncenter.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/event/wilsons_forays_into_mexico.pdf; https://edsitement.neh.gov/lesson-plan/elect-good-men-woodrow-wilson-and-latin-america]

## 7. The Constitution of 1917
- Drafted at Querétaro (Teatro Iturbide) by a congress opening November 1916; approved 5 February 1917 (Constitution Day). 220 delegates, all Carrancistas; Villistas, Zapatistas and Huertistas excluded; mostly middle-class professionals, with a small group of revolutionary generals such as Francisco J. Múgica. [source: CON; MR]
- Carranza's draft was a minor revision of the 1857 constitution; delegates "mauled" it. The most contentious debates were over education and the Church; Articles 27 and 123 "passed easily". [source: CON; MR]
- Tannenbaum (in CON): "The Constitution was written by the *soldiers* of the Revolution, not the lawyers ... Article 27 was a compromise." (Full wording in Quotations.) [source: CON; MR]
- Article 27: national ownership of land, water and subsoil; basis for land reform (expropriation of haciendas, ejidos) and for the 1938 oil expropriation; foreigners barred from owning land at borders and coasts. Opening of the original text (DOF, 5 February 1917): "La propiedad de las tierras y aguas comprendidas dentro de los límites del territorio nacional, corresponde originariamente a la Nación" ("Ownership of the lands and waters within the limits of the national territory belongs originally to the Nation"). Andrés Molina Enríquez (*Los grandes problemas nacionales*) is called its "intellectual father". [source: CON; MR; CAR2; WebSearch confirming the SCJN "Texto Original": https://www.scjn.gob.mx/sites/default/files/cpeum/documento/CPEUM-027.pdf]
- Article 123: eight-hour day, right to strike, minimum wage, equal pay for women, bans on child labour and company stores, payment in cash not scrip; drafted by a committee under Pastor Rouaix and José Natividad Macías; many demands came from the Mexican Liberal Party (PLM) programme of 1906. Carranza had not intended to put labour protection in the constitution. [source: CON; MR]
- Article 3 (secular education) and Article 130 (no legal status for churches; states could limit the number of priests; foreign-born clergy banned; clergy denied the vote) plus Articles 5, 24, 27 restricted the Church. [source: CON; CRI]
- CON claims it was "the first such document in the world to set out social rights", before the Soviet Russian constitution of 1918 and Weimar (1919). Use as "often described as". [source: CON]
- Women: Hermila Galindo asked the convention to extend the vote to women; the committee explicitly refused ("under the conditions in which Mexican society finds itself, the granting of the vote to women is considered unnecessary"). Women voted nationally only from 1953. [source: CON]
- Carranza, elected president in 1917, largely ignored the radical articles. 1992 Salinas reforms amended Article 27 to end land redistribution and allow ejido privatisation, and repealed most anticlerical provisions. [source: MR; CON]

## 8. The Sonorans, Calles and the Cristero War (1920–29)
- Obregón president 1 December 1920 – 1924; José Vasconcelos education secretary (SEP created 1921; Vasconcelos in office 1921–24); Obregón halved the army; Bucareli Treaty (August 1923) gained US recognition with concessions on oil; De la Huerta's rebellion (1923–24) defeated with US arms. Calles president 1924–28. [source: MR; OBR; VAS]
- Obregón fought a Yaqui rebellion demanding land in 1926–27, although he had "first risen to military prominence commanding Yaqui troops, to whom he promised land". [source: OBR]
- Calles Law (June 1926) enforced the anticlerical articles: fines for wearing clerical garb in public, five years' prison for priests criticising the government; Chihuahua allowed one priest for the whole state. The bishops suspended public worship ("the Church ... going on its version of a strike") and organised an economic boycott. [source: CRI; MR]
- Cristero War, 3 August 1926 – 21 June 1929; rebel cry "¡Viva Cristo Rey!" ("Long live Christ the King"); centred on Jalisco, Michoacán, Guanajuato, Aguascalientes, Zacatecas; the formal rising followed a manifesto of 1 January 1927. Leaders included General Enrique Gorostieta (killed) and priests such as José Reyes Vega. [source: CRI; MR]
- Numbers (CRI infobox): government ~100,000 men, Cristeros ~50,000 men and women (1929); government 56,882 dead; Cristeros 30,000–50,000 dead; "estimated 250,000 dead, including civilians". Treat all as estimates. [source: CRI]
- MR: "a lengthy, major uprising against the revolutionary vision of the Mexican state in central Mexico, not a short-lived, localized rebellion". CRI: interpreted both as church–state conflict and "the last major peasant uprising" after 1920; many rebels were also fighting for land. [source: MR; CRI]
- Women: the Feminine Brigades of St Joan of Arc, founded 21 June 1927 in Zapopan (16 women and one man), smuggled arms and money; about 10,000 women by March 1928, some 25,000 by the end of the war (CRI also gives a "17,000" figure; use "thousands, perhaps 25,000"). [source: CRI]
- Jesuit Miguel Pro executed without trial, 23 November 1927 (beatified 1988). Pope John Paul II canonised 25 Cristero-era martyrs on 21 May 2000. [source: CRI]
- Obregón, re-elected in 1928 after a constitutional change, was assassinated on 17 July 1928 at La Bombilla by José de León Toral, a Catholic militant, before taking office. [source: OBR; MR]
- The *arreglos* (21 June 1929), brokered by US Ambassador Dwight Morrow (the "ham and eggs diplomat"), restored worship; the rebels were not consulted, and the government afterwards executed some 500 Cristero leaders and 5,000 other Cristeros (CRI). Priests licensed fell from about 4,500 before the war to 334 by 1934. [source: CRI]
- "Second Cristiada" in the 1930s against socialist education: former Cristeros attacked rural teachers, cutting off ears (*maestros desorejados*); estimates of about 300 rural teachers killed 1935–39, or at least 223 in 1931–40. [source: CRI]
- Calles founded the Partido Nacional Revolucionario (PNR) in 1929 and dominated the *Maximato* (1928–34) as *Jefe Máximo*, through presidents Portes Gil, Ortiz Rubio and Abelardo Rodríguez. [source: MR; PRI; CAL]

## 9. Cárdenas (1934–40)
- Lázaro Cárdenas (21 May 1895 – 19 October 1970), from Jiquilpan, Michoacán; governor of Michoacán 1928–32. Calles's candidate in 1934, he broke Calles's power: Calles was deported to the United States on 9 April 1936 (CRI/MR say "between 1935 and 1936"; CAR2 says 1935; CAL's specific date is the most precise). [source: CAR2; CAL; MR]
- Land reform "sweeping, rapid, and, in some respects, innovative": some 180,000 km² (18 million hectares) redistributed; collective ejidos (members worked plots without private title) created from commercial estates, notably cotton-growing La Laguna and henequen Yucatán, also Baja California, Sonora, Michoacán and Chiapas. Many peasants would have preferred individual titled plots; Molina Enríquez criticised the state replacing private landlords. [source: CAR2; MR; EJI]
- Department of Indigenous Affairs created 1936; national Indigenous congresses; influence of Moisés Sáenz's *indigenismo*. The 1940 government report: "the indigenous problem is one of the most serious that the revolutionary government has had to confront." [source: CAR2]
- Labour: CTM founded 1936 under Vicente Lombardo Toledano; railways nationalised; peasants and workers armed in militias; socialist education amendment to Article 3 (13 December 1934). [source: MR; CAR2; CON]
- Oil: the oil workers' union demanded a collective contract; the arbitration board awarded 26 million pesos (December 1937); the Supreme Court upheld it on 1 March 1938; the companies refused. On 18 March 1938 Cárdenas expropriated the US and Anglo-Dutch (Mexican Eagle / Royal Dutch Shell) companies, announcing it by radio; Pemex created by decree 7 June 1938. A crowd put at 200,000 rallied in the Zócalo; on 12 April 1938 people queued at Bellas Artes to donate (from chickens to jewellery) towards compensation. 18 March is a civic holiday. [source: OIL; CAR2]
- Britain broke off relations (1938); companies organised a boycott; FDR's Good Neighbor Policy kept the US from intervening. Compensation: a US–Mexican experts' agreement of April 1942 set about $24 million for the American companies (with interest, about $29.1 million, paid off by 1947); Britain settled in 1947 (a State Department history gives $130 million; verify before using the figure). [source: OIL; CAR2; WebSearch results: https://history.state.gov/milestones/1937-1945/mexican-oil; https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/DOM19420421.2.88; https://time.com/archive/6897564/oil-soap-for-harry]
- Production regained pre-expropriation levels only in 1942. [source: CAR2]
- Party: Cárdenas refounded the PNR as the Partido de la Revolución Mexicana (PRM, 1938) with four sectors (workers, peasants, "popular"/middle class, army); Ávila Camacho dropped the army sector; renamed Partido Revolucionario Institucional (PRI) in 1946. Under its three names the party held the presidency from 1929 to 2000. [source: MR; PRI]
- Women's suffrage amendment passed by both houses (1937–38) and most states but never published in the *Diario Oficial*, so never took effect, partly from fear that women would vote as priests told them. [source: CAR2]
- Refugees: Mexico took in 20,000–40,000 Spanish Republican refugees; Trotsky allowed to settle in Mexico (CAR2: 1936). Cedillo's revolt (1938) was "the last military major revolt in Mexico". [source: CAR2]
- Knight on the 1940 election: "a requiem for Cardenismo ... hopes of a democratic succession were illusory; ... electoral endorsement of the regime had to be manufactured". Cárdenas is remembered as "the moral conscience of the Revolution". [source: MR; CAR2]

## 10. Death and displacement (give as ranges)
- MR's lead: "around one million people, mostly non-combatants"; MR body: "Perhaps 1.5 million people died, and nearly 200,000 refugees fled abroad, especially to the United States"; MR infobox: 1.7–2.7 million Mexican deaths (the high figure from R. J. Rummel's democide tables—weak). [source: MR]
- Robert McCaa, "Missing Millions: The Demographic Costs of the Mexican Revolution", *Mexican Studies/Estudios Mexicanos* 19:2 (2003): total demographic loss 1910–21 of about 2.1 million (plus a 1921 census undercount of about one million), of which excess deaths were about two-thirds (≈1.4 million: 900,000 males, 500,000 females) and lost births about a quarter; earlier estimates ranged from about 0.5 million to 2 million excess deaths (Collver's low and high models); Ordorica and Lezama ≈1.5 million. [source: WebSearch results: https://users.pop.umn.edu/~rmccaa/missmill/abstract.htm; https://users.pop.umn.edu/~rmccaa/missmill/mxrev.htm]
- **Writer guidance:** say "between roughly one and two million people died, most of them civilians, many from hunger and disease, including the 1918 influenza; the best demographic study puts excess deaths at about 1.4 million". The seed's "perhaps a million" is the low end; the neighbouring chapter's "perhaps 1.5 million" sits inside the range.
- Mexican immigration to the US rose sharply in 1910–20; the Mexican economy did not regain its 1910 level for about twenty years. [source: MR]
- Cristero refugees went to Los Angeles, Chicago, Milwaukee and San Antonio; LA staged a 40,000-strong Cristero parade in 1934. (CRI's quoted claim that as much as 5% of Mexico's population fled is implausible; do not use.) [source: CRI]
- The revolutionary factions did not wage a systematic "revolutionary terror" after victory, unlike France and Russia; there were no prisoner-of-war camps; defeated rank and file were often absorbed by the victors. [source: MR]

## 11. Women and soldaderas
- *Soldaderas* ("Adelitas", from the corrido "La Adelita") ranged from camp followers to combatants and a few *coronelas*; "there are no reports of a woman achieving the rank of general". The word derives from *soldada*, a payment to whoever provisions a soldier, though most were unpaid. [source: SOL]
- They were most numerous in the north, where the Federal Army and, from 1913, the railway-borne revolutionary armies relied on women to find and cook food and nurse the wounded; women and children rode on top of the boxcars while horses rode inside. Zapatista forces, based in their villages, had few. [source: SOL; MR]
- Joining was often not voluntary: kidnapping and forced recruitment were reported (e.g. the *Mexican Herald*, April 1913, on 40 women taken from Jojutla); others followed husbands for protection or out of conviction. [source: SOL]
- After defeat General Salvador Mercado's federal army crossed into the US with 1,256 women and 554 children (interned at Fort Bliss) alongside 3,357 officers and men. [source: SOL]
- Reported atrocity: in December 1916 Villa had about 90 Carrancista soldaderas killed (accounts differ; Elena Poniatowska gives a different version). [source: SOL]
- Individuals: Petra Herrera (served as "Pedro" with Villa, later led an all-women troop); Ángela/Ángel Jiménez (explosives expert, Oaxaca); Amelio Robles Ávila, Zapatista colonel who lived as a man for the rest of his life (d. 9 December 1984, aged 95) and was recognised as a veteran; María Arias Bernal defended Madero's grave under Huerta; Elena Arizmendi Mejía founded the Neutral White Cross (1911) when the Red Cross refused to treat revolutionaries. [source: SOL; MR]
- Hermila Galindo (b. 1886, Lerdo, Durango), Carranza's private secretary; founded *La Mujer Moderna* (1915); stood for Congress in 1917 (the historian Gabriela Cano calls it the first time a woman contended in a Mexican election); the result was rejected. First Feminist Congress of Yucatán, 1916. [source: GAL]
- 1927 amendment restricted peasant women's right to hold ejidos in their own name unless they were "the sole support of the family unit". [source: CON]
- MR: women's role "has not been an important aspect of official historical memory"; *coronelas* who dressed and identified as men "do not fit the stereotypical image of *soldadera* and are not celebrated". [source: MR]
- Literature: Nellie Campobello's *Cartucho* (1931), Villista memory from a woman writer; Mariano Azuela's *Los de abajo* (*The Underdogs*), serialised during the war. [source: MR]

## 12. Indigenous and peasant perspectives
- Zapatismo rooted in Nahua and mestizo villages with colonial land titles; Zapata spoke Nahuatl (eyewitness). [source: ZAP]
- Yaqui and Mayo soldiers formed a core of Obregón's Sonoran forces; the Yaqui rebelled again over land in 1926–27. (Neighbour notes: the Porfiriato deported Yaqui to Yucatán; Yaqui joined all factions.) [source: OBR]
- Huerta was of Huichol (Wixárika) descent—a reminder that "Indigenous" did not mean one side. [source: HUE]
- The Zapatista slogan "Tierra y Libertad" and Zapata's image were taken up by the Zapatista Army of National Liberation (EZLN), whose Chiapas rising of 1 January 1994 followed the 1992 end of land reform and NAFTA; EZLN territory signs read "here the people command and government obeys". In Chiapas and Tepoztlán Zapata is venerated as "Votán Zapata". [source: ZAP; MR; AYA]
- Effects in the countryside were mixed: MR cites rural wages falling 25% from 1934 to 1940 while urban wages rose 20%, and ejidatarios remaining poor. [source: MR]
- Mary Kay Vaughan's study of 1930s rural schools in Puebla and Sonora (including Yaqui communities) shows policy negotiated between state, teachers and peasants rather than imposed. [source: WebSearch: https://uapress.arizona.edu/book/cultural-politics-in-revolution]

## 13. The muralists, culture and memory
- Obregón's education minister José Vasconcelos (1921–24) commissioned murals on colonial-era public buildings; the first state project was at the Escuela Nacional Preparatoria (San Ildefonso), where Orozco painted *The Trench* among others (1922–24); Rivera's first government mural *Creation* (1922). "Los Tres Grandes": Diego Rivera, José Clemente Orozco, David Alfaro Siqueiros; Rivera "utopian", Orozco "critical and pessimistic", Siqueiros the most radical. Dr. Atl (Gerardo Murillo) had backed Carranza and encouraged Orozco and Siqueiros; Orozco worked for the Constitutionalist/Red Battalion side; Siqueiros served in the Constitutionalist Army. [source: MUR; MR; ORO; SIQ; CAR]
- Rivera's Chapingo chapel mural (1923–27) shows the bodies of Zapata and Otilio Montaño in the earth fertilising the maize; his National Palace murals of Mexican history were painted 1929–1945 (date from image captions). All three were communists; Siqueiros took part in the 1940 armed attack on Trotsky. [source: RIV; MUR; SIQ]
- Scholars divide the movement into a "heroic" phase (to 1924/1930) and a "statist" phase; "hard liners" see later murals as "propaganda for a corrupt state" (Mary Coffey's terms). [source: MUR]
- Vasconcelos's *La raza cósmica* (The Cosmic Race) praised mestizaje as humanity's future. [source: VAS]
- Corridos ("La Adelita", "La Cucaracha") served as news and propaganda; photography (Casasola archive) and film recorded the war. [source: MR; WebSearch on Poniatowska/Casasola]
- Memory: the popular heroes are "the two radicals who lost", Zapata and Villa. The state appropriated Zapata's memory from 1921; Villa was excluded until his remains entered the Monument to the Revolution (VIL: 1976; MR: 1979—say "in the 1970s"). The monument (repurposed from Díaz's unfinished legislative palace in 1933) holds the remains of Madero, Carranza, Calles, Cárdenas and Villa—men who fought one another. Huerta is the enduring villain. [source: MR; VIL]
- Knight: the memory of the revolution became a kind of "secular religion" legitimising the ruling party. PRI ruled 1929–2000; Cuauhtémoc Cárdenas's breakaway in 1988 and the Party of the Democratic Revolution (PRD) contested its claim to the revolution. Vicente Fox (2000) presented himself as heir to Madero. [source: MR]

## Debates
- **Orthodox vs revisionist (and Knight's response).** The "old orthodoxy", rooted in Frank Tannenbaum (*The Mexican Agrarian Revolution*, 1929/1930; *Peace by Revolution*, 1933) and echoed in the post-1920 regime's own story, saw a popular, agrarian, nationalist revolution. MR: Knight "has identified 'orthodox' interpretation of the revolution as a monolithic, popular, nationalist revolution, while revisionism has focused on regional differences, and challenges its credentials [as a] revolution". Revisionists (from the late 1960s–80s; regional studies such as Joseph, Ankerson, O'Malley named in reviews; Ruiz) stressed elite and middle-class leadership, regional variety and the building of a strong state that crushed popular movements. Knight's two volumes (1986) and essays ("The Mexican Revolution: Bourgeois? Nationalist? Or Just a 'Great Rebellion'?", *Bulletin of Latin American Research* 4:2, 1985; "Viewpoint: Revisionism and Revolution", *Past & Present* 134, 1992) defended the view that it was a genuinely popular, largely agrarian movement that brought real change, while accepting regional complexity—sometimes called "neo-populist" or "post-revisionist" (label not confirmed in sources read; avoid). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Revolution; WebSearch results: https://forum.lasaweb.org/files/vol41-issue4/debates-1.pdf; https://es.scribd.com/document/83177696/Alan-Knight-Interpreting-the-Mexican-Revolution; https://moderna.historicas.unam.mx/index.php/ehm/article/download/3016/2572/2968]
- **"A great rebellion"?** Ramón Eduardo Ruiz, *The Great Rebellion: Mexico, 1905–1924* (1980), argued it was not a true social revolution but "a long violent specifically bourgeois reform which crushed other populist revolts for the sake of better business" (RUIZ's paraphrase of the reception) — a "great rebellion" unworthy to be compared with the French or Russian revolutions. Knight's 1985 article title answers him. Wasserman notes Ruiz relied on secondary sources predating the regional-history boom. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ram%C3%B3n_Eduardo_Ruiz; WebSearch: https://forum.lasaweb.org/files/vol41-issue4/debates-1.pdf]
- **Where Womack fits (writer: do not file him as a revisionist).** Womack's *Zapata* (1968) is a sympathetic history of villagers' revolution that "inspired many other scholars to pursue projects on grassroots rural history" — closer to the popular reading, though local rather than national. Tannenbaum is the founder of the orthodox reading, not a revisionist. The prompt's grouping "revisionists (Womack, Ruiz, Tannenbaum and others)" is therefore inaccurate; the revisionist camp is better represented by Ruiz and the regional historians, with Jean Meyer's Cristero work as a challenge from the Catholic countryside. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Womack; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Tannenbaum; WebSearch result https://moderna.historicas.unam.mx/index.php/ehm/article/download/3016/2572/2968]
- **Marxist readings:** Adolfo Gilly's *La revolución interrumpida* (1971) saw a peasant revolution "interrupted" by the bourgeois Constitutionalists. [source: WebSearch results on Gilly]
- **International dimension:** Katz, *The Secret War in Mexico* (1981), showed that "foreign activities figured crucially in the Revolution's course, not simple antagonism from the U.S. government, but complicated Euro-American imperialist rivalries". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Revolution]
- **When did it end?** 1917, 1920, 1940 or 1946 (see §1). [source: MR]
- **Cristeros:** church–state conflict or the last great peasant rising? Many Cristeros also wanted land; regional studies (Jennie Purnell on Michoacán agraristas vs Cristeros; Matthew Butler) show peasant communities split. [source: CRI]
- **Cárdenas:** moral conscience of the revolution (Krauze: "an icon, a kind of moral *Jefe Máximo*") vs architect of the corporatist party that underpinned PRI hegemony. [source: CAR2]
- **Muralism:** "heroic" art of the people vs "propaganda for a corrupt state" after 1930 (Coffey's soft/hard lines; Folgarait). [source: MUR]
- **Huerta:** usually a self-serving dictator; one biographer argues he pursued reformist policies until October 1913. [source: MR; HUE]
