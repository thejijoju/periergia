## 1. Guatemala: the "Ten Years of Spring" and the 1954 coup
- The Guatemalan Revolution (1944–54) is also known as "the Ten Years of Spring". Protests against the dictator Jorge Ubico began at the university in May 1944 and, joined by middle-class people, junior officers, students and workers, forced his resignation at the end of June 1944. [source: GREV; ARB]
- Ubico had made large concessions to the United Fruit Company (UFC), including 200,000 ha of public land and tax exemptions; the UFC was Guatemala's largest landowner and employer, controlled the railways, docks and communications, and virtually owned Puerto Barrios, the only Atlantic port. Ubico admired Mussolini and Hitler. [source: GUA]
- Juan José Arévalo won the 1944 election; the 1945 constitution charged the state with protecting communal land. The largely free 1950 election, won by Árbenz, was the first transfer of power between democratically elected leaders in Guatemala. [source: D900; GUA]
- Árbenz (14 September 1913 – 27 January 1971) was president 1951–54. He had personal ties to some members of the communist Guatemalan Party of Labour (PGT), which was legalised under him, but did not try to build a communist state, choosing "a moderate capitalist approach"; the PGT committed itself to working within the legal framework. Congress observed a minute's silence when Stalin died in 1953. [source: GUA; ARB]
- **Decree 900** (Agrarian Reform Law), passed 17 June 1952, redistributed uncultivated land on large estates; it abolished unpaid labour, labour as rent and forced relocation of Indigenous workers; owners were compensated in 25-year bonds valued on their own previous tax declarations. Local Agrarian Committees handled petitions. [source: D900]
- By 1954 about 1.4 million acres had been expropriated and about 100,000 families (about 500,000 people, one-sixth of the population) had received land plus credit (Gleijeses; Harbour). Árbenz's own land (1,700 acres) was expropriated. Corn, coffee and banana output rose in 1953–54. Indigenous Guatemalans were major beneficiaries, though Trefzger argues Ladinos benefited disproportionately. [source: D900]
- In 1953 the government expropriated about 234,000 acres of uncultivated UFC land (estimates of UFC's losses range 178,000–653,000 acres). Historians differ on the relative weight of UFC's interests versus anticommunism in US motives (Streeter). [source: D900]
- Truman authorised Operation PBFortune in 1952 (aborted); Eisenhower authorised **PBSuccess** in August 1953. The US "drew exaggerated conclusions about the extent of communist influence among Árbenz's advisers". CIA planning included assassination lists of over 58 Guatemalans; the CIA later said none were carried out and that it had lost the records, which journalists such as Annie Jacobsen doubt. [source: GUA]
- In 1954 Árbenz secretly bought arms from Czechoslovakia — what would have been the first Soviet-bloc arms shipment to the Americas, which Washington presented as a communist beachhead. [source: GUA]
- Local resistance was real: a 122-man rebel column at Zacapa was beaten by 30 soldiers; at Puerto Barrios police and armed dockworkers repelled the attackers. Air raids with improvised bombs caused little damage but had a large psychological effect. Árbenz resigned on 27 June 1954. [source: GUA; global-cold-war research notes]
- Castillo Armas became president on 8 July 1954, repealed Decree 900 and reversed about 95% of the redistribution; all UFC land was returned. His government executed many Árbenz supporters — estimates range from hundreds to 5,000. He won a single-candidate election with 99% and was assassinated by a member of his guard in 1957. [source: D900; GUA]
- Che Guevara, aged 25, was in Guatemala City during the coup, sheltered in the Argentine embassy and went on to Mexico, where he joined the Cuban revolutionaries. [source: GUA]
- Árbenz went into exile; his family fell apart and his daughter died by suicide; he died in Mexico City in 1971. On 20 October 2011 President Álvaro Colom formally apologised to his son, calling the coup "a crime to Guatemalan society and ... an act of aggression to a government starting its democratic spring". [source: ARB; GUA]

## 2. Guatemala's civil war and the genocide of the Maya
- The civil war ran from 1960 to 1996 between the government and leftist rebel groups (the CEH page dates its start to 1962). Peace accords with the URNG guerrilla coalition were signed on 29 December 1996 under President Álvaro Arzú. [source: GCW; CEH]
- **Toll — give as a range:** GCW: 140,000–200,000 killed or disappeared by war's end; also "40,000 to 200,000 ... including 40,000 to 50,000 disappearances"; GEN: "an estimated 200,000 ... including at least 40,000" enforced disappearances; CEH: "over 200,000 killed or disappeared". The CEH itself registered 42,275 named victims (23,671 arbitrary executions, 6,159 forced disappearances). David Stoll argues the commission's projections are too high (citing a high-end estimate of 40,000–60,000 for its counted cases). Recommended wording: "about 200,000 killed or disappeared, according to the UN-backed truth commission; some scholars put it lower." [source: GCW; GEN; CEH]
- Death squads: the MANO/Mano Blanca ("White Hand"), formed June 1966, was absorbed into the state's counter-terror apparatus. [source: GCW]
- **Panzós, 29 May 1978:** Q'eqchi' peasants from several villages marched into the town square to press land claims and were massacred by the army (Grandin: "some three dozen" killed). Systematic killing of Indigenous people escalated afterwards. Grandin's *The Last Colonial Massacre* opens and closes with it. [source: GEN; WebSearch: https://notevenpast.org/last-colonial-massacre-latin-america-cold-war/ ; https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/L/bo11643711.html]
- **Spanish Embassy, 31 January 1980:** displaced K'iche' and Ixil peasants occupied the Spanish Embassy in Guatemala City to protest army killings in Uspantán; among those who died was Vicente Menchú, father of Rigoberta Menchú; his funeral drew hundreds of thousands. (Death count not verified here.) [source: GCW]
- Rigoberta Menchú's memoir *I, Rigoberta Menchú* (1983) gained worldwide attention; in 1999 she, as a Nobel Peace laureate, filed a complaint in Spain's Audiencia Nacional for genocide and torture against Ríos Montt and other generals. [source: GCW; RM]
- The army carried out at least 626 massacres against the Maya; the army itself acknowledged destroying 440 Maya villages between 1981 and 1983. Most state massacres occurred 1981–83. REMHI (the Catholic Church's report) lists 422 massacres. [source: GEN]
- **Ríos Montt** (16 June 1926 – 1 April 2018), de facto president 1982–83, an evangelical; he told Reagan the strategy was not "scorched earth" but "scorched Communists". Carlos Sabino argues the highland counter-insurgency began in late 1981, before his coup, and that massacres peaked in May 1982 and then fell. [source: RM]
- Reagan visited Ríos Montt in December 1982 (meeting in Honduras) and said he was getting a "bum rap"; in January 1983 Elliott Abrams defended resuming military aid, blaming massacres "on the guerrillas". [source: GCW]
- **CEH (Commission for Historical Clarification)**, established in 1994, worked February 1997 – February 1999 with three commissioners (a Guatemalan man, a non-national man and a Maya woman); its 12-volume report *Memory of Silence* (*Memoria del Silencio*; K'iche' title *Tz'inil Na'tab'al*) was presented on 25 February 1999. Findings: 93% of violations by state forces and allied paramilitaries, 3% by insurgents; 83% of fully identified victims Maya, 17% Ladino. [source: CEH]
- **Genocide finding:** the CEH concluded that "agents of the State of Guatemala, within the framework of counterinsurgency operations carried out between 1981 and 1983, committed acts of genocide against groups of Mayan people which lived in the four regions analysed"; 1981–83 accounts for 81% of the violations it recorded. [source: WebSearch: https://www.ictj.org/sites/default/files/subsites/challenging-conventional-truth-commissions-peace/guatemala.html ; https://www.ca1.uscourts.gov/sites/ca1/files/citations/Guatemala%20Memory%20of%20Silence%20Report%20of%20the%20Commission%20for%20Historical%20Clarification%20Conclusions%20and%20Recommendations.pdf]
- Clinton apologised in Guatemala in March 1999 for US support of forces that committed atrocities, soon after the CEH report documented that support. [source: GUA (NYT, 11 March 1999)]
- Bishop Juan Gerardi, head of the Archdiocese's human rights office (REMHI report *Guatemala: Nunca Más*), was murdered in 1998. [source: RM; CEH]
- **Ríos Montt trial:** trial for the genocide of at least 1,771 Ixil Maya began March 2013; on 10 May 2013 he was convicted of genocide and crimes against humanity and sentenced to 80 years (50 + 30) — the first former head of state tried for genocide by his own country's courts. Ten days later the Constitutional Court overturned the verdict; his retrial was unfinished when he died on 1 April 2018. Some peace-accord signatories (e.g., former vice-presidents Eduardo Stein and Luis Flores Asturias) publicly called the genocide charge "a juridical fabrication". [source: GEN; GCW; RM]

## 3. Cuba: revolution, its critics, and the missile crisis from Havana
- 26 July 1953: Fidel and Raúl Castro attacked the Moncada Barracks in Santiago; the attack failed; Castro's trial speech was published as *History Will Absolve Me*. Haydée Santamaría and Melba Hernández were the only women in the attack. The group took the name "26th of July Movement" on 12 June 1955. Batista amnestied the attackers on 15 May 1955. [source: CR; FC]
- The *Granma* landing (December 1956): no more than about twenty of the 82 men survived the first encounters and reached the Sierra Maestra. Celia Sánchez and Vilma Espín were leading strategists; Frank País led the urban underground until his death in 1957. [source: CR]
- Batista fled to the Dominican Republic on 1 January 1959. By then the revolution was a coalition of the Popular Socialist Party, the 26th of July Movement and the Revolutionary Directorate. [source: CR]
- Estimates of those killed by Batista's forces range from 1,000 to 20,000. Revolutionary tribunals executed "several hundred" people in 1959 (CHE; FC: "hundreds of executions"); at La Cabaña, which Guevara commanded, 55–105 executions fell under his jurisdiction. [source: CHE; FC]
- May 1959 First Agrarian Reform capped holdings at 993 acres and barred foreign ownership. After the US cut Cuban sugar imports, Cuba nationalised most US-owned assets (August–October 1960); the US froze assets, broke relations and tightened its embargo in January 1961. [source: FC; CR]
- 1961 literacy campaign: about 105,664 student volunteers (Conrado Benítez brigades); about 1,000,000 Cubans took part as teachers or learners; literacy reached 96% by 1962 (UNESCO). [source: CLC]
- Bay of Pigs: about 1,400 Cuban exiles of Brigade 2506, trained by the CIA in Guatemala and launched from Nicaragua, landed 17 April 1961 and surrendered 20 April; about 1,202 were captured and later exchanged (1,113 prisoners) for US$53 million in food and medicine. Castro publicly identified as a Marxist–Leninist from December 1961. [source: BOP; FC]
- Critics: in June 1961 Castro's "Words to the Intellectuals" set limits on culture; from 1965 gay men were sent to UMAP labour camps (closed 1967); in 1971 the arrest of the poet Heberto Padilla drew international condemnation from former sympathisers. Huber Matos, a rebel commander, was among early dissidents (detail not checked). [source: FC]
- Emigration: the first wave of "Golden exiles" (1959–62) was mainly upper and middle class; the **Mariel boatlift** (15 April – 31 October 1980) brought about 125,000 Cubans to Florida. [source: CR; MAR]
- The 26th of July Movement became the Communist Party of Cuba in October 1965. [source: CR]
- Cuban "export" of revolution: in 1959 Cuba backed unsuccessful landings in Panama and the Dominican Republic; in July 1964 the OAS sanctioned Cuba after a cache of arms destined for Venezuela's FALN guerrillas was found on the Venezuelan coast (Cuba had been excluded from the inter-American system at Punta del Este in January 1962; Mexico, Brazil and Argentina objected to sanctions); in May 1967 Cuban troops landing at Machurucuto, Venezuela, were captured; Castro proclaimed armed solidarity at the 1966 Tricontinental in Havana. Guevara called for "many Vietnams" and was executed in La Higuera, Bolivia, on 9 October 1967. [source: FIC (a partisan-toned article; use only these basic facts); CHE; WebSearch: https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/22675553612DB09913F9BA6CB6FA823D/core-reader]
- Missile crisis: cross-refer to the Global Cold War chapter. From the Cuban side, note the October Crisis name, the sabotage campaign (Operation Mongoose) that preceded it, and that Khrushchev settled without consulting Castro (the last point not re-verified here — see NOT VERIFIED). [source: global-cold-war research notes, CMC]

## 4. The Alliance for Progress
- Proposed by Kennedy on 13 March 1961; charter signed at Punta del Este, Uruguay, in August 1961. The US would supply or guarantee $20 billion over ten years; Latin American governments pledged $80 billion of investment; target: 2.5% annual growth in per-capita income. Critics noted $20 billion was only about $10 per Latin American. [source: AFP]
- Kennedy at the first anniversary (13 March 1962, White House): "Those who make peaceful revolution impossible will make violent revolution inevitable." (Encyclopedia.com gives a variant, "Those who make democracy impossible will make revolution inevitable" — use the first, attributed to the anniversary address.) [source: WebSearch: https://jfklibrary.org/asset-viewer/archives/jfkpof-037-026 ; https://wist.info/kennedy-john/2247/]
- During the 1960s thirteen constitutional governments in Latin America were replaced by military regimes; by the early 1970s the programme was widely seen as a failure; the OAS disbanded its permanent committee in 1973. Peter Smith: "The most striking failure of the Alliance of Progress occurred within the political realm." [source: AFP]
- A 2022 *Journal of Cold War Studies* article ("Making Peaceful Revolution Impossible") argues Kennedy undermined his own goals by thwarting democratic left-wing reformers. [source: WebSearch: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/843080]
- Other US interventions of the decade (brief): Trujillo was killed in May 1961 with CIA-supplied weapons; Juan Bosch, freely elected December 1962, was overthrown in September 1963; Johnson sent troops into the 1965 Dominican civil war; Balaguer beat Bosch in 1966. [source: USLA]

## 5. Brazil, 1964–1985
- 31 March – 1 April 1964: the military overthrew President João Goulart, whose "base reforms" (including land reform) stalled in Congress; Congress declared his office vacant on 2 April while he was still in Brazil; he arrived in exile on 4 April. Gen. Humberto Castelo Branco took office on 15 April. Military presidents followed until 1985. [source: BR64]
- US role: Operation **Brother Sam** prepared tankers, munitions and a naval task force led by the carrier USS Forrestal; Castelo Branco told the Americans logistical support was not needed and it was stood down. Ambassador Lincoln Gordon feared Brazil might become "the China of the 1960s"; in 1977 he admitted US involvement; in 2005 he said he had no more evidence of Goulart's dictatorial plans than press rumours. Historians (e.g., Carlos Fico) find no firm evidence Goulart planned a coup. [source: BR64; BRMD]
- AI-5 (Institutional Act No. 5), issued by Costa e Silva on 13 December 1968 — "the coup within the coup" — suspended habeas corpus and enabled arbitrary detention, torture and killing. [source: AI5]
- The "economic miracle" of 1968–73 came with rising income concentration, followed by crisis in the 1980s. [source: BR64]
- Journalist Vladimir Herzog was murdered in DOI-CODI custody (1975), showing torture continued under Geisel's "opening". [source: BRMD]
- Amnesty Law signed by Figueiredo on 28 August 1979 covered "political" and "related" crimes 1961–78 on both sides; no perpetrator has been punished; the Inter-American Court of Human Rights has condemned it. The 1984 *Diretas Já* amendment for direct elections failed; Congress chose Tancredo Neves; José Sarney took office in March 1985. [source: BRMD]
- **Toll:** 434 confirmed killed or disappeared and about 20,000 tortured (BRMD); human-rights groups say the figure should include thousands of Indigenous people, which the armed forces dispute. [source: BRMD]
- **Comissão Nacional da Verdade (CNV):** created 2011; final report delivered to President Dilma Rousseff on 10 December 2014: 434 dead and disappeared (up from an official 362; it stresses these are only proven cases), 377 state agents named as responsible for crimes against humanity; Volume II estimates at least 8,350 Indigenous people killed. It also said the United States had taught torture techniques to Brazil's military (via BRMD). [source: WebSearch: https://hrw.org/news/2014/12/10/brazil-panel-details-dirty-war-atrocities ; https://www.congressoemfoco.com.br/noticia/53424/comissao-aponta-434-mortos-e-desaparecidos-na-ditadura ; https://cimi.org.br/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Report_Brazil_memorytruth_g2511616.pdf; BRMD]

## 6. Chile: Allende and Pinochet
- 4 September 1970: Allende (Popular Unity) won 36.6% to Jorge Alessandri's 35.3% and Radomiro Tomic's 27.8–28.1%; Congress confirmed him on 24 October. [source: ALL; CH73]
- US reaction: at the 40 Committee on 27 June 1970 Kissinger is recorded as saying he did not see why the US should "stand by and watch a country go communist due to the irresponsibility of its own people" (NSA cites another memo with "sit around and let a country go communist" — wording varies). On 15 September 1970 Helms's handwritten notes of a meeting with Nixon read "$10,000,000 available, more if necessary ... make the economy scream". Track I sought to block Allende via Congress; Track II sought a military coup. [source: WebSearch: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d41 ; https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/chile/2020-09-15/extreme-option-overthrow-allende ; https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d93; ALL]
- General René Schneider, a "constitutionalist", was shot resisting a kidnap attempt on 20 October 1970 by a group led by Gen. Roberto Viaux; he died, and his death briefly ended military opposition to Allende. [source: ALL]
- Popular Unity nationalised copper (over half of export receipts) and banking; inflation, cut to about 14% in early 1971, reached 140–150% in 1972; black markets spread; truckers' strike from October 1972; the "Tanquetazo" coup attempt, 29 June 1973. The CIA funded the opposition press; El Mercurio received at least $1.5 million (Church Committee). [source: ALL; WebSearch: https://www.aarclibrary.org/publib/church/reports/vol7/pdf/ChurchV7_4_Treverton.pdf ; https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve16/d145]
- 11 September 1973: the armed forces overthrew Allende; he gave a last radio address and died by suicide in La Moneda (confirmed by a 2011 exhumation; a judge so ruled in December 2011). [source: ALL; CH73]
- US responsibility: a 2000 US intelligence report (echoing the Church Committee) says the CIA "did not instigate the coup" but was aware of plotting; Kissinger told Nixon "we didn't do it" but "we helped them"; the Chilean historian Sebastián Hurtado finds no documentary evidence of active US coordination of the coup itself. [source: CH73; ALL; MDC]
- Repression: tens of thousands held in the National Stadium; the singer Víctor Jara was tortured and killed; the "Caravan of Death" killed about 70 more. Cardinal Raúl Silva Henríquez founded COPACHI in response to the repression; DINA's Contreras threatened him. At least 200,000 Chileans (about 2% of the population) went into exile. [source: CH73; MDC]
- DINA (under Manuel Contreras) killed Gen. Carlos Prats in Buenos Aires (1974) and Orlando Letelier and Ronni Moffitt by car bomb in Washington (1976). [source: COND; MDC]
- **Toll, as a range:** Rettig Report (National Commission for Truth and Reconciliation, set up by President Aylwin, finalised February 1991): 2,279 victims — 2,115 of human-rights violations and 164 of political violence (1,068 killed, 957 disappeared after arrest, 90 killed by private citizens); Aylwin apologised on behalf of the state. Valech Report (2004–05): 35,865 testified, 27,255 recognised as political prisoners/torture victims; Valech II (released 26 August 2011): total of 38,254 political prisoners, most tortured; in 2011 the state recognised 3,095 killed or disappeared and 36,948 torture survivors. Recommended wording: "about 3,000 killed or disappeared and some 38,000 political prisoners, most tortured". [source: RET; VAL; MDC]
- The **1980 Constitution** (author Jaime Guzmán) provided for a single-candidate plebiscite; on 5 October 1988, 56% voted "No" to Pinochet; Aylwin took office 11 March 1990; Pinochet stayed army commander until March 1998, then senator for life. [source: PIN; MDC]
- Pinochet was arrested in London in October 1998 on a Spanish warrant; he returned to Chile in March 2000; at his death on 10 December 2006 about 300 criminal charges were pending (human rights, tax evasion, embezzlement; Riggs Bank accounts). [source: PIN; MDC]
- **Chicago Boys:** Chilean economists trained at the University of Chicago (Friedman, Harberger, Sjaastad) through a Catholic University exchange; their blueprint *El ladrillo* ("the brick") dates to 1969; from 1975 Pinochet gave them key posts (Friedman's memoir). "Shock therapy": austerity, deregulation, privatisation. By 1982 GDP fell by roughly 15% in a severe crisis, and inequality widened; the "Chilean miracle" gave the regime international legitimacy. Alejandro Foxley: about 44% of families were below the poverty line by the end of the regime. [source: CB; PIN]

## 7. Argentina, 1976–1983
- Before the coup: Isabel Perón's minister José López Rega organised the Triple A death squad; CONADEP counted 458 assassinations 1973–75 (19, 50, 359). Guerrillas (Montoneros, ERP) also killed and kidnapped (e.g., executives; Aramburu, 1970). Videla, 1975: "As many people as necessary must die in Argentina so that the country will again be secure." [source: DW]
- 24 March 1976: junta of Gen. Jorge Videla, Adm. Emilio Massera and Brig. Orlando Agosti; the "National Reorganization Process". [source: DW]
- Methods: about 340 secret detention centres (one estimate: 12,000 prisoners); some 3,000 deaths at the Navy Mechanics School (ESMA) alone; "death flights" dropped prisoners into the South Atlantic — exposed by the naval officer Adolfo Scilingo, later convicted in Spain. [source: DW]
- **The disappeared — range:** CONADEP documented 8,961 forced disappearances (1976–83), noting the true number is higher; a 1978 Argentine intelligence (601st Battalion) estimate in a DINA cable, published by John Dinges (2004), gave 22,000 killed or disappeared since 1975; human-rights organisations give 30,000. "An estimated 13,000 to 30,000" is a fair range. About 30% of the disappeared were women. President Macri was condemned in 2016 for questioning the 30,000 figure. [source: CON; DW]
- Stolen children: the junta justified taking newborns because "subversive parents will raise subversive children". The Grandmothers (Abuelas) of the Plaza de Mayo, founded 1977, estimate up to 500 children were appropriated; the 140th grandchild was identified on 7 July 2025 (born 17 April 1977 in "La Escuelita", Bahía Blanca); about 300 remain missing. [source: DW; WebSearch: https://misionesonline.net/2025/07/07/abuelas-de-plaza-de-mayo-restitucion-nieto-140/ ; https://www.lacapital.com.ar/politica/quien-es-el-nieto-140-nacio-cautiverio-y-su-hermana-lo-busco-toda-la-vida-n10205779.html]
- **Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo:** on 30 April 1977 Azucena Villaflor and about a dozen mothers walked to the Plaza de Mayo; they marched every Thursday in defiance of the ban on assembly, wearing white headscarves (originally nappies) embroidered with their children's names. In December 1977 Villaflor, Esther Ballestrino and María Ponce de Bianco, and the French nuns Alice Domon and Léonie Duquet, were disappeared; DNA later identified their remains, and Villaflor's ashes were buried in the Plaza in December 2005. The movement split in 1986 (the Founding Line vs Hebe de Bonafini's association). [source: MPM]
- US: Congress approved military aid in 1976; Kissinger congratulated the junta for "wiping out terrorism" and attended the 1978 World Cup as Videla's guest; Patricia Derian (Carter's human-rights official): "It sickened me that with an imperial wave of his hand, an American could sentence people to death." [source: COND; DW]
- Defeat in the Falklands/Malvinas War (1982) forced the military out; Raúl Alfonsín took office on 10 December 1983. [source: DW]
- **CONADEP** (December 1983, chaired by the writer Ernesto Sábato): report ***Nunca Más*** delivered 20 September 1984, based on 50,000 pages of testimony; continuously in print. [source: CON]
- **Trial of the Juntas** opened 22 April 1985 (prosecutors Julio César Strassera and Luis Moreno Ocampo); on 9 December 1985 Videla and Massera got life, Viola 17 years, Lambruschini 8, Agosti 4½; the other four were acquitted. Called the only such large-scale trial of a former dictatorship by a democratic government in Latin America. [source: TJ]
- Full Stop (1986) and Due Obedience (1987) laws halted prosecutions; Menem pardoned the convicted in 1989–90; Congress repealed the laws in 2003 and the Supreme Court declared them unconstitutional in 2005; trials resumed in 2006; Videla's pardon was struck down in 2007. [source: DW; TJ]

## 8. Uruguay and Operation Condor
- Uruguay: the Tupamaro urban guerrillas (who killed US adviser Dan Mitrione in 1970; over 100 escaped prison in September 1971) prompted army-led counter-insurgency; President Juan María Bordaberry's self-coup of 27 June 1973 began a "civic-military" dictatorship that lasted to 1 March 1985. Uruguay had the highest number of political prisoners per capita in the world; more than 5,000 were arrested for political reasons and almost 10% of Uruguayans emigrated. About 180 Uruguayans are known to have been killed, most in Argentina and neighbouring countries (36 in Uruguay). [source: URU]
- In 1980 voters rejected the military's constitution by 57.2% — "the start of a negotiated return to democracy". About 500,000 rallied in Montevideo on 27 November 1983. The 1986 Expiry Law (*Ley de Caducidad*) shielded the military; voters upheld it in referendums in 1989 and 2009. Bordaberry was held under house arrest and died in 2011. Former Tupamaro José Mujica was president 2010–15. [source: URU]
- **Operation Condor** was formally created in November 1975 when DINA's Manuel Contreras hosted about 50 intelligence officers from Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Paraguay and Uruguay in Santiago; Ecuador and Peru later joined peripherally; it ran 1975–83. The CIA kept Contreras as a paid contact until 1977. Victims included Prats, Letelier and Moffitt, the former Chilean interior minister Bernardo Leighton (attacked 1975), former Bolivian president Juan José Torres and two former Uruguayan legislators murdered in Buenos Aires. Paraguay's "Archives of Terror" were found in 1992. [source: COND]
- **Condor's toll — handle carefully:** COND reports claims of "at least 60,000 deaths" or "50,000 killed, 30,000 disappeared and 400,000 imprisoned" (via the Paraguayan archives) — these lump together all the regimes' repression. Patrice McSherry estimates 400–500 killed in cross-border Condor operations proper. Say: "the regimes linked by Condor killed tens of thousands; cross-border Condor operations themselves killed several hundred." [source: COND; DW]

## 9. Central America: El Salvador
- A coup on 15 October 1979 and killings of protesters are widely seen as the start of the civil war; the FMLN was formed in October 1980 from five guerrilla groups and launched its offensive on 10 January 1981. Peace: Chapultepec Accords, Mexico City, 16 January 1992. [source: SCW; TCES]
- Toll: the UN reports more than 75,000 killed (1979–92) and about 8,000 disappeared; more than a quarter of the population was displaced. [source: SCW]
- US aid: Carter's outgoing administration raised military aid; by 1984 Reagan's government spent nearly $1 billion in economic aid; overall nearly $3 billion in US economic assistance while per-capita income fell by a third. The CIA financed Duarte's 1984 campaign (about $2 million) to block D'Aubuisson. [source: SCW]
- **Óscar Romero** (15 August 1917 – 24 March 1980), appointed archbishop of San Salvador 3 February 1977 as a conservative, was changed by the murder of his friend the Jesuit Rutilio Grande (12 March 1977): "If they have killed him for doing what he did, then I too have to walk the same path." In February 1980 he wrote to Carter opposing military aid. In his homily of 23 March 1980 he told soldiers that no soldier must obey an order contrary to God's law: "In the name of God ... I beg you, I beseech you, I order you: stop the repression!" (translations vary). He was shot while saying Mass in a hospital chapel the next day. The 1993 UN Truth Commission named Roberto D'Aubuisson (founder of ARENA, 1981) as having ordered the killing. Canonised by Pope Francis on 14 October 2018. [source: ROM; WebSearch: https://www.vaticannews.va/en/saints/03/24/st--oscar-romero--martyr.print.html ; https://diario.elmundo.sv/nacionales/les-suplico-les-ruego-les-ordeno-en-nombre-de-dios-cese-la-represion-la-ultima-homilia-dominical-de-monsenor-romero]
- **El Mozote**, Morazán, 11–12 December 1981: the US-trained Atlácatl Battalion under Domingo Monterrosa killed more than 811 civilians — "the largest massacre in the Americas in modern times"; survivor Rufina Amaya lost her husband and four children. Raymond Bonner (NYT) and Alma Guillermoprieto (Washington Post) reported it in January 1982; Assistant Secretary Thomas Enders told the Senate there was "no evidence" of a systematic massacre; Bonner was recalled. El Salvador's government apologised in December 2011. [source: MOZ]
- 16 November 1989: Atlácatl soldiers killed six Jesuit priests (including rector Ignacio Ellacuría) at the Central American University. [source: SCW]
- **Truth Commission for El Salvador** (UN; July 1992 – March 1993), report *From Madness to Hope* (15 March 1993), 22,000 testimonies; overall the UN attributed about 85% of atrocities to state forces and allied death squads and 5% to the FMLN. Five days later (20 March 1993) the government passed a blanket amnesty. [source: TCES; SCW]
- The School of the Americas trained many Salvadoran officers, including D'Aubuisson and officers linked to the Jesuit murders; it was renamed WHINSEC in 2001; its 1980s Spanish-language intelligence manuals promoted torture and executions. [source: SCW; SOA]

## 10. Nicaragua: the Sandinistas and the Contras
- The newspaper editor Pedro Joaquín Chamorro, Somoza's leading critic, was murdered on 10 January 1978, sparking riots. Anastasio Somoza Debayle fled; the Sandinistas (FSLN) took power on 19 July 1979. The 1978–79 insurrection killed 10,000–50,000; 600,000 were left homeless and 150,000 became refugees. [source: NICR; CTR]
- The 1980 literacy crusade claimed to cut illiteracy from 50.3% to 12.9% in five months. The Sandinistas nationalised Somoza's properties but kept a private sector of 50–60% of GDP. [source: NICR; CTR]
- Opponents (Contras) included ex-National Guardsmen (FDN; military chief Enrique Bermúdez), disillusioned revolutionaries (Edén Pastora, "Comandante Cero", ARDE, 1982; the FDN director Édgar Chamorro, who later testified that the CIA encouraged terror tactics), and Miskito, Mayangna and Rama peoples of the Atlantic coast, who clashed with the government over land from December 1981; thousands of Miskitos fled to Honduras. A 1987 autonomy statute largely defused Miskito resistance. [source: CTR; NICR]
- The 4 November 1984 election: 75.4% turnout; Ortega won 67%; observers called it "free and fair", while the Reagan administration called it a "Soviet style sham" (the main opposition coalition under Arturo Cruz did not take part — boycott detail not verified here). [source: NICR; CTR]
- The CIA mined Nicaraguan harbours and attacked refineries; the Boland Amendment (December 1982, extended later) barred funding. In *Nicaragua v. United States* (judgment 27 June 1986) the International Court of Justice found the US had violated international law by supporting the Contras and mining the harbours; the US blocked enforcement in the Security Council. [source: CTR]
- **Iran–Contra:** officials secretly trafficked arms to Iran (1981–86) and diverted proceeds to the Contras in defiance of Boland; revealed November 1986 by the Lebanese magazine *Ash-Shiraa*; eleven convictions (some overturned, e.g., Oliver North's); Elliott Abrams pleaded to withholding evidence; George H. W. Bush pardoned Weinberger and five others on 24 December 1992. [source: IC]
- Contra War toll 1981–90: 32,000–43,000 killed (NICR infobox; total for 1978–90 42,000–78,000). Peace: Sapoá (1988) and Tela accords; Contra demobilisation from December 1989. In February 1990 Violeta Chamorro (UNO) beat Ortega 55% to 41% after the White House said the embargo would continue unless she won; she took office 25 April 1990. [source: NICR; CTR]

## 11. Liberation theology and the churches
- The Peruvian priest Gustavo Gutiérrez (8 June 1928 – 22 October 2024) coined "theology of liberation" (outline at Chimbote, July 1968; book *A Theology of Liberation*, 1971). Other exponents: Leonardo Boff (Brazil), Jon Sobrino (El Salvador), Juan Luis Segundo (Uruguay), Frei Betto. Key idea: the "preferential option for the poor"; it drove grassroots "base ecclesial communities". The episcopal conferences at Medellín (1968) and Puebla (1979) backed the "option for the poor". [source: LT; GG]
- In 1984 the Vatican's Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith under Cardinal Ratzinger criticised its use of Marxist analysis while accepting the term itself as "thoroughly valid". [source: LT; GG]
- Churches as protectors: Chile's COPACHI; Archbishop Romero; Guatemala's REMHI report; Bishop Gerardi's murder (above). Many victims of the repression were priests, nuns and catechists (COND victim list). [source: MDC; COND; RM]

## 12. Return to democracy (summary dates)
- Argentina December 1983; Uruguay March 1985; Brazil March 1985 (indirect; first direct presidential election 1989 — not re-verified); Chile March 1990 after the 1988 plebiscite; Nicaragua's elected transfer April 1990; El Salvador peace 1992; Guatemala peace December 1996. [source: DW; URU; BRMD; MDC; CTR; SCW; GCW]

## Debates
- **Who drove Latin America's Cold War?** Older "radical"/revisionist accounts saw the US suppressing popular movements; conservative accounts saw US intervention vindicated by the eventual triumph of democracy and markets. Brands (2010) rejects both, presenting overlapping domestic, ideological and geopolitical struggles in which Latin Americans held centre stage, with the Cuban Revolution as a watershed that militarised the conflict; Joseph and Spenser (2008) push "bottom-up", cultural history; Harmer (2011) stresses an "inter-American" Cold War in which Havana, Santiago and Brasília were actors, not pawns (Brazil aided counter-revolution in the Southern Cone). [source: https://issforum.org/roundtables/PDF/Roundtable-XII-27.pdf ; https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/41549 ; https://muse.jhu.edu/book/68911]
- **Grandin's "two democracies":** the Cold War in Latin America as a struggle between two visions of democracy (social, participatory vs. a narrower liberal order), not liberalism vs. Soviet communism; challenges the view that US intervention "paved the way" for capitalist democracy; Panzós as "the last colonial massacre" before the genocide. Joseph (2008 event) called the era the bloodiest in the region's modern history. [source: https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/L/bo11643711.html ; https://wilsoncenter.org/event/the-cold-latin-americas-new-encounter-the-cold-war?1209672000]
- **Guatemala 1954 — bananas or anticommunism?** Historians differ on the weight of United Fruit's interests versus anticommunist conviction (Streeter's realist / revisionist / post-revisionist survey); Gleijeses and Immerman stress exaggerated perceptions of communist influence. [source: D900; GUA]
- **Chile — how much did Washington do?** US covert action 1963–73 was "extensive and continuous" (Church Committee) and Nixon ordered efforts to stop Allende in 1970; yet the 2000 US intelligence report says the CIA did not instigate the 1973 coup, and Sebastián Hurtado finds no evidence of US coordination of the coup itself. Domestic polarisation, economic crisis and Chilean actors are central in Harmer and Hurtado. [source: CH73; MDC; https://www.aarclibrary.org/publib/church/reports/vol7/pdf/ChurchV7_4_Treverton.pdf]
- **Cuba's role:** Cuba backed guerrilla movements (Venezuela's FALN, Guevara's Bolivian foco) and the Sandinistas and FMLN (US State Department claims of Cuban/Nicaraguan/Soviet arms to the FMLN); critics of US policy argue Cuban "subversion" was used to justify intervention and was exaggerated (e.g., in Chile, where a review of Harmer notes Cuban involvement went "well beyond token gestures" but was "blown out of proportion" by CIA-fed reporting). Gleijeses on Africa (already in the Global Cold War chapter) supports Cuban autonomy from Moscow. [source: FIC; SCW; WebSearch: https://www.proquest.com/docview/1323925557]
- **Brazil 1964:** whether Goulart planned his own coup (Gordon's claim, later retracted) and how far Brother Sam mattered (it was never used). [source: BR64]
- **Counting the dead:** Argentina's 8,961 documented vs. 30,000 claimed by human-rights groups (and 22,000 in the junta's own 1978 count); Guatemala's 200,000 vs. Stoll's lower estimates; Condor's "60,000" (all repression) vs. McSherry's 400–500 cross-border killings; Brazil's 434 vs. the CNV's ~8,350 Indigenous dead. [source: CON; DW; GEN; COND; BRMD; CNV search above]
- **Genocide in Guatemala:** the CEH's "acts of genocide" finding and the 2013 conviction vs. the Constitutional Court's annulment and Sabino's and some peace-signatories' rejection of the genocide label. [source: CEH; GEN; RM]
- **Chicago Boys:** "Chilean miracle" (growth, legitimacy for the regime) vs. the 1982 crash, inequality and poverty (Foxley's 44%). [source: CB; PIN]
- **Amnesty vs. justice:** Brazil (1979 law still in force), Uruguay (Caducidad upheld twice by popular vote), El Salvador (1993 amnesty), Argentina (laws annulled 2003–05 and trials resumed) — different national paths. [source: BRMD; URU; TCES; DW]
