## 1. The Ming state: Hongwu's institutions

- Zhu Yuanzhang was born in 1328 into a peasant family in Zhongli village, Haozhou (present-day Fengyang, Anhui). In 1344 his parents and a brother died in an epidemic; he became a novice monk, begging for alms, and in 1352 joined the Red Turban rebels. He took Nanjing in 1356. [source: HW]
- He founded the Ming in 1368 and reigned as the Hongwu emperor, 1368–1398. [source: MING; HW]
- In 1380 he had Chancellor Hu Weiyong executed on suspicion of conspiracy and abolished the chancellorship; emperors thereafter governed the Six Ministries directly, a precedent followed for most of the Ming. He set up the Embroidered Uniform Guard as a secret police. [source: MING]
- Purges: "approximately 100,000 people" were killed in political purges during his 30-year reign (Ebrey; Fairbank and Goldman), the largest wave in 1390, and another in 1393. Treat 100,000 as an approximate total. [source: HW; MING]
- The *weisuo* military system (from 1364) made soldiering a hereditary household obligation: each military family supplied one soldier per generation, and garrison soldiers farmed to feed themselves. By 1393 the forces comprised 326 guards and 65 battalions. The early Ming standing army exceeded one million troops. [source: HW; MING]
- The *lijia* system of village self-government was introduced in the Yangzi basin in 1371 and empire-wide in 1381. A *li* was 110 households: ten *jia* of ten households each, plus the ten wealthiest leading families, which supplied headmen in rotation to collect taxes and organise labour service. [source: HW]
- Registers: the Yellow Registers recorded households and population; the Fish-Scale Registers recorded land parcels, quality, tax quotas and ownership. [source: HW]
- Census of 1381: 59,873,305 people; 1391: 56,774,561; recount of 1393: 60,545,812, while the real population was "likely closer to 75 million" (Brook). The census was a fiscal count and people evaded it. [source: HW; POP]
- Hongwu aimed at a society of self-sufficient farming villages. Land tax was low and fixed by region (intended as about 3% of the harvest), mostly paid in kind. Peasants were given land, new land was tax-free for three years, slavery and the slave trade were banned, and about 214,000 Buddhist and 300,000 Taoist monks and nuns were returned to lay life. [source: HW]
- He distrusted merchants: he imposed heavy taxes on Suzhou, forcibly moved wealthy families (14,300 from Zhejiang and the Yingtian area to Nanjing), banned travel beyond 100 *li* (about 58 km) without a permit, and fixed prices. Merchants were excluded from the examinations: of 110 *jinshi* in 1400 none came from merchant families, nor did any of the 312 in 1544. [source: HW]
- Official contempt for merchants coexisted with a fast-commercialising society by the 16th century. The late-Ming gazetteer writer Zhang Tao (Sheh County, 1609) contrasted an idealised early Ming of self-sufficient households with his own time, when "Those who went out as merchants became numerous, and the ownership of land was no longer esteemed." (Quoted from Brook, *The Confusions of Pleasure*.) [source: ECON]
- Hongwu's administrative, fiscal, examination and *weisuo* institutions lasted, with minor changes, to the end of the dynasty and in part to the end of imperial China (Dreyer). [source: HW]

## 2. Eunuchs, factions and the late-Ming court

- Hongwu forbade eunuchs to learn to read or to engage in politics. From Yongle (r. 1402–1424) onward they ran imperial workshops, commanded armies and went as envoys; Yongle put 75 eunuchs in charge of foreign missions. They formed a bureaucracy parallel to the civil service, with the Eastern Depot (later also the Western Depot) as a secret service. [source: MING]
- Eunuch power was exercised by Wang Zhen (behind the 1449 Tumu campaign), Liu Jin and others, but became "excessive" from the 1590s, when the Wanli emperor (r. 1572–1620) let eunuchs collect provincial taxes. [source: MING]
- From 1597–1599 Wanli sent eunuch mining-tax commissioners (*kuangshi*) to raise money; von Glahn estimates silver-mine tax revenue rose to an average of 3,650 kg a year in 1597–1606. Wanli abandoned the scheme in January 1606. [source: WL]
- The eunuch Wei Zhongxian (1568–1627) dominated the Tianqi emperor (r. 1620–1627) and had critics from the Donglin faction tortured to death. The Donglin Academy in Wuxi was restored in 1604 by Gu Xiancheng (1550–1612) and Gao Panlong; it was closed in 1622 and its leader Yang Lian and five others were tortured and executed in 1624 (the article gives these dates; some sources date the deaths to 1625). [source: MING; DL]

## 3. The Single Whip and silver

- Zhang Juzheng (1525–1582) was senior grand secretary and de facto ruler for the young Wanli emperor from 1572 until his death on 9 July 1582. He ordered a nationwide land survey in 1581–1582, the first since the Song. After his death he was posthumously disgraced. [source: ZJZ]
- The Single Whip reform (*yitiao bianfa*) commuted land tax, poll tax, labour service and local levies into a single payment in silver. It began piecemeal in the 16th century (the economy article dates gradual conversion from 1552; local experiments with labour service go back to 1443) and was extended across the empire in 1580 by Zhang Juzheng. Conversion was never complete: by the end of the Ming about 70% of labour obligations in the south, and about 50% in the north, had been turned into silver surcharges. [source: SW; ECON]
- Taxing in silver raised China's demand for silver from Japan and Spanish America. Where silver was scarce, as on the northern frontier, farmers found it harder to pay. [source: SW]
- Silver inflow estimates (all estimates; give as attributed ranges):
  - Official domestic silver production in the mid-15th century averaged 22–30 tons a year; American silver imports alone ran at 150–345 tons a year in 1597–1602 (Brook, *Confusions of Pleasure*, p. 205). [source: ECON]
  - In the first decade of the 17th century Japan supplied 33–48 tons a year; China received 2–3 million pesos (57.5–86.25 tons) a year of American silver through the Philippines; in total "at least 250–265 tons" a year entered China in the first third of the 17th century, probably more (Wakeman). [source: ECON]
  - About 20% of American silver went directly to the Philippines and China, the rest via Europe and Central Asia; "ultimately, half" of American silver production ended up in China (Wakeman). The published Zheng He chapter says "a third or more"; present the range "between a third and a half". [source: ECON]
  - Richard von Glahn, the leading specialist, calls 1550–1650 China's "silver century", notes that after 1670 imports were almost entirely Latin American silver, and argued in 1996 that Chinese demand for money, not short-term swings in imports, drove the silver trade. A reviewer of his *Fountain of Fortune* (1996) judged his import estimates too low. [source: WebSearch summary of von Glahn, "The Changing Significance of Latin American Silver in the Chinese Economy", *Revista de Historia Económica* (2020), https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/D3045EA55D654DCDCDCB3874E481A0B4/core-reader ; von Glahn, "Myth and Reality of China's Seventeenth-Century Monetary Crisis", *Journal of Economic History* 56 (1996), https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jechis/v56y1996i02p429-454_01.html ; review, https://muse.jhu.edu/article/179288/pdf]
- The Manila galleons sailed between Manila and Acapulco from 1565 to 1815, carrying mostly Chinese goods eastward ("La Nao de China") in exchange for American silver. Manila was founded by the Spanish in 1571 (published Zheng He chapter; Flynn and Giráldez date the "birth" of world trade to 1571). [source: MG]
- In the 1620s–1640s silver became scarce and dear: the exchange rate fell from 1,000 copper cash = 1 tael (*liang*) in the 1630s to half a tael by 1640 and a third by early 1643 (Spence), squeezing peasants who sold in copper and paid tax in silver. Causes cited include Japan's closure in 1639 and Spanish restrictions; Brook and von Glahn doubt that a silver shortage was the decisive cause of the Ming fall. [source: ECON; SW]

## 4. The commercial economy and Jiangnan

- Late-Ming population estimates vary: Fairbank 160 million, Brook 175 million, Ebrey 200 million, Mote 231 million by 1600. The Ming article gives a range of 160–200 million. [source: ECON; MING]
- Jiangnan (southern Nanzhili, roughly southern Jiangsu and Anhui, and Zhejiang) was the most commercialised region: dense cities, high rice yields grown for market, money in wide use. Suzhou was the economic hub; in the early 16th century Beijing had nearly 1 million people, Nanjing over 1 million, Suzhou under 650,000. Suzhou grew from 339,000 in the mid-16th century to 650,000 by century's end. [source: ECON]
- The main long-distance trade goods were rice, then cotton (Songjiang prefecture was the hub of cotton cloth), then silk (Suzhou, Hangzhou, Nanjing). In some Jiangnan districts cotton overtook rice as the main crop in the 16th century. [source: ECON]
- Merchants from Huizhou (Anhui) ran much of the national wholesale network. [source: ECON]
- Jingdezhen porcelain: about 50 kilns in the 15th century, about 300 by the late 16th; production became mostly private in the late Ming. [source: ECON]
- Putting-out: in the 16th century merchants in Zhejiang rented looms to household weavers, supplied the raw materials and bought the cloth; private silk workshops in Suzhou, Nanjing and Hangzhou employed up to several hundred wage workers. [source: ECON]
- In 1567 the Ming lifted the maritime ban (*haijin*) for Chinese merchants sailing from Yuegang in Fujian (published Zheng He chapter); the Portuguese settled at Macau from 1557 (published Chinese History chapter). Cross-refer rather than repeat.

## 5. Print culture, literature and thought in the late Ming

- Printed books outnumbered manuscripts by the early 16th century; during the Wanli era (1573–1620) rising literacy, urban readership and cheaper production created "unprecedented growth in commercial publishing". About two-thirds of output was non-literary (Classics commentaries, histories, encyclopedias, devotional works for women). Suzhou specialised in luxury illustrated editions, Fujian (Jianyang) in cheap illustrated editions, Anhui in fine engraving; multicolour printing reappeared around 1600. Private libraries could exceed 10,000 titles; "bookshop boats" plied the waterways. [source: MLIT]
- The novel: oldest surviving edition of *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* 1522; *Water Margin* printed around 1550; *Journey to the West*, attributed to Wu Cheng'en (about 1500–1582), most refined version about 1570. [source: MLIT]
- *Jin Ping Mei* (*The Plum in the Golden Vase*), 100 chapters, published around 1610 under the pseudonym Lanling Xiaoxiao Sheng ("the Scoffing Scholar of Lanling"); a novel of manners centred on the merchant Ximen Qing's household and three women, Pan Jinlian, Li Ping'er and Pang Chunmei. [source: JPM]
- Feng Menglong (1574–1646) published the *Three Words* collections of vernacular stories, 120 stories in all, in 1620–1625. [source: MLIT]
- Drama: Tang Xianzu's *The Peony Pavilion* (*chuanqi*); the Suzhou *kunqu* style spread from Jiangnan in the 16th century. [source: MLIT]
- Women's writing: anthologies of women's poetry began with Tian Yiheng's *Shi nüshi* (1557), with work by 36 Ming women poets; Shen Yixiu edited an anthology in 1636. Some texts attributed to women may have been forged by editors to meet demand. [source: MLIT]
- Li Zhi (1527–1602), a critic of Zhu Xi orthodoxy influenced by Wang Yangming (1472–1529), argued for the "childlike heart-mind" and denied that women were inferior to men in native intelligence. Accused of heresy, he was imprisoned in 1602 and killed himself in prison. [source: LZ]
- Matteo Ricci (1552–1610) reached Macau in 1582 and in 1601 became the first European to enter the Forbidden City, at the Wanli emperor's invitation; he died in Beijing on 11 May 1610. With Xu Guangqi (1562–1633, baptised Paul) he translated the first parts of Euclid's *Elements* in 1607. Xu also experimented with sweet potatoes and wrote the *Complete Treatise on Agriculture*. [source: RICCI; XGQ]

## 6. Women and gender

- **Footbinding**: practised from the Song (earliest archaeological evidence: tombs of Huang Sheng, d. 1243, and Madame Zhou, d. 1274), more severe forms and wider spread by the late Ming, reaching lower classes under the Qing. [source: FB]
- The Manchus banned footbinding in 1636, 1638 and 1664 (Kangxi); few Han complied and Kangxi abandoned the effort in 1668. Manchu women did not bind (some wore "flower bowl" platform shoes imitating the bound-foot gait); Hakka women did not bind. By the 19th century an estimated 40–50% of Chinese women had bound feet. [source: FB]
- Variation by class and labour: some rural families bound only one daughter; in textile areas binding was tied to household labour strategies. Laurel Bossen and Hill Gates (*Bound Feet, Young Hands*, 2017) argue that binding kept girls at hand-spinning and weaving, and declined when factory textiles destroyed that work, rejecting beauty, status or male control as the main explanation. [source: FB]
- The Natural Foot Society (Tianzu hui) was founded in Xiamen in 1874; Kang Youwei founded an anti-footbinding society in 1883; the Republic banned the practice in 1912, though enforcement lagged. (The Taiping chapter covers the Taiping ban.) [source: FB]
- **Dorothy Ko's revisionism**: Ko (b. 1957, Barnard College/Columbia) wrote *Teachers of the Inner Chambers: Women and Culture in Seventeenth-Century China* (1994), *Every Step a Lotus: Shoes for Bound Feet* (2001) and *Cinderella's Sisters: A Revisionist History of Footbinding* (2005). She argues that footbinding was understood as attire and a marker of civility and femininity: a Chinese encyclopedia of 1591 classed it under "Female Adornments" with hairstyles and ear-piercing. The practice was carried out by women on girls, and elite women's writings show pride as well as pain. Her work challenges the reading of binding only as male erotic coercion, while not denying the suffering. [source: DK; FB]
- **Chastity cult**: in the Ming chaste widows became cultural heroes, commemorated with chastity arches (*zhenjie paifang*), shrines and biographies; widow suicide rose sharply in the Ming (T'ien Ju-k'ang, *Male Anxiety and Female Chastity*, 1988). The Qing promoted widow chastity officially as a "bureaucratic tool of moral reform": local leaders nominated exemplary women, typically widowed before 30 who stayed unmarried for over 20 years, and the court granted commendations. [source: WOM]
- Interpretations: Mark Elvin ("Female Virtue and the State", 1984) and Susan Mann documented the steep rise in honoured chaste widows in the Qing; Mann called it a "cult" tied to the Manchus' wish to display Confucian credentials; T'ien Ju-k'ang linked widow suicide to the anxieties of failed examination candidates, while Mann stressed women's own despair; Jennifer Holmgren linked it to widows' shrinking property rights; Angela Leung dated the spread of widow homes in Jiangnan to the late 18th century. [source: WebSearch summary, Mark Elliott, "Manchu Widows and Ethnicity in Qing China", *CSSH* (1999), https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/elliott/files/manchu_widows_and_ethnicity_in_qing_china.pdf ; Leung (1993), https://www.hkihss.hku.hk/filemanager/content/others/angela-ki-che-leung/pdf/articles/1993_chasten.pdf]
- **Susan Mann** (b. 1943; UC Davis 1989 to retirement): *Precious Records: Women in China's Long Eighteenth Century* (1997, Levenson Prize) showed elite women in the "inner quarters" as writers and actors, not "mere victims of Confucian patriarchy", using their poetry; *The Talented Women of the Zhang Family* (2007, Fairbank Prize) follows three women from the 1790s to the 1860s. Mann was one of a cohort of scholars (Ebrey, Furth, Ko, Rawski, Ropp, Waltner, Widmer) who from the 1980s rewrote late-imperial women's history from texts by women. [source: SM]
- Women writers and courtesans: Liu Rushi (1618–1664), a courtesan-poet, married the scholar Qian Qianyi; after 1644 she urged him to die for the Ming, and he instead aided the anti-Qing resistance. The Qing discouraged officials from visiting courtesans, and the literary courtesan culture of the late Ming declined. [source: LRS; WOM]
- Wang Zhenyi (1768–1797), astronomer and mathematician, wrote on the precession of the equinoxes and an *Explanation of Lunar Eclipses*. [source: WZY]
- Gu Taiqing (1799–about 1877), a Manchu poet famed for her *ci*, wrote *Honglou meng ying*, a sequel to *Dream of the Red Chamber* (1877), the earliest extant Chinese novel by a woman. [source: GTQ]
- Women soldiers in the Ming crisis: Qin Liangyu (1574–1648) held Ming military rank in Sichuan. [source: WOM]
- The Ming saying "A woman without talent is virtuous" (*nüzi wucai bian shi de*) circulated alongside the boom in women's writing. [source: WOM]

## 7. The fall of the Ming and the Manchu conquest

- Famine from the dry, cold weather of the Little Ice Age struck northern China in the early 17th century (Spence); from 1640 starving peasants formed huge rebel bands. [source: MING]
- Li Zicheng's rebels took Beijing on 24 or 25 April 1644 (sources differ by a day); the Chongzhen emperor hanged himself on a tree in the imperial garden. [source: TRANS (24 April); MING (25 April)]
- Wu Sangui surrendered to the Qing on the morning of 27 May 1644 at the Shanhai Pass; Dorgon's cavalry routed Li. The Qing dated its founding in China to June 1644, but fighting went on until 1683. [source: TRANS]
- Hong Taiji renamed his people Manchu (from Jurchen) in 1635 and proclaimed the Qing in 1636. [source: EB; QING]
- Zhang Xianzhong (1606–1647) held Sichuan from 1644; the *Ming History* figure of 600 million deaths is impossible, and historians estimate up to one million killed under his rule. Sichuan's depopulation was refilled by a resettlement programme from about 1670 lasting over two centuries. [source: ZXZ; TRANS]
- Yangzhou, May 1645: Prince Dodo's forces sacked the city, defended by Shi Kefa. Wang Xiuchu's *A Record of Ten Days in Yangzhou* gives over 800,000 victims, a number considered likely exaggerated; his diary shows the killing ended on the sixth day. [source: YZM]
- The queue order: on 21 July 1645 the regent Dorgon ordered all Han men to shave the forehead and braid the rest into a Manchu queue, on pain of death. The slogan ran "keep the hair and lose the head, or keep the head and lose the hair". Han objected mostly to shaving the forehead. Enforcement took up to ten years; massacres of resisters, such as three at Jiading within a month, were carried out largely by Han defectors such as Li Chengdong. [source: Q]
- The Great Clearance: edicts of 1661, 1664 and 1679 forced coastal populations inland to cut off Koxinga's loyalists. [source: GC]
- Wu Sangui's Revolt of the Three Feudatories began in 1673; the Zheng regime on Taiwan surrendered in 1683. [source: TRANS; KX]
- The queue remained a symbol of submission until 1912; Puyi cut his in 1922. Philip Kuhn's *Soulstealers: The Chinese Sorcery Scare of 1768* (1990) studies a panic over sorcerers clipping queues. [source: Q]

## 8. Banners, Manchu identity and the multiethnic empire

- Nurhaci organised his men into companies of 300 in 1601 (the Banners article says "300 households"), with four banners (Yellow, White, Red, Blue); in 1615 they were doubled to eight by adding "bordered" banners. Mongol Eight Banners were formed from 1635 and Han (Hanjun) banners between 1637 and 1642. [source: EB]
- By 1644 an estimated two million people lived in the banner system (Elliott). After the conquest bannermen received stipends, land, grain and housing, lived in garrisons, and took over Beijing's inner city. By the 1730s about 1.5 million bannermen and dependants were an expensive burden. [source: EB]
- Han Chinese Green Standard troops did most of the routine military governing; Han bannermen (defectors before 1644) were a privileged category distinct from Han civilians. [source: EB]
- The Qing divided rule: Han China through the Ming-style bureaucracy; Mongols and Tibetans through the Lifan Yuan (Court of Colonial Affairs). The Qing called its state "China" (*Zhongguo*; Manchu *Dulimbai Gurun*) and included Manchuria, Mongolia, Xinjiang and Tibet in it. [source: QING; NQH]
- Tibet: Qing rule over Tibet is dated 1720–1912; scholars variously call it a protectorate or a subordinate part of the empire; Gernet says the Qing gained a "firm hold" in 1751. [source: TIB]
- Xinjiang: conquest of the Dzungars 1755–1759 (house figures above; *Central Asia* chapter). [source: TGC]
- New Qing History: see Debates. Keep consistent with the *Chinese History* chapter (Rawski 1996; Ho 1998 "In Defense of Sinicization"; Elliott says the school does not separate the Qing from "China").

## 9. The High Qing: Kangxi, Yongzheng, Qianlong

- Kangxi (r. 1661–1722) took the throne aged seven; he arrested the regent Oboi in 1669, defeated the Three Feudatories (1673–1681), took Taiwan in 1683, signed Nerchinsk (1689), and personally led campaigns against Galdan's Dzungars in 1696–1697 (Galdan defeated at Jao Modo; he died the next year). His 61-year reign was the longest of any Chinese emperor. [source: KX; QING]
- Tax freeze: in 1712 Kangxi froze the *ding* (labour-service/poll) tax quota, promising never to raise it (*yongbu jiafu*); one source puts the frozen *ding* count at 24.62 million. Sources give the base year as 1711, 1712 or 1713. [source: WebSearch summary of Cambridge Economic History of China, https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/3C88EF0F10D658B6F9E23BD58AD300FF/core-reader ; LSE working paper 324, https://www.lse.ac.uk/asset-library/information/wp-324.pdf]
- Yongzheng (r. 1722–1735, reign era from 1723): his succession was disputed, and some Chinese historians (Qin Hui) believe he forged the succession edict. [source: YZ]
- Yongzheng's reforms: he merged the *ding* tax into the land tax (*tanding rumu*), which Lin Qian says continued the Single Whip by lightening the burden on the landless; he made the "meltage fee" (*huohao*) a formal surcharge of about 10–30% on the land tax to pay local officials' salaries, to curb informal extortion; and he used a secret palace-memorial system and set up the Grand Council (*Junjichu*) in 1729 for the Dzungar campaign, institutionalised by 1733. Surcharges crept back under Qianlong. [source: YZ; QECON; GCQ]
- Yongzheng's emancipation edicts (from 1723) abolished the hereditary "mean" (*jianmin*) status of several groups: the musician households (*yuehu*) of Shanxi and Shaanxi (on the censor Nian Xi's memorial), the "fallen people" (*duomin*) beggar households of northern Zhejiang, the boat people of Guangdong and Guangxi, the "Fisherfolk of the Five Surnames" of the Qiantang, and hereditary servants of southern Anhui (Anders Hansson, *Chinese Outcasts*, 1996). The legal division between "good" (*liangmin*) and "mean" people (slaves, bondservants, entertainers including prostitutes and actors, yamen runners) persisted to the end of the Qing. [source: WebSearch summary, Hansson review, *China Review International* 5.1, https://muse.jhu.edu/journals/china_review_international/v005/5.1.hammond.pdf ; ANU *East Asian History* 25–26, https://eah.anu.edu.au/sites/default/files/article-content/25-26/EAH25-26_04.pdf ; QING]
- Yongzheng proscribed Catholicism in 1724; Kangxi had issued an edict of toleration in 1692 but banned Christian missions in 1721 after Pope Clement XI condemned the Chinese rites (*Cum Deus optimus* 1704; *Ex illa die* 19 March 1715). The ban on the rites was lifted by Pius XII on 8 December 1939. [source: RITES]
- Jesuits at court ran the imperial observatory; the Jesuit painter Giuseppe Castiglione (1688–1766) served Yongzheng and Qianlong. [source: RITES]
- Qianlong (born 25 September 1711; reigned 1735–1796; died 7 February 1799) abdicated in 1796 after 60 years so as not to outreign his grandfather Kangxi, but kept power until his death. [source: QL]
- Ten Great Campaigns: Dzungars and Xinjiang (1755–1759), Jinchuan (1747–1749, 1771–1776, the costliest), Burma (1765–1769), Taiwan (1787–1788, against an uprising there; the article says "Taiwanese aboriginals"), the Tây Sơn in Vietnam (1788–1789), and two against the Gurkhas on the Tibet–Nepal border (1790–1792). Only the Dzungar and Xinjiang campaigns enlarged the empire; the rest were frontier "police actions". Qianlong styled himself "Old Man of the Ten Completions" (1792). The campaigns cost more than 151 million taels (Zhuang Jifa). [source: TGC]
- Heshen (1750–1799), Qianlong's favourite, epitomised late-reign corruption; the Jiaqing emperor forced him to kill himself on 22 February 1799. [source: HS; QING]

## 10. Siku Quanshu and the literary inquisition

- The *Siku Quanshu* was commissioned in 1772 and completed in 1782, with Ji Yun as chief editor, more than 361 scholars and about 3,826 copyists. Each of seven copies had 36,381 volumes, about 2.3 million pages and about 800 million characters. The catalogue covers 3,593 included titles and about 6,793 others. [source: SKQS]
- Over 2,855 books were banned during the compilation, and 400–500 more were edited or censored; most were late-Ming works with anti-Manchu content. The literary-inquisition article says as many as 3,000 works may have been lost and an estimated 151,723 volumes destroyed between 1772 and 1793. The published *Chinese History* chapter's "some 3,100 works" lies within this range. [source: SKQS; LI]
- There were 53 cases of literary persecution under Qianlong (Wong 2000). Earlier cases: the Ming History case of 1661–1663 (70 people executed); Yongzheng's case against Zeng Jing, a follower of Lü Liuliang. In 1799 Jiaqing ordered the review of earlier cases, ending the era of mass inquisitions. [source: LI; YZ]
- Of the seven copies, the Old Summer Palace copy was destroyed in 1860, and the Zhenjiang and Yangzhou copies were destroyed in the Taiping war. [source: SKQS]

## 11. Qing literature and dissent

- Cao Xueqin wrote *Dream of the Red Chamber* (*The Story of the Stone*) from the 1740s until his death in 1763 or 1764; 80-chapter manuscripts circulated; Gao E and Cheng Weiyuan printed a 120-chapter edition in 1791–1792. [source: DRC]
- Huang Zongxi's *Waiting for the Dawn* condemns selfish autocracy and argues ministers should openly criticise the ruler; he and Gu Yanwu are the best-known Ming loyalist thinkers. [source: HZX]

## 12. Population growth and its debates

- Population (POP estimates; census in brackets): 1600 about 160 million; 1650 about 140 million (123 million); 1700 about 160 million (126 million); 1750 about 225 million (177 million); 1791 about 303 million; 1821 about 384 million; 1851 about 408–436 million. Rowe: roughly 150 million in 1700, doubled over the century, about 450 million by 1850. House wording: 150–160 million in 1700, about 303 million in 1791. [source: POP]
- Causes (Rowe): fast-ripening rice allowing two or three crops a year; New World crops (peanuts, potatoes, especially sweet potatoes), grown on poor land as staples for the poor; smallpox inoculation; better midwifery; government campaigns against infanticide. Growth was greatest in borderlands and highlands, not cities. Millions of Han migrated to Yunnan, Guizhou, Taiwan and, after the 1750s conquests, Xinjiang agricultural colonies. [source: POP]
- Sweet potatoes are traditionally said to have been brought from Luzon to Fujian by the merchant Chen Zhenlong in 1593 or 1594 and promoted by governor Jin Xuezeng during a famine; dates and the first introducer are disputed, and other routes ran through Yunnan and Guangdong. [source: WebSearch summary of several secondary sources, https://faroutliers.com/2006/01/08/fujians-ode-to-the-sweet-potato ; https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/doi/10.1007/978-1-4020-9475-0_15 — treat as "traditionally said"]
- Qianlong remarked: "The population continues to grow, but the land does not." [source: QING]
- Hong Liangji's essay *Zhi Ping Pian* (1793), in his collection *Opinions*, argued that households had multiplied twentyfold in a century while land could at most increase fivefold. Hong (jinshi at 44) was sentenced to death in 1799 for criticising the court over Heshen-era corruption; the sentence was commuted. [source: HLJ]
- Ho Ping-ti (1917–2012), *Studies on the Population of China, 1368–1953* (Harvard, 1959): showed that *ding* figures were fiscal units tied to labour service, not head counts, and attributed growth to institutions as well as nature. Ho was also author of *The Ladder of Success in Imperial China* (1962) and the first Asian-born president of the Association for Asian Studies (1975). [source: HPT; WebSearch summary, https://book.douban.com/review/7168197/]
- James Z. Lee and Wang Feng, *One Quarter of Humanity: Malthusian Mythology and Chinese Realities, 1700–2000* (Harvard, 1999), reject Malthus's picture of China as a land of early universal marriage, unchecked fertility and positive checks: Chinese families controlled fertility within marriage within marriage, and through female infanticide and neglect (a 2006 review summarises that "an extra one-tenth or more of baby girls" died this way). A *Journal of Interdisciplinary History* reviewer criticised its heavy generalisation. [source: WebSearch summary, https://shss.hkust.edu.hk/node/223 ; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/16165/pdf]
- Strain: an 1873–1893 example shows the population rising 8% while cultivated land rose only 1%; marriages and births declined in the early 19th century; migration to the upper Yangzi and Han highlands caused soil erosion and flooding and fed the White Lotus rising. [source: QECON]

## 13. Canton, Macartney and the late-18th-century strains (brief; cross-refer)

- Kangxi reopened maritime trade in 1684 with customs houses along the coast. In 1757 Qianlong confined Western trade to Canton (Guangzhou); the Cohong was officially sanctioned as a monopoly in 1760. The system lasted until 1842. [source: CS] *The Opium Wars* chapter covers it in depth.
- Macartney's audience with Qianlong took place on 14 September 1793 in the imperial tent at Chengde; Qianlong had offered a single prostration in place of the full kowtow. The failure was "not due to Macartney's refusal to kowtow". [source: MAC]
- Wang Lun's millenarian rising in Shandong, 1774 (he died 1 November 1774). [source: WLUN]
- White Lotus Rebellion: 1794 (infobox) or 1796 (open rising) to 1804, in the Sichuan–Hubei–Shaanxi highlands; it began as tax protest among poor settlers; it was put down by stockaded villages and gentry militias after 1800, with some 100,000 rebels killed. It "ended the myth of the military invincibility of the Manchus". Rebels said "the officials have forced the people to rebel". [source: WLR; QING]
- The Eight Trigrams sect under Lin Qing attacked the Forbidden City in 1813. [source: ET]
- After 1800, cross-refer to *The Opium Wars*, *The Taiping Rebellion and the Late Qing* and *Modern China* (fall in 1912; Puyi's abdication on 12 February 1912).

## 14. Historiography

- Kenneth Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence* (Princeton, 2000; Fairbank Prize 2001; joint World History Association book of the year): the Yangzi Delta comparable with England until about 1800; coal and colonies made the difference. [source: KP; GD]
- Philip C. C. Huang (UCLA 1966–2004; founding editor of *Modern China*): *The Peasant Economy and Social Change in North China* (1985) and *The Peasant Family and Rural Development in the Yangzi Delta, 1350–1988* (Stanford, 1990) argued for "involution": in the Ming–Qing, more labour per unit of land through labour-intensive cash crops and low-return household sidelines raised total output while returns per workday fell — "growth without development". He criticised Pomeranz in "Development or Involution in Eighteenth-Century Britain and China?" (*Journal of Asian Studies* 61:2, 2002); Pomeranz replied in "Facts are Stubborn Things" (*JAS* 62:1, 2003). [source: PH; KP; WebSearch summary of Stanford University Press description, https://sup.org/books/cite/?id=2761]
- Jack Goldstone's 2021 review puts the divergence after 1750 or even 1800; others (Broadberry, Allen, Maddison) see Europe ahead earlier — the *Chinese History* chapter already gives these. [source: GD]
- Jonathan Spence (1936–2021), Sterling Professor at Yale, president of the AHA 2004–2005: *Ts'ao Yin and the K'ang-hsi Emperor* (1966), *Emperor of China: Self-Portrait of K'ang-hsi* (1974), *The Death of Woman Wang* (1978; rural Tancheng in the 17th century), *The Memory Palace of Matteo Ricci* (1984), *The Search for Modern China* (1990, starting from the late Ming), *Treason by the Book* (2001, the Zeng Jing case), *Return to Dragon Mountain* (2007). [source: JS]
- Timothy Brook (b. 1951, Toronto): *The Confusions of Pleasure: Commerce and Culture in Ming China* (1998; Levenson Prize 2000), *Vermeer's Hat* (Mark Lynton Prize 2009), *The Troubled Empire: China in the Yuan and Ming Dynasties* (2010), *Great State* (2019). [source: TB]
- Social history from below: Michael Szonyi's *The Art of Being Governed* (2017) uses Fujian genealogies of hereditary military households to show Ming subjects negotiating with the state. [source: WebSearch, https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691174518/the-art-of-being-governed]

## Debates

- **Did silver make or break the Ming?** Flynn and Giráldez (world trade "born" in 1571) and many surveys stress the silver inflow, and the older "silver crisis" thesis links its fall in the 1630s–40s to the Ming collapse; von Glahn (1996) and Brook argue that Chinese demand for money drove the trade and doubt a decisive 1640s shortfall. [source: ECON; von Glahn 1996, see §3]
- **Footbinding: oppression, fashion or labour?** Feminist and May Fourth readings stress violence against women; Ko stresses women's agency, fashion and civility; Bossen and Gates stress girls' labour in household textile production. [source: FB; DK]
- **Women: victims or agents?** Mann, Ko and their cohort against the older picture of passive victims of Confucian patriarchy. The chastity cult: state ideology (Mann), examination anxieties (T'ien), property law (Holmgren). [source: SM; WOM; §6]
- **New Qing History vs sinicisation** (Rawski 1996 vs Ho 1998; Elliott, Crossley, Perdue, Millward; criticism in China, intensified under Xi Jinping; Zhao Gang and Yuanchong Wang argue that the Qing presented itself as "China"). Keep consistent with the *Chinese History* chapter. [source: NQH]
- **Population: Malthus or not?** Hong Liangji, older Malthusian readings and Huang's involution (pressure, falling returns per workday) against Lee and Wang (fertility controlled within marriage; China not a Malthusian trap) and Pomeranz (living standards comparable with Europe's core to about 1800). [source: HLJ; §12; §14]
- **Great Divergence / California School** against Huang's involution and Broadberry/Allen/Maddison; Goldstone's late divergence. [source: GD; PH; KP]
- **Was Yongzheng's succession legitimate?** Qin Hui and others say the will was forged; others dispute it. [source: YZ]

## Conflicts with published chapters

- **Hongwu's purges.** *Ming China and the Voyages of Zheng He* says "tens of thousands of officials and their families executed". MING and HW give "some 100,000" or "approximately 100,000" killed in purges (Ebrey; Fairbank and Goldman). Not strictly contradictory; this chapter should say "about 100,000 by common estimates".
- **Late-Ming population.** The Zheng He chapter says "perhaps 150 million or more by 1600"; estimates run 160 million (Fairbank) to 175 (Brook), 200 (Ebrey) and 231 million (Mote). Say "160–200 million, perhaps more".
- **Share of American silver reaching China.** The Zheng He chapter says "by some estimates a third or more"; ECON (Wakeman) says "half". Use "between a third and a half".
- **Zheng He's men.** The Zheng He chapter says "some 27,000 men", the *Chinese History* chapter "almost 28,000". Not this chapter's topic; avoid giving a number.
- **Qing population in 1700.** House standard is 150–160 million. QING says the 18th-century population "ballooned from 100 million to 300 million", and HLJ says it "tripled from 100 million (1651–1661) to 300 million (1790)". Do not use the 100 million figure.
- **Canton System date.** *The Opium Wars* says "formalized in 1757"; CS shows 1757 as the date trade was confined to Canton, with the Cohong officially sanctioned as a monopoly in 1760 (and Cohong legal responsibility from 1725 per MAC). Compatible if this chapter says "confined to Canton from 1757".
- **Literary inquisition.** The *Chinese History* chapter says "some 3,100 works" were suppressed; SKQS gives over 2,855 banned plus 400–500 censored, LI "as many as 3,000" lost. Compatible; say "about 3,000".
- **White Lotus dates.** The Taiping chapter says "from the mid-1790s … took eight years to crush"; WLR gives 1794–1804, QING 1796–1804, and a pacification edict of 1805. Compatible.
- **Fall of Beijing.** 24 April (TRANS) vs 25 April 1644 (MING); published chapters give no day. Say "April 1644".
- **Qianlong's edict.** *Chinese History* uses Backhouse and Bland ("Our Celestial Empire possesses all things…"); *The Opium Wars* uses another rendering ("We possess all things…"). Not a factual conflict; use the house version.

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use

- Precise national counts of honoured chaste widows or widow suicides (Elvin's and Mann's figures were not retrieved).
- FB's statement that "most modern historians" put women with bound feet at "10 and 20 million" at the peak — it conflicts with the same article's 40–50% of women in the 19th century; use only the 40–50% estimate, attributed as an estimate.
- Von Glahn's own cumulative tonnage for 1550–1645 (often quoted at about 7,000+ tons) — not found in any source read.
- The Yongzheng treasury rising "from 32.6 million taels in 1721 to about 60 million in 1730" (YZ, unclear whether reserves or revenue).
- The Donglin article's description of Gu Xiancheng as "a Grand Secretary" (doubtful).
- Exact introduction dates of maize and peanuts into China.
- Any claim that the Wanli emperor went "on strike" for decades: WL says he left some memoranda unanswered but responded to others and kept the tax machinery running; use cautious wording or omit.
- Exact date of the Donglin martyrs' deaths (DL says 1624; other accounts say 1625).
- Kangxi's 1712 edict wording "never increase taxes" as a direct quotation (only paraphrased in secondary sources).
