## Qing prosperity and population pressure (c. 1700–1850)
- Under the Yongzheng (r. 1723–35) and Qianlong (r. 1735–96) emperors the Qing was at the height of its power; Qianlong led the "Ten Great Campaigns" into present-day Xinjiang and Mongolia and sponsored the *Siku Quanshu*, the largest collection of books in Chinese history. Jonathan Spence describes the empire at the end of Qianlong's reign as "like the sun at midday", with signs of decay already visible. [source: QING]
- Late Qianlong corruption: the favourite Heshen became one of the most corrupt officials of the dynasty; the Jiaqing Emperor (r. 1796–1820) forced him to commit suicide. [source: QING]
- Population: about 150 million in 1700 (widely accepted estimate), doubled over the following century, and reached about 450 million on the eve of the Taiping Rebellion in 1850. Causes cited: better irrigation, fast-ripening rice allowing two or three crops a year, New World crops (peanuts, potatoes, especially sweet potatoes), smallpox inoculation, better midwifery. Growth was greatest in the borderlands and highlands, not the cities. [source: POP]. The POP table gives 1851 at about 432 million (range 408–436 million); QING gives "rose to 400 million" — treat the mid-century figure as roughly 400–450 million. [source: POP; QING]
- The Qing taxed lightly and at fixed rates, so revenue did not keep pace with population, which led to fiscal crisis. [source: QING]
- The Qianlong Emperor remarked: "The population continues to grow, but the land does not." [source: QING]
- Hong Liangji (1746–1809) wrote the essay *Zhi Ping Pian* (published 1793) on population outrunning land, raising many of the same issues as his contemporary Malthus; he was banished to Ili (Xinjiang) for a critical memorial and pardoned in 1800. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Liangji]
- White Lotus Rebellion, 1794–1804: millenarian Buddhist-inspired uprising in the mountain borderlands of Hubei, Sichuan and Shaanxi; it took eight years to crush and marked a sharp decline in Qing strength and prosperity; the government had to rely more on Han Green Standard troops and local militia. Its slogan, per QING: "the officials have forced the people to rebel". QING dates the open rising to 1796; the rebellion article dates the start to 1794. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Lotus_Rebellion; QING]
- Mobility: millions of Han migrated to Yunnan, Guizhou and Taiwan in the 18th century, and to Southeast Asia. [source: POP]
- Pomeranz's *The Great Divergence* (2000) argues the most advanced regions of Europe and Asia (e.g. England and the Yangzi Delta) had comparable development levels as late as c. 1800, facing similar land constraints; Britain pulled ahead after 1800 thanks to coal and colonies. Won the 2000 John K. Fairbank Prize. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Divergence_(book)]

## The Opium Wars (brief context only)
- Lin Zexu confiscated opium in 1839; the First Opium War ended in the 1842 Treaty of Nanjing (indemnity; Canton, Amoy, Fuzhou, Ningbo, Shanghai opened; Hong Kong Island ceded). [source: QING]
- The Second Opium War ended in 1860; the Anglo-French force burned the Old Summer Palace and the Xianfeng Emperor fled to Rehe (Chengde), where he died on 22 August 1861. [source: CIXI]
- Prince Gong (Yixin, 1833–98) negotiated and signed the Convention of Beijing (1860). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Gong]

## Hong Xiuquan: origins and visions
- Hong Xiuquan, born Hong Huoxiu, 1 January 1814 (date established by Jian Youwen; nine different dates exist), a Hakka from Hua county near Guangzhou; died 1 June 1864. [source: HXQ]
- In 1836 in Guangzhou he received Liang Fa's Christian tracts *Good Words to Admonish (Exhort) the Age*; Liang Fa was a Chinese convert and assistant to missionary Edwin Stevens. [source: HXQ; THK]
- He failed the provincial examination repeatedly (pass rate under one percent); after his third failure in 1837 he had a breakdown and dreamed of Heaven: a father with a golden beard gave him a sword to slay demons, aided by an "elder brother". In later tellings he saw Confucius punished. [source: HXQ; TR]
- After his fourth and final failure in 1843 he read the tracts closely and concluded he was the son of God and younger brother of Jesus, sent to destroy demon worship. He began smashing idols and Confucian tablets. [source: HXQ; TR]
- 1847: studied for a short time in Guangzhou with the American Southern Baptist missionary Issachar Jacox Roberts, who refused to baptize him. [source: HXQ; TR]
- Feng Yunshan founded the God Worshipping Society in Guangxi (TR dates it to 1844); by 1850 the sect had over 10,000 followers. In 1848 Yang Xiuqing (an illiterate charcoal burner) and Xiao Chaogui began claiming to speak in trance as God the Father and Jesus respectively; Hong accepted their claims in 1849. [source: TR; HXQ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang_Xiuqing]
- Hong rewrote the Bible (based on Gützlaff's translation) and presented his faith as a restoration of ancient Chinese worship of Shangdi; one historian (Reilly) calls it a "dynamic new Chinese religion ... Taiping Christianity". [source: HXQ; TR]

## The Taiping war, 1850–1864: course
- Rising at Jintian (Guiping, Guangxi): late December 1850 / early January 1851 a rebel army of about 10,000 routed Qing forces. On 11 January 1851 Hong proclaimed himself Heavenly King of the Taiping Tianguo (Heavenly Kingdom of Great Peace). [source: TR]
- Marched north via Hunan; besieged Changsha; took Wuchang in December 1852 and Anqing in February 1853. [source: TR]
- Nanjing captured 19 March 1853 and renamed Tianjing ("Heavenly Capital"); capital until July 1864. The Viceroy of Liangjiang's office became Hong's Palace of the Heavenly King. [source: TR; THK]
- On taking Nanjing the Taiping killed the Manchu population; THK and TR say about 40,000 Manchus; the Xiang Army article says 30,000 Manchu civilians and bannermen — figures vary. [source: THK; TR; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiang_Army]
- Northern Expedition (May 1853–early 1855) under Lin Fengxiang and Li Kaifang came near Tianjin but was destroyed; Western Expedition had limited success. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Expedition_(Taiping_Rebellion); TR]
- Tianjing Incident, September–October 1856: on Hong's orders Wei Changhui and Qin Rigang killed the East King Yang Xiuqing (died 2 September 1856), his family and followers; over 20,000–27,000 died in the purges (sources differ); Hong then executed Wei and Qin; Shi Dakai left the capital with his army. The incident is widely seen as the turning point. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tianjing_Incident; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Rengan; TR]
- Hong Rengan (1822–64), Hong's cousin, had lived in Hong Kong, worked with missionaries (Theodore Hamberg, James Legge); arrived in Nanjing 22 April 1859 and became Shield King, effectively prime minister. His *New Treatise on Aids to Administration* (1859) proposed railways, steamships, banks, patents, a postal service, mining and newspapers. Executed in Nanchang on 23 November 1864, the only Taiping prince who never recanted. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Rengan; THK]
- May 1860: Taiping broke the Qing Jiangnan siege camp and overran southern Jiangsu and Zhejiang, the richest region of the empire (Suzhou taken 2 June 1860). Attacks on Shanghai (1860, 1861–62) were repulsed, with Western-officered forces. [source: TR]
- Battle of Anqing: Xiang Army siege from September 1860; city taken 5 September 1861 by Zeng Guoquan (Zeng Guofan's brother), helped by a British-enforced blockade against rice sales to the rebels; starvation and cannibalism reported. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Anqing]
- Ever Victorious Army: Chinese soldiers with Western officers, raised by the American Frederick Townsend Ward (killed 22 September 1862 at Cixi, Zhejiang), later commanded by the British officer Charles Gordon ("Chinese Gordon"). After Suzhou surrendered in late 1863 Qing forces executed the Taiping prisoners Gordon had promised to spare, enraging him. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_George_Gordon; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Townsend_Ward; TR]
- Shi Dakai surrendered near Chengdu and was executed by slow slicing on 25 June 1863. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shi_Dakai; TR]
- Siege of Nanjing from May 1862. Hong ordered his people to eat "manna", ate weeds himself, fell ill and died 1 June 1864 (illness, possibly suicide by poison — uncertain). His son Hong Tianguifu (about 15) succeeded; Nanjing fell after the wall was mined on 19 July 1864 (Third Battle of Nanjing); the city burned until 26 July. [source: HXQ; TR; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Battle_of_Nanjing]
- Sack of Nanjing: Zeng Guofan reported 100,000 Taiping killed; the battle article gives 200,000–300,000 civilians massacred — high uncertainty. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Battle_of_Nanjing; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiang_Army]
- Hong's body was exhumed, burned, and the ashes fired from a cannon on Zeng Guofan's orders (HXQ says 30 July 1864; TR says 1 August). [source: HXQ; TR]
- Li Xiucheng, the Loyal King, was captured 22 July and wrote a confession before being executed on 7 August 1864; he advised the Qing to buy and copy the best Western cannon. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Battle_of_Nanjing; TR]
- Remnants fought on; Wang Haiyang defeated January 1866; the last army (Li Fuzhong) destroyed August 1871. Spillover into Vietnam: Black Flag Army of Liu Yongfu grew out of Taiping remnants. [source: TR]
- Western stance: European powers stayed officially neutral at first; a February 1861 British expedition (Sir James Hope) visited Nanjing; after the Taiping refused to spare Shanghai and took Ningbo (December 1861), Britain and France intervened in a limited way. TR adds that Western aid was partly driven by fear that a successful rebellion would make China stronger. [source: TR]

## Taiping ideology, society, land and women
- Banned in Taiping territory, on pain of death: opium, gambling, tobacco, alcohol, polygamy and concubinage, slavery, prostitution. Footbinding banned (Hakka women never bound feet). Solar calendar replaced the lunar one. [source: THK]
- Examinations: the Taiping held civil examinations with Bible texts; THK states that for the first time in Chinese history exams were held for women, and Fu Shanxiang of Nanjing passed and served at the Eastern King's court. (Single-source; treat with caution.) [source: THK]
- Strict separation of the sexes: separate women's camps/units; until 1855 even married couples could not live together; the policy tapered off later. Partly attributed to a translation of the seventh commandment that forbade "licentiousness" as well as adultery. [source: THK; TR]
- Women fought in large numbers early on (distinguishing the Taiping from other 19th-century armies), but far fewer after 1853. Women leaders: Hong Xuanjiao (Hong's sister), Su Sanniang, Qiu Ersao. [source: TR; THK]
- Hong lived in seclusion in his palace from 1853 with many women in his inner chambers, ruling by written proclamation — contradicting the separation rules. [source: TR; THK]
- Land System of the Heavenly Dynasty (天朝田畝制度), promulgated 1853: abolished private landownership in principle; land graded into nine grades by yield and allotted equally to individuals regardless of sex according to household size; society organized in units of 25 families, each with a public granary and church under a sergeant, surplus going to a common ("holy") treasury. Sources agree it was never systematically implemented, largely because of continuous war; in practice the Taiping often left existing landholding and taxes in place. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_System_of_the_Heavenly_Dynasty; details on grades/25 families from WebSearch summaries of the Fordham Modern History Sourcebook translation (https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/taiping.asp) and a Chinese-history journal article (https://en.lishiyushehui.cn/article/download/310) — pages not directly readable from this session]
- Iconoclasm: Buddhist and Confucian books and temples destroyed or converted; libraries in the Yangzi delta almost wiped out. [source: TR; THK]
- Taiping called the Manchus "demons" (yao); the Qing called the Taiping "Yue bandits" and "long-haired bandits" because they refused the queue. [source: TR]
- Social base: Hakka, Guangdong locals and Zhuang (many miners) at first; later Yangzi Delta recruits. Almost none were landlords; few came from the bureaucracy. The landed gentry largely sided with the Qing. [source: TR]
- Missionary disillusion: Roberts, briefly Hong's director of foreign affairs, called Taiping toleration "a farce ... worse than useless"; missionary Divie McCartee saw "no signs of anything resembling Christianity" in Nanjing. [source: TR]

## Death toll (give as ranges)
- Most widely cited estimate: about 20–30 million dead, roughly 5–10% of the population, most from famine and plague rather than battle. Some estimates run to 70 million or 73–100 million (about 25% of the population); figures over 70 million are poorly sourced and contested. There was no census; only about 10% of Taiping records survive. [source: TR; QING says 20–30 million; WebSearch summaries of popular sources giving 20–70 million]
- Often called the deadliest civil war in history and the largest conflict of the 19th century; Platt compares it to the First World War in deaths. [source: TR citing Platt 2012 p. xxiii]
- Some 30 million people fled the war zones. Population of China fell by over 30 million between 1850 and 1873 (Taiping, drought, famine). [source: TR; POP]
- Both sides committed massacres: Taiping massacres of Manchus; Qing massacres of Hakka in Guangdong after the war and of civilians in Anhui and Nanjing; the Xiang Army used scorched-earth tactics and refused to take prisoners. [source: TR]
- Aftermath: Anhui, southern Jiangsu, northern Zhejiang and northern Jiangxi were badly depopulated and resettled from Henan and elsewhere; refugees boosted Shanghai's growth. [source: TR]

## Naming debate
- Qing sources called it a "rebellion"/"chaos"; the PRC calls it the "Taiping Heavenly Kingdom Movement"; Jian Youwen called it the "Taiping Revolutionary Movement"; in 2013 Tobie Meyer-Fong and Stephen Platt argued "Taiping Rebellion" presumes Qing legitimacy and preferred "civil war"; Jürgen Osterhammel calls it the "Taiping Revolution". [source: TR]

## The Nian and other contemporary rebellions
- Nian Rebellion, 1851–1868 (some sources 1853–68), northern China (Huaibei, the Anhui–Henan–Shandong–Jiangsu region). "Nian" was a local word for bands/gangs. Led by Zhang Lexing ("Bright King of the Great Han"); slogan "kill the rich and aid the poor"; mobile cavalry and fortified "earthwall" villages. Triggered partly by the Yellow River floods of 1851 and 1855 (the 1855 flood changed the river's course). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nian_Rebellion]
- The Nian cooperated with the Taiping at times; Taiping commander Lai Wenguang joined them after 1864. Mongol general Sengge Rinchen killed Zhang Lexing in 1863 but was himself killed in an ambush in 1865. Zeng Guofan's canal-and-trench containment strategy was slow; Li Hongzhang and Zuo Zongtang crushed the Nian by 1868. Political scientists Hudson and den Boer link the Nian to a surplus of unmarried young men ("bare branches") caused by female infanticide — a hypothesis. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nian_Rebellion]
- Punti–Hakka Clan Wars in Guangdong, 1855–1867/68: 500,000 to over 1 million dead. Miao Rebellion in Guizhou, 1854–1873. Red Turban (Tiandihui) risings in Guangdong 1854–56; Small Swords Society seized Shanghai's walled city 1853. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punti%E2%80%93Hakka_Clan_Wars; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miao_Rebellion_(1854%E2%80%931873); TR]
- Spence argues a key reason for Taiping defeat was failure to coordinate with other rebellions. [source: TR citing Spence 1999]

## Muslim rebellions: Panthay (Yunnan) and Dungan (Northwest)
- Panthay Rebellion, 1856–1873 (TR says 1855–73), Yunnan; "Panthay" is a Burmese name, not used in Yunnan itself. Sparked by a 1856 anti-Muslim massacre in Kunming (about 3,000 killed per the Du Wenxiu article) instigated by a Manchu official; earlier Han–Hui violence included the 1845 Baoshan massacre and miners' conflicts. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panthay_Rebellion; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Du_Wenxiu]
- Du Wenxiu (1823–1872), from a Han family raised Muslim, a xiucai degree holder, took Dali (1856/57) and ruled the Pingnan Guo ("Pacified South State") as Sultan Sulayman; he used anti-Manchu rather than anti-Han rhetoric and appointed Han to a third of military and most civil posts; non-Muslim Shan, Kachin, Bai and others joined. The Hui leader Ma Rulong defected to the Qing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panthay_Rebellion; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Du_Wenxiu]
- In 1872 Du sent his adopted son Prince Hassan to Britain with a letter to Queen Victoria seeking recognition; Britain refused. Dali fell to Qing forces (with French artillery experts) — Du died 26 December 1872 (took opium and was beheaded before it acted); Dali's fall is dated January 1873 in the Panthay article. Up to a million people died in Yunnan; refugees founded Panglong in the Wa State, Burma, about 1875. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panthay_Rebellion; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Du_Wenxiu]
- Dungan Revolt (Tongzhi Hui Revolt), 1862–1877, Shaanxi, Gansu, Ningxia, then Xinjiang. Began with local brawls and rumours (including that Hui were aiding the Taiping), Hui massacres of Han and Han revenge massacres of Hui. The article gives a population loss of about 21 million (massacres, migration, famine, plague); Gansu lost 74.5% of its population; at least 4 million Hui in Shaanxi before, about 20,000 after. Infobox: 8–12 million deaths. These are very high estimates from Chinese demographic research — present as estimates. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dungan_Revolt_(1862%E2%80%931877)]
- Zuo Zongtang's siege of Ma Hualong's stronghold Jinjibao lasted 16 months; Ma surrendered January 1871 and was executed by slicing with over 80 officials. Zuo spared "Old Teaching" leaders (Ma Zhan'ao, Ma Anliang) who became Qing commanders. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dungan_Revolt_(1862%E2%80%931877)]
- In Kashgaria, Yaqub Beg (a Kokandi commander) built a state; Zuo's army reconquered Xinjiang in the late 1870s; Russia returned most of Ili by the Treaty of Saint Petersburg (1881), seen as a Qing diplomatic success; Xinjiang became a province in 1884. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zuo_Zongtang; SSM; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qing_dynasty_in_Inner_Asia]

## Zeng Guofan and the regional armies
- Zeng Guofan (26 November 1811 – 12 March 1872), from Xiangxiang, Hunan; jinshi 1838, Hanlin Academy. In mourning for his mother in Hunan in 1852 when ordered to raise local forces against the Taiping. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeng_Guofan]
- The Xiang (Hunan) Army grew from local tuanlian militia; financed by local gentry, merchants and diverted provincial transit taxes (lijin-type tolls) rather than Beijing; soldiers were loyal to their commanders ("soldiers belonged to the general"); its personal loyalty structure is often linked to later warlordism — a link some historians (cited by Diana Lary) doubt. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiang_Army; TR]
- Zeng was defeated early (Jiujiang, January–February 1855) and attempted suicide. Became Viceroy of Liangjiang in June 1860. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeng_Guofan; TR]
- After Nanjing, Zeng disbanded most of his 120,000 men. By 1865, five of eight viceroys were Hunanese. Li Hongzhang (Zeng's pupil) formed the Huai Army from Anhui in 1862; Zuo Zongtang later formed the Chu Army. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiang_Army; TR; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_Hongzhang]
- Qing forces c. 1851: Eight Banners about 250,000; Green Standard Army 611,200; Xiang Army up to 130,000; Huai Army 60,000–70,000 (Osprey figures). [source: TR]
- Zeng handled the 1870 Tianjin Massacre (about 60 killed, mainly French Catholic nuns, priests and Chinese converts, after rumours that orphanages killed children); his conciliatory handling was criticized. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tianjin_Massacre; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeng_Guofan]
- Legacy contested in China: criticized as a "traitor" serving a foreign dynasty; praised by both Mao Zedong and Chiang Kai-shek; rehabilitated in the PRC after the Cultural Revolution (e.g. Tang Haoming's 1992 novel trilogy). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeng_Guofan]

## Tongzhi Restoration and the Self-Strengthening Movement (c. 1861–1895)
- Tongzhi Restoration (c. 1860–1874), named for the Tongzhi Emperor (r. 1861–75): an attempt to restore the Confucian order while adopting "practical" Western techniques. Mary C. Wright called it "the last stand of Chinese conservatism" and wrote that a dynasty and civilization "which appeared to have collapsed was revived to last for another sixty years by the extraordinary efforts of extraordinary men in the 1860s." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tongzhi_Restoration]
- 1861: Zongli Yamen (de facto foreign ministry) set up under Prince Gong and Wenxiang; replaced by the Waiwubu under the 1901 Boxer Protocol. [source: SSM; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zongli_Yamen]
- Tongwen Guan language school in Beijing (1862); astronomy and mathematics added 1867. [source: SSM]
- Imperial Maritime Customs Service under foreign inspectors (Horatio Nelson Lay, then Robert Hart from 1863); customs revenue rose from 8.5 million taels (1865) to 14.5 million (1885) and funded arsenals and schools. [source: SSM]
- Feng Guifen's essays to Zeng Guofan (1861) articulated "self-strengthening"; Li Hongzhang used the term in 1864. The phrase comes from the *Yijing*. [source: SSM]
- Arsenals and shipyards: Jiangnan Arsenal, Shanghai, 1865 (Zeng Guofan and Li Hongzhang, with Yung Wing; largest arsenal, budget over 400,000 taels a year from 1869; translated 143 Western books 1868–79); Fuzhou (Foochow) Navy Yard, planned 1866 by Zuo Zongtang, built 1867–74 with French officer Prosper Giquel; Jinling Arsenal, Nanjing (Li Hongzhang); Tianjin arsenal. [source: SSM; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiangnan_Shipyard; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foochow_Arsenal]
- "Government-supervised merchant undertakings": China Merchants' Steam Navigation Company (1872; grew from 4 to 29 steamers 1872–77), Kaiping Mines (1877), Imperial Telegraph Administration (1881), Shanghai Cotton Mill (1882). Zhang Zhidong's Hanyang Ironworks and Hubei Textile Mill (1889–94). [source: SSM]
- Chinese Educational Mission (1872–1881): 120 boys sent to New England (headquartered in Hartford) under Yung Wing (Yale 1854, first Chinese graduate of an American university); recalled in 1881 after conservative opposition and after US military academies refused them. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Educational_Mission; SSM]
- Li Hongzhang (15 February 1823 – 7 November 1901), founder of the Huai Army and Beiyang Fleet; over 90% of second-phase modernization projects were launched under him; Viceroy of Zhili from 1870; foreigners called him the "Bismarck of the East". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_Hongzhang; SSM]
- Great North China Famine, 1876–79: 9.5–13 million dead, about 5.5 million in Shanxi. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Chinese_Famine_of_1876%E2%80%931879]
- Evaluation: historians split — Hsu and others see the movement as superficial (no institutional reform, provinces uncoordinated), contrasted with Meiji Japan; Luke Kwong argued it was meant only as adaptive reform and should not be judged a failure; it is credited with keeping the dynasty alive another half-century. [source: SSM]

## Empress Dowager Cixi
- Cixi (29 November 1835 – 15 November 1908), Manchu of the Yehe Nara clan; concubine of the Xianfeng Emperor; mother of his only surviving son (Tongzhi Emperor, born 27 April 1856). [source: CIXI]
- 1861 Xinyou Coup: with Empress Dowager Ci'an and Prince Gong she ousted the eight regents; Sushun beheaded, two others ordered to hang themselves. Ruled "behind the curtain" with Ci'an (died 1881). [source: CIXI]
- After Tongzhi died (1875) she installed her nephew as the Guangxu Emperor; formally retired 1889 but remained decisive. [source: CIXI]
- 1898: ended the Hundred Days' Reform and kept Guangxu under house arrest until his death. Guangxu died 14 November 1908, Cixi the next day; 2008 forensic tests found arsenic about 2,000 times normal levels in Guangxu's remains — who poisoned him is not proven. [source: CIXI; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guangxu_Emperor; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Policies]
- Reputation: the hostile image owes much to Bland and Backhouse's *China Under the Empress Dowager* (1910), based on gossip (Backhouse is now regarded as a forger — see NOT VERIFIED, wording); Sue Fawn Chung's work in the 1970s first used court documents; Jung Chang's *Empress Dowager Cixi* (2013) is a strongly favourable revisionist account that others criticize. [source: CIXI]

## Sino-French War (1884–85)
- Undeclared war, August 1884 – April 1885 (Battle of Fuzhou article: December 1883 – April 1885), over suzerainty in northern Vietnam (Tonkin). The Black Flag Army of Liu Yongfu, a former Taiping-linked force, fought the French for the Vietnamese court. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-French_War; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Fuzhou]
- Battle of Fuzhou, 23 August 1884: Admiral Courbet's squadron virtually destroyed the Fujian Fleet and damaged the Fuzhou Navy Yard. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Fuzhou; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foochow_Arsenal]
- Chinese land victory at Zhennan Pass (Bang Bo), 23–24 March 1885, under Pan Dingxin and Feng Zicai; the French retreat from Lang Son brought down Jules Ferry's government. Treaty of Tianjin (1885): China gave up its claims in Tonkin and Annam. Qing armies "performed better than in their other nineteenth-century wars." Taiwan was made a separate province in 1885/1887 (sources give 1887 for formal establishment), with Liu Mingchuan as first governor. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-French_War; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Bang_Bo_(Zhennan_Pass); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liu_Mingchuan]

## First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95)
- 25 July 1894 – 17 April 1895; called the Jiawu War in China; formally declared 1 August 1894. Trigger: Korea's Donghak (Tonghak) peasant rebellion; Qing sent troops at King Gojong's request (June 1894); Japan sent 8,000 troops, seized the king and palace (23 July). The British-owned transport *Kowshing*, carrying about 1,100–1,200 Qing troops, was sunk on 25 July. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Sino-Japanese_War]
- Battle of Pyongyang 15 September; Beiyang Fleet defeated at the Yalu River 17 September 1894; Port Arthur (Lüshunkou) taken 21 November 1894, followed by a massacre (estimates 2,600 to 20,000, some sources up to 60,000); Weihaiwei fell February 1895 (siege 20 January – 12 February); Admiral Ding Ruchang killed himself. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Sino-Japanese_War; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Arthur_massacre_(China); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Weihaiwei]
- On 24 March 1895 at Shimonoseki, Li Hongzhang was shot in the face by a Japanese would-be assassin (Koyama Toyotarō); the embarrassment led Japan to agree a ceasefire. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_Hongzhang]
- Treaty of Shimonoseki, 17 April 1895: China recognized Korea's full independence (ending tribute); ceded Taiwan, the Penghu Islands and the Liaodong Peninsula; paid 200 million Kuping taels; opened Shashi, Chongqing, Suzhou and Hangzhou; Japanese allowed to operate factories in treaty ports. The Triple Intervention (Russia, Germany, France) forced Liaodong's return for a further 30 million taels; Russia then leased Port Arthur. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Shimonoseki; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Sino-Japanese_War]
- Taiwanese resistance: the Republic of Formosa was proclaimed 23 May 1895 (Tang Jingsong; later Liu Yongfu) and lasted 151 days; Japan's takeover was complete by October 1895; about 14,000 Taiwanese killed (including civilians). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Formosa; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1895_Japanese_invasion_of_Taiwan]
- "Scramble for concessions" 1897–98: Germany seized Jiaozhou Bay after the Juye Incident (November 1897, two German missionaries killed); Russia, Britain and France took leases. [source: BOX; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Shimonoseki]

## Hundred Days' Reform (1898)
- 1895 Gongche Shangshu ("Petition of the Examination Candidates"): on 22 April 1895 examination candidates in Beijing led by Kang Youwei signed a long petition against the Shimonoseki treaty calling for reform; considered China's first modern political movement. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gongche_Shangshu_movement]
- Kang Youwei (19 March 1858 – 31 March 1927), Guangdong scholar; argued in *A Study of Confucius as a Reformer* that Confucius was a reformer and (1891) that the "Old Text" classics were forged; utopian *Datong Shu*. After 1898 led the Protect the Emperor Society among overseas Chinese; backed the failed 1917 restoration of Puyi. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kang_Youwei]
- Liang Qichao (23 February 1873 – 19 January 1929), Kang's student, journalist; fled to Japan in 1898 and stayed 14 years; Lin Yutang called him "the greatest personality in the history of Chinese journalism". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liang_Qichao]
- Reform: 11 June – 21 September 1898, edicts of the Guangxu Emperor on education (Imperial University of Peking, now Peking University, the main lasting result), examinations, administration, army and industry. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_Days%27_Reform]
- Coup of 21 September 1898: Tan Sitong reportedly asked Yuan Shikai to kill Ronglu and move on Beijing; Yuan told Ronglu instead. Cixi put Guangxu under house arrest. The "Six Gentlemen" (Tan Sitong, Yang Shenxiu, Lin Xu, Liu Guangdi, Kang Guangren, Yang Rui) were beheaded at Caishikou on 28 September 1898; Kang and Liang escaped to Japan. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_Days%27_Reform; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tan_Sitong]

## The Boxers and the eight-nation intervention (1899–1901)
- Society of Righteous and Harmonious Fists (Yihetuan) arose in Shandong amid drought, Yellow River flooding (1898), resentment of missionaries who protected converts in lawsuits, and foreign spheres of influence. Spirit possession and claimed invulnerability to bullets. Slogan from October 1898: "Support the Qing, destroy the foreigners" (fu Qing mie yang). [source: BOX]
- Joseph Esherick calls "Boxer Rebellion" a misnomer, since the Boxers never rebelled against the Qing. [source: BOX]
- Red Lanterns: young women's units (roughly 11–17 years old), also Blue and Black Lanterns for older women; leader Lin Hei'er, the "Holy Mother of the Yellow Lotus"; Boxer ideology treated adult women as polluting. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Lanterns_(Boxer_Uprising)]
- Allies stormed the Dagu forts on 17 June 1900; on 21 June Cixi issued a de facto declaration of war against the foreign powers (11 countries). German minister von Ketteler was killed on 20 June. Siege of the Legations 20 June – 14 August 1900 (55 days): 473 foreign civilians, 409 soldiers and about 3,000 Chinese Christians sheltered. [source: BOX; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_Defense_Pact_of_the_Southeastern_Provinces; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_the_International_Legations]
- Deaths among Christians: BOX infobox gives 32,000 Chinese Christians and 200 Western missionaries killed in north China; 100,000 total deaths (estimate). In Taiyuan, Shanxi governor Yuxian reportedly executed 44 foreigners including women and children (July 1900); up to 2,000 Chinese Christians killed in Shanxi. [source: BOX]
- Eight-Nation Alliance: Britain, Russia, Japan, France, Germany, the US, Italy, Austria-Hungary. Japan sent the largest contingent (20,840). Britain's force included Indian units (Bengal Lancers, Rajputs, Punjabis, Sikhs) and the Hong Kong Regiment. About 20,000 troops marched on Beijing, arriving 14 August; Russia sent large forces into Manchuria. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight-Nation_Alliance; BOX]
- Cixi and Guangxu fled to Xi'an. Foreign troops looted Beijing and carried out punitive expeditions with summary executions, rape and village burnings; German troops arrived after the main fighting. The Hanlin Academy library burned during the siege (each side blamed the other); part of the *Yongle Encyclopedia* was destroyed. [source: BOX; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight-Nation_Alliance; CIXI]
- Mutual Protection of the Southeast: Viceroys Li Hongzhang, Liu Kunyi, Zhang Zhidong and others (Yuan Shikai in Shandong also ignored the decree) refused the war edict and kept their provinces neutral — for the first time most Han regional authorities refused to aid the court. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_Defense_Pact_of_the_Southeastern_Provinces; BOX]
- Boxer Protocol, 7 September 1901 (signed by Li Hongzhang, who died two months later): indemnity of 450 million taels over 39 years at 4% interest, totalling about 982 million taels by 1940; execution or suicide of pro-Boxer officials; foreign troops in Beijing; Zongli Yamen replaced by a Foreign Ministry. The US remitted part of its share in 1908 to fund Chinese students and what became Tsinghua University. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxer_Protocol; BOX; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_Hongzhang]

## Late-Qing New Policies (1901–1911)
- Begun 1901 with Cixi's backing; more radical than Self-Strengthening. Military examination abolished 1901; the civil service examination system abolished by decree on 2 September 1905 (sponsors Yuan Shikai, Zhang Zhidong, Yin Chang); modern schools; students sent abroad (mainly to Japan). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Policies; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_examination]
- New Army: Beiyang Army reorganized 1905; target 500,000 men but only about 190,000 well trained by 1911. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Policies]
- Constitutionalism: five ministers sent abroad 1905–06 (met Theodore Roosevelt); decree of 1 September 1906 announcing preparation for a constitution; "Constitutional Outline" of August 1908 with a nine-year timetable; provincial assemblies and Advisory Council elected 1909, met 1910. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Policies]
- May 1911 "royal cabinet" under Prince Qing: 13 members, 8 Manchu (7 of them imperial clan), 4 Han, 1 Mongol — alienated constitutionalists. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Policies]
- Puyi (born 7 February 1906) enthroned 2 December 1908 aged two, with his father Prince Chun (Zaifeng) as regent. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puyi]
- Historians differ: Immanuel Hsu called the New Policies largely "a noisy demonstration without much substance" apart from ending the exams, new schools and study abroad; Douglas Reynolds (*China, 1898–1912: The Xinzheng Revolution and Japan*, 1993) treats them as a revolution in their own right. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Policies]

## Sun Yat-sen and the revolutionaries
- Sun Yat-sen (12 November 1866 – 12 March 1925), from Cuiheng, Xiangshan county, Guangdong; schooled in Honolulu (ʻIolani School) from 1878; baptized 1884; graduated from the Hong Kong College of Medicine for Chinese in 1892. In 1894 petitioned Li Hongzhang with reform proposals (rejected). Founded the Revive China Society in Honolulu on 24 November 1894. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Yat-sen; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revive_China_Society]
- Detained at the Chinese Legation in London 11 October 1896, released after 12 days through James Cantlie and the press, which made him famous. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Yat-sen]
- Sun came from the same area as Hong Xiuquan and reportedly identified with him; Taiping veterans joined the Revive China Society. [source: HXQ; TR]
- Tongmenghui (Revolutionary Alliance) founded in Tokyo on 20 August 1905 by Sun, Song Jiaoren and others, from student groups; 963 members by 1906; branches in Singapore and Penang funded by overseas Chinese. Three Principles of the People: nationalism, democracy, people's livelihood. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tongmenghui; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Yat-sen; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Principles_of_the_People]
- Many uprisings failed (four in 1907 alone, including Zhennanguan on the Vietnam border, December 1907); Second Guangzhou (Yellow Flower Mound) Uprising, 27 April 1911, led by Huang Xing — commemorated as the "72 martyrs" (86 casualties per the infobox). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Yat-sen; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Guangzhou_Uprising]
- Qiu Jin (8 November 1875 – 15 July 1907): feminist revolutionary; left husband and children to study in Japan; edited a vernacular journal; wrote "A Respectful Proclamation to China's 200 Million Women Comrades" against footbinding and forced marriage; headed the Datong School in Shaoxing; arrested after Xu Xilin's failed assassination of the Anhui governor and beheaded at Shanyin aged 31. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qiu_Jin]

## The 1911 Revolution
- Railway Protection Movement (1911), centred on Sichuan: in May 1911 the court moved to nationalize provincial railway companies (the Sichuan–Hankou company had raised about 12 million taels, 77.5% from a land tax on gentry shareholders) and finance them with foreign bank loans; troops sent from Hubei to suppress it left Wuchang thinly held. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_Protection_Movement; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wuchang_Uprising]
- 9 October 1911: a bomb went off accidentally in the Russian concession in Hankou where Sun Wu was making explosives; exposure forced the plotters' hand. 10 October 1911: New Army units (Literary Society and Progressive Association) rose in Wuchang; the Viceroy Ruicheng fled; Li Yuanhong was put at the head. Sun Yat-sen was in the United States and learned of it from the newspapers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wuchang_Uprising; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Yat-sen]
- Province after province declared independence; 1 November 1911 the court made Yuan Shikai prime minister. Revolutionaries massacred Manchu banner garrisons in several cities (Xi'an: estimated 10,000–20,000 Manchus killed in October 1911; also Nanjing, Zhenjiang, Taiyuan, Fuzhou, Wuchang). [source: REV]
- 1 January 1912: Republic of China proclaimed in Nanjing with Sun as provisional president. Sun agreed to resign in Yuan's favour if Yuan secured the abdication. [source: REV; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Yat-sen]
- 12 February 1912: abdication edict of the six-year-old Xuantong Emperor (Puyi), accepted by Empress Dowager Longyu, ending 267 years of Qing rule and over two millennia of imperial rule. Yuan sworn in as president 10 March 1912. Tibet and Mongolia broke away. [source: REV; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puyi]
- Afterward: Yuan proclaimed himself emperor (December 1915), abandoned it March 1916, died 6 June 1916 — opening the warlord era. 10 October is National Day in Taiwan (ROC); both the ROC and PRC claim the revolution's legacy. [source: REV; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuan_Shikai]

## Debates
- What to call it: rebellion, revolution or civil war (Meyer-Fong and Platt vs Qing usage; Jian Youwen; Osterhammel). [source: TR]
- Death toll: 20–30 million is the standard range; higher figures (up to 70–100 million) count famine, flight and lost births differently and are contested. [source: TR]
- Communist/Nationalist memory: Mao and the CCP treated the Taiping as a proto-communist peasant revolution; Marx wrote about the Taiping but saw only destruction; Fairbank compared the Taiping and Mao's Communists (zeal, utopian order, farmer-soldiers, translated foreign ideology). Zeng Guofan: traitor to the Han or saviour of order? [source: TR; HXQ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeng_Guofan]
- Regional armies and warlordism: Franz Michael linked the Xiang/Huai armies to later warlord armies; others (reviewed by Diana Lary) are skeptical. [source: TR]
- Impact vs internal dynamics: Kuhn helped move the field from Fairbank's "impact–response" to change originating inside China ("what was happening in eighteenth century China?"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_A._Kuhn]
- Western intervention: British policy shifted from armed neutrality to limited intervention around 1862; historians debate motive (wanting a weak Qing vs a stable one for trade) and how decisive it was; J. S. Gregory (1969) argued early neutrality favoured neither side; Platt sets British choices in the global context of the American Civil War. [source: WebSearch summaries of https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/output/384963/british-attitudes-and-policy-towards-the-taiping-rebellion-in-china-1850-64 and https://www.thedailybeast.com/autumn-in-the-heavenly-kingdom-by-stephen-r-platt-review — Platt's exact thesis not read directly]
- Self-Strengthening: failure (Hsu, Gasster, Liu) vs adaptive success (Kwong); comparison with Meiji Japan. [source: SSM]
- Cixi: reactionary despot (Bland & Backhouse tradition) vs scapegoated pragmatist (Chung; Jung Chang). Whether she diverted navy funds to the Summer Palace (Marble Boat) before 1894 is contested; CIXI/SSM cite Jung Chang that Weng Tonghe and Guangxu cut naval funding — treat as one side of a debate. [source: CIXI; SSM]
- Boxers: anti-imperialist patriots (PRC textbooks, Esherick in part) vs irrational xenophobes (older Western and Taiwan/HK textbooks); Cohen's three keys separate event, lived experience and myth. [source: BOX; Cohen book]
- Great Divergence: Pomeranz/California School (divergence after 1800, coal and colonies) vs Broadberry and others (England already ahead of the Yangzi Delta by 1600–1700). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Divergence_(book)]
- New Policies: "noisy demonstration" (Hsu) vs real "Xinzheng revolution" (Reynolds). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Policies]
