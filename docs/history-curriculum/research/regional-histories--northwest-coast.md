## Peoples, languages and the region
- The potlatch-holding peoples of the Northwest Coast include the Heiltsuk, Haida, Nuxalk, Tlingit, Makah, Tsimshian, Nuu-chah-nulth, Kwakwaka'wakw and Coast Salish. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- Haida is spoken on Haida Gwaii and on Prince of Wales Island, Alaska; it is generally considered a language isolate, though some linguists (e.g. Enrico 2004) place it in Na-Dené. Sapir included Haida in Na-Dené in 1915; Boas first suggested a link with Tlingit in 1894. Dialects: Alaskan (Kaigani), Masset, Skidegate (Ninstints extinct). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haida_language]
- Haida speaker numbers are uncertain: the Wikipedia infobox gives 105, the text "24 native speakers"; UNESCO lists it as critically endangered. As of 2003 most speakers were aged 70–80. Treat any single figure as approximate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haida_language]
- Pre-contact Haida population about 15,000; the first smallpox epidemic soon after contact reduced it to about 10,000. (Date of first contact: see NOT VERIFIED.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haida_language]
- The first feature film acted entirely in Haida, *SGaawaay K'uuna* ("Edge of the Knife"), premiered at the 2018 Toronto International Film Festival. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haida_language]
- Haida society is divided into two moieties, Raven and Eagle (Skaay belonged to the Eagle side). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skaay]
- Haida oral history begins with the arrival of the ancestresses of the matrilineages in Haida Gwaii, including SGuuluu Jaad (Foam Woman), Jiila Kuns (Creek Woman) and KalGa Jaad (Ice Woman). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haida_people]
- The Haida Nation "has rooted its democracy in potlatch law." In December 2009 British Columbia officially renamed the Queen Charlotte Islands as Haida Gwaii. In November 1985, 72 Haida were arrested during the Lyell Island logging blockade. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haida_people]
- Tlingit society is divided into two moieties, Raven and Eagle, subdivided into clans and house groups; descent and inheritance are matrilineal; clans hold heraldic crests displayed on poles, canoes, feast dishes, weavings and other objects. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tlingit]
- Tlingit language: UNESCO "critically endangered"; Golla (2007) reported at most 500 speakers in Alaska and 185 in Canada; another estimate is ~50 highly proficient L1 speakers in the US (2020). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tlingit_language]
- Tsimshian society is matrilineal; clans are Gispwudwada (Killer Whale/Fireweed), Ganhada (Raven/Frog), Laxgibuu (Wolf), Laxsgiik (Eagle). The Coast Tsimshian language is Sm'algyax ("real or true tongue"); about 160 speakers in Canada in 2016. Gitxsan and Nisga'a are related Tsimshianic languages. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsimshian]
- Gitxsan ("people of the River of Mist", i.e. the Skeena) are matrilineal, with Frog, Eagle, Wolf and Fireweed clans divided into Houses (*Wilp*), each with a high chief, territories and fishing sites. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gitxsan]
- The Wet'suwet'en, Delgamuukw co-plaintiffs, are not linguistically Northwest Coast people: they speak Witsuwit'en, an Athabaskan (Dene) language, a branch of the Dakelh/Carrier; five clans, 13 house groups, matrilineal. Their oral history is called *kungax*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wet%CA%BCsuwet%CA%BCen]
- Kwak'wala is a Wakashan language; 2021 census: 255 speakers (text says "about 150"). The name "Kwakiutl" derives from Kwagu'ł, a single community at Fort Rupert, and was popularized by Boas for the whole group. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwak%CA%BCwala ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwakwaka%CA%BCwakw]
- Kwak'wala has four competing orthographies; younger learners are taught U'mista or NAPA, while older generations often use the "Boas" system. Guy Buchholtzer: "The anthropological discourse had too often become a long monologue, in which the Kwakwa̱ka̱ʼwakw had nothing to say." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwakwaka%CA%BCwakw]
- Heiltsuk is a Northern Wakashan language (with Wuikyala, Haisla, Kwak'wala). The Heiltsuk shared the Tánis (Hamatsa) ceremony with the Kwakwaka'wakw; potlatching revived publicly from the late 1960s through the 1980s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heiltsuk]
- Nuu-chah-nulth: Cook's 1778 encounter at Yuquot gave rise to the name "Nootka". Whaling (over 4,000 years old by carbon dating) "is reflected in stories, songs, names, family lines, and numerous place names." The Yuquot Whalers' Shrine is held at the American Museum of Natural History, New York; George Hunt was instrumental in its purchase in 1904. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuu-chah-nulth ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hunt_(ethnologist)]
- Coast Salish: speakers of fourteen contiguous Salishan languages around the Salish Sea, Puget Sound and the Lower Mainland of BC. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coast_Salish_peoples]
- Stó:lō (Coast Salish, Fraser River) elders say "we have always been here"; their landscape was "transformed by Xexá:ls" (the Transformers); transformer stones mark sites such as Xá:ytem (Hatzic). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St%C3%B3%EA%9E%89l%C5%8D]
- Chinookan peoples live along the Columbia River. Boas published *Chinook Texts* in 1894. Hymes analysed texts told by Charles Cultee, a Kathlamet Chinook speaker, to Boas at Bay Center, Washington, in 1890 (and the "Salmon's Myth" told twice, in 1890 and 1894). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinookan_peoples ; https://journals.uvic.ca/index.php/WPLC/article/view/5511/2118]
- Chinook Jargon (Chinuk Wawa) began as a pidgin trade language on the lower Columbia and spread in the 19th century to Oregon, Washington, BC and parts of Alaska; about 15% of its vocabulary is French-derived; a creolized form survives at Grand Ronde, Oregon. Its origins are debated (pre-contact Chinookan–Nuu-chah-nulth lingua franca vs. post-contact Nootka Sound origin vs. Barbara Harris's "dual genesis"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinook_Jargon]
- The word "potlatch" comes from Chinook Jargon ("to give away"/"a gift"), originally from Nuu-chah-nulth *paɬaˑč*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- Status in BC overall: the First Peoples' Cultural Council 2022 report covered all 34 First Nations languages in BC; about 10,355 fluent speakers; fluent speakers fell 18% between 2018 and 2022; seven languages had five or fewer fluent speakers; about 17,000 learners (≈3,000 more than 2018). [source: https://fpcc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/FPCC-LanguageReport-23.02.14-FINAL.pdf (via search summary of CBC/CTV reports)]

## Genres: Raven, transformers, crest histories
- Raven Tales are creation stories of Northwest Coast peoples, most prominent among the Haida, Tsimshian, Tlingit and Tahltan. Raven is a shape-shifter, "a keeper of secrets, and a trickster often focused on satisfying his own gluttony"; his stories tell how things came to be. Some storytellers hold that Raven stories (as opposed to tall tales and children's stories) "are not entertainment and can be the cultural property of the clan or individual". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravens_in_Native_American_mythology]
- A widespread episode: Raven steals light — he turns into a speck of dirt, is swallowed by the daughter of the owner of daylight, is reborn as a fussy child who cries for the boxes of light and releases them. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravens_in_Native_American_mythology]
- Haida name for Raven: *Yáahl*. Haida see Raven as more than a trickster — "magician, a transformer, a potent creative force, ravenous debaucher but always a cultural hero"; he released the sun from a box and found the first humans in a clamshell. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haida_mythology]
- Bill Reid's sculpture *The Raven and the First Men* (laminated yellow cedar, begun autumn 1978, completed/unveiled 1 April 1980 by Prince Charles; celebrated by the Haida 5 June 1980) at the UBC Museum of Anthropology shows Raven coaxing the first Haida from a clamshell on Rose Spit; carving assisted by Gidansda Guujaaw, George Norris, George Rammell, Reg Davidson and Jim Hart. It appeared on the Canadian $20 note (2004–2011). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Raven_and_the_First_Men]
- Bringhurst co-wrote *The Raven Steals the Light* (1984) with Bill Reid. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bringhurst]
- Kwakwaka'wakw *numaym* (house) identity came from an ancestral founder, "usually a mythical animal who descended to earth and removed his animal mask, thus becoming human"; the mask and the founder's name passed to the leader, who embodied the founder. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- Boas came to argue *numaym* meant "a bundle of privileges, for which there is no English word" rather than "clan". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Boas]
- Nisga'a totem poles record family histories called *adaawak*; scholar Amy Parent (Sigidimnak' Nox̱s Ts'aawit) called the Ni'isjoohl pole "a living constitutional and visual archive". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni%27isjoohl_totem_pole]
- Gitxsan *adaawḵ* are described as personal/house bloodline histories; Wet'suwet'en *kungax* as songs about trails between territories (as characterized in the Delgamuukw literature). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delgamuukw_v_British_Columbia]
- Kwakwaka'wakw stories from north Vancouver Island, Cowichan, Makah, Quileute and Huu-ay-aht stories tell of a great night-time earthquake and flood; a regional art motif shows a battle between Thunderbird and Whale (see Cascadia section). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1700_Cascadia_earthquake]

## The potlatch as the setting of oral literature
- The potlatch is "traditionally the primary governmental institution, legislative body, and economic system" of Northwest Coast peoples; it reaffirms family, clan and international ties; "The honouring of the supernatural and the recitation of oral histories are a central part of many potlatches." Held for births, deaths, adoptions, weddings and succession. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- At potlatches, titles associated with masks were "fastened on" to new holders; ranked "seats" carried rights to hunting, fishing and berrying territories. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- A 19th-century Speaker Figure (Brooklyn Museum): an orator standing behind it spoke through its mouth, announcing arriving guests. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- Practice varied: Tlingit potlatches were for succession and funerals; Kwakwaka'wakw for marriages and incorporating new members. Most detailed knowledge comes from the Kwakwaka'wakw around Fort Rupert in 1849–1925, a period of crisis when potlatching was intensified by depopulation and government pressure — so generalizing from Boas's Kwakiutl data is risky. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- Pre-contact Northwest Coast societies were ranked (nobles, commoners, slaves); slaves could be potlatch gifts. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwakwaka%CA%BCwakw]
- Marcel Mauss used the potlatch in *The Gift* (1925) as a model of "total prestations". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]

## The potlatch ban (1885–1951) and the Cranmer potlatch
- The Indian Act amendment banning the "Potlatch" and the "Tamanawas" dance was passed in 1884 (signed 19 April 1884) and is usually dated as in force from 1885; penalty two to six months' imprisonment. Sources variously say 1884 or 1885; the ban ran until 1951, when the section (s. 149) was dropped in the Indian Act revision. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch_ban ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Act ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- 1896 amendment extended the ban to dances/ceremonies involving wounding or giving away money or goods; 1895 Sun Dance ban; a 1927 amendment barred raising funds for land claims without permission. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Act]
- Missionary William Duncan (1875) called the potlatch "by far the most formidable of all obstacles in the way of Indians becoming Christians, or even civilized". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- Not all Indigenous people opposed the ban: in 1883 Coast Tsimshian and Nisga'a chiefs at Port Simpson, Kincolith and Greenville petitioned that potlatching "be put down". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch_ban]
- Boas opposed the ban; in 1888 he wrote that the law "is not a good one, and can not be enforced without causing general discontent." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- Duncan Campbell Scott had the offence changed from criminal to summary, so Indian agents as justices of the peace could "try a case, convict, and sentence". First person charged: Bill Uslick (Stó:lō), arrested 1 February 1896. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch_ban]
- Arrests were few until the 1921 raid after Chief Dan Cranmer's Christmas 1921 potlatch at 'Mimkwamlis (Memkumlis), Village Island ('Namgis/Mamalilikulla). Wikipedia: 45 people arrested and charged; 22 suspended sentences; 20 men and women sent to Oakalla Prison; two months for first offenders, three for second. KnowBC (via search) gives 49 charged and 26 jailed — give a range. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch_ban ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memkumlis ; search summary of https://aptnnews.ca/?p=148427 and https://www.knowbc.com/knowbc/Books/Encyclopedia-of-BC/P/Potlatch]
- Participants were offered suspended sentences if they surrendered their regalia; U'mista says the Kwakwaka'wakw later showed that Indian agent William Halliday had illegally pressured them. The Canadian Museum for Human Rights reports about 750 items confiscated and dispersed among museums in Canada and the US. [source: search summaries of https://umista.ca/pages/collection-history and https://humanrights.ca/story/bringing-potlatch-home ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U%27mista_Cultural_Society]
- Repatriation: the Kwagiulth Museum (now Nuyumbalees Cultural Centre) at Cape Mudge, Quadra Island, opened 29 July 1979; the U'mista Cultural Centre at Alert Bay opened 1 November 1980 (society incorporated 22 March 1974). "U'mista" is a Kwak'wala word for the return of something important that had been taken. The Royal Ontario Museum returned its portion in 1988; the National Museum of the American Indian returned pieces (dates given as 1993 and 2002/2003). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U%27mista_Cultural_Society ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloria_Cranmer_Webster ; search summaries of https://humanrights.ca/story/bringing-potlatch-home and https://umista.ca/pages/collection-history]
- Gloria Cranmer Webster (1931–2023), Dan Cranmer's daughter and George Hunt's great-granddaughter, was a driving force behind U'mista; she narrated the 1973 film *The Potlatch: A Strict Law Bids Us Dance* and helped develop a Kwak'wala orthography. A founding board member said "Indians don't go to museums." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloria_Cranmer_Webster]
- After 1951 potlatching revived openly, gaining strength in the 1970s–80s. Sergei Kan, invited by Tlingit to potlatches in 1980–87, noted a language gap between elders and younger participants. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch_ban ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- Return of a crest pole: the Ni'isjoohl pole (Nisga'a, carved 1860s by Oyea Tait), removed by Marius Barbeau in 1929 and held in Edinburgh, was agreed for return in 2022 and arrived at Lax̱g̱alts'ap in September 2023 — the second pole returned from Europe after the G'psgolox pole (Sweden, 2006). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni%27isjoohl_totem_pole ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marius_Barbeau]
- Robert Davidson (Haida, b. 1946) raised a pole (*Bear Mother*) at Masset on 22 August 1969, widely described as the first on Haida Gwaii in roughly a century (gap given variously as ~90 years, since 1871, or since the 1880s); over 1,000 attended; elders insisted on raising it the traditional way. [source: search summaries of https://www.theglobeandmail.com/arts/art-and-architecture/standing-tall-robert-davidson-at-the-centre-of-a-rebirth-in-northwest-coast-art/article16186174/ and https://mediaspace.nfb.ca/epk/now-is-the-time/]

## The collectors and the Indigenous co-authors
### Franz Boas and George Hunt
- Franz Boas (b. Minden, 9 July 1858 – d. 21 December 1942) met Nuxalk visitors at the Berlin ethnological museum, then made a three-month trip to British Columbia in 1886; settled in the US from 1887; professor at Columbia from 1899. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Boas]
- Boas organized the Jesup North Pacific Expedition (from 1897, five years). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Boas]
- Boas's method relied on "literate native ethnographers (among the Kwakiutl, most often George Hunt)"; he urged students to treat them as partners. He later criticized his own early Kwakiutl work. Before his death he appointed Helen Codere to edit his Kwakiutl manuscripts. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Boas]
- George Hunt (14 February 1854 – 1933), born at Fort Rupert to Robert Hunt (HBC, from Dorset) and Mary Ebbetts/Anislaga (Tlingit, Raven clan, Tongass, a master Chilkat weaver); he learned Kwak'wala and Kwakwaka'wakw culture by upbringing, marriage and adoption. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hunt_(ethnologist)]
- Hunt's collaboration with Boas began in 1886; Boas taught him to write Kwak'wala; Hunt wrote as many as ten thousand pages for Boas. Boas credited Hunt as co-author of *Kwakiutl Texts* (Jesup publications; first series 1902–1905 in parts, second series 1906). In 1931 Boas wrote to Hunt: "In some cases, I can guess what is wrong but I had rather have you correct it than use my own uncertain knowledge of Kwakiutl." Hunt's manuscripts are at Columbia; correspondence at the American Philosophical Society. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hunt_(ethnologist) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Boas]
- Colonial entanglements: Hunt collected hundreds of objects for the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition, Chicago, and took a group of Kwakwaka'wakw (17 per the Hunt article; 14 per the Boas article) to live in a mock village there and perform ceremonies banned in Canada; objects went to the Field Museum. He also helped buy the Yuquot Whalers' Shrine (1904), still contested. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hunt_(ethnologist) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Boas]
- Judith Berman, "George Hunt and the Kwak'wala Texts," *Anthropological Linguistics* 36:4 (1994) is the key study of Hunt's authorship. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hunt_(ethnologist)]
- 1986: Boas and Hunt families held a "reunion" at Tsaxis (Fort Rupert). Hunt's descendants include Gloria Cranmer Webster and filmmaker Barbara Cranmer, and the Hunt family of carvers (Henry, Tony, Richard, Calvin; Corrine Hunt designed the 2010 Olympic medals). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hunt_(ethnologist)]
- A painting of the 1894 potlatch at Tsaxis appears in Boas's *The Social Organization and the Secret Societies of the Kwakiutl Indians* (1897). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Boas]

### Tsimshian: Henry Tate, William Beynon, Marius Barbeau
- Henry Wellington Tate (c. 1860–1914), Tsimshian oral historian, mailed transcribed narratives to Boas from 1903; they probably never met. Result: Boas's *Tsimshian Mythology* (1916). Boas complained Tate wrote stories in English first then translated into Tsimshian. Ralph Maud, *Transmission Difficulties: Franz Boas and Tsimshian Mythology* (2000), analyzes the distortions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_W._Tate]
- Marius Barbeau (5 March 1883 – 27 February 1969), founder of Canadian anthropology; fieldwork at Lax Kw'alaams (Port Simpson) from December 1914 with interpreter William Beynon; Wilson Duff called it "one of the most productive field seasons in the history of [North] American anthropology". Barbeau is controversial for seeking only "authentic" non-political stories, for removing the Ni'isjoohl pole (1929), and for theories that Tsimshian, Haida and Tlingit were recent migrants from Asia; his view that totem poles were post-contact has been disproved. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marius_Barbeau]
- William Beynon (Gusgai'in; 1888–1958), Tsimshian hereditary chief (took the Gitlaan title Gwisk'aayn in 1913 by matrilineal succession), worked for Barbeau, Boas, Sapir and others; sent Boas about 250 narratives (1932–39, the "Beynon Manuscripts", Columbia); wrote a 200-page account of a four-day potlatch and pole raising at Gitsegukla in 1945 (published 2000 as *Potlatch at Gitsegukla*); co-founded the Native Brotherhood of BC in 1931. The Barbeau–Beynon collection (Canadian Museum of History) now serves Tsimshian, Gitxsan, Wet'suwet'en and Nisga'a research into territories and oral traditions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Beynon]
- Under Beynon's influence Barbeau promoted the idea that the region's oral histories of migration have real historiographic value. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marius_Barbeau]

### John R. Swanton and the Haida mythtellers Skaay and Ghandl
- John R. Swanton (19 February 1873 – 2 May 1958), Bureau of American Ethnology; spent roughly a year with the Haida; published *Haida Texts and Myths: Skidegate Dialect* (1905), *Contributions to the Ethnology of the Haida* (1905), *Tlingit Myths and Texts* (1909). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_R._Swanton]
- Swanton arrived in Haida country in autumn 1900 and spent about ten months transcribing several thousand pages in Haida, assisted by Henry Moody. [source: search summary of https://douglas-mcintyre.com/products/9781771623773 and related listings]
- Skaay of the Qquuna Qiighawaay (b. c. 1827), of Ttanuu, Eagle moiety; dictated to Swanton with Henry Moody over the winter of 1900; works include the *Qquuna Cycle* — "the longest poem recorded in Haida" — and *Raven Travelling*. Baptized "Robert McKay" (1884), later "John Sky". Could neither read nor write. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skaay]
- Ghandl of the Qayahl Llaanas (b. c. 1851, Qaysun), blinded by smallpox or measles; dictated to Swanton in November 1900, when epidemics had killed over ninety per cent of the Haida. [source: search summary of https://douglas-mcintyre.com/products/9781771623773 ; https://goodminds.com/products/nine-visits-to-the-mythworld-told-by-ghandl-of-the-qayahl-llaanas]

### Edward Sapir
- Edward Sapir (26 January 1884 – 4 February 1939) did first fieldwork on Wishram Chinook (1905; *Wishram Texts* 1909); as head of the Geological Survey of Canada's anthropology division (from 1910) his first fieldwork was on Nuu-chah-nulth ("Nootka") on Vancouver Island. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Sapir]

### Tlingit: Nora Marks Dauenhauer and Richard Dauenhauer
- Nora Marks Dauenhauer (Keixwnéi; 8 May 1927 – 25 September 2017), Raven moiety, first language Tlingit; learned English at school from age 8; BA anthropology 1976; Alaska State Writer Laureate 2012–14. Richard Dauenhauer (10 April 1942 – 19 August 2014), PhD 1975 on Tlingit oral tradition, Alaska poet laureate 1981–88. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nora_Marks_Dauenhauer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dauenhauer]
- Their *Classics of Tlingit Oral Literature* series (Sealaska Heritage Foundation / University of Washington Press): *Haa Shuká, Our Ancestors: Tlingit Oral Narratives* (1987); *Haa Tuwanáagu Yís, for Healing Our Spirit: Tlingit Oratory* (1990); *Haa Ḵusteeyí, Our Culture: Tlingit Life Stories* (1994); plus *Anóoshi Lingít Aaní Ká / Russians in Tlingit America* (2008, with Lydia Black; American Book Award). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dauenhauer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nora_Marks_Dauenhauer]

## Ethnopoetics: how to write a performance
- Jerome Rothenberg coined "ethnopoetics" in the 1960s; Dennis Tedlock and Dell Hymes developed it as a field. Ethnopoetic texts use lines, verses and stanzas instead of prose paragraphs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnopoetics]
- Tedlock (19 June 1939 – 3 June 2016): pauses mark line breaks; his texts are like musical scores marking loudness, tone, silence, gesture; *Finding the Center: Narrative Poetry of the Zuni Indians* (1972); PEN translation prize for the *Popol Vuh*. His work was on Zuni and Maya, not the Northwest Coast. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Tedlock ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnopoetics]
- Dell Hymes (7 June 1927, Portland, Oregon – 13 November 2009): dissertation a grammar of Kathlamet Chinook (from Boas's texts); developed "ethnography of speaking" and "communicative competence"; argued that dictated texts recorded as prose contain poetic organization recoverable from grammatical and syntactic repetition — "measured verse". Key works: "Discovering oral performance and measured verse in American Indian narrative," *New Literary History* 8 (1976/1977); *"In Vain I Tried to Tell You": Essays in Native American Ethnopoetics* (1981); *Now I Know Only So Far* (2003). Early analyses used Victoria Howard's Clackamas texts recorded by Melville Jacobs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dell_Hymes ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnopoetics]
- Hymes's units are identified through syntactic and semantic parallelism; a recurring pattern is "onset–ongoing–outcome". Verse analysis of Cultee's two tellings (1890, 1894) of the "Salmon's Myth" showed, Hymes argued, deliberate reshaping for different effect. [source: https://lingpapers.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2018/03/1984_Morrison.pdf (search summary) ; https://journals.uvic.ca/index.php/WPLC/article/view/5511/2118]
- The debate: Tedlock privileges the recorded voice (pause, pitch) and individual performance; Hymes privileges formal patterning recoverable even from old dictated texts. They "disagreed on some analytic details" but agreed on aims. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnopoetics]
- Victoria Howard (c. 1865–1930), Clackamas Chinook storyteller at Grand Ronde; dictated to Melville Jacobs from 1928 (*Clackamas Chinook Texts*); a 2021 publication set 25 of her performances in verse. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_Howard]

## Bringhurst's Haida translations and the controversy
- Robert Bringhurst (b. 16 October 1946, Los Angeles), poet and typographer (*The Elements of Typographic Style*, 1992); began "recording the Haida canon" in 1987 through friendship with Bill Reid. Trilogy *Masterworks of the Classical Haida Mythtellers*: *A Story as Sharp as a Knife: The Classical Haida Mythtellers and Their World* (Douglas & McIntyre, 1999); *Nine Visits to the Mythworld* (Ghandl; 2000, some sources 2001); *Being in Being* (Skaay; 2001/2002). *A Story as Sharp as a Knife* was nominated for the 2000 Governor General's Award. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bringhurst ; https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/the-epic-art-of-the-haida-mythtellers ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skaay]
- Bringhurst set Swanton's Haida texts as poetry, treating Skaay and Ghandl as classical poets; chapter 19, "The Prosody of Meaning", is a key statement on oral poetics. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bringhurst]
- Criticism: accused of academic exploitation and cultural appropriation. A 1999 *Globe and Mail* report by Adele Weder quoted Haida critics (later criticized for citing only two Haida sources). A 2001 CBC *Ideas* series, "Land to Stand On", featured Haida saying the work was "about keeping us in our place," written "without asking us," and "replete with 'serious errors'". Linguist Jeff Leer's 2001 review in *IJAL* questioned Bringhurst's Haida and favoured John Enrico's *Skidegate Haida Myths and Histories*; the journal retracted the review and apologized. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bringhurst]
- Defence: Dell Hymes said the trilogy "should become a classic reference point"; it won the 2004 Edward Sapir Prize (Society for Linguistic Anthropology); Margaret Atwood and Cree elder Wilna Hodgson defended it. Bringhurst says "culture is not genetic" and that the translations were "[exercises] in literary history, not in the interpretation of present-day Haida culture". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bringhurst]
- The underlying issues for the writer: who owns stories recorded in 1900 from Haida tellers; whether Swanton's texts (dictated, in a declining speech community) can be "classics" of individual poets; and the authority of a non-Haida, non-fluent translator vs. Haida speakers and linguists. ## Oral histories as law and evidence: Delgamuukw
- Background: *Calder v British Columbia* (1973), brought by Frank Calder and the Nisga'a in 1969, first acknowledged Aboriginal title predating colonization; split 3–3 on extinguishment. Justice Judson: "the fact is that when the settlers came, the Indians were there, organized in societies and occupying the land as their forefathers had done for centuries." The Nisga'a treaty was concluded 4 August 1998 and the Nisga'a Final Agreement took effect in 2000. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calder_v_British_Columbia_(AG) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delgamuukw_v_British_Columbia ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nisga%CA%BCa]
- On 24 October 1984, 35 Gitxsan and 13 Wet'suwet'en hereditary chiefs filed a claim to Aboriginal title and jurisdiction over 58,000 km² in northwest BC, on behalf of their Houses. Lead plaintiff Delgamuukw (Earl Muldoe, 1936–2022); Gisday'wa (Alfred Joseph) for the Wet'suwet'en. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delgamuukw_v_British_Columbia]
- Trial: 374 days (318 of evidence, 56 of argument), 11 May 1987 – 30 June 1990, in Vancouver and Smithers; 61 witnesses, many in their own languages with translators; some sang or described ceremonial songs relating the *adaawḵ* and *kungax*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delgamuukw_v_British_Columbia]
- Chief Justice Allan McEachern's judgment (8 March 1991) dismissed the title claims; he was "unable to accept adaawk, kungax and oral traditions as reliable bases for detailed history" and described pre-contact life as "nasty, brutish, and short". The Canadian Anthropology Society said it was "laced with ethnocentric bias". Julie Cruikshank's critique: "Invention of Anthropology in British Columbia's Supreme Court: Oral Tradition as Evidence in Delgamuukw v. B.C.," *BC Studies* 95 (1992). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delgamuukw_v_British_Columbia]
- BC Court of Appeal (25 June 1993) rejected blanket extinguishment. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delgamuukw_v_British_Columbia]
- Supreme Court of Canada: heard 16–17 June 1997, decided 11 December 1997, [1997] 3 SCR 1010; Lamer CJ for the majority. A new trial was ordered (errors in pleadings — amalgamating 51 House claims into two — and in the treatment of evidence); so much of the ruling is technically obiter, restated in *Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia* (2014). No second trial has been held. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delgamuukw_v_British_Columbia]
- The Court required that oral histories be placed "on an equal footing" with documentary evidence and held that the trial judge's treatment of them was an error (paras 87, 107; see Quotations). It defined Aboriginal title as a *sui generis* right of exclusive use and occupation, with a test of occupation before sovereignty, continuity and exclusivity. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delgamuukw_v_British_Columbia]
- Caution: the SCC did not itself "affirm" Gitxsan/Wet'suwet'en title (Wikipedia's Gitxsan article says so loosely); it ordered a new trial. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delgamuukw_v_British_Columbia ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gitxsan]
- Don Monet and Skanu'u (Ardythe Wilson, Gitxsan), *Colonialism on Trial: Indigenous Land Rights and the Gitksan and Wet'suwet'en Sovereignty Case* (New Society, 1991/1992) — a trial account with cartoons, since courtroom photography was banned. [source: https://cbra.library.utoronto.ca/items/show/10513 (search summary)]
- Antonia Mills, *'Hang on to These Words': Johnny David's Delgamuukw Evidence* (University of Toronto Press, 2005). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delgamuukw_v_British_Columbia]

## Stories and geology: the 1700 Cascadia earthquake
- The Cascadia earthquake occurred about 9 p.m. on 26 January 1700, magnitude ~8.7–9.2, rupture ~1,000 km from mid-Vancouver Island to northern California; dated from Japanese tsunami records (waves hit Japan on 27–28 January 1700) and ghost-forest tree rings. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1700_Cascadia_earthquake]
- Oral traditions from BC to northern California describe a great earthquake and flood; they give no dates, and "not all earthquake stories in the region can be ascribed to the 1700 quake". The Huu-ay-aht (Nuu-chah-nulth) account of Pachena Bay places it on a winter night after people had gone to sleep — consistent with the reconstructed time; only Masit, on a hillside, survived. Kwakwaka'wakw, Cowichan, Makah and Quileute stories also describe a night-time quake or flood. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1700_Cascadia_earthquake]
- Key study: Ludwin et al., "Dating the 1700 Cascadia Earthquake: Great Coastal Earthquakes in Native Stories," *Seismological Research Letters* 76:2 (2005), 140–148 (co-authors include Makah and Quileute heritage staff). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1700_Cascadia_earthquake]
- The Huu-ay-aht rebuilt their administration building on high ground as a tsunami refuge. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1700_Cascadia_earthquake]

## Ethics: ownership, sacred material, consent
- Northwest Coast stories, songs, names and crests are owned property of clans/houses/individuals and are validated by being performed and witnessed at feasts; some Raven narratives are treated as clan property, not entertainment. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravens_in_Native_American_mythology]
- Hamatsa and other secret-society dances are sacred; Boas posed as a Hamatsa figure for a US National Museum display (1895 or before) — an example of how sacred material entered museums. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Boas ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch]
- Barbeau's informants were often unwilling to work with him; Nisga'a say the Ni'isjoohl pole was taken without consent while people were away. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marius_Barbeau]

## Debates
- Salvage ethnography vs. Indigenous authorship: Boas's texts depended on Hunt, Tate and Beynon, often under-credited; Berman (1994) and Maud (2000) examine how Boas's editing shaped what counts as "the" Kwakwaka'wakw and Tsimshian canon. Buchholtzer: anthropological discourse a "monologue". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hunt_(ethnologist) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_W._Tate ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwakwaka%CA%BCwakw]
- How to write oral performance: Tedlock (score of the voice) vs. Hymes (recoverable measured verse in old dictated texts); critics of both ask whether verse lineation imposes Western poetics. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnopoetics]
- Bringhurst controversy: appropriation and accuracy (Haida critics, CBC *Ideas*, Leer/Enrico) vs. literary recovery (Hymes, Atwood, Sapir Prize). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bringhurst]
- Oral history as evidence: McEachern (1991) vs. SCC (1997); scholarly critique by Cruikshank and the "nasty, brutish and short" literature (Waldram et al. 1992); later commentators argue the courts still constrain oral histories (e.g. a *Canadian Journal of Law and Society* article "Delgamuukw: A Legal Straightjacket for Oral Histories?"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delgamuukw_v_British_Columbia ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-law-and-society-la-revue-canadienne-droit-et-societe/article/abs/delgamuukw-a-legal-straightjacket-for-oral-histories/EC08DFEBCD0737BAAD75ABDB5C4BDC32]
- Oral tradition and science: Cascadia stories show oral histories can preserve real events across ~300 years, but caution against matching every story to 1700. Barbeau–Beynon's migration claims show the opposite risk (speculative Asian-origin theories). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1700_Cascadia_earthquake ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marius_Barbeau]
- The potlatch ban and Indigenous agency: some Tsimshian and Nisga'a chiefs petitioned for the ban (1883); others resisted underground. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch_ban]
- Boas's ambiguous role: opposed the ban and worked with Indigenous co-authors, yet collected sacred objects and staged people at the 1893 fair. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch_ban ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hunt_(ethnologist)]
