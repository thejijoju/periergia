## Historiography: McNeill, Crosby, Rosenberg, the new science
- William H. McNeill, *Plagues and Peoples* (Garden City, NY: Anchor Press/Doubleday, 1976; UK edition Basil Blackwell, 1977). McNeill taught at the University of Chicago. He analysed epidemics as social as well as biological events, shaped by ecology, economy and culture and in turn shaping the fate of cultures. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plagues_and_Peoples]
- McNeill distinguished disease-causing "microparasites" from human "macroparasites" (rulers, landlords, conquerors who live off producers) and compared civilisation itself to a macroparasite. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plagues_and_Peoples]
- With Alfred Crosby's *The Columbian Exchange* (1972), *Plagues and Peoples* popularised the view that disease made European expansion into the Americas possible. Critics found some assertions thinly evidenced and the book Eurocentric; some arguments have been overturned, e.g. McNeill's rejection of an American origin for syphilis. Its basic view of epidemics as both biological and social is now accepted in epidemiology. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plagues_and_Peoples]
- McNeill argued that the Antonine Plague and the Plague of Cyprian were the first transfers to humans of two different diseases, smallpox and measles (not necessarily in that order). Genetic dating of measles (see below) tells against the measles part. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonine_Plague; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Cyprian; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measles]
- Alfred W. Crosby (15 January 1931 – 14 March 2018), University of Texas at Austin: *The Columbian Exchange* (1972); *Ecological Imperialism: The Biological Expansion of Europe, 900–1900* (Cambridge, 1986); *Epidemic and Peace, 1918* (1976), republished as *America's Forgotten Pandemic: The Influenza of 1918* (1989). He coined "Neo-Europes" and "virgin soil epidemic" (article: "Virgin Soil Epidemics as a Factor in the Aboriginal Depopulation in America", *William and Mary Quarterly* 33:2, 1976, pp. 289–299). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_W._Crosby]
- Crosby defined a virgin-soil epidemic as one "in which the populations at risk have had no previous contact with the diseases that strike them and are therefore immunologically almost defenseless". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin-soil_epidemic]
- Charles E. Rosenberg (born 11 November 1936, New York City), historian of medicine at the University of Pennsylvania (1963–2001) and Harvard (from 2001). Books include *The Cholera Years: The United States in 1832, 1849 and 1866* (1962) and *Explaining Epidemics* (Cambridge, 1992). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_E._Rosenberg]
- *Framing Disease: Studies in Cultural History* (Rutgers, 1992), edited by Rosenberg with Janet Golden. Rosenberg's introduction argues that in some ways a disease does not exist until a society has agreed that it does, by perceiving, naming and responding to it. Use "framing" for this idea, not a direct quotation. [source: https://ubcpress.ca/framing-disease]
- Rosenberg's essay "What Is an Epidemic? AIDS in Historical Perspective" (1989; reprinted in a 1992 Rosenberg collection) set out a "dramaturgic" model, written in response to AIDS. It has been criticised as too neat and linear, and historians of how epidemics end use it as a starting point (see Quotations). [source: https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/129792; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8722652]
- The ancient-DNA ("new science") turn: in 1998 Drancourt et al. reported *Y. pestis* DNA in medieval dental pulp, which others doubted. Definitive confirmation came in 2010 (Haensch et al., *PLOS Pathogens*). In 2011 Bos et al. (*Nature*) published a draft genome from the East Smithfield cemetery in London (1348–49) and showed that the Black Death strain is ancestral to most modern strains. In 2013 researchers confirmed that *Y. pestis* caused the Plague of Justinian. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death]
- Before this, some historians (the text names Lars Walløe; Twigg, Cohn and others are not quoted in the source) doubted that the Black Death was plague, or that the rat-flea model applied. Sloane (2011) and Dean et al. (2018) argued for person-to-person spread by human lice and fleas. Monica Green urged attention to non-commensal animals in transmission. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death]
- Ancient genomes put plague in humans in the Late Neolithic/Early Bronze Age (Andrades Valtueña et al., 2017). *Y. pestis* is linked to the "Neolithic decline" around 3000 BCE in a Swedish tomb (Rascovan et al., 2019). Flea-transmissible bubonic plague is first known from Bronze Age remains about 3,800 years ago (Spyrou et al., 2018). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death]

## The Neolithic disease transition and "crowd diseases"
- Settled farming spread disease faster than in hunter-gatherer times. Poor sanitation and domesticated animals helped pathogens jump from animals to people; influenza, smallpox and measles are examples. Neolithic diets were lower in fibre, micronutrients and protein. Studies consistently find more tooth decay and shorter stature after the shift to farming, and many conclude that infant mortality and infectious disease rose. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Revolution]
- About 60% of known human infectious diseases, and up to 75% of emerging ones, are estimated to be zoonotic. The emergence of zoonoses began with animal domestication. Measles, smallpox, influenza, HIV and diphtheria are zoonotic in origin, and tuberculosis has animal-adapted relatives. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoonosis]
- Measles evolved from rinderpest, a cattle virus. Genomic estimates place the split between 900 and 2,400 years ago. Düx et al. (2020) suggested the human virus began infecting people as early as the 4th century BCE, or as late as after 500 CE. The Persian physician al-Razi (Rhazes, 860–932) wrote the first systematic description distinguishing measles from smallpox, in *The Book of Smallpox and Measles*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measles]
- Malaria began to have a major impact on human survival about 10,000 years ago, with farming. It selected for sickle-cell, thalassaemias, G6PD deficiency and Duffy-negative blood. *P. falciparum* DNA shows greater diversity in Africa, so humans carried it before leaving Africa. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_malaria]
- Tuberculosis: phylogenetics indicates an African origin and co-evolution with humans. One study of 259 strains suggests it arose about 70,000 years ago. It is found in human remains from at least the Neolithic. Animal-adapted *M. bovis* evolved from human strains, not the reverse. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_tuberculosis]
- Abdel Omran's theory of the "epidemiological transition" (1971) runs from an "Age of Pestilence and Famine" (life expectancy 20–40), through receding pandemics, to an age when chronic diseases replace infection as the main killers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemiological_transition]
- Kyle Harper, on Roman skeletons being shorter than those of pre- and post-Roman Italy: "not for the last time in history, a precocious leap forward in social development brought biological reverses" (*The Fate of Rome*, 2017, pp. 75–79). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonine_Plague]

## Ancient pandemics: Athens, Antonine, Cyprian
- The plague of Athens (430 BCE) struck the city crowded behind its walls, killing perhaps a quarter of the population, including Pericles in 429 BCE. This is already in the published Ancient Greece chapter; cross-refer. [source: published chapter history--classical-antiquity--ancient-greece; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Athens]
- Antonine Plague, 165–180 CE, also called the Plague of Galen, who described it in *Methodus Medendi*. Ancient sources tie its appearance to the Roman siege of Seleucia in Mesopotamia in winter 165–166 during Lucius Verus' Parthian campaign. It reached Rome in 166 and nearly every corner of the empire by 172. Lucius Verus died in 169. The cause was probably smallpox, possibly measles, and there is no genetic evidence yet. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonine_Plague]
- Antonine toll: estimates range from 2% to 33% of the empire's population (1.5–25 million). Most coalesce around 10% (about 7.5 million of about 75 million), with up to 15% in cities and the army. Cassius Dio reports up to 2,000 deaths a day in Rome in a recurrence of 189. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonine_Plague]
- Antonine debate: Harper says the pandemic "in any account of Rome's destiny ... merit[s] a place squarely in the forefront". Six historians (Haldon, Elton, Huebner, Izdebski, Mordechai, Newfield, 2018) criticised his "extreme" position but did "not doubt that disease and climate had some of the impact Harper describes". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonine_Plague; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyle_Harper]
- Plague of Cyprian, about 249–262 (or 251/2–270), named for Cyprian, bishop of Carthage (c. 210–258), who wrote *On the Plague*. Bishop Dionysius of Alexandria's letters place its outbreak around Easter 249 in Egypt. Reportedly 5,000 a day died in Rome at the peak. One calculation has Alexandria falling from 500,000 to 190,000 (partly flight). Its pathogen is unknown. Harper argues the symptoms fit a viral haemorrhagic fever like Ebola, while holding that the Antonine was smallpox. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Cyprian]
- Global angle: the Silk Roads chapter already notes that epidemics struck the Roman and Han empires in the 2nd–3rd centuries and may have travelled along trade routes, though their origins are uncertain. Cross-refer; do not overclaim a link. [source: published chapter history--classical-antiquity--silk-roads]

## The first plague pandemic: Justinian (541–c. 750)
- The Plague of Justinian (541–549) was the first episode of the first plague pandemic, which recurred until the mid-8th century (often given as 541–750 or 541–767). Procopius first reported it in 541 at Pelusium near Suez in Egypt. It struck the Byzantine and Sasanian empires, the Mediterranean, Europe and the Near East. Justinian caught it and recovered in 542. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Justinian]
- Procopius, in a passage modelled on Thucydides, claimed it killed 10,000 a day at its peak in Constantinople. The figure's accuracy is doubted. WP says it killed "about a fifth" of the capital's population. Other eyewitnesses were John of Ephesus and Evagrius Scholasticus, who survived buboes as a child in Antioch and lost his wife, children and grandchild in later recurrences. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Justinian]
- Evidence beyond Constantinople: epitaphs in Byzantine Palestine and Arabia support the 541 dating, a mass grave was found at Jerash (Jordan), and there are possible Sabaic inscriptions from Himyar in southern Arabia. Ancient DNA from 6th-century burials at Aschheim (Bavaria) was reported in 2005, and Harbeck et al. 2013 confirmed *Y. pestis* there. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Justinian]
- Origin: genetic studies suggest Central Asia (Tian Shan). Others propose sub-Saharan/East African origins via Aksumite merchants, which would fit the south-to-north spread from Egypt. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Justinian]
- Toll, maximalist: an estimated 15–100 million deaths over two centuries of recurrence, said to equal 25–60% of Europe's population at the first outbreak. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Justinian]
- Toll, minimalist: Lee Mordechai and Merle Eisenberg, "Rejecting Catastrophe: The Case of the Justinianic Plague", *Past & Present* 244 (August 2019), 3–50. Also Mordechai, Eisenberg, Newfield, Izdebski, Kay and Poinar, "The Justinianic Plague: An inconsequential pandemic?", *PNAS* 116 (17 December 2019), 25546–25554. They examined texts, legislation, coinage, papyri, inscriptions, pollen, ancient DNA and burials. They concluded that high-mortality estimates rest on a small subset of texts, and that pollen shows no fall in agricultural production associable with plague. Plague may have caused high mortality in some places but no widespread demographic collapse. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Justinian; https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019PNAS..11625546M; https://www.princeton.edu/index%2ephp/news/2019/12/02/maybe-first-plague-wasnt-bad-say-researchers]
- Response: Peter Sarris ("New Approaches to the 'Plague of Justinian'", *Past & Present* 254, February 2022) criticised their methods and handling of sources. He discussed genetic evidence that plague may have entered western Eurasia by more than one route, perhaps reaching England before Constantinople. Eisenberg and Mordechai followed with "The Justinianic Plague and Global Pandemics: The Making of the Plague Concept", *AHR* 125:5 (December 2020). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Justinian]
- Procopius's *Secret History* (Anekdota 23.20f) charges Justinian with demanding taxes even for dead neighbours after the pestilence (see Quotations). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Justinian]
- Islamic conceptions: Lawrence Conrad, "Tāʿūn and Wabāʾ: Conceptions of Plague and Pestilence in Early Islam" (1982). The hadith advised people in a plague region to stay and others not to enter it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Justinian; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarantine]

## The second plague pandemic: the Black Death (1346–53) and after
- The Black Death (1346–1353) was caused by *Yersinia pestis*. WP gives "up to 50 million" deaths, around 30–60% of Europe's population, and about 33% of the Middle East's. A broader, poorly sourced estimate of 75–200 million for Eurasia is flagged by WP itself as needing a better source. Do not use the upper figure. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death]
- European mortality is given variously as 25–60%. Agents of Pope Clement VI in 1351 counted 23,840,000 dead in Christian Europe, about 31% of an estimated 75 million. Ole Benedictow argues for about 60% of Europe's population. Local figures run from about 50% (East Anglia, Tuscany, parts of Scandinavia) to under 15% (Bohemia, Galicia). Florence fell from 110,000–120,000 (1338) to 50,000 (1351). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death]
- "Black Death" was not a contemporary name. The term is first attested in English in 1755, translating Danish *den sorte død*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death]
- Origin: in 2022 ("The source of the Black Death in fourteenth-century central Eurasia", team led by Maria Spyrou) researchers studied two cemeteries in the Chüy Valley near Lake Issyk-Kul (Kyrgyzstan). Syriac tombstones record a spike in deaths from "pestilence" in 1338–1339, and the DNA proved ancestral to the Black Death strain. Alternative views exist: John Norris saw a small sporadic outbreak there; Benedictow argues for a Caspian focus; some still favour China. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death]
- China and India: research on the Delhi Sultanate and Yuan shows no evidence of a serious 14th-century epidemic in India and no specific evidence of plague in China (Sussman 2011). Richard von Glahn: China's population fell by at least 15% and perhaps a third between 1340 and 1370, but war and famine were probably the main causes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death]
- Monica H. Green, "The Four Black Deaths", *American Historical Review* 125:5 (December 2020), 1601–1631. Genetics places a "Big Bang" diversification of *Y. pestis* into four lineages around the 13th century, roughly a century or more before 1346. Green links its spread to the Mongol conquests (secondary summaries mention Mongol grain movements) and argues that different lineages became predominant in different parts of Eurasia and Africa. Attribute this as "Green argues"; the exact mechanisms come from summaries. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monica_Green_(historian); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death; https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/did-black-death-rampage-across-world-more-century-previously-thought-180977331/; https://news.exeter.ac.uk/research/plague-transmission-may-have-begun-a-century-before-the-black-death-study-shows/]
- Green also edited *Pandemic Disease in the Medieval World: Rethinking the Black Death* (Arc Medieval Press, 2015; the first volume of *The Medieval Globe*). Her essay there is "Taking 'Pandemic' Seriously: Making the Black Death Global". She wrote "Putting Africa on the Black Death map" (*Afriques*, 2018). The Medieval Academy of America created the Monica H. Green Prize in 2021. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monica_Green_(historian); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death]
- Africa: George Sussman argued that plague did not reach East Africa until the 20th century. Green (2018) argues that the second pandemic did reach sub-Saharan Africa. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death]
- Spread: the Golden Horde siege of Caffa (1345–46) is linked to the story of catapulted corpses, though infected rats crossing siege lines are also likely. Plague reached Sicily by Genoese galleys in October 1347 and Genoa and Venice in January 1348. Alexandria was infected by autumn 1347 and Cairo by late summer 1348, where a third to 40% of up to 600,000 people died within eight months. Egypt lost about 40%. Mecca was infected in 1348 through the Hajj. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death]
- Recurrence: according to Biraben, plague was present somewhere in Europe every year from 1346 to 1671. In France at least 2 million died of plague in 1600–1670 (Geoffrey Parker), and about 1.7 million died in Italy in the first half of the 17th century. Plague struck the Islamic world almost every year between 1500 and 1850: Algiers lost 30,000–50,000 in 1620–21, and Cairo had more than fifty epidemics in the 150 years after 1347. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death]

## Quarantine and its institutions
- Ibn Sina's *Canon of Medicine* (1025) recommended isolation, especially for tuberculosis. The hadith tradition counselled staying in place during plague. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarantine]
- Venice appointed three guardians of public health in 1348. Ragusa (Dubrovnik) imposed a thirty-day isolation (*trentino*) in 1377 on arrivals from plague areas. This was later extended to forty days, *quarantena* (Venetian), the root of "quarantine". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarantine; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death]
- The first lazaretto was founded by Venice on the island of Santa Maria di Nazareth (now Lazzaretto Vecchio). WP's Lazaretto article gives 1423, its Quarantine article 1403, so give "in the early fifteenth century (1403 or 1423)". The name came from "Nazaretto", influenced by St Lazarus, patron of lepers. Genoa followed in 1467, and Marseille converted its leper hospital in 1476. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lazaretto; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarantine]
- In 1910–11 the pneumonic Manchurian plague killed about 60,000, thought to have spread from tarbagan marmots hunted for fur. Wu Lien-teh (1879–1960), a Cambridge-trained doctor born in Penang, led China's response and promoted masks. It is credited with the first use of personal protective equipment. Wu was nominated for the Nobel Prize in 1935. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchurian_plague; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wu_Lien-teh]

## The third plague pandemic (1855–1960)
- It began in Yunnan, China, in 1855. Copper mining had drawn a rapid influx of Han Chinese and the population had passed 7 million by 1850. Plague spread along the south-east coast. Canton was struck from March 1894 (80,000 dead in a few weeks, by WP's figure). Hong Kong's first case was in May 1894, and over 6,000 died there May–October 1894, with a third of the population fleeing. Plague was endemic there until 1929. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_plague_pandemic]
- It spread by steamship to all inhabited continents: Bombay 1896, Calcutta 1898, Madagascar 1898, Honolulu and San Francisco 1900, among others. Deaths were at least 15 million in all: about 10–12 million in British India, 2.2 million in China and about 3 million elsewhere. WHO considered it active until 1960, when deaths fell below 200 a year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_plague_pandemic]
- In Hong Kong in 1894, Alexandre Yersin (Swiss-born, of the Pasteur Institute) and Kitasato Shibasaburō (Japanese, of Koch's school) independently isolated the bacterium. Contradictory statements by Kitasato led to Yersin being accepted as the primary discoverer. Yersin named it *Pasteurella pestis*, and it was later renamed *Yersinia pestis* (the renaming date is inconsistent in the source; see NOT VERIFIED). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yersinia_pestis; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_plague_pandemic]
- In 1898 Paul-Louis Simond demonstrated the rat flea as vector. On 10 January 1897 Waldemar Haffkine tested his plague vaccine on himself in India. By about 1900, four million people had been inoculated in India. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yersinia_pestis; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_plague_pandemic]
- Colonial responses: harsh British quarantines in India bred resentment. In Cape Colony, Black South Africans were forcibly moved to the city outskirts, which historians read as racial segregation. In Honolulu in 1900 the Board of Health burned much of Chinatown, leaving over 7,000 Chinese and Japanese residents homeless. San Francisco quarantined all of Chinatown after a single case, a measure historians question as racially motivated. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_plague_pandemic]
- Myron Echenberg, *Plague Ports: The Global Urban Impact of Bubonic Plague, 1894–1901* (NYU Press, 2007) studies ten port cities on five continents: Hong Kong, Bombay, Sydney, Honolulu, San Francisco, Buenos Aires, Rio de Janeiro, Alexandria, Cape Town and Porto. Sheldon Watts's review gives 39 bubonic deaths in San Francisco (35 Chinese, 4 Euro-Americans). [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/book/10647; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/247684]

## Smallpox: Americas, variolation, vaccination, eradication
- Smallpox (variola virus) emerged 3,000–4,000 years ago according to genetic evidence. Reliable written descriptions date from about 500–1000 CE. In 18th-century Europe about 400,000 died of it a year. It killed an estimated 250–500 million in the 20th century (WP also says "up to 300 million"). In 1967 the WHO estimated 15 million cases and 2 million deaths that year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallpox]
- It was the leading killer among the Afro-Eurasian diseases that devastated the Americas. Details (1518 Caribbean, 1520 Tenochtitlan, 1520s Andes, cocoliztli 1545 and 1576, population ranges) are already in the Conquest of the Americas, Colonial Latin America and Columbian Exchange chapters; cross-refer rather than repeat. [source: published chapters history--renaissance-and-reformation--conquest-of-americas, --columbian-exchange; history--early-modern-period--colonial-latin-america]
- Pacific and elsewhere: measles killed 20% of Hawaii's population in the 1850s and over 40,000 Fijians in 1875, about a third of the population. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measles]
- Variolation deliberately infected people with material from smallpox cases. Only 1–2% of those variolated died, against about 30% of natural cases. It was practised in China, India, parts of Africa and the Middle East before it reached England and North America in the 1720s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variolation]
- China: the oldest documented variolation is from the 16th century (some sources say 11th), by nasal insufflation of powdered scabs (Wan Quan). Joseph Needham dated the first variolation to 1567–1572. The Kangxi Emperor inoculated his children and troops. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variolation]
- India: 18th-century accounts describe itinerant Brahmins inoculating. In 1731 Oliver Coult reported it begun about 150 years earlier, and Holwell (1768) said it had been practised for centuries. Aspects of these accounts are questioned. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variolation]
- Africa: in Sennar (Sudan), *tishteree el jidderi*, "buying the smallpox", saw mothers bargain for pustules. It was still practised in Sudan in the late 19th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variolation]
- Ottoman world: Greek doctors Emmanuel Timoni and Iacob Pylarino described Constantinople's practice. Timoni's letter appeared in the Royal Society's *Philosophical Transactions* (1714). Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, scarred by smallpox in 1715, described it in a letter to Sarah Chiswell in April 1717. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variolation]
- Boston: Cotton Mather learned of inoculation from Onesimus, an enslaved West African man (WP gives 1706 and 1707 in different places), and promoted it in the 1721 epidemic. Zabdiel Boylston inoculated 300 people, of whom 6 died, while about 1,000 of 6,000 natural cases died. Already in the Health in Africa chapter; cross-refer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variolation]
- Edward Jenner (17 May 1749 – 26 January 1823). On 14 May 1796 he inoculated James Phipps, the eight-year-old son of his gardener, with cowpox from milkmaid Sarah Nelmes (the cow was called Blossom). On 1 July 1796 he challenged Phipps with variolous material and no disease followed. In 1798 he published *An Inquiry into the Causes and Effects of the Variolae Vaccinae*. "Vaccine" comes from *vacca*/*variolae vaccinae*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Jenner]
- Jenner was not the first: John Fewster (by 1768) and others, including the Dorset farmer Benjamin Jesty (1774), knew or tested cowpox protection. The story that Jenner was inspired by milkmaids' complexions appears to be a myth. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Jenner]
- The Spanish Balmis Expedition (1803–1806) carried the vaccine to the Americas, the Philippines, Macao and China (see Quotations for Jenner's comment). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Jenner]
- Eradication: the WHO intensified its campaign in 1967 (the global effort ran 1958/59–1977). Rahima Banu in Bangladesh, October 1975, was the last natural case of variola major. Ali Maow Maalin, a hospital cook in Merca, Somalia, diagnosed on 26 October 1977, was the last natural case of all (variola minor). An international commission certified eradication on 9 December 1979, and the World Health Assembly endorsed it on 8 May 1980. Smallpox is the only human disease eradicated; rinderpest (declared eradicated by the FAO 14 October 2010, officially 2011) is the only animal one. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eradication_of_infectious_diseases; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallpox]
- During the eradication campaign, vaccinators still met variolators in remote Pakistan and Afghanistan. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variolation]
- Live variola stocks remain only at the CDC (Atlanta) and the VECTOR institute (Koltsovo, Russia). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallpox]
- Religious responses included worship of the Hindu goddess Shitala to prevent or cure smallpox. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallpox]

## Cholera and the birth of public health
- Seven cholera pandemics since 1817. The first (1817–1824) began in Bengal near Calcutta and spread through trade routes to South-East Asia, the Middle East, East Africa, China, Java (over 100,000 dead) and Korea (perhaps 100,000 in 1821). The seventh, El Tor, began in Indonesia in 1961 and is officially ongoing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cholera]
- India is estimated to have lost over 15 million to cholera in 1817–1860 and another 23 million in 1865–1917. The Russian Empire lost over 2 million in a similar period. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cholera]
- The Hajj was a recurrent route: over 15,000 died in Mecca in 1846, and in the fourth pandemic 30,000 of 90,000 pilgrims died in its first year. Cholera broke out 27 times during the Hajj between the 19th century and 1930. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cholera]
- Other tolls: Tokyo 100,000–200,000 in 1858–1860; Hamburg 8,600 in 1892 (Richard Evans, *Death in Hamburg*, 1987); the Philippines 200,000 in 1902–04, including Apolinario Mabini. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cholera]
- John Snow and Broad Street (Soho, London, from 31 August 1854): 127 died in three days and 420 in all. With the Reverend Henry Whitehead, Snow traced the deaths to the Broad Street pump, addressed the St James's parish Guardians on Thursday 7 September, and the handle was removed the next day. Officials then rejected his water theory, and miasma theory stayed dominant. Snow's dot map is famous, and the brewery workers who drank beer were spared. Covered in detail in the Victorian Era chapter; cross-refer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1854_Broad_Street_cholera_outbreak]
- The bacterium *Vibrio cholerae* was described by Filippo Pacini in 1854, but this was not accepted until decades later. Robert Koch identified it again in 1883–84. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1854_Broad_Street_cholera_outbreak; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cholera]
- Modern cholera: Peru 1991–94 (1.04 million cases, almost 10,000 deaths). Haiti from October 2010: blamed on a camp of Nepalese UN peacekeepers, disputed but since acknowledged by the UN. Yemen from 2016, tied to civil war, malnutrition and broken sanitation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cholera]
- Rosenberg's *The Cholera Years* (1962) studied US epidemics of 1832, 1849 and 1866. In the US, cholera was blamed on immigrants, especially the Irish. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_E._Rosenberg; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cholera]

## International health: sanitary conferences to the pandemic agreement
- The International Sanitary Conferences ran from 1851 to 1938, fourteen in all. The first opened in Paris on 23 July 1851, organised by France to standardise quarantine against cholera, plague and yellow fever. Twelve states each sent a physician and a diplomat, the Ottoman Empire among them. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Sanitary_Conferences]
- Later conferences: Constantinople 1866, after the 1865 cholera. Venice 1892 adopted the first International Sanitary Convention (cholera via the Suez route). Paris 1894 dealt with pilgrim traffic and Venice 1897 with plague. In 1907 the Rome Arrangement created the Office international d'hygiène publique (OIHP) in Paris. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Sanitary_Conferences; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarantine]
- The WHO was established on 7 April 1948 and began work on 1 September 1948. It absorbed the League of Nations Health Organization and the OIHP. Szeming Sze of the Republic of China helped initiate it at the 1945 San Francisco conference. Brock Chisholm was its first Director-General, and its early priorities were malaria, tuberculosis and STIs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization]
- The International Health Regulations were adopted in 1969 (replacing the sanitary regulations) and revised in 2005 after SARS. IHR (2005) entered into force on 15 June 2007, binding 196 States Parties, and created the "Public Health Emergency of International Concern" (PHEIC). Amendments in 2024 (77th WHA) added a higher alert, a "pandemic emergency". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Health_Regulations]
- WHO Pandemic Agreement: adopted at the 78th World Health Assembly on 20 May 2025. WP says 124 members voted in favour and 11 abstained (including Poland, Israel, Italy, Russia, Slovakia, Iran), with no objections; the US did not take part. It explicitly denies WHO any authority to order national measures. It opens for signature only after the WHA adopts an annex on Pathogen Access and Benefit-Sharing (PABS) and enters into force after 60 ratifications. In May 2026 the 79th WHA did not adopt the PABS annex and extended negotiations toward the 80th WHA or an earlier special session. As of November 2026 the agreement is not open for signature. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WHO_Pandemic_Agreement; https://www.paho.org/en/paho-and-open-ended-intergovernmental-working-group-igwg; https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/pandemic-prevention--preparedness-and-response-accord]
- The US withdrawal from the WHO took effect on 22 January 2026, a year after President Trump's notice of 20 January 2025. It was the first withdrawal of a member state in WHO history; Argentina has also withdrawn. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization]

## Malaria, quinine, yellow fever and empire
- Cinchona bark was used by Quechua people in the Andes and observed by Jesuits (Agostino Salumbrino in Lima). It was used against malaria from at least 1632 and reached Spain by 1636. Pelletier and Caventou isolated quinine in 1820. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_malaria; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine]
- Manuel Incra Mamani collected high-quinine cinchona seeds in Bolivia, whose export was banned, for the English trader Charles Ledger (1865). The Dutch bought them, planted *Cinchona ledgeriana* in Java, and by the late 19th century held a world monopoly. Mamani was arrested and died after a beating (the source text is cut off here; see NOT VERIFIED). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_malaria; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine]
- Quinine is credited with ending Africa's reputation as the "white man's grave". A historian quoted on WP says "it was quinine's efficacy that gave colonists fresh opportunities to swarm into the Gold Coast, Nigeria and other parts of west Africa". Already in the Imperial Expansion chapter; cross-refer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine]
- Science of transmission: Laveran saw the parasite in blood in 1880. Patrick Manson showed mosquitoes carry filaria and proposed the mosquito–malaria theory (1894). Ronald Ross, an Indian Medical Service surgeon in Secunderabad, showed mosquito transmission in 1897–98 and won the 1902 Nobel Prize. Grassi and colleagues showed in 1898 that only *Anopheles* transmits human malaria, which led to a priority dispute. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_malaria; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Manson]
- Tropical medicine: Manson, "the father of tropical medicine", had worked for the Chinese Imperial Maritime Customs in Formosa and Amoy. He experimented using his gardener Hin Lo, founded the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine in 1899, and wrote *Tropical Diseases* (1898). The Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine also exists. Deborah Neill's *Networks in Tropical Medicine* (Stanford, 2012) shows the field shaped by sleeping-sickness campaigns. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_medicine; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Manson]
- Sleeping sickness (*Trypanosoma brucei*, tsetse-borne): major epidemics 1896–1906 in Uganda and the Congo Basin, and again around 1920 and 1970. Eugène Jamot (1879–1937), a French colonial military doctor, led campaigns in Ubangi-Shari (1917) and Cameroon (Ayos, 1922), was celebrated in Paris in 1931, and remains honoured in Cameroon. Pentamidine was first used against it in 1937; Lachenal's *The Lomidine Files* documents disasters from mass preventive injections. Details are in the Health in Africa chapter; cross-refer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_trypanosomiasis; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Jamot; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentamidine]
- In 1955 the WHO launched the Global Malaria Eradication Programme, relying on DDT and treatment. It eliminated malaria from North America, Europe and the USSR, Taiwan, much of the Caribbean and elsewhere, and cut mortality in Sri Lanka and India. It was suspended in 1969, and attention shifted to control and bednets. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria]
- Tu Youyou isolated artemisinin under China's Project 523 (announced 23 May 1967) and received the 2015 Nobel Prize. Chloroquine resistance emerged in the 1950s (Cambodia–Thailand border, Colombia). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_malaria]
- Malaria today: 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths in 2024 (WHO World Malaria Report 2025). The WHO African Region has about 95% of cases and deaths, and children under five account for 75% of African malaria deaths. Vaccines: RTS,S (piloted from 2019) and R21/Matrix-M (WHO-endorsed 2023). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria]
- The claim that malaria killed "half of all humans who ever lived" (a 2002 *Nature* article) is unsourced and doubted by statisticians. Do not use it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_malaria]
- Yellow fever originated in Africa and probably crossed to the Americas with the Atlantic slave trade. The first definitive New World outbreak was Barbados in 1647; Yucatán followed in 1648 (Maya called it *xekik*, "blood vomit") and Recife in 1685. Africans in endemic areas often had immunity from childhood infection, while newcomers died. J. R. McNeill links outbreaks to the ecology of sugar plantations. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_fever]
- Haiti: J. R. McNeill estimates yellow fever inflicted 35,000–45,000 casualties on Napoleon's army under Leclerc in Saint-Domingue, with only about a third returning to France. British garrison mortality in Jamaica was seven times that in Canada. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_fever]
- J. R. McNeill, *Mosquito Empires: Ecology and War in the Greater Caribbean, 1620–1914* (Cambridge, 2010; Beveridge Award 2011). Its argument rests on "differential immunity": seasoned locals and defenders resisted yellow fever and malaria while invading armies died. It explains Spanish success holding colonies and revolutionary victories, including in Saint-Domingue. Critics warn of determinism. [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/404907; https://e-ir.info/?p=52062]
- Carlos Finlay (Cuban) proposed in 1881 that a mosquito (later *Aedes aegypti*) transmits yellow fever. The US Army commission under Walter Reed (with Carroll, Agramonte and Lazear) confirmed it in 1900–01, and Reed credited Finlay. Oswaldo Cruz led Brazil's campaign from 1903. Max Theiler developed the 17D vaccine in 1937 and won a Nobel Prize. The 1878 Lower Mississippi Valley epidemic killed about 20,000. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_fever]
- Panama: the French effort (1881–1889) lost an estimated 22,000 dead from disease and accidents, as many as 5,000 of them French. In 1888 nine-tenths of about 40,000 workers were Afro-Caribbean. Under the US (1904–1914), William C. Gorgas's mosquito campaign nearly eliminated yellow fever and malaria. Still, about 5,600 workers died from disease and accidents, the great majority West Indian (especially Barbadian), against about 350 Americans. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panama_Canal]

## Tuberculosis
- Called consumption, phthisis and the "White Plague". In 19th-century Europe it killed perhaps a quarter of adults. In England and Wales about 4 million died of TB between 1851 and 1910, including over a third of deaths at ages 15–34. It was romanticised (*La traviata*, *La bohème*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_tuberculosis]
- Robert Koch announced the tubercle bacillus at the Physiological Society of Berlin on 24 March 1882, now World TB Day. Sanatoriums followed from Brehmer, and Trudeau's at Saranac Lake opened in 1884. BCG (Calmette and Guérin) was first used on humans in 1921. Streptomycin was isolated in 1944 by Schatz, Bugie and Waksman. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_tuberculosis]
- Thomas McKeown showed that TB mortality in England and Wales had fallen 90–95% before streptomycin and BCG, crediting living standards. This is a key debate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_tuberculosis]
- The WHO declared TB a global health emergency in 1993. In 2024 TB killed 1.23 million people (WHO Global TB Report 2025), making it the world's leading infectious killer; India accounted for 28% of TB deaths. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_tuberculosis; https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/11/13/tuberculosis-kills-1-23-million-people-last-year-who-says]

## The 1918–1920 influenza
- An H1N1 influenza A pandemic. About 500 million were infected, about a third of the world. Deaths are "25–50 million (generally accepted)", with estimates from 17 to 100 million: 21.6 million (1927), 25–39 million (Patterson and Pyle 1991), 50 million and possibly 100 million (Johnson and Mueller 2002), about 17 million (2018 reassessment, contested), and "well above 100 million" (John M. Barry, 2021). That is 1–6% of a world population of 1.8–1.9 billion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu]
- Its origin is undetermined. Claims include Haskell County, Kansas (January 1918; Camp Funston, 4 March 1918), Étaples/Aldershot 1916–17 (John Oxford), and China via the Chinese Labour Corps (Mark Humphries, 2014; a 2016 study found no evidence). "Spanish" because neutral Spain's uncensored press reported it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu]
- Blame names: "Spanish flu", "Flanders flu", "Brazilian flu" in Senegal, "German flu" in Brazil, the "Bolshevik disease" in Poland. Some Africans called it a "white man's sickness", and Japan blamed sumo wrestlers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu]
- Regional tolls: India 12–17 million, about 5% (13.88 million in British-ruled districts), and India's population fell in 1911–21. Iran 902,400–2,431,000 (8–22%), during the 1917–19 famine. Dutch East Indies about 1.5 million. Western Samoa (occupied by New Zealand) lost 22% of 38,000 in two months. Tahiti lost 13% in a month. US 500,000–850,000. Europe about 2.64 million excess deaths in 1918–19. China's figures are disputed (5–9 million per Patterson and Pyle vs 1–1.28 million per Iijima). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu]
- Quarantine worked in places: American Samoa and New Caledonia had no deaths through maritime quarantine, and Australia avoided the first two waves. October 1918 was the deadliest month, and young adults died at unusual rates (contested). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu]
- Laura Spinney's *Pale Rider* (WP cites it as Spinney 2018; first-publication year not verified) is a key global history and the source of many of WP's global details. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu]

## HIV/AIDS
- HIV-1 descends from the SIV of chimpanzees (*Pan troglodytes troglodytes*) in west-central Africa. The pandemic group M emerged in Léopoldville (Kinshasa), Belgian Congo; its common ancestor is dated to about 1910–1931 (one estimate 1931, range 1915–1941). The earliest documented human sample is from Kinshasa in 1959. Michael Worobey and others link its spread to colonial cities, labour migration and, per Jacques Pépin's *The Origins of AIDS* (2011), unsterile colonial injection campaigns. The oral polio vaccine theory is discredited. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_HIV/AIDS]
- HIV reached Haiti from Central Africa around 1966–67, probably with a returning worker; Haitians had served in the Congo after 1960. It reached the US around 1969. Gaëtan Dugas, the "Patient Zero" of legend, was not the source: HIV reached New York around 1971, before he started at Air Canada in 1974. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_HIV/AIDS; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_in_Haiti]
- AIDS was officially recognised on 5 June 1981 in the CDC's *MMWR* (five gay men in Los Angeles with PCP), and was first called "GRID". In May 1983 Barré-Sinoussi and Montagnier at the Pasteur Institute isolated the virus (LAV) and shared the 2008 Nobel Prize; Gallo's priority was disputed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_HIV/AIDS]
- The Haitian "4-H" stigma: in 1982 the CDC noted 34 Haitian cases and labelled Haitians a risk group, alongside homosexuals, haemophiliacs and heroin users. This damaged Haiti's tourism and exports. GHESKIO's research found Haitian risk factors no different from elsewhere. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_in_Haiti]
- Paul Farmer, *AIDS and Accusation: Haiti and the Geography of Blame* (University of California Press, 1992; updated 2006) was the first full ethnography of AIDS in a poor society. It asked whether the theory of a Haitian origin of North American AIDS rested on racism rather than evidence. Farmer co-founded Partners In Health in 1987. [source: https://www.ucpress.edu/flyer/books/aids-and-accusation/paper; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_in_Haiti]
- The toll: at least 42.3 million deaths through 2024 (WP infobox gives 43.2 million cumulative), and 40.9 million living with HIV in 2025. New infections were 1.2 million in 2025, down from 2.1 million in 2010. Deaths peaked at about 2 million in 2005. Eastern and southern Africa hold over half of people with HIV. South Africa's denialism and treatment struggle are in the Health in Africa chapter; cross-refer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS]

## SARS, Ebola, COVID-19
- SARS: first identified in Foshan, Guangdong, in November 2002. China delayed reporting, and the WHO issued a global alert in March 2003. There were 8,096 cases and 774 deaths in about 30 countries/territories (mainland China 349, Hong Kong 299, Taiwan 73). WHO declared it contained on 5 July 2003, and it led to the IHR revision of 2005. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002%E2%80%932004_SARS_outbreak; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Health_Regulations]
- West African Ebola, December 2013 – June 2016. The index case was probably Emile Ouamouno, aged 1–2, in Meliandou, Guinea (December 2013); WHO reported the outbreak on 25 March 2014. There were 28,616 cases (infobox 28,646) and 11,310 deaths, centred on Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone, and about 17,000 survivors. It was declared a PHEIC on 8 August 2014, ended 29 March 2016. Nigeria was declared Ebola-free on 20 October 2014, credited largely to Dr Ameyo Adadevoh. The rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine was approved in 2019. Already in the Health in Africa chapter; cross-refer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_African_Ebola_epidemic]
- 2026 Ebola epidemic (Bundibugyo virus, against which existing Zaire-ebolavirus vaccines and treatments were not designed): first reported 14 May 2026 in Ituri, DRC, and declared a PHEIC on 16 May 2026. As of early November 2026 there were about 11,073 confirmed cases and 5,391 deaths, almost all in the DRC, with cases in Uganda, Kenya, France and Nigeria. Figures are moving and conflict hampers detection. The Health in Africa chapter already mentions it ("more than 5,000 deaths" by early November 2026); keep to one sentence and cross-refer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Ebola_epidemic]
- COVID-19: the outbreak began in Wuhan in December 2019. The WHO declared a PHEIC on 30 January 2020 and characterised it as a pandemic on 11 March 2020. The names COVID-19 and SARS-CoV-2 were finalised on 11 February 2020. The PHEIC ended on 5 May 2023. The scientific consensus favours zoonotic origin; a laboratory leak has been proposed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic]
- COVID deaths: about 7.1 million reported (7,116,673 as of 6 September 2026). WHO estimated 14.9 million excess deaths in 2020–21 against 5.4 million reported (May 2022), reaffirmed as about 14.8 million in December 2022. *The Economist* estimated 18.5–35.2 million (central about 27.4 million) by 25 January 2024, and IHME over 18.5 million by 1 April 2023. Vaccines prevented an estimated 14.4–19.8 million deaths in their first year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic]
- Blame and inequality: prejudice against people of Chinese and East Asian descent was documented worldwide. Donald Trump's "Chinese Virus" and "Kung Flu" were condemned as racist. COVAX (WHO, Gavi, CEPI) aimed at vaccine equity. The contemporary chapters already cover Karikó/Weissman, COVAX and "vaccine nationalism"; cross-refer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic]

## Blame and stigma (cross-cutting)
- Jews were accused of well-poisoning in 1348–51. The first plague massacre was in Toulon in April 1348 (40 killed), followed by Barcelona and other Catalan cities. In Strasbourg in February 1349 about 2,000 Jews were murdered, and Mainz and Cologne were annihilated in August 1349. By 1351, 60 major and 150 smaller communities had been destroyed, and many Jews moved to Poland under Casimir III. Clement VI's bulls of 6 July and 26 September 1348 called the accusers "seduced by that liar, the Devil". Emperor Charles IV undercut them by letting authorities seize murdered Jews' property. Already in the Black Death chapter; cross-refer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Jews_during_the_Black_Death; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death]
- Other patterns: Chinatowns quarantined and burned (Honolulu, San Francisco, 1900); the 1918 flu named after enemies and neighbours; the Haitian "4H" label; anti-Asian prejudice in 2020. In the US, cholera was blamed on Irish immigrants. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_plague_pandemic; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_in_Haiti; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cholera]
- Frank Snowden's *Epidemics and Society* emphasises how epidemics fed scapegoating and repression; *Nature*'s review highlighted this. [source: https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-019-03048-8/email/correspondent/c1/new]

## Debates
- **Disease as historical actor vs. social framing.** McNeill and Crosby made pathogens drivers of history. Rosenberg's "framing" and "dramaturgy" stress that societies define and narrate epidemics. Most historians now combine biology with social response. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plagues_and_Peoples; https://ubcpress.ca/framing-disease]
- **"Virgin soil" and the Americas.** Crosby's concept was popularised by Diamond's *Guns, Germs and Steel*. David S. Jones, "Virgin Soils Revisited", *William and Mary Quarterly* 60:4 (2003), 703–742, argued the term implies genetic predisposition and obscures social, environmental and biological factors that raise or lower susceptibility. Paul Kelton stresses the European-driven Indigenous slave trade in smallpox's spread. Cristobal Silva treats New England accounts as products of circumstance. Gregory Cushman says Pacific deaths came more from TB and dysentery among overworked, exploited people. Jeffrey Ostler and Christopher Browning argue colonialism made epidemics deadly, and Browning that disease does not exempt settler colonialism from the charge of genocide. Andrés Reséndez's Indigenous slavery argument is already in the Conquest chapter. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin-soil_epidemic; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_history_of_the_Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas]
- **The Justinianic toll.** Maximalists (15–100 million; transformative) are set against Mordechai and Eisenberg (2019, "inconsequential"), with Sarris (2022) rebutting them. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Justinian]
- **Rome's fall and disease/climate.** Harper's *The Fate of Rome* (2017) argues for climate plus pandemics. Haldon, Mordechai, Newfield and colleagues (2018) call this extreme but accept some impact. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyle_Harper; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonine_Plague]
- **Black Death: what, where from, how far.** Earlier sceptics doubted it was plague, before aDNA settled it in 2010–11. Its origin is debated: Issyk-Kul 1338–39 (Spyrou 2022), Caspian (Benedictow), China. Green's "Four Black Deaths" places a 13th-century divergence tied to the Mongols, and Green and Chouin argue it reached sub-Saharan Africa, against Sussman's doubts about India, China and East Africa. Mortality is put at 30–60%, with Benedictow at 60%. Transmission by rats is debated against human ectoparasites. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death]
- **Why TB declined.** McKeown credited nutrition and living standards over medicine, a famous and contested thesis. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_tuberculosis]
- **Colonial medicine.** Tropical medicine and sleeping-sickness and plague campaigns served empire (Neill; Echenberg). Coercive quarantine in India, Cape Town, Honolulu and San Francisco stands against real scientific gains (Yersin and Kitasato, Ross, Finlay). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_medicine; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_plague_pandemic]
- **Disease and empire in the Caribbean.** J. R. McNeill's "differential immunity" explains imperial and revolutionary outcomes; critics warn of determinism. [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/404907]
- **1918 numbers and origin.** Global deaths range from 17 to over 100 million; the origin may be Kansas, France or China. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu]
- **Origins of HIV.** Colonial urbanisation and medical campaigns (Pépin, Worobey) explain it; the "Haitian origin" in the US is rejected (Farmer). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_HIV/AIDS; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS_in_Haiti]
- **COVID's toll and origin.** Official deaths (about 7 million) sit against excess estimates of 14.9 million (WHO, 2020–21 only) up to 18.5–35.2 million (*Economist*). Zoonotic origin is the consensus against lab-leak claims. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic]
