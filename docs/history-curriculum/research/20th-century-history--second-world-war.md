## 1. Framing, dates, overall scale
- Conventional dates: 1 September 1939 – 2 September 1945. [source: WW2]
  - WW2 lists Japan's invasion of Manchuria (1931), the Spanish Civil War, and the 1937 outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War among the key events before the war.
  - SSJ dates the Sino-Japanese war from 7 July 1937 to 9 September 1945: "eight years, two months, and two days". [source: SSJ]
- Overy's *Blood and Ruins* dates a "last imperial war" from 1931. He treats the 1931 Japanese invasion of Manchuria as the start of an Asian war that merged with the European war when Japan attacked the US. [source: https://ssi.armywarcollege.edu/SSI-Media/Recent-Publications/Display/Article/3596982/blood-and-ruins-the-last-imperial-war-19311945 (search summary)]
- Nearly all the world's countries were involved, and the war was the deadliest conflict in history. [source: WW2]
- **Total deaths (estimates conflict):**
  - WW2 lead and CAS lead: "60 to 75 million", about 3% of a 1940 world population of about 2.3 billion. CAS cites Beevor.
  - CAS's country table totals 70–85 million. That total adds 19–28 million deaths from war-related famine and disease (in the USSR, China, Indonesia, Vietnam, the Philippines and India) that "are often omitted from other compilations".
  - The seed's "70–85 million" matches the CAS table. Write: "estimates run from about 60 million to 85 million, depending on whether famine and disease deaths are counted".
  - Military deaths were 21–25 million (CAS lead) or 21–25.5 million (table), including about 5 million prisoners of war who died in captivity.
  - Civilian deaths were a majority of the total. [source: WW2; CAS]
- More than half of all deaths were in China and the Soviet Union. [source: CAS]
- An estimated 7–10 million people died in the Dutch, British, French and US colonies in South and Southeast Asia, mostly from war-related famine. [source: CAS]
- "Casualty statistics are notoriously unreliable": CAS quoting the *Oxford Companion to World War II*. [source: CAS]

## 2. China, 1937–45: where the war began
- **Marco Polo Bridge incident, 7 July 1937**, near Beijing (Wanping / Lugou Bridge). [source: MPB]
  - Japanese garrison troops held an unusual night manoeuvre, said a soldier was missing, and demanded entry to Wanping. Fighting broke out while this was still being negotiated; the soldier had already returned.
  - It is generally regarded as the start of the Second Sino-Japanese War, and it escalated into full-scale invasion.
- **Shanghai and Nanjing.** After the protracted Battle of Shanghai (Shanghai was captured in November 1937), Japan took the capital, Nanjing, in December 1937. About 50,000 Chinese defenders were killed, including 17 generals. [source: SSJ; NAN]
- **The Nanjing Massacre** (the "Rape of Nanking"): mass rape and murder of civilians, non-combatants and POWs, December 1937 – March 1938. [source: NAN]
  - It began on 13 December 1937, when troops entered the city.
  - Prince Yasuhiko Asaka, installed as temporary commander, issued an order to "kill all captives". Neither General Iwane Matsui nor Asaka stopped the massacre.
  - About a third of the city was destroyed by arson.
- **Nanjing death toll — give a range:**
  - The IMTFE (Tokyo tribunal) estimated more than 200,000 killed; "many scholars support" its validity.
  - Newer estimates mostly fall between 100,000 and 200,000. Kasahara Tokushi: "more than 100,000 and close to 200,000, or maybe more". Wakabayashi concurs: "far exceed[s] 100,000 but fall[s] short of 200,000".
  - Extremes run from about 40,000 (the city alone) to over 340,000 (the whole Shanghai–Nanjing region).
  - The 1947 Nanjing War Crimes Tribunal gave more than 300,000. That figure is officially endorsed by the PRC and carved on the Memorial Hall. Ikuhiko Hata calls 300,000 a "symbolic figure".
  - Rapes: estimates run from 4,000 to over 80,000; about 20,000 is most commonly cited (the IMTFE's figure for the first month).
  [source: NAN; SSJ]
- **Dates of the massacre:** traditionally six weeks from 13 December. Kasahara and Wakabayashi extend it to 4 December 1937 – 28 March 1938, including six surrounding counties. [source: NAN]
- **Foreign witnesses:** the Nanking Safety Zone's International Committee, including the German Nazi Party member John Rabe, is credited with saving at least 200,000 lives. [source: NAN]
- **Aftermath:** Matsui was convicted and executed. Asaka was granted immunity as a member of the imperial family. Japanese nationalists and revisionists, including senior officials, have denied or minimised the massacre. [source: NAN]
- **Wuhan and the retreat inland.** Wuhan fell in 1938, and the Nationalist government moved to Chongqing. [source: SSJ]
- **1938 Yellow River flood.** From 13 June 1938, Nationalist forces deliberately broke the Yellow River dikes at Huayuankou to block the Japanese advance. [source: YRF]
  - 30,000–89,000 civilians drowned immediately.
  - In all, 400,000–500,000 died from drowning, famine and plague.
  - The river stayed diverted until January 1947.
- **Bombing of Chongqing,** 18 February 1938 – 19 December 1944: Japanese terror bombing of the wartime capital. [source: CHO; SSJ]
  - The infobox gives more than 30,000 civilian casualties, including more than 10,000 deaths.
  - SSJ calls these the war's first massive air raids on civilian targets.
- **Stalemate, communists and collaborators.** [source: SSJ]
  - From 1939 the war was a stalemate.
  - The CCP's Hundred Regiments Offensive came in August 1940.
  - Wang Jingwei's collaborationist regime was "very unpopular and largely ineffective".
  - Nationalist forces fought the 22 major battles; the communists mostly fought a guerrilla war.
- **Three Alls Policy** ("Kill all, loot all, burn all"), 1941–42 onwards in North and Central China. It involved massacres, poison gas and deliberate starvation, killed millions of civilians, and lasted until March 1945. [source: SSJ]
- **Weapons of mass destruction.** Japan carried out "the largest attacks in the history of biological warfare, causing at least 200,000 deaths", and used chemical weapons including blister agents. [source: SSJ]
- **Outside aid.** [source: SSJ]
  - Soviet aid came after the Sino-Soviet Non-Aggression Pact and stopped with the Soviet–Japanese Neutrality Pact in April 1941.
  - US Lend-Lease to China followed (LL: $1.6 billion to China).
  - Supplies came by the Burma Road, then flown over "the Hump", then by the Ledo Road (1945).
- **Operation Ichi-Go (1944)** was the largest Japanese campaign of the war in China: 500,000 troops, 100,000 horses, 1,500 guns and 800 tanks. Chinese forces suffered 300,000 casualties. [source: SSJ]
- **Chinese deaths — a range:** [source: SSJ; CAS]
  - Modern estimates run from 10 to 20 million; SSJ says the "consensus" is 15–16 million.
  - Westad: about 12 million civilian and 2 million military deaths. Mitter considers Westad's figures conservative.
  - PRC official figure: over 35 million casualties, including over 20 million dead.
  - CAS table: 14–20 million.
- **Refugees:** "The war created 95 million refugees" (SSJ; the attribution is unclear). Use "tens of millions" or "some 95 million (one estimate)". [source: SSJ]
- **China's status.** China was recognised as one of the "Big Four" and the "Four Policemen", regained Taiwan, and became a permanent member of the UN Security Council. [source: SSJ]

## 3. Europe, 1939–41 (brief; the Interwar chapter covers the road to war)
- **Poland.** The invasion of Poland ran from 1 September to 6 October 1939. Germany and Slovakia attacked first; the Soviet Union invaded on 17 September under the Molotov–Ribbentrop secret protocol. Poland was divided under the German–Soviet Frontier Treaty. [source: POL]
- **Declarations of war.** Britain and France declared war on 3 September 1939. [source: FRA]
- **Soviet expansion and the German north.** In 1940 the USSR annexed the Baltic states and parts of Finland and Romania. Germany conquered Norway, Denmark, Belgium, Luxembourg and the Netherlands. [source: WW2]
- **The fall of France** (10 May – 25 June 1940). [source: FRA]
  - German armour advanced through the Ardennes (*Fall Gelb*).
  - The Dunkirk evacuation (Operation Dynamo) took off 338,226 men in total.
  - Italy entered the war on 10 June, and Paris was occupied on 14 June.
  - The armistice of 22 June 1940 at Compiègne was followed by the Vichy regime under Pétain.
  - 1,540,000 French prisoners were taken to Germany.
  - "At least 3,000 Senegalese Tirailleurs were murdered after being taken prisoner."
- **Battle of Britain.** The British date it 10 July – 31 October 1940; the Blitz ran from 7 September 1940 to 11 May 1941. German historians treat both as a single campaign from July 1940 to May 1941. It was "the first major military campaign fought entirely by air forces" and "the first major German defeat". Hitler postponed, then cancelled, Operation Sea Lion. [source: BOB]
  - No. 303 (Polish) Squadron "became the highest-scoring Allied unit".
  - The Czech pilot Josef František, flying with 303, had the highest RAF score in the battle.
- **The Balkans, the Mediterranean and East Africa.** By mid-1941 the Axis had defeated Yugoslavia and Greece. Fighting continued in the Mediterranean, the Middle East and East Africa, along with the Battle of the Atlantic. [source: WW2]
- **Britain and the US.** Lend-Lease was signed into law on 11 March 1941. [source: LL]

## 4. The Eastern Front: the war's centre
- **Operation Barbarossa,** 22 June 1941. [source: BAR]
  - More than 3.8 million Axis troops attacked on a front of about 2,900 km. EAS gives 3,767,000 in the east on 22 June, "80% of the German Army".
  - It was "the largest and costliest military offensive in human history", with over 8 million casualties by 5 December 1941.
  - It opened "the largest and deadliest land war in history". [source: EAS]
- **A war of annihilation.** [source: BAR; EAS]
  - *Generalplan Ost* planned the removal of Slavs by deportation, Germanisation, enslavement and genocide.
  - The Hunger Plan aimed at "the extermination and ethnic cleansing of more than a hundred million Eastern Europeans".
  - The Germans captured five million Red Army soldiers and "deliberately starved to death or otherwise killed 3.3 million" of them.
  - WW2 says "almost 60 per cent of Soviet prisoners of the Germans" died. Write "about 3 million or more".
  - Over a million Soviet Jews were murdered by shooting and gassing (BAR). HOL gives 1.5–2 million Jews murdered after the invasion.
- **Weight of the front.** Geoffrey Roberts: "more than 80 percent of all combat during the Second World War took place on the Eastern Front". EAS calls the front "the main cause of the defeat of Nazi Germany". [source: EAS]
- **Factories moved east.** In 1941 thousands of trains moved factories and workers from Belarus and Ukraine to the Urals, Central Asia and Siberia. [source: EAS]
- **Lend-Lease to the USSR** was $11.3 billion, of a $50.1 billion total; the UK received $31.4 billion. [source: LL]
  - Military hardware was about 20% of aid to the USSR; the rest was food, metals, chemicals, aviation fuel and machinery.
  - Lend-Lease supplied 16% of Soviet explosives.
  [source: EAS]
- **Siege of Leningrad,** 8 September 1941 – 27 January 1944, 872 days. Germany and Finland besieged the city, which was never captured. [source: LEN]
  - Deaths:
    - the lead gives about 1.5 million, from a pre-war population of 3.2 million;
    - the infobox gives 1.3–2.0 million;
    - Glantz gives 1.6–2 million soldiers and civilians.
  - From November 1941 to February 1942 the bread ration was 125 grams a day, 50–60% of it sawdust and other inedible fillers.
  - Supplies came across Lake Ladoga, over the ice on the "Road of Life".
  - Shostakovich's Seventh ("Leningrad") Symphony was performed in the city on 9 August 1942.
  - "Some historians have classified it as a genocide."
- **Stalingrad.** The German offensive began on 23 August 1942. Operation Uranus encircled the 6th Army on 19 November. Paulus surrendered on 2 February 1943, the first of Hitler's field armies to do so. It was "the largest and deadliest urban battle in military history", generally regarded as the turning point in Europe. [source: STA]
  - Stalin's Order No. 227 of 27 July 1942 brought tribunals for unauthorised retreat and blocking detachments.
  - Women crewed parts of the 1077th Anti-Aircraft Regiment.
  - Hitler made Paulus a field marshal because no German field marshal had ever surrendered.
  - Losses:
    - about 91,000 prisoners were taken, of whom only 5,000–6,000 came home, the last in 1955;
    - the infobox gives 1.1–3 million+ total dead;
    - "over one million soldiers and civilians combined".
- **Kursk and Bagration.** Kursk "crippled German offensive capabilities permanently". Operation Bagration (1944) destroyed Army Group Centre, after which Romania and Bulgaria changed sides. [source: EAS]
- **Soviet losses (a range):**
  - About 27 million is the commonly cited total; the Soviet-era official figure was 20 million.
  - The Russian government gives 26.6 million (1993 Academy of Sciences study), including 8,668,400 military deaths (Krivosheev). Most historians outside Russia accept that military figure.
  - Others give 10.6–11.4 million military deaths.
  - Some Russian scholars give over 40 million in all.
  - CAS table: 20–27 million in total; 8.7–11.4 million military; 4.5–10 million civilians from military action and crimes; 8–9 million from famine and disease.
  [source: SOV; CAS]
- **Poland's losses:** Poland lost 5.6–5.8 million (IPN, 2009); the CAS table gives 5.9–6.0 million, about 17% of the 1939 population, the highest proportion of any country. [source: CAS]
- **German military deaths:** Overmans (2000) gives 5.3 million dead and missing; the CAS table gives 4.44–5.318 million. "The Soviet Red Army claimed responsibility for the majority of Wehrmacht casualties." [source: CAS]

## 5. The Pacific and Southeast Asia
- **The road to Pearl Harbor.** In July 1941 Japan occupied southern Indochina. The US, the UK and others froze Japanese assets and imposed "a total oil embargo"; many Japanese officers saw the embargo as "an unspoken declaration of war". [source: WW2]
- **7 December 1941** (8 December in Asian time zones). Japan attacked near-simultaneously: Pearl Harbor and the Philippines; invasions of Guam, Wake, Malaya, Thailand and Hong Kong. [source: WW2]
- **Pearl Harbor:** 2,403 Americans were killed (68 of them civilians) and 1,178 wounded. Japan lost 29 aircraft, five midget submarines and 130 personnel. The three US carriers were at sea. Roosevelt called the day "a date which will live in infamy". [source: PH]
- **The fall of Singapore, 15 February 1942.** About 80,000 British, Indian, Australian and local troops became prisoners, on top of the 50,000 taken in Malaya. It was Britain's "largest surrender in history". [source: SOS]
- **Sook Ching massacre** of Chinese in Singapore, February–March 1942: the infobox gives 40,000–50,000 deaths ("consensus"). Lee Kuan Yew put it at "about 70,000" including Malaya. [source: SOOK]
- **Conquests by April 1942:** Malaya, the Dutch East Indies (the Dutch surrendered on 8 March 1942), Singapore, Rabaul and most of Burma. [source: WW2; JOI]
- **Bataan Death March** (1942): the forced transfer of 60,000 Filipino and 15,000 American POWs; the death toll is uncertain. [source: FIL]
- **Midway,** 4–7 June 1942: US code-breakers knew the plan, and all four Japanese fleet carriers present (Akagi, Kaga, Sōryū, Hiryū) were sunk. It halted Japan's advance in the Pacific. [source: MID; WW2]
- **The "Co-Prosperity Sphere".** Japan called its empire the "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere", claiming to liberate colonised peoples. Japanese forces were "sometimes welcomed as liberators", but war crimes "frequently turned local public opinion against them". By 1943 Dutch East Indies oil output was back to 76% of its 1940 rate. [source: WW2]
- **Prisoners of Japan:** "about a third of those taken prisoner by the Japanese died". Japan and the USSR had not ratified the Geneva POW convention. [source: WW2]
- **Rōmusha** ("paid conscripted labourer"): Indonesians forced to work for the Japanese military. [source: ROM; JOI]
  - 4–10 million on Java (US Library of Congress estimate).
  - 200,000–500,000 were sent off Java, as far as Burma and Siam; "not more than 70,000 survived the war".
  - About 4 million people died in the Dutch East Indies from famine and forced labour under Japanese occupation (a later UN report); this includes 30,000 European internees. The CAS table gives 3–4 million deaths in all.
  - Caveat: the term was used loosely, so totals may include unpaid labour and auxiliaries.
- **Indonesian politics under occupation:** Sukarno headed a Central Advisory Board. Japan created PETA (Defenders of the Homeland) in October 1943, and Supriyadi led a PETA revolt in February 1945. Independence was proclaimed on 17 August 1945, followed by a war of independence against the Dutch. [source: JOI; AFT]
- **"Comfort women"** (*ianfu*, a euphemism): women and girls forced into sexual slavery by the Japanese armed forces. [source: CW]
  - They came mostly from Korea, but also from China, the Philippines, Indonesia, Burma, Taiwan, the Netherlands, Australia, East Timor, New Guinea and Japan itself.
  - **Numbers:** "most historians settling somewhere in the range of 20,000–200,000".
    - Yoshimi Yoshiaki: about 200,000, with some 2,000 comfort stations.
    - Hata Ikuhiko: fewer than 20,000.
    - Chinese scholars Su Zhiliang (360,000–410,000) and Qiu Peipei (200,000–400,000) estimate higher totals and say half were Chinese.
    - Write "estimates range from under 20,000 to over 400,000; most historians say between 20,000 and 200,000".
  - **Testimony:** Kim Hak-sun's public testimony in 1991 was pivotal; she was 17 when she was seized in 1941.
  - **Apologies:** the Kōno Statement (1993) acknowledged coercion. Japan offered apologies and compensation from the 1990s; victims and scholars criticise these as insincere. The Tokyo tribunal did not address the system.
- **Burma (Thai–Burma) Railway,** the "Death Railway": 415 km from Ban Pong to Thanbyuzayat. [source: BUR]
  - Building ran from June 1942 to its completion on 17 October 1943. (BUR's lead says "1940 to 1943"; the body says Japan began in June 1942, so use 1942–43.)
  - At least 250,000 Southeast Asian civilians were forced to work on it (the body text says over 180,000 were conscripted), and more than 90,000 of them died. They included Tamils, Malays, Burmese, Javanese and Chinese.
  - About 12,000 of some 60,000 Allied POWs died.
  - Allied POWs received some compensation; the Asian labourers received none from Britain.
- **The Philippines:** losses of 557,000 to over 1.4 million (CAS table). [source: CAS]
- **Vietnam:** the famine of 1944–45 in northern Vietnam, under Japanese occupation and Vichy French administration, killed between 400,000 and 2 million. [source: VFM]
- **Korea:** Korean deaths were 483,000–533,000 (CAS table). Korean residents and conscripted Korean workers were among the dead at Nagasaki. [source: CAS; AB]
- **Okinawa** (from 1 April 1945): about 12,000 US servicemen, 107,000 Japanese soldiers and over 150,000 Okinawan civilians were killed. [source: SB]
- **Japan's own dead:** the Japanese government puts them at 3.1 million; the CAS table gives 2.5–3.1 million. [source: CAS]

## 6. India and Burma (keep the Bengal famine brief)
- **War without consent.** Viceroy Linlithgow declared war for India in September 1939 without consulting Congress leaders. [source: IND]
- **Indian service and losses.** "Over two and a half million" Indian soldiers served, more than 2.5 million at the peak (IND). The British Empire chapter adds "largest volunteer army in history". Over 87,000 Indian troops died. They served in North and East Africa, the Middle East, Italy and Burma. [source: IND]
- **Auchinleck:** Britain "couldn't have come through both wars [World War I and II] if they hadn't had the Indian Army." [source: IND]
- **Quit India,** August 1942. Congress (Gandhi, Patel, Azad) refused to cooperate without independence. Over 60,000 national and local leaders were arrested; Gandhi was released in May 1944, others in June 1945. The Muslim League rejected Quit India and worked with the Raj. [source: IND]
- **Bengal famine, 1943** (one sentence; cross-refer to the British Empire chapter). Deaths are estimated at 0.8–3.8 million; Ó Gráda's "scholarly consensus is about 2.1 million". Wartime inflation and "poor British wartime policies" were among the causes. [source: BEN]
- **Indian National Army (Azad Hind Fauj).** [source: INA]
  - The first INA was founded in 1942 by Major Iwaichi Fujiwara and Captain Mohan Singh; it was disbanded in December 1942.
  - Subhas Chandra Bose arrived from Germany in May 1943 and re-founded it.
  - Its peak strength was about 43,000. It was drawn mostly from Indian Army POWs, with about 18,000 civilian volunteers from Malaya and Burma.
  - Bose's "Jai Hind" slogan became highly popular.
  - Its women's unit, the Rani of Jhansi Regiment, was led by Lakshmi Sahgal (spelled Sehgal in the INA article's lead).
  - Around 12,000 Indian POWs and 100,000 Indian civilians were used as forced labour by Japan.
  - The INA joined the 1944 invasion of India, which was halted at Imphal (and Kohima).
  - Afterwards the British charged 300 INA officers with treason in the INA trials, then backtracked in the face of Congress opposition. The trials became "a galvanising point" for independence.
  - Balance: the INA is described as a collaborationist force with poor battlefield performance; it "did not oppose Japanese Fascism, nor protest Japanese war crimes". Its anticolonial meaning in India is also strong. Present both views.
- **Bose's Indian Legion** was raised in Germany and was for a time part of the Waffen-SS. [source: IND]
- **Burma campaign.** [source: BC]
  - In 1942 there was a catastrophic retreat to India with refugees.
  - Japan's 1944 invasion of India failed at Imphal and Kohima.
  - Slim's Fourteenth Army retook Burma in late 1944–45.
  - British Empire forces peaked at about 1 million land and air forces, "drawn primarily from British India", including 100,000 East and West African colonial troops.

## 7. African and Caribbean soldiers
- **East Africa (KAR).** The King's African Rifles fought Italy in East Africa, Vichy France in Madagascar, and Japan in Burma. [source: KAR]
  - The 11th and 12th African Divisions (1940–43) combined East African, Nigerian, Gold Coast and South African troops. The 11th (East Africa) Division, formed in 1943, fought in Burma.
  - By the war's end the KAR had raised 43 battalions.
  - Many later Mau Mau-era and Malayan Emergency units descend from it.
- **West Africa (RWAFF).** The Royal West African Frontier Force formed the 81st and 82nd (West Africa) Divisions, which served in Italian Somaliland, Abyssinia and Burma. [source: WA]
  - 28 battalions were raised in Nigeria, the Gold Coast, Sierra Leone and the Gambia.
  - Only two Black officers were commissioned by the war's end.
  - 273 Polish officers served with West African units.
- **Total Africans in British service (a range):** [source: search summaries of https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/81057 ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-modern-african-studies/issue/7C0FF4BCA0C3324AEC88C0854EE94E43 ; https://www.christophersomerville.co.uk/mgtrust/afr2.htm]
  - David Killingray's *Fighting for Britain* (2010) gives "over half a million" African troops, combatant and non-combatant, in the Horn of Africa, the Middle East, Italy and Burma. About 15,000 were killed (per the THE notice).
  - Other estimates: about 470,000 (*Journal of Modern African Studies* listing); 372,000–375,000 (one popular site).
  - Write "roughly half a million".
- **French colonial troops (Tirailleurs Sénégalais).** [source: TIR]
  - Up to 200,000 were active in the war, about 9% of French forces.
  - German troops massacred captured Black soldiers repeatedly in 1940: for example, 24 May at Aubigny; 9 June around Erquinvillers, where 150–500 were executed; 19 June at Chasselay near Lyon.
  - They served in Italy and Corsica, and fought from Toulon to the Swiss border in August–November 1944.
  - Black soldiers were then withdrawn from units for the Paris victory parade and replaced by white French recruits: the "blanchiment" (whitening).
- **Thiaroye massacre,** 1 December 1944, near Dakar. Repatriated tirailleurs, former POWs of 1940, protested over unpaid wages and were shot by the French Army; 35–300 were killed. Declassified documents suggest it was planned in advance and that most or all victims were unarmed. [source: THI]
- **Caribbean volunteers:** [source: TCC; STR; search summary of https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/windrush-75/second-world-war-military-records-relating-to-the-caribbean/ and https://rafmuseum.org.uk/research/online-exhibitions/pilots-of-the-caribbean/answering-the-call/the-second-world-war-1939-to-1945-coming-home]
  - About 16,000 West Indians volunteered for British service (the National Archives estimate).
  - About 6,000 African-Caribbean men and women served in the RAF (RAF Museum; other counts go higher). Roughly 450–500 of them were aircrew.
  - The Caribbean Regiment (about 1,000 volunteers) went overseas in July 1944 and served in Italy, Egypt and Palestine. [source: TCC]
  - **Example — Billy Strachan,** from Jamaica: as a teenager he sold all his possessions to travel to Britain and join the RAF. He survived 33 bombing operations and rose to flight lieutenant, "an extremely rare achievement for a Black person in Britain during the 1940s". Many Caribbean RAF recruits were given lowly jobs and met racism on bases. [source: STR]
- **African Americans and the Double V.** The *Pittsburgh Courier*'s Double V campaign began in February 1942, from a letter by James G. Thompson: "the first V for victory over our enemies from without, the second V for victory over our enemies from within". The US armed forces were segregated until Truman's Executive Order 9981 (DV gives 28 July 1948; the exact date was not cross-checked, so write "1948"). [source: DV]

## 8. North Africa and the Mediterranean
- **The North African campaign** ran from 11 June 1940 to 13 May 1943. [source: NAC]
  - Italy invaded Egypt in September 1940 (WW2/NAC give Italy's declaration of war as 10 June 1940).
  - Operation Compass (December 1940) destroyed the Italian 10th Army.
  - The *Afrika Korps* arrived in February 1941.
  - Ultra code-breaking mattered.
  - About 250,000 German and Italian personnel surrendered in Tunisia in May 1943.
  - German and Italian forces committed atrocities against POWs and against Maghrebi Jews, Berbers and Arabs.
- **Second Battle of El Alamein,** 23 October – 4 November 1942. Montgomery's Eighth Army defeated *Panzerarmee Afrika*. [source: ALA]
  - The Eighth Army included the 4th Indian, 2nd New Zealand (with the 28th Māori Battalion), 9th Australian and 1st South African divisions.
  - Operation Torch, the Anglo-American landings in French North Africa, followed on 8 November 1942.
- **Italy.** The Allied invasion of Italy in July 1943 led to the fall of Mussolini's regime. [source: WW2]

## 9. Resistance movements
- **Yugoslav Partisans** (Tito): "Europe's most effective anti-Axis resistance movement". [source: YUG]
  - Their strength was about 650,000 in late 1944 (four armies, 52 divisions) and over 800,000 by April 1945.
  - They fought the occupiers and also the royalist Chetniks (Mihailović). The Allies switched support to Tito in late 1943.
  - With the Red Army they liberated Belgrade in autumn 1944.
  - German reprisals in Serbia shot up to 100 civilians for every German soldier killed.
  - The Antifascist Front of Women (AFŽ) claimed two million women. Use as a claim.
- **Poland — the Home Army (Armia Krajowa).** It was formed in February 1942, loyal to the London government-in-exile, and was the armed wing of the Polish Underground State. Its 1944 strength is estimated at 200,000–600,000, with "most estimates average at about 400,000". Its intelligence work covered the Holocaust (the first reports reaching the Allies) and the V-1 and V-2. Its "attitude toward Jews remains a controversial topic". [source: HA]
- **Warsaw Uprising,** 1 August – 2 October 1944 (63 days): "the single largest military effort taken by any European resistance movement". [source: WAR]
  - The Red Army halted outside the city.
  - About 16,000 insurgents were killed.
  - 150,000–200,000 civilians died, mostly in mass executions; 20,000–50,000 were killed in Wola and Ochota alone.
  - About 700,000 people were expelled and the city was destroyed.
  - Do not confuse it with the 1943 Warsaw Ghetto Uprising, which belongs in the Holocaust chapter.
- **Philippines.** [source: FIL; HUK]
  - About 260,000 people took part in the anti-Japanese resistance in some form (postwar studies); 30,000 guerrillas were organised in ten sectors by spring 1944.
  - By the war's end Japan controlled only 12 of the 48 provinces.
  - The communist-led Hukbalahap ("People's Army Against the Japanese"), formed in March 1942 by Central Luzon farmers, had about 30,000 fighters. It later fought the postwar government (the Huk rebellion, from 1946).
- **Malaya.** The Malayan Peoples' Anti-Japanese Army (MPAJA), 1941–45, was communist-led and mainly ethnic Chinese, and was the largest resistance group in Malaya. [source: MPA]
  - It numbered about 6,500 (claimed) or about 10,000 (estimated).
  - It worked with Britain's Force 136; John Davis's first party landed on 24 May 1943.
  - Many veterans formed the Malayan National Liberation Army in the Malayan Emergency (1948–60).
- **Elsewhere:** women took part in the French, Italian, Polish and Yugoslav resistance, and in the SOE and OSS. [source: WOM]

## 10. Home fronts and women
- **Scale:** "several hundred thousand women served in combat roles, especially in anti-aircraft units". [source: WOM]
- **Soviet Union.** About one million women served in the Red Army (lead); the body says more than 800,000 (about 3% of personnel), mostly as medics. About 300,000 served in anti-aircraft units. There were women combat pilots, and the sniper Lyudmila Pavlichenko was credited with 309 kills. Bob Moore: "the Soviet Union was the only major power to use women in front-line roles". [source: WOM]
- **Britain.** By late 1941 the National Service Act conscripted women aged 20–30, a first in British history. Married women were exempt. About 600,000 women served in the WRNS, the WAAF and the ATS. [source: WOM]
- **United States.** About 350,000 women served in uniform, in non-combat roles; as many as 543 died. "Rosie the Riveter" became iconic. [source: AWW; WOM]
- **Germany.** 14.6 million women were working on the eve of war (51% of working-age women). Participation fell, then recovered; by 1944 British and German women's war-work rates were "nearly equal". Cross-refer to the Hitler notes. [source: WOM]
- **Japan and Okinawa.** Japanese women were not generally formed into auxiliary units. On Okinawa, girls' school students (the Himeyuri students) were mobilised as army nurses. [source: WOM]
- **Canada:** 600,000 women held permanent jobs before the war, rising to 1.2 million by 1943. [source: WOM]
- **India:** the Women's Auxiliary Corps (India) peaked at 850 officers and 7,200 auxiliaries; Indian women trained for air-raid precautions in Bombay. [source: WOM; IND]
- **Forced labour across the Axis empires.** [source: WW2]
  - Greater Germany held 7.6 million foreign workers (including POWs) and about 500,000 concentration-camp slave labourers by late 1944.
  - Between 200,000 and one million Soviet POWs and civilians repatriated from German camps were sent to the gulag as suspected collaborators.
- **Japanese American incarceration:** about 120,000 Japanese Americans on the West Coast were incarcerated under Executive Order 9066. They received an apology and reparations under the Civil Liberties Act of 1988. [source: JAI]
- **Racial unrest in the US:** riots broke out in Los Angeles, Beaumont, Detroit and Harlem (1943). [source: DV]

## 11. Strategic bombing
- **Germany bombed first.** The Luftwaffe bombed Warsaw (1939; 2,500–7,000 killed, per Overy's estimate), Rotterdam, and Britain in the Blitz. British civilian deaths from bombing are about 60,000. [source: SB; BOB]
- **Japan bombed Chinese cities** from 1938 (Chongqing). [source: CHO]
- **The Allied turn to area bombing.** [source: SB]
  - Lindemann's 1942 "dehousing paper" was accepted by the Cabinet, and Arthur Harris took over Bomber Command.
  - Proponents argued that bombing shortened the war.
  - About 160,000 Allied airmen died in Europe.
- **Hamburg (Operation Gomorrah),** 24 July – 3 August 1943. The firestorm of 27–28 July killed an estimated 18,474 that night; an estimated 35,000–40,000 died during the whole operation (the infobox says "up to 41,000"). [source: HAM]
- **Dresden,** 13–15 February 1945: 772 RAF and 527 USAAF heavy bombers. "Up to 25,000" were killed, a figure confirmed by a 2010 city-commissioned study. The Nazi government published a falsified figure of 200,000, and inflated figures were spread by the Holocaust denier David Irving. [source: DRE]
- **German civilian deaths from bombing:** 353,000 (Overy's estimate, *The Bombing War*) to 635,000. Overy notes his figure "is a little over half the figure of 625,000 arrived at in the 1950s". Write "between about 350,000 and 600,000". [source: SB]
- **Japan.** [source: SB; AB]
  - Precision raids from June 1944 largely failed, and the US switched to firebombing.
  - SB says the firebombing destroyed 67 cities; AB says 64 cities were devastated by conventional and fire bombing.
  - As many as 500,000 died and about 5 million were made homeless. SB's infobox gives 330,000–500,000 Japanese civilians killed.
- **Tokyo, 9–10 March 1945 (Operation Meetinghouse):** 279 B-29s, under Curtis LeMay, burned out much of eastern Tokyo. 90,000–100,000+ were killed, mostly civilians, and more than one million were left homeless. It was "the most destructive single air attack" in history, deadlier than either atomic bomb. 14 US aircraft were lost. [source: TOK]

## 12. Hiroshima and Nagasaki
- **The ultimatum.** The Potsdam Declaration of 26 July 1945 (Truman, Churchill and Chiang Kai-shek) demanded unconditional surrender or "prompt and utter destruction". [source: ABD; AB]
- **The bombs.** "Little Boy" (uranium) fell on Hiroshima on 6 August and "Fat Man" (plutonium) on Nagasaki on 9 August. The B-29s flew from Tinian. Britain's consent was obtained under the Quebec Agreement. [source: AB]
- **Tolls — give ranges:** [source: AB]
  - Hiroshima: 90,000–166,000.
  - Nagasaki: 60,000–80,000.
  - Total by the end of 1945: 150,000–246,000, roughly half of them on the first day; about 90% were civilians.
  - The dead included Koreans: Nagasaki had about 10,000 Korean residents and 2,500 conscripted Korean workers, plus 600 conscripted Chinese workers and Allied POWs.
- **Truman's statement** after Hiroshima warned of "a rain of ruin from the air, the like of which has never been seen on this earth". [source: AB]
- **Hibakusha** ("explosion-affected people"). Japan has recognised about 650,000 of them; 91,105 were alive as of 31 March 2026. They faced discrimination. [source: AB]
- **Soviet entry.** The USSR declared war on Japan on 8 August and invaded Manchuria. Yalta had promised Soviet entry three months after Germany's defeat, in return for southern Sakhalin and the Kurils. [source: AB; YAL; SSJ]

## 13. The Holocaust (one paragraph only; separate chapter)
- The genocide of European Jews: around six million murdered, from 1941 to 1945, by Nazi Germany and collaborators — about two-thirds of Europe's Jews. [source: HOL]
- **Methods:** mass shootings in Eastern Europe (1.5–2 million Jews after June 1941) and gas chambers at Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka, Bełżec, Sobibor, Chełmno and Majdanek. [source: HOL]
- **Wannsee Conference,** 20 January 1942, chaired by Heydrich. [source: HOL]
- **Other victims:** Roma and Soviet POWs, among others. [source: HOL]
- **Link to the war:** connect the Holocaust to the war in the East (Barbarossa). Leave detail to the Holocaust chapter.

## 14. The end of the war
- **D-Day,** 6 June 1944 (Operation Neptune / Overlord): "the largest seaborne invasion in history". [source: DD]
  - Nearly 160,000 troops landed on the first day.
  - Allied casualties were at least 10,000, with 4,414 confirmed dead; German casualties were 4,000–9,000.
- **Yalta,** 4–11 February 1945 (Roosevelt, Churchill, Stalin). Later "a subject of intense controversy" over the division of Europe. [source: YAL]
- **Germany's surrender.** Germany surrendered unconditionally on 8 May 1945 (VE Day), after the fall of Berlin. [source: WW2]
- **Potsdam,** 17 July – 2 August 1945 (Stalin, Truman, Churchill and then Attlee). [source: POT]
- **Japan's surrender.** [source: HIR; WW2]
  - On 15 August 1945 Hirohito's recorded broadcast (*Gyokuon-hōsō*) announced acceptance of the Potsdam Declaration.
  - The formal surrender was signed on 2 September 1945.
  - Fighting in China formally ended on 9 September 1945 (SSJ end date).
- **Tribunals:** the Nuremberg trials tried German leaders and the Tokyo tribunal (IMTFE) tried Japanese leaders. [source: WW2]

## 15. Consequences
- **The United Nations.** The Charter was drafted at the San Francisco Conference (from 25 April 1945), signed on 26 June 1945 and in force on 24 October 1945, with 51 original members. The five permanent members were the US, China, France, the USSR and the UK. [source: UN]
  - Its preamble aims "to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war, which twice in our lifetime has brought untold sorrow to mankind". [source: UN]
- **Superpowers and the Cold War.** The USSR and the US emerged as rival superpowers, setting the stage for the Cold War. [source: WW2]
- **Occupation and trials.** Germany, Austria, Japan and Korea were occupied. [source: WW2]
- **Korea** was divided at the 38th parallel; separate states were formed in 1948, and the Korean War followed. [source: AFT]
- **Expulsion of Germans,** 1944–50: about 12 million (West German estimate 14.6 million) moved from Eastern and Central Europe; 500,000 to 2.5 million died. [source: EXP]
- **Decolonisation.** European power waned, "triggering the decolonisation of Africa and of Asia". [source: WW2; AFT]
  - Burma, Malaya and Singapore were returned to Britain and Indochina to France.
  - Indonesia fought the Dutch for independence.
  - Overview: nearly a third of humanity was under colonial rule in 1945.
- **Marshall Plan,** 1948–52: $13 billion to Western Europe. The USSR refused it. [source: AFT]

## Debates
- **Why did the Allies win?**
  - Richard Overy (*Why the Allies Won*, 1995) rejected the idea that sheer material superiority made victory inevitable. In Kirkus's rendering (a reviewer's wording, not Overy's), the Allies won "because they turned their economic strength into effective fighting power, and turned the moral energies of their people into an effective will to win".
  - The Wilson Quarterly's summary names four dimensions: economic strength, military technology, decision-making and morality.
  - [source: https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/a/richard-overy-2/why-the-allies-won-2 ; https://www.wilsonquarterly.com/quarterly/undefined/victory-under-scrutiny (both via search summary)]
  - Counter-emphases:
    - the Soviet contribution: Roberts's "80 percent" (EAS), and Soviet industry moved to the Urals;
    - Lend-Lease: a debated share of Soviet supply (EAS);
    - China's role in tying down Japanese armies (Mitter: China as the "forgotten ally").
    [source: EAS; MIT]
- **An imperial war?** Overy (*Blood and Ruins*, 2021) argues that the Axis states fought to build empires to match Britain's and France's and were defeated by two anti-imperial powers, the USSR and the US. Some reviewers say this framing underplays ideology, and dispute the word "last". [source: https://ssi.armywarcollege.edu/SSI-Media/Recent-Publications/Display/Article/3596982/blood-and-ruins-the-last-imperial-war-19311945 (search summary)]
- **The atomic bombings.**
  - **Orthodox view:** the bombs ended the war and averted an invasion (Operation Downfall: Kyūshū in November 1945, Honshū later). Truman said in 1953 he was advised US casualties could be 250,000 to one million. Richard B. Frank rejects as a "myth" the claim that Truman's top advisers told him the bomb was unnecessary. [source: ABD]
  - **The 1946 US Strategic Bombing Survey** concluded that "even without the atomic bombing attacks, air supremacy over Japan could have exerted sufficient pressure to bring about unconditional surrender". Gentile, Hasegawa and Newman criticise Paul Nitze for going beyond the evidence. [source: ABD]
  - **Soviet entry as decisive:** Tsuyoshi Hasegawa (*Racing the Enemy*, 2005) argues that "the two atomic bombs ... alone were not decisive in inducing Japan to surrender ... The Soviet invasion was." Robert Pape argues similarly. [source: ABD]
  - **"Atomic diplomacy":** Gar Alperovitz (1965) argued that the bombs were meant mainly to intimidate the USSR. [source: ABD]
  - **Moral and legal objections:** the Indian judge Radhabinod Pal, dissenting at the Tokyo tribunal, is cited by Mark Selden as the "most trenchant contemporary critique". [source: ABD]
  - **Unresolved:** J. Samuel Walker (2005): "the controversy over the use of the bomb seems certain to continue". [source: ABD]
- **The "good war".**
  - Studs Terkel's *"The Good War"* (1984) put the phrase in quotation marks. A 1991 *American Heritage* essay said the quotation marks "dripped with irony", but that the irony cut both ways because many participants did see it as a good war. [source: TER; https://development.americanheritage.com/my-guns (search summary)]
  - Paul Fussell's *Wartime* (1989) attacked what a reviewer calls the "Disneyfication" of American memory of the war. [source: FUS; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/468508/pdf (search summary)]
  - John Bodnar's *The "Good War" in American Memory* (2010) argues the label took hold only years after 1945, and challenges the "myth" that Americans agreed on why they fought. [source: https://press.jhu.edu/books/title/10129/good-war-american-memory (search summary)]
  - Global counterpoints to a "good war" framing:
    - the Bengal famine;
    - Japanese American incarceration and the segregated US military (the Double V);
    - "blanchiment" and Thiaroye;
    - Soviet atrocities and expulsions;
    - Allied area bombing.
    [source: BEN; JAI; DV; TIR; THI; EXP; SB]
- **Numbers as politics.**
  - Nanjing: 300,000 is the Chinese official figure, against 100,000–200,000 in much scholarship and minimisation by Japanese revisionists.
  - "Comfort women": totals from under 20,000 to 410,000.
  - Soviet dead: 20 million (Soviet era), then 26.6–27 million (Russian official), against claims of over 40 million.
  - Dresden: a falsified 200,000 against a researched 25,000.
  [source: NAN; CW; SOV; DRE]
- **The INA:** collaborators or anticolonial patriots? The INA trials of 1945–46 made them nationalist heroes in India, while historians stress its dependence on Japan and its poor military record. [source: INA]
