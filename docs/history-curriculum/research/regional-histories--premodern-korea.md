# Verified facts — Premodern Korean History (researched 2026-11-11)

Node: `history/regional-histories/asia/premodern-korean-history` (section "Long Histories of Regions" → Asia).

Required coverage (seed summary, verbatim): "Korea before the modern rupture: Gojoseon and the peninsula's early states; the Three Kingdoms — Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla — and Silla's unification with Tang help (668); Goryeo — the celadon age, the Tripitaka Koreana carved against the Mongols, and the world's first metal movable type; the Joseon dynasty (1392–1897) — Neo-Confucian state-building, King Sejong and the invention of hangul (1443), the yangban aristocracy; the Imjin War — Hideyoshi's invasions and Admiral Yi Sun-sin's turtle ships (1592–98); the Manchu invasions and tributary ties to Qing China; the 'hermit kingdom' and its opening in 1876 — with the deep patterns that still shape both Koreas."

**Scope note for the writer.** End around 1876. The years 1876–1910 (Gapsin coup, Donghak revolution of 1894, Sino-Japanese War, Korean Empire, annexation) are told in *The Meiji Restoration*, *The Taiping Rebellion and the Late Qing* and *Japan's Empire in Asia*. Give them one or two forward-pointing sentences only. Two seed phrases need care. (1) "the invention of hangul (1443)" is right for the *announcement*, but the promulgation was in 1446 and its exact day is unknown (Section 9). (2) "the world's first metal movable type": the first *recorded* metal-type book (c. 1234) does not survive. The *oldest surviving* metal-type book is the *Jikji* of 1377 (Section 7).

**Romanisation.** Sources mix Revised Romanisation (Goguryeo, Baekje, Joseon, Gwanggaeto) and McCune–Reischauer (Koguryŏ, Paekche, Chosŏn, Kwanggaet'o). Use Revised Romanisation throughout, as the seed does, and give the M–R form once in brackets for the main states.

## Conflicts with published chapters
1. **"Armoured" turtle ships (Tokugawa chapter).** TS: "There are no contemporary Korean sources from Yi Sun-sin's time that refer to the turtle ship as ironclad"; historians are split. The spiked roof is well attested. Samuel Hawley traces the "ironclad" idea to Western writers from about 1880. Say "spike-roofed, covered turtle ships" and do not call them armoured or ironclad without a caveat. [source: TS]
2. **Ganghwa 1875 casualties (Meiji chapter).** The Meiji chapter says "35 Korean soldiers were killed". GII: "The number of casualties of the incident was recorded at 35 in the Joseon Dynasty with two Japanese soldiers wounded." "Casualties" may include wounded. Minor; say "some 35 Korean casualties". [source: GII]
3. **Dates of the second invasion (Tokugawa chapter).** "In 1592 and 1597" is correct. Note that the war ended in December 1598 (Noryang, 16 December 1598), after Hideyoshi's death in September 1598. No contradiction. [source: IMJ]
4. **Treaty date.** The Meiji chapter gives only "1876". Sources differ by a day: T76 says the treaty was concluded/signed **26 February 1876**; GII says **27 February 1876**. Use "February 1876" or "26 February 1876". [source: T76; GII]
5. No other contradictions were found. The Han, Tang–Song, Mongol, Medieval Japan, Renaissance and Taiping chapters are consistent with the sources read here.

## 1. Prehistory
- Paleolithic tools from several provinces date the Korean Paleolithic to about half a million years ago (estimates range from 400,000 to 600,000–700,000 years ago). No fossil proven to be *Homo erectus* has been found on the peninsula. [source: HOK]
- The earliest known Korean pottery dates to around 8000 BC. Comb-pattern (*Jeulmun*) pottery appears after 7000 BC, at sites such as Amsa-dong in Seoul. [source: HOK]
- The Mumun pottery period (c. 1500–300 BC) saw intensive dry-field and paddy-rice agriculture (Early Mumun 1500–850 BC), the first chiefdoms (Middle Mumun, 850–550 BC) and elite burials (Late Mumun, c. 550–300 BC). [source: HOK]
- The Bronze Age is "often held to have begun around 900–800 BC" (possibly as early as 2300 BC). Iron culture was developing by the 4th century BC. [source: HOK]
- Dolmens: the peninsula has over 35,000 dolmens, "approximately 40% of the world's total". The Gochang, Hwasun and Ganghwa sites (UNESCO World Heritage, 2000) hold over 1,000. Korean dolmens date from about the 7th to the 3rd century BCE and mark elite graves. [source: DOL]
- Migration from the peninsula, bringing wet-rice farming, is linked to the Yayoi culture in Japan from around 300 BC. [source: HOK]

## 2. Gojoseon and the Dangun myth (treat as myth)
- Gojoseon (Old Chosŏn) was the first Korean polity, in the north of the peninsula and southern Manchuria. It is first mentioned in the Chinese *Guanzi* (trading with Qi). By about the 4th century BC it was known in China. Its capital later moved to the Pyongyang area (Wanggeom-seong), perhaps after losing territory to the state of Yan around 300 BCE. [source: GJS; HOK]
- Caution: Mark Byington says that although Old Chosŏn existed before the 2nd century BCE, "it is entirely unclear as to whether it was a state-level polity". Its location is debated. Since the 1980s–90s, South Korean scholarship has extended it into Manchuria/Liaoxi, a view "not widely supported by Chinese and Japanese scholars". [source: GJS]
- The Dangun (Tan'gun) myth: Hwanung, son of the Lord of Heaven Hwanin, descends to Mount Taebaek. A bear and a tiger ask to become human. Given mugwort and garlic and told to avoid sunlight for 100 days, the tiger gives up and the bear becomes a woman, Ungnyeo. Her son by Hwanung is Dangun Wanggeom, founder of Gojoseon. [source: DG]
- The earliest recorded version is in the *Samguk yusa*, compiled by the monk Iryeon around 1280 (HOK gives 1281). [source: SGY; DG; HOK]
- The traditional founding date of 2333 BC comes from the *Tongguk t'onggam* (1485), which set it in the reign of the legendary Chinese emperor Yao. The *Samguk yusa* and other texts give different years in Yao's reign. [source: DG]
- South Korea's official era (*Dangi*) counted from 2333 BC until 1961. 3 October is National Foundation Day (*Gaecheonjeol*). After "excavating" a supposed Dangun tomb in 1994, North Korea declared Dangun a real person and dates the founding to the early 30th century BC. [source: DG]
- Scholarly view: Seth: "Most textbooks and professional historians, however, treat him as a myth." Schmid: "Most [Korean historians] treat the [Tangun] myth as a later creation." [source: HOK (footnote quoting Seth 2010 and Schmid 2013)]
- Gija (Jizi), a Shang prince said to have founded "Gija Joseon" (traditionally 1122 BCE), was promoted in Joseon as the bringer of Chinese civilization. His existence is no longer mainstream. Peterson and Margulies: the Dangun myth "became more popular with groups that wanted Korea to be independent; the Kija myth was more useful to those who wanted to show that Korea had a strong affinity to China." [source: GJS; HOK]
- In 194 BCE Wiman (Wei Man), a refugee from the Han vassal state of Yan, overthrew Gojoseon's king (Wiman Joseon). In 108 BCE Han Wudi conquered it and set up four commanderies. Lelang (near Pyongyang) lasted until Goguryeo conquered it in 313 CE. [source: GJS; HOK]

## 3. The Three Kingdoms and Gaya
- Traditional founding dates (from the 12th-century *Samguk sagi*): Silla 57 BCE, Goguryeo 37 BCE, Baekje 18 BCE. They are not reliable. The Gwanggaeto Stele implies an older Goguryeo, and archaeology suggests Goguryeo predates Silla. Present them as "traditional dates". [source: GGR; SL; BJ]
- The *Samguk sagi* was completed in 1145 by Kim Busik and is the oldest surviving Korean chronicle. [source: SGS]
- Buddhism: Goguryeo received it in 372, when the monk Sundo was sent from Former Qin to King Sosurim. The Indian monk Marananta reached Baekje in 384. In Silla, the martyrdom of the court official Ichadon in 527 led to official acceptance. KB gives state adoption as Goguryeo 372, Silla 528, Baekje 552. [source: KB]
- **Goguryeo** (37 BCE (trad.)–668): Gwanggaeto (r. 391–412) and Jangsu (r. 413–491). Jangsu moved the capital to Pyongyang in 427. At its height Goguryeo ruled most of Manchuria and took the Seoul region from Baekje (475). [source: GGR; BJ]
- Gwanggaeto Stele: erected in 414 by Jangsu near Ji'an on the Yalu (now in China's Jilin province). About 6.39 m tall, inscribed in Classical Chinese with about 1,775–1,802 characters (counts differ). It records the foundation myth, campaigns, and the tomb guardians. [source: GS]
- Goguryeo–Sui War (598–614): the *Book of Sui* claims 1,133,800 combat troops were mobilized in 612 (modern estimate about 670,000). A detachment of about 305,000 marched on Pyongyang. At the Battle of Salsu (612) Eulji Mundeok's forces routed them, and "it is said that only 2,700 escaped". The war was "one of the pivotal factors in the collapse" of Sui (618). Treat the troop figures as the chronicles' own numbers. [source: GSW; GGR]
- Yeon Gaesomun seized power in a coup in 642. Tang Taizong's invasion of 645 failed. After Yeon's death in 666 his sons fought each other; one defected to Tang. Tang–Silla forces took Pyongyang in 668. [source: GGR; HOK]
- **Baekje** (18 BCE (trad.)–660): capitals at Wirye (Seoul area, to 475), Ungjin (Gongju, 475–538) and Sabi (Buyeo, 538–660). At its 4th-century peak under Geunchogo it defeated Goguryeo at Pyongyang (371). [source: BJ]
- Baekje transmitted Chinese writing, Buddhism, pottery and other culture to Yamato Japan. The *Nihon shoki* dates King Seong's gift of a Buddha image and sutras to Emperor Kinmei to 552; other sources give 538; "both dates are thought to be unreliable". [source: BJ; BIJ]
- Baekje fell in 660 to Silla and Tang (General Gyebaek was defeated at Hwangsanbeol). Restoration forces brought Prince Buyeo Pung back from Japan. At the Battle of Baekgang (Hakusukinoe), 27–28 August 663 lunar (4–5 October 663), the Tang–Silla side defeated a Yamato fleet. Wikipedia lists Yamato losses as 400 ships and 10,000 troops. [source: BJ; BGK]
- **Silla** (57 BCE (trad.)–935), capital Gyeongju. The bone-rank (*golpum*) system set office, dress, house size and marriage by birth ("sacred bone", "true bone"). [source: SL]
- Silla had three reigning queens. Seondeok (r. 632–647) was the first. Silla traced descent through both parents, "suggesting women enjoyed a relatively high status compared to other contemporary East Asian states." Cheomseongdae, an observatory, dates from Seondeok's reign. [source: SL; QSD]
- Hwarang: elite aristocratic youth groups established under King Jinheung (540–576), which became a source of military leaders. [source: SL]
- **Gaya**: a confederacy in the Nakdong valley, traditionally AD 42–532/562, known for iron. Geumgwan Gaya (Gimhae) fell to Silla in 532 and Daegaya (Goryeong) in 562. General Kim Yu-sin was a descendant of the last king of Geumgwan Gaya. [source: GAYA]
- Unification: Silla and Tang took Baekje in 660 and Goguryeo in 668. In the Silla–Tang War (670–676) Silla expelled Tang from the land south of the Taedong River; the naval victory at Gibeolpo came in November 676. [source: STW; SL]
- Population estimates cited by SL (unattributed figures, use with caution): Baekje about 3.8 million, Goguryeo about 3.5 million, Silla about 850,000. [source: SL]

## 4. Unified Silla and Balhae ("North–South States")
- Unified Silla (668/676–935). Bulguksa was begun in 751 under King Gyeongdeok, by the chief minister Kim Daeseong. The Seokguram grotto was begun in 751 and completed in 774. Both became UNESCO World Heritage sites in 1995. [source: BGS; SKG]
- A printed dharani sutra found at Bulguksa in 1966 dates to between 704 and 751 and is among the world's earliest woodblock prints. [source: BGS; USL]
- Silla monks such as Wonhyo and Uisang were influential. Silla communities lived in Shandong. The maritime magnate Jang Bogo dominated 9th-century East Asian sea trade. [source: HOK; USL]
- Late Silla fragmented into the Later Three Kingdoms (892–936): Later Baekje (Gyeon Hwon, 892), Later Goguryeo/Taebong (Gungye, 901), and Silla. [source: HOK]
- **Balhae** (Parhae/Bohai), 698–926, founded by Dae Joyeong (Da Zuorong) and named "Jin" until 713. It ruled much of Manchuria, northern Korea and Primorsky Krai. Tang recognized it as a kingdom in 762. It sent envoys to Japan 34 times. The Khitan Liao conquered it in 926. [source: BH]
- Goryeo took in a large Balhae influx, "between 100,000 and 200,000" refugees according to HOK, including the crown prince Dae Gwang-hyeon. [source: HOK; BH]
- Balhae's ethnic make-up is disputed (see Debates). Korean scholarship holds that a Goguryeo-descended elite ruled Mohe subjects. Chinese scholarship treats the Mohe as the majority and Balhae as a Tang local regime. Russian scholars say the evidence cannot settle it. In a mission to Japan in 727/728 the Balhae envoy said Balhae had "recovered the lost land of Goguryeo and inherited the old traditions of Buyeo". [source: BH]
- Balhae entered Korean histories late. Yi Seung-hyu's *Jewang ungi* (1287) included it, and the Joseon scholar Yu Deuk-gong coined the term "North and South States Period" in the 18th century. [source: HOK]

## 5. Goryeo (918–1392): state and society
- Wang Geon (Taejo) founded Goryeo in 918 and unified the peninsula by 936. Silla's last king surrendered in 935. The name "Goryeo" (from Goguryeo) is the source of "Korea". The capital was Gaegyeong (Kaesong). [source: GRY; HOK]
- Taejo married 29 women from powerful regional (*hojok*) families. [source: GRY]
- Civil service examinations (*gwageo*) were introduced in 958 under Gwangjong on the advice of Shuang Ji, a Hanlin scholar from Later Zhou. They lasted until 1894. [source: GWG]
- Khitan Liao wars 993–1019: in 1018–19 Gang Gam-chan defeated a Liao army of 100,000 at Kwiju. Goryeo then accepted nominal tributary ties to Liao. [source: GRY; HOK]
- Henry Em: until 1270 Goryeo rulers held a "pluralist" outlook, claiming "a ritual status reserved for the Son of Heaven" while recognizing empires in China and Manchuria. Song, Liao and Jin claims that Goryeo was a vassal were largely fiction. [source: GRY]
- Military regime 1170–1270: officers' coup of 1170; the Choe family ruled from 1196 (Choe Chung-heon). Yi Ui-min, a ruler of *nobi* (slave) background, held power before him (from 1183). [source: GRY]
- A revolt led by Myocheong in 1135 aimed to move the capital to Pyongyang; Kim Busik put it down. [source: GRY]

## 6. Goryeo culture: Buddhism, celadon, the Tripitaka
- Buddhism was the state religion and flourished. [source: GRY]
- Celadon: techniques came from China. Goryeo's own style was established by the 12th century, which was the "zenith". Goryeo invented *sanggam* inlay, "a way of inlaying that was unique to Goryeo celadon". The jade-green colour was called *bisaek*. Quality declined after the Mongol invasions. Many wares came from the Gangjin kilns. [source: GW]
- Tripitaka Koreana: the first edition was begun in 1011 during the Khitan wars and completed in 1087. It was destroyed by fire in the Mongol invasion of 1232. Recarving began in 1237 and took 12 years, sponsored by Choe U and Choe Hang. In 1398 the blocks were moved to Haeinsa. [source: TK]
- Size: 81,352 blocks (TK lead); TK also gives "81,258 pages". HOK and many sources say "81,258 blocks". Say "more than 81,000 woodblocks". The canon holds 1,496 titles and about 52 million characters. It is the oldest intact version of the Chinese Buddhist canon. UNESCO Memory of the World, 2007. [source: TK; HOK]
- Robert Buswell compared the project's national commitment to the Apollo programme and proposed calling it the "Korean Buddhist Canon". [source: TK]
- It is a misconception that the Tripitaka Koreana contains no errors; a survey found missing characters and errors. [source: TK]

## 7. Metal movable type, the Jikji and the Mongol period
- Recorded earliest metal type: the *Sangjeong gogeum yemun* (ritual texts) was printed with metal type in about 1234 (some sources say 1234–1241), according to Yi Gyu-bo's writings, under Choe U. No copy survives. Copy counts differ (28 or 50). [source: GRY; WebSearch results: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%27oe_Yun%C5%ADi ; https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/689336-first-use-of-metal-moveable-type-printing]
- *Jikji* (*Baegun hwasang chorok buljo jikji simche yojeol*): compiled by the monk Baegun (1298–1374), printed with metal type at Heungdeok Temple, Cheongju, in July 1377 (per its colophon). It is the oldest extant book printed with movable metal type, "78 years before" Gutenberg's 42-line Bible (1452–55). [source: JIK]
- Only the second volume of the metal edition survives, held by the Bibliothèque nationale de France. It became known through Maurice Courant's *Bibliographie coréenne* (1901). The librarian Park Byeong-seon drew attention to it in 1972. UNESCO Memory of the World, 2001. The temple site was confirmed by excavation in 1985. [source: JIK]
- Mongol invasions began in 1231. The court moved to Ganghwa Island in 1232. In 1232 the monk Kim Yun-hu killed the Mongol commander Sartai with an arrow at Cheoin. Six major campaigns took place (1231, 1232, 1235, 1238, 1247, 1253), followed by further invasions to 1258–59. Peace came in 1259. [source: GRY; MIK]
- Toll: "An estimated 206,800 captives had been taken by the Mongols" in the 1254 campaign alone. The Hwangnyongsa pagoda and the first Tripitaka were destroyed. [source: MIK]
- The Sambyeolcho resisted the peace from the southern islands from 1270 to 1273. After 1270 Goryeo became a "son-in-law" vassal of the Yuan for about 80 years. Its kings married Mongol princesses, and Empress Gi, the last Yuan empress, was Korean. [source: GRY; MIK]
- Goryeo was made to support Khubilai's invasions of Japan. For 1274, Kim Bang-gyeong led a force of 30,500 to build ships, and the invasion force included "6,000 to 8,000 Korean troops, and 7,000 Korean sailors". [source: MIJ]
- Gongmin (r. 1351–74) pushed back Yuan influence and regained the northern territories by 1356. Goryeo repulsed Red Turban invasions in 1359 and 1360 and fought *wokou* pirates, using naval guns developed by Choe Mu-seon in the 1370s–80s. [source: GRY; HOK]
- In 1388 Yi Seong-gye, ordered to attack Ming Liaodong, turned back his army from Wihwa Island (the "Wihwado retreat") and seized power. He founded Joseon in 1392. [source: WHD; GRY]

## 8. Joseon (1392–1897): the Neo-Confucian state
- Joseon was founded by Yi Seong-gye (Taejo) in 1392 and lasted until the proclamation of the Korean Empire in 1897 (505 years). Its capital was Hanseong (Hanyang, modern Seoul) from 1394, with Gyeongbokgung palace. Its tributary relations with the Ming and then the Qing ran from 1401 to 1895. [source: JOS; HOK]
- Neo-Confucianism was the state ideology. "Buddhism was accordingly discouraged, and occasionally Buddhists faced persecution." Jeong Do-jeon was the chief ideologue of the founding. [source: JOS]
- Taejong (Yi Bang-won) seized power through fratricidal struggles (1398–1400), abolished private armies and strengthened the crown. [source: JOS]
- The great code *Gyeongguk daejeon* was compiled under Sejo and Seongjong. The final version (*Eulsa daejeon*) was issued in 1485. [source: GD; JOS]
- Examinations: the literary *gwageo* was the most prestigious route. Thirty-three candidates reached the final exam before the king. Yangban status was lost if a family produced no official for three generations (per JOS). [source: JOS]
- Yangban means "two branches": civil (*munban*) and military (*muban*) officials. The social order ran from yangban through *jungin* (technical specialists: interpreters, physicians, scribes) and *sangmin* (commoners, the largest group) to *cheonmin* (the "base": *nobi*, butchers/*baekjeong*, entertainers, shamans). [source: YB; HOK; JOS]
- The literati purges of 1498, 1504, 1519 and 1545 pitted the *sarim* (rural literati) against the merit elite. The reformer Jo Gwang-jo was executed in the purge of 1519. [source: LP; JOS]
- Yi Hwang (Toegye, 1501–1570) and Yi I (Yulgok, 1536–1584) were the greatest Joseon Neo-Confucian philosophers. Toegye appears on the 1,000-won note. [source: YH; YI]
- Private academies (*seowon*) multiplied. The Daewongun taxed and closed many in the 1860s–70s. [source: HOK]
- Famines: the Gyeongsin famine of 1670–71 and the Eulbyeong famine of 1695–96 "may have killed 23–33% of Korea's population" (scholars' estimate, as cited in JOS). [source: JOS]
- Joseon population figures "are disputed as government records of households are considered unreliable". [source: JOS]
- The Dutchman Hendrick Hamel was shipwrecked on Jeju in 1653 and escaped in 1666. His account was one of Europe's few early sources on Korea. [source: HAM; JOS]

## 9. Sejong and Hangul
- Sejong (Yi Do), born 15 May 1397, reigned 1418–1450 and died 8 April 1450. In 1420 he re-established the Hall of Worthies (*Jiphyeonjeon*). In 1419 he launched the Ōei (Gihae) expedition against Tsushima. He pushed the northern border to roughly its current line against the Jurchens. [source: SJ]
- Science and learning under Sejong: the agricultural treatise *Nongsa jikseol* (1429); a 1432 solar eclipse that came two days off prediction spurred new instruments, including an armillary sphere (1433); the medical compendium *Hyangyak jipseongbang* (1433); new metal types (1420); the rain gauge (*cheugugi*), credited to Jang Yeong-sil. [source: SJ; JYS]
- Sejong granted female state *nobi* 100 days' maternity leave in 1426, extended in 1430. In 1434 he gave their husbands 30 days. [source: NOBI]
- **Hangul: exact dates.** Hangul was announced to the court in the 12th month of 1443 by the Korean calendar (c. 30 December 1443 – 28 January 1444 Gregorian). This is the source of the seed's "1443". It was promulgated in the **9th month of 1446** through the *Hunminjeongeum* and its commentary the *Hunminjeongeum Haerye*. "The specific day of publication is not known." [source: HG; HGD]
- The *Haerye*'s postface is dated to the first ten days (*sanghan*) of the 9th month of 1446. The *Haerye* was rediscovered only in 1940. South Korea's **Hangul Day, 9 October**, was fixed after 1945 as the Gregorian equivalent of the 10th day of that month. HGD: "October 9 is only a guess". North Korea marks 15 January, apparently for the 1443 announcement. The holiday was created in 1926 by the Korean Language Society. [source: HGD]
- Elite opposition: critics argued that a native script departed from Chinese civilization. Modern historians add elitism and self-interest as motives. [source: HG]
- Hangul spread slowly. In the 15th century it was used mainly at court; women and Buddhists were significant early adopters. Yeonsangun persecuted its use in 1504 after criticisms of him were posted in hangul. On 21 November 1894 the government made hangul the preferred script for official documents. [source: HG]
- The *Hunminjeongeum Haerye* was made a National Treasure in 1962 and inscribed in UNESCO Memory of the World in 1997. [source: HG]

## 10. Slavery (nobi) and its scale
- *Nobi* were divided into state-owned (*gongnobi*) and privately owned (*sanobi*). The status was hereditary and could also be imposed as punishment for crime or debt. Some commoners entered it voluntarily in famine. Non-resident *nobi* farmed separately, kept houses and families, and far outnumbered household *nobi*. [source: NOBI; SIK]
- They could be bought, sold, inherited and given away. NOBI says that if a *nobi* died from a beating, the owner was not legally responsible. *Nobi* could buy their freedom, earn it by military service, or receive it from the state. [source: NOBI]
- Scale (attributed ranges): "at least 30 percent of the population between the 15th and 17th centuries", possibly 40 per cent in the south. In a 1663 Seoul register, 1,729 of 2,374 people (73 per cent) were *nobi*. Another estimate cited in JOS and SIK: "up to about one-third … but on average … about 10%". Before Joseon, slaves/serfs were "no more than 10 percent". [source: SIK; JOS]
- Decline: measures in 1731, 1744, 1783 and 1801. Most government *nobi* were freed in 1801. *Nobi* fell to about 1.5 per cent of the population by 1858. Hereditary status was abolished in 1886–87, and the whole system in the Gabo Reform of 1894. Vestiges lasted into the 20th century. [source: SIK; JOS]
- Imjin War captives taken to Japan: "figures ranging from tens of thousands to over 100,000" (De Sousa 2019); IMJ gives 50,000–60,000. Many were resold through Portuguese traders. [source: SIK; IMJ]

## 11. Women under Neo-Confucianism
- In Goryeo, women "could freely mingle with men, have their own possessions, and inherit land". Their position worsened over Joseon, especially after the Imjin War, as patrilineal clans and printed genealogies (*jokbo*, from c. 1600) spread. [source: WIJ]
- Daughters gradually lost inheritance rights, households headed by women disappeared, and men could divorce wives for the "seven evils". Women could not divorce. [source: WIJ]
- Remarriage: a 1477 measure under Seongjong did not ban remarriage itself. It barred the sons (and grandsons) of remarried women from the examinations and office. The 1485 *Gyeongguk daejeon* included it. Lower-class remarriage continued. [source: YN; WIJ; WebSearch: https://edspace.american.edu/justinjacobs/wp-content/uploads/sites/986/2022/12/16-Prohibition-Against-Remarriage-of-Women.pdf]
- The state rewarded "virtuous women" (chaste widows, *yeolnyeo*) from 1434. Yangban women were segregated: they left home by covered litter, and houses had separate men's and women's quarters. [source: WIJ]
- Exceptions and voices: women were early users of hangul. Lady Hyegyeong (1735–1816), wife of Crown Prince Sado, wrote the *Hanjungnok* (*Memoirs of Lady Hyegyeong*), translated by JaHyun Kim Haboush (1996). The Neo-Confucian scholar Im Yunjidang (1721–1793) and the poet Hwang Jini (16th century) are notable. [source: WIJ; LH; JKH]

## 12. The Imjin War (1592–98)
- Dates: 23 May 1592 (landing at Busan; 13th day, 4th month lunar) to 16 December 1598 (Gregorian). Korean names: *Imjin waeran* (1592–93) and *Jeongyu jaeran* (1597–98). In China it is the "Wanli Korean campaign". [source: IMJ]
- Hideyoshi's aim was to conquer Ming China through Korea; he told the Jesuit Gaspar Coelho in 1585 of a wish to conquer East Asia. He never left Japan. Before the war, Korean envoys returned with conflicting reports; Kim Seong-il called Hideyoshi's threat a bluff. [source: IMJ; HOK]
- Japanese forces: 158,800 in 1592 (including labourers and sailors) and 141,900 in 1597, "c. 300,000" in total. The Ming sent 166,700 men in total but probably never more than 80,000 at once, plus 17 million *liang* of silver. Arquebuses (*tanegashima*) gave Japan the edge on land; Korean cannon-armed ships gave Korea the edge at sea. [source: IMJ]
- Course: Busan and Dongnae fell at once. The capital Hanseong was taken on 10 June 1592; King Seonjo had fled the day before, and parts of the city, including slave registers, were burned. Pyongyang was taken on 20 July 1592. [source: IMJ]
- Yi Sun-sin's fleet cut Japanese sea supply. At Hansan Island (August 1592) the Japanese lost 59 ships and the Koreans none. [source: IMJ]
- Righteous armies (*uibyeong*), led by men such as Gwak Jae-u, and warrior monks under Hyujeong fought behind the lines. The first siege of Jinju (1592) failed. At the second (1593) the city fell and almost all defenders were killed. Gwon Yul won at Haengju (1593). Ming forces under Li Rusong retook Pyongyang (1593). [source: IMJ]
- After the second invasion of 1597, Wŏn Gyun's fleet was destroyed at Chilcheollyang, "Japan's only decisive naval victory of the war". At Myeongnyang (26 October 1597) Yi, with 13 ships, defeated about 133 Japanese warships. The "oft-cited number of 333 ships" counts support vessels. [source: IMJ]
- Hideyoshi died in 1598 and the Council of Five Elders ordered withdrawal. At Noryang (16 December 1598) Yi was killed by a bullet. [source: IMJ; YSS]
- Yi Sun-sin: 28 April 1545 – 16 December 1598. "Generally accepted that he fought in at least 23 naval battles, achieving victory in all." His war diary, the *Nanjung ilgi*, is in UNESCO Memory of the World. [source: YSS]
- Turtle ships (*geobukseon*) are first mentioned in 1413 and 1415. Yi revived them as covered, spike-roofed ships. "Ironclad" is disputed (see Conflicts). [source: TS]
- Losses (attributed): George H. Jones estimated about one million total military and civilian casualties. Combat dead are given as over 100,000 Japanese, 185,000 Korean and over 29,000 Chinese troops. Arable land fell to 66 per cent of the prewar total. The palaces of Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung and Changgyeonggung were burned, and so was Bulguksa (1597). Of 50,000–60,000 captives, 7,500 were returned. [source: IMJ]
- The Mimizuka ("Ear Mound", originally "Nose Mound") in Kyoto was dedicated on 28 September 1597. It holds the noses of "at least 38,000 Koreans and over 30,000 Chinese". [source: MZ]
- Hideyoshi's order for the second invasion, as quoted: "Mow down everyone universally, without discriminating between young and old, men and women, clergy and the laity… and send the heads to Japan." [source: IMJ]
- Captured Korean potters founded Japanese kilns; Arita porcelain began in 1616 with Korean potters. Captive Korean scholars helped bring Neo-Confucianism to Japan. [source: IMJ]
- Relations were restored through Tsushima. Joseon sent an emissary (IMJ: 1608; see NOT VERIFIED on the exact year), and prisoners were returned and trade restored. Embassies (*tongsinsa*) later went to Edo (see the Tokugawa chapter). [source: IMJ]

## 13. Manchu invasions and the Qing tributary order
- The Later Jin invasion of 1627 (Jeongmyo) involved 30,000 men under Amin. It ended with Joseon accepting "brotherly relations". The background was the 1623 coup by the Westerners, which deposed Gwanghaegun and installed the pro-Ming Injo. [source: LJI; JOS]
- The Qing invasion (Byeongja horan) ran from 9 December 1636 to 30 January 1637. Hong Taiji led about 120,000 men. King Injo held out in Namhansanseong with 13,800 men. After Ganghwa fell (27 January 1637) he surrendered. At Samjeondo he knelt three times and bowed nine times before Hong Taiji. [source: QIJ; JOS]
- Terms: break with the Ming and hand over the Ming seals; send two princes as hostages (Crown Prince Sohyeon and the future Hyojong); adopt the Qing calendar. A stele praising the Qing emperor's "virtues" was erected at Samjeondo. [source: QIJ]
- Women abducted by Qing forces who returned (*hwanhyang-nyeo*) were often rejected by their families. The government refused divorce petitions and said such women should not be considered disgraced. [source: QIJ]
- Joseon's elite kept their loyalty to the fallen Ming and saw Joseon as the true heir of civilization, "Little China" (*sojunghwa*). The court and literati kept using Ming reign names long afterwards. [source: JOS]
- About 200 years of external peace followed. Yeongjo (r. 1724–76) and Jeongjo (r. 1776–1800) led a revival. Jeongjo founded the Kyujanggak library in 1776. [source: HOK]
- Hwaseong Fortress at Suwon was built 1794–96 by Jeongjo to honour his father, Crown Prince Sado, who was put to death by his own father Yeongjo in 1762. Jeong Yak-yong designed it. UNESCO, 1997. [source: HWS; JYY]

## 14. Silhak, Catholicism, Donghak
- *Silhak* ("practical learning") was a reform current of the late 17th to early 19th centuries. Most of its scholars came from factions excluded from power. They urged land reform, technology, study of Korea's own past, and openness to foreign knowledge. Key figures: Yu Hyeong-won (1622–1673, "public land system"), Yi Ik (1681–1764), Pak Ji-won (1737–1805). [source: SH]
- Jeong Yak-yong (Dasan, 1762–1836) was exiled 1801–1818 to Gangjin because his family was linked to Catholicism. He is said to have written 500 volumes, including *Mongmin simseo* (on the art of governing), published after his return from exile. [source: JYY]
- Catholicism arrived through Chinese-language Jesuit books read by scholars of the Southerner (*Namin*) faction. Yi Seung-hun was baptized in Beijing in 1784. The early church was led by lay aristocrats. [source: JYY; KM; SP]
- Persecutions came in 1791, 1801 (Sinyu, ordered by the regent Queen Jeongsun from 8 April 1801), 1839, 1846 and 1866. "Between 8,000–10,000 Korean Christians were killed" (KM elsewhere says "more than 10,000"). Andrew Kim Taegon, the first Korean priest, was executed in 1846 at 26. In 1984 John Paul II canonized 103 martyrs in Seoul. [source: KM; SP]
- Donghak ("Eastern Learning") was founded by Choe Je-u (18 December 1824 – 15 April 1864) after a revelation on 25 May 1860. It drew on shamanism, Daoism, Buddhism and Neo-Confucianism and was set against Catholic *Seohak* ("Western Learning"). Choe was beheaded in Daegu in 1864. Choe Si-hyeong led the movement afterwards (executed 1898). It was renamed Cheondogyo in 1905. [source: CJ]
- Forward note: the Donghak Peasant Revolution (11 January 1894 – 25 December 1895, led by Jeon Bong-jun) triggered the First Sino-Japanese War. It belongs to the next chapter. [source: DPR]

## 15. The "hermit kingdom" and the opening of 1876
- The Andong Kim in-law "*sedo* politics" (from 1800) brought corruption in land tax, military service and the grain loan system, and frequent revolts. [source: HOK; JOS]
- Heungseon Daewongun (Yi Ha-eung, 1821–1898; HOK gives 1820), regent for Gojong from 1864. He rebuilt Gyeongbokgung with heavy levies, cut back the *seowon* and enforced isolation. Bruce Cumings sums up his foreign policy: "no treaties, no trade, no Catholics, no West, and no Japan". He fell from power in 1873. [source: HDW; HOK]
- 1866: the great persecution (Byeongin) killed Korean converts and nine French priests (the 8,000–10,000 figure in Section 14 covers all persecutions 1791–1866, not 1866 alone). On 11 October–22 November 1866 a French squadron under Rear Admiral Roze occupied Ganghwa and then withdrew. Roze: "Since [the kingdom of] Choson killed nine French priests, we shall avenge by killing 9,000 Koreans." The French seized books from the royal archive on Ganghwa. [source: FCK]
- 1866: the American armed merchant ship *General Sherman* ran aground near Pyongyang. It was burned and its crew killed. [source: HOK]
- 1871 (*Sinmiyangyo*): a US expedition (Rear Admiral John Rodgers, Minister Frederick Low) attacked the Ganghwa forts. US casualties: 3 killed. Korean dead: 243 (American estimate) to about 350 (Korean estimate). [source: USK]
- "Hermit kingdom" comes from William Elliot Griffis, *Corea: The Hermit Nation* (1882), a Western label applied to an isolation that was largely a 19th-century policy. [source: HK]
- 20 September 1875: the Japanese gunboat *Un'yō* surveyed without permission and clashed with Korean forts near Ganghwa (35 Korean casualties). [source: GII]
- Japan–Korea Treaty of 1876 (Ganghwa Treaty), 26 February 1876. Article 1 called Korea "an independent state enjoying the same sovereign rights as does Japan", undermining Qing suzerainty. It opened Busan and two more ports (later Wonsan and Incheon), gave Japanese extraterritoriality, and allowed free survey of Korean coasts. HOK calls it the first formal treaty between the two countries since the Treaty of Gyehae (1443). [source: T76; HOK]

## Debates
- **Nationalist historiography (Sin Chae-ho).** Sin Chae-ho (8 December 1880 – 21 February 1936) founded *minjok* (nation/ethnos) historiography with *Doksa sillon* (*A New Reading of History*, 1908) and *Joseon sanggosa* (*Early History of Joseon*, published 1931). He made Dangun the founder of the Korean *minjok*, claimed Manchuria as ancestral Korean land, and rejected a "peninsular" self-image. He became an anarchist and died in Lüshun prison. Critics such as Hyung Il Pai see Dangun studies as reflecting "the progressively ultra-nationalistic trend in Korean historical and archaeological scholarship". [source: SCH; GJS]
- **Colonial "stagnation" (*teitairon*) thesis.** The Japanese economist Fukuda Tokuzō, in a 1904 essay on Korea's economic organization (using Karl Bücher's stage theory), placed Korea far behind in development and used this openly to argue for Japanese domination. Owen Miller argues that the politics of "stagnation" were more complicated than the nationalist rebuttal allows, since Marxists and postwar scholars also used stage frameworks. [source: WebSearch: https://pan-asian.blogspot.com/2019/04/owen-miller-idea-of-stagnation-in.html ; https://www.scribd.com/document/463383166/15-Owen-Miller]
- **Colonial "heteronomy" (*taritsusei*) thesis.** This was the claim that Korean history was driven by outside powers (China, the northern peoples, Japan) rather than by its own development. Stella Xu notes that Lelang drew "exceptional attention … by Japanese colonial scholars for making claims of the innate heteronomy of Koreans". Hyung Il Pai notes that the colonial finds at Lelang "seemed to verify Japanese colonial theories concerning the dependency of Korean civilization on China". Sang Mee Oh shows these "stagnancy and heteronomy" themes passed into postwar American writing on Korea. The Government-General's Korean History Compilation Committee was set up in June 1925. [source: HOK footnotes (Xu 2007; Pai 2000); KHCC; https://escholarship.org/content/qt3cd6x8pv/qt3cd6x8pv.pdf]
- **Rejection of these theses.** Paek Nam-un (1894–1979) applied Marxist stage theory in *Korea Social Economic History* (1933) and *Korea Feudal Society* (1937) to show that Korea followed universal development, against colonial theories. After 1945 the "internal development" school and the "sprouts of capitalism" thesis argued that commercialization and proto-industry grew after the 17th century until Japanese interference. That thesis is itself now debated. [source: PNU; SOC]
- **Goguryeo/Balhae and the Northeast Project.** The Chinese Academy of Social Sciences' Northeast Project ran 2002–2007 (approved 2001). It treated Gojoseon, Buyeo, Goguryeo and Balhae as regional or minority regimes of China, based on the "unified multi-ethnic state" idea. It caused outrage in South Korea in 2004. A 2004 diplomatic compromise kept Goguryeo claims out of Chinese textbooks. UNESCO listed Goguryeo sites in both China and North Korea in 2004. Premodern China itself regarded Goguryeo as Korean. [source: NEP; GGR]
- **Balhae's ethnicity.** Korean scholars: a Goguryeo elite ruled Mohe subjects. Chinese scholars: a Mohe-majority Tang local regime. Russian scholars: neither can be shown. Alexander Kim and Min Kyounghyoun: positions rest on "intrinsically subjective positions". [source: BH]
- **The Gwanggaeto Stele and Mimana/Imna.** The "sinmyo" (391) passage was read by Japanese army-sponsored scholars (copy obtained by Sakō Kageaki in 1883, published 1889) to say that Wa (Japan) subjugated Baekje and Silla. Korean scholars read Goguryeo as the subject. In 1972 the Zainichi scholar Lee Jin-hui accused the Japanese army of altering the stele with lime. The Chinese scholars Wang Jianqun and Xu Jianxin found no evidence of deliberate falsification (the lime was added by rubbing-makers), and Xu found a rubbing made before 1881. Suematsu Yasukazu's 1949 thesis of a Japanese colony "Mimana" (3rd–6th centuries) has lost support since the 1970s for lack of archaeological evidence. A Japanese colonial ruling office (*Mimana Nihonfu*) "is denied by historical academia in both Korea and Japan". Alternative readings include a Gaya trading or diplomatic office (Han Yong-u) and Peninsular Japonic speakers (Vovin, Janhunen). [source: GS; MIM]
- **Were nobi slaves, and how many?** James Palais, in a 1995 *Harvard Journal of Asiatic Studies* article and in *Views on Korean Social History* ("Definition of Slave Society, the 30 Percent Factor"), called Joseon a "slave society" because *nobi* were roughly 30 per cent or more of the population. Rhee Young-hoon (Yi Yŏnghun) and Yang Donghyu compared *nobi* with American slavery. Yi argued that most non-household *nobi* were closer to serfs. A 2010 Seoul National University critique says Palais lacked empirical support and that 18th-century *nobi* were effectively tenants. Note that "the Korean word for an actual slave … is *noye*, not *nobi*". Range to cite: about 10 per cent on average to 30–40 per cent at the peak, in the south and in Seoul. [source: NOBI; SIK; JOS; WebSearch: https://s-space.snu.ac.kr/handle/10371/73146 ; https://www.hanbooks.com/viewonkorsoc.html]
- **Women's status under Neo-Confucianism.** Deuchler (1992): Goryeo kinship was bilateral, women inherited equally and could remarry; Neo-Confucian patrilineal reforms from the 15th–17th centuries excluded daughters from inheritance, ended uxorilocal marriage and stigmatized remarriage. Critics (Choi Jae-seok, 2005) argue that patrilineal elements were older (from Silla) and that her model is teleological. Others stress women's agency through hangul writing and household management (see Haboush on Lady Hyegyeong). [source: MD; WIJ; WebSearch: https://muse.jhu.edu/journals/korean_studies/summary/v018/18.duncan01.html ; https://accesson.kr/rks/assets/pdf/7556/journal-8-4-209.pdf]
- **Was Goryeo a tributary vassal?** Henry Em and Remco Breuker argue that Goryeo (to 1270) was a pluralist, quasi-imperial state equal in ritual to Song, Liao and Jin, not a mere vassal. [source: GRY]
- **"Hermit kingdom".** The term is Griffis's 1882 label. Joseon in fact kept tribute missions to Beijing and embassies and trade with Japan via Tsushima. The strict exclusion of the West was a 19th-century policy, especially the Daewongun's in 1864–73. [source: HK; HDW; IMJ]

## NOT VERIFIED — do not use
- Exact day of Hangul's promulgation (e.g. "9 October 1446"). The sources state the day is unknown; 9 October is a modern reconstruction. Say "the ninth month of 1446".
- Tōgō Heihachirō's quote "It may be proper to compare me with Nelson, but not with Korea's Yi Sun-sin". It is quoted in Hawley (p. 490) and widely, but wording and setting vary and no primary source was found.
- "Gyeongju was the fourth-largest city in the world" and the figure of 178,936 households. Asserted in HOK/USL without solid sourcing.
- The number of state *nobi* freed in 1801 (often given as about 66,000). Not found in the sources read.
- That the 2010 Japan–Korea Joint History Research Committee formally rejected "Mimana Nihonfu". Seen only in secondary or mirror summaries; the report itself was not read.
- Captives taken in the 1636–37 Qing invasion (figures of 50,000 or 500,000 circulate). Not found in sources read.
- Whether Korean envoys first returned in 1607 or 1608 (IMJ says an emissary was sent "in 1608"; other accounts say 1607). Say "by 1607–08".
- Baekje "the Phoenicia of East Asia" and Silla's "unique gold metalwork… Persian influence". Stated in HOK, but these are colourful generalizations; avoid.
- Precise Imjin War total dead "over 1 million". This is George H. Jones's estimate, cited in IMJ; use only as an attributed estimate.
- Exact Joseon population totals. Sources say household registers are unreliable.
