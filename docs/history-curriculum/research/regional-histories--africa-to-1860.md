## 1. Sources and method
- Griots are called *jèli* (northern Mande) or *jali* (southern Mande), *guewel* in Wolof, *gawlo* in Pulaar; children of jeli families are trained from early age in verbal art, music and genealogy. Balla Fasséké (Sunjata's griot in the epic) is considered the founder of the Kouyaté griot line, which still exists. The "caste" label for griots and other *nyamakala* (artisan specialists) is increasingly criticised as a distortion (Thomas Hale). [source: GRI]
- The Sundiata epic: no single authoritative version (over 40 episodes across variants). Arabic written summaries existed before 1890; French officials collected and published versions from 1898. The modern standard version is the griot Djeli Mamoudou Kouyaté's account, put into French by Djibril Tamsir Niane in 1960 (*Soundjata ou l'Épopée mandingue*, Présence Africaine); English translation by G. D. Pickett, *Sundiata: An Epic of Old Mali* (Longmans, 1965). [source: SUN]
- Jan Vansina (14 September 1929 – 8 February 2017), Belgian historian and anthropologist, doctorate Leuven 1957, long at the University of Wisconsin–Madison; "a major innovator in the historical methodology of oral tradition". Works: *Oral Tradition: A Study in Historical Methodology* (English 1965, French original 1961 — French date not separately verified), *Kingdoms of the Savanna* (1966), *The Children of Woot* (1978), *Oral Tradition as History* (1985), *Paths in the Rainforests* (1990), *How Societies Are Born* (2004). David Beach: *Oral Tradition as History* "provided a worldwide theoretical framework on oral tradition that rendered nearly all of its predecessors obsolete". Vansina helped Alex Haley identify words in *Roots* as Mandinka. [source: JV]
- Linguistics as a source: the near-similarity of the languages of sub-equatorial Africa is the core evidence for the Bantu expansion; genetic evidence agrees. [source: BANTU]
- Arabic geographers: the first mention of Ghana is in an Arabic source of 788–93 (al-Fazari, "land of gold"); al-Khwarizmi's map of c. 830/833; al-Ya'qubi (872), al-Mas'udi (c.944), Ibn Hawqal (970s, calling the king of Ghana the "wealthiest king on the face of the earth"); al-Bakri (11th c.). [source: GH]
- Local Arabic chronicles: the *Tarikh al-Sudan* by Abd al-Rahman al-Sa'di covers Songhai from the mid-15th c. to the Moroccan invasion of 1591 and Timbuktu under Moroccan rule up to 1655; partial English translation by John Hunwick (*Timbuktu and the Songhay Empire*, Brill 1999). The *Tarikh al-Fattash* is attributed to Mahmud Kati. [source: TS; MM; GH]
- Source criticism example: the *Tarikh al-Fattash* proposed that Ghana's rulers were "not black" (Sanhaja Berber origin); modern scholars reject this foreign-origin idea; Patrick Munson's 1969 excavations at Dhar Tichitt showed a complex culture by 1600 BCE ancestral to the Soninke. [source: GH]
- After Ibn Khaldun's death (1406) there are no further Arabic primary sources on Mali except Leo Africanus; for later Mali the main written sources are Portuguese. [source: MALI]
- Archaeology: Jenne-jeno (Djenné-Djenno) occupied c. 250 BCE–1400 CE (Roderick and Susan McIntosh, excavations from 1977); over 33 hectares by c.1000 CE; African rice production likely fuelled growth. [source: JJ]

## 2. Early agriculture, iron, Nok, and the Bantu expansion
- Bantu expansion: series of migrations of Proto-Bantu speakers from a homeland in north-western Cameroon / south-eastern Nigeria across Central, Eastern and Southern Africa, between about 6,000 and 1,500 years ago (c. 4000 BCE–500 CE). Settlers absorbed, displaced or possibly replaced hunter-gatherers and pastoralists. [source: BANTU]
- Two streams: the Western Stream (south along the coast/rivers through the rainforest, reaching central Angola c. 500 BCE) and the Eastern Stream (reaching west of Lake Victoria c. 500 BCE, Urewe pottery; one of Africa's oldest iron-smelting centres). Bantu speakers reached KwaZulu-Natal by c. 300 CE and Limpopo by c. 500 CE. [source: BANTU]
- Proto-Bantu speakers lived in villages, grew oil palm, nuts, grains and possibly yams, kept goats and guinea fowl, used boats; the oldest pottery in a Bantu-speaking area (Shum Laka, Cameroon) dates to c. 5000 BCE. Firm evidence they used iron appears only c. 400 BCE. Cause of the expansion "still unclear"; consensus on multiple dispersals. [source: BANTU]
- Before the expansion the south and east were home to Khoisan-speaking foragers, Pygmy/Batwa foragers, Nilo-Saharan herders and Cushitic-speaking pastoralists; intermarriage and exchange of languages, rituals and genes were common; herding reached the far south centuries before Bantu speakers. [source: BANTU]
- Critique: Manfred Eggert calls the Central African rainforest record "extremely spotty"; Seidensticker (2024) criticises circular reasoning that equates pottery with Bantu speakers. [source: BANTU]
- Iron origins debate: whether iron smelting diffused from the Mediterranean/Meroë or was invented independently in sub-Saharan Africa is still unresolved. Archaeologists of 1945–65 mostly favoured diffusion (from Carthage or Meroë); radiocarbon dates from Niger, Rwanda and Burundi from the late 1960s revived independent-invention arguments; mid-2000s reviews found technical flaws (old carbon, insecure associations); Oboui (Central African Republic) yielded dates of c. 2000 BCE, disputed (Bernard Clist). Eggert (2014) and Augustin Holl (2018) think independent invention most likely. Sub-Saharan Africa largely lacks a separate Copper Age. [source: IRON]
- Burkina Faso furnace near Douroula dated to the 8th c. BCE (World Heritage site); Opi in the Nsukka region c. 750 BCE; Lejja claims of c. 2000 BCE are contested. [source: IRON]
- Nok culture (central Nigeria): named after Nok village where terracottas were found in 1928 in a tin mine; c. 1500 BCE – 1 CE; earliest terracottas perhaps c. 900 BCE; iron metallurgy may have developed there independently c. 750–550 BCE; Bernard Fagg recognised the significance from 1943 (a head had served for a year as a scarecrow in a yam field); Taruga excavated 1961 with iron slag and furnace fragments. Nok sculptures are regarded as the earliest large figurative sculpture in sub-Saharan Africa (excluding Egypt). [source: NOK]
- Igbo-Ukwu (south-eastern Nigeria): bronzes made by lost-wax casting, radiocarbon-dated to the 9th century CE (c. 850), centuries before other known bronzes in the region; first found 1938 by Isaiah Anozie digging by his house; excavated by Thurstan Shaw from 1959; linked to the Nri kingdom; trade links via Gao to North Africa. [source: IU]

## 3. Aksum and Christian Ethiopia to 1860 (Aksum itself: cross-refer to Kush and Aksum chapter)
- Zagwe dynasty: c. 1137–1270 in northern Ethiopia/Eritrea. [source: ZAG]
- Lalibela's 11 rock-hewn churches are traditionally attributed to the Zagwe king Gebre Meskel Lalibela (r. c.1181–1221); the site is widely understood as a symbolic Jerusalem, perhaps after Saladin's capture of Jerusalem in 1187; some scholars date parts from the 7th to 13th centuries; UNESCO World Heritage Site 1978. [source: LAL]
- Solomonic dynasty founded by Yekuno Amlak c. 1270; it lasted until the 1974 Derg coup; Ge'ez literature entered its most productive period after 1270. [source: EE]
- Zara Yaqob, emperor 1434–1468: Ge'ez literature flourished; he founded Debre Berhan as capital after seeing a bright light (identified with Halley's Comet, 1456); reputedly wrote theological works. [source: ZY]
- Ethiopian–Adal War 1529–1543: Imam Ahmad ibn Ibrahim (called Gragn, "left-handed", in Ethiopian tradition) of Adal defeated a larger Ethiopian army at Shimbra Kure (1529) and brought about three-quarters of Christian Abyssinia under his control (*Futuh al-Habasha*). A Portuguese force under Cristóvão da Gama arrived in 1541; da Gama was captured and killed on 28 August 1542; survivors joined Emperor Gelawdewos; Ahmad was killed on 21 February 1543 (Wayna Daga). [source: ADAL]
- After the war much of the empire's southern territory was lost through the Oromo migrations. [source: EE]
- Emperor Fasilides founded Gondar as a permanent capital in 1636 (Gondarine period: Fasil Ghebbi royal complex). [source: EE]
- Zemene Mesafint ("Era of the Princes/Judges"), 1769–1855: regional lords held power and Solomonic emperors at Gondar were figureheads. [source: ZM]
- Kassa Hailu was crowned Emperor Tewodros II on 11 February 1855 (r. 1855–1868), ending the Zemene Mesafint; he died at Magdala in 1868 during the British expedition (post-1860, mention only). [source: TEW]

## 4. The Sahel empires and the trans-Saharan world (cross-refer to Ghana–Mali chapter; recap only)
- Ghana (Wagadu) — Soninke state; "Ghana" means "warrior/war chief", title of the ruler; *kaya maghan* "king of gold". It became a vassal of Mali in the 13th century. Modern Ghana (Gold Coast) took the name in 1957. [source: GH]
- Camel introduced to the western Sahara in the 3rd century CE. [source: GH]
- Almoravid "conquest" of 1076–77: Conrad and Fisher, "The conquest that never was" (*History in Africa*, 1982 and 1983), argued it is folklore from misreading Arabic sources; Dierk Lange agrees there was no conquest but argues for Almoravid political agitation; the first hint is in Ibn Khaldun 300 years later. Ghana's rulers did convert to Islam around 1076. [source: GH]
- Mali Empire c. 1235 – c. 1600 (Wikipedia gives 1235–1610 in summary; last mansa Mahmud IV defeated at Jenne in 1599 after the Moroccan conquest of Songhai; the Gbara assembly is said to have lasted until 1645) — give "c. 1235 to around 1600". Founded by Sundiata Keita (c. 1214–1255), who defeated Sumanguru (Soumaoro Kanté) of Sosso at Kirina c. 1235. In 1285 the freed slave-general Sakura seized power. [source: MALI]
- Kouroukan Fouga assembly: tradition says 29 clan delegates met to set laws. [source: MALI] (Cross-refer; the "first constitution" label is modern.)
- Mansa Musa (r. c. 1312 – c. 1337): hajj 1324–25; arrived outside Cairo July 1324, crossed into Cairo 19 July, stayed three months; met Mamluk sultan al-Nasir Muhammad and initially refused to prostrate, then bowed "for God alone". Reported figures (12,000 slaves carrying gold bars; 80 camels with 50–300 lb of gold each) "are treated by modern historians as rhetorical rather than statistical". On the way home he ran out of money and borrowed from Cairo merchants. Michael Gomez estimates Mali may have captured over 6,000 slaves a year in preparing for the hajj. Death date uncertain (1332 or 1337). [source: MM]
- Musa's predecessor (probably Muhammad ibn Qu) is said in Musa's own account to have sent 200 ships, then 2,000 ships into the Atlantic and gone himself, never returning. Unverifiable. [source: MM]
- Gold: Mali was likely the largest gold producer in the world at the time (Richard Smith, quoted in MM). [source: MM]
- Ibn Battuta crossed the Sahara from February 1352 via Taghaza, and returned in 1353 with a caravan carrying 600 enslaved women. [source: IB]
- Songhai c. 1464–1591: Sunni (Sonni) Ali r. 1464–1492, took Timbuktu 1468 and Djenné after a seven-year siege; Muslim chroniclers (al-Sa'di) depict him as a tyrant who honoured his mother's traditional religion. Askia Muhammad (Askia the Great) seized power 1493; deposed by his sons 1528. [source: SON]
- Moroccan invasion: Ahmad al-Mansur sent Judar Pasha (Spanish-born, captured as a child, a eunuch) in October 1590 with about 1,500 light cavalry and 2,500 infantry, many with arquebuses, plus eight cannon and 8,000 camels; most of his ten qa'ids were converts from Christianity. Battle of Tondibi near Gao, 13 March 1591; Songhai forces estimated from 18,000 cavalry and 9,700 foot (Tarikh al-Fattash) up to 80,000 total. The Songhai planned a stampede of 1,000 cattle. Al-Mansur "erroneously presumed" Songhai had gold mines. [source: TON; SON]
- Kanem–Bornu (Lake Chad), c. 700–1902: the ruling house converted to Islam in the 11th century, when Hummay founded the Sayfawa dynasty (c. 1070s–1085); Ali Gaji founded the capital Ngazargamu c. 1472; in the 19th c. Muhammad al-Kanemi became de facto ruler and resisted the Sokoto jihad, debating its legitimacy by letter with Muhammad Bello. [source: KB; SOK]

## 5. West African Islamic scholarship
- Sankoré: one of three medieval mosques/centres of learning in Timbuktu (with Djinguereber and Sidi Yahya); founded 14th or early 15th c., funded by a Tuareg woman of the Aghlal; restored 1578–82 by qadi al-Aqib to the dimensions of the Ka'ba; golden age in the 16th c. under the Askias. There is "no evidence of a centralized teaching institution" like a European university — scholars taught private students. [source: SANK]
- Ibn Battuta (1352) found Timbuktu's inhabitants mostly Massufa (Sanhaja Berbers) and said nothing about learning; the scholarly Aqit clan arrived about a century later. Leo Africanus (1526) noted the lucrative book trade there. [source: SANK]
- Timbuktu manuscripts: mostly Arabic, some in African languages in Arabic script (ajami: Fula, Songhay, Tamasheq, Bambara, Soninke); dated late 13th to early 20th century; total number unknown. In 2012–13, 4,203 were burned or stolen when Ansar Dine held Timbuktu; some 350,000 were carried to safety, 300,000 still in Bamako in 2022 (Abdel Kader Haidara, SAVAMA-DCI). The Ahmed Baba Institute holds over 18,000. [source: TM; AB]
- Ahmad Baba al-Timbukti (26 October 1556 – 1627), of the Sanhaja Berber Aqit family, wrote more than 40 books; deported to Morocco in 1594 after the Moroccan conquest, lived in Fez/Marrakesh until al-Mansur's death; wrote the biography of al-Maghili. In *Mi'raj al-Su'ud* (1615) he rejected the association of Black Africans with slavery and the "Curse of Ham" but did not oppose slavery itself — he argued free Muslims of any ethnicity could not lawfully be enslaved (William Phillips: religion-based rather than race-based slavery). [source: AB]
- Ajami: Arabic-derived scripts for African languages (Songhai, Mande, Fula, Hausa, Swahili, Wolof, Kanuri, Yoruba). First used c. 10th–12th centuries; oldest surviving examples are 13th-century tomb inscriptions in Niger with Songhay words; full books and poetry by the 17th century; Fulfulde poetry in Futa Jallon from the mid-18th c.; Nana Asma'u wrote Fulfulde ajami poems (e.g. one of 1822). 19th-century Islamic revival produced a wave of ajami writing. [source: AJ]

## 6. Forest kingdoms and the Atlantic age (cross-refer to Kongo–Asante–Dahomey chapter; recap only)
- Kongo: by the late 16th c. probably over half a million people in a core of c. 130,000 km²; a 1623 Jesuit baptismal count suggests c. 780,000 in the kingdom and c. 100,000 around the capital Mbanza Kongo, which the Portuguese compared to Évora in 1491. Kongolese law protected the freeborn from enslavement; most slaves were war captives — the mechanism that also made Kongo an efficient slave exporter. [source: KON]
- Thornton's *The Kingdom of Kongo: Civil War and Transition, 1641–1718* (1983) argued Kongo's centralisation rested on slave-worked plantations around the capital. [source: JT]
- Benin: grew out of the Edo kingdom of Igodomigodo (11th c.); Oba Ewuare ("the Great") came to power in 1440 and expanded it; city renamed Edo c. 1470; Benin's earthworks are described as the largest such military structures in the world (rural walls of perhaps 16,000 km took an estimated 150 million man-hours); traded slaves, ivory, pepper and palm oil for manillas and guns; annexed by Britain in 1897. [source: BEN]
- Oyo: cavalry was its "long arm" (reports of very large cavalry forces); Bashorun Gaha forced two alaafins to suicide between June and October 1754; general Afonja's revolt at Ilorin (from 1817; by 1823 Ilorin had joined the Sokoto Caliphate); Ilorin destroyed the capital Oyo-Ile in 1835; a new capital was built further south. [source: OYO]
- Asante: Osei Tutu and Okomfo Anokye defeated Denkyira at Feyiase in 1701; the Asante kingdom later had a population of about 3 million over 250,000 km² (Wikipedia figure; estimates vary). Killed British general Sir Charles MacCarthy in 1824 (Nsamankow) and kept his skull as a gold-rimmed cup. [source: AS]
- Dahomey (c.1600–1904): Agaja conquered Allada in 1724 and Ouidah (Whydah) in 1727; Oyo invaded 11 times before subjugating it in 1748 (OYO); Ghezo took the throne in 1818, ended Oyo tribute and raised the women's corps (Agojie; figures of c. 4,000 often given); Dahomey sent embassies to Portugal/Brazil in 1750, 1795, 1805, 1811 and 1818 to negotiate trade terms; Britain blockaded it in 1851 and Ghezo signed a treaty in 1852 ending slave exports, promoting palm oil instead. [source: DAH; OYO]
- Aro Confederacy (Igbo hinterland): the Aro, using the Ibini Ukpabi oracle at Arochukwu, were the main suppliers of captives to riverine Igbo/Ijo ports (Onitsha, Kalabari); destroyed by Britain 1901–02. [source: ARO]

## 7. "Stateless" societies
- The anthology *African Political Systems* (ed. Meyer Fortes and E. E. Evans-Pritchard, Oxford University Press for the International African Institute, 1940) set up the comparative study of African political organisation; it is the origin of the widely used contrast between states and societies "without government". [source: APS] (The exact Group A/Group B wording was not read; describe it in general terms.)
- "Acephalous" (headless) or "stateless" societies: described as having "no government in the sense of a group able to exercise effective control over both the people and their territory"; examples usually given include the Tiv and Igbo of Nigeria and the Nuer. [source: ACE]
- Igbo political organisation: most communities governed by republican consultative assemblies of the commoners, with exceptions such as kings (Obi) at Onitsha and priest-kings at Nri and Arochukwu. [source: IGBO]
- Jenne-jeno shows urbanism without evidence of centralised rule (see Ghana–Mali chapter; McIntosh's "heterarchy" idea not separately verified). [source: JJ]

## 8. Southern and East Africa
- Mapungubwe: K2/Bambandyanalo c. 1000–1220 (c. 1,500 people by 1200); capital moved to the hilltop c. 1220 during a drought; c. 5,000 people by 1250, state of c. 30,000 km²; exported ivory and gold via Swahili towns. Thomas Huffman sees the first class society in southern Africa; Mark Horton argues the interior gained less from long-distance trade. [source: MAP]
- Great Zimbabwe: settled c. 1000; stone building from the 11th c. for over 300 years; Hill Complex (11th–13th c.), Great Enclosure (13th–15th c.), Valley Complex (14th–16th c.); 8 soapstone Zimbabwe Birds; abandoned 16th–17th c.; looted by European antiquarians 1890s–1920s. [source: GZ]
- Cape: Dutch East India Company station founded 1652 by Jan van Riebeeck; Khoikhoi–Dutch wars over grazing; enslaved people imported from Madagascar, Mozambique and Asia; British occupation 1795 and again January 1806 (Blaauwberg); ceded formally 1814. [source: CAPE]
- Great Trek: about 12,000 Dutch-speaking settlers (about a fifth of that population) left the Cape colony from 1835–36; Ndebele attacked Potgieter's party at Vegkop (20 October 1836). [source: TREK]
- Xhosa cattle-killing 1856–57: after the prophecies of Nongqawuse, over 400,000 cattle were slaughtered and about 40,000 people starved; Governor Grey blamed a "Chiefs' Plot". J. B. Peires, *The Dead Will Arise* (1989; year not separately verified) is the standard study. [source: XHOSA]
- Sierra Leone: 411 settlers including freed slaves arrived in 1787; about 1,200 Black Nova Scotians in 1792; 551 Jamaican Maroons in 1800; Crown colony 1808, later base for resettling Africans freed from slave ships. [source: SL]
- Merina (Madagascar), 1540–1897: Andrianampoinimerina reunited Imerina c. 1800; Radama I signed a treaty with Britain in 1817; between 1828 and 1840 Merina forces reportedly killed more than 100,000 and enslaved more than 200,000. [source: MER]
- Buganda: kabaka and clan heads; Speke (1862) admired the palace's size and order; Kabaka Suna's wars took many women captives. [source: BUG]

## 9. The Zulu kingdom and the Mfecane
- Shaka (born c. 1787), Zulu king 1816–1828; assassinated September 1828 by half-brothers Dingane and Mhlangana (with Mbopa); military changes attributed to him include the *iklwa* thrusting spear, regiments quartered in military homesteads, and the "horns of the buffalo" formation; his regiments built on existing age-set institutions. [source: SH; MF]
- Rivals before Shaka: the Mthethwa (under Dingiswayo) and Ndwandwe (under Zwide); Ndwandwe killed Dingiswayo in the late 1810s; Shaka destroyed the Ndwandwe in 1826. Breakaway groups under Soshangane (Gaza kingdom), Zwangendaba and Mzilikazi (Ndebele) moved north. [source: MF]
- Mzilikazi (c. 1790 – 9 September 1868) broke with Shaka c. 1821, moved onto the highveld (Magaliesberg 1827–28), and after defeats by Boers and others founded the Ndebele (Matabele) kingdom in south-west Zimbabwe. [source: MZ; MF]
- Moshoeshoe gathered refugees into the Basotho kingdom from 1824 (Thaba Bosiu); invited French Protestant missionaries 1833; Basutoland was proclaimed British territory on 12 March 1868. [source: MOS; MF]
- Dingane had Piet Retief and his party killed (February 1838); Battle of Blood River (Ncome), 16 December 1838: about 600 Voortrekkers under Andries Pretorius against about 15,000 Zulu. Mpande took power in 1840 with Boer help; Britain annexed Natal (war with the Boers 1842). [source: ZK]
- The term *mfecane* ("crushing"; Sotho *difaqane/lifaqane*): broadly late 18th to mid-19th century, intense phase 1810s–1840s. Traditional death-toll estimates of 1–2 million are "controversial", with some scholars revising them sharply downward. [source: MF]
- Revisionist causes: pre-existing political centralisation, the Delagoa Bay ivory (and later slave) trade, drought (aggravated by the volcanic eruptions of 1809 and 1815), and raiding by mounted, armed Griqua, Kora and other groups from the Cape frontier. [source: MF]

## 10. Islamic reform and the jihad states
- Fula jihads: first rising in Futa Jallon 1725; first almamy Alpha Ibrahima Sambegu elected 1725 at Timbo; the Futa Jallon state lasted until 1898. [source: FJ]
- Usman dan Fodio (15 December 1754 – 20 April 1817), Fulani scholar; wrote *Ihya al-Sunna* by 1773 and more than 100 works; his hijra from Gobir in 1804 (1218 AH) started the jihad. Bello reports 2,000 dead at Tsuntua, including 200 Qur'an memorisers. By 1808 the jihadists controlled Hausaland. In 1815 he divided the state between his brother Abdullahi (Gwandu, west) and son Muhammad Bello (Sokoto). [source: UDF; SOK]
- Sokoto Caliphate: over 30 emirates; by 1837 a population of perhaps 10–20 million, the most populous state in West Africa; at least half the population enslaved in the 19th c.; by 1900 "at least 1 million and perhaps as many as 2.5 million slaves" (comparison: about 4 million in the US South in 1860). Lasted until the British conquest of 1903. [source: SOK]
- Nana Asma'u (1793–1864), daughter of Usman dan Fodio: knew Arabic, Fula (Fulfulde), Hausa and Tamasheq; from about 1830 trained women teachers (*jajis*) who travelled to teach women in their homes, forming the *'yan taru* ("those who congregate together"). *The Collected Works of Nana Asma'u* (eds Jean Boyd and Beverly Mack, 1997). [source: NA]
- Massina (Hamdullahi) Caliphate founded by Seku Amadu in 1818 after defeating the Bambara at Noukouma; later absorbed by Umar Tall's Toucouleur empire (1852–1903). [source: MAS; TOU; FJ]

## 11. Omani Zanzibar and the East African trades
- Oman took Mombasa from the Portuguese in 1698 (Fort Jesus), bringing Zanzibar under Omani rule. [source: ZAN]
- Said bin Sultan (5 June 1791 – 19 October 1856), ruler of Oman from 1804, took Mombasa in 1837 and moved his capital from Muscat to Stone Town, Zanzibar (1840 per SBS; ZAN says "1832 or 1840"); encouraged clove plantations worked by enslaved labour and Indian merchants; signed the Moresby (1822) and Hamerton (1845) treaties restricting the slave trade. On his death in 1856 Oman and Zanzibar were divided (Majid became Sultan of Zanzibar). [source: SBS; ZAN]
- Tippu Tip (c. 1837–1905), Afro-Omani ivory and slave trader in the interior (post-1860 career mostly). [source: Tippu_Tip]

## 12. The slave trades and African societies
- Atlantic: Slave Voyages estimates c. 12.5 million (12,520,000) embarked and c. 10.7 million disembarked (1501–1866). [source: https://legacy.slavevoyages.org/blog/methodology-trans-atlantic; AST quoting Anderson et al. 2013]
- Deaths inside Africa: Gomez says deaths from capture to the coast averaged 10–15%, up to 40% in Angola; Nunn cites 10–50% on forced marches; Patrick Manning estimates 4 million died inside Africa after capture. [source: AST]
- Trans-Saharan: estimates of all Black slaves taken from sub-Saharan Africa to the Arab/Islamic world range 10–18 million from the 7th to the 20th century; Lovejoy estimates about 6 million across the Sahara 650–1500; about 2 million in the 19th century. [source: TSST]
- Indian Ocean / East Africa: N'Diaye and Pétré-Grenouilleau estimate 8 million taken from East Africa from the 7th c. to 1920 (and 9 million across the Sahara) — high-end estimates, contested; c. 200,000 sent to European plantations in the western Indian Ocean in the 19th century; between 1808 and 1860 the Royal Navy captured about 1,600 slave ships and freed over 150,000 Africans. [source: IOST]
- Thornton: "a very large preexisting slave population in Africa" when Europeans arrived; Europeans usually bought people captured in endemic warfare. [source: AST]
- "Gun-slave cycle": Inikori, Lovejoy and Whatley argue Europeans stimulated slaving indirectly through guns, gunpowder and horses. [source: AST]
- Loango, Kakongo and Ngoyo never allowed Europeans to build permanent forts (Ana Lucia Araujo). [source: AST]

## Debates
- **Rodney vs Thornton.** Walter Rodney (1942–1980; PhD SOAS 1966 on the Upper Guinea Coast slave trade, published 1970; *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa*, 1972; assassinated in Georgetown 13 June 1980) argued the trade was an unequal, "colonial" relationship that diverted merchants from production and disrupted labouring people through war and kidnapping. Thornton (b. 1949, Boston University; *Africa and Africans in the Making of the Atlantic World*, 1992) calls Rodney's views "radical": African states and elites engaged voluntarily, controlled the trade on their coasts, and African manufacturing could compete. Others (Inikori, Lovejoy, Whatley) stress the gun-slave cycle; Joseph Miller attributes West Central African stagnation mainly to domestic factors; Inikori compares actual population to counterfactual population without exports. [source: WR; JT; AST]
- **Nunn on long-run effects.** Nathan Nunn, "The Long-Term Effects of Africa's Slave Trades", *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 123(1) (Feb 2008): 139–176 — robust negative relationship between slave exports by country and current income; uses shipping records and slave-ethnicity data and instrumental variables. Nunn & Leonard Wantchekon, "The Slave Trade and the Origins of Mistrust in Africa", *American Economic Review* 101(7) (Dec 2011): 3221–3252 — descendants of heavily raided groups trust neighbours, relatives and local government less; effect works mainly through internal norms; instrument: historical distance from the coast. Nunn & Puga: rugged terrain protected some regions. [source: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:123:y:2008:i:1:p:139-176. ; https://nber.org/papers/w14783 ; NN] Criticisms of these papers were not researched — say "debated" without naming critics.
- **The Mfecane.** J. D. Omer-Cooper's *The Zulu Aftermath: A Nineteenth-Century Revolution in Bantu Africa* (Longmans, Ibadan History Series, 1966) popularised the term and portrayed Zulu expansion as a positive "revolution" of state-building, building on Theal. Julian Cobbing, "The Mfecane as Alibi: Thoughts on Dithakong and Mbolompo" (*Journal of African History* 29, 1988) argued the Zulu-centric story was a self-serving settler/apartheid-era construct that hid the labour demands of Delagoa Bay slavers and Cape settlers ("the Cobbing controversy"). Elizabeth Eldredge countered that there is scant evidence of a Delagoa Bay slave trade before 1823 and that Griqua raiders, not missionaries, drove Cape-side slaving, while still rejecting the Zulu-centric explanation. Dan Wylie: settler writers (Nathaniel Isaacs's *Travels*, 1836; Henry Francis Fynn) exaggerated Shaka's brutality to justify colonisation — Fynn was bidding for land "allegedly ... depopulated by Shaka". Omer-Cooper replied with "Has the Mfecane a future?" (*JSAS* 19(2), 1993). Current consensus: multiple causes; the "empty land" idea served colonial land claims. [source: MF; SH; https://cambridge.org/core/journals/africa/article/abs/zulu-aftermath-a-nineteenthcentury-revolution-in-bantu-africa-by-j-d-omercooper-ibadan-history-series-london-longmans-1966-pp-182-pl-7-maps-8-35s/EE08C1B33573E12E4E93473116869FA6]
- **Did the Almoravids conquer Ghana in 1076?** Conrad & Fisher 1982–83: no; Lange: no conquest but political pressure. [source: GH]
- **Iron: diffusion or independent invention?** Unresolved; recent specialists (Eggert, Holl) lean to independent invention; early dates contested. [source: IRON]
- **Bantu expansion as a migration model.** Mainstream; critics question pottery–language correlations. [source: BANTU]
- **"Stateless" societies.** The state/stateless typology comes from colonial-era anthropology (Fortes & Evans-Pritchard 1940); societies like the Igbo had complex consensual institutions, which the "acephalous" label can obscure. [source: APS; ACE; IGBO]
- **Mansa Musa and Cairo's gold.** Al-Umari's 25→<22 dirhams; Schultz: within normal fluctuation. [source: MM]
- **Great Zimbabwe complexes:** successive royal residences vs "structuralist" functional interpretation. [source: GZ]
