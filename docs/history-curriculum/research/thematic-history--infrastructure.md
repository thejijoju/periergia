## What "infrastructure" is: definitions and the scholarly turn
- Brian Larkin, "The Politics and Poetics of Infrastructure", *Annual Review of Anthropology* 42 (2013): 327–343. His definition: "Infrastructures are built networks that facilitate the flow of goods, people, or ideas and allow for their exchange over space" (p. 328), and the much-quoted "Infrastructures are matter that enable the movement of other matter" (p. 329). His point: they are both things and the relations between things, and their *form* (the "poetics": spectacle, promise, aspiration) is political, not only their function. Wording confirmed only through secondary quotations; keep quotation marks to these two sentences. [source: search results incl. https://culanth.org/fieldsights/emplacement and https://blay.se/2016-03-26-the-anthropological-study-of-infrastructure.html]
- Susan Leigh Star, "The Ethnography of Infrastructure", *American Behavioral Scientist* 43:3 (1999), lists properties of infrastructure including embeddedness, being "built on an installed base", and "becomes visible upon breakdown". The related phrase "invisible until breakdown" comes from Star and Karen Ruhleder (1996). Larkin and others contest the invisibility claim: many infrastructures are built to be seen (dams, bridges, opening ceremonies). [source: https://www.imtfi.uci.edu/files/articles/Star.pdf (via search) ; https://blay.se/2016-03-26-the-anthropological-study-of-infrastructure.html]
- Paul N. Edwards, "Infrastructure and Modernity: Force, Time, and Social Organization in the History of Sociotechnical Systems", in Thomas J. Misa, Philip Brey and Andrew Feenberg (eds), *Modernity and Technology* (MIT Press, 2003), pp. 185–225. Cited for the idea that to be modern is to live within and by means of infrastructures operating at many scales. [source: https://www.schweitzer-online.de/en/buch/Misa/Modernity-Technology/9780262134217/A1379547/ and https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-3-030-43965-1_6 (via search)]
- Michael Mann introduced "infrastructural power" in 1984 ("The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results", *European Journal of Sociology* 25:2, 185–213). He contrasts the "despotic power" of state elites over civil society with infrastructural power, "the institutional capacity of a state, despotic or not, to penetrate its territories and logistically implement decisions" (quoted as in WP; WP cites Mann but the exact page is not given). Shorthand often used: despotic power is power *over* society, infrastructural power is power *through* society. Mann's four means include communications and transport, standard weights, measures and currency, literacy, and a division of labour in state services. In 1993 he clarified that despotic states also rely on infrastructural power. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrastructural_power ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Mann_(sociologist)]
- Mann's main work is *The Sources of Social Power* in four volumes (1986, 1993, 2012, 2013), built on the IEMP model: ideological, economic, military and political power. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Mann_(sociologist)]
- Infrastructure is commonly defined as the facilities and systems that serve a country, city or area: roads, railways, bridges, airports, transit, tunnels, water supply, sewers, grids, telecommunications. A 1987 US National Research Council panel adopted the term "public works infrastructure". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrastructure]

## Ancient systems: roads
- Persian Royal Road: reorganised and rebuilt by the Achaemenids, probably perfected under Darius I (r. 522–486 BCE), from Susa to Sardis, about 1,677 miles (about 2,700 km). Mounted royal couriers (the *angarium*) were supposed to cover it in nine days; the journey took ninety days on foot. It had 111 posting stations with fresh horses. Western sections may go back to Assyrian kings. (Persian Empire chapter already covers it: cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Road]
- Herodotus on the couriers: "There is nothing in the world that travels faster than these Persian couriers." The line "Neither snow nor rain nor heat nor gloom of night stays these couriers from the swift completion of their appointed rounds" (a translation of Herodotus) was inscribed on the James Farley Post Office in New York. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Road]
- Roman roads were built from about 300 BC; the Via Appia dates to 312 BC. At the empire's height 29 great military highways radiated from Rome and the 113 provinces were linked by 372 great roads, a total of more than 400,000 km, of which over 80,500 km were stone-paved. Augustus founded the *cursus publicus*, the official relay post. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_roads]
- The Laws of the Twelve Tables (about 450 BC) required public roads to be 8 Roman feet (about 2.37 m) wide on straight stretches. A recent study using the ORBIS model and coin finds suggests roads helped integrate distant regions economically over time. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_roads]
- Inca road system (*Qhapaq Ñan*, "royal road"): about 40,000 km in total per WP's lead; estimates proposed range from 23,000 to 60,000 km. Parts were built by earlier cultures (Wari, Tiwanaku). Relay runners (*chasquis*) had stations at short intervals and *tambos* (lodgings) at about a day's walk; storehouses (*qullqas*) lined it. No wheeled transport: chasquis and llama caravans. Upkeep was owed as labour (mit'a). It crosses six modern countries (Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Chile, Argentina) and became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2014. (Inca Empire chapter gives 30,000–40,000 km: compatible.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_road_system]
- After the conquest, Spanish horses and carts suited the roads poorly, and the forced resettlement of Andean people into *reducciones* led to much of the network being abandoned in favour of new roads to centres of Spanish power. A 2021 study found that communities within 20 km of the Inca road still have higher wages, nutrition and schooling than similar communities farther away. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_road_system]

## Ancient systems: water
- Rome's first aqueduct, the Aqua Appia, was commissioned in 312 BC by the censor Appius Claudius Caecus (the same man as the Via Appia, in the same year, during the Samnite wars); it ran mostly underground from a spring 16.4 km away. By the 3rd century AD Rome had eleven state-funded aqueducts, with a combined conduit length of about 780–800 km (about 47 km above ground). Modern estimates of supply, from Frontinus's figures, range from about 520,000–635,000 m³ a day up to 1,000,000 m³, for a city of about a million. Most water went to public baths. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_aqueduct]
- Maintenance and its politics: Augustus created the office of *curator aquarum*; Frontinus held it from AD 97 and wrote *De aquaeductu*, blaming theft by private users and corrupt officials for losses (WP notes he may have overstated theft). Vitruvius recommended a gradient of at least 1 in 4,800. The Pont du Gard falls only 17 m in 50 km and could carry up to 40,000 m³ a day. In 537, besieging Ostrogoths cut Rome's aqueducts during the Gothic War; the city's reduced population never fully restored them. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_aqueduct]
- Qanat (*kārīz*): an underground gravity-fed channel tapping an aquifer, with vertical shafts for maintenance; developed in ancient Iran, most sources say in the early 1st millennium BCE (some suggest a southeast Arabian origin); "originating approximately 3,000 years ago". Known as *kārēz* in Afghanistan and Pakistan, *foggara* in Algeria, *khettara* in Morocco, *falaj* in Oman and the UAE; large working systems survive in Iran, Afghanistan, Xinjiang (Turpan), Oman and Pakistan; carried to Spain by Romans and on a larger scale in al-Andalus, and thence to Spanish America. Most Iranian qanats run under 5 km; some near Kerman about 70 km. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qanat]
- Qanats are run by gravity, resist drought and war damage, and need communal maintenance; in Afghanistan farmers have abandoned *kariz* kept for centuries for diesel-pumped tube wells. Darius I is said (per Olmstead) to have introduced qanat irrigation to Egypt's oases. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qanat]
- Bali's *subak*: rice-terrace irrigation managed cooperatively through water temples, developed from the 9th century, covering nearly 20,000 ha; UNESCO World Heritage 2012. A counter-example to Wittfogel: complex irrigation run without a central despot (the anthropologist J. Stephen Lansing's work; his book title not verified here). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subak_(irrigation)]

## China's Grand Canal
- The oldest sections date to the early 5th century BC (King Fuchai of Wu). Emperor Yang of Sui completed the network in 604–609, linking the rice of Jiangnan to his capital at Luoyang and to his northern armies; the first major section (605) is recorded as using the labour of five million people. Conscription for the canals and the failed Goguryeo war were among the chief causes of rebellion and the fall of the Sui (618). (Tang–Song chapter covers this: cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canal_(China)]
- The Song official Qiao Weiyue invented the pound lock in 984 on the canal. Under the Yuan (capital moved to Beijing) a summit section across Shandong gave a direct Hangzhou–Beijing waterway of about 1,800 km. The Ming renovated it almost entirely in 1411–1415 with 165,000 labourers (Ming chapter: "rebuilt in 1415"). It is the longest artificial waterway in the world; UNESCO World Heritage 2014. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canal_(China)]
- Fragility: in 1447 an official likened the canal to "a throat that could be easily strangled" (paraphrase in WP, not a verified quotation). In 1855 the Yellow River changed course and severed the canal in Shandong; with the sea route for tribute grain and new railways (Tianjin–Pukou, Beijing–Hankou), the canal languished. (Opium Wars chapter notes the British threat to the canal in 1842.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canal_(China)]

## Hydraulic despotism: Wittfogel and his critics
- Karl August Wittfogel (6 September 1896 – 25 May 1988), German-American sinologist; a Communist in Weimar Germany, held in Nazi camps in 1933, later a fierce anticommunist. His *Oriental Despotism: A Comparative Study of Total Power* (1957) argued that large-scale irrigation and flood control required central coordination and a bureaucracy, producing "hydraulic" states of total power (Egypt, Mesopotamia, China, India, Mexico, Peru), different in kind from Western feudal societies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_August_Wittfogel ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic_empire]
- Critics: Joseph Needham argued Wittfogel misread Chinese history. Robert Carneiro (*Science*, August 1970): "Archeological evidence now makes it appear that in at least three of the areas that Wittfogel cites as exemplifying his 'hydraulic hypothesis'—Mesopotamia, China, and Mexico—full-fledged states developed well before large-scale irrigation." Robert McC. Adams found nothing linking early Mesopotamian dynastic authority to canal administration; Jacques Gernet argued existing state structures made great irrigation works possible, not the reverse. Carneiro granted that irrigation "unquestionably" increased state power once states existed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic_empire]

## Canals of the industrial age
- Erie Canal: construction began 4 July 1817 at Rome, New York; opened 26 October 1825; 363 miles (584 km) from Albany to Buffalo, 40 ft wide and 4 ft deep; the legislature voted $7 million in 1817. Opponents called it "Clinton's Folly" / "Clinton's Ditch" after Governor DeWitt Clinton. Built largely by Irish labourers (about 5,000 newly arrived) and others. When built it was the world's second-longest canal after the Grand Canal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erie_Canal]
- Effects: a canal could cut transport costs by about 95%; shipping a barrel of flour from Rochester to Albany fell from $3 to 75 cents. It gave New York City a decisive advantage over other US ports and grew Albany, Utica, Syracuse, Rochester and Buffalo. Clinton poured Lake Erie water into New York harbour in the "Wedding of the Waters". Peak traffic was 1855 (33,000 shipments); tolls were abolished from 1 January 1883 (WP elsewhere says "about 1902": use 1883). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erie_Canal]
- An old claim that over 1,000 Erie workers died of "swamp fever" is doubted by recent research (no contemporary reports or mass graves). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erie_Canal]
- Suez predecessors: a canal from the Nile to the Red Sea was begun under Necho II (610–595 BCE) and completed by Darius I, who set up stelae: "I ordered to dig this canal from the river that is called Nile and flows in Egypt, to the sea that begins in Persia" (translation). Herodotus's claim that 120,000 men died under Necho is "doubtless exaggerated" (WP). (Persian Empire chapter mentions Darius's canal.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal]
- Suez Canal: Ferdinand de Lesseps obtained concessions from Sa'id Pasha in 1854 and 1856 (99 years from opening); work began 25 April 1859; opened 17 November 1869; 193.3 km. Forced labour (corvée) was used until 1864. Some sources estimate 1.5 million people from various countries worked on it, over 30,000 at any one time. Death toll disputed: Nasser cited 120,000 in his 26 July 1956 nationalisation speech; the company's chief medical officer reported no more than 2.49 deaths per thousand in 1866, implying fewer than 3,000 (or fewer than 1,000 on reported data). Give the range and say it is contested. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal]
- It cut the voyage from the Arabian Sea to London by about 8,900 km; in the 19th century the steamship time saved to Bombay was 24 days from London, Liverpool or Hamburg, 31 from Marseille. In 1875 Khedive Isma'il, unable to pay his debts, sold Egypt's 44% stake to Britain for £4 million. By 1955 about two-thirds of Europe's oil passed through it; around 8% of world sea trade uses it. Closed from 5 June 1967 to 5 June 1975. Record revenue $9.4 billion (FY to June 2023); Houthi attacks from late 2023 cut traffic (Sisi: losses of $800 million a month). (British Empire, Modern Middle East, Decolonization chapters cover 1875, 1882 and 1956: cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal]
- Panama: the French effort under de Lesseps began 1 January 1881 and went bankrupt in 1889, with an estimated 22,000 dead (as many as 5,000 French) and the savings of 800,000 investors lost; in 1888 the workforce was about 40,000, nine-tenths Afro-Caribbean. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panama_Canal]
- After Colombia's Senate rejected the Hay–Herrán Treaty (1903), the US backed Panama's secession: US warships blocked Colombian troops, Panama declared independence on 3 November 1903, and the Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty (6 November 1903) gave the US the Canal Zone. Theodore Roosevelt later said, "I took the Isthmus, started the canal and then left Congress not to debate the canal, but to debate me." In 1921 the US paid Colombia $25 million. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panama_Canal]
- US construction 1904–1914; William C. Gorgas's mosquito control nearly eliminated yellow fever and malaria; still about 5,600 workers died, the great majority West Indians, especially Barbadians; about 350 Americans. Opened 15 August 1914 (SS *Ancon*); 82 km. Torrijos–Carter Treaties signed 7 September 1977; full Panamanian control from noon 31 December 1999. Expanded locks opened 26 June 2016. About 200 million litres of fresh water per ship; drought in 2023 cut daily transits from 36 to 22. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panama_Canal]
- Canal Zone pay was split into a "gold roll" (skilled and white-collar, mostly white Americans, paid in US gold currency) and a "silver roll" (mostly West Indian labourers, paid in Panamanian silver); Julie Greene argues the division rested on class, gender, race and nationality together. [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/501695 (via search)]

## Railways
- Stockton and Darlington Railway, opened 27 September 1825: the world's first public railway to use steam locomotives (*Locomotion No. 1*), built to move coal; passengers went by horse-drawn coach until 1833. Liverpool and Manchester Railway, opened 15 September 1830: first inter-city railway, steam only, double track, timetabled. (Industrial Revolution chapter covers both: cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockton_and_Darlington_Railway ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liverpool_and_Manchester_Railway]
- Path dependence: George Stephenson's 4 ft 8½ in gauge, taken from colliery tramways, was copied by the Liverpool and Manchester, then Britain, Europe and North America; more than half the world's railways use it, though Iberian, Russian and Indian broad gauges show nothing inherent made it best. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_dependence]
- Australia's colonies chose three gauges in the 1850s; in 1937 Brisbane to Perth meant four railway systems, three gauges and changes at eight stations; standard gauge did not link all mainland capitals until 1995. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_gauge_in_Australia]
- India: the Great Indian Peninsula Railway was incorporated 1 August 1849 under a guarantee system offering free land and a guaranteed 5% return to private English companies (risk borne by Indian revenues). First passenger train 16 April 1853, Bombay to Thane, 34 km, 14 carriages, 400 people, three locomotives (Sahib, Sindh, Sultan). In 1951 42 companies were merged into Indian Railways (about 55,000 km); today about 68,584 km route, over 1.2 million employees. (British Empire chapter gives 1,349 km in 1860 to 25,495 km in 1890: cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_India ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Railways]
- Economists' assessment: Dave Donaldson ("Railroads of the Raj", *American Economic Review* 108, 2018) estimates that when the railway reached the average district, real agricultural income rose by about 16% (an earlier draft said 18%). Robin Burgess and Donaldson find that famine intensity and mortality became much less sensitive to rainfall shocks once railways arrived. These are contested by historians who stress the guarantee system's cost and food exports during famines (see the British Empire chapter's famine section). [source: https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/southasia/2014/08/06/railroads-of-the-raj-estimating-the-impact-of-transportation-infrastructure ; https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/54255 (via search)]
- First US transcontinental railroad: Pacific Railroad Act signed by Lincoln 1 July 1862 (after Southern secession removed the rival-route deadlock). Central Pacific built 690 miles east from Sacramento, Union Pacific 1,085 miles west from Omaha/Council Bluffs; joined at Promontory Summit, Utah, 10 May 1869 (the "Last Spike"). Federal subsidy bonds totalled $55,092,192; the railways were entitled to 21.1 million acres of federal land under the 1862 and 1864 Acts (WP also gives a disputed "about 130 million acres" for all federal railway land grants: do not mix the two). Crossing went from months to days at one tenth the cost of stagecoach or wagon. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_transcontinental_railroad]
- Chinese workers did most of the Central Pacific's heavy labour, many from the Siyi districts of Guangdong; a late-1865 snapshot showed about 3,000 Chinese and 1,700 white workers; they struck in 1867 and won a raise from $31 to $35 a month. Total numbers are uncertain: the conventional figure is 10,000–12,000 (some payroll analyses say about 9,000). Do not use "15,000–20,000" or a precise percentage. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_transcontinental_railroad ; https://web.stanford.edu/group/chineserailroad/cgi-bin/website/three-interpretations-of-the-role-of-chinese-railroad-workers-in-the-construction-of-the-central-pacific-railroad (via search)]
- The line ran through Plains nations' lands in violation of treaties; Union Pacific hired marksmen to kill bison, the Plains peoples' main food source, and the army protected construction. Sherman wrote to Grant in 1867: "we are not going to let thieving, ragged Indians check and stop the progress" of the railroads. The Crédit Mobilier scandal grew from Union Pacific's construction company. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_transcontinental_railroad]
- Trans-Siberian Railway: construction began 9 March 1891 (inaugurated by Tsarevich Nicholas at Vladivostok); opened 1904; a continuous all-Russian Moscow–Vladivostok route only from 1916; 9,289 km, eight time zones, the world's longest railway line. Convicts did some construction. It brought millions of peasant migrants: about four million arrived in Siberia in 1906–1914. (Imperial Russia chapter covers 1891/1904: cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Siberian_Railway]
- Uganda Railway: built 1896–1901 from Mombasa to Kisumu on Lake Victoria, metre gauge; more than 30,000 indentured labourers, mostly from Punjab and Gujarat; several thousand died (disease, accidents, the Tsavo lions); a racial hierarchy of Europeans, Indians and Africans; many Indians stayed, founding East Africa's Indian community. Nandi resistance under Koitalel Arap Samoei ended when Richard Meinertzhagen killed him in 1905. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uganda_Railway]
- Congo–Ocean Railway (French Equatorial Africa), Pointe-Noire to Brazzaville, 502 km, built 1921–1934 with forced labour recruited from what is now southern Chad and the Central African Republic; deaths estimated at over 17,000 workers. France did not ratify the ILO Forced Labour Convention (1930) until 1946. Resentment fed the Kongo-Wara rebellion (1928–1931). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo–Ocean_Railway]
- TAZARA (Tanzania–Zambia Railway), 1,860 km, built 1970–1975 as a Chinese-financed turnkey project costing $406 million, with about 13,000 Chinese technicians and 38,000 African labourers, to give Zambian copper an outlet avoiding white-ruled Rhodesia and South Africa. Freight fell to 88,000 tonnes in 2014/15, under 2% of design capacity; a Chinese upgrade MoU was signed in September 2024. (Maoism chapter mentions TAZARA: cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TAZARA_Railway]

## Telegraph and cables
- Gutta-percha, latex of the *Palaquium gutta* tree, introduced to Europe from Singapore by William Montgomerie (East India Company surgeon) in the 1840s, made submarine cables possible. The first cross-Channel cable (August 1850) failed; a protected cable worked from September 1851. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_communications_cable]
- First transatlantic cable: first messages 16 August 1858 (Queen Victoria to President Buchanan); destroyed after three weeks when Wildman Whitehouse applied excessive voltage. *Great Eastern* laid a durable cable in July 1866 (connection 27 July) and recovered the 1865 cable; slogan "Two weeks to two minutes". Permanent link since 1866. (Second Industrial Revolution chapter covers this: cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transatlantic_telegraph_cable]
- Bombay linked to London by submarine cable in 1870; in 1872 the companies merged into John Pender's Eastern Telegraph Company and Australia was linked via Singapore; by 1872 every continent but Antarctica was connected. British systems dominated until 1911 (still 42.7% of cables in 1923). Britain sought an "All Red Line" through imperial territory; its first act in the First World War was to cut Germany's five Atlantic cables. First trans-Pacific cables 1902–1903. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_communications_cable]
- Today: first fibre-optic transatlantic cable TAT-8, 1988; submarine cables carry about 99% of data traffic across the oceans; a transoceanic system costs several hundred million dollars. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_communications_cable]
- Daniel R. Headrick (born 1941, Roosevelt University, Chicago): *The Tools of Empire: Technology and European Imperialism in the Nineteenth Century* (Oxford UP, 1981) on steamships, quinine, firearms and communications, treating technology as an element rather than a sufficient explanation; then *The Tentacles of Progress* (1988, technology transfer 1850–1940), *The Invisible Weapon: Telecommunications and International Politics, 1851–1945* (1991, on cables), *Power over Peoples* (2010). (Imperial Expansion chapter quotes "necessary, if not sufficient": cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_R._Headrick]

## Sanitation: London's sewers and the Great Stink
- London cholera deaths: 6,536 (1831), 14,137 (1848–49), 10,738 (1853–54). John Snow argued for water-borne transmission (1849, updated after Broad Street 1854) but miasma theory prevailed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Stink]
- The Great Stink, July–August 1858: shade temperatures in June averaged 34–36 °C. Disraeli called the Thames "a Stygian pool, reeking with ineffable and intolerable horrors" and tabled a bill (15 June 1858) letting the Metropolitan Board of Works borrow £3 million, repaid by a threepenny levy on London households over forty years. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Stink]
- Joseph Bazalgette's system: 82 miles of intercepting brick main sewers and 1,100 miles of street sewers, built from 1859 to 1875, with pumping stations at Crossness (opened 1865) and Abbey Mills and the Victoria (opened 1870), Albert and Chelsea Embankments, which reclaimed over 52 acres from the Thames. Its premise (removing miasma) was wrong, but it removed the cholera bacterium from the water supply and also reduced typhus and typhoid. (Victorian Era chapter covers this: cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Bazalgette ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Stink]
- Who is bypassed: the 1866 outbreak killed 5,596 Londoners, 93% of them in an East End area (Aldgate to Bow) not yet connected to the system and supplied by the East London Water Company. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Stink]
- Long life and limits: Bazalgette's sewers still serve a city of over nine million, but overflow in storms; the Thames Tideway Tunnel (about 25 km) to catch overflows from 34 outfalls became fully operational in February 2025 and opened in May 2025 at a final cost of £5 billion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thames_Tideway_Tunnel]

## Electrification
- First public supply: Godalming, Surrey, autumn 1881 (not a commercial success); Holborn Viaduct, London, 1882; Edison's Pearl Street Station, New York, September 1882 (85 customers at first, 508 by 1884). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrification]
- Lenin: "Communism is Soviet power plus the electrification of the whole country" (translation). The GOELRO commission was set up 21 February 1920 under Gleb Krzhizhanovsky; its plan was approved by the 8th Congress of Soviets on 22 December 1920. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GOELRO]
- US rural electrification: in 1934 fewer than 11% of US farms had electricity, against nearly 90% in France and Germany. The Rural Electrification Administration was created by executive order on 11 May 1935; the Rural Electrification Act became law 20 May 1936, lending to cooperatives; nearly 50% of farms had power by 1942 and almost all by 1952. Debate: David Beito argues private firms had already quadrupled rural electrification (2.8% to 11.8% of rural homes, 1924–32); others note the rate stalled at about 11% until 1938. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rural_Electrification_Act]
- Global access: 49% of the world's population had electricity in 1970, 76% in 1990, 81–83% by the early 2010s; about 840 million people, mostly in Africa, lacked grid access in 2017 (down from 1.2 billion in 2010). Newer figures not verified here. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrification]

## Dams and displacement
- Hoover Dam (Colorado River): built 1931–1936 by Six Companies, Inc.; dedicated 30 September 1935 by Franklin Roosevelt; cost over 96 lives (official count); employment peaked at 5,251 (July 1934). The contract barred Chinese labour, and Black workers never exceeded thirty, in a segregated crew. For six years while Lake Mead filled virtually no water reached the Colorado delta in Mexico, which turned into an "inverse estuary". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoover_Dam]
- Tennessee Valley Authority, created 18 May 1933 (New Deal) for navigation, flood control, power and regional development in a valley where 30% of people had malaria; under David Lilienthal it became the US model for modernising agrarian societies abroad. Historians criticise its use of eminent domain and the displacement of over 125,000 valley residents. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tennessee_Valley_Authority]
- Aswan High Dam: built 1960–1970 with Soviet help after the US and UK withdrew funding offers in July 1956 (prompting Nasser's nationalisation of the Suez Canal Company on 26 July 1956). Lake Nasser flooded much of Lower Nubia: 100,000–120,000 people were resettled, including 50,000–70,000 Sudanese Nubians from Wadi Halfa and about 50,000 Egyptian Nubians to "New Nubia" near Kom Ombo; Egypt began compensating Nubians only in 2019–20. UNESCO's rescue campaign (from 1960) moved Abu Simbel. Trapped silt led to Delta coastal erosion and the collapse of the Mediterranean sardine catch (35,000–40,000 tons a year to 20,000 or less by 1971). The earlier Aswan Low Dam was British-built, 1898–1902. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aswan_High_Dam]
- Kariba Dam (Zambezi), built 1955–1959 for the colonial Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland, opened 17 May 1960; about 57,000 Tonga people were resettled; 86 construction workers died. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kariba_Dam]
- Akosombo Dam (Ghana), built 1961–1965 under Nkrumah mainly to power a Kaiser/Valco aluminium smelter at Tema; Lake Volta is the largest reservoir by surface area (8,502 km², 3.6% of Ghana); about 80,000 people (1% of the population) from 700 villages were moved to 52 resettlement villages, many unsuccessfully. (Decolonization chapter mentions Kaiser's terms: cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akosombo_Dam]
- India: Nehru called the Bhakra Dam (1948–1963) the "New Temple of Resurgent India". (The more often quoted "temples of modern India" was not verified.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakra_Dam]
- Narmada: Nehru laid the Sardar Sarovar foundation on 5 April 1961; the Narmada Bachao Andolan, founded by Medha Patkar in 1985, mobilised Adivasis and farmers; after Patkar's 22-day fast the World Bank's independent Morse review found its own resettlement and environmental policies violated, and Bank participation ended in 1993; the Supreme Court halted work in 1995, later allowed it; the dam was inaugurated 17 September 2017. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narmada_Bachao_Andolan ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardar_Sarovar_Dam]
- The World Commission on Dams (1997–2001) reported in November 2000 (*Dams and Development: A New Framework for Decision-Making*, launched under Nelson Mandela's patronage) that large dams had displaced an estimated 40–80 million people worldwide, many neither counted nor compensated, with downstream livelihoods affected but unrecognised. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Commission_on_Dams ; https://wrm.org.uy/other-information/the-world-commission-on-dams-report (via search)]
- Three Gorges Dam: approved by the NPC in 1992 by 67.75% (1,767 for, 177 against, 664 abstaining); construction from 14 December 1994; dam body completed 2006; fully operational July 2012; 22,500 MW, the world's largest power station; displacement estimates 1.13 to about 1.4 million. Sun Yat-sen proposed such a dam in 1919. (China Today chapter covers it: cross-refer, do not repeat.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Gorges_Dam]

## Roads, highways and who gets bypassed (United States)
- In 1919 the US Army's Motor Transport Corps convoy took 62 days to cross 3,200 miles; Lt Col Dwight Eisenhower went along "through darkest America with truck and tank", and later admired Germany's Reichsautobahn. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_Highway_System]
- Federal-Aid Highway Act signed 29 June 1956; original authorisation $25 billion; the Highway Trust Fund, fed by gasoline tax, paid 90% of construction. Declared complete in 1992 (I-70 through Glenwood Canyon, 14 October 1992); final cost about $114 billion; 48,890 miles as of 2022; about a quarter of all US vehicle miles. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_Highway_System ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal-Aid_Highway_Act_of_1956]
- Between 1957 and 1977 Interstate construction displaced over 475,000 households and about one million people, routed often through cheap land in minority neighbourhoods and sometimes used to divide white from Black areas (Nashville, Miami, Chicago, Detroit among cases); freeway revolts cancelled or rerouted some urban routes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_Highway_System]
- Redlining: the National Housing Act of 1934 created the FHA; in 1935 the Federal Home Loan Bank Board asked the Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) to map 239 cities, grading areas A (green) to D (red, "hazardous"). FHA manuals warned against "inharmonious racial groups". Between 1945 and 1959 African Americans received less than 2% of federally insured home loans. The term "redlining" was coined by the sociologist John McKnight in the 1960s. The Fair Housing Act (1968), Home Mortgage Disclosure Act (1975) and Community Reinvestment Act followed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redlining]
- Robert Moses (18 December 1888 – 29 July 1981), never elected, ran New York's parks, parkways, bridges and expressways from the 1920s to the 1960s: Jones Beach (1929), the Long Island parkways, Triborough and Verrazzano bridges, Cross Bronx Expressway (completed 1963), Shea Stadium, Lincoln Center. He did not drive. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Moses]
- Robert Caro's *The Power Broker: Robert Moses and the Fall of New York* (Knopf, 16 September 1974; 1,336 pages; Pulitzer Prize) cast him as a power-hungry builder whose works hurt poor and minority communities. Caro alleged Moses built parkway overpasses too low for buses to keep poorer people from his beaches; the claim (made famous by Langdon Winner's essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" — essay title from memory, verify before use) is disputed: Steve Woolgar and Geoff Cooper call it an "urban legend", Kenneth T. Jackson rejects nefarious intent, though Moses's aide Sidney Shapiro said low bridges were meant to deter future commercial traffic. Three 2007 exhibitions (Hilary Ballon and Jackson) prompted a reappraisal; Jackson: "Every generation writes its own history." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Moses ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Power_Broker]
- Cross Bronx Expressway displacement figures conflict within WP (from "an estimated 1,500" to "as many as 60,000"); a 1940s estimate was 1,462 families for the central section. Use "thousands of families" or attribute Caro. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_Bronx_Expressway]
- Stephen Graham and Simon Marvin, *Splintering Urbanism: Networked Infrastructures, Technological Mobilities and the Urban Condition* (Routledge, 2001): the 20th-century ideal of standardised networks serving everyone is giving way to "premium network spaces" serving the well-off and bypassing the rest. [source: https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/1127251/splintering-urbanism-networked-infrastructures-technological-mobilities-and-the-urban-condition (via search)]

## Legibility and high modernism
- James C. Scott (2 December 1936 – 19 July 2024), Yale. *Seeing Like a State: How Certain Schemes to Improve the Human Condition Have Failed* (Yale UP, March 1998): states impose "legibility" (surnames, cadastres, censuses, standard measures) and "high modernist" schemes that ignore local practical knowledge (*metis*): Soviet collectivisation, Brasília, Tanzania's forced villagisation. Russell Hardin replied that collectivisation failed from bad incentives, not lack of local knowledge. (Quantitative Data chapter introduces Scott: cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seeing_Like_a_State ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_C._Scott]
- Tanzania: Nyerere's Arusha Declaration (5 February 1967); voluntary *ujamaa* villages became forced villagisation under Operation Vijiji, 1973–1975. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ujamaa]
- Tanganyika groundnut scheme: launched 1946 by the Attlee government to grow oilseeds for margarine on 3.21 million acres around Kongwa; ignored warnings on soil and rainfall; £36 million spent; abandoned 1951; called in 1953 "the worst fiasco in recent British colonial history". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanganyika_groundnut_scheme]

## Costs, white elephants, maintenance and collapse
- Bent Flyvbjerg's "iron law of megaprojects": "over budget, over time, under benefits, over and over again" (Oxford Handbook of Megaproject Management, 2017; a 2014 PMI summary uses "over budget, over time, over and over"); he told a UK parliamentary committee about one in ten major projects is on budget. Books: *Megaprojects and Risk: An Anatomy of Ambition* (2003), *How Big Things Get Done* (2023, with Dan Gardner). Critics say the overrun data can mislead. [source: https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/what-you-should-know-megaprojects-11656 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bent_Flyvbjerg]
- The counter-view: Albert O. Hirschman's "Principle of the Hiding Hand" (*Development Projects Observed*, Brookings, 1967): planners underestimate difficulties, and this ignorance lets projects start that later succeed because people's problem-solving ability is also underestimated. [source: https://www.brookings.edu/book/development-projects-observed/ (via search)]
- Boston's Big Dig: planned at $2.8 billion for completion in 1998; finished December 2007 at $14.6 billion; the most expensive US highway project. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Dig]
- Western "gridlock": California High-Speed Rail, authorised by 2008's Proposition 1A ($9 billion in bonds), has spent $13.8 billion (August 2025); Phase 1 is now estimated at $89–128 billion; the first Merced–Bakersfield segment is projected to open in 2033–34. Britain's HS2 lost its northern legs (cancelled by Rishi Sunak, October 2023); as of May 2026 the remaining line was expected to cost £87.7–102.7 billion (2025 prices), with London–Birmingham services from 2036–2039. (China's high-speed rail buildout is in the China Today chapter: cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_High-Speed_Rail ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Speed_2]
- "White elephant" derives from the sacred white elephants of Southeast Asian kings (Siam, Burma), costly to keep. Example: Sri Lanka's Mattala Rajapaksa International Airport (opened March 2013 in the president's home district; $209 million, $190 million of it high-interest Chinese Exim Bank loans), dubbed by Forbes "the world's emptiest international airport". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_elephant ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mattala_Rajapaksa_International_Airport]
- Collapse after deferred maintenance: Minneapolis I-35W bridge (opened 1967) collapsed 1 August 2007, killing 13 and injuring 145; the NTSB cited an undersized gusset plate (a design flaw) and load; it had been rated "structurally deficient" since 1990, like about 75,000 other US bridges in 2007. Genoa's Morandi bridge (opened 4 September 1967) partly collapsed on 14 August 2018, killing 43; its lightly prestressed concrete was vulnerable to corrosion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I-35W_Mississippi_River_bridge ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponte_Morandi]

## Belt and Road and infrastructure as geopolitics
- Xi Jinping announced the "Silk Road Economic Belt" at Nazarbayev University, Astana, on 7 September 2013, and the "21st-Century Maritime Silk Road" to Indonesia's parliament in October 2013. Written into the CCP constitution in 2017. Projects in more than 150 countries (146–150 members per Fudan University, May 2025). Withdrawals: Italy let its MoU lapse (March 2024), the Philippines dropped projects (November 2023), Panama withdrew (February 2025). Since about 2021 Beijing has promoted "small and beautiful" projects. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belt_and_Road_Initiative]
- Motives (per WP's synthesis): new markets, exporting surplus industrial capacity, resources, developing China's west, reducing reliance on the Strait of Malacca; Chinese loans usually fund Chinese contractors. Between 2017 and 2022 China built 34 terrestrial and dozens of undersea cables in 12 BRI countries (a "Digital Silk Road"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belt_and_Road_Initiative]
- Kenya's Mombasa–Nairobi Standard Gauge Railway opened 31 May 2017 (passengers 1 June) at $3.6 billion, built by China Road & Bridge Corporation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mombasa–Nairobi_Standard_Gauge_Railway]
- Hambantota: construction from January 2008, opened 18 November 2010; phase one financed by a China Exim loan of $306.7 million at 6.3%; in July 2017 China Merchants Port took a 70% stake and a 99-year lease for $1.12 billion, used to repay unrelated sovereign bonds. (China Today chapter covers it: cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hambantota_International_Port]

## Debates
- Hydraulic despotism: Wittfogel (1957) says irrigation made despotic states; Needham, Adams, Gernet and Carneiro reply that states came first and built the irrigation; Bali's subak shows irrigation run without a despot. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic_empire ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subak_(irrigation)]
- Infrastructure and empire: Headrick treats technology as an element, not the sole cause, of conquest; colonial railways (Uganda, Congo–Ocean) were built by indentured or forced labour for export and control. For India, Donaldson's econometrics find real income gains and less famine sensitivity; critics point to the guaranteed returns paid from Indian revenues and continued grain exports during famines. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_R._Headrick ; https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/southasia/2014/08/06/railroads-of-the-raj-estimating-the-impact-of-transportation-infrastructure ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_India]
- Moses: Caro (1974) as villain versus the 2007 reappraisal (Ballon, Jackson); the low-bridges story contested as an "urban legend" (Woolgar and Cooper). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Moses]
- Redlining: the HOLC maps are the famous symbol, but Fishback, Rose, Snowden and Storrs (NBER w29244, 2021; *Journal of Urban Economics* 2022) argue the FHA built its own redlining method before the HOLC maps and that the HOLC maps had little effect on lending; others still give the HOLC maps a major role. [source: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29244/w29244.pdf ; https://www.chicagofed.org/research/content-areas/mobility/policy-brief-federal-housing-programs-redlining (via search)]
- Rural electrification: New Deal success (Rayburn: 3% of farm homes in the early 1930s to 90% by 1959) versus Beito's view that private utilities were already spreading it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rural_Electrification_Act]
- Megaprojects: Flyvbjerg's "iron law" (systematic overruns, optimism bias) versus Hirschman's "Hiding Hand" (underestimation lets worthwhile projects begin). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bent_Flyvbjerg ; https://www.brookings.edu/book/development-projects-observed/]
- Debt-trap diplomacy: Brahma Chellaney coined the term in "China's Debt-Trap Diplomacy", Project Syndicate, 23 January 2017, with Sri Lanka as "exhibit A". Rebuttals: Brautigam and Rithmire (*The Atlantic*, February 2021: Hambantota was about 5% of Sri Lanka's 2017 debt service of $4.5 billion; Chinese banks have never seized an asset); Lee Jones and Shahar Hameiri (Chatham House); Rhodium Group (2019: in 40 renegotiations asset seizures "very rare"); Center for Global Development (2018: China restructured or waived payments for 51 debtors 2001–2017 without seizing assets). Sceptical counterpoints: AidData (2021) found unusual confidentiality clauses in 100 Chinese contracts; Lowy Institute (2025) found 54 developing countries owe more in repayments to China than to Paris Club lenders. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt-trap_diplomacy ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belt_and_Road_Initiative]
- Invisibility: Star's "visible upon breakdown" versus Larkin's emphasis on infrastructure as spectacle and promise. [source: https://blay.se/2016-03-26-the-anthropological-study-of-infrastructure.html]
