## 1. Names and definitions
- Officially "Mao Zedong Thought" (Mao Zedong sixiang). Mao himself rejected the term "Maoism", preferring "Mao Zedong Thought"; he held these ideas were not a complete system but still developing. [source: MAO-ISM]
- The English term "Maoism" was coined in 1951 by Benjamin I. Schwartz (*Chinese Communism and the Rise of Mao*), to describe Mao's strategy of a disciplined, hierarchical Communist Party resting on a mobilized peasant mass base. [source: MAO-ISM]
- The CCP distinguishes "Thought" (Mao Zedong Thought) from "Theory" (Deng Xiaoping Theory); "Thought" carries more weight. [source: MAO-ISM]
- "Marxism–Leninism–Maoism" is the later, universalised form adopted by movements abroad (e.g. Shining Path), claiming Maoism is applicable everywhere, as distinct from Mao Zedong Thought as "Marxism adapted to Chinese conditions". [source: MAO-ISM]
- Mao: born 26 December 1893 near Shaoshan, Hunan; died 9 September 1976. [source: MZ]

## 2. The sinification of Marxism: peasant revolution, Yan'an, mass line, continuous revolution
- **Hunan Report** (*Report on an Investigation of the Peasant Movement in Hunan*), published 28 March 1927 in the Central Committee periodical, after a visit of about 32 days. At a time when CCP strategy rested on urban workers (Chen Duxiu held the orthodox urban line), Mao argued for a revolution based on the poor peasantry and endorsed peasant violence against landlords. Its rhetoric was later taken up by the Naxalites and the Shining Path. [source: HUN]
- Hunan Report prophecy: "several hundred million peasants will rise like a mighty storm, like a hurricane, a force so swift and violent that no power, however great, will be able to hold it back." [source: HUN]
- "Political power grows out of the barrel of a gun": first used by Mao at an emergency CCP meeting on 7 August 1927, after Chiang Kai-shek's April 1927 break with the Communists (the White Terror; CCP membership fell from 60,000 to 10,000). Used again 6 November 1938; popularised via the *Quotations* (1964). [source: GUN]
- **People's war:** protracted war based in the countryside, building base areas, then mobile warfare, then "surrounding the cities from the countryside". [source: PW; MAO-ISM]
- **Sinicization of Marxism:** Mao first used the term in October 1938 in his report *On the New Stage* to the sixth plenum of the 6th Central Committee: "the universal truth of Marxism must have a national form before it can be useful". Chen Boda had used "Sinification of Marxism" earlier. [source: SIN] (Note: the SIN article's link label wrongly says "Communist Party of Vietnam"; the meeting was of the CCP.)
- Yan'an period (c. November 1935 – March 1947) after the Long March: Mao produced most works later canonised as Mao Zedong Thought (e.g. *Strategic Problems of China's Revolutionary War*, Dec 1936; *On Contradiction*; *On Practice*); the period established ideological independence from Moscow. [source: MAO-ISM]
- Mao's 1942 image: "The target is the Chinese revolution, the arrow is Marxism–Leninism." [source: MAO-ISM]
- **Yan'an Rectification Movement** (zhengfeng), 1942–1945: consolidated Mao's paramount role; "thought reform" (sixiang gaizao) through study groups, confession and criticism; Kang Sheng's July 1943 "Rescue the Fallen" speech drove the "Rescue Campaign" of forced confessions; the writer Wang Shiwei, who criticised hierarchy and privilege in Yan'an, was labelled a Trotskyist, arrested and beheaded in 1947. Regarded by many as the origin of Mao's personality cult. Historian Gao Hua's *How the Red Sun Rose* (2000) reinterpreted it as a power struggle. [source: YAN; RSR]
- Growth: the CCP arrived at Yan'an with under 10,000 worn-out troops; by the end of the period it had nearly 2.8 million members and governed 19 base areas with nearly 100 million people; by the mid-1940s the party was about 90% peasants. [source: YAN]
- 1945, 7th Party Congress: the first historical resolution made Mao Zedong Thought the party's unified ideology, written into the party constitution. [source: MAO-ISM; RES]
- **Mass line**: "from the masses, to the masses" — gather the scattered ideas of the masses, concentrate them through Marxist analysis, return them as policy, and repeat. Formally articulated in a CC resolution on methods of leadership of 1 June 1943; reflected in the slogan "Serve the People". Who coined the term is disputed (Li Lisan or Zhou Enlai). [source: ML; MAO-ISM]
- **Contradiction**: in *On Contradiction* and *On the Correct Handling of Contradictions Among the People* (1957), Mao held that contradiction is universal and that "the struggle of opposites is absolute". [source: MAO-ISM]
- **New Democracy**: a "national, popular, democratic, anti-feudal and anti-imperialist revolution, run by the communists" as the road to socialism in semi-colonial societies. [source: MAO-ISM]
- **Continuous revolution** (continuing revolution under the dictatorship of the proletariat): Mao first fully theorised it in 1956; class struggle continues and even intensifies under socialism, so revolution can never be "over"; it was the guiding ideology of the Cultural Revolution and was written into the preamble of the 1975 constitution. [source: CRT; MAO-ISM]
- Peasants as "a clean sheet of paper" ("a clean sheet of paper has no blotches, and so the newest and most beautiful words can be written on it"). [source: MAO-ISM]
- **Three Worlds Theory**: presented by Deng Xiaoping at the UN General Assembly's 6th Special Session on 10 April 1974: the US and USSR as the "first world", their allies the second, the non-imperialist poor countries the third. Grew from Mao's earlier "intermediate zones". [source: TWT; MAO-ISM]
- Edgar Snow's *Red Star Over China* (1937), based on months with the Red Army in 1936 (mostly at Bao'an), was the most influential Western book on China in the 1930s alongside *The Good Earth*; Lovell opens her global history with Snow. [source: RSOC; https://asianreviewofbooks.com/maoism-a-global-history-by-julia-lovell/ (search snippet)]

## 3. Maoism in power: land reform and collectivization
- **Land Reform** (1946–1953): land confiscated from landlords and redistributed; public struggle sessions and "speaking bitterness"; landlords killed by the CCP and former tenants. Death toll estimates range "from hundreds of thousands to millions" (Cambridge History of China cites a possible 200,000–800,000; other estimates much higher). Over 25 million hectares redistributed by 1949; Brian DeMare argues land reform was critical to CCP victory in the civil war. [source: LRM]
- Mao in April 1956 (per Red Guard collections): "two to three million counter-revolutionaries had been executed, imprisoned or placed under control in the past" — note this mixes deaths with imprisonment. [source: LRM]
- Collectivization stages: mutual aid teams (5–15 households) → from 1953 elementary cooperatives (20–40 households) → from 1956 "higher cooperatives" (100–300 families) → 1958 communes. Taxation and compulsory grain purchase took about 30% of the harvest by 1957. The hukou household registration system restricted movement from 1956 (dated so in GLF). [source: GLF]
- **Hundred Flowers** (1956–57): "Let a hundred flowers bloom; let a hundred schools of thought contend"; followed by the **Anti-Rightist Campaign** from 8 June 1957, persecuting at least 550,000 people, mostly intellectuals. Whether Hundred Flowers was a deliberate trap is debated ("some scholars retrospectively concluded" it was a ploy). [source: HFC; GLF; MZ]

## 4. The Great Leap Forward (1958–1962)
- Unveiled by Mao at a Nanjing meeting in January 1958; aimed to transform China from agrarian to industrial through mass mobilisation and people's communes; slogan of the General Line: "go all out, aim high, and build socialism with greater, faster, better and more economical results". Mao forecast surpassing Britain's industrial output within 15 years. [source: GLF]
- First experimental commune: Chayashan, Henan, April 1958 (private plots abolished, communal kitchens). August 1958 Politburo (Beidaihe) decided to spread communes; by year's end about 25,000 communes averaging 5,000 households. [source: GLF]
- Backyard furnaces: the 1958 steel target of 10.7 million tons was "reached", but more than 3 million tons was unusable; peasants melted down cooking utensils. [source: GLF]
- Inflated reports: local officials announced ever higher "sputnik" yields; procurement was set on false figures. Dikötter says officials often reported 30–40% above actual output; Yang Jisheng gives a Henan example of 1,200 jin per mu claimed vs about 600 actual. [source: GLF]
- Grain output: about 200 million tons (1958) → 170 million (1959) → 143.5 million (1960) per Chinese government data. [source: GCF]
- **Lushan Conference** (July–August 1959): Marshal Peng Dehuai alone openly criticised the Leap; Mao dismissed him as defence minister (replaced by Lin Biao) and launched the "Anti-Right Deviation Struggle"; over 3 million party members purged or penalised. Misreporting and coercive procurement intensified afterwards. [source: GLF; LUS]
- China remained a net grain exporter 1958–60; Ashton et al. calculated that net grain exports in 1959 and 1960 would have fed 16 million people at 2,000 calories a day. Yang Jisheng found some 22 million tons of grain in public granaries at the height of the famine. [source: GLF]
- The worst-hit provinces by death rate: Anhui (18% dead), Chongqing (15%), Sichuan (13%), Guizhou (11%), Hunan (8%) (GCF figures). Xinyang prefecture (Henan) is the notorious case: people died at granary doors; the post office confiscated 1,200 letters begging for help. [source: GCF; GLF]
- **Seven Thousand Cadres Conference**, Beijing, 11 January – 7 February 1962: Liu Shaoqi attributed the disaster "30% to natural disasters, 70% to man-made errors" (三分天灾，七分人祸); Mao made a self-criticism and stepped back, leaving the economy to Liu and Deng; Lin Biao continued to praise Mao. [source: 7K; GCF]
  **Writer: do not confuse Liu's 1962 "30% nature / 70% human error" verdict on the famine with the later "70% right / 30% wrong" verdict on Mao (section 8).**
- Official naming: until the early 1980s the famine was called the "Three Years of Natural Disasters"; in June 1981 the party renamed it "Three Years of Difficulty" and attributed it mainly to the Leap and the Anti-Right Deviation Struggle. [source: GCF]
- Mao told Field Marshal Montgomery in autumn 1961 that "unnatural deaths" exceeded 5 million in 1960–61 (per a declassified CIA report). [source: GCF; GLF]
- Population fell in 1960 and 1961 — only the third time in 600 years China's population decreased (Banister). [source: GLF]
- Dams built in the Leap at Zhumadian, Henan, collapsed in 1975 (Banqiao), deaths estimated from tens of thousands to 240,000. [source: GLF]

### 4a. The famine death toll: estimates and who gives them
Most estimates are of "excess deaths" against a baseline death rate, so they depend on the baseline chosen, on flawed data (statistics taken over by cadres in 1957; State Statistical Bureau material burned in the Cultural Revolution) and on assumptions about migration and under-registration. [source: GLF]
- Ansley Coale (1984): 27 million (demographic, 1958–63). [source: GCF; GLF]
- Basil Ashton, Kenneth Hill, Alan Piazza, Robin Zeitz (1984): about 30 million premature deaths 1958–62, plus 33 million births lost or postponed; "It would not be inaccurate to say that 30 million people died prematurely as a result of errors of internal policy and flawed international relations." [source: GCF; GLF]
- Judith Banister (1987, *China's Changing Population*): 30 million excess deaths 1958–61; Jasper Becker called it "the most reliable estimate we have". [source: GCF; GLF]
- Peng Xizhe (1987): 23 million. [source: GCF]
- Jasper Becker, *Hungry Ghosts* (1996): most estimates 30–60 million; "50 and 60 million deaths were cited at internal meetings of senior Party officials". [source: GCF]
- Chen Yizi (adviser to Zhao Ziyang, 1994): 43–46 million, from an unpublished internal investigation; unverifiable (Ó Gráda, Riskin); Chris Bramall and Gao Mobo call it politically motivated. [source: GCF; GLF]
- Cao Shuji (2005): 32.5 million, from local gazetteers covering 1,050 counties. [source: GCF]
- Jung Chang and Jon Halliday (2005): 38 million (Stuart Schram thought this "may well be the most accurate"); Rummel accepted 38 million. [source: GLF]
- Yang Jisheng, *Tombstone* (Hong Kong 2008; English 2012): 36 million deaths 1958–62 plus 40 million births lost — "China's total population loss during the Great Famine then comes to 76 million"; he calls 36 million a figure that "approaches the reality but is still too low". Ó Gráda argues a fertility survey points to a total perhaps ten million lower. [source: GCF; GLF; YJ]
- Frank Dikötter, *Mao's Great Famine* (2010): at least 45 million premature deaths 1958–62, of which at least 2.5 million (elsewhere "two to three million") beaten, tortured or summarily killed; he judged Chen Yizi's 43–46 million "in all likelihood a reliable estimate". Critics: Felix Wemheuer (says the figure comes from adding 40–50% to Cao's estimate), Cormac Ó Gráda, Andrew Walder, Anthony Garnaut (sampling falls short of best practice), Daniel Vukovich (the 2.5 million rests on an unpublished interview figure). Won the 2011 Samuel Johnson Prize. [source: GCF; GLF; MGF]
- Lower estimates: Houser, Sands and Xiao (2009): 15.4 million (elsewhere 14.8), 69% attributable to national policy, 31% to weather; Chinese Academy of Sciences team (1989): at least 15 million; Li Chengrui (former head of the State Statistical Bureau, 1997): 22 million. [source: GCF; GLF]
- Outliers: Yu Xiguang (2015) 55 million; Utsa Patnaik 11 million; Sun Jingxian 3.66 million; Yang Songlin 2.6–4 million (argues registration artefacts); Cheng Enfu and Zhan Zhihua ~5 million. These very low figures are rejected by most demographers. [source: GCF; GLF]
- Summary statements: GCF infobox 15–46 million, "newer estimates tend to fall in a range between 30 and 46 million"; GLF lead 15–55 million. Gao Mobo notes anti-communist writers tend to push the figure up and sympathisers down. [source: GCF; GLF]
- Weather dispute: Yang Jisheng (using 350 weather stations) and Chinese meteorological data say 1959–61 weather was within normal range; Houser et al. and others find weather mattered in some provinces; the 1959 Yellow River flood and Dikötter's argument that many floods came from badly built Leap irrigation works. [source: GCF]
- Amartya Sen: "no substantial famine has ever occurred in a democratic country"; but also that India's ordinary peacetime excess mortality "fill[s] its cupboard with more skeletons every eight years than China put there in its years of shame." [source: GCF]
- Disputed quotation: Dikötter quotes Mao at a secret meeting in Shanghai, 25 March 1959: "When there is not enough to eat, people starve to death. It is better to let half of the people die so that the other half can eat their fill." Thomas Bernstein calls it hyperbole; Anthony Garnaut argues that in context Mao was speaking about cutting the 1,078 major industrial projects to 500. **Use only with this caveat.** [source: MGF]

## 5. The Cultural Revolution in brief (separate chapter covers it)
- Dates: 16 May 1966 (the "16 May Notification") to Mao's death in 1976 (arrest of the Gang of Four, 6 October 1976). Mao said "bourgeois elements" had infiltrated party and state; called youth to "Bombard the Headquarters" (his own big-character poster, August 1966, aimed at Liu Shaoqi) and declared "to rebel is justified". Red August 1966 in Beijing. [source: CR]
- Death toll estimates vary: commonly 1–2 million (CR lead); Andrew Walder (2014, county annals, 1966–71) 1.1–1.6 million; CCP History Research Center (1996) 1.728 million "unnatural deaths"; Rummel much higher. Walder: most deaths came after the mass movements ended, in organised campaigns to restore order: "The cure for factional warfare was far worse than the disease." Over 10 million urban youth sent down to the countryside. Liu Shaoqi, denounced as a traitor, died in 1969 after being denied medicine. [source: CR; GCF]
- Lin Biao in the Cultural Revolution: "the atom bomb of the spirit is much more powerful and more useful than the material atom bomb." [source: MAO-ISM]
- Continuous revolution as its theory: see section 2. [source: CRT]

## 6. The cult: Little Red Book, badges, posters
- *Quotations from Chairman Mao* (Mao zhuxi yulu; in China the "Treasured Red Book", hong bao shu) began in the PLA under Lin Biao: from 1961 *PLA Daily* printed a Mao quote daily; first internal edition May 1964 (30 topics, 355 quotations) with Lin Biao's endorsement; the definitive 1965 version had 427 quotations in 33 chapters drawn from 1929–1964; red vinyl covers from August 1965. [source: LRB]
- Print run: initial PLA run 4.2 million; 12 million by August 1965; one million copies given to Red Guards in Beijing on National Day 1966. Overall figures disputed: "over a billion" official copies 1966–69 alone (plus untold unofficial reprints); others say "billions"; some claim over 6.5 billion. It was the most printed book in the world from the mid-1960s to the mid-1970s. **Say "more than a billion copies" with confidence; flag higher totals as claims.** [source: LRB]
- Abroad: by May 1967 sold in bookstores in 117 countries and territories; translated into more than 50 languages. In March 1966 China had briefly tried to recall foreigners' copies. [source: LRB]
- Decline: emphasis waned after the 9th Party Congress (April 1969); Lin Biao's preface torn out after his death in September 1971; on 12 February 1979 the Publicity Department halted publication, calling it a distortion of Mao Zedong Thought with "widespread and pernicious influence". [source: LRB]
- In Africa it inspired *Quotations from President Karume* and *Axioms of Kwame Nkrumah*. [source: LRB]
- **Mao badges:** first homemade at Yan'an in the 1930s (from toothpaste-tube metal); exploded in 1966, especially after Red Guards presented badges to Mao at the Tiananmen rally of 18 August 1966; Shanghai output rose from 32,000 (July 1966) to 1.3 million (September 1966); an estimated 2–5 billion produced 1968–71 ("several billion" overall). Wearing was a test of loyalty; "class enemies" were forbidden to wear them; some pinned them into their skin. Aluminium use prompted Mao's "Give me back the airplanes"; a June 1969 CC document banned unauthorised production; wearing declined after Lin Biao's death (1971). [source: BADGE]
- Posters: Cultural Revolution propaganda art showed nearly every figure holding the Little Red Book, often with Mao badges; the book was "the single most visible icon in mainland China, even more visible than images of Mao himself". [source: LRB]
- The 1981 Resolution criticised Mao for developing a cult of personality in his later years. [source: RES]

## 7. Global Maoism
### 7a. Framework (Lovell)
- Julia Lovell (b. 1975), professor of Modern Chinese History and Literature, Birkbeck, University of London. *Maoism: A Global History* (2019; Bodley Head in UK, 606 pp.; Knopf in US) won the 2019 Cundill History Prize and was shortlisted for the Baillie Gifford Prize. [source: LOV; https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/76484/ (search snippet for publisher/pages)]
- Her stated aim: to challenge the assumptions "that Maoism is a story of China; and ... that Maoism is a story of the past." Chapters span Peru, Indonesia, Africa, Southeast Asia, India and Nepal, and the West. [source: https://fivebooks.com/best-books/maoism-julia-lovell/ (search snippet)]
- Reception: Ian Johnson (NYT) praised it as the first comprehensive accessible history; Andrew Nathan (Foreign Affairs) and Jacobin argued she overstates Mao's influence: "Mao often served as a symbol for activists who did things their own way, including the Black Panthers in the United States and the Shining Path in Peru." Julian Gewirtz (Harper's): it shows "just how difficult it is to export a political idea wholesale". [source: LOV]
- After the Sino-Soviet split (from 1962) pro-Chinese parties split off worldwide, often named "Communist Party (Marxist–Leninist)"; Albania sided with China; among Western established communist parties only New Zealand's did. After 1976 the international movement split three ways (pro-Deng, anti-Deng, pro-Albanian/Hoxha); Deng's China showed little interest in foreign Maoists. [source: MAO-ISM]
- China gave military training in Mao's guerrilla doctrine to Palestinian fedayeen yearly 1965–71. [source: PW]

### 7b. India: Naxalbari and the Naxalites
- Naxalbari uprising, Darjeeling district, West Bengal, 1967: peasant land seizures from jotedars from March 1967, led by Charu Majumdar, Kanu Sanyal and the Santhal (Adivasi) leader Jangal Santhal (the "Siliguri group"), against the CPI(M), which was then in the state coalition government. On 25 May 1967 police fire killed nine women and one child. Paramilitary forces crushed it from 19 July 1967. [source: NAXB; NAX]
- China's CCP backed the uprising, wrecking relations with the CPI(M). The CPI(Marxist–Leninist) was founded April 1969 (announced by Sanyal in Calcutta). [source: NAXB; NAX]
- Charu Majumdar (1918–28 July 1972; author of the "Historic Eight Documents") made the *Quotations* central, requiring it to be read aloud to illiterate peasants; he declared an "annihilation line" of assassinating individual "class enemies"; Calcutta students joined. He died in police custody 28 July 1972 (official cause heart attack; Naxalites call it custodial murder). By 1973 the movement had fractured (eventually 40+ groups). [source: CM; NAX; MAO-ISM]
- Revival: People's War Group (founded 22 April 1980 by Kondapalli Seetharamaiah, Andhra Pradesh). The CPI (Maoist) is now the leading organisation and is banned as terrorist under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act; PLGA armed wing founded 2 December 2000. [source: NAX; MAO-ISM]
- Manmohan Singh called the Naxalites the "single biggest internal security challenge ever faced by our country". The "Red Corridor" peaked at nearly 180 districts in the late 2000s; 2009–10 the deadliest years (>1,000 dead annually); Operation Green Hunt from 2009. [source: NAX]
- Deaths: South Asia Terrorism Portal: more than 12,102 killed 2000–2025 (4,134 civilians, 2,722 security personnel, 5,001 Naxalites); BBC: more than 6,000 in 1990–2010; Al Jazeera: 10,000 in 1980–2011; MAO-ISM gives 13,834 total as of 2018. Figures depend on period counted. [source: NAX; MAO-ISM]
- Very recent (verify before publishing): on 30 March 2026 the Indian government declared victory over the insurgency (influence down to 3 districts by early 2026; >10,000 surrenders 2015–25); a few senior commanders vowed to fight on. Treat as a government claim. [source: NAX]

### 7c. Peru: Shining Path
- Founded 1969 by Abimael Guzmán (3 December 1934 – 11 September 2021), philosophy professor at San Cristóbal of Huamanga University, Ayacucho; nom de guerre "Presidente Gonzalo"; split from Red Flag (Bandera Roja). Guzmán attended cadre training in China in 1965 and returned in 1967, during the Cultural Revolution, influenced by the future Gang of Four. Its doctrine became "Marxism–Leninism–Maoism–Gonzalo Thought"; followers called him the "Fourth Sword of Communism" (after Lenin, Stalin, Mao). [source: SP; AG]
- The 1976 fall of the Gang of Four convinced Guzmán China had become revisionist. On 26 December 1980 dead dogs were found hanging from Lima lampposts with signs such as "Deng Xiaoping son of a bitch" (Teng Siao Ping hijo de perra), attributed to Shining Path sympathisers (7 dogs per DOGS infobox; some accounts place a similar episode earlier in 1980). [source: AG; DOGS; https://ageofrevolutions.com/2021/06/21/the-dogs-of-war-the-animals-of-the-internal-armed-conflict-in-peru-1980-2000/ (search snippet)]
- War began 17 May 1980 with the burning of ballot boxes at Chuschi on the eve of Peru's first elections in 12 years. About 500 members in 1980; about 3,000 armed members at peak (1990). Lucanamarca massacre (April 1983) as reprisal against rondas. Tarata Street bombing, Miraflores, Lima, 16 July 1992: 25 killed. [source: SP]
- Guzmán captured 12 September 1992 in Surquillo, Lima, by the GEIN police intelligence unit (Operation Victoria); life sentence by secret military court, then civilian retrial 13 October 2006 (life). [source: SP; AG]
- Women: 40% of fighters and 50% of the Central Committee were women (SP claim). Carlos Iván Degregori argued Shining Path treated violence as a value in itself, beyond typical Maoist bounds. [source: SP; MAO-ISM]
- **Truth and Reconciliation Commission** (final report 28 August 2003; president Salomón Lerner Febres; Degregori a commissioner): estimated 69,280 dead or disappeared 1980–2000 (earlier assumption ~24,000). Attribution: Shining Path about 54% of the deaths and disappearances *reported to the commission*; the statistical estimate attributes 31,331 (46%) of the 69,280 to Shining Path; the state about 37% (TRC article; HRW summary "roughly one-third"); MRTA 1.5%. Rural people were 29% of the population but 79% of victims; 75% of the dead spoke Quechua or another indigenous first language (16% of the population). 83% of sexual violence was attributed to state agents. [source: TRC; SP]
- Dissent: a 2019 study estimated about 48,000 killings and that the state accounts for a significantly larger share than Shining Path; the TRC responded. [source: SP]
  **Writer: say Shining Path was the principal perpetrator according to the TRC. Do not call this share unique among Latin American truth commissions; that comparison is not verified here.**

### 7d. Nepal: the People's War, 1996–2006
- CPN (Maoist), led by Pushpa Kamal Dahal ("Prachanda", "fierce"; b. 11 December 1954), with Baburam Bhattarai, who delivered 40 demands to the government on 4 February 1996. The war began 13 February 1996 with seven simultaneous attacks in six districts (incl. Holeri police station, Rolpa). [source: NEP; PRA]
- King Gyanendra seized direct power on 1 February 2005; the April 2006 mass movement forced him to restore parliament; ceasefire 26 April 2006; Comprehensive Peace Accord 21 November 2006. [source: NEP; PRA]
- Toll: more than 17,000 killed (infobox 17,800–20,000), including over 4,000 killed by Maoists and over 8,200 by government forces 1996–2005; 100,000–150,000 displaced; 3,093 enforced-disappearance complaints. Note: the state killed about twice as many as the Maoists. [source: NEP]
- Afterwards: Maoists became the largest party in the 2008 Constituent Assembly election; the monarchy was abolished (republic 28 May 2008 per PRA's office dates); Prachanda became prime minister in August 2008 and served three terms (2008–09, 2016–17, Dec 2022–July 2024). [source: PRA]
- Nepal's Maoists drew on Shining Path's ideology and the Revolutionary Internationalist Movement. [source: SP]

### 7e. Cambodia: the Khmer Rouge's dark variant
- Pol Pot (Saloth Sâr, 1925–1998) read Mao (especially *On New Democracy*) in Paris; visited China in 1965 for several months; met Mao in Beijing in June 1975. Mao's recorded advice (Short, *Pol Pot*): "You have a lot of experience. It's better than ours. We don't have the right to criticise you … So rectify yourselves; do rectification! … The road is tortuous." [source: PP]
- China was the regime's main patron: at least 90% of its foreign aid; Mao pledged US$1 billion in military and economic aid with an immediate $20 million grant (1975); thousands of Chinese advisers; but China had "little influence on Pol Pot's domestic policies" (Ciorciari). After 1979 China (with the US, Thailand, ASEAN) backed the Khmer Rouge–dominated coalition holding Cambodia's UN seat. [source: KR; PP; CG]
- Maoist traits: peasants rather than proletariat as revolutionary base, Great Leap-type campaigns (the "Moha Lout Plaoh", Super Great Leap Forward), communal eating, voluntarism; but in a more extreme form, fused with xenophobic Khmer nationalism; Ben Kiernan: "neither a communist proletarian revolution that privileged the working class, nor a peasant revolution that favored all farmers". Pol Pot boasted the Cambodian revolution was "brilliant red" while Mao's standard "has faded and is wavering". Some Maoists and Marxists deny the regime was Maoist. [source: KR; PP; MAO-ISM]
- Toll: 1.5–2 million dead by January 1979, 20–25% of the 1975 population (infobox range 1.2–2.2 million), including 200,000–300,000 ethnic Chinese. [source: CG]

### 7f. Africa and the Third World
- Tanzania: Nyerere's Arusha Declaration (5 February 1967) and ujamaa drew on Maoist themes of self-reliance, mass politics and the centrality of the peasantry, and borrowed symbols such as the Long March; forced villagization (Operation Vijiji) 1973–75. [source: UJ; MAO-ISM]
- TAZARA (Tanzam / "Uhuru" railway), 1,860 km, Dar es Salaam to Kapiri Mposhi, Zambia: agreement signed in Beijing 6 September 1967; interest-free loan of RMB 988 million (c. US$406 million); built 1970–1975; China's largest foreign-aid project at the time; about 50,000 Chinese personnel sent 1965–76 (30,000–40,000 workers) and about 60,000 Africans; purpose to free Zambian copper from routes through white-ruled Rhodesia and South Africa. The West called it the "bamboo railway". [source: TAZ]
- Comoros under Ali Soilih was a Maoist-influenced state (militia partly inspired by the Red Guards). [source: MAO-ISM]
- Also influenced: Communist Party of the Philippines (founded 26 December 1968, Mao's birthday, by Jose Maria Sison; New People's Army); Turkey's TKP/ML; Afghanistan's Progressive Youth Organization (1965); Bangladesh's Siraj Sikder. [source: MAO-ISM]

### 7g. The West
- **France:** a Maoist circle formed at the École Normale Supérieure in 1964 among Louis Althusser's students; Union of Marxist-Leninist Communist Youth (December 1966); after May 68 the Gauche prolétarienne (Proletarian Left) was the most visible Maoist group (dissolved 1974); Jean-Paul Sartre nominally edited its newspaper after its editor was arrested; Michel Foucault was active in its Prison Information Group; French Maoists were early champions of gay and lesbian rights and fed the feminist movement. Alain Badiou is the central intellectual of French Maoism's legacy. May 68 slogan: "Marx, Mao, Marcuse!" [source: MAO-ISM; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_68]
- Godard's *La Chinoise* (1967), about young Paris Maoists, released months before May 68; Grand Jury Prize, Venice 1967. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Chinoise (search snippet)]
- Tel Quel delegation to China, 1974 (about three weeks, tightly managed): Philippe Sollers, Julia Kristeva, Roland Barthes, Marcelin Pleynet, François Wahl; Barthes's notebooks later published as *Travels in China*. [source: https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/the-sideways-gaze-roland-barthess-travels-in-china/ (search snippet)]
- Italy: Servire il Popolo modelled itself on the Red Guards; Red Brigades and Lotta Continua cited Mao. West Germany: K-Groups. Portugal: MRPP; José Manuel Durão Barroso was a Maoist in the 1970s. [source: MAO-ISM]
- **United States:** Mao's statement of 8 August 1963, issued at the request of the exiled Robert F. Williams, supported Black Americans against racial discrimination. In 1967 Bobby Seale and Huey Newton bought *Quotations* at a San Francisco Chinese bookstore and resold them at UC Berkeley to buy guns. Every Black Panther had to study the Little Red Book; "serve the people" survival programmes (Free Breakfast for Children). Newton led a delegation to China in late September 1971 for 10 days, met Zhou Enlai (not Mao), and called China "a free and liberated territory with a socialist government". Maoism was a major influence on the New Communist Movement (Revolutionary Communist Party, October League). [source: LRB; BPP; MAO-ISM; https://digitalarchive.wilsoncenter.org/document/240255 (search snippet)]

## 8. Afterlife: Deng's and Xi's China
- **1981 Resolution** (*Resolution on Certain Questions in the History of Our Party since the Founding of the People's Republic of China*): adopted unanimously 27 June 1981 by the 6th Plenum of the 11th Central Committee (the second of the party's three historical resolutions: 1945, 1981, 2021); drafted under Hu Qiaomu over about 15 months, repeatedly revised to Deng's views; Hua Guofeng resigned as chairman the day before. [source: RES]
- Key wording: "Chief responsibility for the grave 'Left' error of the 'cultural revolution,' an error comprehensive in magnitude and protracted in duration, does indeed lie with Comrade Mao Zedong"; yet Mao's contributions "far outweigh his mistakes": "His merits are primary and his errors secondary." It affirmed 1949–56, described setbacks 1957–64 and major mistakes from 1965, blamed Lin Biao, Jiang Qing and Kang Sheng, criticised the cult, rehabilitated Liu Shaoqi, and kept "the guidance of Mao Zedong Thought" among the Four Cardinal Principles. It called the Cultural Revolution "responsible for the most severe setback and the heaviest losses suffered by the people, the country, and the party since the founding of the People's Republic". [source: RES; MAO-ISM; CR]
- **The "70% right, 30% wrong" formula — careful:** it does **not** appear in the 1981 Resolution. Timothy Cheek: "the interpretation has evolved that this was its point." It is commonly attributed to Deng Xiaoping (MZ: "first attributed to Deng"). In his August 1980 interview with Oriana Fallaci Deng said of Mao: "In evaluating his merits and mistakes, we hold that his mistakes were only secondary." The formula itself goes back to Mao, who in 1956 applied a 70:30 achievements-to-mistakes ratio to Stalin (secondary sources; primary wording not verified), and reportedly said he would be satisfied with a 70–30 verdict on himself (not verified). **Write: "the shorthand that Mao was '70 per cent right and 30 per cent wrong', usually credited to Deng and echoing the ratio Mao himself had applied to Stalin, is not in the Resolution's text, but became its popular summary."** [source: RES; MZ; EVAL; https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/e/9781003069683/chapters/10.4324/9781003069683-43 (search snippet, Stalin 70/30)]
- Deng redefined the "true spirit" of Mao Zedong Thought as "seek truth from facts"; separated Mao the fallible man from the Thought. Matthew Galway: "The CPC did not de-Maoize but instead reassessed his legacy". Followed by a three-year campaign to "totally negate" the Cultural Revolution. [source: MAO-ISM; RES]
- Chen Yun: "Had Mao died in 1956, his achievements would have been immortal. Had he died in 1966, he would still have been a great man but flawed. But he died in 1976. Alas, what can one say?" [source: MZ]
- Global Times poll, December 2013: about 85% of 1,045 respondents felt Mao's achievements outweighed his mistakes. Rising nostalgia among low-income Chinese for Mao-era egalitarianism (as of 2025). [source: MZ]
- Neo-Maoism: Bo Xilai in Chongqing (red songs, mass mobilisation); leftist activism such as the Jasic incident (2018); Maoist posts by young people revived amid inequality and "996" overwork, sometimes censored. [source: MAO-ISM]
- **Xi:** in a 2013 speech Xi summarised Mao Zedong Thought as seeking truth from facts, the mass line, and independence; Xi Jinping Thought (named at the 19th Congress, October 2017) is presented as building on Marxism–Leninism and Mao Zedong Thought; the November 2021 third historical resolution says it "achieved a new leap in the Sinicization of Marxism" and largely echoes the 1981 verdict on Mao. [source: MAO-ISM; SIN; XJT; RES]
- Lovell argues that since 2012 the party has "publicly renormalised aspects of Maoist political culture — political self-criticisms, the personality cult, the 'mass-line' and rectification campaigns", while Xi suppresses the bottom-up mobilisation of the Cultural Revolution. [source: https://asianreviewofbooks.com/maoism-a-global-history-by-julia-lovell/ (search snippet)]

## Debates
- **Famine toll:** demographers (Coale 27m; Ashton et al. and Banister 30m; Peng 23m) vs archive-based historians (Cao 32.5m; Yang 36m; Dikötter 45m+) vs very low revisionist figures (Sun Jingxian, Yang Songlin: under 5m). Most scholarship now c. 30–45 million. [source: GCF; GLF]
- **Intent and responsibility:** Dikötter and Rosefielde stress coercion, violence and "terror-starvation" and that the leadership knew; others (Bernstein, Garnaut, Wemheuer) stress policy failure, hyperbole taken out of context, and collective responsibility (historian Tao Kai: the Leap "wasn't the problem of a single person"). Weather's role disputed (Yang Jisheng: negligible; Houser et al.: ~31%). [source: GLF; GCF; MGF]
- **Was the Hundred Flowers a trap?** Some (e.g. Chang and Halliday) see it as a ploy to flush out critics; contested. [source: GLF]
- **How Maoist were global Maoisms?** Lovell stresses Mao's global reach; Andrew Nathan and Jacobin say she overstates it, with Mao often a symbol for movements that did things their own way. [source: LOV]
- **Khmer Rouge:** Maoist in its peasant base and Great Leap imitation (Gidley; Vietnam's contemporary view), versus a racialised nationalist project sui generis (Kiernan) on which China had little domestic influence (Ciorciari). [source: KR; PP]
- **Peru's dead:** TRC (69,280; Shining Path principal perpetrator) vs 2019 study (c. 48,000; state share larger). [source: TRC; SP]
- **The Mao verdict in China:** official 1981 balance ("merits primary"); Galway: reassessment not de-Maoization; critics outside China see it as legitimising a return to capitalism; Lovell sees a partial Maoist revival under Xi. [source: MAO-ISM; RES]
- **Death tolls under Mao overall:** Chang and Halliday's "as many as 70 million" peacetime deaths is at the top; Sorace, Franceschini and Loubere critique how such tolls are framed. [source: MAO-ISM]
