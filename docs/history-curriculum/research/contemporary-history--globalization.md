## 1. Definitions and the deep history (keep brief)
- The word: the first use of "globalization" in its modern sense is credited to French economist François Perroux in the early 1960s (French *mondialisation*); Theodore Levitt is "sometimes inaccurately cited" as inventing it in the mid-1980s but popularised it among business readers. It came into popular use in the 1990s. [source: GLOB]
- Periodisation debate: Andre Gunder Frank (dependency theorist) pushed "archaic globalization" back to Sumer–Indus trade in the third millennium BCE; critics say this rests on an over-broad definition. 1600–1800 is sometimes called "proto-globalization". [source: HG]
- Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giráldez date globalization to the 16th century — the first circumnavigation (Magellan–Elcano) and the global silver trade, with China at its centre ("China and the Birth of Globalization in the 16th Century"). [source: GLOB; HG]
- Kevin O'Rourke and Jeffrey Williamson, "When did globalisation begin?", *European Review of Economic History* 6:1 (2002): large-scale globalization began in the 1820s (commodity-price convergence); a transport revolution between 1820 and 1850 (steamships, railways). Globalization in this period "was decisively shaped by nineteenth-century imperialism and colonialism". [source: GLOB]
- O'Rourke, Prados de la Escosura and Daudin list factors promoting globalization 1815–1870: post-Napoleonic peace; transport innovations cutting trade costs; new industrial military technologies (used to force open markets). [source: GLOB]
- Keynes's 1919 passage ("The inhabitant of London could order by telephone, sipping his morning tea, the various products of the whole earth … What an extraordinary episode in the economic progress of man was that age which came to an end in August 1914") — already used in published chapters; at most a one-line echo. [source: HG]
- First wave of deglobalization 1914–1945: WWI, Depression, Smoot–Hawley (1930), British Imperial Preference, WWII. [source: DEG]

## 2. Trade-openness statistics (with caveats)
- World trade (exports + imports) as % of world GDP (World Bank series via IndexMundi): lowest 25.0% in 1970; peak 61.0% in 2008; fell to 52.5% in 2009; 60.2% by 2011. [source: WebSearch: https://www.indexmundi.com/facts/world/trade]
- Chicago Fed alternative measure (trade as share of final global expenditure): ~19% in 1970, peak 55% in 2008. Different denominators give different levels; all agree on a rise to a 2008 peak. [source: WebSearch: https://www.chicagofed.org/publications/chicago-fed-letter/2021/456]
- Long run (Federico & Tena-Junguito, World Trade Historical Database): world export/GDP ratio for 38 countries ~12.5% in 1913 vs ~22.5% in 2007 (about 80% higher); a 36-polity sample gives 6.3% (1913) and 11.5% (2007) — do not mix the two series. Openness collapsed in the Depression (about 30%) back to mid-19th-century levels; grew fast from 1995 to an all-time peak in 2008. [source: WebSearch: https://weforum.org/stories/2016/04/whats-the-future-of-globalization-perhaps-history-can-give-us-a-clue; https://cepr.org/article/world-trade-historical-database]
- "Peak globalisation" caveat: one VoxEU analysis attributes about 60% of the post-2008 fall in the goods-trade/GDP share to falling value of mining and fuel trade, not retreat from integration. [source: WebSearch: https://voxeu.org/voxeu/columns/peak-globalisation-myth-part-2-why-goods-trade-ratio-declined]
- Great Trade Collapse (Q3 2008–Q2 2009): world GDP fell ~1% while world trade fell ~10%, synchronised across almost every country; causes cited: demand/supply drops, credit constraints, disrupted global value chains. [source: GTC]

## 3. Bretton Woods and GATT (post-1945 architecture)
- Bretton Woods Conference (United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference): delegates of 44 Allied countries at the Mount Washington Hotel, Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, 1–22 July 1944; created the IMF and the IBRD (now part of the World Bank Group). The US, holding two-thirds of the world's gold, insisted the system rest on gold and the dollar; dollar fixed at $35 per ounce. [source: BW]
- Keynes (UK) proposed forcing surplus countries to adjust ("use-it-or-lose-it"); Harry Dexter White (US Treasury) rejected his plan. Brad DeLong: on almost every point where Keynes was overruled, he was later proved right. [source: BW]
- End: on 15 August 1971 the US ended dollar convertibility to gold, effectively ending the system. [source: BW] (Matches overview's "closed the gold window (1971)".)
- GATT: signed by 23 nations in Geneva on 30 October 1947, applied provisionally from 1 January 1948; nine negotiating rounds 1947–1995. Average tariffs of major GATT participants ~22% in 1947, ~5% after the Uruguay Round (1999). [source: GATT]
- Uruguay Round agreements signed by 123 nations at Marrakesh, 15 April 1994; GATT superseded by the WTO on 1 January 1995. [source: GATT; WTO]

## 4. The container (Levinson's *The Box*)
- Malcom McLean (1913–2001), North Carolina trucking entrepreneur, with engineer Keith Tantlinger developed the modern intermodal container (1955). McLean's insight: the business was moving cargo, not ships. [source: CONT; MCL]
- 26 April 1956: the converted WWII T-2 tanker *Ideal X* sailed from Port Newark, New Jersey, to Houston with 58 35-foot trailer vans. [source: MCL; CONT]
- Hand-loading cost $5.86 a ton then; containers cut it to 16 cents a ton (MCL says "36-fold"). [source: MCL]
- First purpose-built service: *Gateway City*, April 1957 (New York–Florida–Texas). Sea-Land transatlantic service to Rotterdam, Bremen, Grangemouth from April 1966. The US government invited Sea-Land to serve South Vietnam in 1967; Vietnam produced 40% of the company's revenue in 1968/69. Far East–US service from late 1968. [source: MCL]
- Standardisation: ISO 668 (January 1968) defined dimensions; before that dozens of incompatible systems (Matson 24 ft, Sea-Land 35 ft). [source: CONT]
- Effects: crews of 20–22 longshoremen per hold no longer needed; docker jobs and old port districts (e.g. San Francisco's piers) declined while deep-water container ports rose. Harvard economist Benjamin Chinitz in the 1950s thought containers would help New York ship to the US South, not foresee cheaper imports — Levinson's point that few anticipated the container's effect on trade. [source: CONT]
- Scale: 2004 world container port traffic 354 million TEU (82% at top 100 ports) [CONT]; about 90% of non-bulk cargo moves by container (2009 estimate) [CONT; IC]; ~20.5 million containers in 2011; ~90% of the world's containers made in China [IC]; 2023 world port throughput 858 million TEU, up 33% from 641 million in 2013 (UNCTADstat); Asia ~63% of container activity (Review of Maritime Transport 2024). [source: WebSearch: https://unctadstat.unctad.org/insights/theme/41; https://unctad.org/publication/review-maritime-transport-2024]
- Levinson: former finance and economics editor of *The Economist*; *The Box* (Princeton UP, 2006; 2nd ed. 2016 with an extra chapter); chapters include "Gridlock on the Docks", "Union Disunion", "Vietnam", "Just in Time". Sequel *Outside the Box: How Globalization Changed from Moving Stuff to Spreading Ideas* (2020). [source: BOX; LEV]

## 5. Free-trade agreements: NAFTA and Mexico
- NAFTA (Canada, Mexico, US) in force 1 January 1994 (Clinton signed 8 December 1993), superseding the 1988 Canada–US FTA; replaced by the USMCA on 1 July 2020. At entry, tariffs immediately removed on more than half of Mexico's exports to the US and more than a third of US exports to Mexico. [source: NAFTA]
- Ross Perot, second 1992 presidential debate: "there will be a giant sucking sound going south". [source: NAFTA]
- Economists' verdict: 2012 IGM survey — 95% said US citizens on average benefited; most analyses found small aggregate gains and harm to a minority of trade-exposed workers. [source: NAFTA]
- Mexico: maquiladoras (assembly plants importing components for export) became a landmark of NAFTA-era Mexico. Corn: subsidised US maize imports; Timothy Wise and others find most smallholder maize producers suffered falling prices and incomes; ~60% of Mexican corn growers are Indigenous (quoted source); commercial northern farms gained. World Bank (2005) study: extreme rural poverty rose from ~37% (early 1990s) to ~52% (1996–98), attributed largely to the 1995 crisis, weak agriculture and falling farm prices — i.e. not simply NAFTA. Supporters' prediction that NAFTA would reduce migration was not met. [source: NAFTA]
- 1992 amendment of Article 27 of the Mexican Constitution ended land redistribution (ejido reform), feeding peasant grievance. [source: EZLN; NAFTA]
- Mexican peso crisis ("Tequila crisis"): sudden devaluation in December 1994, "one of the first international financial crises ignited by capital flight", after the Chiapas uprising and the assassination of candidate Luis Donaldo Colosio raised risk premia. [source: MPC]

## 6. Hyper-globalization, the WTO, China, supply chains
- WTO established 1 January 1995 (Marrakesh Agreement); 166 members covering over 98% of world trade and GDP. [source: WTO]
- Doha Development Round launched November 2001 at the fourth ministerial (which also approved China's entry); still incomplete — deadlines of 1 January 2005 missed; agriculture/subsidies the main obstacle; bilateral FTAs multiplied instead. [source: WTO]
- Since 2019, the Trump administration's blocking of appointments left the Appellate Body unable to rule; March 2020 EU and 15 others created the interim appeal arbitration arrangement (MPIA). [source: WTO]
- China: GATT observer, worked to join from 1986; WTO member 11 December 2001; recognised as a non-market economy for 15 years under Article 15 of its accession protocol (disputed after 2016); then pursued export-led growth and became a key link in supply chains. [source: CHWTO]
- China's share of world merchandise exports 14.2% in 2023 (MOFCOM citing WTO); top exporter for consecutive years. [source: WebSearch: https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/statistics/202404/13/content_WS661a3c4ec6d0868f4e8e604f.html]
- In 1991 China accounted for only 1% of US imports. [source: CS]
- "Hyperglobalization": Rodrik's term in *The Globalization Paradox* (2011) for an "impractical version" of globalization aiming to eliminate all transaction costs; Subramanian and Kessler (PIIE, 2013), "The Hyperglobalization of Trade and Its Future", used it for the trade surge since 1995. [source: HYP]
- Textiles: Multi Fibre Arrangement 1974–1994 imposed quotas on developing-country textile/garment exports; its successor ATC expired 1 January 2005. Bangladesh was expected to suffer from Chinese competition but its exports rose (~$500m more in 2006). [source: MFA]
- Supply-chain diversification 2018–2023: firms adopted "China-plus-many", with Vietnam, India, Mexico, Thailand, Taiwan and the US frequent destinations. [source: OFF]
- GVCs (UNCTAD 2013): value-added trade contributes ~30% of GDP in developing countries vs 18% in developed. [source: GVC]

## 7. Baldwin's "Great Convergence"
- Richard Baldwin, *The Great Convergence: Information Technology and the New Globalization* (Harvard UP, November 2016; FT and Economist best books of 2016). [source: BALD]
- Argument: the share of world income of today's rich nations rose from ~20% (1820) to almost 70% (1990), then fell to roughly its 1900 level. First unbundling (steam: production separated from consumption) vs second unbundling (ICT from ~1990 slashed the cost of moving ideas, letting firms split production stages across borders). Gains concentrated in a few countries — reviewers cite an "Industrializing Six" (China, Korea, India, Poland, Indonesia, Thailand). A 2018 Baldwin essay puts the G7 share at a fifth in 1820 and two-thirds in 1988 — figures vary by measure. [source: WebSearch: https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c12590/c12590.pdf; https://theworthyhouse.com/?p=1359]

## 8. Winners and losers: the China shock and factory towns
- Autor, Dorn and Hanson, "The China Syndrome: Local Labor Market Effects of Import Competition in the United States", *American Economic Review* 103:6 (2013): trade-exposed regions saw "higher unemployment, lower labor force participation, and reduced wages". [source: CS]
- Estimates of US manufacturing jobs lost: 550,000 (~16% of the 2000–07 manufacturing decline) up to 2.0–2.4 million (Acemoglu, Autor, Dorn, Hanson and Price, *Journal of Labor Economics* 2016). Also losses in Norway, Spain, Canada, Germany. [source: CS]
- Autor, Dorn and Hanson, "The China Shock" (*Annual Review of Economics* 2016): offsetting gains in other industries "have yet to materialize". [source: CS]
- Counter-evidence: Caliendo, Dvorkin and Parro (2019): ~15% of 2000–07 manufacturing losses due to China; de Chaisemartin & Lei (2023) found a methodological flaw; Amiti et al. (2020) credit two-thirds of the effect to China's own tariff cuts; Jaravel & Sager (2019): consumer savings ~$411,000 per lost job; Autor-Dorn-Hanson (2021) themselves found that, counting lower prices, only ~6.3% of the US population lost on net. Caliendo & Parro (2023): aggregate welfare gains in both countries; not the leading cause of US manufacturing decline. Shock for consumer goods largely over by 2006–07. [source: CS]
- ADH oppose protectionism as a remedy, favour adjustment policies (and supported the TPP). [source: CS]
- "China Shock 2.0" (2020s): surge of Chinese EVs, solar panels, batteries, wind turbines, especially into the EU. FT (2025): China itself losing labour-intensive manufacturing to automation and to Vietnam/Indonesia. [source: CS]
- Politics: Autor, Dorn, Hanson and Majlesi, "Importing Political Polarization?" (*AER* 110:10, 2020): trade-exposed US areas showed ideological realignment 2000–2016 and shifted toward Republican presidential candidates. Colantone and Stanig, "Global Competition and Brexit" (*American Political Science Review*, online March 2018) use regional exposure to Chinese imports 1990–2007 (specific results not read — say only that they link import exposure to the Leave vote, as the title and design indicate, or omit). [source: WebSearch: https://gps.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty/hanson/importing_polarization.pdf; https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/C843990101DB9232B654E77130F88398]

## 9. Winners and losers in the Global South
- **Bangladesh garments:** export industry began late 1970s; Desh Garments with South Korea's Daewoo sent 130 Bangladeshis to Busan for training in 1979; production began 1980. Now ~US$38.7bn of garment exports in FY2025–26, ~80.6% of merchandise exports; world's second-largest apparel exporter (6.9% share, 2024, WTO data). ~4.2 million workers, ~54% women (ILO/IFC Better Work 2025); women's share has fallen from earlier decades. Minimum wage Tk 12,500/month (Nov 2023). [source: BDT]
- **Tazreen Fashions fire**, November 2012: more than 110 killed. [source: BDT]
- **Rana Plaza**, Savar near Dhaka, 24 April 2013, ~9:00 am: nine-storey building with five garment factories (about 5,000 workers), a bank and shops; cracks found 23 April, shops and bank closed but factory owners ordered workers back. Death toll 1,134 (RP; BDT says "at least 1,132" — use "more than 1,100"); ~2,500 injured rescued; search lasted 19 days. Built 2006 on a former pond without proper permits; floors 5–8 added. Brands sourcing there included Benetton, Mango, Primark, The Children's Place, El Corte Inglés, Matalan (and Joe Fresh). Two factories had passed BSCI social audits that did not cover structural safety. Owner Sohel Rana (reportedly Jubo League) caught fleeing towards India. [source: RP]
- **Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh** (2013): legally binding, over 200 brands with global unions; Bangladesh operations passed in June 2020 to the RMG Sustainability Council; as of 31 August 2026 covering 1,759 factories, ~2.8 million workers. [source: RP; BDT]
- September 2013: police fired rubber bullets and tear gas at workers demanding a $100/month minimum wage. [source: RP]
- **Philippines:** Commission on Filipinos Overseas (2013): ~10.2 million Filipinos abroad, ~11% of population; >15 million by 2019. Government calls OFWs national "heroes". Remittances $31bn in 2018, nearly 10% of GDP; $32.2bn in 2019; informal flows may be 30–40% above official figures. Filipinos are a fifth to a quarter of the world's merchant-marine crews; nurse emigration strained Philippine hospitals (1:40–60 nurse–patient ratios vs 1:15–20 in the 1990s). [source: OF]
- **Remittances:** World Bank estimate: $685bn to low- and middle-income countries in 2024 (+5.8%), "larger than FDI and ODA combined" (World Bank wording; other agencies frame it differently); top recipients India $129bn, Mexico $68bn, China $48bn, Philippines $40bn, Pakistan $33bn. Over the decade remittances grew 57% while FDI fell 41%. Official flows only — true total higher. [source: WebSearch: https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/peoplemove/in-2024--remittance-flows-to-low--and-middle-income-countries-ar] Remittances are ~35% of GDP in Tajikistan, ~25% in Nepal; 18% El Salvador, 21% Honduras, up to 30% Haiti (older figures). [source: REM]
- **African commodity exporters:** 2000s commodities boom ("China boom", 2000–2014) driven largely by emerging-market, especially Chinese, demand; peaked 2011; by 2013 copper down 33%, iron ore 36% from peaks; mine closures e.g. Luanshya copper mine, Zambia (6 March 2009). [source: CB] "Africa Rising" coined by *The Economist* in December 2011; Africa's economy grew 50% in 2005–15 vs world 23%; Patrick Bond argued the label appeared just as the commodity-led 2002–11 growth ended, followed by fiscal crises, debt defaults and protests. [source: AR]
- **Poverty:** World Bank: share of world population in extreme poverty fell from ~38% (1990) to ~8.5% (2019) (from ~2 billion to ~660 million); Covid reversed some progress (~682 million, 8.5%, in 2022); most of the decline came from China. The line was $1.90 (2011 PPP), then $2.15 (2017 PPP) from September 2022; in 2025 updated to $3.00 (2021 PPP) — figures shift with each revision, so cite vintage. [source: WebSearch: https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/opendata/progress-challenges-ending-extreme-poverty; https://datatopics.worldbank.org/sdgatlas/goal-1-no-poverty/; Extreme_poverty]

## 10. Inequality: Milanović's elephant curve
- Christoph Lakner and Branko Milanović, "Global Income Distribution: From the Fall of the Berlin Wall to the Great Recession", World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 6719 (December 2013): the global growth incidence curve, 1988–2008, "a distinct supine S shape". [source: EC; BM]
- Reading: big gains for the global middle (roughly 50th–60th percentiles, largely Chinese and other Asian households — EC gives 70–80%); near-stagnation around the 80th percentile (working and middle classes of rich countries); strong gains for the top 1% (EC: ~60%); little for the very poorest (much of sub-Saharan Africa). [source: EC]
- Milanović: former lead economist at the World Bank research department; at CUNY Graduate Center since 2014; books *Worlds Apart* (2005), *Global Inequality: A New Approach for the Age of Globalization* (Harvard UP, April 2016), *Capitalism, Alone* (2019). [source: BM]
- Critique: Adam Corlett, Resolution Foundation, "Examining an elephant" (September 2016): changing country composition and weak Japan and ex-Soviet/Baltic performance drive the dip; holding populations fixed, the weakest point grows ~40%, so rich-world lower/middle incomes did not simply stagnate. Lakner and Milanović replied on the World Bank blog; Milanović says his work was misread. [source: WebSearch: https://www.resolutionfoundation.org/app/uploads/2016/09/Examining-an-elephant.pdf; https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/rebuttal-elephant-graph-discussion-or-elephants-are-tough-animals]
- Alvaredo, Chancel, Piketty, Saez and Zucman (World Inequality Report 2018) redid the curve for 1980–2016: top 1% captured about twice the growth of the bottom 50%. [source: EC]

## 11. Financial contagion
- Washington Consensus: term coined 1989 by John Williamson for ten reforms (trade and financial liberalisation, privatisation, fiscal discipline) promoted by the IMF, World Bank and US Treasury; Williamson objected to its broader use as a "neoliberal manifesto". [source: WC]
- Asian financial crisis: began 2 July 1997 when Thailand floated the baht (Thai "Tom Yum Kung crisis"); spread to Indonesia, South Korea, Malaysia, the Philippines, Hong Kong, Laos; IMF $40bn programme for South Korea, Thailand, Indonesia; Suharto forced out 21 May 1998 after riots. Mahathir blamed George Soros; Malaysia imposed capital controls and a 3.80 ringgit peg. Recovery 1998–99 was fast. [source: AFC]
- 2008: Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy on 15 September 2008, the largest in US history, is often seen as the climax; developing countries hit through falling trade, commodity prices, investment and remittances. [source: GFC] Great Trade Collapse: see §2.

## 12. Critics and defenders
- **Stiglitz:** 2001 Nobel laureate (with Akerlof and Spence, information economics); CEA chair under Clinton from 1993, World Bank chief economist from 1997. *Globalization and Its Discontents* (June 2002; title echoes Freud): IMF policies — austerity, high interest rates, trade and capital-market liberalisation, privatisation — worsened the 1997 Asian crisis and Argentina's 1998–2002 depression; Washington Consensus "a blend of ideology and bad science". Critics (e.g. D. W. MacKenzie in *Public Choice*) say he mislabels government failures as market failures. [source: GID; STIG]
- **Rodrik:** *Has Globalization Gone Too Far?* (1997); trilemma first presented in "How Far Will International Economic Integration Go?", *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 14:1 (2000); *The Globalization Paradox* (2011); *Straight Talk on Trade* (2017). Trilemma: "democracy, national sovereignty and global economic integration are mutually incompatible: we can combine any two of the three, but never have all three simultaneously and in full." Postwar (Bretton Woods) states chose democracy plus national autonomy and limited globalization; the backlash is read as a bid to reclaim democracy and sovereignty. Rebuttal noted by *The Economist*, after Albert Hirschman: diversified trade can strengthen sovereignty against coercion. [source: ROD; TRI]
- **Bhagwati:** born 1934 (Bombay), Cambridge, Oxford, MIT PhD; Columbia University Professor; adviser to GATT DG Arthur Dunkel and to the WTO. *In Defense of Globalization* (Oxford UP, 2004): properly governed globalization is the most powerful force for social good, helping women, reducing child labour, increasing literacy. Yet "The Capital Myth: The Difference between Trade in Widgets and Dollars", *Foreign Affairs* 77:3 (May/June 1998) argued free capital flows, unlike free trade, are crisis-prone and blamed a "Wall Street-Treasury complex"; IMF's Shailendra Anjaria replied. Useful: a free-trader who opposed capital-account liberalisation. [source: BHAG; WebSearch: https://www.imf.org/external/np/vc/1998/111098.htm; https://typeset.io/pdf/the-capital-myth-the-difference-between-trade-in-widgets-and-nw580pcchz.pdf]

## 13. Movements: Zapatistas, Seattle, Porto Alegre, Genoa, fair trade
- **Zapatistas (EZLN):** went public on 1 January 1994, the day NAFTA took effect, with the First Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle; ~3,000 armed insurgents, mostly Maya, seized six towns in the Chiapas highlands, then retreated; ceasefire 12 January; Bishop Samuel Ruiz mediated with Manuel Camacho Solís. Spokesman Subcomandante Marcos (later "Galeano"). Demands: work, land, housing, food, health, education, independence, liberty, democracy, justice, peace. EZLN hosted the Intercontinental Encounter for Humanity and Against Neoliberalism (1996). [source: EZLN; WSF]
- **Seattle ("Battle of Seattle", "N30"):** WTO third Ministerial, 30 November–3 December 1999, Washington State Convention and Trade Center, meant to launch a "Millennium Round". At least ~40,000 protesters — larger than any earlier US protest against such a body. Coalition: NGOs (Global Exchange), AFL–CIO and unions, students, religious groups (Jubilee 2000), anarchists (black bloc), even Pat Buchanan; "Teamsters and turtles" (blue–green alliance). Police used pepper spray, tear gas, stun grenades; mayor Paul Schell declared emergency, curfew and a 50-block no-protest zone; governor Gary Locke called in National Guard; >500 jailed on 1 December; police chief Norm Stamper resigned; Independent Media Center created. [source: SEA]
- Talks ended without agreement on 3 December: developing countries resented exclusion as the US and EU sought an agriculture deal, and demanded Uruguay Round commitments (textiles access, anti-dumping, TRIPS) be honoured first. WTO official history (Craig VanGrasstek): "For free-traders the Seattle Ministerial Conference was the worst of times". Martin Khor (Malaysian economist, opponent of the round) blamed "the untransparent and undemocratic nature of the WTO system, the blatant manipulation of that system by the major powers, and the refusal of many developing countries to continue to be on the receiving end." Negotiations reconvened in Doha, November 2001, where protest could be excluded. [source: WTO99]
- **Genoa, July 2001 G8:** Carlo Giuliani, 23, shot dead by a Carabinieri officer while throwing a fire extinguisher at a van — the first death at an anti-globalization demonstration since Seattle; ECHR later found the force within limits but faulted procedure. [source: GIU]
- **World Social Forum:** first met January 2001 in Porto Alegre, Brazil, timed against the World Economic Forum at Davos; slogan "another world is possible"; 2004 held in Mumbai (first outside Brazil, Stiglitz a key speaker); 2005 Porto Alegre had 155,000 registered participants and the "Porto Alegre Manifesto" of 19 signatories (incl. Aminata Traoré, Adolfo Pérez Esquivel, Eduardo Galeano, José Saramago). Charter forbids statements on behalf of all participants. [source: WSF]
- Terms: "anti-globalization" vs "alter-globalization"/"global justice movement"; activists objected to the first label. Influences: Naomi Klein's *No Logo*, *Le Monde diplomatique* (Ignacio Ramonet's editorial led to ATTAC), Susan George, James Tobin's transaction tax. [source: AGM]
- **Fair trade:** Ten Thousand Villages (Mennonite Central Committee, 1946) and SERRV (1949) first fair-trade supply chains; first certification label Max Havelaar, Netherlands, 1988 (Nico Roozen, Frans van der Hoff, Solidaridad); Fairtrade Labelling Organizations International 1997; International Fairtrade Certification Mark 2002; 2011: >1.2 million farmers and workers in >60 countries, €65m premium. Critics: certification costs fall on Southern producers; benefits to producers disputed. [source: FT]

## 13b. Backlash and "deglobalization" (handle carefully; recent)
- Jeffry Frieden: 2016 was the first time since the 1930s both major US parties had candidates openly hostile to economic integration. [source: T2]
- **Brexit:** referendum June 2016, Leave 51.9%; UK left the EU at 23:00 GMT 31 January 2020. [source: BRX]
- **US–China trade war:** first major US tariffs took effect 22 January 2018; Chinese retaliation from 2 April 2018; "phase one" deal January 2020 (China did not buy the $200bn of extra imports pledged); Biden kept tariffs and added levies on EVs and solar panels. In 2025 escalation reached 145% US tariffs on Chinese goods and 125% Chinese tariffs on US goods (later modified). [source: TW]
- **2025–26 US tariffs (as Wikipedia states them on 2026-11-11; may change):** 2 April 2025 "Liberation Day": minimum 10% tariff on almost all imports under IEEPA; average effective US tariff rate rose from 2.5% (January) to ~27% (April 2025), the highest in over a century; February 2026 the Supreme Court (*Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*) ruled the IEEPA tariffs illegal, requiring refunds of ~$166bn; Trump then imposed a 10% global tariff under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 for 150 days (to 24 July 2026), which the Court of International Trade also ruled illegal (on appeal); effective rate 12.1% as of 21 July 2026. Promised manufacturing job growth not realised (per article). [source: T2]
- **Deglobalization debate:** two long periods of global deglobalization — 1930s and the 2010s "World Trade Slowdown" after the Great Trade Collapse; drivers cited: 2008 crisis, Brexit, US–China trade war, Covid-19 supply-chain shocks (production falls in 2020: Bangladesh −77.6%, Mexico −75.8%, Vietnam −18.3% at worst), and the "Second Cold War". Contested by former WTO DG Pascal Lamy and Michael Bordo (too soon to diagnose). In the 1930s deglobalization was driven by autocracies; in the 2010s by democratic votes (Trump, Brexit). [source: DEG]
- Baldwin (2024 talk): offshoring phase slowed or stopped around 2014; his later titles *The Great Trade Hack: How Trump's trade war fails and the world moves on* (CEPR Press, 2025). [source: BALD; WebSearch: https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c12590/c12590.pdf]

## Debates
- **When did globalization begin?** Frank (Bronze Age), Flynn & Giráldez (1571/16th-century silver), O'Rourke & Williamson (1820s, measured by commodity-price convergence), popular usage (post-1990). Depends on whether "globalization" means contact or integrated markets. [source: HG; GLOB]
- **Who won?** Milanović's elephant (Asian middle classes and global top 1% won; rich-world lower-middle classes lagged) vs Corlett (composition effects; Japan and ex-Soviet states drive the dip); WID version stresses the top 1%. Poverty fell from ~38% to ~8.5% (1990–2019), mostly in China — defenders (Bhagwati) credit trade-led growth; critics stress that sub-Saharan Africa gained least. [source: EC; WebSearch as above]
- **China shock:** ADH's concentrated local damage vs general-equilibrium and consumer-gain studies; even ADH say tariffs are not the cure. Political effects (polarisation, Trump, Brexit) argued by ADH-Majlesi and Colantone–Stanig. [source: CS; WebSearch]
- **Trade vs capital:** Stiglitz and Bhagwati — from different camps — both argued that premature capital-account liberalisation (pushed by the IMF/US Treasury) caused crises like 1997; the IMF disputed this. Rodrik's trilemma frames the political cost. [source: GID; BHAG; TRI]
- **NAFTA:** small aggregate gains (95% of IGM economists) vs Mexican maize farmers' losses, falling real wages for low earners (Public Citizen's Global Trade Watch, an advocacy source), and migration; peso crisis confounds attribution. [source: NAFTA]
- **Garments and Rana Plaza:** factory work as women's empowerment and poverty exit (Kabeer's Dhaka women renegotiating purdah) vs deadly conditions and failed private audits; the Accord as a binding model. [source: BDT; RP; Kabeer search]
- **Is globalization over?** "Deglobalization" proponents vs Lamy/Bordo and the VoxEU "peak globalisation myth" view (much of the fall is commodity prices); Baldwin: goods offshoring plateaued ~2014 while services/digital trade grows. [source: DEG; WebSearch]
