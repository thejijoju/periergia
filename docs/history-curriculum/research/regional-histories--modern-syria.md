## 1. Late Ottoman Syria (Tanzimat, 1860, Arab nationalism)
- Ottoman Syria was organised after the conquest from the Mamluks (1516) as provinces (eyalets) of Damascus, later Aleppo (1534), Tripoli (1579) and others. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Syria]
- Under the Tanzimat Vilayet Law of 1864 the eyalets became smaller vilayets — the Syria (Damascus) Vilayet, the Aleppo Vilayet and the Beirut Vilayet — each under a vali appointed by the Porte but with new provincial assemblies; Jerusalem became a separate mutasarrifate in 1872. "Syria" in late Ottoman usage often meant the whole land from the Taurus to Sinai. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Syria]
- 1860: a Druze–Maronite civil war in Mount Lebanon (killing most often estimated at 7,000–11,000, some claims over 20,000) spilled into Damascus. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1860_civil_conflict_in_Mount_Lebanon_and_Damascus]
- Damascus, 9–17 July 1860: crowds of Druze, Bedouin, Muslim city commoners and Kurdish irregulars attacked the Christian quarter of Bab Tuma ("Ṭoshat an-Naṣṣāra", "the riot of the Christians"); the quarter was completely destroyed; the French, Russian, Dutch, Austrian and Belgian consulates were attacked. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1860_civil_conflict_in_Mount_Lebanon_and_Damascus]
- Damascus death toll is disputed: Eugene Rogan (2024) gives about 5,000 Christians dead over eight days; other estimates run from about 2,500 to 10,000+ (WP infobox cites Fawaz for up to 12,000). Say "thousands — perhaps around 5,000". [source: https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/eugene-rogan/the-damascus-events/9781541604278/ (search snippet); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1860_civil_conflict_in_Mount_Lebanon_and_Damascus]
- The Algerian exile Abd al-Qadir al-Jaza'iri sheltered numerous Christians in his home in Damascus and with his men faced down rioters who tried to storm it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1860_civil_conflict_in_Mount_Lebanon_and_Damascus]
- Aftermath: the Ottoman minister Fuad Pasha restored order; Ottoman and European sources record about 357 executions in Damascus, and the governor Ahmed Pasha himself was executed; France sent about 6,000 troops (in Syria August 1860–June 1861); Mount Lebanon became an autonomous Mutasarrifate under a Christian governor (Daud Pasha, appointed 9 June 1861). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1860_civil_conflict_in_Mount_Lebanon_and_Damascus]
- Abd al-Rahman al-Kawakibi (Aleppo, 1854/5–1902), author of *Taba'i' al-Istibdad* ("The Nature of Tyranny") and *Umm al-Qura*, an early Arab critic of Ottoman despotism; died in Cairo in 1902, allegedly poisoned. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Rahman_al-Kawakibi]
- Al-Fatat (Young Arab Society), secret Arab nationalist society, founded by students in Paris on 14 November 1909; HQ moved to Beirut (1913) then Damascus (1914). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Fatat]
- The Arab Congress met in Paris, 18–23 June 1913 (25 official delegates), seeking autonomy and reform within the empire, not independence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Congress_of_1913]
- First World War: Djemal (Cemal) Pasha, governor in Syria, called *as-Saffāḥ* ("the Butcher") by Arabs, hanged Syrian and Lebanese nationalists in Damascus and Beirut on 6 May 1916 (commemorated as Martyrs' Day); 34 executed in 1915–16 in total. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djemal_Pasha]
- Faisal received the Damascus Protocol (1915) from al-Fatat and al-'Ahd in Damascus. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faisal_I]

## 2. Faisal's Arab Kingdom and Maysalun (1918–20)
- An Arab government was set up in Damascus after its capture (Faisal's administration dated from 5 October 1918). Syrian National Congress elected May 1919, first session 3 June 1919 under Hashim al-Atassi. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Kingdom_of_Syria]
- The King–Crane Commission arrived in Damascus 25 June 1919 and was met with leaflets reading "Independence or Death". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Kingdom_of_Syria]
- 8 March 1920: the Syrian National Congress proclaimed Faisal king of the Arab Kingdom of Syria, rejecting a French mandate; it lasted to 24/25 July 1920. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Kingdom_of_Syria; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faisal_I]
- Gouraud's ultimatum of 14 July 1920 demanded disbandment of the Arab army by 20 July; Faisal accepted on 18 July, but War Minister Yusuf al-Azma marched out to fight. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Maysalun]
- Battle of Maysalun, 24 July 1920, about 25 km west of Damascus, about four hours: French force ~12,000 (with tanks and aircraft; mostly Senegalese and Algerian units) under Gouraud/Goybet vs 1,400–4,000 Syrian regulars plus Bedouin and volunteers; French claims: 42 French killed; ~150 Syrians killed, ~1,500 wounded. Al-Azma killed. French entered Damascus 25 July (WP mandate article says 24 July); Aleppo taken 23 July. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Maysalun]
- Faisal went on to become king of Iraq (1921). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faisal_I]

## 3. The French Mandate: statelets (1920–36)
- France was granted the mandate at San Remo (25 April 1920); mandate text dated 24 July 1922; in force 29 September 1923; League of Nations Class A mandate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_for_Syria_and_the_Lebanon]
- Partition: State of Greater Lebanon proclaimed 1 September 1920 (Arrêté 318 of 31 Aug), a "safe haven" for Maronites, with Beirut as capital; Alawite State (Arrêté 319, 31 Aug 1920); separate States of Damascus and Aleppo (1 Sept 1920); Jabal Druze (1921); autonomous Sanjak of Alexandretta. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_for_Syria_and_the_Lebanon]
- A Syrian Federation (28 June 1922) joined Aleppo, Damascus and the Alawite State; on 1 January 1925 Aleppo and Damascus were united as the State of Syria while the Alawite State stood apart; the Jabal Druze and Alawite (Latakia) states were incorporated into Syria in 1936 (Latakia on 5 December 1936). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_for_Syria_and_the_Lebanon]
- Most Muslims in Greater Lebanon rejected the new state at first. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_for_Syria_and_the_Lebanon]
- Between 1926 and 1939 Alawites and other minorities provided the majority of the locally recruited part of the Army of the Levant (France's garrison) — the root of later minority prominence in the army. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alawites]
- 1936–37 autonomist agitation among Assyrians, Kurds and some Bedouin in the Jazira; Circassian leaders sought autonomy in 1938 — minorities feared an Arab-nationalist republic. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_for_Syria_and_the_Lebanon]

## 4. The Great Syrian Revolt (1925–27)
- Spark: French treatment of the Druze (Captain Carbillet's forced labour, disarmament and taxation); High Commissioner General Maurice Sarrail imprisoned a Druze delegation sent to complain. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Syrian_Revolt]
- Sultan al-Atrash won the Battle of al-Kafr (21 July 1925) and al-Mazraa (2 August 1925), and formally declared revolt on 23 August 1925; within six weeks nationalists allied with him, with a provisional government under al-Atrash and Dr Abd al-Rahman Shahbandar (People's Party) as vice-president. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultan_al-Atrash; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Syrian_Revolt]
- Hama rose on 4–5 October 1925 under Fawzi al-Qawuqji. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Syrian_Revolt]
- Damascus: rebels under Hasan al-Kharrat, *qabaday* (strongman) of the al-Shaghour quarter, briefly seized the Azm Palace residence of Sarrail and withdrew under heavy French bombardment (October 1925); by spring 1926 much of Damascus had been destroyed by artillery; al-Kharrat was killed in a French ambush in the Ghouta on 25 December 1925. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasan_al-Kharrat; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Syrian_Revolt]
- Rebels' bodies were displayed in Marjeh Square, Damascus (photographed 1925). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Syrian_Revolt]
- France raised troop numbers to about 50,000 by early 1926; the Druze were decisively defeated in spring 1927; al-Atrash, sentenced to death, fled to Transjordan, was pardoned and returned in 1937. Sarrail was replaced by Henry de Jouvenel. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Syrian_Revolt; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultan_al-Atrash]
- Casualties (weakly sourced — attribute and hedge): commonly cited summaries give about 6,000 rebels killed and about 2,000 French troops and auxiliaries killed, with over 100,000 left homeless; Wikipedia gives no total. Say "thousands of Syrians and perhaps 2,000 French and colonial troops". [source: web search 2026-11-11, results citing Provence secondhand (search snippet) — see NOT VERIFIED]
- Michael Provence calls it the largest and longest-lasting anti-colonial insurgency in the inter-war Arab East. [source: https://utpress.utexas.edu/9780292706804/the-great-syrian-revolt-and-the-rise-of-arab-nationalism/ (search snippet)]

## 5. The National Bloc, Alexandretta and independence (1928–46)
- National Bloc formed under Hashim al-Atassi; 1928 Constituent Assembly with a nationalist majority clashed with France; 1933 French draft treaty opposed; al-Atassi called a 50-day strike (1936). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Syrian_Republic]
- Franco-Syrian Treaty of Independence, 9 September 1936, negotiated with Léon Blum's Popular Front government, but never ratified by the French parliament. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Syrian_Republic]
- Alexandretta: 1936 French estimate of 220,000 people — 39% Turks, 28% Alawite Arabs, 11% Armenians, 10% Sunni Arabs, 8% other Christians, 4% Circassians/Kurds/Jews. The sanjak got League-brokered autonomy (November 1937); became the Hatay State (2 September 1938 – 29 June 1939); annexed by Turkey (de facto 7 July, de jure 23 July 1939). Syria never recognised it. Women demonstrated in Damascus against the loss. Atatürk's government coined "Hatay". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatay_State; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanjak_of_Alexandretta; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Syrian_Republic]
- Zaki al-Arsuzi, an Alawite Arab nationalist from Alexandretta, was influential there (later a Ba'th precursor thinker). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatay_State]
- 1940–41 Vichy control; Allied invasion 1941; independence proclaimed 1941/1944, recognised 1 January 1944. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Syrian_Republic]
- Levant Crisis: 29 May 1945, French forces shelled Damascus and stormed the parliament trying to arrest President Shukri al-Quwatli and Speaker Saadallah al-Jabiri (both escaped); roughly 1,000 Syrians killed (WP); Churchill sent British forces from Transjordan, and the French were confined to barracks. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant_Crisis; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Syrian_Republic]
- UN founding member/de jure recognition 24 October 1945; last French troops left 17 April 1946 (Evacuation Day, Syria's national day). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Syrian_Republic]

## 6. Coups and the parliamentary republic (1949–58)
- First coup, 30 March 1949 (bloodless): Col. Husni al-Za'im overthrew al-Quwatli after the 1948 war defeat; four days later his government ratified the Tapline (Saudi oil pipeline) deal parliament had blocked. CIA involvement (Stephen Meade, Miles Copeland) is alleged and "highly controversial"; Copeland's memoirs contradict each other. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_1949_Syrian_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husni_al-Za%27im]
- Second coup, 14 August 1949: Sami al-Hinnawi; Za'im executed the same day; third coup December 1949: Adib Shishakli, overthrown February 1954 and parliamentary rule restored. Three coups in one year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Syrian_Republic; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Syria]
- Za'im proposed votes for women; women's suffrage was achieved under the following civilian administration of Hashim al-Atassi. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husni_al-Za%27im]
- 1946–56: 20 different cabinets and four constitutions. November 1956 arms pact with the USSR after Suez. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Syria]
- The Arab Socialist Ba'th Party, founded 1947, helped overthrow Shishakli. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Syria]

## 7. The United Arab Republic (1958–61)
- Union proposed by Syrian political and military leaders fearing communism; Nasser's terms: "a plebiscite, the dissolution of parties, and the withdrawal of the army from politics". UAR established 1 February 1958; referendums 21 February 1958. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Arab_Republic]
- Egyptian centralisation (Amer, Abd al-Hamid al-Sarraj's security apparatus) and nationalisations (banks, insurance, heavy industry, 23 July 1961) alienated Syrian business and army circles; Syrian coup of 28 September 1961 ended the union. Egypt kept the name UAR until 1971. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Arab_Republic]

## 8. The Ba'th in power (1963–70)
- 8 March 1963 coup ("March 8 Revolution") by the Ba'th Military Committee (leading figures Muhammad Umran, Salah Jadid and Hafez al-Assad — all Alawites), with Nasserist and independent allies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1963_Syrian_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat]
- The state of emergency imposed in 1963 lasted until 21 April 2011 ("48-year emergency law"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba%27athist_Syria; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_revolution]
- 1964 Hama riot suppressed; Assad voted for violent suppression. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad]
- 21–23 February 1966: neo-Ba'thist coup by Salah Jadid overthrew the party founders (Aflaq, Bitar, Amin al-Hafiz); Assad became defence minister. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1966_Syrian_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat]
- June 1967: Israel captured the Golan Heights with Assad as defence minister. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad]
- 1962 Hasakah census (5 October 1962): about 120,000 Kurds (roughly 20% of Syrian Kurds) stripped of citizenship and classed as *ajanib* ("foreigners") or unregistered *maktumin*; Assad's Decree 49 (April 2011) offered citizenship to registered *ajanib*. Kurds are 5–10% of the population. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurds_in_Syria]

## 9. Hafez al-Assad's Syria (1970–2000)
- Born 6 October 1930 in Qardaha to a poor Alawite peasant family. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad]
- "Corrective Movement": 13–16 November 1970 (arrests ordered after the party congress ended 12 November); confirmed president by referendum 12 March 1971. Jadid imprisoned in Mezze prison until his death. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba%27athist_Syria; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad]
- 1973 constitution, Article 8: the Ba'th "the leading party in the society and the state"; Article 3: Islamic jurisprudence "a main source" of legislation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba%27athist_Syria]
- To answer Sunni objections, Assad had Lebanese Shia cleric Musa al-Sadr issue a fatwa (1973) that Alawites were part of Shia Islam. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamist_uprising_in_Syria]
- Security state: Alawite near-monopoly of security and intelligence posts (Hinnebusch); Rifaat al-Assad's Defence (Struggle) Companies; several *mukhabarat* agencies (Military, Air Force, General, Political Security). Sunnis held prominent posts (Defence Minister Tlass, Chief of Staff Shihabi) but "none had power separate from" Assad. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba%27athist_Syria]
- State-sponsored personality cult. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad]
- Economy: Tabqa Dam on the Euphrates completed 1974; Gulf/OPEC aid after 1973; worsening economy in the early 1980s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad]
- Lebanon: Syrian intervention began 31 May 1976 (initially ~2,000 troops and 60 tanks; Hafez article says 30,000 troops in 1976 — give as "thousands rising to tens of thousands"), first against the PLO and leftists on behalf of the Maronites; legitimised as the Arab Deterrent Force (October 1976); Taif ended the war (1989); 1991 Treaty of Brotherhood; occupation ended 30 April 2005 after Rafic Hariri's assassination (14 February 2005, 22 killed) and the Cedar Revolution. The UN-backed tribunal (18 August 2020) convicted a Hezbollah member and found no direct evidence of Syrian involvement. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_occupation_of_Lebanon; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bashar_al-Assad]
- Succession: Rifaat exiled after a bid for power in 1984; heir Basil died in a car crash in 1994; Bashar (then training in London as an ophthalmologist) recalled. Hafez died 10 June 2000. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez_al-Assad; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bashar_al-Assad]

## 10. The Islamist uprising and Hama (1976–82)
- Islamist uprising 1976–82, led first by Marwan Hadid's Fighting Vanguard, joined by the Muslim Brotherhood from 1979; most victims of assassinations were Alawites. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamist_uprising_in_Syria]
- After a failed attempt on Hafez's life, Rifaat's forces killed imprisoned Islamists in Tadmor (Palmyra) prison on 27 June 1980 (reports: 600–1,000 prisoners). Law No. 49 (1980) made Brotherhood membership a capital offence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_Hama_massacre; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadmor_Prison]
- Hama, 2–28 February 1982: insurgents rose at 2 a.m. on 2 February, killing about 70 Ba'thists; the army and Rifaat's Defence Companies besieged the city for 27 days with about 12,000 troops, shelled and bombed the old city. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_Hama_massacre]
- Hama death toll (attribute): estimates range from 5,000 to 40,000 (including ~1,000 soldiers); Amnesty initially 10,000–25,000; Robert Fisk first 10,000, later 20,000; Syrian Human Rights Committee 25,000 or 30,000–40,000; Rifaat reportedly boasted of 38,000. WP infobox: 25,000–40,000 civilians. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_Hama_massacre]
- Hama became for decades an unspoken memory; Salwa Ismail's *Rule of Violence* has a chapter on memories of Hama. [source: https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BB2666837X?l=en (search snippet)]

## 11. Bashar al-Assad, the "Damascus Spring" and the 2000s
- Born 11 September 1965; constitution amended to lower the presidential minimum age from 40 to 34; referendum 10 July 2000 (97.29%); president from 17 July 2000; married Asma Akhras (2000). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bashar_al-Assad]
- Damascus Spring (June 2000 – autumn 2001): political "forums" (*muntadayat*), notably the Riad Seif Forum and the Jamal al-Atassi National Dialogue Forum; the Statement of 99 and the Statement of 1,000 (2001). Suppressed in 2001: Riad Seif and Ma'mun al-Homsi given five years; Riad al-Turk, Aref Dalila, Kamal al-Labwani and others 2–10 years. The Atassi forum survived until 2005. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus_Spring]
- Damascus Declaration for democratic reform, October 2005, signed by most of the opposition including the Muslim Brotherhood. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus_Spring]
- "Social market economy" (2005): liberalisation enriched regime cronies; Bashar's cousin Rami Makhlouf the most favoured oligarch. Official unemployment 8.1% (2009) vs economists' estimate of 24.4%. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Background_and_causes_of_the_Syrian_revolution; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bashar_al-Assad]
- Drought 2006/7–2010, the worst on instrumental record; Syrian Agricultural Workers Union complained in February 2011 that lifting input subsidies worsened its impact. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Background_and_causes_of_the_Syrian_revolution; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Syria]
- Referendums 2007 (97.6%); 2021 election 95.2%. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bashar_al-Assad]

## 12. The 2011 uprising
- Daraa: in early 2011 (protests triggered on 6 March per WP) security forces under Assad's cousin Atef Najib arrested and tortured about 15 youths for graffiti including "The people want the fall of the regime" and "Your turn, Doctor". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_revolution]
- 15 March 2011 "Day of Rage" in Damascus; 18 March "Friday of Dignity" protests in several cities; demonstrators chanted "peaceful, peaceful" (HRW interviews). 25 March funerals: "We do not want your bread, we want dignity". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_revolution]
- 30 March 2011 speech: Assad blamed "conspirators" and a "foreign plot". Emergency law lifted 21 April 2011; Daraa besieged from 25 April. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_revolution]
- Hamza Ali al-Khateeb, 13, arrested 29 April 2011; mutilated body returned 24 May, became a national symbol. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_revolution]
- Huge protest in Hama's Assi Square, 22 July 2011. Free Syrian Army formed by defected officers 29 July 2011; Syrian National Council 23 August 2011. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_revolution]
- Arab League suspended Syria in November 2011; readmitted 2023. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_civil_war]
- Razan Zaitouneh, human-rights lawyer, founded the Violations Documentation Center (April 2011) and the Local Coordination Committees; abducted in rebel-held Douma on 9 December 2013 (most likely by Jaysh al-Islam) with Wael Hamadeh, Samira Khalil and Nazem Hammadi; fate unknown. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Razan_Zaitouneh]

## 13. The civil war: actors and interventions (2011–24)
- Iran intervened in 2013 (Quds Force; Afghan Fatemiyoun and Pakistani Zainabiyoun militias); Hezbollah fought openly from 2013 (al-Qusayr) — 1,139–1,250 Hezbollah fighters killed by March 2018 (SOHR 1,736 by March 2023). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_intervention_in_the_Syrian_civil_war; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hezbollah_involvement_in_the_Syrian_civil_war]
- Russia began air strikes 30 September 2015 (Hmeimim base agreement 26 August 2015; naval port at Tartus); civilian deaths from Russian strikes 4,431–6,522 (Airwars) or 8,763 (SOHR). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_intervention_in_the_Syrian_civil_war]
- Battle of Aleppo, 19 July 2012 – 22 December 2016 ("Syria's Stalingrad"); 31,273 deaths in the province incl. 21,452 civilians; ~130,000 civilians left east Aleppo in the final offensive (SOHR). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Aleppo_(2012%E2%80%932016)]
- Opposition: FSA; Syrian Interim Government; jihadist al-Nusra Front (founded 2012 by Abu Mohammad al-Julani = Ahmed al-Sharaa, with al-Qaeda backing), later Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham (2017) ruling Idlib via the Syrian Salvation Government; Turkish-backed Syrian National Army. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_civil_war; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_al-Sharaa]
- Kurds: Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria ("Rojava"), de facto autonomous from 2012; army the SDF (Arab-majority but YPG-led), backed by the US-led coalition against ISIS from 2014. Siege of Kobanî 15 September 2014 – 20 March 2015 (city retaken late January 2015). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Autonomous_Administration_of_North_and_East_Syria; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Koban%C3%AE]
- Turkey: operations against the SDF from 2016 (Afrin/"Olive Branch" January–March 2018; October 2019 offensive), occupying parts of the north. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_civil_war; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Autonomous_Administration_of_North_and_East_Syria]
- ISIS: Raqqa its de facto capital from 2014; SDF captured Raqqa 17 October 2017 (≈80% of the city destroyed; 1,300–1,600 civilians killed by US-led strikes per Airwars/Amnesty); last territory lost at Baghuz, 9 February – 23 March 2019. ISIS captured and blew up Tadmor prison (May 2015). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Raqqa_(2017); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Baghuz_Fawqani; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadmor_Prison]
- Diplomacy: Geneva talks (formally from 1 February 2016); Astana process (Russia, Iran, Turkey) from January 2017, "de-escalation zones" 4–6 May 2017; Idlib ceasefire 2020 – November 2024. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_civil_war]
- Sieges: e.g. Yarmouk camp, 20,000 residents facing starvation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_civil_war]

## 14. Barrel bombs, chemical weapons, prisons
- Barrel bombs first identified August 2012 (open-source work of Eliot Higgins); Aleppo the focal point; UN Security Council Resolution 2139 (February 2014) demanded an end to them. An opposition SNC claim (Dec 2013): >20,000 killed by barrel bombs (partisan — avoid or attribute). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrel_bomb]
- Obama's "red line", 20 August 2012. Ghouta sarin attack 21 August 2013: 281–1,729 killed (various estimates); UN found "clear and convincing evidence" of sarin; September 2013 deal to destroy the declared arsenal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use_of_chemical_weapons_in_the_Syrian_civil_war; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghouta_chemical_attack]
- Khan Shaykhun sarin attack 4 April 2017 (at least 89 killed); US fired 59 cruise missiles at Shayrat air base 7 April. OPCW-UN JIM blamed the government. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khan_Shaykhun_chemical_attack; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use_of_chemical_weapons_in_the_Syrian_civil_war]
- Douma 7 April 2018 (43 killed); US, UK, France strikes 14 April 2018; OPCW IIT (January 2023) attributed it to the Syrian Air Force (chlorine). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douma_chemical_attack]
- GPPi documented 336 chemical attacks (23 Dec 2012 – 18 Jan 2019), mostly chlorine; OPCW suspended Syria's privileges April 2021. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use_of_chemical_weapons_in_the_Syrian_civil_war]
- "Caesar" (military-police photographer) smuggled ~55,000 images of ~11,000 dead detainees; report released 21 January 2014; HRW found the photos genuine. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Syrian_detainee_report]
- Sednaya prison ("Human Slaughterhouse"): Amnesty (Feb 2017) 5,000–13,000 extrajudicially executed Sept 2011–Dec 2015; SOHR (2021) estimate 30,000; opened by rebels 8 December 2024. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sednaya_Prison]
- Disappeared: SNHR (Dec 2024) recorded 96,321 enforced disappearances and 15,102 deaths under torture by government forces; UNGA created an independent institution on the missing (June 2023). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bashar_al-Assad]

## 15. The human cost (reuse Modern Conflicts figures)
- Dead: UN ≥350,209 identified (March 2011–March 2021, "certainly an under-count"); OHCHR ≥306,887 civilians (to March 2021, ~1.5% of pre-war population); monitors' totals 580,000 (May 2021) to ~656,493 (March 2025, SOHR). SNHR: ≥202,000 civilians killed by government forces (Dec 2024). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casualties_of_the_Syrian_civil_war; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bashar_al-Assad]
- Displacement: by 2014 ~9.5 million, half the population, displaced (4 million abroad); 13.4 million forcibly displaced by October 2025; Turkey hosted 3.6 million (2019), almost 3 million at Assad's fall. World Bank: GDP contracted 84% 2010–2023; >90% in poverty. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refugees_of_the_Syrian_civil_war]
- Returns since Assad's fall (figures vary by method): UNHCR-cited Syrian dashboard ~1.7 million refugee returnees by late June 2026; ~1.7 million IDP returns by February 2026; Turkish figures >667,000 returns from Turkey (Dec 2024–April 2026). [source: https://www.unhcr.org/sites/default/files/docs/default/2026-07/unhcr-returns-to-syria-regional-update-April-May-2026_0.pdf (search snippet); https://www.ecoi.net/en/file/local/2142531/Comprehensive%20Overview%20of%20Refugee%20Returnees%2025%206%20%202026.pdf (search snippet)]

## 16. The fall of Assad (27 November – 8 December 2024)
- HTS-led "Deterrence of Aggression" offensive launched 27 November 2024 (with the Turkish-backed SNA); entered Aleppo 29 November; Hama 5 December; Homs 7 December; Southern Operations Room advanced on Damascus from the south. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Syrian_opposition_offensives; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Assad_regime]
- 8 December 2024: Assad flew from Latakia/Hmeimim to Moscow and received asylum; the army command declared its soldiers out of service; Sednaya opened. PM al-Jalali stayed and handed over; Mohammed al-Bashir named caretaker PM on 10 December 2024. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Assad_regime]
- Israel declared the 1974 disengagement agreement void, moved into the buffer zone and struck military sites. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Assad_regime]

## 17. The transition (2025–26) — keep brief and attributed
- Ahmed al-Sharaa (born 29 October 1982, Riyadh; former al-Nusra/HTS leader "al-Julani") declared president 29 January 2025. Constitutional declaration 13 March 2025: five-year transitional period, presidential system, Islamic jurisprudence "the main source" of legislation. Transitional government formed 29 March 2025 with ministers from minority communities (an Alawite, a Druze, a Christian woman — Hind Kabawat — and a Kurd). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_al-Sharaa; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_transitional_government]
- Coastal massacres of Alawites, from 6 March 2025: UN Commission of Inquiry (report 14 August 2025) said some 1,400 people, mostly civilians, killed; Syria's national inquiry counted at least 1,426; the UN found acts that may amount to war crimes by government-affiliated factions but no evidence the central government ordered them. Other tallies: SOHR 1,659+ civilians; Reuters 1,479 Alawites (7–9 March). [source: https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/8/14/war-crimes-likely-committed-by-both-sides-in-syria-sectarian-violence-un-commission-says (search snippet); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_massacres_of_Syrian_Alawites]
- Suwayda (Druze south), from 13 July 2025: Druze–Bedouin fighting, army deployment 14–16 July, Israeli strikes including on Damascus; SOHR: 823 mostly Druze civilians executed by government forces; >192,000 displaced; Suwayda still largely outside government control in 2026. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Syria_clashes_(July%E2%80%93September_2025); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Syrian_parliamentary_election]
- Parliament: indirect elections 5 October 2025 (with by-elections into 2026) for 140 of 210 People's Assembly seats via government-appointed committees and electoral colleges; 70 appointed by the president; no direct popular vote. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Syrian_parliamentary_election]
- International: met Trump in Riyadh (May 2025); UN Security Council removed al-Sharaa from ISIL/al-Qaida sanctions list (November 2025); US delisted him 7 November 2025; first Syrian president at the White House, 10 November 2025; US repealed the Caesar Act in the FY2026 NDAA, signed 18 December 2025 (some outlets say 19 December). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_al-Sharaa; https://www.thenationalnews.com/news/mena/2025/12/20/syria-welcomes-repeal-of-sweeping-us-sanctions (search snippet)]
- Kurds: 10 March 2025 al-Sharaa–Mazloum Abdi integration agreement stalled; January 2026 government offensive in the northeast (13–30 January) took up to 80% of AANES territory; decree recognising Kurdish as a national language and Newroz a holiday; AANES fully integrated 20 August 2026 and the SDF dissolved. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_al-Sharaa; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Autonomous_Administration_of_North_and_East_Syria]
- Accountability: France issued arrest warrants for Bashar al-Assad (2025); a Damascus court issued a warrant (September 2025). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bashar_al-Assad]

## 18. Society: the Alawite question, minorities, voices
- Alawites (formerly "Nusayris", now a slur) ~9–12% of Syrians; historically among the poorest, rural and marginalised (Latakia city 80% Sunni, countryside 62% Alawite in the mandate era); remained relatively poor even under the Assads. Sunnis ~74% of population, Kurds 5–10%. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alawites; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurds_in_Syria]
- Yassin al-Haj Saleh (born 1961, Raqqa): imprisoned 1980–96 for membership of the Syrian Communist Party (Political Bureau), the last year in Tadmor; hid 21 months in 2011–13, then exile; wife Samira Khalil abducted in Douma December 2013. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yassin_al-Haj_Saleh]
- Samar Yazbek (born 1970, Jableh, Alawite family): joined the 2011 uprising, exiled months later; *A Woman in the Crossfire: Diaries of the Syrian Revolution* won the 2012 PEN Pinter "writer of courage" award; founded Women Now for Development (2012). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samar_Yazbek]
- Salwa Ismail (SOAS): *The Rule of Violence* argues the political prison and the massacre were instruments of government shaping Syrians' subjectivities; based on 150+ interviews in Syria 2002–11. [source: https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BB2666837X?l=en (search snippet)]

## Debates
- **Notables and the Mandate (Khoury).** Khoury asks why one urban landowning-bureaucratic elite (the "notables") kept dominating politics after the Ottomans and under France; his National Bloc chapters are titled "Honorable Cooperation, 1928–1933" — nationalism as negotiation with France. Critics: Daniel Pipes (Orbis) praised the research but alleged a "dogmatic Marxism" and neglect of pan-Syrian nationalism. [source: https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt7zvwbs (search snippet); https://press.princeton.edu/titles/2507.html (search snippet); https://www.danielpipes.org/11142/syria-and-the-french-mandate (search snippet)]
- **The Great Revolt (Provence vs the notables model).** Provence reads 1925 as a popular, rural, locally rooted insurgency led by war-hardened ex-Ottoman officers (the "last Ottoman generation"), with Damascus landowning elites often collaborating; critics say he downplays the notables' politics, and earlier accounts were said to overemphasise the urban elite. [source: https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.7560/706354/html (search snippet); https://utpress.utexas.edu/9780292706804/the-great-syrian-revolt-and-the-rise-of-arab-nationalism/ (search snippet)]
- **Seale on Assad.** Seale's biography, written with access to Hafez, stresses his patience and caution without hiding his ruthlessness (Publishers Weekly); Daniel Pipes called Seale the regime's leading apologist in the West. Present as an indispensable but sympathetic insider portrait. [source: https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780520066670 (search snippet); https://www.danielpipes.org/31/asad-of-syria-the-struggle-for-the-middle-east (search snippet)]
- **Wedeen's "politics of as if".** The cult worked not by making Syrians believe but by making them act as if they believed — public dissimulation that isolates citizens and enforces compliance; extended to Bashar in *Authoritarian Apprehensions* (2019). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisa_Wedeen; Cambridge symposium above (search snippet)]
- **Sectarianism vs class/region.** Van Dam (1979) stresses sectarian, regional and tribal loyalties: minorities (Alawis, Druze, Ismailis) and the rural poor rose through army and Ba'th, ending in an Alawi-dominated officer faction. Batatu (1999) foregrounds the rural "lesser notables" and peasants as the Ba'th's social base (with chapters on "the paramount influence of the 'Alawi officers" and "Is Asad's regime sectarian?"); a *Middle East Quarterly* review framed it as one side of the "great debate" — whether the regime's character comes from its rural or its 'Alawi background. Note also: Alawites stayed relatively poor; Sunnis held high posts; 2025 coastal massacres showed how "regime" became conflated with "Alawite". [source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/review-of-middle-east-studies/article/nikolaos-van-dam-the-struggle-for-power-in-syria-sectarianism-regionalism-and-tribalism-in-politics-19611978-new-york-st-martins-press-1979-103-pp-appendices-tables-notes-maps/43009277C94187382764126FD4900436 (search snippet); https://www.meforum.org/middle-east-quarterly/book-reviews/syrias-peasantry-the-descendants-of-its-lesser (search snippet); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alawites; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_massacres_of_Syrian_Alawites]
- **Causes of 2011 — the drought argument.** Kelley et al. (PNAS 2015) argued human-caused climate change made the 2007–10 drought 2–3 times more likely, leading to agricultural collapse, migration and unrest. Selby, Dahi, Fröhlich and Hulme (*Political Geography* 2017, "Climate change and the Syrian civil war revisited") found no sound basis to attribute the drought to climate change specifically, that migration was far smaller than claimed, and no evidence it contributed to the war's onset. Other causes named: repression, crony "social market" liberalisation, unemployment, corruption, the Arab Spring example, and the Daraa trigger. [source: https://sro.sussex.ac.uk/id/eprint/69311/ (search snippet); https://academiccommons.columbia.edu/doi/10.7916/D8MG7NDB (search snippet); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Background_and_causes_of_the_Syrian_revolution]
- **Revolution or civil/proxy war?** Syrian voices (al-Haj Saleh, Yazbek, Yassin-Kassab and Al-Shami) insist on the revolution's civic, nonviolent origins (LCCs, documentation work of Razan Zaitouneh) before militarisation and foreign intervention; the conflict is also widely described as a proxy war. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yassin_al-Haj_Saleh; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_civil_war]
- **CIA and the 1949 coup** — alleged, "highly controversial"; Copeland's memoirs unreliable. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_1949_Syrian_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat]

## Conflicts with published chapters
- *First World War* gives Arab forces reaching Damascus on 30 September 1918; WP's Faisal article says Damascus was taken "in October 1918" (Allied entry 1 October). Compatible — keep "30 September–1 October 1918".
- *First World War* says Faisal "proclaimed" the kingdom in 1920; sources say the Syrian National Congress proclaimed Faisal king on 8 March 1920 (as *Modern Middle East* correctly says). Minor; follow the Congress wording.
- *Modern Middle East* says France split the mandate into "Syria, Greater Lebanon, an Alawite State and a Jabal Druze State" — simplification: there were separate States of Damascus and Aleppo (1920–24) plus the autonomous Sanjak of Alexandretta. Not a contradiction; this chapter gives the detail.
- Ghouta: WP's chemical-weapons overview says "more than 1,729" killed, while the Ghouta article and *Modern Conflicts* give 281–1,729. Use the range.
- *Modern Conflicts* (SOHR, 1,614 civilians killed in March 2025 Alawite massacres, "provisional") vs later figures: UN COI ~1,400 (mostly civilians), national inquiry ≥1,426, SOHR later 1,659+. Not a contradiction; give the range and note figures were revised.
- No other contradictions found with the *Ottomans*, *Interwar* or *Modern Middle East* chapters.
