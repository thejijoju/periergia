## Women's history and gender history: the field
- Gerda Lerner (born Gerda Kronstein, Vienna, 30 April 1920; died Madison, Wisconsin, 2 January 2013), Austrian-born American historian, a founder of the academic field of women's history. She was jailed with her mother after the 1938 Anschluss and later emigrated to New York. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerda_Lerner]
- Lerner offered the first regular college course in women's history in 1963. She founded Sarah Lawrence College's MA in Women's History (offered from 1972), the first American graduate degree in the field, and later launched the first PhD programme in women's history at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, where she moved in 1980. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_history ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerda_Lerner]
- Lerner's other works include *Black Women in White America: A Documentary History* (1972), *The Majority Finds Its Past: Placing Women in History* (1979), and *The Creation of Feminist Consciousness* (1993), volume two of *Women and History*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerda_Lerner]
- The field of women's history "exploded" after 1970, alongside the new social history. Deborah Gray White's *Ar'n't I a Woman? Female Slaves in the Plantation South* (1985) was a pioneering work that brought women into the history of race and slavery. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_history]
- Joan Kelly's essay "Did Women Have a Renaissance?" questioned whether a standard period such as the Renaissance applied to women at all. Its date is given as 1977 in one Wikipedia article and as 1976 in another (see Conflicts). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_history ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Kelly]
- Gender history grew out of women's history. It studies how gender difference was perceived and configured at different times and places, usually assuming that such differences are socially constructed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_history]
- Joan Wallach Scott (born 18 December 1941, Brooklyn), historian of France, professor emerita at the Institute for Advanced Study, Princeton. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Wallach_Scott]
- Scott's "Gender: A Useful Category of Historical Analysis" appeared in the *American Historical Review* 91, no. 5 (December 1986), pp. 1053–1075. It was reprinted in her *Gender and the Politics of History* (1988; revised edition 1999). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender:_A_Useful_Category_of_Historical_Analysis]
- Scott's definition has two parts: gender is based on perceived differences between the sexes, and it is also a primary way of signifying relationships of power. William Sewell called the second part "important and contentious" because it claims that gender matters in all areas of history, not only in women's history. (Paraphrase only. The exact wording of the definition was not verified; see NOT VERIFIED.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender:_A_Useful_Category_of_Historical_Analysis]
- The essay drew on Derrida, Foucault and the linguistic turn. When Scott first presented it at a seminar in 1985, she said, the audience "were, to a man, appalled". Joan Hoff accused it of "nihilism, presentism, ahistoricism, obfuscation, elitism, obeisance to patriarchy, ethnocentrism, irrelevance, and possibly racism". Linda Kerber called it "dazzling" and Sewell an "instant classic". By 2008 it was the *AHR*'s most-accessed article on JSTOR, with more than 38,000 accesses since 1997. Joanne Meyerowitz credited it with a "significant part in the broader shift from social to cultural history". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender:_A_Useful_Category_of_Historical_Analysis]
- Scott later published "Unanswered Questions" (2008) and "Gender: Still a Useful Category of Analysis?" (2010). [source: same]
- Louise Tilly and Joan Scott, *Women, Work, and Family* (1978), describe three stages in European history: a preindustrial household economy in which production was mostly for home use; a "family wage economy" of early industrialisation; and a later family consumer economy. They stress continuity in women's status. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_history ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Wallach_Scott]
- The history of masculinity emerged as a specialty in the 1990s. Gail Bederman distinguished "men's history" that builds on women's history from a kind that "mostly ignore[s] women's history findings and methodology". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_history]

## Patriarchy as a historical creation: Lerner
- Gerda Lerner, *The Creation of Patriarchy* (Oxford University Press, 1986), volume one of *Women and History*, won the American Historical Association's Joan Kelly Prize in 1986. It was followed by *The Creation of Feminist Consciousness*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Creation_of_Patriarchy]
- Lerner treats the creation of patriarchy in the ancient Near East as a process of about 2,500 years, from about 3100 BCE to about 600 BCE, rather than a single event. She argues that misogyny has historical rather than biological causes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Creation_of_Patriarchy]
- Her main claims: private property and class society formed after men appropriated women's sexual and reproductive capacities; archaic states had an interest in the patriarchal family; men learned to dominate other groups by first dominating women, and slavery began with the enslavement of women from conquered groups; laws divided "respectable" from "non-respectable" women, a division institutionalised through veiling; goddesses were displaced by a dominant male god, and Hebrew monotheism and Genesis made the creative power male; Aristotle cast women as incomplete humans. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Creation_of_Patriarchy]
- She also argued that women took part in their own subordination, for self-preservation or for class (and later "race") benefits. [source: same]
- Lerner held that patriarchy's main strength was ideological: in Western societies it "severed the connection between women and the Divine", replacing priestesses and powerful goddesses with a "male religious bureaucracy and an all-powerful male divinity". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerda_Lerner]
- Reception: Catharine Stimpson compared its "boldness, authority, and richness" to *The Second Sex*. Virginia Hunter's review (*Labour/Le Travail*, 1988) notes that Lerner spent eight years studying ancient Mesopotamia, and faults a missing citation at a key point about what preceded patriarchy. Geetanjali Gangoli (2017) values Lerner's arguments on women's complicity and on the reification of women's sexuality and reproduction, but questions how far a framework built on Western society applies to non-Western societies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Creation_of_Patriarchy ; https://releve.erudit.org/fr/revues/llt/1988-v22-llt_22/llt22re01.pdf ; https://core.ac.uk/download/96781436.pdf]

## Women rulers and powerful women
- **Enheduanna** (about 2300 BCE), daughter of Sargon of Akkad, was EN-priestess of the moon god Nanna at Ur, probably appointed by her father to bind Akkadian and Sumerian religion together. She is celebrated as the earliest known named author, credited with *The Exaltation of Inanna* (*Nin-me-šara*, 154 lines, about 100 surviving copies) and the *Temple Hymns*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enheduanna]
- Caveat on Enheduanna: her authorship is debated among Assyriologists. Black et al. note that all the manuscripts date from at least six centuries after her lifetime, come from scribal settings, and show no traces of Old Sumerian. Miguel Civil suggested that "Enheduanna" may name an office rather than a person. William Hallo defended her authorship. Marta Weigle introduced her to feminist scholars in 1978 as "the first known author in world literature". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enheduanna]
- **Hatshepsut**, daughter of Thutmose I and wife of Thutmose II, ruled first as regent for her stepson Thutmose III (who came to the throne at about age two, about 1479 BCE). She took the full royal titulary by about Year 7 (about 1472 BCE) and reigned until about 1458 BCE (Low Chronology). She was Egypt's second confirmed woman to rule in her own right, after Sobekneferu of the Twelfth Dynasty. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatshepsut]
- Hatshepsut's Punt expedition (about Year 9, about 1471 BCE) brought back 31 live myrrh trees and is commemorated in reliefs at Deir el-Bahari. Toward the end of Thutmose III's reign and under his son, her cartouches and images were chiselled off. Donald Redford saw this as political necessity rather than hatred. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatshepsut]
- **Wu Zetian** (Wu Zhao; born 17 February 624, died 16 December 705) was the only undisputed female sovereign in Chinese history and the only woman to take the title *huangdi*. She was empress consort of Gaozong (from 655), held de facto power from about 665, and was empress dowager from 683. She proclaimed her own Zhou dynasty in October 690 and was forced out in a palace coup of 20 February 705; the Tang were restored in March 705. She expanded the imperial examinations to recruit officials of humbler origin. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wu_Zetian]
- **Njinga Mbande** (about 1583 – 17 December 1663) ruled Ndongo (1624–1663) and Matamba (1631–1663) in present-day northern Angola. In 1621–22 she was her brother's envoy to the Portuguese in Luanda. Portugal declared war in 1626. She allied by marriage with the Imbangala warlord Kasanje, conquered Matamba (1631–1635), allied with the Dutch West India Company after the Dutch took Luanda in 1641, and made peace with Portugal in 1656. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nzinga_of_Ndongo_and_Matamba]
- Njinga and gender: in the 1630s she adopted male titles and clothing, kept an all-female bodyguard, and ordered her male concubines to wear women's clothing and call her king. Lurid tales of a harem of 50–60 men dressed as women who fought to the death come from Dapper's *Description of Africa* and are labelled legend "having no proof". Her opponents tried to use her gender to delegitimise her. In the 104 years after her death, queens ruled Ndongo/Matamba for at least 80. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nzinga_of_Ndongo_and_Matamba]
- The Ottoman "Sultanate of Women" (about 1534 to 1715) was a period of great political influence by harem women, headed by the *valide sultan* (the sultan's mother). Most consorts were of slave origin. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Imperial_Harem]
- Leslie Peirce, *The Imperial Harem: Women and Sovereignty in the Ottoman Empire* (Oxford University Press, 1993), argues that royal women's political power was a foreseeable product of the dynasty's structure rather than a corruption of it. Royal women trained princes, acted as regents, and shaped the "public culture of sovereignty" through ceremony, building and patronage. [source: https://academic.oup.com/book/47982 ; https://search.worldcat.org/title/27811454]

## Learned women, religion and gender
- **Ban Zhao** (45 or 49 – about 117/120 CE), courtesy name Huiban, was the first known female Chinese historian. She completed her brother Ban Gu's *Book of Han* after he died in prison in 92 CE, and tutored Empress Deng Sui. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ban_Zhao]
- Ban Zhao's *Lessons for Women* (*Nüjie*, also translated "Admonitions for Women") has seven chapters, beginning with "Humbleness". It sets out four virtues (virtue, speech, countenance, conduct) and also argues that women should be educated. As one of the *Four Books for Women*, it circulated widely in the late Ming and Qing. Modern readers disagree over whether it is proto-feminist or a Confucian endorsement of women's subordination. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lessons_for_Women ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ban_Zhao]
- **Hypatia** (born about 350–370; died March 415), Neoplatonist philosopher, astronomer and mathematician in Alexandria. Amid a political feud between the prefect Orestes, whom she advised, and Bishop Cyril, rumours blamed her for blocking their reconciliation. In March 415 she was murdered by a Christian mob led by a lector named Peter. Charles Kingsley's novel *Hypatia* (1853) romanticised her, and in the 20th century she became a feminist icon. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypatia]
- **Khadija bint Khuwaylid** (about 554 – November 619), an affluent Quraysh merchant, employed Muhammad to lead a trade caravan to Syria and then proposed marriage to him (595). She is credited as the first convert to Islam. Her death year was called the "Year of Sorrow". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khadija_bint_Khuwaylid]
- **Aisha bint Abi Bakr** (about 614 – about July 678) is credited with narrating over 2,000 hadiths, on law, ritual and theology as well as on Muhammad's private life. She was active for 44 years after his death and fought against Ali at the Battle of the Camel (656). Sunnis revere her as a leading scholar; Shia view her critically. (D. A. Spellberg, 1994, is a cited source on her legacy.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aisha]
- Leila Ahmed, *Women and Gender in Islam: Historical Roots of a Modern Debate* (Yale University Press, 1992), traces discourses on women from pre-Islamic Middle Eastern gender systems through Arabia at Islam's founding, classical Iraq, and modern Egypt. She argues that later misogyny was not originally or exclusively Muslim but came from Islam's negotiation with urban Middle Eastern norms that already existed. [source: https://nationalhumanitiescenter.org/?p=55957 ; https://www.pomoculture.org/?p=5426]
- Purdah ("curtain") is practised in some Muslim, Hindu and Zoroastrian communities. Many scholars argue that veiling and seclusion predate Islam: upper-class women's mobility was restricted in the Babylonian, Persian and Byzantine empires, and Muslims are thought to have adopted the practice after the 7th-century conquest of Iraq. Under Mughal rule purdah spread to upper-class Hindus. By the 19th century it was customary among high-caste Hindu and elite communities across India, and lower-class women did not observe it strictly. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdah]
- Buddhist nuns: according to the canon, the bhikkhunī order was founded five years after the monks' order at the request of women led by Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī, the Buddha's aunt and foster mother. Historians question the historicity of this account. The "Eight Garudhammas" made nuns subordinate to monks; their authorship "cannot be traced to the Buddha". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhikkhun%C4%AB]
- The *Therīgāthā* ("Verses of the Elder Nuns"), in the Pali Canon's Khuddaka Nikāya, contains 73 poems in 16 chapters, some dating to the late 3rd century BCE. It is called the earliest known collection of women's literature in India and worldwide. Attributions to named nuns may be conventional. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ther%C4%ABg%C4%81th%C4%81]
- The Theravada nuns' order, long lapsed, was revived in 1996 when 11 Sri Lankan women received full ordination at Sarnath, India. Full Tibetan Buddhist bhikkhunī ordination recommenced on 23 June 2022 in Bhutan, with 144 nuns. Full ordination lineages had survived in East Asian Mahayana (China, Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhikkhun%C4%AB]
- Christian convents: Hildegard of Bingen (about 1098 – 17 September 1179), Benedictine abbess, author of *Scivias* (1151), was named a Doctor of the Church on 7 October 2012. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hildegard_of_Bingen]
- Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz (12 November 1648 – 17 April 1695; a birth year of 1651 is also argued) was a Hieronymite nun in New Spain, called "The Tenth Muse". In her *Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz* she defended women's right to education and to intellectual authority. In 1691 she was reprimanded and ordered to stop writing. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juana_In%C3%A9s_de_la_Cruz]
- In the Ancien Régime, few French women held formal power except some queens and the heads of Catholic convents. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_history]

## Household economies, women's work, marriage and property
- Alice Clark argued (pessimistically) that 17th-century English capitalism lowered women's status by stripping their productive role in the household economy. Ivy Pinchbeck argued (optimistically) that capitalism created conditions for women's emancipation. Tilly and Scott stressed continuity. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_history]
- Ester Boserup (18 May 1910 – 24 September 1999), Danish economist, wrote *Woman's Role in Economic Development* (1970). It is described as "the first investigation ever undertaken into what happens to women in the process of economic and social growth throughout the Third World" and is credited as a motivation behind International Women's Year (1975). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ester_Boserup]
- Jack Goody (1976), drawing on Boserup, linked dowry ("diverging devolution", property passed to daughters at marriage) to intensive plough agriculture, private property and monogamy, where farming was largely men's work. He linked bridewealth to extensive hoe or shifting cultivation, where women did most of the farm work, polygyny was common, and bridewealth compensated the bride's family for her labour. Dowry is found across Eurasia from Japan to Ireland. Sylvia Yanagisako and others dispute the theory. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dowry]
- Bridewealth is practised in parts of Africa (where a bride price can be required for a customary marriage to be valid), East Asia, the Middle East and Melanesia. In Islamic law the *mahr* is a gift from the groom to the bride and is distinct from dowry (*jahez*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bride_price ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dowry]
- India's Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961, banned dowry. Dowry nonetheless remains common, and dowry deaths and murders persist. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dowry]
- In 1965 John Hajnal identified a "Western European marriage pattern" west of a line from roughly St Petersburg to Trieste: late marriage (mid-twenties) especially for women, a small age gap between spouses, a significant share never marrying, and neolocal nuclear households. To the east, early marriage and extended households were the norm. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_European_marriage_pattern]
- Most Ottoman women could use the courts, buy and sell property, inherit and bequeath wealth, and bring divorce claims. European visitors such as Lady Mary Wortley Montagu found these rights unusual by European standards of the time. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_the_Ottoman_Empire]
- The 1870 US census, the first to count women by occupation, found women were 15% of the workforce (1.8 million of 12.5 million), one-third of factory "operatives" and two-thirds of teachers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_history]
- Parliamentary representation: in 1900 no woman had ever been elected to a national legislature. Finland elected the first in 1907. Women averaged 3% of legislators by 1945 and 20% by 2015. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_history]

## Footbinding and seclusion
- Footbinding probably began among court dancers in the 10th century (Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms; the story of Li Yu's dancer Yao Niang). It spread among Song elites and reached lower classes by the Qing. The earliest possible references date from about 1100. In the 13th century Che Ruoshui wrote the first known criticism of it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_binding]
- Manchu emperors tried to ban footbinding in the 17th century, and Kangxi abandoned the effort in 1668. By the 19th century an estimated 40–50% of Chinese women had bound feet, rising to almost 100% among upper-class Han women; rates varied greatly by region and group. On Taiwan's 1905 census, more than two-thirds of Hoklo women had bound feet, against under 1% of Hakka and Indigenous women (John Shepherd). Laurel Bossen and Hill Gates found binding nearly universal among inland Han women born before 1910. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_binding]
- The first Western-led anti-footbinding society was formed in Xiamen (Amoy) in 1874 (60–70 Christian women; missionary John MacGowan). Alicia Little founded a Natural Foot Society in Shanghai in 1895. The Republic of China banned footbinding in 1912, though the ban was not actively enforced. The practice had virtually disappeared by 1949; the last new case was reported in 1957; and the last lotus-shoe factory (Harbin) closed in 1999. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_binding]
- Dorothy Ko (*Cinderella's Sisters*, 2005) proposes that footbinding was understood as Confucian civility and correct attire or adornment. A 1591 encyclopedia classed it under "Female Adornments". (Published chapters already cover this; see Conflicts.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_binding]

## The witch-hunts
- From about 1400 to 1775, about 100,000 people were prosecuted for witchcraft in Europe and British America (Levack 2013), and 40,000–60,000 were executed, almost all in Europe. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witch_trials_in_the_early_modern_period]
- Attributed estimates of executions: Scarre and Callow (2001) about 40,000; Levack (2006) about 45,000; Hutton (2010) 40,000–50,000; Behringer (2004) and Roper (2004), calculating independently, 50,000–60,000. [source: same]
- The peak came in 1560–1630, with more than 40,000 deaths. Prosecutions were particularly severe in parts of the Holy Roman Empire. Accusations usually came from neighbours. [source: same]
- Roughly 80% of those convicted were women, most of them over 40. In Iceland, Finland, Estonia and Russia, the majority of the accused were men. [source: same]
- The "nine million" figure comes from Gottfried Christian Voigt (1784), who used it to argue that Voltaire's estimate of "several hundred thousand" was too low. It survived as a "popular myth", especially in feminist and neo-pagan literature. Barbara Ehrenreich and Deirdre English (1973) argued that the victims were traditional healers and midwives. Historians found that cunning folk were a minority of the accused. [source: same]
- *Malleus Maleficarum* was written by Heinrich Kramer (Institoris) and first published at Speyer in 1486 (the infobox gives 1487 as the date of issue). It followed Innocent VIII's bull *Summis desiderantes affectibus* (1484). The faculty of Cologne condemned it. It argued that women were more susceptible to demonic temptation through the "weaknesses of their sex", and its title uses the feminine *maleficarum*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malleus_Maleficarum]
- Silvia Federici, *Caliban and the Witch: Women, the Body and Primitive Accumulation* (Autonomedia, 2004), argues that the witch-hunts restructured family relations and women's roles to serve emerging capitalism. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliban_and_the_Witch]
- Witch-hunts after Europe and beyond it: the Santhal witch trials of 1792 are the earliest documented witch-hunts in India. The East India Company banned witch-persecution in Gujarat, Rajputana and Chota Nagpur in the 1840s–1850s. More than 3,000 people were reportedly killed by lynch mobs as alleged witches in Tanzania between 2005 and 2011. Saudi Arabia is the only country where witchcraft remains legally punishable by death. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witch_hunt ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witch_trials_in_the_early_modern_period]
- In 1782 Anna Göldi was executed in Glarus, Switzerland, officially for "poisoning"; the case is widely called the last European witch execution in substance. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witch_trials_in_the_early_modern_period]

## Empire and colonial encounters
- **Malintzin / Doña Marina / La Malinche** (about 1500 – about 1529), a Nahua woman, was one of 20 enslaved women given to the Spaniards in 1519 by the people of Tabasco. She spoke Nahuatl and Maya, and became interpreter, adviser and intermediary for Cortés during the invasion of the Aztec Empire (1519–1521). Her son Martín Cortés was one of the first mestizos of New Spain. In Mexico she is seen variously as traitor, victim and symbolic mother of the nation; *malinchista* means a disloyal compatriot. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Malinche]
- Camilla Townsend, *Malintzin's Choices: An Indian Woman in the Conquest of Mexico* (University of New Mexico Press, 2006), tries to show events "through indigenous eyes" (Restall's review) and rejects earlier myths. [source: https://ubcpress.ca/malintzins-choices ; https://muse.jhu.edu/journals/the_americas/v064/64.2restall.pdf]
- **Sati.** William Carey counted 438 cases within 30 miles of Calcutta in 1803. The British began systematic data collection in Bengal in 1815, and recorded cases in the Bengal Presidency rose from 378 (1815) to 839 (1818). Ram Mohan Roy campaigned against sati from 1812. Governor-General Lord William Bentinck issued Regulation XVII on Sunday 4 December 1829, making sati illegal and punishable in the criminal courts. The Privy Council rejected an orthodox Hindu petition and upheld the ban in 1832. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sati_(practice)]
- After the 1987 sati of 18-year-old Roop Kanwar in Rajasthan, India passed the Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act, 1987, criminalising the aiding or glorifying of sati. There were 30 reported cases or attempts in India in 1943–1987 (the official figure is 28). [source: same]
- Lata Mani, *Contentious Traditions: The Debate on Sati in Colonial India* (University of California Press, 1998). She argues that the women who burned "were neither subjects nor even the primary objects of concern in the debate on its prohibition" but rather "the ground" for struggles over Hindu tradition, colonial and evangelical civilising missions, and the role of the colonial state. She also notes that all parties shared a belief in a "golden age" of Indian women followed by decline. [source: https://www.ucpress.edu/books/contentious-traditions/paper ; https://timeshighereducation.com/cn/node/90357 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sati_(practice)]
- Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak, "Can the Subaltern Speak?", in Nelson and Grossberg (eds.), *Marxism and the Interpretation of Culture* (Macmillan, 1988), pp. 271–313. It analyses British abolitionist rhetoric on sati as a civilising mission that claimed to rescue women while ignoring their voices. (For the famous line, see NOT VERIFIED.) [source: https://scalar.libraries.wsu.edu/fournier/spivak-can-the-subaltern-speak ; https://www.electrostani.com/2023/03/can-subaltern-speak-in-plain-english.html]
- Mrinalini Sinha, *Colonial Masculinity: The "Manly Englishman" and the "Effeminate Bengali" in the Late Nineteenth Century* (Manchester University Press, 1995), studies masculinity in four controversies: the Ilbert Bill (1883), the Native Volunteer movement (1885), the Public Service Commission (1886) and the Age of Consent Bill (1891). [source: https://gwonline.unc.edu/node/1482 ; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/179238]
- Hijras under British rule: beginning in the 1850s, colonial officials treated hijras as "a breach of public decency". Jessica Hinchy calls the reaction to the 1852 murder of a hijra named Bhoorah a "moral panic". Hijras were placed under the Criminal Tribes Act (1871), subject to registration and surveillance. Hinchy, *Governing Gender and Sexuality in Colonial India: The Hijra, c.1850–1900* (Cambridge University Press, 2019), argues that criminalisation also drew on local factors, including elite Indian attitudes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijra_(South_Asia) ; https://newbooksnetwork.com/jessica-hinchy-governing-gender-and-sexuality-in-colonial-india-the-hijra-c-1850-1900-cambridge-up-2019]

## Third genders and LGBTQ+ histories (handle with care)
- General caution: "third gender" is an outside analytical label applied to very different roles (hijra, fa'afafine, Balkan sworn virgins, and others). Recognising a third gender "does not in itself mean that they were valued by that culture"; some such practices developed in direct reaction to the devaluation of women. Scholars such as Johanna Schmidt (on Samoan fa'afafine) criticise Western readings that recast such roles as sexual orientation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_gender]
- **Hijra** (South Asia; in Pakistan *khawaja sira*) live in guru–chela kinship communities. The identity has roots in ancient Hinduism and evolved under the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughals. In April 2014 India's Supreme Court (*NALSA v. Union of India*) recognised hijras, transgender people, eunuchs and intersex people as a "third gender" in law. Nepal, Pakistan, India and Bangladesh all legally recognise a third gender. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijra_(South_Asia)]
- **Two-spirit** is a contemporary pan-Indian umbrella term adopted in 1990 at the Third Annual Inter-tribal Native American, First Nations, Gay and Lesbian American Conference near Winnipeg, to replace the anthropologists' term *berdache*. The Ojibwe translation *niizh manidoowag* was coined at the same time. Neither term appears in the historical record, and the term is not meant to replace specific Indigenous terms such as Lakota *winkte* or Navajo *nádleehí*. Not all Native cultures had such roles. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-spirit]
- **Balkan sworn virgins** (*burrnesha*): people assigned female at birth who take a vow of chastity and live socially as men. The practice is documented from the 15th century, especially in northern Albania, Kosovo and Montenegro, under the customary *Kanun of Lekë Dukagjini*. It allowed household headship and men's work in a strongly patriarchal society. In 2002 fewer than 102 were estimated to remain; around 2022, about twelve. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkan_sworn_virgins]
- **Bugis** (South Sulawesi, Indonesia; about 3 million, mostly Muslim) recognise five genders: *makkunrai*, *oroané*, *calalai*, *calabai* and *bissu* (revered androgynous priests or shamans). According to anthropologist Sharyn Graham Davies, the concept is at least six centuries old. Whether calabai, calalai and bissu are "third genders" is disputed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_in_Bugis_society]
- Michel Foucault, *The History of Sexuality*, vol. 1 (*La volonté de savoir*, 1976; English 1978), rejected the "repressive hypothesis". He argued that in the 19th century the man who committed sodomy, once seen as a sinner, became a member of a new "species", the homosexual. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_History_of_Sexuality]

## Masculinity
- Raewyn Connell (born 3 January 1944, Sydney), sociologist, published as R. W. Connell until 2005, coined "hegemonic masculinity". The concept was first developed in work on Australian schools and in *Gender and Power*, and draws on Gramsci's idea of hegemony. Connell describes multiple masculinities in hierarchy; contemporary hegemonic masculinity rests on the domination of women, a hierarchy among men, and the stigmatisation of homosexuality. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hegemonic_masculinity ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raewyn_Connell]
- Connell, *Masculinities* (Polity Press / University of California Press, 1995; 2nd ed. 2005). [source: https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9780520089983]
- John Tosh urged historians of masculinity to attend to behaviour, agency and family experience, not only cultural representation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masculinity]

## Suffrage and its global timing
- Early, limited or short-lived cases of women voting include the Corsican Republic (1755–1769), the Pitcairn Islands (1838), Wyoming Territory (1869), the Isle of Man (1881) and the Cook Islands (1893). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage]
- New Zealand was the first self-governing nation in which all women could vote in parliamentary elections; royal assent was given on 19 September 1893. Suffrage petitions gathered over 9,000 signatures (1891), almost 20,000 (1892) and nearly 32,000 (1893), almost a quarter of the adult European female population. Kate Sheppard of the Women's Christian Temperance Union led the campaign, and Māori women were included. Women could not stand for the House of Representatives until 1919. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage_in_New_Zealand]
- South Australia granted women the right to stand for parliament in 1895, a first for the modern world; Catherine Helen Spence stood in 1897. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage]
- Finland (an autonomous Grand Duchy of the Russian Empire) introduced racially equal women's suffrage in 1906. In 1907 it elected the world's first female MPs: 19 women, under 10% of the 200 seats. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage_by_country]
- Norway: middle-class women gained the parliamentary vote in 1907, and universal women's suffrage was adopted unanimously in 1913. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage_by_country]
- Switzerland granted women the federal vote in 1971. Appenzell Innerrhoden granted women the local vote only in 1991, under order of the Federal Supreme Court. Liechtenstein followed in 1984, after failed referendums in 1968, 1971 and 1973. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage_by_country]
- Saudi Arabia: under King Abdullah's decision, women voted and stood for office for the first time in the December 2015 municipal elections, and some won seats. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage]
- Seneca Falls Convention, Wesleyan Chapel, Seneca Falls, New York, 19–20 July 1848, organised by local Quaker women with Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott. The Declaration of Sentiments (principal author Stanton) was signed by 100 of about 300 attendees, 68 women and 32 men. Mott urged dropping the suffrage resolution, and Frederick Douglass, the sole African American attendee, argued successfully to keep it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_Falls_Convention ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_Sentiments]
- Huda Sha'arawi founded the Egyptian Feminist Union in 1923. (Covered in the Modern Middle East chapter; cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huda_Sha%27arawi]

## The global women's movement and the UN
- The UN General Assembly passed the Declaration on the Elimination of Discrimination Against Women on 7 November 1967. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Conference_on_Women,_1975]
- The UN proclaimed 1975 International Women's Year. The World Conference on Women was held in Mexico City from 19 June to 2 July 1975, the first UN conference focused solely on women's issues. Helvi Sipilä organised it. Cold War divisions shaped it: the US stressed legal and political rights, while the Soviet bloc stressed women as nurturers working for peace. A parallel NGO Tribune (about 4,000 participants, per the UN) for the first time fed input into an official meeting. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Conference_on_Women,_1975 ; https://www.un.org/en/node/98004]
- Domitila Barrios de Chungara (7 May 1937 – 13 March 2012), a Bolivian miners' wife and labour leader, gained international attention at the 1975 Tribune by speaking for mineworkers' struggles. [source: https://www.wikipedia.com/wiki/Domitila_Ch%C3%BAngara ; https://mail.twn.my/title2/resurgence/2012/259/women1.htm]
- The UN Decade for Women ran from 1976 to 1985, with conferences in Mexico City (1975), Copenhagen (1980) and Nairobi (1985). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Decade_for_Women]
- CEDAW (Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women) was adopted on 18 December 1979 and entered into force on 3 September 1981. It has 189 states parties, and over fifty ratified with reservations. The United States and Palau have signed but not ratified; the Holy See, Iran, Somalia, Sudan and Tonga have not signed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_on_the_Elimination_of_All_Forms_of_Discrimination_Against_Women]
- The Fourth World Conference on Women was held in Beijing, 4–15 September 1995. Representatives of 189 governments and 17,000 participants attended, with about 30,000 at the parallel NGO Forum. It adopted the Beijing Platform for Action. Hillary Rodham Clinton spoke on 5 September 1995. Beverley Palesa Ditsie, on 13 September 1995, became the first African lesbian to address the UN on LGBT rights. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Conference_on_Women,_1995]

## Intersectionality and critiques from the Global South
- Kimberlé Crenshaw (born 5 May 1959, Canton, Ohio), law professor at UCLA and Columbia, coined "intersectionality" in 1989 in "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex: A Black Feminist Critique of Antidiscrimination Doctrine, Feminist Theory and Antiracist Politics" (*University of Chicago Legal Forum*). She drew on *DeGraffenreid v. General Motors* (1976), in which Black women auto workers could not prove race or sex discrimination separately. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimberl%C3%A9_Crenshaw ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersectionality]
- Precursors: Sojourner Truth's 1851 "Ain't I a Woman?" speech (its wording as published is disputed); Anna J. Cooper, Maria W. Stewart and Ida B. Wells; Pauli Murray's "Jane Crow"; and the Combahee River Collective (Boston, organised 1974), which wrote of "interlocking oppressions" and "simultaneity". Patricia Hill Collins developed the "matrix of domination". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersectionality]
- Chandra Talpade Mohanty (born 1955, Bombay), "Under Western Eyes: Feminist Scholarship and Colonial Discourses", *boundary 2* 12:3/13:1 (1984), critiqued Western feminism's construction of a homogeneous, victimised "Third World woman" whom Western feminists must save. It is reprinted in her *Feminism Without Borders* (Duke University Press, 2003), where she reaffirms the possibility of cross-border solidarity. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandra_Talpade_Mohanty]
- Oyèrónkẹ́ Oyěwùmí (born 10 November 1957, Ogbomoso, Nigeria), *The Invention of Women: Making an African Sense of Western Gender Discourses* (1997), won the American Sociological Association's 1998 Distinguished Book Award. She argues that among the Oyo-Yoruba seniority, not gender, organised society. (The Women in African History chapter covers this and its critics in depth; cross-refer.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oy%C3%A8r%C3%B3nk%E1%BA%B9%CC%81_Oy%C4%9Bw%C3%B9m%C3%AD]

## Feminist "waves" debate
- Nancy A. Hewitt (ed.), *No Permanent Waves: Recasting Histories of U.S. Feminism* (Rutgers University Press, 2010), argues that the wave metaphor cannot account for overlapping movements, chronologies and sites. She proposes thinking of radio waves rather than ocean waves. She traces the metaphor to Frances Power Cobbe in 1884. By 1968 US feminists had lumped their predecessors into a "first wave" going back to the 1840s, and younger activists called themselves a "third wave" in the 1990s. [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/books/9780813549170/9780813549170-5.pdf ; https://search.worldcat.org/oclc/642204450]
- Simone de Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949) became a foundational text of contemporary feminism. The contraceptive pill (1960) is cited as a trigger for change in the late 1960s and 1970s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_history]

## Debates
- **Women's history versus gender history.** Women's history recovers women as subjects ("supplementary" history, which Scott argued also exposes why women were left out). Gender history, following Scott (1986), treats gender as a way of signifying power in all history, including male institutions, masculinity and sexuality. Joan Hoff attacked Scott's post-structuralism; Kerber and Sewell praised it. Meyerowitz credits the essay with helping move the profession from social to cultural history. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender:_A_Useful_Category_of_Historical_Analysis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_history]
- **Origins of patriarchy.** Lerner places its creation in the archaic states of the ancient Near East (about 3100–600 BCE), stressing ideology and women's complicity. Critics question her evidence chain (Hunter) and the reach of a Western-centred framework (Gangoli). Goody and Boserup tie gender systems to agricultural regimes, plough against hoe. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Creation_of_Patriarchy ; https://core.ac.uk/download/96781436.pdf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dowry]
- **Capitalism and women's status.** Alice Clark (pessimistic: loss of the household economy), Ivy Pinchbeck (optimistic: conditions for emancipation), Tilly and Scott (continuity in three stages). Federici links the witch-hunts to primitive accumulation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_history ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliban_and_the_Witch]
- **Witch-hunts.** Scholarly figures (40,000–60,000 executed) against the myth of "nine million". Gendered persecution (about 80% women; Barstow stresses misogyny) against evidence that women were often accusers and that men were the majority of the accused on Europe's margins. The healer/midwife thesis (Ehrenreich and English 1973) is not supported. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witch_trials_in_the_early_modern_period]
- **Footbinding.** May Fourth and feminist readings of bodily violence; Ko's reading of it as women's culture and civility; Bossen and Gates's economic argument (girls kept at spinning and weaving). (Ming–Qing chapter.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_binding]
- **Seclusion and veiling.** Islamic, or older Mediterranean and Near Eastern practice? Purdah's spread to Hindu elites through emulation of the nobility; class limits on seclusion. Ahmed locates misogyny in Islam's encounter with pre-existing urban norms. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdah ; https://www.pomoculture.org/?p=5426]
- **Colonial "rescue" and women's agency.** Mani (women as the "ground" of debate), Spivak (the civilising mission's silencing of women), Mohanty (the homogenised "Third World woman"), Oyěwùmí (gender as a colonial category). Debate over whether such readings neglect women's material suffering: some reviewers say Mani does attend to it. [source: https://www.ucpress.edu/books/contentious-traditions/paper ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandra_Talpade_Mohanty]
- **Malinche:** traitor, victim, or "mother of the nation"? Townsend's attempt to recover her choices from Indigenous sources. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Malinche]
- **Third genders.** Recovering gender diversity against Western projection: two-spirit is a 1990 coinage; "third gender" labels are disputed for Bugis roles and fa'afafine; recognition does not equal high status. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_gender ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-spirit]
- **Waves.** Hewitt and others reject or rework the wave metaphor because it erases continuity and non-white, non-Western activism. Intersectionality (Crenshaw 1989; Combahee) challenges a single category "woman". [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/books/9780813549170/9780813549170-5.pdf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersectionality]
- **Enheduanna's authorship** (Hallo against Civil and Black) shows how much "firsts" in women's history depend on contested evidence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enheduanna]
