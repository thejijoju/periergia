## The long view: humans have always moved (cross-refer, do not repeat)
- *Homo sapiens* left Africa more than once; the dispersal that produced nearly all living non-Africans came "sometime after about 60,000 years ago" — this is the wording of the published **Human Origins** chapter; cross-refer rather than restate. [source: supabase/content/history--before-writing--human-origins.sql]
- Bantu expansion: between about 6,000 and 1,500 years ago speakers of Proto-Bantu languages moved out of a homeland in north-western Cameroon and south-eastern Nigeria across Central, Eastern and Southern Africa — wording of the published **Africa to 1860** chapter; cross-refer. [source: supabase/content/history--regional-histories--africa-to-1860.sql]
- Austronesian expansion began from Taiwan around 5,000 years ago — wording of the published **Pacific Voyagers** chapter; cross-refer. [source: supabase/content/history--regional-histories--pacific-voyagers.sql]
- Climate history suggests successive waves of Eurasian nomadic movement had origins in climatic cycles that expanded or contracted pastureland in Central Asia (a hypothesis in the field, not settled). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_migration]

## The word and concept of "diaspora"
- "Diaspora" derives from the Ancient Greek verb *diaspeirō*, "I scatter, I spread about", from *dia* ("between, through, across") + *speirō* ("I sow, I scatter"); *diaspora* meant "scattering". The sowing metaphor is built into the word. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaspora]
- Its first recorded use is in the Septuagint (the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible), first at Deuteronomy 28:25, "thou shalt be a dispersion in all kingdoms of the earth"; the word occurs 14 times in the Septuagint (Stéphane Dufoix, *The Dispersion: A History of the Word Diaspora*, Brill, 2016). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaspora ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_diaspora]
- The broader social-science sense was first introduced to academia in Simon Dubnow's entry "Diaspora" in the 1931 *Encyclopedia of the Social Sciences*; the word became more widely used in English for other dispersed populations by the mid-1950s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaspora]
- Diaspora studies grew rapidly: use of "diaspora" in dissertation abstracts rose from once or twice a year in the 1970s to about 130 by 2001; the field was institutionalised by the founding in 1991 of the journal *Diaspora: A Journal of Transnational Studies*, edited by Khachig Tölölyan. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaspora]
- **William Safran** (1991), "Diasporas in Modern Societies: Myths of Homeland and Return", *Diaspora* 1(1): 83–99, set out six criteria distinguishing diasporas from migrant communities, including a myth or collective memory of the homeland, regarding the ancestral homeland as true home to which they will eventually return, commitment to the homeland's restoration, and relating "personally or vicariously" to it. His definitions were influenced by the Jewish case. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaspora]
- Safran (8 July 1930 – 22 January 2026), political scientist at the University of Colorado Boulder, was himself a Holocaust survivor born in Dresden who spent more than three years in a ghetto, forced-labour camp and concentration camp, and four months in a UN displaced persons camp. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Safran]
- **Robin Cohen**, *Global Diasporas: An Introduction* (London: UCL Press & Seattle: University of Washington Press, 1997; revised 2nd edition Routledge, 2008), argued that a diasporic group could leave its homeland voluntarily and assimilate deeply. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Cohen ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaspora]
- Cohen's typology (as reported in reviews; search summary): **victim** diasporas (Jews, Africans, Armenians), **labour** (indentured Indians), **imperial** (British), **trade** (Lebanese, Chinese) and **cultural** (Caribbean); later editions added a "deterritorialised" type. Sources slightly disagree on which example sits under which label. [source: https://www.postcolonialweb.org/diasporas/cohen.html ; https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/87316 (search summary)]
- **Rogers Brubaker**, "The 'diaspora' diaspora", *Ethnic and Racial Studies* 28(1) (2005), proposed three criteria: dispersion (voluntary or forced); "the orientation to a real or imagined 'homeland' as an authoritative source of value, identity and loyalty"; and boundary maintenance. He found 17 of 18 books on diaspora published 1900–1910 were on the Jewish diaspora, but in a 2002 sample only 2 of 20 were. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaspora]
- **Paul Gilroy**, *The Black Atlantic: Modernity and Double Consciousness* (Harvard University Press and Verso, 1993), describes a distinct black Atlantic culture combining African, American, British and Caribbean elements; the first chapter deals with the "double consciousness" of Africans in the diaspora; Gilroy calls the black Atlantic a "modern political and cultural formation" and wants it freed from nation-state frames. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Black_Atlantic]
- **Brent Hayes Edwards** (Columbia University), *The Practice of Diaspora: Literature, Translation, and the Rise of Black Internationalism* (Harvard University Press, 2003). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brent_Hayes_Edwards]
- The term "African diaspora" most commonly refers to people of African heritage, including descendants of enslaved Africans; less commonly to recent emigration from Africa. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_diaspora]
- The Armenian word for the diaspora is *spyurk*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_diaspora]

## Jewish diaspora
- In 722 BCE the Assyrians under Sargon II conquered the Kingdom of Israel and deported many Israelites to Mesopotamia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_diaspora]
- After Nebuchadnezzar II overthrew the Kingdom of Judah in 586 BCE and deported much of its population, Jews had two cultural centres, Babylonia and the land of Israel. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_diaspora]
- Jewish communities existed in the Aegean islands, Greece, Asia Minor, Cyrenaica, Italy and Egypt from as early as the third century BCE — i.e. well before 70 CE. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_diaspora]
- Roman defeats of the Jews in Judaea in 70 CE and 135 CE (Bar Kokhba revolt) sent many captives into slavery and increased emigration. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_diaspora]
- The Alhambra Decree (1492) expelled Spain's Jews: of an estimated 300,000 people of Jewish origin, over 200,000 had converted, and between 40,000 and 100,000 who remained Jewish were expelled; Sephardi Jews went to North Africa, the Ottoman Empire, Portugal and Italy; Sultan Bayezid II welcomed them, especially to Thessaloniki and İzmir. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alhambra_Decree]
- Beginning in the 1880s, waves of pogroms in the Russian Empire spurred a great outward migration: more than two million Jews left Russia between 1880 and 1920, mostly for the United States and Palestine. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Russia]

## Armenian diaspora
- The Armenian diaspora is one of the oldest and largest in the world; the modern diaspora was largely formed by the genocide committed by the Ottoman Empire. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_diaspora]
- On 24 April 1915 Ottoman authorities arrested and deported hundreds of Armenian intellectuals and leaders from Constantinople; at the orders of Talaat Pasha an estimated 800,000 to 1.2 million Armenians were sent on death marches to the Syrian desert in 1915. Deaths estimated at 600,000–1.5 million. About two million Armenians lived in Ottoman territory in 1914. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_genocide]
- Earlier commercial diaspora: the New Julfa Armenian merchant network is already covered in the published **Safavid** chapter (Sebouh Aslanian); cross-refer. [source: supabase/content/history--early-modern-period--safavid.sql]
- From 1924 the League's Nansen arrangement was extended to Armenian refugees (see Passports below). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nansen_passport]
- A further emigration wave from (Soviet/post-Soviet) Armenia followed the dissolution of the USSR in the 1990s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_diaspora]

## Lebanese (Syro-Lebanese) diaspora
- Akram Fouad Khater, *Inventing Home: Emigration, Gender, and the Middle Class in Lebanon, 1870–1920* (University of California Press, 2001): publisher's description states that "Between 1890 and 1920 over one-third of the peasants of Mount Lebanon left their villages and traveled to the Americas"; the preface puts peasant emigrants at over 200,000 in the 25 years after 1890 (search summary). [source: https://ucpress.edu/books/inventing-home/paper ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akram_Fouad_Khater]
- A broader estimate: about half a million people left the Ottoman eastern Mediterranean for abroad between the 1870s and 1930s (MESPI reading guide; search summary). [source: https://mespi.org/?p=2890]
- The Great Famine of Mount Lebanon, 1915–1918, killed about 200,000 people (by some accounts half the population of the Mount Lebanon Mutasarrifate). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_of_Mount_Lebanon]
- Lebanese migration to Africa began in the late 19th century from Ottoman Mount Lebanon; about 450,000 people of Lebanese origin are estimated in Africa, mostly West and Central Africa (Côte d'Ivoire, Senegal, Ghana, Nigeria, Sierra Leone). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_diaspora]
- Size of the diaspora today is highly uncertain: estimates range from about 4 million to 15.4 million (the latter, an Arab News figure, against about 6 million in Lebanon); Brazil is usually cited as the largest community, with claims of 5.8–7 million Brazilians of Lebanese ancestry, but a 2008 IBGE survey suggested far fewer. Give as a disputed range only. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_diaspora]
- The Mahjar ("emigrant") literary movement: Kahlil Gibran was the most influential of the Mahjari poets; the first American Arabic-language newspaper, *Kawkab America*, was founded in New York in 1892; the Pen League (al-Rabita al-Qalamiyya) of New York formed (re-founded) in 1920. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahjar]
- Lebanese diaspora members include Brazil's president Michel Temer and the Mexican businessman Carlos Slim. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_diaspora]

## Slave diasporas (Atlantic and Indian Ocean) — cross-refer
- Atlantic slave trade figures already published: about 12.5 million embarked and about 10.7 million arrived — use exactly these and cross-refer to **The Atlantic Slave Trade**. [source: supabase/content/history--renaissance-and-reformation--atlantic-slave-trade.sql]
- Indian Ocean slave trade: estimating numbers is "controversial" (William Gervase Clarence-Smith). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_slave_trade]

## Other forced migration: convict transportation
- Britain's Transportation Act 1717 allowed courts to sentence convicts to seven years' transportation to America; returning early became a capital offence. Estimates of convicts sent to North America range from 50,000 (John Dunmore Lang) to 120,000 (Thomas Keneally). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penal_transportation]
- After American independence closed that outlet, the First Fleet sailed for Australia in 1787; transportation to Australia lasted until 1868 and totalled about 162,000 men and women (published **Australia** chapter: 162,000–164,000, sources differ). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penal_transportation ; supabase/content/history--regional-histories--australia.sql]
- British India transported convicts and political prisoners to the Andaman Islands; the Cellular Jail at Port Blair ("Kala Pani", black waters) was built 1896–1906 with 698 cells. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penal_transportation]
- France used Devil's Island / French Guiana (1852–1953) and New Caledonia (1860s–1897, about 22,000 convicts and political prisoners including Communards). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penal_transportation]
- Early modern religious refugees: after Louis XIV's Edict of Fontainebleau (1685) revoked Protestant rights, Huguenots fled; an estimated 50,000 Protestant Walloons and Huguenots went to England around the time of the 1708 Foreign Protestants Naturalization Act. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huguenots]

## The great migrations, 1840–1940
- **Adam McKeown** (1965–2017), "Global Migration, 1846–1940", *Journal of World History* 15(2) (June 2004): 155–189, showed Asian migration was comparable to transatlantic migration. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_McKeown]
- McKeown's three main long-distance circuits, 1846–1940 (Table 1 of the article; figures confirmed only through search summaries of a Macmillan teaching table and blog quotations — attribute explicitly to McKeown and give as ranges): (1) Europe to the Americas, **55–58 million**; (2) India and southern China to Southeast Asia, the Indian Ocean rim and the South Pacific, **48–52 million** (roughly 29–30 million Indians and 19–20 million Chinese); (3) Russia and northeast Asia to Manchuria, Siberia, Central Asia and Japan, **46–51 million** (including perhaps 28–33 million Chinese into Manchuria and Siberia, nearly 2 million Koreans, over 500,000 Japanese). [source: https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/strayersources3ehs/asset/snapshot/snapshot_long_distance_migration_in_an_age_of_empire_1846-1940.html (search summary); https://www.doi.org/10.1353/JWH.2004.0026]
- McKeown argued that the usual end date of 1914 for the age of mass migration is too early from a global perspective: global migration reached new peaks in the 1920s; and that the segmentation of migration into separate systems was as much the product of political intervention as of economics (search summary of the article). [source: https://www.doi.org/10.1353/JWH.2004.0026 (search summary)]
- McKeown's *Melancholy Order: Asian Migration and the Globalization of Borders* (Columbia University Press) argued that modern border control grew out of attempts to control Asian migration from the 1880s. Wikipedia's bibliography gives 2011 (likely the paperback); first edition year not verified — see NOT VERIFIED. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_McKeown]
- From 1815 through 1932, about sixty million people left Europe, mainly for "areas of European settlement" in the Americas, Australia, New Zealand and Siberia; from 1500 to the mid-20th century 60–65 million left Europe, less than 9% to tropical areas. Europe's population (incl. Russia) rose from 188 million (1800) to 432 million (1900). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_emigration]
- About 11 million Europeans went to Latin America in 1815–1918, of whom 38% were Italians, 28% Spaniards and 11% Portuguese; the foreign-born share in Brazil peaked in 1920 at about 7% (2 million). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_emigration]
- Published **Second Industrial Revolution** chapter: "More than 30 million Europeans migrated to the United States between 1836 and 1914"; cross-refer and keep consistent. [source: supabase/content/history--modern-era-19th-century--second-industrial-revolution.sql]
- Irish Great Famine (1845–1852): roughly one million died and over one million emigrated; Ireland's population fell by 20–25% between 1841 and 1871. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_(Ireland)]
- Ellis Island (opened 1 January 1892, closed 1954) processed over 12 million immigrants (Library of Congress). Angel Island Immigration Station in San Francisco Bay, the "Ellis Island of the West", operated from 21 January 1910 to 5 November 1940, and was designed largely to enforce Chinese exclusion. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellis_Island ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel_Island_Immigration_Station]
- Sunil Amrith (*Crossing the Bay of Bengal*, Harvard University Press, 2013) estimates that about 28 million people crossed the Bay of Bengal in both directions between 1840 and 1940; a review notes that from 1834 to 1940 about 90% of Indian migrants (more than 30 million) went to Ceylon, Burma and Malaya — not to the indenture colonies (search summary of reviews). [source: https://reviews.history.ac.uk/print/review/2042 (search summary); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunil_Amrith]
- Most Indian labour to Ceylon, Malaya and Burma went under the *kangani* system (from Tamil, "overseer"), recruitment through a headman-supervisor, rather than formal indenture; it grew more popular from the late 19th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kangani_system ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system]
- About 40 million ethnic Chinese lived abroad as of 2026 (Wikipedia estimate); Taishan in Guangdong and the provinces of Fujian and Guangdong were major sources; Sun Yat-sen used overseas Chinese communities to raise money for his revolution. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_Chinese]

## Indenture after abolition: Indian and Chinese
- The Indian indenture system carried more than 1.6 million workers (published chapters say "more than 1.6 million", 1834–1917; keep consistent). It began with the *Atlas* carrying 36 labourers from Calcutta to Mauritius in 1834; early voyages had mortality over 17%. Destinations included Mauritius, Natal, Réunion, British Guiana, Trinidad, Jamaica, Suriname and Fiji. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system]
- Emigration was briefly banned, then reauthorised on 2 December 1842 (to Mauritius), and in 1845 to the West Indies; in 1843 alone 30,218 men and 4,307 women entered Mauritius as indentured immigrants. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system]
- Women were few; from 1856–57 the Colonial Office required women to form 25% of emigrants to Demerara. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system]
- Indenture to Natal ended after Gokhale's 1910 resolution; the whole British Indian system ended in 1917 (published chapters), with remaining contracts running into the 1920s. The Economist: the Imperial Legislative Council ended it because of nationalist pressure and declining profitability "rather than from humanitarian concerns". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system ; supabase/content/history--modern-era-19th-century--abolition-and-emancipation.sql]
- Hugh Tinker, *A New System of Slavery: The Export of Indian Labour Overseas, 1830–1920* (Oxford University Press, 1974), argued voluntariness was a legal fiction; the phrase echoes Lord John Russell's 1840 fear (published chapters). Counter-view: David Northrup, *Indentured Labor in the Age of Imperialism* (Cambridge University Press, 1995; Wikipedia's citation gives the subtitle years as 1834–1920 — check the end year before printing it). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system]
- Indo-Fijians: once about 52% of Fiji's population in the late 20th century, about 33% by 2017, after emigration following the coups (from 1987). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Fijians]
- In 1972 Idi Amin gave Uganda's South Asians 90 days to leave (order in early August 1972); about 80,000 people of South Asian descent lived there; 27,200 went to the United Kingdom. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expulsion_of_Asians_from_Uganda]
- Chinese "coolie" trade: in 1847 two ships carried Chinese workers from Amoy (Xiamen) to Havana; demand was strong in Peru for silver mines and guano; Macau became the centre; about 215,000 Chinese were shipped from Macau 1851–1874, mainly to Cuba and Peru; Macau's trade ended in 1874 under British and Chinese pressure. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coolie]
- Average shipboard mortality on voyages to Cuba 1847–1859 was about 15%; losses on ships to Peru reached 40% in the 1850s; more than two-thirds of Chinese coolies arriving in Peru 1849–1874 died within their contract period. The trade was called the "pig trade". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coolie]
- Cuba's 1860 regulation forced coolies at the end of contract to recontract or leave at their own expense within two months — most could not afford to leave. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coolie]
- "Coolie" is today generally regarded as an offensive term/racial slur — the writer should use it only in quotation marks or as "the so-called coolie trade". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coolie]
- Published chapters already give 140,000–150,000 Chinese to Cuba before the mid-1870s; cross-refer. [source: supabase/content/history--modern-era-19th-century--second-industrial-revolution.sql]

## Exclusion and the racial border, 1875–1965
- US Page Act of 1875 banned Chinese women from entering, to prevent family settlement. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Exclusion_Act]
- The Chinese Exclusion Act, signed by President Chester A. Arthur on 6 May 1882, prohibited immigration of Chinese labourers for 10 years — the first major US law to bar a specific national group; renewed by the Geary Act (1892), made permanent in 1902; repealed in 1943, which allowed a quota of 105 Chinese immigrants a year. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Exclusion_Act]
- US Immigration Act of 1924 (Johnson–Reed Act) prevented immigration from Asia and set national-origin quotas at 2% of each nationality as counted in the 1890 census (down from 3% of 1910 under the 1921 Emergency Quota Act). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_Act_of_1924]
- The Hart–Celler Act, signed by Lyndon Johnson on 3 October 1965, abolished the national origins formula. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hart-Celler_Act]
- Canada: the Chinese Immigration Act of 1885 imposed a $50 head tax on Chinese entrants after completion of the Canadian Pacific Railway; raised to $100 in 1900 and $500 in 1903 (about two years' wages). The government collected about CA$23 million from about 81,000 payers. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_head_tax]
- Canada's Chinese Immigration Act, 1923, effective 1 July 1923 (Dominion Day), barred almost all Chinese immigration; Chinese Canadians called 1 July "Humiliation Day". Repealed 14 May 1947. Prime Minister Stephen Harper apologised in the House of Commons on 22 June 2006. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Immigration_Act,_1923 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_head_tax]
- Australia: the Immigration Restriction Act 1901, drafted by Alfred Deakin, used a dictation test in "any European language", modelled on the Natal Act 1897; the Migration Act 1958 abolished the test; the Holt government removed discrimination in 1966; the Whitlam government removed the last racial elements in 1973, and the Racial Discrimination Act 1975 outlawed race-based selection. Published **Australia** chapter covers this (dismantled 1949–1973) — cross-refer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Australia_policy]

## Passports and borders after 1914
- In the later 19th century until the First World War, passports were on the whole not required for travel within Europe; during the war European governments introduced passport requirements for security and to stop skilled people emigrating. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passport]
- Britain's British Nationality and Status of Aliens Act 1914 defined citizenship and created a booklet passport; British tourists in the 1920s complained of photographs and descriptions as "nasty dehumanisation". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passport]
- In 1920 the League of Nations' Paris Conference on Passports & Customs Formalities and Through Tickets produced guidelines and a standard booklet design; the modern passport was "universally adopted and standardised in 1920". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passport]
- Earlier precedent: Henry V of England is credited with an early form of British passport, referred to in a 1414 Act of Parliament (safe conducts). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passport]
- **Nansen passport**: triggered by the Soviet government's 1921 revocation of citizenship of Russians abroad, including some 800,000 Civil War refugees; agreed at an intergovernmental conference convened by Fridtjof Nansen in Geneva, 3–5 July 1922; extended to Armenians in 1924 and to Assyrian, Bulgarian and Turkish refugees in 1928; honoured by 52 countries by 1942; about 450,000 issued; issued 1922–1938. After Nansen's death in 1930 the Nansen International Office for Refugees took over; it received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1938. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nansen_passport ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nansen_International_Office_for_Refugees]
- **John Torpey**, *The Invention of the Passport: Surveillance, Citizenship and the State* (Cambridge University Press, 2000; some catalogues 1999; 2nd ed. 2018): states have "monopolized the 'legitimate means of movement'". Chapter 1 title (verified via Cambridge listing): "Coming and Going: On the State Monopolization of the Legitimate 'Means of Movement'". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Torpey ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/CBO9780511520990A012/type/BOOK_PART (search summary)]

## Population "unmixing" and refugees after 1914
- Greek–Turkish population exchange: Convention signed at Lausanne on 30 January 1923; about 1,221,489 Greek Orthodox Christians from Asia Minor, Eastern Thrace, the Pontic Alps and the Caucasus and 355,000–400,000 Muslims from Greece were exchanged, defined by religion rather than language. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_exchange_between_Greece_and_Turkey]
- Flight and expulsion of Germans 1944–1950: 12 million to 14.6 million displaced; deaths estimated from 500,000 to 2.5 million (strongly disputed range). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_and_expulsion_of_Germans_(1944%E2%80%931950)]
- UNRRA was established in 1944 to support millions of displaced people in Europe. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_High_Commissioner_for_Refugees]
- UNHCR's statute was annexed to UN General Assembly Resolution 428(V) of 1950; it was meant to operate for only three years from January 1951; its budget grew from US$300,000 in 1951. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_High_Commissioner_for_Refugees]
- **1951 Refugee Convention** (Geneva Convention of 28 July 1951): approved 28 July 1951, in force 22 April 1954; initially limited to people displaced by events before 1 January 1951 (states could also limit it to Europe). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_Relating_to_the_Status_of_Refugees]
- **1967 Protocol**: entered into force 4 October 1967; removed the temporal and geographic limits; 146 states are parties. The United States, Cape Verde and Venezuela are party only to the Protocol; Madagascar and Saint Kitts and Nevis only to the Convention; Turkey retains the geographic (Europe-only) limitation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protocol_Relating_to_the_Status_of_Refugees]
- **Partition, 1947**: use the published **Partition** chapter's figures exactly — 12–20 million displaced (1951 censuses counted about 14.5 million displaced persons), 200,000 to 2 million dead (Talbot and Singh). Wikipedia: about 14.5 million crossed the new borders; some 12 million were displaced in Punjab alone. [source: supabase/content/history--20th-century-history--partition.sql ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_India]
- **Palestinians, 1948**: more than 700,000 Palestinian Arabs, about half of Mandatory Palestine's Arab population, were expelled or fled (published chapters: 700,000–750,000; keep that). UN General Assembly Resolution 302(IV) of 8 December 1949 established UNRWA, passed unopposed (Soviet bloc and South Africa abstaining). Over 5.9 million Palestinians were registered with UNRWA as refugees as of 2026; descendants of Palestine refugee males are eligible to register. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_Palestinian_expulsion_and_flight ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNRWA]
- Algeria: after independence in 1962 about 800,000 *pieds-noirs* of French nationality left for mainland France; about 200,000 remained. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pieds-noirs]
- **Vietnamese boat people**: almost 800,000 reached other countries safely between 1975 and 1995; UNHCR estimates 200,000–250,000 died at sea (published chapters also give others' figures up to 400,000 and Barry Wain's 30,000 — cross-refer). Ethnic Chinese (Hoa) were targeted in 1978–79; a 1979 international conference led Vietnam to limit departures; UNHCR ran the Orderly Departure Program and the Comprehensive Plan of Action. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_boat_people]
- **Syria**: of a pre-war population of about 22 million, 6.7 million were refugees and 13 million displaced overall by December 2022 (published **Modern Conflicts** wording); over 13.4 million had been forcibly displaced by October 2025, at least 6.7 million of them abroad, more than half in Turkey; almost one million asylum applications in Europe by August 2017. Published **Modern Syria** chapter: some 1.7 million refugees had returned by late June 2026 (one UN-cited count). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refugees_of_the_Syrian_civil_war ; supabase/content/history--contemporary-history--modern-conflicts.sql]
- **Ukraine 2022**: the EU invoked the Temporary Protection Directive (first time) to let displaced Ukrainians stay, work and study across the EU; published **Modern Conflicts** gives UNHCR's 5.7 million Ukrainian refugees in September 2025 — cross-refer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_refugee_crisis]
- **UNHCR Global Trends (published June 2026)**: 117.8 million people forcibly displaced at the end of 2025, down from 123.2 million at end-2024 — the first fall in a decade; 41.6 million refugees (of whom 35.6 million under UNHCR's mandate, i.e. excluding UNRWA-registered Palestinians); 68.7 million internally displaced; Sudan the largest internal displacement (9.1 million); about 1 in 70 people worldwide displaced. ## Postwar guest workers and post-colonial migration
- West Germany signed recruitment agreements with Italy (22 November 1955), Spain and Greece (March 1960), Turkey (30 October 1961), Morocco (1963), South Korea (1963), Portugal (1964), Tunisia (1965) and Yugoslavia (1968). The Berlin Wall (August 1961) cut East German labour and sharpened demand. Recruitment was stopped in 1973 (the oil-crisis year) under Willy Brandt. About half the Turkish guest workers went home; many others brought families and stayed. East Germany used "contract workers" (*Vertragsarbeiter*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastarbeiter]
- Later, West Germany paid a "repatriation grant" (*Rückkehrprämie*) of DM 10,500 plus DM 1,500 per spouse and per child to jobless Turks, Moroccans and Tunisians who returned. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastarbeiter]
- Bracero Program (USA–Mexico), 1942–1964: 4.6 million work contracts (contracts, not individuals) — covered in **Modern America**; cross-refer. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bracero_Program ; supabase/content/history--regional-histories--modern-america.sql]
- *Empire Windrush* docked at Tilbury on 21 June 1948 and its 1,027 passengers (plus two stowaways) began disembarking the next day; the manifest shows 802 gave a Caribbean last residence. Until 1962 the UK had no immigration control on Citizens of the UK and Colonies; the Commonwealth Immigrants Act 1962 was the first control. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMT_Empire_Windrush]
- **Kafala** ("sponsorship") system binds a migrant worker's residency and employment to a sponsor (*kafeel*), usually the employer. Origins disputed: Gilbert Beaugé — an old Bedouin custom of protecting visitors; Omar AlShehabi (2021) — British colonial administrators who introduced sponsorship to control labour migration (linked to pearling). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kafala_system]
- Qatar announced several times between 2014 and 2021 that kafala had been abolished; human-rights organisations rejected the claim; in 2025 foreign workers were about 91% of Qatar's population and 95% of its labour force. Saudi Arabia reformed its labour law (March 2021) and announced kafala's abolition in June 2025 (reported October 2025); domestic workers remained vulnerable. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kafala_system]
- In every GCC state more than 50% of the workforce consists of migrant workers; non-nationals peaked at 54% of Bahrain's population in 2010. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migrant_workers_in_the_Gulf_Cooperation_Council_region]
- Published **Modern South Asia**: India counted 35.4 million citizens and people of Indian origin abroad in 2024, about 9 million in the Gulf states — cross-refer. [source: supabase/content/history--regional-histories--modern-south-asia.sql]

## Migrants today: numbers, remittances, diaspora politics
- UN DESA: a record **304 million** international migrants in 2024 (about 3.7% of 8.2 billion people), almost double the 153.9 million of 1990; women and girls about 48% (search summary). Earlier IOM series: 84.5 million (2.3%) in 1970; 152.6 million (2.9%) in 1990; 280.6 million (3.6%) in 2020. The share has stayed in the 2–4% range for half a century. [source: https://www.migrationpolicy.org/article/top-statistics-global-migration-migrants-2025 (search summary); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_migration]
- Much migration is South–South: in 2013, 38% of migrants moved between developing countries and 23% between high-income OECD countries (World Bank *Migration and Remittances Factbook 2016*). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_migration]
- **Remittances**: World Bank estimate — about **US$685 billion** to low- and middle-income countries in 2024 (published **Globalization** chapter: led by India $129 bn and Mexico $68 bn; more than FDI and official aid combined) — use these numbers. Earlier: $529 bn (2018) and $626 bn (2022). A 2025 figure of $728.6 bn appears in press reports but was not verified against the World Bank. [source: supabase/content/history--contemporary-history--globalization.sql ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remittance ; https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/peoplemove/in-2024--remittance-flows-to-low--and-middle-income-countries-ar (search summary)]
- Chinese emigrant letters with remittances (*qiaopi*) are documented in *Dear China: Emigrant Letters and Remittances, 1820–1980* (Gregor Benton and Hong Liu) — title verified on Wikipedia, authors from memory: see NOT VERIFIED. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_emigration]
- Diaspora politics: Yossi Shain and Tamara Cofman Wittes call ethnic diasporas "inevitable" and "endemic" features of the international system, able to organise in host states, press homeland politics, and deal directly with third states and international organisations. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaspora_politics]
- India's Pravasi Bharatiya Divas (Overseas Indian Day), observed since 2003 on 9 January, commemorates Gandhi's return from South Africa to Bombay on 9 January 1915; the Overseas Citizen of India status was launched there in 2006. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pravasi_Bharatiya_Divas]
- Lebanon gives diaspora Lebanese no automatic right of return/citizenship for descendants; diaspora voting registrations are tracked for parliamentary elections (2018, 2022, 2026). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_diaspora]
- Global Compact for Safe, Orderly and Regular Migration: adopted at Marrakech on 10 December 2018 and endorsed by the UN General Assembly on 19 December 2018; not legally binding. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Compact_for_Migration]

## Theories and historiography of migration
- **E. G. Ravenstein** (Ernst Georg Ravenstein, born Frankfurt, died 13 March 1913), "The Laws of Migration", *Journal of the Statistical Society of London* 48(2) (1885): 167–235 (a second paper followed in 1889). Laws include: most migrants move a short distance; every flow produces a counter-flow; long-distance migrants favour big cities; migration proceeds by stages (step migration); large towns grow more by migration than by natural increase. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_migration ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Georg_Ravenstein]
- The explicit **push–pull** model is Everett S. Lee's, "A Theory of Migration", *Demography* 3(1) (1966): 47–57 — not Ravenstein's own term. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_migration]
- Other theories: neoclassical (wage differentials), dual labour market (demand in rich economies pulls migrants), new economics of labour migration (household risk diversification), world-systems theory (migration follows lines of colonial and economic dependence). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_migration]
- **Wilbur Zelinsky**, "The Hypothesis of the Mobility Transition", *Geographical Review* 61(2) (April 1971): 219–249 — the "migration transition": mobility types change in step with the demographic transition (premodern low migration; early transitional "massive movement from the countryside to cities" and high emigration; later urban-to-urban and circular moves). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zelinsky_Model]
- **Jan Lucassen and Leo Lucassen**, "The mobility transition revisited, 1500–1900: what the case of Europe can offer to global history", *Journal of Global History* (2009): measured "cross-cultural migration rates" (CCMR) for Europe 1501–1900 across six types of migration (including temporary, seasonal, soldiers, sailors, colonists), challenging the idea of an immobile premodern Europe; their edited volume *Globalising Migration History: The Eurasian Experience (16th–21st Centuries)* (Brill, 2014) extended CCMRs to Russia, China and Japan, describing "a common start, a long divergence and a recent convergence"; reviewer Patrick Manning noted China and Japan had rates roughly half Europe's until the late 20th century. Specific percentages differ between versions — do not quote numbers. (search summary) [source: https://iisg.amsterdam/en/research/publications/key-publications/globalising-migration-history-eurasian-experience-16th-21st-centuries ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/57832D7F0931EA55BC6AF2B3705FBED7 (search summary)]
- **Hein de Haas**: development initially increases emigration (an inverted-U relation); poverty constrains movement; middle-income countries are most migratory; migrants come disproportionately from better-off sections of origin populations. Book: *How Migration Really Works* (Viking, 2023; Penguin paperback 2024), structured around 22 myths; argues international migrants' share of world population has been relatively stable for 60 years. Lead author of the 6th edition of *The Age of Migration* (2020). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hein_de_Haas ; https://heindehaas.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/de-haas-imi-157-2.pdf (search summary); https://www.chathamhouse.org/publications/the-world-today/2023-10/review-honest-migration-debate-tackle-myths (search summary)]
- **Stephen Castles** (1944–2022) and Mark J. Miller, *The Age of Migration: International Population Movements in the Modern World* (Guilford/Macmillan, 1st ed. 1993; 6th ed. 2020 with Hein de Haas as lead author). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Castles ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_migration]
- **Patrick Manning** (b. 1941; Andrew W. Mellon Professor of World History emeritus, Pittsburgh), *Migration in World History* (Routledge; 1st ed. 2004 in UK, US printing dated 2005; 2nd ed. 2012; 3rd ed. 2020 with Tiffany Trimmer): a survey from early hominids to today. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Manning_(historian) ; https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1131130854143987968?lang=en (search summary)]
- **Dirk Hoerder**, *Cultures in Contact: World Migrations in the Second Millennium* (Duke University Press, 2002). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_migration]
- **Oscar Handlin**, *The Uprooted* (1951; Pulitzer Prize for History 1952) cast immigrants as uprooted peasants; John Bodnar, *The Transplanted: A History of Immigrants in Urban America* (Indiana University Press, 1985), stressed continuity and family strategies — the "uprooted vs transplanted" debate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Uprooted ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Handlin (Bodnar via search summary)]

## Debates
- **What counts as a diaspora?** Safran (1991) built a homeland-and-return model on the Jewish ideal type; Cohen (1997) widened it to voluntary labour, trade, imperial and cultural diasporas; Brubaker (2005) warned of a "'diaspora' diaspora" and treated diaspora as a stance/claim rather than a bounded group; Gilroy (1993) rejected homeland-centred and national framings in favour of a transatlantic culture of "routes". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaspora ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Black_Atlantic]
- **Did Rome create the Jewish diaspora?** The popular "expulsion after 70/135 CE" story is challenged: Erich Gruen argues compulsory dislocation in the Second Temple period explains only a fraction of a diaspora already well established; Howard Adelman and Elazar Barkan call the expulsion account "misleading"; Israel Bartal rejects Shlomo Sand's broader claims. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_diaspora]
- **Indenture: slavery by another name?** Tinker (1974) — "a new system of slavery", voluntariness a legal fiction; revisionists such as David Northrup (1995) treat it as harsh but distinct contract labour with some migrant agency. Published chapters already frame this debate; cross-refer. For Chinese labour in Cuba, Evelyn Hu-DeHart (1994) framed the question as "free labor or neo-slavery". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coolie]
- **Eurocentric periodisation of the "age of mass migration".** McKeown (2004): Asian flows rivalled the Atlantic, and the era ended around 1940 not 1914; a 2006 *International Review of Social History* critique (Prabhu Mohapatra) questioned his framing (search summary; argument not verified in detail). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_McKeown ; https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0020859006002823 (search summary)]
- **Push–pull versus structural and transition models.** Ravenstein (1885) and Lee (1966) explain migration by individual push and pull factors; critics (world-systems theory; de Haas) argue migration follows colonial and economic ties, rises with development before falling, and is selective of the better-off; the Lucassens' CCMRs (2009, 2014) show premodern Europe and Asia were highly mobile, undermining Zelinsky's (1971) idea of a premodern sedentary stage. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_migration ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zelinsky_Model ; https://iisg.amsterdam/en/research/publications/key-publications/globalising-migration-history-eurasian-experience-16th-21st-centuries]
- **Uprooted or transplanted?** Handlin (1951) stressed rupture and alienation; Bodnar (1985) stressed continuity, family and community strategies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Uprooted]
- **Where did borders come from?** Torpey: states' monopolisation of the "means of movement", accelerated by the First World War; McKeown: modern border control was built first to exclude Asians in the 1880s (US, Canada, Australia). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Torpey ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_McKeown]
- **Kafala's origins**: Bedouin custom (Beaugé) vs British colonial labour control (AlShehabi). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kafala_system]
- **The Refugee Convention today**: some (e.g. Policy Exchange researchers) argue courts and NGOs have stretched it into obligations states did not accept; defenders see it as the core of protection. Present neutrally. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_Relating_to_the_Status_of_Refugees]
