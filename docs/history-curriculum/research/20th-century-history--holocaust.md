## 1. Definition, terms, scale
- HOL: "the genocide of European Jews during World War II. From 1941 to 1945, Nazi Germany and its collaborators systematically murdered around six million Jews across German-occupied Europe, approximately two-thirds of Europe's Jewish population." Chiefly by mass shootings in Eastern Europe and gas chambers at Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka, Belzec, Sobibor, Chełmno and Majdanek. [source: HOL]
- "Holocaust" derives from a Greek word for "burnt offering"; it became a proper noun for the Nazi murder of the Jews in the 1950s; by the 1970s "Holocaust", capitalised, was the default. The Hebrew *Shoah* ("catastrophe"; HOL also "catastrophic destruction") refers exclusively to Jewish victims. The perpetrators used "Final Solution" as a euphemism. [source: HOL]
- Scope debate: the term is sometimes used to include Roma and Sinti, Soviet POWs, Polish and Soviet civilians and others; most scholars quoted in HOL restrict "Holocaust" to the Jews while stressing that other groups were persecuted and murdered **for different reasons and under different policies**. Browning (2010, as quoted in HOL) describes three "clusters of genocidal projects": euthanasia/"racial purification" (disabled people, Sinti and Roma); eradication of Slavic populations in the East; and the Final Solution proper. [source: HOL]
- The USHMM definition quoted in the seed summary could not be fetched from USHMM (host blocked); use it as the seed gives it, attributed to USHMM.
- About half of the Jews killed died by poison gas. [source: HOL]
- HOL ranks it "the single deadliest genocide in human history"; it is "the single most documented and studied genocide in history" and "the archetype of genocide and the benchmark in genocide studies". [source: HOL]
- The world Jewish population was 16.6 million in 1939 and 15.2 million at the end of 2020 (Israel Central Bureau of Statistics), still about 1.4 million below the pre-war level. [source: HOL]

## 2. Jewish life before the war (Europe and North Africa)
- Europe's pre-war Jewish population: close to 9.5 million, 57% of world Jewry. [source: HJE] SURV: about 9.5 million Jews lived in countries that Germany controlled or would occupy, and over two-thirds of them were killed. [source: SURV]
- Jews had lived in Europe for over 2,000 years. Medieval Christian antisemitism blamed them for killing Jesus. In the 19th century many states granted full citizenship. By the early 20th century most Jews in central and western Europe were well integrated, while in eastern Europe, where emancipation came later, many lived in small towns, spoke Yiddish and practised Orthodox Judaism. Political antisemitism (the "Jewish question", an international Jewish conspiracy) emerged in the 18th–19th centuries with nationalism; racial theories cast Jews as an alien race. [source: HOL]
- Interwar Jewish populations, c. 1933 (HJE table): Poland 3,000,000 (9.5% of population; 32.7% of Europe's Jews); USSR 2,525,000; Romania 756,000; Germany 525,000; Hungary 445,000; Czechoslovakia 357,000; UK 300,000; France 250,000; Austria 191,000; Netherlands 156,000; Lithuania 155,000; Latvia 95,600; Greece 73,000; Yugoslavia 68,000; Belgium 60,300; Turkey 50,000; Bulgaria 48,500; Italy 48,000; Denmark 5,700. [source: HJE] (See conflicts: Poland 3.0/3.3/3.5 million.)
- German Jews: roughly 500,000, under 1% of the population in 1933; wealthier on average and largely assimilated. [source: HOL]
- Lithuanian Jews (Litvaks); Łódź had over 230,000 Jews, 31.1% of the city, in September 1939. [source: LT; LG]
- Greece: 72,000–77,000 Jews in 27 communities; about 50,000 in Salonica (Thessaloniki), mostly Judeo-Spanish-speaking Sephardim, plus Greek-speaking Romaniotes. [source: GR]
- Bulgaria: about 48,000 Jews (0.8%), more than half in Sofia; the Sofia Synagogue (1909) was the third largest in Europe. [source: BG]
- **North Africa:** about 700,000 Jews lived in French-ruled territory in 1940, of whom 400,000 lived in French Algeria (legally part of France) and the protectorates of Tunisia and Morocco. [source: FR; JOE] Tunisia: about 100,000 Jews in November 1942. [source: TN; KAW] Libya (Italian): 21,000 Jews in 1931 (4% of 550,000), mostly in Tripoli; Jews were 25% of Tripoli's population in 1941, with 44 synagogues. [source: LY] Morocco: "the country's 250,000 Jews". [source: M5] Ancient roots: Jewish communities in Cyrenaica (Libya) from the 3rd century BCE; the El Ghriba synagogue on Djerba (Tunisia) is a pilgrimage site. [source: LY; TN]

## 3. Persecution in Germany, 1933–39 (brief; Hitler chapter has detail)
- About 1,500 anti-Jewish laws were issued by agencies, party bodies and local authorities. Jews were barred from professions and the civil service in 1933. The Nuremberg Laws (1935) reserved full citizenship for those of "German or related blood" and criminalised new marriages and sexual relations between Jews and non-Jewish Germans. A Jew was defined as someone with three or four Jewish grandparents; *Mischlinge* had varying rights. [source: HOL]
- Kristallnacht, 9–10 November 1938: over 7,500 of 9,000 Jewish shops looted; more than 1,000 synagogues damaged or destroyed; "at least 90" Jews murdered; up to 30,000 Jewish men arrested; a special levy of over 1 billion RM. [source: HOL] (Hitler notes say "at least 91"; see conflicts.)
- Emigration: of 560,000 Jews in Germany, 130,000 emigrated 1933–37 and 120,000 more in 1938–39. "Almost no country lowered the restrictions to immigrate." By 1939 about 100,000 were in the USA; 50,000 each in Palestine, the UK and Argentina; 30,000 each in the Netherlands, Belgium, France, South Africa and Shanghai. Germany took nearly 1 billion RM in emigration taxes. [source: HOL]
- Bulgaria, Hungary, Poland, Romania and Slovakia passed antisemitic legislation in the 1930s and 1940s. In October 1938 Germany deported many Polish Jews after a Polish law allowed revocation of citizenship for Polish Jews abroad. [source: HOL]
- **Romania** passed the first antisemitic legislation in Europe after Germany, before the Anschluss: Goga's Decree-law 169 of 22 January 1938 stripped 225,222 Jews of citizenship. The legislation was "entirely home-grown". [source: RO]

## 4. Flight and closing doors
- **Évian Conference**, 6–15 July 1938, at Roosevelt's initiative: 32 countries and 24 voluntary organisations. The US sent no government official (Myron C. Taylor represented it). Only the Dominican Republic (up to 100,000 refugees; Sosúa settlement, 1940) and later Costa Rica offered real places. Australia agreed to 15,000 over three years; Canada made no commitment. Golda Meir, observer from Mandatory Palestine, was not allowed to speak. The US agreed that the German and Austrian quota of 30,000 a year be made available; 1938–40 it exceeded this by 10,000, and Britain took almost as many. [source: EVI]
- Trujillo's motive, per EVI: he was "desperately anxious to introduce a leavening of white immigration stock". [source: EVI]
- **MS *St. Louis***: sailed from Hamburg 13 May 1939 with 937 passengers, most of them Jewish refugees; Cuba admitted only 28; the US and Canada refused (Canada's immigration director Frederick Blair persuaded Mackenzie King not to intervene, 9 June). Britain took 288; France 224, Belgium 214, Netherlands 181. Research tracing each passenger found 254 of those in Belgium, France and the Netherlands died in the Holocaust (USHMM research quoted in STL). Canada formally apologised on 7 November 2018. [source: STL]
- **Kindertransport**, 1938–39: the UK took in nearly 10,000 children, most of them Jewish, from Germany, Austria, Czechoslovakia and Danzig; the government waived visa requirements and set no numerical limit; the war ended it. Often they were the only members of their families to survive. [source: KT]
- **Sugihara** (see §13) and Shanghai (30,000 German Jews there by 1939, per HOL) are the Asian dimensions of flight. [source: HOL; SUG]

## 5. Poland 1939–41 and the ghettos
- Germany gained control of 1.7 million Polish Jews (HOL). FS: the invasion brought 3.5 million Polish Jews under Nazi and Soviet control. Around 7,000 Jews were killed in September 1939 alongside thousands of Poles. Up to 260,000 Jews were deported by the Soviets to the interior and largely survived. [source: HOL; FS]
- November 1939: Jews in the General Government ordered to wear white armbands; forced labour from October 1939. The Madagascar Plan (1940, Eichmann) was abandoned. [source: HOL; FS]
- Ghettos were set up from 1939–40 on the initiative of local German administrators; the largest (Warsaw, Łódź) were walled or fenced; Jewish councils (*Judenräte*) tried to sustain communities while obeying German demands; many sought survival by making ghettos useful as labour. By mid-June 1941 about 30,000 Jews had died, 20,000 of them of starvation in ghettos. [source: HOL]
- **Warsaw Ghetto**: established November 1940 and sealed on 15 November 1940 (RA says 16 November); at its height up to 460,000 Jews in 3.4 km², 9.2 persons per room. Rations in 1941 averaged 184 calories a day for Jews vs 699 for Poles and 2,613 for Germans. Around 100,000 died of hunger and disease before the 1942 deportations (the lead gives 92,000 victims of starvation and disease). Children smuggled food across the wall. [source: WG]
- Great Deportation (*Grossaktion Warschau*), 22/23 July – 21 September 1942: about 254,000 (some accounts at least 300,000) sent to Treblinka. HOL: of 350,000, 250,000 killed at Treblinka, 11,000 sent to labour camps, 10,000 shot in the ghetto, 35,000 allowed to remain, 20,000–25,000 hid. Judenrat head Adam Czerniaków killed himself when told to deliver 7,000 a day. Janusz Korczak went to Treblinka with his orphans in August 1942, refusing to escape. [source: WG; WGU; HOL]
- **Łódź (Litzmannstadt) Ghetto**: second largest; sealed 30 April 1940 with 163,777 residents; a war-production centre under Chaim Rumkowski, it survived until August 1944; 210,000 Jews passed through it; 877 were alive when the Soviets arrived (19 January 1945). Of 223,000 pre-invasion Łódź Jews, about 10,000 survived elsewhere. In the last phase some 25,000 were murdered at Chełmno, the rest sent to Auschwitz. Henryk Ross buried his negatives and recovered them. [source: LG]
- Rumkowski's "Give me your children" speech, 4 September 1942 (see Quotations). Rumkowski "is still considered one of the most controversial figures in the history of the Holocaust." [source: LG]
- **Ringelblum Archive (Oyneg Shabbos)**: led by historian Emanuel Ringelblum; collected diaries, essays, drawings, posters and testimonies; buried in three caches (milk cans and metal boxes). Two were recovered (18 September 1946; 1 December 1950): about 6,000 documents, some 35,000 pages, now in the Jewish Historical Institute, Warsaw. Only three members survived; Ringelblum and his family were found in hiding and shot in 1944 with those who hid them. In January 1942 Szlama Ber Winer, an escapee from Chełmno, gave the group a report (the Grojanowski Report) that reached London by June. UNESCO Memory of the World, 1999. [source: RA] (Start date: RA says September 1939; Kassow-related sources say November 1940 — see conflicts.)

## 6. The war against the Soviet Union and the "Holocaust by bullets"
- 22 June 1941: Germany and its allies Slovakia, Hungary, Romania and Italy invaded the USSR; Hitler saw a war of extermination against "Jewish Bolshevism". Planners foresaw removing 31 million people (HOL; EG says Himmler spoke of reducing the Soviet population by 30 million) and planned mass starvation. [source: HOL; EG]
- Soviet POWs: 60%, **3.3 million**, died, mainly of starvation, "the second largest group of victims of Nazi mass killing after European Jews"; Jewish POWs and commissars were systematically executed. [source: HOL]
- About a million civilians were killed in anti-partisan warfare, over 300,000 in Belarus. [source: HOL]
- Einsatzgruppen: four units (A–D), 500–990 men each, about 3,000 in total, under Heydrich's RSHA; attached to Army Groups North, Centre, South and the 11th Army. Initially they targeted Jewish men in state and party posts; after Himmler's visits in July–August 1941 they began killing women and children. Himmler mobilised 21 Order Police battalions; Wehrmacht, Waffen-SS and local auxiliaries also shot Jews. Killings peaked at 40,000 a month in Lithuania in August–September 1941. By the end of 1941 more than 80% of Jews in central Ukraine, eastern Belarus, Russia, Latvia and Lithuania had been shot. [source: HOL; EG]
- Totals: 1.5–2 million Jews shot by the end of the war, and up to 225,000 Roma (HOL). Hilberg: the Einsatzgruppen and related agencies killed more than 2 million people, including 1.3 million Jews (EG). [source: HOL; EG]
- Method: victims marched a few kilometres from town, made to undress, shot into pits, often with a single shot to the neck; many were buried alive; the killings were often public, and property was looted by occupiers and locals. [source: HOL]
- Pogroms by local inhabitants killed up to 50,000 Jews in Latvia, Lithuania, eastern Poland, Ukraine and the Romanian borderlands; Germany's role in inciting them is "controversial". [source: HOL]
- **Babi Yar** (Babyn Yar), Kyiv, 29–30 September 1941: **33,771** Jews shot (Einsatzgruppe operational report), by Sonderkommando 4a of Einsatzgruppe C with Police Battalions 45 and 303 and Ukrainian auxiliary police, with Wehrmacht (Sixth Army) involvement. Decision by Eberhard, Jeckeln and Rasch after NKVD bombings in Kyiv. 100,000–150,000 people of all groups were killed there during the occupation (also Soviet POWs, Roma, psychiatric patients, Ukrainian nationalists such as Olena Teliha). At least 29 survivors are known, including Dina Pronicheva. In 1943 prisoners were forced to exhume and burn the bodies (Aktion 1005). Soviet memorials spoke of "peaceful victims of fascism", obscuring the Jewish identity of the victims; Yevtushenko's poem, set in Shostakovich's 13th Symphony. [source: BY; REIN]
- Jäger Report (1 December 1941): Einsatzkommando 3 in Lithuania reported 137,346 people killed in five months, overwhelmingly Jews. [source: EG]
- Other mass shootings: Ponary (Paneriai) near Vilnius, July 1941 – August 1944: up to 100,000 killed, including 70,000 Jews [PON]; Rumbula near Riga, 30 November and 8 December 1941: about 25,000 Jews (24,000 Latvian and about 1,000 German) [RUM]; Mykolaiv, 16–30 September 1941: 35,782 [UA]; Odessa, 22–24 October 1941: 25,000–34,000 Jews shot or burned by Romanian troops with Einsatzgruppe men, and "well over 100,000" in Odessa and Transnistria overall [ODE].
- "Holocaust by bullets": the phrase is associated with the French Catholic priest **Patrick Desbois** (born 1955), founder of Yahad-In Unum (2004), which has located mass-grave sites in Ukraine, Russia, Belarus, Lithuania, Poland, Moldova and Romania and interviewed local witnesses. Book: *The Holocaust by Bullets* (English 2008; National Jewish Book Award). [source: DES]
- Snyder (quoted in UA): at least 1.7 million Soviet Jews were killed by the end of 1942. [source: UA]
- The Einsatzgruppen trial (1947–48): 24 defendants; 14 death sentences, four carried out on 7 June 1951. Ohlendorf's claim of a pre-invasion order to kill all Soviet Jews is now widely judged a defence fabrication (Streim). [source: EG]
- Einsatzkommando Egypt (Walter Rauff), formed 1942, was intended to murder the Jews of Egypt and Palestine had Rommel broken through; plans set aside after El Alamein (Mallmann and Cüppers). [source: EG]

## 7. The decision, Wannsee and the camps
- 31 July 1941: Göring's written authorisation to Heydrich to prepare a "total solution of the Jewish question". [source: WAN]
- September 1941: German Jews required to wear the yellow star; in October Hitler decided to deport them east and banned emigration. Mid-October to end of 1941: 42,000 Jews and 5,000 Roma from Germany/Austria deported to Łódź, Kovno, Riga and Minsk. [source: HOL]
- 12 December 1941: Hitler told party leaders, referring to his 1939 prophecy, "The world war is here; the annihilation of the Jews must be the necessary consequence" (Goebbels diary, 13 December). [source: HOL; FS]
- **Wannsee Conference**, 20 January 1942, Berlin suburb of Wannsee; convened by Heydrich; 15 officials, state secretaries and SS; about 90 minutes. Heydrich spoke of about 11 million Jews in Europe (Eichmann's list, including 330,000 in England, 4,000 in Ireland and 5,000,000 in the USSR). Its purpose was co-ordination and SS control, not a new decision (Longerich; Cesarani; Rees calls the attendees "second-level functionaries"; Hitler, Himmler and Goebbels were absent). The minutes, edited by Eichmann and Heydrich, used euphemisms ("evacuation"). One copy survived, found by Robert Kempner in March 1947. The house became a memorial in 1992. [source: WAN]
- Gas: gas vans from November 1941; **Chełmno** (Kulmhof, Warthegau) the first extermination camp, gassing from 8 December 1941; **Belzec** the first purpose-built camp with stationary gas chambers (carbon monoxide, T4 technology); Zyklon B tested on Soviet POWs at Auschwitz in August–September 1941 and preferred from early 1942. T4 personnel and technology were reused. [source: HOL; AUS]
- Operation Reinhard (March 1942 – November 1943): to murder the Jews of the General Government at Belzec, Sobibor and Treblinka; "as many as two million" killed; about 1.47 million Jews were murdered in 100 days from late July to early November 1942 (Lewi Stone). HOL: 1.5 million Polish Jews killed in the ghetto liquidations, "the largest killing operation of the Holocaust". Over 3 million Jews were murdered in 1942. The three camps needed fewer than 3,000 Jewish prisoners, about 1,000 Trawniki men (largely Ukrainian auxiliaries, recruited from Soviet POWs) and few Germans; they reported RM 178.7 million in loot. [source: REIN; HOL]
- Höfle Telegram (January 1943), intercepted by Bletchley Park but not understood: 1,274,166 arrivals at the four Reinhard camps (incl. Majdanek) by the end of 1942; 434,508 at Belzec. [source: REIN; BEL]
- Camp death tolls (Jews), with ranges:
  - Chełmno: 150,000 (HOL); at least 152,000, up to 200,000 (museum) or 340,000 (early Polish estimates) [CHE]
  - Belzec: 440,823–596,200 (HOL); 430,000–500,000 (BEL text); 434,508–600,000 (BEL infobox). Only seven Jewish prisoners survived; Rudolf Reder's account is known. [BEL]
  - Sobibor: 170,618–238,900 (HOL); 170,000–250,000 (SOB)
  - Treblinka: 780,863–951,800 (HOL); 800,000–925,000 Jews plus 2,000 Roma (TRE); operating 23 July 1942 – 19 October 1943
  - Auschwitz-Birkenau: 900,000–1,000,000 Jews (HOL); 960,000 Jews (AUS, Piper)
  - Majdanek: estimated 78,000 (MAJ); 18,400 Jews shot there on 3 November 1943 in Operation Harvest Festival (*Erntefest*, 3–4 November, about 43,000 Jews in all, the largest single massacre of Jews by German forces). [source: HOL; MAJ]
- **Auschwitz**: a complex of over 40 camps in annexed Polish territory (Auschwitz I, opened 1940 for Polish political prisoners; first transport 728 Poles from Tarnów, 14 June 1940; Auschwitz II-Birkenau; Auschwitz III-Monowitz, the IG Farben Buna works). At least 1.3 million sent, at least 1.1 million died: **960,000 Jews (865,000 gassed on arrival), 74,000 non-Jewish Poles, 21,000 Roma, 15,000 Soviet POWs, up to 15,000 others** (Franciszek Piper, published by Yad Vashem 1991; Piper regards 1,082,000 as a minimum). 400,207 prisoners were registered. Selections on arrival began July 1942; about 20–25% selected for labour. [source: AUS; HOL]
- Earlier Auschwitz figures: the Soviet statement of 4 million (May 1945, based on crematoria capacity; on a museum plaque until 1990–91); Höss's 2.5 million (from Eichmann; Höss himself thought it "far too high"); Hilberg's up to 1 million; Wellers 1983, 1,471,595. [source: AUS; DEJ] (Useful for the denial section: deniers exploit the revision; the overall six million total was never based on the 4 million.)
- **Hungary 1944**: from 14/15 May to 9 July 1944, 434,351 Jews in 147 trains (Ferenczy) or 437,402 (Veesenmayer) were deported, mostly to Auschwitz; about 80% gassed on arrival. Summer 1944 capacity was 20,000 bodies a day. [source: HU; AUS]
- Last mass gassing at Auschwitz: November 1944. Auschwitz liberated by Soviet troops on 27 January 1945 (now International Holocaust Remembrance Day, UN 2005). [source: HOL; AUS]
- Only 789 Auschwitz SS (no more than 15%) were ever tried; Höss was executed. [source: AUS]
- Death marches: January 1945, 67,000 Auschwitz prisoners marched west; over 700,000 people in the camp system in January 1945, up to a third died before the end; 70,000–100,000 Jews died in the last months. [source: HOL]
- Himmler's Posen speeches, 4 and 6 October 1943: recorded; the first known documents in which a Nazi minister spoke openly of extermination. [source: POS]

## 8. Deaths by country (ranges) and totals
**USHMM's per-country table and Yad Vashem's could not be fetched (hosts blocked in this session). The figures below come from Wikipedia country articles; give them as approximate and as ranges.**
- Totals: "around six million" (HOL); "conservative estimate 5.7 million killed by Germans and Romanians, 5.4 million under Nazi occupation" (HOL); Hilberg 5.1 million (rounding range 4.9–5.4 million) (FS; DEJ); Dawidowicz (1975) 5,933,900 (DEJ); a scholar quoted in HOL: "Between 5,100,000 and 6,200,000". Yad Vashem's names database holds about 4.5 million names of Jewish victims (as of 2018). [source: HOL; FS; DEJ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence_and_documentation_for_the_Holocaust]
- Hilberg's breakdown: ghettoisation and privation over 800,000; open-air shootings over 1,300,000; camps up to 3,000,000. [source: FS]
- Half the victims came from Poland; 2.6 million within the 1941 Soviet borders. About 1.3 million Jews who had lived under Nazi rule or in an allied state survived; protection by Germany's allies saved about 600,000. [source: HOL]
- Poland: about 3 million Jews killed, about 90% (VNG). [source: VNG]
- Soviet territory (1941 borders): 2.6 million (HOL). Ukraine: 850,000–1,600,000 (UA); pre-1990s estimate about 900,000 (Hilberg). [source: HOL; UA]
- Hungary: just over 564,000 dead 1941–45 (Braham); of over 800,000 within its wartime borders, about 255,500 survived. [source: HU]
- Romania (including Romanian-controlled Soviet territory): 250,000–380,000 (RO); Arad: 176,000–179,000 in Romanian-occupied Moldova. About 132,000 Jews of Hungarian-ruled Northern Transylvania were killed (counted with Hungary). [source: RO]
- Lithuania: 190,000–195,000 of 208,000–210,000; more than 95%, "a more complete destruction than befell any other country". [source: LT]
- Latvia: about 70,000 of 93,000. EG adds: of about 83,000 Jews in German hands in Latvia not more than 900 survived (a different base). [source: LV; EG]
- Netherlands: 102,000 murdered, about 75% of 140,000–150,000; of 107,000 deported only 5,200 survived; 25,000–30,000 went into hiding (Anne Frank). [source: NL]
- France: about 72,500 killed of more than 75,000 deported (of 340,000 Jews in 1940, two-thirds French citizens); overall survival rate up to 75%. HOL: 25% of Jews in France killed, most of them non-citizens or recent immigrants. [source: FR; HOL]
- Greece: 82–92% killed; about 10,000 survived. [source: GR]
- Slovakia: about 69,000 of 89,000 (1940); 58,000 deported March–October 1942, the Slovak state paying 500 RM per Jew; 13,500 more deported after August 1944. [source: SK]
- Italy: 7,172 of 38,994 Italian Jews arrested and became victims; 31,822 remained. [source: IT]
- Bulgarian-occupied Thrace, Macedonia and Pirot: 11,343 deported to Treblinka, almost all killed; Bulgaria proper: about 48,000 survived. [source: BG]
- Denmark: 99% of Jews survived. [source: DK]
- Germany: no verified death figure found (see NOT VERIFIED).
- In Hungary Jews were a majority of civilian deaths; in Poland a majority or about half. In the USSR, France, Greece and Yugoslavia non-Jewish civilian losses outnumbered Jewish deaths. [source: HOL]

## 9. Roma, Soviet POWs, Poles and other victims (brief)
- **Roma and Sinti (Porajmos / Pharrajimos / Samudaripen)**: 26 November 1935 supplementary decree to the Nuremberg Laws placed them with the Jews as "enemies of the race-based state". Estimates: USHMM (as cited in ROM) 220,000–500,000; "at least 250,000, possibly 500,000" (ROM lead); Weiss-Wendt "96,000 to 500,000, with the likely figure in excess of 200,000"; Gaunt: historians use "just above 200,000", activists 500,000. Ian Hancock's 1.5 million is called "untenable" (Fings). Yad Vashem: about 90,000 Roma killed in Yugoslavia (Ustaše Croatia, Jasenovac). Romania deported about 25,000 Roma to Transnistria, of whom about 11,000 died. [source: ROM]
- Auschwitz "Gypsy family camp": in May 1944 Roma armed with tools resisted liquidation and the SS withdrew; on 2 August 1944 the SS gassed the remaining 2,898 (2,897 in some accounts); at least 19,000 of 23,000 Roma sent to Auschwitz died (ROM). 2 August is now the Roma genocide remembrance day (Polish parliament 2011). West Germany recognised the genocide in 1982. Romanian President Băsescu apologised in 2007. [source: ROM]
- Roma were excluded from restitution because West German authorities denied racial motives; claims became possible only from 1979. [source: ROM]
- Gerlach estimates 6–8 million non-Jewish civilians and POWs killed by Nazi persecution 1933–45; Gilbert over 10 million. [source: HOL]
- Poland: more than 1.8 million ethnic Poles killed under occupation. [source: VNG]
- Disabled people (T4), gay men (Paragraph 175), Black Germans, Jehovah's Witnesses and political opponents: see Hitler notes; mention briefly. HOL: T4 killed around 70,000 by August 1941, including all 4,000–5,000 institutionalised Jews; decentralised killing caused about 120,000 more deaths. [source: HOL]

## 10. Collaboration and allied states
- Gerlach: non-Germans "not under German command" caused 5–6% of Jewish deaths, but their involvement was "crucial in other ways". Perpetrators included Dutch, French and Polish policemen, Romanian soldiers, foreign SS and police auxiliaries, Ukrainian Insurgent Army partisans and civilians. Millions benefited from Jewish property, jobs and homes. [source: HOL]
- **Vichy France**: Statut des Juifs, 3 October 1940 (Pétain personally made the draft harsher, per a 2010 document); applied also to Algeria, Tunisia and Morocco; second Statut 2 June 1941; General Commissariat for Jewish Affairs, March 1941. Vel' d'Hiv roundup, 16–17 July 1942, by French police: 13,152 arrested including 4,115 children, held in the Vélodrome d'Hiver then sent via Drancy, Pithiviers and Beaune-la-Rolande, mainly to Auschwitz. Italian-occupied zone spared Jews until September 1943. Gaullist resistance myth delayed acknowledgement; Paxton (*Vichy France*, 1972) and Marrus & Paxton (*Vichy France and the Jews*, 1981) broke it; Chirac's acknowledgement, 16 July 1995; Macron 2017. Algerian Jews' citizenship, revoked under Vichy, restored 20 October 1943. [source: FR; VDH]
- The Grand Mosque of Paris: Si Kaddour Benghabrit and Abdelkader Mesli saved hundreds of Jews (HOL). [source: HOL] (Number not independently verified; keep "hundreds" and attribute.)
- **Romania** (Antonescu): "the only ally of the Third Reich that carried out a genocidal campaign without the intervention of" the SS; "Romania ranks first among Holocaust perpetrator countries other than Germany", yet had more survivors than any other Axis country (the "Antonescu paradox": murderous in Bessarabia, Bukovina and Transnistria, less so in the Old Kingdom). Iași pogrom, 28–30 June 1941: 13,266 killed per Romanian authorities (RO gives up to 14,850 including the death trains). Deportations of 154,000–170,000 Jews from Bessarabia and Bukovina to Transnistria; deaths there up to 160,000 (HOL). Bogdanovka mass murder, winter 1941–42. Antonescu was executed at Jilava on 1 June 1946. The International Commission on the Holocaust in Romania (Wiesel Commission) reported later. [source: RO; IASI; HOL; ANT]
- **Hungary**: anti-Jewish laws from 1938; Jewish men in labour service (*munkaszolgálat*); Kamianets / expulsions of 1941 and the Novi Sad raid (1942). Germany occupied Hungary 19 March 1944; Sztójay government; Eichmann's staff at the Majestic Hotel; ghettoisation and deportation "in less than eight weeks, with the enthusiastic help of the Hungarian authorities, particularly the gendarmerie". Horthy halted deportations on 6 July 1944 (they stopped three days later) under diplomatic pressure and Allied bombing of Budapest; by then the countryside Jews were gone. Arrow Cross (Szálasi) rule from October 1944: death marches to the Austrian border; 10,000–15,000 Jews shot on the Danube banks (November 1944 – February 1945). About 119,000 Jews liberated in Budapest. [source: HU]
- **Baltic states**: Lithuania — Lithuanian Activist Front; Kaunas pogrom 25–29 June 1941 (Lietūkis garage massacre); historians attribute the near-total destruction to "massive collaboration ... by the non-Jewish local paramilitaries, though the reasons for this collaboration are still debated"; the topic "remained unsettled" as of 2020. Latvia — Arājs Kommando (about 300 volunteers in 1941) took part in Rumbula; Arājs convicted in West Germany only in 1979. Estonia was declared *judenfrei* by Wannsee (4,500 remaining Jews murdered by end 1941). [source: LT; KAU; LV; RUM; WAN]
- **Ukraine**: Einsatzgruppen C and D shot Jews near their homes; Dieter Pohl: around 100,000 Ukrainians joined police units that provided key assistance; OUN propaganda and militias in the Lviv pogroms (30 June – 2 July and 25–29 July 1941), triggered by the discovery of NKVD prison massacres blamed on "Jewish Bolsheviks"; Snyder: the majority of collaborators "were not politically motivated". The OUN's role "has been widely debated". [source: UA; LVIV]
- **Poland**: no collaborationist government. Jedwabne, 10 July 1941: 300–1,600 Jews killed by at least 40 ethnic Poles with German involvement; Jan T. Gross's work. Some Poles dug up remains near the camps. [source: JED; HOL]
- **Slovakia** was the first ally to hand over its Jews (1942); **Croatia** killed most of its Jews (and more Serbs) in its own camps. [source: HOL; SK]
- **Netherlands**: higher death rate than neighbours, attributed to difficulty of hiding or Dutch police collaboration; about 140 trains from Westerbork. [source: HOL; NL]
- **Bulgaria**: Law for the Protection of the Nation (1941); Belev–Dannecker agreement, 22 February 1943; Bulgarian authorities rounded up and deported 11,343 Jews (7,122 from Macedonia, 4,221 from Thrace) from 4 March 1943 via Lom and Vienna to Treblinka. [source: BG]

## 11. North Africa
- **Tunisia**: Vichy's statutes applied from 1940; Germans occupied Tunisia November 1942 – May 1943. Walter Rauff's Einsatzkommando imposed a Judenrat, hostages, fines, confiscations and the yellow badge. More than 5,000 Jews sent to forced labour camps (JOE: more than 30 camps); per Yad Vashem, 265 are known to have been murdered (TN; but KAW says 46 died — see conflicts); 160 Tunisian Jews living in France were sent to death camps. Tunisian Jews were spared mass deportation. [source: TN; JOE; KAW]
- **Khaled Abdul-Wahab** (1911–1997), "the Tunisian Schindler", sheltered Jewish families; the first Arab nominated for Righteous Among the Nations. [source: KAW; TN]
- **Libya** (Italian): Fascist racial laws from late 1938; men 18–45 conscripted to labour; in 1942 Germans looted Benghazi's Jewish quarter and deported more than 2,000 Jews across the desert, over a fifth of whom died; Cyrenaican Jews interned at **Giado** (May 1942 – liberated by the British January 1943): of about 2,600 prisoners, 562 died, mostly of hunger and typhus — the highest toll of any North African camp. Some Libyan Jews with British citizenship were deported to Europe (JOE mentions German orders of February 1942). Postwar: the Tripolitania pogrom of 5–7 November 1945 killed more than 140 Jews. [source: LY; GIA; JOE]
- **Algeria**: Vichy revoked Jews' French citizenship (the Crémieux decree's grant), barred them from many jobs and schools, confiscated property in 1941; Muslim religious leaders in Algiers preached against taking part in the spoliation; Jews took part in the underground that helped the Allied landing (November 1942). [source: JOE]
- **Morocco**: Résident Général Noguès applied Vichy decrees in 1940; Sultan Muhammad V "reportedly refused" to sign off on ghettoisation and deportation, invited rabbis to the 1941 throne celebrations, yet signed two decrees barring Jews from certain schools and posts; "competing accounts"; Michel Abitbol says he "was more passive than Moncef Bey" of Tunisia. Some 7,700 Jews trying to flee were held, some in labour camps. After Operation Torch Noguès persuaded the Allies to keep the laws for a time. [source: MA; M5; WebSearch: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-moroccan-sultan-who-protected-his-countrys-jews-during-world-war-ii-180983903/]
- Wannsee's "Unoccupied France 700,000" figure included the Jews of French North Africa (JOE). Distance and shipping needs prevented deportation from North Africa (TN quoting a historian). [source: JOE; TN]

## 12. Resistance (armed and unarmed)
- Jews were often over-represented in resistance movements but not always welcome; some nationalist groups killed Jews. 20,000–25,000 Jews joined Soviet partisans (HOL) / Jewish partisans totalled 20,000–30,000 (JP); 10,000–13,000 non-combatants lived in forest family camps (HOL). [source: HOL; JP]
- Bielski partisans (Naliboki forest, Belarus): brothers Tuvia, Zus, Asael and Aron; up to 1,236 members by 1944, most non-combatants. [source: BP]
- Vilna: Abba Kovner's manifesto (start of 1942), "Let us not go like lambs to the slaughter!" — the first identification by a target that Hitler meant to kill all Jews, and the first Holocaust use of the "sheep to the slaughter" phrase (authorship contested). FPO (United Partisan Organisation). [source: AK]
- About 100,000 Jews fought in the Polish army in 1939 (10% of it); about 30,000 killed, captured or missing. [source: JP]
- **Warsaw Ghetto Uprising**: ŻOB (left-wing, founded 28 July 1942 per ZOB; WG says October 1942) led by Mordechai Anielewicz (24); right-wing ŻZW (Jewish Military Union); about 600 ŻOB and 400 ŻZW fighters. First armed resistance on 18 January 1943. The uprising began 19 April 1943, on the eve of Passover, against Jürgen Stroop's forces and ended 16 May with the blowing up of the Great Synagogue. Anielewicz and others died in the bunker at 18 Miła Street on 8 May; Marek Edelman escaped through the sewers. Stroop claimed 56,065 Jews killed or captured (his own figures total 57,065) and reported 16 Germans killed (110 casualties). At least 13,000 Jews were killed, some 6,000 burned or suffocated. "The largest single revolt by Jews during World War II." Women leaders included Zivia Lubetkin. Szmul Zygielbojm killed himself in London on 10 May (see Quotations). [source: WGU; ZOB]
- Other ghetto revolts: Białystok (16–23 August 1943), Vilna, Częstochowa, Glubokoje. [source: POS; LG; HOL]
- **Treblinka** revolt, 2 August 1943: of about 840 prisoners, nearly 400 escaped, fewer than 70 survived the war (TU); TRE says 200 escaped, almost 100 survived. [source: TU; TRE]
- **Sobibor** revolt, 14 October 1943 (organisers Leon Feldhendler and Alexander Pechersky, a Soviet-Jewish political commissar brought from the Minsk Ghetto, who led the revolt): eleven SS men killed; about 300 escaped and roughly 60 survived the war; the camp was then closed. [source: SOB]
- Auschwitz Sonderkommando revolt, 7 October 1944; at least 802 escape attempts, 144 successful. Vrba and Wetzler escaped in April 1944 (AUS text: 27 April; the commonly given date is 7 April — see conflicts); their report and the Rosin–Mordowicz and Polish Major's (Tabeau) reports formed the "Auschwitz Protocols", published by the War Refugee Board in November 1944; publication in June 1944 helped halt the Hungarian deportations. [source: AUS]
- Spiritual and unarmed resistance: Oyneg Shabbos; Łódź photographers; ghetto schooling, culture (Łódź Cultural Centre opened 1 March 1941). Yehuda Bauer argued resistance included "any activity that gave the Jewish people dignity and humanity", and that what is surprising "is not how little resistance there was, but rather how much" (paraphrased in YB). [source: RA; LG; YB]
- Roma resistance at Auschwitz, May 1944 (see §9). [source: ROM]

## 13. Rescue
- Gerlach: about 200,000 Jews survived in hiding across Europe; rescuers' motives ranged from altruism to sex or money; helpers sometimes betrayed Jews when money ran out; the death penalty for helping was enforced especially in the East. [source: HOL]
- **Righteous Among the Nations**: Yad Vashem (established by the Knesset 1953); title awarded since 1963 by a commission headed by a Supreme Court justice to non-Jews who risked their lives to save Jews. As of 1 January 2024: 28,707 people. Largest national totals: Poland 7,318; Netherlands 6,137; France 4,303; Ukraine 2,713. Lithuania 924; Hungary 887; Germany 666; Albania 75; Denmark 22; Bulgaria 20; Japan 1; Egypt 1; Turkey 1. Yad Vashem stresses the figures do not measure rescue per country. [source: RAN]
- **Denmark**: Georg Ferdinand Duckwitz leaked the deportation plan on 28 September 1943; the resistance and citizens ferried 7,500 of 8,000 Jews plus 686 non-Jewish spouses to Sweden; 464 deported to Theresienstadt; 99% of Danish Jews survived. [source: DK]
- **Bulgaria (contested record)**: the deportation of Jews from Bulgaria proper, planned for 9 March 1943, was stopped after leaks and protests; Dimitar Peshev and 42 other government MPs protested (17 March); Metropolitan Kiril of Plovdiv and Metropolitan Stefan of Sofia intervened; Boris III later approved expelling Sofia's 25,743 Jews to the countryside (May 1943) instead of deporting them; men aged 20–46 in labour corps. About 48,000 survived. **But** the same government deported 11,343 Jews from occupied Thrace, Macedonia and Pirot to their deaths. Credit to Boris is disputed (Bar-Zohar pro-Boris; others credit Peshev, the Church and public protest); in 2023 Jewish community leader Alexander Oscar boycotted a ceremony over "the imaginary role of King Boris in rescuing" Jews. [source: BG; WebSearch: https://www.justsecurity.org/86658/the-ambiguity-of-evil-and-good-a-tale-of-holocaust-rescue-and-deportation-in-bulgaria/; https://jta.org/?p=1795153]
- **Chiune Sugihara** (1 January 1900 – 31 July 1986), Japanese vice-consul in Kaunas: against Foreign Ministry instructions he issued over 2,100 transit visas between 18 July and 28 August 1940 (continuing until 4 September), letting refugees cross the USSR by the Trans-Siberian Railway to Japan (Tsuruga, Kobe); many held Curaçao notations from Dutch honorary consul Jan Zwartendijk (over 2,200, 16 July – 3 August 1940). Numbers saved are disputed: "around 6,000" often cited; Kitade (2022) argues about 3,000. Descendants estimated 40,000–100,000. Recognised as Righteous (1984 per infobox; ceremony 1985). [source: SUG]
- **Raoul Wallenberg** (Swedish) and **Carl Lutz** (Swiss) issued protective passes in Budapest; Switzerland was allowed 7,800 Schutzpässe, Sweden 4,500, the Vatican, Portugal and Spain 3,300 combined; Lutz's mission was "the largest diplomatic rescue mission of the Holocaust". [source: HU]
- **Le Chambon-sur-Lignon** (Protestant village, Haute-Loire): sheltered an estimated 800–5,000; collectively honoured in 1990 with Nieuwlande (Netherlands). [source: CHA]
- Poland: Żegota (Council to Aid Jews) and Irena Sendler are standard rescue examples (articles fetched; only names verified here).
- Switzerland admitted nearly 30,000 Jews but turned away some 20,000. [source: SURV]

## 14. Bystanders: what the Allies knew
- 26 June 1942: BBC broadcast in all languages a Bund report (via the Polish government-in-exile) documenting the killing of 700,000 Polish Jews. [source: HOL]
- **Riegner telegram**, 8 August 1942, from Gerhart Riegner (World Jewish Congress, Geneva), based on information from industrialist Eduard Schulte, sent via the British Foreign Office and US State Department to Stephen Wise and Sydney Silverman. The State Department considered it "a wild rumor, fueled by Jewish anxieties"; it reached Wise on 28 August; at Sumner Welles's request he withheld it until November confirmation. [source: RIE]
- 10 December 1942: Polish Foreign Minister Edward Raczyński's note to the Allied governments, "the first official denunciation by any Government of the mass extermination". 17 December 1942: Joint Declaration by Members of the United Nations condemning the extermination, read by Eden in the Commons. [source: IR]
- **Jan Karski** (born Kozielewski, 24 June 1914 – 13 July 2000), courier of the Polish underground: twice smuggled into the Warsaw Ghetto (with Bund leader Leon Feiner); visited the transit camp at Izbica Lubelska (he wrote "Bełżec"); briefed Eden; met Roosevelt on 28 July 1943 ("Roosevelt asked no questions about the Jews"), also Frankfurter and Cordell Hull; *Story of a Secret State* (1944); Righteous, 1982; Presidential Medal of Freedom 2012. Puławski: facts about the Holocaust were available to the Allies for at least a year and a half before Karski met Roosevelt. [source: KAR]
- Bermuda Conference (April 1943): no change in US quotas or British Palestine policy. Morgenthau's Treasury report "on the Acquiescence of this Government to the Murder of the Jews" led to the War Refugee Board (January 1944). [source: IR]
- The Joint Distribution Committee raised $70 million during the war; 81,000 Jews emigrated with its help 1939–44. [source: HOL]
- The ICRC did little and later apologised. Pius XII's record remains debated (aid to hidden Jews in Rome vs public silence). [source: IR]
- **Bombing Auschwitz**: Polish prisoners asked in a report of December 1940 for the camp to be bombed (sent via Sikorski, January 1941; RAF said it was not feasible). Rabbi Michael Dov Weissmandl proposed bombing the rail lines on 16 May 1944. John McCloy (Assistant Secretary of War) told his aide to "kill" the request; the USAAF had decided in February 1944 not to bomb "for the purposes of rescuing victims of enemy oppression". The British Air Ministry declined for "operational reasons". The Fifteenth Air Force bombed the IG Farben plant at Monowitz (20 August, 13 September, 18 and 26 December 1944) and hit SS barracks by accident on 13 September 1944. RAF reconnaissance photographed Birkenau (23 August 1944); the photos were examined only in the 1970s. [source: AUS; ABD]
- Debate: David Wyman, "Why Auschwitz Was Never Bombed" (*Commentary*, May 1978), and *The Abandonment of the Jews* (1984): it could and should have been done; Wasserstein (1979) and Gilbert (1981) on British inaction; later historians (e.g., Rubinstein; Neufeld's 2000 volume) on bombing accuracy and the problems of counterfactuals; Kevin Mahoney: no request could "outweigh overriding military aims". [source: AUS; ABD]
- Survivor Olga Lengyel (quoted by Wyman): "If the Allies could blow up the crematory ovens! The pace of the extermination would at least be slowed." [source: ABD]

## 15. Aftermath: liberation, survivors, displaced persons
- Majdanek liberated by the Red Army in July 1944 (MAJ gives 22 and 24 July); Auschwitz 27 January 1945; western camps by the Western Allies in spring 1945. [source: MAJ; HOL]
- Up to 200,000 Jews survived inside the concentration camps. [source: HOL]
- Displaced persons: at least 40 million displaced in Europe, about 11 million in Allied-occupied Germany; about 1.2 million refused repatriation; some 850,000 lived in DP camps two years after the war. The Harrison Report (1945) found overcrowding, "pathetic malnutrition" and Jews housed with former collaborators. Jews made up about 25% of DP camp populations; Jews in Germany rose from about 88,000 at war's end to 250,000 by 1947, as survivors fled post-war violence in eastern Europe. The last DP camp, Föhrenwald, closed in February 1957. US admitted 137,450 European Jewish DPs 1941–57. [source: DP; HOL]
- The survivors called themselves *Sh'erit ha-Pletah* ("the surviving remnant"), first organised at St. Ottilien on 27 May 1945. [source: SURV]
- Kielce pogrom, 4 July 1946: 42 Jews killed after a blood-libel rumour; prompted mass emigration from Poland. [source: KIE]
- Many survivors went to Palestine/Israel after 1948 or to the US after about 1950. [source: HOL]
- Reparations: West Germany's 1952 agreement — DM 3 billion to Israel and DM 450 million to the Claims Conference; Germany paid $86.8 billion 1945–2018. Property losses estimated at about $10 billion in 1944 dollars. [source: HOL]
- North African Jewish emigration followed later (Tunisia, Libya after the 1945 and 1948 pogroms; Morocco about 90,000 to Israel 1949–56). [source: LY; MA] (Context only.)

## 16. Justice and the word "genocide"
- **Lemkin** (24 June 1900 – 28 August 1959), Polish Jewish lawyer, shaped by the Armenian genocide and the 1921 Talaat assassination case; fled 1939; Duke University; coined "genocide" (Greek *genos*, Latin *-cide*) in *Axis Rule in Occupied Europe* (Carnegie Endowment, November 1944); lost 49 relatives; worked on Jackson's Nuremberg team; drove the Genocide Convention. [source: LEM]
- Churchill's broadcast of 24 August 1941 ("We are in the presence of a crime without a name") is widely cited as an inspiration (date given as 14 August in one source). [source: WebSearch: https://www.niod.nl/en/longreads/what-genocide; https://www.opendemocracy.net/en/naming-crimes-genocide-and-public-opinion-in-unit/]
- **Genocide Convention**: adopted unanimously 9 December 1948; in force 12 January 1951; five acts "committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such". Not used in Holocaust trials because of non-retroactivity. The US did not ratify in Lemkin's lifetime. [source: GC; HOL; LEM]
- **Nuremberg (IMT)**: 20 November 1945 – 1 October 1946; four Allies; charges of conspiracy, crimes against peace, war crimes, crimes against humanity (term adopted at Jackson's suggestion). 24 indicted (Bormann in absentia; Krupp unfit; Ley suicide); 12 sentenced to death; ten hanged 16 October 1946 (Göring killed himself the day before); three acquitted. Aggression was "the supreme international crime", but in closing statements the Holocaust upstaged it; Jews were mentioned as victims more than any other group. The twelve US subsequent trials (177 defendants, 142 convicted, 25 death sentences) focused more on the Holocaust. Weizmann's request for a Jewish role was rejected. [source: NT; HOL]
- Wider justice: West Germany investigated about 100,000 and tried over 6,000; Willi Dressen: of 106,000 investigated, only 6,500 sentenced, 166 to life — "purely statistically, each murder cost ten minutes in prison". [source: HOL]
- **Eichmann**: captured by Mossad in Buenos Aires on 11 May 1960; tried in Jerusalem 11 April – 15 August 1961 (three judges: Landau, Halevy, Raveh); prosecutor Gideon Hausner; convicted on all 15 counts; hanged at Ramla (night of 31 May – 1 June 1962; see conflicts). Unlike Nuremberg the trial built its case on survivor testimony, a strategy that "increased publicity but has proven controversial". Fritz Bauer tipped off Israel (1957). [source: ET; HOL]
- Hannah Arendt (*New Yorker*; *Eichmann in Jerusalem*, 1963) coined "the banality of evil", criticised the trial's didactic aim and the role of Jewish councils — criticised in turn as victim-blaming. [source: ET]
- Other trials: Ulm Einsatzkommando trial 1958; Frankfurt Auschwitz trials (not fetched). Antonescu executed 1946. [source: EG; ANT]

## 17. Memory
- Holocaust memory was long confined to survivor communities; it peaked in the 1990s; communist states marginalised antisemitic persecution and their nationals' collaboration; West German self-critical memory grew in the 1970s–80s. [source: HOL]
- France's acknowledgement came only in 1995 (Chirac). [source: FR]
- Babi Yar memorials spoke of "peaceful victims of fascism". [source: BY]
- Survivor writing: Primo Levi (31 July 1919 – 11 April 1987), Italian Jewish chemist and partisan, deported from Fossoli to Monowitz on 21/22 February 1944; *If This Is a Man* (*Se questo è un uomo*), published 11 October 1947 (2,000 copies; Einaudi's revised edition 1958). Other survivor writers: Elie Wiesel, Viktor Frankl, Edith Eger. [source: PL; AUS]

## 18. Denial (brief, as a phenomenon)
- Definition: the "negationist and antisemitic fringe claim" that the genocide did not happen, contrary to overwhelming evidence; typical claims: no extermination plan, no gas chambers, far fewer than six million, a hoax. Roots: Maurice Bardèche (1948) and Paul Rassinier; Institute for Historical Review (Willis Carto, 1978); Arthur Butz (1976); Faurisson; the Leuchter report; Zündel trials in Canada. Iran is described as the leading state sponsor; in former Eastern Bloc countries deniers deny their own nationals' participation. Illegal in Canada, Israel and many European countries including Germany; UN General Assembly resolutions 2007 and 2022. Deniers call themselves "revisionists". [source: DEN]
- Irving v Penguin and Lipstadt: judgment 11 April 2000 (Mr Justice Gray) — **already told in the *What Is History?* chapter; cross-refer.** [source: IVP]
- Deniers misread *Ausrottung* in Himmler's Posen speech as "rooting out"/deportation. [source: POS]

## Debates
- **When and how was the genocide decided? (intentionalism vs functionalism, decision version).** Terms from Tim Mason (1981). Intentionalists: Lucy Dawidowicz (decided no later than 1919); Jäckel (decided before 1939, citing the 30 January 1939 prophecy); moderate intentionalists Richard Breitman and Saul Friedländer (between 1939 and 1941). Functionalists: Martin Broszat (began "bit by bit" from improvised local killings in autumn 1941, "a way out of the blind alley"); Karl Schleunes's "twisted road to Auschwitz"; Götz Aly (population planning). Synthesis (Bauer, Longerich, Kershaw, Burleigh, Marrus, Mayer): no master plan, but Hitler the decisive force; "cumulative radicalisation". No written Hitler order has been found. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functionalism%E2%80%93intentionalism_debate; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Final_Solution]
  - Timing positions: Browning — a decision to kill Soviet Jews in mid-July 1941 in the euphoria of victory, and the Final Solution for all European Jews crystallising 18 September – 25 October 1941; Burrin — late August 1941, from frustration as the advance slowed; Kershaw — the expansion after mid-August 1941 implies a (probably verbal) Hitler order; Gerlach (1997) — announced at Hitler's 12 December 1941 meeting with Gauleiter, after the US entered the war; Longerich — futile to look for a single decision; mass killing understood as the Final Solution only by summer 1942; Cesarani — improvised, "shaped by war"; Bauer — Himmler's notebook, 18 December 1941: "What to do with the Jews of Russia?" / "Exterminate them as partisans" as near to an order as historians will get. [source: FID; FS; YB]
- **Hilberg's "machinery of destruction"**: four bureaucracies (party, civil service, industry, army) fused; stages of definition, expropriation, concentration, annihilation; "a kind of structural determinism". His claim of "almost complete lack of resistance" angered Yad Vashem (which reneged on publishing); Princeton rejected the manuscript on Arendt's advice; Bauer and Lustiger reject the passivity image. [source: FS; DEJ; RH]
- **Ordinary men or willing executioners?** Browning (*Ordinary Men*, 1992) on Reserve Police Battalion 101 (middle-aged Hamburg men; Józefów and Łomazy; fewer than a dozen of about 500 opted out when offered): conformity, deference, peer pressure, influenced by Milgram. Goldhagen (*Hitler's Willing Executioners*, 1996): a unique German "eliminationist antisemitism" made Germans willing killers; a "publishing phenomenon" with a "mostly scathing" scholarly reception (Hilberg, Bauer "patent nonsense", Birn and Riess on sources, Bartov, Clendinnen, Fritz Stern); Bauer said the thesis fitted Romania better than Germany; Democracy Prize 1997 (laudatio by Habermas and Reemtsma). Browning replied in his 1998 afterword. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitler%27s_Willing_Executioners; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_R._Browning]
- **Friedländer's "integrated history"**: refuses to separate German policy, bystanders and victims; puts victims' voices (diaries, letters) at the centre; "redemptive antisemitism" as the Nazi worldview; against Broszat's call to "historicize" the Nazi era (1980s exchange). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saul_Friedl%C3%A4nder; WebSearch: https://www.lbilondon.ac.uk/events/lecture-series-2007-08/years-extermination-plea-integrated-history-holocaust; https://newrepublic.com/article/62897/books-the-whole-horror]
- **Snyder's *Bloodlands* (2010) and its critics**: 14 million non-combatants killed 1933–45 by both regimes in Poland, Belarus, Ukraine, the Baltics and western Russia, Germany responsible for about two-thirds; "it's wrong to focus on the camps" (Mikanowski's summary). Praise: Applebaum, Roseman. Critics: Richard Evans (LRB 2010) — no causal argument, "simply equating Nazi genocide with the mass murders" of Stalin; Omer Bartov (Slavic Review 2011) — no new evidence, "pro-Polish bias", neglect of local interethnic violence; Efraim Zuroff and Dovid Katz — risk of feeding the "double genocide" thesis of Baltic nationalists; Daniel Lazare — "whiff of Ernst Nolte"; *Contemporary European History* forum 2012 (Baberowski, Diner, Kühne, Mazower). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloodlands:_Europe_Between_Hitler_and_Stalin]
- **Uniqueness and comparison**: Jäckel, Bauer (the "worst single case", every member of a people selected; Roma suffered genocide but not "the Holocaust"), Katz, Dawidowicz, Friedländer argued uniqueness/unprecedentedness; the Historikerstreit (Nolte, 6 June 1986, "The Past That Will Not Pass": Auschwitz a "copy" of the Gulag and a reaction to Bolshevism) was opposed by Habermas, Wehler, Mommsen, Broszat, Jäckel. Since about 2000 scholarship has moved to comparison: critics call uniqueness Eurocentric and say it demotes other victims (Gerlach); A. Dirk Moses's "catechism" debate (2021); Zimmerer and others on continuities with the Herero and Nama genocide (contested). "By 2021, there were few scholars who were still making the uniqueness argument" (HUD), though it remains strong in public memory. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocaust_uniqueness_debate; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historikerstreit; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yehuda_Bauer] (HUD is a recently rewritten, partly contested article; phrase cautiously.)
- **Who counts as a victim of "the Holocaust"?** Jews only (most scholars quoted in HOL) vs inclusive usage (Henry Friedlander includes Roma and the disabled). [source: HOL; YB]
- **Bombing Auschwitz** (§14): Wyman vs later sceptics on feasibility; a moral question "emblematic" of the Allied response (Berenbaum). [source: ABD]
- **Bulgaria's rescue and Boris III** (§13). [source: BG; WebSearch above]
- **Muhammad V**: protector (most scholars, Assaraf) vs exaggerated role (Abitbol). [source: M5]
- **Local collaboration in the East**: German incitement of pogroms "controversial" (HOL); Lithuanian motives "still debated"; OUN role in Lviv "widely debated"; Gross on Jedwabne. [source: HOL; LT; LVIV; JED]
- **Eichmann trial**: survivor-testimony strategy and Arendt's critique of the Jewish councils. [source: ET]
- **Karski's mission**: hero who alerted the world vs Puławski's view that the Holocaust was a side-issue of his courier mission and already known. [source: KAR]
- **Hilberg vs Bauer on resistance** (above).

## Conflicting figures (between sources and with other chapters' notes)
- Poland's Jewish population: 3.0 million c. 1933 (HJE); 3.3 million (IR); 3.5 million under German and Soviet control in 1939 (FS); Germany gained 1.7 million (HOL). Say "over three million".
- Warsaw Ghetto: 460,000 at height (WG) vs 350,000 at the start of the 1942 deportations (HOL); deported to Treblinka 254,000 (WG) vs 250,000 (HOL) vs "at least 300,000 by different accounts"; starvation deaths 92,000 vs about 100,000. Sealed 15 November (WG) vs 16 November 1940 (RA).
- ŻOB founded 28 July 1942 (ZOB) vs October 1942 (WG).
- Ringelblum archive start: September 1939 (RA) vs November 1940 (Kassow-based search summary).
- Camp tolls: see §7 ranges (HOL table vs camp articles). Belzec 430,000–500,000 vs 440,823–596,200 vs 434,508–600,000; Chełmno 150,000 vs ≥152,000–340,000.
- Auschwitz Jewish deaths 900,000–1,000,000 (HOL) vs 960,000 (AUS). Soviet "4 million" statement dated 8 May 1945 (AUS) vs 12 May 1945 (DEJ).
- Roma killed at Auschwitz: 21,000 (AUS; Hitler notes "about 21,000 of 23,000") vs "at least 19,000 of 23,000" (ROM). Roma total: 220,000–500,000 (USHMM via ROM; Hitler notes) vs 250,000–500,000 (ROM lead, VNG). Roma shot in the East: up to 225,000 (HOL) — inconsistent with low totals; avoid.
- Vrba–Wetzler escape: AUS text says 27 April 1944; standard date is 7 April 1944 (from memory; not verified here — use "April 1944").
- Treblinka revolt escapees: nearly 400, fewer than 70 survived (TU) vs 200 escaped, almost 100 survived (TRE).
- Jewish partisans: 20,000–25,000 in Soviet partisans (HOL) vs 20,000–30,000 Jewish partisans in total (JP).
- Tunisian forced-labour deaths: 265 (TN, citing Yad Vashem) vs 46 (KAW). Say "dozens to a few hundred" or omit.
- Hungarian deportations: 434,351 (Ferenczy) vs 437,402 (Veesenmayer) vs "437,000" (HOL) vs "440,000" (ET); deportees to camps "600,000" including annexed territories (WAN). Hungarian dead 564,000 (Braham).
- Kristallnacht deaths: "at least 90" (HOL) vs "at least 91" (Hitler notes). Synagogues "more than 1,000" consistent.
- T4: "around 70,000" plus 120,000 decentralised (HOL) vs 275,000–300,000 total incl. outside Germany (Hitler notes). Sterilised: 400,000 (HOL, period unspecified) vs 360,000 for 1933–39 (Hitler notes).
- Soviet POW deaths: 3.3 million, 60% (HOL) vs WW2 notes "about 3 million or more", "almost 60 per cent". Consistent enough.
- Einsatzgruppen victims: Hilberg 1.3 million Jews (EG) vs 1.5–2 million Jews shot overall (HOL; WW2 notes) — different scopes (all shooters vs Einsatzgruppen).
- Nuremberg IMT: 24 indicted (NT) vs "23 Nazi leaders" tried (HOL) vs Hitler notes "23 ... all but three convicted". Say 24 indicted, 22 tried in person.
- Eichmann execution: 1 June 1962 (ET lead) vs "early on 31 May 1962" (ET body). Use "just after midnight on 31 May–1 June 1962" or "June 1962".
- Majdanek liberation: 22 July (infobox) vs 24 July 1944 (text).
- Sugihara Righteous: 1984 (infobox) vs 1985 ceremony (text). Visas: "over 2,100" (SUG) vs commonly 6,000 people saved; Kitade 3,000.
- Hitler's 1939 prophecy: two English versions (FS: "once more ... Bolshevization of the earth"; FID/Hitler notes: "once again ... victory of Jewry"). Coordinate with the Hitler chapter.
- Wannsee Hungarian Jews in Eichmann's list: 742,800 (WAN) vs 825,000 in 1944 (HU/HOL) — different dates/definitions.
- Libya: "more than 2,000 deported, over a fifth died" (LY) vs Giado 2,600 interned, 562 died (GIA).
- Lemkin's coinage: "1943 or 1944" (LEM); published November 1944.
- Holocaust as "1941–1945" (HOL definition) vs USHMM's 1933–1945 persecution framing in the seed summary — explain the difference.
