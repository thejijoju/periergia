## 1. Scale, name and periodisation
- By 1913 the empire held sway over 412 million people (about 23% of world population); by 1920 it covered 35.5 million km² (about 24% of Earth's land). At its height it was the largest empire in history. [source: BE]
- "The empire on which the sun never sets" was first applied to the Spanish empire of Charles V and Philip II (Francis Bacon and others used it of Spain in the early 17th century) before it was applied to Britain. [source: SUN]
- John Dee was the first to use the term "British Empire" (late 16th century). Henry VIII's Statute in Restraint of Appeals (1533) declared "that this realm of England is an Empire". [source: BE]
- Historians distinguish a "First British Empire" (17th century to the loss of the Thirteen Colonies, based on settler migration and Caribbean sugar) and a "Second" (trading ports and naval bases, then India as keystone, Africa, Australia, NZ). P. J. Marshall (1999): the concept has "held their ground in historians' usage without serious challenge"; Vincent Harlow argued for overlap rather than a clean break in 1783; Timothy Parsons (2014): "there were several British empires that ended at different times and for different reasons". [source: HBE]
- Britain's "imperial century" (1815–1914): around 10 million sq mi and roughly 400 million people added. Britain's trade dominance gave it "informal empire" over countries such as China, Argentina and Siam. [source: BE]
- 1945–1965: the number of people under British rule outside the UK fell from 700 million to 5 million, 3 million of them in Hong Kong. [source: BE]

## 2. The first empire: Ireland, the Atlantic, sugar and slavery (c. 1600–1783)
- England's "first modern colonisation" was in 16th-century Ireland, with "plantations" of English and Welsh Protestants; men from the Munster plantations (the "West Country Men") later helped colonise North America. From 1585 to 1603 England fully conquered Ireland. [source: BE]
- Plantation of Ulster: private plantations from 1606, the official plantation from 1609 under James I; mostly on land confiscated from Gaelic chiefs, several of whom had fled in 1607 after the Nine Years' War; about half a million acres of arable land in six counties. [source: UL]
- Cromwellian conquest of Ireland in the 1650s "completed the British colonisation of Ireland" (HBE wording). [source: HBE]
- Act of Union 1800 created the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland after the 1798 rebellion. [source: BE] The Great Famine (1845–52): excess deaths 775,000 to 1.5 million, emigration of 1 million or more (the Nationalism chapter says "roughly a million" died; consistent). Charles Trevelyan, in charge of relief, limited the government's food-aid programme; large quantities of food were exported from Ireland during the famine. [source: GFI]
- Jamestown (1607) was the first permanent English settlement in the Americas, under the Virginia Company; the Crown took direct control in 1624. Plymouth 1620; Maryland 1634 (Catholics); New Netherland annexed 1664 (New Amsterdam became New York). [source: BE]
- Caribbean: St Kitts (1624), Barbados (1627), Nevis (1628). Large sugar plantations began in Barbados in the 1640s with help from Dutch merchants and Sephardic Jews from Brazil; at first white indentured labour, then enslaved Africans. Jamaica taken from Spain 1655. The West Indies were initially England's most lucrative colonies. [source: BE]
- Royal African Company (chartered 1672) raised England's share of the trade from 33% (1673) to 74% (1683). British ships carried about a third of all Africans shipped across the Atlantic, approximately 3.5 million, until abolition of the trade in 1807. People of African descent rose from 25% to about 80% of the British Caribbean population between 1650 and 1780 (10% to 40% in the Thirteen Colonies). Average Middle Passage mortality was one in seven. [source: BE]
- Navigation Act 1651 restricted colonial trade to English ships, leading to the Anglo-Dutch Wars. Hudson's Bay Company chartered 1670 (Rupert's Land). [source: BE]
- Treaty of Utrecht (1713/14): Britain gained Newfoundland, Acadia, Gibraltar, Menorca and the *asiento* (the right to sell enslaved Africans in Spanish America). [source: BE]
- Seven Years' War (1756–63) and the Treaty of Paris (1763) ended France as a North American colonial power and left Britain "the world's most powerful maritime power". [source: BE]
- Royal Proclamation of 7 October 1763 forbade new settlement west of a line along the Appalachians, delineated as an "Indian Reserve"; it became one of the first disputes between Britain and the Thirteen Colonies. [source: RP63]
- American independence acknowledged at the Peace of Paris, 1783; between 40,000 and 100,000 Loyalists migrated to British North America; New Brunswick split off in 1784; the Constitutional Act 1791 created Upper and Lower Canada. Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776) argued colonies were redundant and free trade should replace mercantilism. [source: BE] (The American Revolution chapter has the detail; cross-refer.)

## 3. The swing East: Company conquest of India (recap only; see Trading Companies and Mughal chapters)
- Plassey 1757; the Company's armies (the Presidency Armies) were overwhelmingly Indian sepoys under British officers. [source: BE]
- Anglo-Mysore Wars: Tipu Sultan killed at Seringapatam in 1799. Anglo-Maratha Wars: after Peshwa Bajirao II's surrender in 1818 the Company controlled a large majority of the subcontinent. [source: BE]
- The 1770 Bengal famine: estimates range from 1 to 10 million deaths; "about a quarter to a third" of Bengal's population; Company revenue collection had been "violently kept up to its former standard". [source: FI] Rajat Kanta Ray calls the new 18th-century economy "plunder"; P. J. Marshall argues the British were "players in what was primarily an Indian play", dependent on Indian elites and bankers, and kept Mughal tax rates; Marshall admits his view is still contested. [source: RAJ; HBE]
- Macaulay's *Minute on Indian Education* (February 1835) argued for forming "a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect" (see Quotations). [source: MAC]
- Indian cloth: British cotton imports into India rose from about 1 million yards (1814) to 13 million (1820), 995 million (1870) and 2,050 million (1890); by 1870–80 Indian producers supplied only 25–45% of local consumption. Britain imposed free trade on India while Europe and the US had tariffs of 30–70% on cotton yarn. [source: RAJ] (Industrial Revolution chapter covers the deindustrialisation debate; cross-refer.)
- Indian population: about 100 million by 1600, nearly stationary until the 19th century; 255 million at the first census (1881). [source: RAJ]

## 4. 1857: rebellion, war of independence, or mutiny?
- Dates: 10 May 1857 (Meerut) – 1 November 1858 (infobox). [source: R57]
- Before 1857: over 300,000 sepoys and about 50,000 British soldiers in the Company army. The Bengal Army recruited high castes (Brahmins, Rajputs, Bhumihars) mainly from Awadh and Bihar. [source: R57]
- Causes: annexation of Awadh (Oudh) in 1856; Dalhousie's Doctrine of Lapse, which denied adopted heirs (e.g. Jhansi, annexed 1853 after the Raja died without a biological male heir); loss of *batta* allowances; taluqdars stripped of estates; fear of religious interference and missionaries. [source: R57]
- The Pattern 1853 Enfield cartridges: rumours that the grease was cow tallow and pig lard. Kim Wagner argues there is little direct evidence that large numbers of greased cartridges were actually issued to Indian troops before the revolt; historians now see the controversy as a catalyst rather than a single cause. [source: R57]
- Mangal Pandey, 34th Bengal Native Infantry, attacked officers at Barrackpore on 29 March 1857; court-martialled 6 April and hanged two days later. [source: R57]
- Rebels reached Delhi on 11 May and proclaimed the 81-year-old Bahadur Shah Zafar Emperor of Hindustan. Delhi retaken by the end of September 1857 (assault from 14 September, John Nicholson killed); Kanpur retaken mid-July 1857; Lucknow retaken March 1858. [source: R57]
- Cawnpore (Kanpur): Nana Sahib led the rebels; after the siege, five men and 206 women and children were confined in the Bibighar and killed; British troops then forced captured sepoys to lick bloodstains before hanging them or blowing them from cannon. Colonel James Neill's punitive killings at Allahabad and Fatehpur began in early June, before the Bibighar killings. [source: R57]
- Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi took Gwalior on 1 June 1858 and died on 17 June 1858 in the battle there. [source: R57]
- Much of India did not rise: the Bengal, Bombay and Madras presidencies "remained largely calm"; Sikh princes and Punjabi troops helped retake Delhi; Hyderabad, Mysore, Travancore, Kashmir and Rajputana stayed out. Canning called the princely states "breakwaters in a storm". Nepalese troops under Jung Bahadur helped retake Lucknow. 80% of Company forces that suppressed it were Indian. [source: R57]
- Deaths: about 6,000 of the 40,000 British in India were killed. Indian deaths: "probably 800,000 Indians were killed, and very likely more", including in famines and epidemics the war caused (infobox, from comparing pre-1857 data with the 1871 census). In Oudh alone some estimates put the toll at 150,000, 100,000 of them civilians. [source: R57] Caveat: these are rough estimates.
- Retribution: "blowing from cannon" (an older Mughal punishment); torture documented; the British press demanded no clemency and mocked the Governor-General as "Clemency Canning". An inquiry ordered by Canning found no convincing evidence of the widely reported rapes of British women, though women and children had been killed. [source: R57]
- Aftermath: Government of India Act 1858 dissolved Company rule; India Office and Secretary of State created; the governor-general became Viceroy. Of 74 regular Bengal Native Infantry regiments, only twelve escaped mutiny or disbandment; recruitment shifted to so-called "martial races" (Sikhs, Gurkhas, Punjabis); the British-to-Indian troop ratio was raised and Indian artillery abolished (except mountain batteries) from 1861. [source: R57; RAJ]
- Queen Victoria's Proclamation of 1 November 1858 promised Indians rights like other British subjects and disclaimed "Our Right and Desire to impose Our Convictions on any of Our Subjects"; Indians later cited it when those rights were withheld. After 1857 the Raj stopped land reform for some 90 years and avoided social/religious intervention. [source: R57; RAJ]
- Naming: "First War of Independence" (common in India and Pakistan), "Indian Mutiny" (common in UK), "Revolt of 1857", "Great Rebellion" etc. In July 1857 Disraeli called it a "national revolt"; Palmerston a "mere military mutiny". [source: R57]
- Historiography: Michael Adas (1971) identified four approaches (nationalist, Marxist, traditionalist rebellion, local studies). Eric Stokes, *The Peasant and the Raj* (1978): those with greatest relative deprivation rebelled, and the presence of prosperous pro-British magnates was decisive. William Dalrymple, *The Last Mughal* (2006), using Urdu sources translated with Mahmood Farooqui, stressed religion and divisions among the rebels and found little proto-nationalism. [source: R57]

## 5. The Raj (1858–1947): rule, economy, famine, nationalism
- Royal Titles Act 1876 recognised Victoria as "Empress of India", at Disraeli's encouragement; Gladstone opposed it as despotic and "un-British". She was proclaimed at the "Proclamation Durbar" in Delhi beginning 1 January 1877, attended by Viceroy Lytton (the monarch herself did not attend; only George V attended a durbar, in 1911). [source: RTA; DUR] Note for the writer: the 1877 Durbar coincided with the Great Famine of 1876–78 (GF dates; the juxtaposition is a fair inference from the dates, not a sourced quotation).
- In 1861 the English population of India was 125,945, of whom about 84,083 were army officers and men; in 1880 the standing army was 66,000 British, 130,000 Indian, plus 350,000 in princely armies. [source: RAJ]
- Railways: built under guaranteed returns of up to 5% to private investors, with the risk borne by Indian taxpayers (some £50 million); route mileage grew from 1,349 km (1860) to 25,495 km (1890); fourth largest network in the world; by 1920 only 10% of "superior posts" were held by Indians; until the 1930s only Europeans were hired as supervisors and engineers (Headrick). [source: RAJ]
- By 1900 the Raj had the largest irrigation system in the world; irrigated land rose eightfold. [source: RAJ]
- India's share of world GDP fell "from above 20% to less than 5%" in the colonial period; the nationalist school argues India was poorer at the end of British rule; per capita income showed no long-term change. [source: RAJ] Caveat: long-run GDP shares are rough reconstructions.
- Jamsetji Tata began in textiles in 1877; Tata Iron and Steel began building at Jamshedpur in 1908, with American technology and Indian capital. [source: RAJ]
- Health: estimated cholera deaths in India over 15 million (1817–60) and 23 million (1865–1917); plague killed about 10 million in India; Ronald Ross proved mosquito transmission of malaria in India in 1898; Haffkine developed cholera and plague vaccines there. [source: RAJ]
- Indian National Congress: founded 28 December 1885 in Bombay; 72 delegates, mostly lawyers; first president W. C. Bonnerjee; A. O. Hume had proposed it in an open letter of 1883. [source: INC] The Ilbert Bill (1883), which would have let Indian judges try Europeans, was partly reversed after a "white mutiny"; RAJ says this turned discontent into political action. [source: RAJ; R57] Congress's early debates "held Great Britain responsible for draining India of its wealth" through unfair trade, restraint of Indian industry, and Indian taxes paying British officials' salaries. [source: RAJ]
- Moderates vs extremists by 1905: Gokhale (Servants of India Society) vs Tilak. [source: RAJ] (Nationalism chapter covers Tilak; cross-refer.)
- World War I: almost 1.3 million Indians had volunteered by 1918; over a million served overseas; at least 74,187 died (the lead says "more than 60,000"). [source: IA1]
- Jallianwala Bagh, Amritsar, 13 April 1919: Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer's troops fired on an unarmed crowd (Hunter Commission estimate 10,000–20,000 people) without warning; Dyer reported firing 1,650 rounds. Deaths: official 379 (Hunter Commission/Sewa Samiti), about 1,100–1,200 wounded; Congress inquiry about 1,000 killed and over 1,500 casualties. Tagore renounced his knighthood (letter of 31 May 1919). Churchill called it "unutterably monstrous"; on 8 July 1920 the Commons voted 247 to 37 against Dyer, yet many Britons hailed him. Britain has never formally apologised. [source: JB; RAJ]
- Gandhi's non-cooperation (1920–22), halted after Chauri Chaura (1922); Lahore Congress (1929, Nehru presiding) demanded *Purna Swaraj*; Salt Satyagraha 1930; Round Table Conference 1931. [source: RAJ]
- World War II: the Indian Army grew from under 200,000 to over 2.5 million by August 1945, "the largest volunteer army in history"; 31 Victoria Crosses. [source: IA2] Britain's 1939 declaration of war included India without consulting Indian leaders (BE: the declaration "included the Crown colonies and India" but did not automatically commit the Dominions). [source: BE]

## 6. Famines and the mortality debate
- Approximately 15 million died in 24 major famines in 1850–99, more than in any other 50-year period; famines measurably slowed population growth especially 1871–1921. [source: FI] BE: "over 15 million" died in late-19th-century famines. [source: BE]
- Great Famine of 1876–78: began after drought in the Deccan; affected Madras, Bombay, Punjab, Mysore, Hyderabad and later the Central Provinces and NWP; 58.5 million people affected; excess deaths 5.6–9.6 million, "careful recent demographic estimate" 8.2 million (other figures in RAJ: 6.1–10.39 million). Part of a global El Niño drought (1877–78) linked to 19–50 million deaths worldwide. [source: GF; RAJ]
- During it Viceroy Lytton oversaw export to England of a record 6.4 million hundredweight (320,000 tons) of wheat. Sir Richard Temple, Famine Commissioner, insisted on *laissez faire* in grain and cut relief rations; the "Temple wage" was 1 lb of grain plus one anna for a day's hard labour; the Madras sanitary commissioner W. R. Cornish argued for at least 1.5 lb plus protein; in March 1877 Madras raised it to 1.25 lb plus 1.5 oz of dal. [source: GF]
- Temple: "everything must be subordinated to the financial consideration of disbursing the smallest sum of money." William Digby: "a famine can scarcely be said to be adequately controlled which leaves one-fourth of the people dead." [source: GF]
- Lytton's instruction: "there is to be no interference of any kind on the part of Government with the object of reducing the price of food." [source: FI] (FI also gives Lytton's "Let the British public foot the bill for its 'cheap sentiment'..." via secondary quotation; use with attribution only.)
- Consequences: the Famine Commission of 1880 and the Indian Famine Codes (passed 1883 under Ripon), early-warning systems later adapted by the UN and FAO. Many South Indian labourers and weavers then emigrated under indenture. Wedderburn and Hume, disturbed by the official response, later helped found Congress; for Naoroji and Dutt the famine "would become a cornerstone of the economic critique of the British Raj". [source: GF; FI; RAJ]
- Famine of 1899–1900 under Curzon: at least 1 million dead (FI); RAJ gives 1.25–10 million. Curzon warned against "indiscriminate alms-giving". [source: FI; RAJ]
- After 1902 no major famine until 1943. [source: FI]
- Bengal famine of 1943: estimates 0.8–3.8 million deaths; Ó Gráda (2007): "today the scholarly consensus is about 2.1 million"; Sen estimated 2.7–3 million (1943–46); the Woodhead Famine Inquiry Commission (1945) about 1.5 million. Over half the deaths came in 1944, from disease, after the food crisis eased. Causes cited: loss of Burmese rice after the Japanese invasion, the "denial policies" for rice and boats (1942), wartime inflation, a cyclone and crop disease (October 1942), inter-provincial trade barriers, hoarding, and the War Cabinet's repeated refusal or reduction of import requests (citing shipping shortages). The provincial government never formally declared famine. [source: BF]
- Wavell (Viceroy from late 1943) called it "one of the greatest disasters that has befallen any people under British rule". Roosevelt in June 1944 declined to divert shipping. [source: BF]
- Debate on 1943: Sen's "entitlements" famine (inflation and speculation priced the poor out); Bowbrick and Tauger stress crop shortfall (Tauger also brown-spot disease); Ó Gráda accepts entitlement framing but weighs shortfall more and stresses "lack of political will" and war priorities; Madhusree Mukerjee accuses Churchill's War Cabinet ("Those assignments show a will to punish"); Tirthankar Roy calls the Churchill-centred view "naive" and blames rivalry and misinformation in the provincial government (Suhrawardy); Zareer Masani argues Churchill was misled and food was the provincial government's responsibility; Utsa Patnaik (2018) blames "profit inflation" policies; a 2019 study (Vimal Mishra et al., *Geophysical Research Letters*) found the 1943 famine, unlike earlier ones, was not caused primarily by drought. [source: BF]
- Late-19th-century famines debate: Mike Davis, *Late Victorian Holocausts* (2000), says 30–60 million died worldwide in El Niño famines whose effects were made lethal by laissez-faire and imperial policy; Indian grain exports rose from 3 to 10 million tons a year between 1875 and 1900. Critics: Alexander Morrison called Davis's "ignorance of Indian history" "profound"; Tirthankar Roy argued famines were better documented under the Raj, not necessarily more frequent, and that excess mortality fell by 1915. Roy elsewhere argues famines were rooted in India's ecology. Sen links British-era famines to the absence of democracy and a free press. [source: LVH; FI]
- Sullivan and Hickel, "Capitalism and extreme poverty: A global analysis of real wages, human height, and mortality since the long 16th century", *World Development* 161 (2023), article 106026: estimate 165 million excess deaths in India 1880–1920 relative to the death rate of 16th–17th-century England. [source: HIC] Their popular op-ed gave "about 100 million" for 1881–1920, and an alternative 1880s baseline gives about 50 million for 1891–1920; critics question the English baseline and whether excess mortality is attributable to British policy; Tirthankar Roy published a rebuttal (not read). [source: WebSearch results incl. https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/117731 and https://mronline.org/2022/12/14/british-empire-killed-165-million-indians-in-40-years/] Caveat: present as a contested estimate highly sensitive to its assumed baseline.

## 7. The drain theory and economic nationalism
- Dadabhai Naoroji (4 September 1825 – 30 June 1917), Parsi from Navsari; Diwan of Baroda 1874; Congress president three times (1886, 1893, 1906); elected Liberal MP for Finsbury Central in 1892, the first British Indian MP; took the oath on the *Khordeh Avesta*; lost his seat in 1895. [source: NAO]
- *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901) estimated a drain of £200–300 million of India's revenue to Britain not recirculated in India. He accepted some "tribute" for services but argued India paid for services and salaries that left the country; his work led to the Royal Commission on Indian Expenditure (1896), of which he was a member. It influenced Gandhi. [source: NAO]
- In 1888 Lord Salisbury said he doubted a British constituency would elect "a black man", meaning Naoroji; the Queen and Liberals criticised the remark. [source: NAO]
- Romesh Chunder Dutt (13 August 1848 – 30 November 1909): ICS officer (entered 1871), managed famine relief in Bengal in the 1870s; Congress president 1899; *Indian Famines, Their Causes and Prevention* (1901); *The Economic History of India Under Early British Rule* (vol. I, 1902) and *in the Victorian Age* (vol. II, 1904). Metcalf and Metcalf call his vol. II and Naoroji's *Poverty* "classic nationalist" accounts of the late-19th-century Indian economy. [source: DUTT]
- Utsa Patnaik's estimate of the drain: about $45 trillion (reported as $44.6 trillion) for 1765–1938, compounding the drain at about 5% across four periods; popularised by Jason Hickel in Al Jazeera (December 2018). Critics cite Tirthankar Roy's estimate that the capital drain was only about 0.9–1.3% of Indian national income (1868–1930s). [source: WebSearch results incl. https://www.aljazeera.com/indepth/opinion/britain-stole-45-trillion-india-181206124830851.html and https://mises.org/power-market/economics-british-colonialism-india] Caveat: neither Patnaik's original text nor Roy's was read; attribute and treat as contested.
- Shashi Tharoor, *Inglorious Empire: What the British Did to India* (2017; first published in India as *An Era of Darkness*), grew out of his 2015 Oxford Union speech on reparations; mixed reviews; 2019 Sahitya Akademi Award. [source: IE]

## 8. Indenture (brief; Abolition chapter has the detail)
- More than 1.6 million Indians transported under indenture (1834 *Atlas* to Mauritius, 36 labourers) until 1917; recruits often deceived, coerced or kidnapped; Gokhale's 1910 bill ended indenture to Natal (effective July 1911); system ended 1917. [source: IIS] More than 500,000 went to British Guiana, Trinidad and other Caribbean colonies. [source: BE]

## 9. Settler colonies and Indigenous peoples
### Canada
- Rebellions of 1837–38 in Upper and Lower Canada led to Lord Durham's *Report on the Affairs of British North America* (1839): union of the Canadas and "responsible government"; it judged Lower Canada "two nations warring within the bosom of a single state", called French Canadians "a people with no literature and no history", and recommended their assimilation. Act of Union 1840 created the Province of Canada (with equal representation for East and West despite Canada East's larger population). Responsible government was first granted to Nova Scotia in 1848. [source: DR; BE]
- British North America Act 1867 created the Dominion of Canada. "Dominion status" was officially introduced at the 1907 Imperial Conference. [source: BE]
- Numbered Treaties: eleven treaties with First Nations, 1871–1921, opening land in what is now Alberta, Manitoba, Saskatchewan and parts of BC, Ontario, NWT, Nunavut and Yukon for settlement and resource extraction. [source: NT]
- Indian Act (introduced 1875, passed 1876) consolidated earlier colonial laws (Gradual Civilization Act 1857, Gradual Enfranchisement Act 1869); defines "status Indians". [source: IACT]
- Residential schools: compulsory from 1894; about 150,000 children attended; peak about 80 schools and 17,000 students in the early 1930s; the last federally funded school closed in 1997; the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2008–2015) documented at least 4,118 student deaths and called the system "cultural genocide"; Pope Francis called it genocide in 2022. [source: RS] Caveat: this was a Canadian federal (Dominion) policy run with churches, not policy made in London; present it as a settler-state legacy.

### Australia
- Cook charted the east coast and claimed it for Britain in 1770; First Fleet arrived 1788. "Unusually, Australia was claimed through proclamation"; Indigenous Australians were considered too uncivilised to require treaties. Convict transportation to NSW until 1840, Tasmania 1853, Western Australia 1868. [source: BE]
- *Terra nullius*: debate over whether the term applied in 1788; in 1819 British law officers advised NSW had been acquired by possession as "desert and uninhabited"; Governor Bourke's 1835 proclamation declared the Crown sole authority for land; the High Court in *Mabo v Queensland (No 2)* (1992) rejected *terra nullius* as a legal basis. Aboriginal Australians had occupied the continent for at least 50,000 years, with about 250 language groups. [source: TN]
- Frontier wars from 1788 to at least 1928 (some to 1934). Smallpox and other diseases may have reduced some communities by 50% or more before settlers arrived inland. [source: AFW]
- Death toll estimates vary widely: Lyndall Ryan's Colonial Frontier Massacres project counts 11,000–14,000 in massacres of six or more; Henry Reynolds (2022) over 30,000; Ørsted-Jensen and Evans about 67,000 in Queensland alone and "probably no less than 100,000" nationally (methodology criticised by Richards and Finnane). [source: AFW]
- Myall Creek, 10 June 1838: at least 28 unarmed Aboriginal people killed by stockmen; seven perpetrators were convicted and hanged, one of the few massacres proven in court. [source: MYC] Coniston massacre 1928: at least 31 Aboriginal men killed by police. [source: AFW]
- W. E. H. Stanner (1968) called historians' omission of the conflict "the great Australian silence"; Keith Windschuttle's *The Fabrication of Aboriginal History* (2002) started the "history wars". [source: AFW]
- Tasmania (Black War, mid-1820s–1832): Aboriginal population 3,000–7,000 in 1803; some 600–900 Aboriginal people and more than 200 colonists died; martial law 1828; the "Black Line" (October–November 1830) of about 2,200 men failed; George Augustus Robinson's "Friendly Missions" with negotiators including Truganini; the 220 survivors removed to Wybalenna on Flinders Island, falling to 46 by 1847. Whether it was genocide is debated; Lyndall Ryan proposes "Tasmanian War". [source: BW]
- Australia federated 1901. [source: BE]

### New Zealand
- Musket Wars from 1818 devastated Māori populations. He Whakaputanga (Declaration of Independence) signed by 34 northern rangatira on 28 October 1835. [source: BE; TW]
- Treaty of Waitangi first signed 6 February 1840 by William Hobson and northern rangatira (Hōne Heke first); about 530–540 Māori (at least 13 women) signed the Māori text, only 39 the English text. Some chiefs refused (Te Wherowhero of Waikato, Tūhoe, Te Arawa, Ngāti Tūwharetoa). Hobson declared sovereignty on 21 May 1840 (North Island by cession, South Island by discovery). [source: TW; BE]
- The texts differ: the English cedes "all the rights and powers of sovereignty"; the Māori grants *kawanatanga* (governorship) while guaranteeing *tino rangatiratanga* (full chieftainship) over lands, villages and treasures. Rangatira may have seen it as sharing power; the Crown as cession. The differences contributed to the New Zealand Wars (1845–72). [source: TW]
- Hobson's words at signing: "He iwi tahi tātou" ("We are [now] one people"). [source: TW]
- In *Wi Parata v Bishop of Wellington* (1877) Chief Justice Prendergast called the treaty "a simple nullity"; this was legal orthodoxy until at least the 1970s. The Treaty of Waitangi Act 1975 created the Waitangi Tribunal; the Office of Treaty Settlements (1988) has negotiated settlements worth roughly $1 billion; the Tribunal gives extra weight to the Māori text. [source: TW]

### Southern Africa
- Britain occupied the Cape in 1795 and acquired it formally in 1806 (about 26,000 colonists then). Boers trekked inland in the late 1830s–40s; Transvaal (1852) and Orange Free State (1854). [source: BE; SBW]
- Cape Frontier Wars with the Xhosa, 1779–1879. [source: CAPE] Xhosa cattle-killing (1856–57): prophecies of the girl Nongqawuse (aged about 15) that killing cattle and destroying crops would bring the ancestors back and sweep the settlers into the sea, amid lung-sickness among cattle; 300,000–400,000 cattle killed; about 40,000 people starved; British Kaffraria's population fell from 105,000 to under 27,000. [source: NONG] (Governor George Grey's use of the famine to recruit labour and seize land is often argued; not checked here.)
- The Cape's 1853/54 constitution created the Cape Qualified Franchise, formally colour-blind for men meeting property qualifications; eroded after Union (Black voters removed from the common roll in 1936, Coloured voters in 1958). [source: CQF; CAPE]
- Second Boer War (1899–1902): British dead 22,092; Boer dead 6,189; over 100,000 Boer civilians in concentration camps, about 26,000 died; Black Africans were interned in separate camps where at least 20,000 died (of about 115,000 interned; infobox flagged "citation needed"). About 10,000 Black men served with Boer units; by 1902 about 30,000 armed Africans served the British. Gandhi organised an Indian Ambulance Corps. Sol Plaatje kept the only known Black diary of the war (siege of Mafeking). [source: SBW]
- Natives Land Act 1913 (Union of South Africa, a self-governing dominion since 1910): barred land sales between Black and white South Africans; reserves initially about 7% of the land, later 13%; repealed 1991. Sol Plaatje and the SANNC (later ANC) went to Britain to protest, in vain; his *Native Life in South Africa* (1916) documents it. [source: NLA]

## 10. Egypt
- Disraeli bought Khedive Isma'il's 44% of Suez Canal shares in 1875 for £4 million; Anglo-French financial control followed Egypt's 1876 bankruptcy. Evelyn Baring (later Lord Cromer) was controller-general in 1879 and pressed for Isma'il's deposition (1879). [source: BE; CRO]
- 'Urabi's movement (1881–82): Joint Note January 1882; riot in Alexandria 11 June 1882 killed 50 Europeans; British fleet bombarded Alexandria 11–13 July; Wolseley's army of over 40,000 landed via the Suez Canal; Tel el-Kebir (13 September 1882); 'Urabi exiled. Gladstone initially wanted to try and execute him but the evidence would not support it. [source: AEW] (Nationalism chapter covers 'Urabi; cross-refer.)
- Debate on why Britain invaded: Robinson and Gallagher (*Africa and the Victorians*, 1961) — to protect the Canal and route to India from perceived anarchy; A. G. Hopkins — no real threat to the Canal; Gladstone's cabinet protected bondholders' interests and sought domestic popularity; after occupation investment rose, interest rates fell, bond prices rose; Galbraith and al-Sayyid-Marsot stress officials (Malet, Seymour) who engineered the crisis. [source: AEW]
- Egypt remained formally an Ottoman vassal, "not part of the British Empire", yet Britain controlled it ("veiled protectorate" is a common label; not checked here). Cromer was agent and consul-general 1883–1907, holding de facto control; he opposed Egyptian independence and in 1901 raised primary-school fees to cut enrolment. [source: BE; CRO]
- Denshawai, 13 June 1906: five British officers shooting villagers' pigeons; an officer died (of heatstroke, per DEN); 52 villagers tried; four hanged (Hassan Mahfouz in front of his own house) and others flogged/imprisoned; it became a turning point for Egyptian nationalism (Peter Mansfield); Cromer resigned in 1907. [source: DEN; CRO]
- Protectorate declared 1914; 1919 revolution after Saad Zaghlul (Wafd) was arrested on 8 March 1919 and exiled to Malta: mass strikes and demonstrations of men and women, Muslims and Copts; at least 800 killed 15–31 March. Britain recognised Egyptian independence in 1922 while keeping major powers; 1936 treaty kept troops in the Canal Zone. [source: EG19; BE]

## 11. West Africa
- Lagos: bombarded in late 1851; Oba Kosoko deposed and Akitoye installed; treaty outlawing slavery on 1 January 1852; annexed 6 August 1861 under threat of force (Oba Dosunmu resisted 11 days); declared a colony 5 March 1862. The Yoruba catechist James White welcomed it in 1853 as tearing out "one of the principal roots of the slave trade". [source: LAG] (Abolition as a justification for expansion; cf. Abolition chapter on the Brussels Conference.)
- Royal Niger Company: George Goldie amalgamated firms (1879); chartered July 1886; its 400+ treaties obliged local rulers to trade only with it despite free-trade pledges at Berlin; territories became the Southern Nigeria Protectorate in 1900; Nigeria amalgamated 1914; the company's successor became part of Unilever. [source: RNC]
- Indirect rule: associated with Frederick Lugard, High Commissioner of Northern Nigeria 1899–1906, after the conquest of the Sokoto Caliphate; ruling through emirs was cheaper and needed fewer officials; set out in *The Dual Mandate in British Tropical Africa* (1922). Where no suitable chiefs existed the British appointed "warrant chiefs" (southern Nigeria, Kenya). Lugard was Governor-General of the amalgamated Nigeria 1914–19. Mahmood Mamdani (*Citizen and Subject*) called the system "decentralised despotism"; critics say it hardened ethnic divisions. [source: IR; LUG]
- Women's War (Ogu Umunwanyi), November 1929, Owerri and Calabar provinces: thousands of Igbo (and Ibibio, Annang) women protested warrant chiefs and feared taxation of women; sparked on 18 November when Nwanyeruwa was told to count her goats, sheep and people ("Was your widowed mother counted?"); "sitting on a man" protests; 16 Native Courts attacked; about 55 women killed by colonial troops; in 1930 the warrant-chief system was abolished and women appointed to Native Courts. [source: WW]
- Ghana (Gold Coast) became independent in 1957, the first in sub-Saharan Africa (BE lists the Gold Coast among the 1950s independences; "first" not explicitly checked). [source: BE]

## 12. The Caribbean after emancipation
- Morant Bay, Jamaica, 11 October 1865: Paul Bogle's march; Governor Edward Eyre's martial law killed 439 people directly; 354 arrested were later executed, many without proper trial; the mixed-race assemblyman George William Gordon was tried under martial law and hanged on 23 October. The Jamaica Committee (Bright, Darwin, Huxley and others) sought to prosecute Eyre; the Eyre Defence Committee included Carlyle, Ruskin, Tennyson, Kingsley and Dickens. The Jamaican Assembly then gave up its charter, ending two centuries of elected representation; Jamaica became a Crown colony. [source: MB] (Abolition chapter has the bare event; this chapter can add the Crown-colony consequence and the British debate.)
- 1934–39 labour unrest across the British West Indies (British Honduras, Trinidad sugar and oilfields, St Kitts, Barbados 1937 with 14–22 dead, Jamaica 1938 with 46 dead); women were central; Indo- and Afro-Trinidadian workers cooperated. The Moyne Commission investigated (1938–39). In Jamaica, Alexander Bustamante's strike and Norman Manley's mediation laid the foundations of the party system. [source: BWI]
- West Indies Federation (1958) collapsed after Jamaica and Trinidad left; both independent 1962; Barbados and Guyana 1966. [source: BE]

## 13. The world wars, overstretch and Ireland
- Alliance with Japan 1902, entente with France 1904 and Russia 1907. Over 2.5 million men served in Dominion armies in WWI; Gallipoli (Anzac Day) and Vimy Ridge as national moments; Imperial War Cabinet 1917. After Versailles the empire reached its greatest extent (+1.8 million sq mi, 13 million subjects, mandates in Palestine, Transjordan, Iraq, Tanganyika etc.). [source: BE]
- Balfour Declaration 1926: Britain and the Dominions "autonomous Communities within the British Empire, equal in status, in no way subordinate one to another"; Statute of Westminster 1931. [source: BE; CW]
- Irish Free State (1922) after the War of Independence and the Anglo-Irish Treaty (1921); Northern Ireland stayed in the UK. [source: BE]
- WWII: Fall of Singapore (February 1942) "irreversibly harmed Britain's standing"; the Atlantic Charter (August 1941) spoke of "the rights of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they live", which Churchill denied applied to India. Britain ended the war "essentially bankrupt", saved by a $3.75 billion US loan (1946). [source: BE]

## 14. Decolonisation
- India: Attlee promised independence by June 1948; Mountbatten brought it forward to 15 August 1947. India and Pakistan came into being at midnight 14–15 August 1947. The Radcliffe Line divided Punjab and Bengal (Sir Cyril Radcliffe, a London barrister, chaired both boundary commissions). Displacement 12–20 million; deaths estimated (as of 2009) between 200,000 and 2 million. India claimed 33,000 Hindu and Sikh women abducted; Pakistan claimed 50,000 Muslim women. [source: PI; BE]
- Burma and Ceylon independent 1948; Palestine Mandate ended 15 May 1948; Malayan Emergency 1948–60, Malaya independent 1957. [source: BE]
- London Declaration 1949 allowed India to remain in the Commonwealth as a republic. The Commonwealth today has 56 members, including states never British-ruled (Mozambique, Rwanda, Gabon, Togo). [source: CW]
- Suez: Nasser nationalised the canal on 26 July 1956; secret Sèvres protocol (22–24 October) between Britain, France and Israel; Israel invaded 29 October; Anglo-French landings followed; UN General Assembly Resolution 997 (2 November) passed 64–5; Eisenhower threatened to sell sterling; Britain withdrew and Eden resigned. Egyptian losses in the Anglo-French operation about 650 dead; about 1,000 Egyptian civilians (unsourced estimate). MPs called it "Britain's Waterloo". [source: SUEZ; BE]
- Macmillan's "wind of change" speech, Cape Town, February 1960; Sudan, the Gold Coast and Malaya independent in the 1950s, nearly ten times as many in the 1960s; Colonial Office abolished 1966. [source: BE]
- Kenya (Mau Mau, Emergency declared 20 October 1952): about 7 million acres had been taken for the "White Highlands"; officially 80,000 interned (Elkins: 160,000–320,000); over a million Kikuyu held in "enclosed villages"; 1,090 executed, more than double the French in Algeria; deaths estimated at 25,000 (Anderson) to 50,000 (Blacker), Elkins "tens of thousands, perhaps hundreds of thousands" (contested by Blacker). Hola massacre 1959. Many Kikuyu fought against Mau Mau (loyalists), and it is also described as an intra-Kikuyu civil war. In 1957 Kenya's attorney general Griffith-Jones wrote that abuse in the camps was "distressingly reminiscent of conditions in Nazi Germany or Communist Russia". [source: MM] In *Mutua and Others v FCO*, the UK on 6 June 2013 agreed to pay £19.9 million (settlement plus costs) for 5,228 claimants, with Foreign Secretary William Hague expressing "sincere regret" while denying liability, and supported a memorial in Nairobi (unveiled 2015). [source: WebSearch results incl. https://www.gov.uk/government/news/statement-to-parliament-on-settlement-of-mau-mau-claims; https://itv.com/news/2013-06-06/uk-to-pay-19-9m-compensation-to-mau-mau-torture-victims; ELK]
- Southern Rhodesia's UDI (1965), civil war to Lancaster House (1979), Zimbabwe 1980 — the last British possession in Africa. Cyprus independent 1960; Aden 1967; withdrawal "East of Suez" announced 16 January 1968. [source: BE]
- Hong Kong: Hong Kong Island ceded 1842, Kowloon 1860, New Territories leased for 99 years in 1898; Sino-British Joint Declaration 1984 ("one country, two systems" for 50 years); handover at midnight 1 July 1997, ending 156 years of British rule; Prince Charles attended; often considered the end of empire. Beijing rejects the phrase "transfer of sovereignty". [source: HK; BE]
- Britain retains 14 overseas territories; 15 Commonwealth realms share the monarch. [source: BE]

## Debates
- **Why the empire? (British angle).** Seeley (1883) told the story as expansion of the English nation, defended rule in India as in India's interest, yet doubted India added to British security; his book inspired Chamberlain, Rosebery, Cromer, Curzon, Milner and Kipling. [source: SEE; HBE] Gallagher and Robinson (1953) argued for continuity of "informal empire" from the 1850s and launched the "Cambridge School"; D. C. M. Platt (1968) and Martin Lynn objected that informal influence was weaker than claimed; Darwin (1997) and Gjersø (2015) criticised the strategic model for East Africa. [source: IFT; HBE] Cain and Hopkins (1980s; *British Imperialism*, 1993): "gentlemanly capitalism" of the City of London and landed interests rather than provincial industry; Hopkins applied it to Egypt in 1882 against Robinson and Gallagher's strategic reading. [source: GC; AEW] Robinson (1972) on collaboration and Hobson/Lenin are in the Imperial Expansion chapter — cross-refer.
- **The drain.** Naoroji and Dutt (1890s–1900s) argued Britain drained India's wealth through "home charges", salaries, unfair trade and suppressed industry; Patnaik's large compounded estimate revives it; critics (Roy) put the transfer at about 1% of national income a year. P. J. Marshall stresses Indian partnership and continuity with Mughal fiscal practice; Ray and the nationalist school stress plunder. [source: NAO; DUTT; RAJ; HBE; WebSearch as above]
- **Famines.** Was Raj-era famine mortality the product of climate and ecology (Roy), of laissez-faire ideology and export markets (Davis, Digby, Sen's democracy argument), or both? Did the Famine Codes (1880/1883) work (no major famine 1902–43)? For 1943: entitlements (Sen) vs availability decline (Bowbrick, Tauger) vs political will and war priorities (Ó Gráda) vs deliberate neglect and racism (Mukerjee; Patnaik) vs provincial failure (Woodhead Commission, Roy, Masani). Aggregate counts of colonial "excess deaths" (Sullivan and Hickel, 50–165 million depending on baseline) are new and contested. [source: FI; GF; BF; LVH; HIC]
- **1857's name and nature.** Mutiny, rebellion or first war of independence; Stokes's local deprivation thesis; Dalrymple's emphasis on religion and rebel division; Wagner on the cartridge myth and on not inflating or understating atrocities by either side. [source: R57]
- **The settler frontier and genocide.** Tasmania (genocide vs no intent to destroy; "Tasmanian War"); Australia's "history wars" (Reynolds, Ryan vs Windschuttle; death-toll range 11,000 to 100,000+); Canada's TRC "cultural genocide" finding and the 2022 genocide recognitions, with continuing scholarly debate over terminology. [source: BW; AFW; RS]
- **Waitangi.** Did Māori cede sovereignty? Crown reading (cession) vs Māori text (*kawanatanga* only; *tino rangatiratanga* retained); the Tribunal gives weight to the Māori text. [source: TW]
- **The balance sheet.** Ferguson calculates a net benefit to the colonies from institutions, law and global economic integration; Elkins argues systematic violence and emergency law were the empire's "legacy"; Sen (famines), Davis and Tharoor stress harm; Biggar's "Ethics and Empire" project (2017) and *Colonialism: A Moral Reckoning* (2023) defend a mixed or positive moral verdict and were widely criticised by historians; Satia argues historians' faith in progress itself licensed imperial violence; Gopal centres anticolonial resisters as authors of British liberal ideas. BE notes British public attitudes to empire have been "broadly positive" and that some correlation exists between British colonial origin and later democracy, weakening after 30 years. [source: BE; ELK; LOV; FER; BIG; SAT; GOP; IE]
- **Kenya's numbers.** Anderson (25,000) and Blacker (50,000) vs Elkins ("perhaps hundreds of thousands"); Blacker's census critique; Mau Mau as anticolonial war vs intra-Kikuyu civil war (Kenyatta himself called it a civil war). [source: MM]
- **Schools of Indian history.** Cambridge (Seal, Johnson, Washbrook), Nationalist, Marxist and Subaltern (Ranajit Guha, from the 1980s); Condos and Wilson argue the Raj was "chronically insecure". [source: HBE]
