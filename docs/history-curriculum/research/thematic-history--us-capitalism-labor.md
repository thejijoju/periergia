## Colonial economy and merchants
- Tobacco was the major crop of the Chesapeake colonies and rice of South Carolina; just before the Revolution tobacco was about a quarter of the value of colonial exports. The colonies produced about 15% of world iron around the time of the Revolution, though iron exports were small. [source: W:Economic_history_of_the_United_States]
- The Navigation Acts (passed 1651–1673) restricted "enumerated" goods (furs, ship masts, rice, indigo, tobacco) to export to Britain. By 1995 the consensus among economic historians was that the costs the trade restrictions imposed on the colonists "were small". [source: W:Economic_history_of_the_United_States]
- New England merchants were tied into the Atlantic slave economy: the "classic" triangular (molasses) trade linked West Africa, the West Indies and the northern colonies. Slaves grew sugar, molasses was distilled into rum, and rum was traded for more slaves. Wikipedia notes that by volume the slave trade ran mainly to South America and that the New England triangle is a classic textbook example. [source: W:Triangular_trade]
- New England and Newfoundland ships also carried salted cod to southern Europe (the "sack trade", 1620–1709) and brought back wine, oil, fruit and Iberian silver mined by Indigenous captives at Cerro Rico and Cerro de Pasco. [source: W:Triangular_trade]
- When the British left in 1783, many wealthy Loyalist merchants left with them. [source: W:Economic_history_of_the_United_States]

## Hamilton's financial program
- Hamilton's *First Report on the Public Credit* was presented to Congress on 9 January 1790 (commissioned 21 September 1789). The roughly 40,000-word report called for paying holders of government securities at face value ("redemption") and for federal assumption of the states' war debts ("assumption"). [source: W:First_Report_on_the_Public_Credit]
- The Report's figures: about $40 million domestic and $12 million foreign debt inherited from the Continental Congress, plus about $25 million owed by the states, a combined $77 million. [source: W:First_Report_on_the_Public_Credit]
- Compromise of 1790: in private dinners in New York in summer 1790, Hamilton won federal assumption of state debts in exchange for siting the permanent national capital on the Potomac (the future District of Columbia), as Jefferson and Madison wanted. [source: W:Compromise_of_1790]
- The Bank of the United States (First Bank) was chartered in 1791 (Bank Bill signed 25 February 1791), headquartered in Philadelphia, and lasted until 1811 when its charter lapsed. Its capital was to be $10 million in stock, with the government buying $2 million and $8 million sold to the public in the US and abroad. Jefferson and Madison opposed it as unconstitutional and as benefiting merchants and investors at the expense of the majority. [source: W:First_Bank_of_the_United_States]
- Hamilton's *Report on Manufactures* was presented to Congress on 5 December 1791. It proposed encouraging manufacturing through bounties (subsidies), moderate tariffs and other government help. Hamilton funded the debt with import tariffs and a controversial whiskey tax. [source: W:Report_on_Manufactures; W:Economic_history_of_the_United_States]
- Later "American System" policies (bank, tariffs, internal improvements) were championed by Henry Clay and the Whigs; Lincoln called himself a "Henry Clay tariff Whig" in his early years. [source: W:Report_on_Manufactures]

## Unfree labour: indenture and slavery
- Between the 1630s and the Revolution, one-half to two-thirds of white immigrants to the Thirteen Colonies came under indentures. [source: W:Indentured_servitude_in_British_America]
- Of some 500,000–550,000 European immigrants to the thirteen colonies before 1775, about 55,000 were involuntary prisoners (convicts). Of the roughly 450,000 voluntary European arrivals, Christopher Tomlins estimates 48% were indentured. About 75% were under 25. [source: W:Indentured_servitude_in_British_America]
- Indenture was regionally uneven: 96% of English emigrants to Virginia and Maryland in 1773–76 were indentured servants, against 2% of those to New England. By 1775 only 2–3% of the colonial labour force were indentured servants. [source: W:Indentured_servitude_in_British_America]
- In the 18th and early 19th centuries many non-British Europeans (e.g. Germans, "Palatines") came as "redemptioners", a particularly harsh form of indenture. [source: W:Indentured_servitude_in_British_America]
- The enslaved population of the United States reached 4 million by the 1860 census. [source: W:Slavery_in_the_United_States]
- About one million enslaved people were forcibly moved from the Upper South to the Deep South through the domestic slave trade; Ira Berlin called this the "Second Middle Passage". More than 193,000 were moved in the 1850s alone. [source: W:Slavery_in_the_United_States]
- In 1860 Southern slaveholders held enslaved people collectively valued at more than $3 billion (about $97 billion in 2022 dollars, per the Smithsonian caption cited). Southern bank notes carried over 125 engravings of enslaved people at work. [source: W:Slavery_in_the_United_States] (The often-repeated comparison "worth more than all railroads and factories combined" was not found in the sources read; see NOT VERIFIED.)
- After emancipation: the Thirteenth Amendment (1865) abolished slavery "except as a punishment for crime", the constitutional basis for convict leasing. In 1898, 73% of Alabama's annual state revenue came from convict leasing. Mississippi ended convict leasing in 1906. Douglas Blackmon (*Slavery by Another Name*) called it a form of bondage distinct from antebellum slavery. [source: W:Convict_leasing]
- Sharecroppers typically paid the landowner one-half to two-thirds of the crop; the crop-lien credit system grew up alongside it. [source: W:Sharecropping]

## Wage work, free labour ideology and early unions
- Commonwealth v. Pullis (1806): Philadelphia journeymen cordwainers (shoemakers) were prosecuted for criminal conspiracy to raise wages; about eighteen similar prosecutions followed over three decades. *Commonwealth v. Hunt* (Massachusetts, March 1842, Chief Justice Lemuel Shaw) settled that peaceable combinations of workers were not in themselves illegal. [source: W:Labor_history_of_the_United_States; W:Commonwealth_v._Hunt]
- Eric Foner's *Free Soil, Free Labor, Free Men: The Ideology of the Republican Party Before the Civil War* (1970) explored the ideals that drove the Northern majority to oppose slavery. [source: W:Eric_Foner]
- Lincoln and the Republicans argued that wage work differed from slavery so long as labourers could expect to rise to self-employment; they "did not challenge the notion that those who spend their entire lives as wage laborers were comparable to slaves". [source: W:Wage_slavery]
- The labour term "wage slave" may originate with the Lowell mill women's protests of 1836. [source: W:Wage_slavery]

## The market revolution and the Lowell mills
- "Market revolution" was popularised by the historian Charles Sellers in *The Market Revolution: Jacksonian America, 1815–1846* (1991), which treated it as a largely negative development, "the triumph of capitalism over democracy". Daniel Walker Howe (*What Hath God Wrought*, 2007) challenged him, arguing most family farmers welcomed larger markets. [source: W:Market_Revolution] (Sellers's book is dated 1991 in the text and 1992 in the bibliography of the same article; 1991 is the usual date. Howe's book is listed there as 2009 in a bibliography line but was first published in 2007, which was not confirmed here; say "Daniel Walker Howe" without a date, or check.)
- Erie Canal: authorised 1817, construction began 4 July 1817, opened 26 October 1825; tolls covered the state's construction debt within the first year. A canal could cut transport costs by about 95%. Much of the construction labour was Irish (the Irish-labour sentence is flagged "citation needed"; a second sentence says many labourers were Irish). The rumour of over 1,000 deaths from "swamp fever" is doubted by recent research. [source: W:Erie_Canal]
- Panic of 1837: began 10 May 1837, with collapse of a western land bubble and cotton prices; a depression lasting until the mid-1840s followed. [source: W:Panic_of_1837]
- The Boston Associates (Nathan Appleton, Patrick Tracy Jackson, Abbott and Amos Lawrence, led by Francis Cabot Lowell) built mills at Waltham (1813), Lowell (1822) and Lawrence (1845). The Boston Manufacturing Company (incorporated 1814) was the first in the US to put cotton-to-cloth production under one roof. In time they controlled 40% of Boston banking capital, 40% of Massachusetts insurance capital and 30% of Massachusetts railroads. [source: W:The_Boston_Associates; W:Waltham-Lowell_system]
- Lowell mill women worked about 73 hours a week (5 a.m. to 7 p.m.), lived in company boarding houses under strict moral codes, and were paid about half of men's wages. [source: W:Lowell_mill_girls]
- In February 1834 Lowell's directors proposed a 12.5% wage cut; the "turn-out" failed within days. In October 1836 a rent rise prompted the Factory Girls' Association and a second turn-out of over 1,500 workers, "nearly twice the number" of 1834 (these strike sentences are partly flagged "citation needed"). In 1845 operatives formed the Lowell Female Labor Reform Association, the first union of working women in the United States. [source: W:Lowell_mill_girls]
- Between 1846 and 1850 dividends to the Boston investors averaged 14% a year. Between 1840 and 1860 spindles rose from 2.25 million to almost 5.25 million, cotton consumed from 300,000 to nearly 1 million bales, and workers from 72,000 to nearly 122,000 (New England textiles). [source: W:Lowell_mill_girls]
- As Yankee mill women left, owners turned to Irish immigrants fleeing the Great Famine (1845–1852). [source: W:Waltham-Lowell_system]
- The mills spun cotton grown by enslaved people in the South (link to the Slavery and Industrial Revolution chapters).

## Slavery's cotton economy and the "new history of capitalism"
- The term "second slavery" (Dale Tomich, 1988), the cotton figures (two-thirds of world cotton, three-quarters of Britain's supply by 1860) and Beckert's "war capitalism" are already in published chapters; cross-refer (see Conflicts).
- Sven Beckert (Laird Bell Professor of History, Harvard; co-director of Harvard's Program on the Study of Capitalism) wrote *Empire of Cotton: A Global History* (2014), winner of the 2015 Bancroft Prize and a 2015 Pulitzer finalist. His first book was *The Monied Metropolis* (2001). [source: W:Sven_Beckert]
- Edward E. Baptist (Cornell) wrote *The Half Has Never Been Told: Slavery and the Making of American Capitalism* (2014), arguing that rising cotton-picking productivity was driven by torture ("the torture hypothesis"). *The Economist*'s September 2014 review caused outrage with the line "Almost all the blacks in his book are victims, almost all the whites villains", prompting "a rare withdrawal of the article and an apology from the magazine". [source: W:Edward_E._Baptist]
- Critics: Alan Olmstead (*Journal of Economic History*): "Edward Baptist's study of capitalism and slavery is flawed beyond repair", attributing productivity gains mainly to improved cotton varieties. Eric Hilt (JEH): "much of its economic analysis is so flawed that it undermines the credibility of the book", and called Baptist's estimate of cotton's share of GDP "a disastrously mishandled undertaking". A 2020 *Economic History Review* study rejected the thesis that slavery was necessary for American economic development. [source: W:Edward_E._Baptist]
- Alan Olmstead and Paul Rhode, "Cotton, slavery, and the new history of capitalism", *Explorations in Economic History* 67 (2018): 1–17, argue that Beckert's *Empire of Cotton*, Walter Johnson's *River of Dark Dreams* and Baptist's book misuse evidence. [source: WebSearch: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:exehis:v:67:y:2018:i:c:p:1-17]
- Seth Rockman (Brown) wrote *Scraping By: Wage Labor, Slavery, and Survival in Early Baltimore* (2009; Merle Curti and Philip Taft awards) and co-edited with Beckert *Slavery's Capitalism: A New History of American Economic Development* (University of Pennsylvania Press, 2016). [source: W:Seth_Rockman] (The Beckert article misdates *Slavery's Capitalism* to 2006; 2016 is correct.)
- Jonathan Levy: *Freaks of Fortune: The Emerging World of Capitalism and Risk in America* (Harvard University Press, 2012; OAH Frederick Jackson Turner Award among others) and *Ages of American Capitalism: A History of the United States* (Random House, 2021), which divides US history into ages of commerce, capital, control and chaos and treats capital as a "process" of valuing assets by expected future gain. Reviewers call it the first big synthesis of the new history of capitalism; one critic says it reduces every major change to a shift in "liquidity preference". [source: WebSearch: https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780674047488; https://www.thenation.com/article/society/jonathan-levy-ages-of-cap; https://casbs.stanford.edu/ages-american-capitalism-history-united-states]

## The corporation and the Gilded Age
- *Dartmouth College v. Woodward* (1819, Chief Justice Marshall) held that Dartmouth's 1769 royal charter was a contract protected by the Constitution's Contract Clause, a landmark of US corporate law. [source: W:Dartmouth_College_v._Woodward]
- *Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad* (1886) is famous for its headnote saying the Fourteenth Amendment recognises corporations as "persons", although the case "did not rule on the Fourteenth Amendment"; *Pembina Consolidated Silver Mining Co. v. Pennsylvania* (1888) reached the same conclusion directly. [source: W:Santa_Clara_County_v._Southern_Pacific_Railroad_Co.]
- *Lochner v. New York* (1905): the Supreme Court struck down a New York law limiting bakers' hours as violating "freedom of contract" under the Fourteenth Amendment; Holmes dissented. [source: W:Lochner_v._New_York]
- "Gilded Age" comes from Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner's novel *The Gilded Age: A Tale of Today* (1873); 1920s historians applied it to the era from roughly the late 1870s to the late 1890s (start dates vary: 1865, 1873 or 1877). [source: W:Gilded_Age]
- From 1865 to 1898 output of wheat rose 256%, corn 222%, coal 800%, and miles of railway track 567%. About 10% of railroad funding came from the federal government, mainly as land grants. [source: W:Gilded_Age]
- About 20 million immigrants came in the Gilded Age's "new immigration", increasingly from Italy, Poland, Austria-Hungary, Russia and Greece, many of them Jews driven from Eastern Europe. [source: W:Gilded_Age]
- The Central Pacific's construction from California to Utah was handled largely by Chinese labourers. The Chinese population counted in the census rose from 63,000 (1870) to 106,000 (1880). [source: W:Gilded_Age] Chinese workers struck in 1867 and the Central Pacific raised wages to $35 a month. The line was completed at Promontory Summit, Utah, on 10 May 1869. [source: W:First_transcontinental_railroad]
- Chinese Exclusion Act signed 6 May 1882: the first major US law barring all members of a specific national group; meant to last ten years, renewed by the Geary Act (1892), made permanent in 1902, and repealed 17 December 1943 (with a quota of 105 a year). [source: W:Chinese_Exclusion_Act]
- Andrew Carnegie, born in Dunfermline, Scotland, emigrated to Pittsburgh in 1848 aged 12 and first worked as a bobbin boy in a cotton mill, 12 hours a day, six days a week. His 1889 essay "The Gospel of Wealth" urged the rich to use their wealth for society. He sold Carnegie Steel to J. P. Morgan in 1901, forming the basis of U.S. Steel; the article gives both $303,450,000 (Carnegie's share, per Hawke) and $480 million (Time) for the sale. [source: W:Andrew_Carnegie]
- John D. Rockefeller co-founded Standard Oil of Ohio on 10 January 1870. By the end of the 1870s Standard refined over 90% of US oil. The Standard Oil Trust was formed on 2 January 1882 (41 investors, 40 companies, nine trustees, valued at $70 million), dissolved by Ohio's Supreme Court in 1892, re-formed as a New Jersey holding company in 1899, and broken up by the US Supreme Court in 1911. Rockefeller became the country's first billionaire on 28 September 1916. [source: W:John_D._Rockefeller; W:Standard_Oil]
- Sherman Antitrust Act: Senate 8 April 1890, House unanimously 20 June 1890, signed 2 July 1890. From *United States v. Workingmen's Amalgamated Council of New Orleans* (1893) it was applied against labour unions, treated as cartels of labourers, until the 1930s; the Clayton Act (1914) and the Norris–La Guardia Act (1932) carved out exemptions. [source: W:Sherman_Antitrust_Act]
- By 1900 trusts dominated steel, oil, sugar, meat and farm machinery. [source: W:Gilded_Age]

## The great strikes
### 1877
- The Great Railroad Strike began 14 July 1877 at Martinsburg, West Virginia, after the Baltimore and Ohio cut wages for the third time in a year; it lasted until 4 September (52 days), involved about 100,000 workers at its height, and was put down by militias, the National Guard and federal troops sent by President Hayes. An estimated 126–160 people were killed. In Baltimore the 6th Regiment killed 10 civilians; Pittsburgh saw the worst violence. In St. Louis it became the first general strike in the US; in Chicago the Workingmen's Party drew crowds of 20,000. [source: W:Great_Railroad_Strike_of_1877]
- Jack Beatty called it "the largest strike anywhere in the world in the 19th century" (via the Gilded Age article, which estimates 80,000 railroad workers plus several hundred thousand others). [source: W:Gilded_Age]

### Haymarket, 1886
- In October 1884 the Federation of Organized Trades and Labor Unions resolved that "eight hours shall constitute a legal day's labor, from and after May 1, 1886". [source: W:Haymarket_affair]
- On 3 May 1886 police killed two strikers at the McCormick Harvesting Machine plant (some papers said six). At the 4 May rally at Haymarket Square, Chicago, an unknown person threw a bomb; the bomb and police gunfire killed seven police officers and at least four civilians. Seven defendants were sentenced to death and one to 15 years; Governor Oglesby commuted two sentences, Louis Lingg killed himself in jail, and four (including August Spies and Albert Parsons) were hanged on 11 November 1887. Governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned the survivors in 1893 and criticised the trial. Haymarket is generally considered the origin of International Workers' Day (1 May). [source: W:Haymarket_affair]

### The Knights of Labor and the AFL
- The Knights of Labor was founded by Uriah Stephens on 28 December 1869; Terence Powderly led it from 1879 and opposed strikes as a "relic of barbarism". Membership: 28,000 (1880), 100,000 (1884), "nearly 800,000" (the article also says "more than 700,000") by 1886, about 20% of workers, then at most 100,000 by 1890 after Haymarket. [source: W:Knights_of_Labor]
- The Knights admitted women and (after 1878) African Americans: Black membership was about 60,000 in 1886, in some 400 all-Black locals; about 65,000 women joined in 1887 (about 10% of members). Leonora Barry was the only woman officer. But Asians were excluded: in November 1885 Knights in Tacoma expelled the city's Chinese residents, and the Rock Springs massacre (Wyoming, 1885) killed scores of Chinese miners. About 50 Black sugar workers organised by the Knights were murdered in the Thibodaux massacre (Louisiana, 1887). [source: W:Knights_of_Labor]
- The American Federation of Labor was founded on 8 December 1886 in Columbus, Ohio, by craft unions; Samuel Gompers (born 27 January 1850 in Spitalfields, London; a cigar maker) was president every year but one until his death on 11 December 1924. The AFL merged with the CIO on 4–5 December 1955 as the AFL-CIO. [source: W:American_Federation_of_Labor; W:Samuel_Gompers]
- Gilded Age craft unions generally barred women, Black and Chinese workers but welcomed most European immigrants. [source: W:Gilded_Age]

### Homestead, 1892
- A lockout and strike at Carnegie Steel's Homestead Works (Pennsylvania), 1 July – 20 November 1892, against the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers. On 6 July strikers defeated some 300 Pinkerton agents arriving by barge. Henry Clay Frick ran the company while Carnegie was the main owner. Casualties (infobox): 7 workers killed; Pinkertons 3–8 killed (that figure flagged as uncertain; William Pinkerton testified to three). On 12 July over 4,000 state militia arrived. On 23 July the anarchist Alexander Berkman (with no link to the union) shot Frick, who survived; this undermined support for the strike. The union was broken; AA membership fell from over 24,000 (1891) to 10,000 (1894). [source: W:Homestead_strike]

### Pullman, 1894
- Nearly 4,000 Pullman Company factory workers struck on 11 May 1894 after wage cuts without rent cuts in the company town. Eugene Debs's American Railway Union called a boycott of trains carrying Pullman cars; at its peak about 250,000 workers in 27 states. The federal government won an injunction and President Cleveland sent the Army (about 12,000 troops). About 30 people were killed in Chicago; David Ray Papke estimated about 40 more elsewhere (infobox: about 70). Debs was jailed for six months (upheld in *In re Debs*, 1895). The boycott ended 20 July 1894 and the ARU dissolved. [source: W:Pullman_Strike]

## Progressive Era, the IWW and Ludlow
- The Industrial Workers of the World was founded at a convention in Chicago on 27 June 1905. Founders included Big Bill Haywood, Eugene Debs, Daniel De Leon, Lucy Parsons and Mary Harris "Mother" Jones. When founded it was the only American union to welcome all workers, including women, immigrants, African Americans and Asians. Peak membership was estimated at over 150,000 in August 1917, with very high turnover. [source: W:Industrial_Workers_of_the_World]
- Lawrence (Massachusetts) "Bread and Roses" strike, 11 January – 14 March 1912: IWW-led, uniting workers of more than 51 nationalities, mostly immigrant women, after a cut in hours (56 to 54) cut pay; strikers sent their children to sympathetic families in other states. [source: W:1912_Lawrence_textile_strike]
- Repression: Everett massacre (5 November 1916, at least five IWW members killed); Bisbee deportation (12 July 1917, 1,186 strikers deported in cattle cars to the New Mexico desert); on 5 September 1917 Justice Department agents raided IWW halls nationwide, and 166 leaders were indicted in Chicago under the Espionage Act. [source: W:Industrial_Workers_of_the_World]
- Ludlow Massacre, 20 April 1914: Colorado National Guard and Colorado Fuel and Iron (Rockefeller-controlled) guards attacked a tent colony of about 1,200 striking miners and families; about 21 killed, mostly wives and children (modern estimate: at least 12 children and 2 women among them). An estimated 69–199 people died in the Colorado Coalfield War (September 1913 – April 1914). [source: W:Ludlow_Massacre]
- Triangle Shirtwaist fire, 25 March 1911 (New York): 146 garment workers died, 123 women and girls and 23 men, mostly Italian and Jewish immigrants aged 14 to 23; stairwell doors were locked; 62 jumped or fell. Owners Max Blanck and Isaac Harris were acquitted of manslaughter; a 1913 civil suit awarded $75 per victim. Frances Perkins, an eyewitness, led a Committee on Public Safety and later became Secretary of Labor; New York's Factory Investigating Commission was chaired by Robert Wagner and co-chaired by Al Smith. [source: W:Triangle_Shirtwaist_Factory_fire]

## Fordism and scientific management
- Frederick Winslow Taylor's *The Principles of Scientific Management* was published in 1911. [source: W:Frederick_Winslow_Taylor]
- "Fordism" was popularised by Antonio Gramsci's essay "Americanism and Fordism" in his *Prison Notebooks* (1934): mass production plus mass consumption. The assembly line had precedents in slaughterhouses. In the 1920s–30s the Soviet Union imported Fordist and Taylorist methods and American engineering firms (Thomas Hughes). [source: W:Fordism]
- Ford's moving assembly line (1913) and the $5 day (1914) are in the Second Industrial Revolution chapter; cross-refer.
- Battle of the Overpass, 26 May 1937: Ford Service Department men under Harry Bennett beat UAW organisers including Walter Reuther and Richard Frankensteen at the River Rouge plant, Dearborn. A 1941 strike at the Rouge ended in a contract; on 21 May 1941 a decisive majority, including most Black employees, voted for the UAW. Ford was the last of the Big Three to recognise the union. [source: W:Battle_of_the_Overpass; W:United_Auto_Workers]
- The Great Migration moved about six million Black Americans from the rural South to Northern, Midwestern and Western cities between 1910 and 1970. [source: W:Great_Migration_(African_American)]
- The Immigration Act of 1924 (effective 26 May 1924) set quotas of 2% of each nationality as counted in the 1890 census and barred Chinese and Japanese labourers. [source: W:Immigration_Act_of_1924] (Cowie argues restriction made the New Deal workforce more cohesive; see Debates.)

## The Crash of 1929 and the Depression
- The Dow peaked at 381.17 on 3 September 1929. Black Thursday (24 October; 12.9 million shares traded), Black Monday (28 October; −12.82%) and Black Tuesday (29 October; 16.4 million shares). The fall continued to 8 July 1932, by which time the market had lost about 90% of its peak value. [source: W:Wall_Street_crash_of_1929]
- Unemployment: Stanley Lebergott's widely accepted estimate peaks at 24.9% in 1933; Michael Darby's at 22.5% in 1932. [source: W:Great_Depression_in_the_United_States] (Matches the Modern America chapter.)
- Throughout the 1920s more than 500 banks failed each year. [source: W:New_Deal]
- About 400,000 Mexicans (including US citizens) were "repatriated" to Mexico in the 1930s, per Wikipedia's Depression article; other estimates are higher, so give it as "hundreds of thousands" or attribute. [source: W:Great_Depression_in_the_United_States]

## The New Deal and labour
- The Glass–Steagall (Banking) Act of 1933 separated commercial and investment banking and created the FDIC, initially insuring deposits up to $2,500. The SEC was created to police securities markets. [source: W:New_Deal]
- National Labor Relations Act (Wagner Act), signed 5 July 1935: guaranteed private-sector workers' right to organise, bargain collectively and strike, and created the National Labor Relations Board. It excludes agricultural and domestic workers (as well as supervisors, government employees and independent contractors). Upheld in *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.* (12 April 1937). [source: W:National_Labor_Relations_Act_of_1935]
- Social Security Act, signed 14 August 1935: excluded agricultural labour and domestic service, among others; 65% of the African American workforce and 27% of white workers were initially excluded; coverage was extended in 1950 and 1954. [source: W:Social_Security_Act] (Already in Modern America with the Katznelson–DeWitt debate; cross-refer.)
- Fair Labor Standards Act, signed 25 June 1938: set maximum hours (44 a week at first) and a minimum wage of 25 cents an hour for most categories of workers, and banned "oppressive child labor"; the standard week became 40 hours with time-and-a-half overtime. Farm workers were excluded at first (some covered from 1966); domestic workers were covered only from 1974. The federal minimum wage has been $7.25 since 2009. [source: W:New_Deal (44 hours, 25 cents); W:Fair_Labor_Standards_Act_of_1938]
- The Committee for Industrial Organization was announced on 9 November 1935 by John L. Lewis (United Mine Workers) and others within the AFL; at the 1935 AFL convention in Atlantic City Lewis punched the carpenters' leader William Hutcheson. It became the separate Congress of Industrial Organizations in 1938. [source: W:Congress_of_Industrial_Organizations]
- Flint sit-down strike, 30 December 1936 – 11 February 1937 (44 days): workers occupied General Motors plants; Governor Frank Murphy refused to use force against them; the women's Emergency Brigade supported the strikers; "the Battle of the Running Bulls" mocked the police. GM recognised the UAW, whose membership grew from 30,000 to 500,000 within a year. [source: W:Flint_sit-down_strike]
- U.S. Steel signed with the CIO's Steel Workers Organizing Committee on 2 March 1937 without a strike. "Little Steel" resisted: at the Memorial Day Massacre (30 May 1937) Chicago police killed ten pickets at Republic Steel; SWOC won Little Steel only in 1941. [source: W:Steel_Workers_Organizing_Committee; W:Congress_of_Industrial_Organizations]
- The CIO was open to African Americans; some left-led CIO unions fought "hate strikes" by white workers against Black promotions during the war. [source: W:Congress_of_Industrial_Organizations]
- A. Philip Randolph organised the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters on 25 August 1925, the first labour union led by African Americans to receive an AFL charter. His threatened 1941 March on Washington led Roosevelt to sign Executive Order 8802 on 25 June 1941, banning discrimination "in the employment of workers in defense industries or government because of race, creed, color, or national origin" and creating the Fair Employment Practice Committee. Randolph headed the 1963 March on Washington, organised by Bayard Rustin. [source: W:A._Philip_Randolph; W:Brotherhood_of_Sleeping_Car_Porters; W:Fair_Employment_Practice_Committee]
- Bracero Program, 1942–1964 (from the Mexican Farm Labor Agreement of 4 August 1942): about 4.6 million contracts for Mexican workers, mainly in agriculture. [source: W:Bracero_Program]

## The postwar labour accord
- The 1945–46 strike wave was the largest in American labour history, involving 4.3 million workers (the same article elsewhere says over five million: give "four to five million"). The UAW struck GM for 113 days from November 1945. [source: W:United_States_strike_wave_of_1945–1946; W:United_Auto_Workers]
- Taft–Hartley Act (Labor Management Relations Act): vetoed by Truman on 20 June 1947, the veto overridden; effective 23 June 1947. It required union leaders to swear they were not Communists (CIO leaders who refused were expelled). It also banned the closed shop and allowed state "right-to-work" laws (Section 14(b)); these provisions are standard but were not in the lines read, so check before stating them in detail. [source: W:Taft–Hartley_Act; W:Congress_of_Industrial_Organizations]
- Operation Dixie, the CIO's postwar drive to organise Southern textile workers, "was a complete failure". [source: W:Congress_of_Industrial_Organizations]
- "Treaty of Detroit" (1950): Walter Reuther (UAW president 1946–1970) and GM's Charles Wilson signed a five-year contract in May 1950 that traded a no-strike commitment for higher wages (with cost-of-living and productivity raises), health care and pensions; *Fortune* magazine named it. [source: W:Walter_Reuther; WebSearch: https://www.cbsnews.com/detroit/news/the-treaty-of-detroit-0518] (Do not quote Fortune; the wording on Wikipedia is unsourced and garbled.)
- Union density peaked in 1954 at almost 35%; the number of union members peaked in 1979 at about 21.0 million. [source: W:Labor_unions_in_the_United_States]
- Economists call 1937–1967 (roughly) the "Great Compression" of incomes. Paul Krugman credited unions, Southern support for the New Deal and wartime mobilisation. [source: W:Income_inequality_in_the_United_States]

## Civil rights and farmworkers
- Memphis sanitation strike, 12 February – 16 April 1968: after Echol Cole and Robert Walker were crushed in a garbage compactor on 1 February, more than 1,300 Black sanitation workers struck, carrying "I Am A Man" placards; Mayor Henry Loeb refused to recognise AFSCME Local 1733. Martin Luther King Jr. gave his "I've Been to the Mountaintop" speech in support on 3 April and was assassinated on 4 April; the city recognised the union. [source: W:Memphis_sanitation_strike]
- Delano grape strike, 8 September 1965 – July 1970: begun by the mainly Filipino Agricultural Workers Organizing Committee (Larry Itliong); the mainly Mexican National Farm Workers Association (César Chávez, Dolores Huerta, Gilbert Padilla) joined a week later. They merged in August 1966 into what became the United Farm Workers. A consumer boycott of grapes won contracts covering more than 10,000 workers in July 1970. The UFW joined the AFL-CIO in 1972. Because farmworkers were excluded from the Wagner Act, California passed its own Agricultural Labor Relations Act in 1975. [source: W:Delano_grape_strike; W:United_Farm_Workers]

## Deindustrialisation, union decline and financialisation
- The Rust Belt (formerly the Steel or Factory Belt) declined from the late 20th century: competition from Germany and Japan, the Volcker shock (1979–82), relocation to the lower-wage South, automation and trade liberalisation. Bethlehem Steel ended most production in 1982 and dissolved in 2003. [source: W:Rust_Belt]
- US manufacturing employment has declined since 1979; in recent years the industry employed about 12.76 million (date in article not given clearly), while manufacturing output reached an all-time high in 2024. [source: W:Manufacturing_in_the_United_States] (The common "19.5 million peak in 1979" figure could not be confirmed here.)
- PATCO, 1981: about 13,000 controllers struck on 3 August 1981; Reagan gave them 48 hours; on 5 August he fired 11,345 strikers and banned them from federal service for life. PATCO was decertified on 22 October 1981. Clinton lifted the ban on 12 August 1993. Joseph McCartin's history is *Collision Course* (2011). (The Labor history article says 16,000 members went out; give "about 13,000" and the 11,345 fired.) [source: W:Professional_Air_Traffic_Controllers_Organization_(1968); W:Labor_history_of_the_United_States]
- Major strikes and lockouts fell from 381 in 1970 to 187 in 1980 and 11 in 2010. [source: W:Labor_unions_in_the_United_States]
- NAFTA: signed into law by Clinton on 8 December 1993, in force 1 January 1994, replaced by the USMCA on 1 July 2020. Its effects on jobs remain politically disputed; a 2007 study found "a substantial impact on international trade volumes, but a modest effect on prices and welfare". Clinton: "NAFTA means jobs. American jobs, and good-paying American jobs." [source: W:North_American_Free_Trade_Agreement]
- The "China shock": David Autor, David Dorn and Gordon Hanson estimate Chinese import competition cost about one million US manufacturing jobs in 1991–2007, and (with Acemoglu and Price) up to 2.4 million jobs in all in 1999–2011. Caliendo, Dvorkin and Parro (2019) attribute only about 15% of 2000–07 manufacturing job losses to China. [source: W:Rust_Belt]
- Financialisation: Greta Krippner defines it as a "pattern of accumulation in which profit making occurs increasingly through financial channels rather than through trade and commodity production"; Gerald Epstein as "the increasing importance of financial markets, financial motives, financial institutions, and financial elites". Financial-sector profits grew about 800% (inflation-adjusted) from 1980 to 2005, against 250% for non-financial profits. [source: W:Financialization]

## The 2008 crisis
- The recession ran from December 2007 to June 2009. About 8.7 million jobs (about 7%) were lost from February 2008 to February 2010; real GDP fell 4.2%; unemployment peaked at 10.0% in October 2009 (U-6 at 17.1%) and returned to 4.7% only in May 2016. [source: W:Great_Recession_in_the_United_States]
- Household wealth fell from $61.4 trillion (Q2 2007) to $50.4 trillion (Q1 2009). Median household wealth fell 35% between 2005 and 2011. White home-owning households recovered faster than Black ones, widening the racial wealth gap (ACLU-commissioned study, 2015). [source: W:2008_financial_crisis]
- The Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of 3 October 2008 created TARP with up to $700 billion. The Dodd–Frank Act was signed on 21 July 2010. (Lehman's 15 September 2008 bankruptcy is in the Neoliberal Age chapter.) [source: W:Troubled_Asset_Relief_Program; W:Dodd–Frank_Act]

## Unions, inequality and organising today
- **Union density (BLS, "Union Members — 2025", released 18 February 2026, delayed by the government shutdown):** 10.0% of wage and salary workers (14.7 million members); 32.9% in the public sector and 5.9% in the private sector. In 1983, the first year of comparable data, the rate was 20.1% (17.7 million). The 2025 estimate uses 11 months of data because the October 2025 survey was not collected. [source: W:Labor_unions_in_the_United_States; WebSearch summarising https://www.bls.gov/news.release/union2.nr0.htm and https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/union2_02182026.pdf] (Do not confuse members with the larger number "represented by" unions.)
- In 2016 the US had the fifth-lowest union density of 36 OECD countries. [source: W:Labor_unions_in_the_United_States]
- Inequality (attribute every figure): CBO: top 1% share of market income rose from 9.6% (1979) to 20.7% (2007), then 17.5% (2016). Piketty–Saez–Zucman: top 1% share of pre-tax income 12% (1980) to 20% (2014) (used in the Neoliberal Age chapter). Census Bureau Gini for pre-tax money income: 0.490 in 2025. The decline of unions may account for "one-third to more than one-half" of the rise in inequality among men (Wikipedia summary of the literature). [source: W:Income_inequality_in_the_United_States]
- Gerald Auten and David Splinter dispute these trends: on their after-tax measure the top 1% share barely changed (about 8.5% in 1960 vs 8.8% in 2014), against Piketty–Saez–Zucman's rise from about 9% to about 15% (2019); the gap comes chiefly from how untaxed business income and tax units are treated. PSZ replied in December 2023. [source: WebSearch: https://equitablegrowth.org/new-research-doesnt-overturn-consensus-on-rising-u-s-income-inequality/; https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2023/12/the-piketty-saez-zucman-response-to-auten-and-splinter.html]
- Nearly half a million workers struck in 2018 and 2019, the most in three decades, including statewide teachers' strikes in states where public-employee strikes are illegal. [source: W:Labor_history_of_the_United_States]
- Gig work: California's Proposition 22 (2020) classified app-based drivers as contractors while granting some benefits (minimum earnings, mileage). [source: W:Gig_worker]
- Amazon: on 1 April 2022 workers at the JFK8 warehouse on Staten Island voted 2,654 to 2,131 for the Amazon Labor Union, founded by Chris Smalls (fired after a 2020 COVID walkout); it was the first NLRB-recognised union of Amazon workers in the US. The ALU affiliated with the Teamsters in June 2024. [source: W:Amazon_Labor_Union; WebSearch: https://www.nlrb.gov/news-outreach/region-29-brooklyn/region-29-brooklyn-announces-results-of-jfk8-amazon-ballot-count]
- UAW "Stand Up" strike, 15 September – 30 October 2023 (46 days): under new president Shawn Fain the UAW struck Ford, GM and Stellantis simultaneously for the first time; President Biden joined a picket line in Michigan on 26 September. Ford's deal included 25% wage increases over the contract; contracts were ratified in mid-November 2023. [source: W:2023_United_Auto_Workers_strike]

## Global connections (for the house rule)
- Cotton tied Lowell and Lancashire to enslaved labour in the South (Industrial Revolution, Civil War, Slavery chapters).
- Chinese and Mexican workers built the West's railways, mines and farms and were excluded or deported in downturns (Chinese Exclusion 1882; 1930s repatriation; Bracero Program 1942–64).
- Haymarket gave the world May Day (the Second International's 1890 call is in the Second Industrial Revolution chapter).
- The IWW had wings in the UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand; Finnish and Swedish immigrants built its cultural life. [source: W:Industrial_Workers_of_the_World]
- Fordism and Taylorism were exported to the Soviet Union and Europe (Gramsci named it). [source: W:Fordism]
- Deindustrialisation in the US was the other face of industrialisation in Mexico (maquiladoras after NAFTA) and China. [source: W:North_American_Free_Trade_Agreement; W:Rust_Belt]
- Werner Sombart's *Why Is There No Socialism in the United States?* (German original 1906) framed the long debate on American labour "exceptionalism". [source: W:Werner_Sombart]

## Debates
- **Slavery and capitalism.** The new history of capitalism (Beckert, Baptist, Johnson, Rockman, Levy) treats slavery and cotton as central to American and global capitalism; economic historians (Olmstead and Rhode 2018; Hilt; a 2020 *Economic History Review* study) say these works overstate cotton's share of the economy, misread productivity (better seed, not torture) and misuse evidence. [source: W:Edward_E._Baptist; WebSearch econpapers link above] The published Slavery chapter already presents this debate: summarise and cross-refer.
- **Market revolution.** Sellers saw capitalism defeating democracy; Daniel Walker Howe saw farmers welcoming markets and a "communications revolution". [source: W:Market_Revolution]
- **Old vs new labour history.** Before the 1960s, US labour history was dominated by the "Wisconsin school" of labour economists (John R. Commons) and focused on unions as institutions. The "new labour history", inspired by E. P. Thompson, studied workers' culture, community and experience; Herbert Gutman ("Protestantism and the American Labor Movement", *AHR* 1966; *Work, Culture, and Society in Industrializing America*, 1976) and David Montgomery (a former machinist and union activist; *Beyond Equality*, 1967; *The Fall of the House of Labor*, 1987) were leaders. Critics charge that it neglects institutions and elites. [source: W:New_labor_history; W:Herbert_Gutman; W:David_Montgomery_(historian); WebSearch for the 1976 Knopf date: https://thecrimson.com/article/1976/7/6/new-history-of-an-old-people]
- **Why no socialism?** Sombart (1906) and the exceptionalism debate. [source: W:Werner_Sombart]
- **The New Deal as norm or exception.** Lizabeth Cohen (*Making a New Deal*, 1990) argues a working-class "culture of unity", forged through mass culture and the Depression's destruction of ethnic institutions, made CIO industrial unionism possible. Jefferson Cowie (*The Great Exception*, 2016; with Nick Salvatore, "The long exception", 2008) argues the New Deal order (roughly 1935–1970s) was an anomaly in American political history, made possible partly by the closing of mass immigration in the 1920s. [source: W:Lizabeth_Cohen; W:Jefferson_Cowie; WebSearch: https://pubs.lib.uiowa.edu/annals-of-iowa/article/id/11888/]
- **Exclusions of farm and domestic workers.** Katznelson (racial design by Southern Democrats) vs DeWitt (administrative reasons): already in the Modern America chapter; cross-refer.
- **What drove union decline?** Candidates in the sources: globalisation and automation, the shift to services and the South, employer resistance and the PATCO signal, Taft–Hartley and right-to-work laws. The Wikipedia summary attributes one-third to more than one-half of rising male wage inequality to union decline. [source: W:Income_inequality_in_the_United_States; W:Rust_Belt; W:Labor_history_of_the_United_States]
- **Trade and jobs.** Autor–Dorn–Hanson's China shock vs Caliendo et al.'s smaller estimate; NAFTA's modest aggregate effects vs concentrated local losses. [source: W:Rust_Belt; W:North_American_Free_Trade_Agreement]
- **Measuring inequality.** Piketty–Saez–Zucman vs Auten–Splinter (see above).
