## What environmental history is: definitions and institutions
- There is no universally accepted definition. Donald Worster's widely quoted 1988 definition: environmental history is the "interaction between human cultures and the environment in the past". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history]
- Worster (1988) divided the field into three clusters: nature itself and its change over time; how humans use nature (human impacts); and human thought about the environment (ideas, ethics, laws, myths). These are "generally taken as a starting point" for students. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history]
- J. Donald Hughes (2006) defined it as "history that seeks understanding of human beings as they have lived, worked and thought in relationship to the rest of nature through the changes brought by time". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history]
- Its tools are those of both history and science: archives plus evidence such as tree rings, ice cores, pollen, glaciers, sediments (see climate examples below). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age]
- Environmental history as a self-conscious field first emerged in the United States out of the environmental movement of the 1960s and 1970s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history]
- The American Society for Environmental History was founded in 1977; Europe's first institute devoted to environmental history was set up at the University of St Andrews in 1991; the journal *Environment and History* (White Horse Press, Cambridge) began in 1995; the European Society for Environmental History was created in 1999. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history]
- Environmental history reached historians of the British Empire only in the 1990s, though it had grown rapidly in the US after 1970. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history]
- A recent historiographical shift emphasises inequality: the burden of pollution shifted to less powerful populations, geographically and socially. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history]
- Older ancestors of environmental thinking: Hippocrates' *Airs, Waters, Places* related cultures and temperaments to surroundings; Ibn Khaldun and Montesquieu treated climate as a key determinant of human behaviour. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history]

## Origins: the Annales school, Braudel, Le Roy Ladurie
- The journal *Annales* was founded in 1929 by French historians (founders Lucien Febvre and Marc Bloch); it turned away from political history toward agriculture, demography and geography, and is described as "in many ways a forerunner of modern environmental history". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annales_school]
- Lucien Febvre (1878–1956) wrote *A Geographical Introduction to History* (1922). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history]
- Fernand Braudel (24 August 1902 – 27 November 1985) published *La Méditerranée et le monde méditerranéen à l'époque de Philippe II* in 1949 (English: *The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II*). Its first level of time, "geographical time", is that of the environment, "with its slow, almost imperceptible change, its repetition and cycles"; the book used the *longue durée* and downplayed events. Much of it was conceived while he was a prisoner of war in Germany (in an Oflag near Lübeck from June 1942). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernand_Braudel]
- Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie (19 July 1929 – 22 November 2023) "was the first to really embrace, in the 1950s, environmental history in a more contemporary form" (per McNeill 2003). He published *Histoire du climat depuis l'an mil* (Flammarion, 1967); his best-known book is *Montaillou* (1975). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmanuel_Le_Roy_Ladurie]

## Origins: the American founders and the classics
- Roderick Nash published *Wilderness and the American Mind* in 1967, "a classic text of early environmental history"; it traces American attitudes to wilderness through transcendentalism, primitivism, preservationism and conservationism (4th ed. Yale, 2001). Nash used the expression "environmental history" in a 1969 address to the Organization of American Historians (published 1970); 1972 is generally taken as when the term was coined. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roderick_Nash]
- Samuel P. Hays, *Conservation and the Gospel of Efficiency* (1959), was an earlier major contribution. Moral inspiration came from Thoreau, John Muir, Aldo Leopold and Rachel Carson. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history]
- Donald Worster (born 1941, Hutchinson, Kansas) taught at Brandeis from 1971 and the University of Kansas from 1989. Books: *Nature's Economy: A History of Ecological Ideas* (1977); *Dust Bowl: The Southern Plains in the 1930s* (1979; Bancroft Prize 1980); *Rivers of Empire: Water, Aridity, and the Growth of the American West* (1985). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Worster]
- William Cronon (born 11 September 1954) wrote *Changes in the Land: Indians, Colonists, and the Ecology of New England* (1983, Francis Parkman Prize), based on a seminar paper for Edmund Morgan at Yale. It argued that the way cultures conceive property shapes economies and ecosystems, and documented that Native Americans actively shaped the ecosystems they lived in. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Cronon]
- Cronon's *Nature's Metropolis: Chicago and the Great West* (1991, his Yale dissertation of 1990) won the 1992 Bancroft Prize and the 1993 George Perkins Marsh Prize and was a 1992 Pulitzer finalist. It is credited with widening the field's gaze to cities and the "elaborate and intimate linkages" between city and country; one chapter shows grain turning from sacks stamped with a farm family's name into a standardised, graded bulk commodity stored in elevators. Chicago and capitalism transformed the Midwestern countryside. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Cronon]
- Cronon's essay "The Trouble with Wilderness; or, Getting Back to the Wrong Nature" (New York Times, 13 August 1995; *Environmental History* 1:1, January 1996, pp. 7–28; and in *Uncommon Ground*, 1995) argued that the idea of pristine untouched wilderness is a fantasy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Cronon]
- Other classics named on WP's list: Carolyn Merchant, *The Death of Nature: Women, Ecology and the Scientific Revolution* (1980); Richard Grove, *Green Imperialism* (1995); Clarence Glacken, *Traces on the Rhodian Shore* (1967). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history]

## Biological exchange and ecological imperialism (Crosby)
- Alfred W. Crosby Jr. (15 January 1931 – 14 March 2018), University of Texas at Austin. He coined "Columbian exchange" in *The Columbian Exchange: Biological and Cultural Consequences of 1492* (Greenwood, 1972); he also coined "Neo-Europes" and "virgin soil epidemic". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_W._Crosby]
- *Ecological Imperialism: The Biological Expansion of Europe, 900–1900* (Cambridge University Press, 1986) asks why Europeans established settler societies in temperate lands worldwide ("Neo-Europes": e.g. North America, Australia, New Zealand, southern South America). His answer is biogeographical: the "portmanteau biota" (disease microbes, weeds, domesticated plants and animals) that travelled with Europeans devastated local populations and remade landscapes. He explicitly rejected explanations based on innate European superiority. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_Imperialism_(book)]
- Neo-Europes received a great influx of European settlers between 1820 and 1930; they lie at latitudes similar to Europe, "two-thirds in the temperate zones". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_Imperialism_(book)]
- Crosby said *Ecological Imperialism* "took The Columbian Exchange up another notch in scope and abstraction." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_Imperialism_(book)]
- Climate connection: the "Orbis spike", a dip of an estimated 7–10 ppm in atmospheric CO2 around 1610 recorded in Antarctic ice (Law Dome, West Antarctic Ice Sheet), is most often explained by reforestation of farmland after the death of tens of millions of Indigenous Americans from introduced disease; a South American drought is an alternative explanation. Lewis and Maslin (*Nature*, March 2015) proposed 1610 as an Anthropocene start. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbis_spike; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene]

## Climate as an actor: the Little Ice Age and the seventeenth-century crisis
- The term "Little Ice Age" was introduced into scientific literature by François E. Matthes in 1939. It was a period of regional cooling, particularly pronounced in the North Atlantic region, "not a true ice age of global extent". Conventionally 16th–19th centuries; some prefer c. 1300 to c. 1850. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age]
- NASA Earth Observatory notes three particularly cold intervals beginning about 1650, 1770 and 1850. The IPCC Third Assessment Report found largely independent regional changes rather than globally synchronous cooling, with "at most" modest Northern Hemisphere cooling. IPCC AR6: global mean surface temperature 1450–1850 averaged –0.03 [–0.30 to 0.06] °C relative to 1850–1900. Caveat for the writer: present the LIA as uneven and regional, not as a uniform global freeze. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age]
- Proposed causes: low solar activity (the Maunder Minimum), heightened volcanism (e.g. Samalas 1257, Kuwae 1450s), ocean circulation changes, orbital factors, internal variability, and (debated) reforestation after population collapses. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age]
- Norse Greenland: diet shifted toward seals as climate worsened after c. 1250; the last written record is a 1408 marriage at Hvalsey Church (WP elsewhere gives the last document as 1412). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age]
- China: warm-weather crops such as oranges were abandoned in Jiangxi; Coching Chu's 1972 study judged 1650–1700 one of the coldest periods in recorded Chinese history; rainfall in Huabei fell 11–47% below average in 1632–1641; scholars link droughts and famines to the fall of the Ming (1644). (Do NOT use WP's sentence that Li Zicheng "led the Later Jin's forces" into Beijing: Li was a rebel against both Ming and the Manchus.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age]
- Early American colonies: Jamestown (1607) was settled in one of the coldest periods of the past 1,000 years, and Roanoke during the worst drought in 800 years (tree-ring studies); drought also hurt Native populations and drove conflict over food. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age]
- Scapegoating: Behringer (1999) and Oster (2004) link the resurgence of witch trials to climatic decline; by the 1480s witches were widely blamed for bad weather. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age]
- The "General Crisis": Eric Hobsbawm's two 1954 *Past & Present* articles, "The Crisis of the Seventeenth Century" (economic crisis); Hugh Trevor-Roper's 1959 "The General Crisis of the Seventeenth Century" (a crisis between society and the state, "Court" v. "Country"). By 1990 Niels Steensgaard thought the debate (1954–1978) superseded. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_General_Crisis]
- Geoffrey Parker, *Global Crisis: War, Climate Change and Catastrophe in the Seventeenth Century* (Yale, 2013) widened it to a global crisis worsened by the Little Ice Age; the 1640s saw more state breakdowns than any period before or since; Ming China collapsed. Publisher/reviewer material says the crisis may have killed as much as one-third of the world's population: treat as Parker's rough, contested estimate ("perhaps"). Reviewers note his evidence includes tree rings, ice cores and pollen beside written records. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_General_Crisis; https://www.americamagazine.org/books/2014/03/12/storm-warning/; https://www2.publishersweekly.com/978-0-300-15323-1]
- Sam White, *The Climate of Rebellion in the Early Modern Ottoman Empire* (Cambridge, 2011): Little Ice Age cold and drought unravelled Ottoman provisioning and settlement systems, feeding the Celali Rebellion (1595–1610), which reversed more than a century of imperial growth. [source: https://digitalcommons.oberlin.edu/faculty_schol/1590; https://ebooks.mpdl.mpg.de/ebooks/Record/EB000738050/Description]
- Dagomar Degroot (Georgetown), *The Frigid Golden Age: Climate Change, the Little Ice Age, and the Dutch Republic, 1560–1720* (Cambridge, 2018): some societies thrived. The Dutch were resilient, even adaptive; merchants exploited harvest failures elsewhere, commanders used shifting winds, inventors profited from the cold. A useful counterweight to pure catastrophe stories. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age; https://newbooksnetwork.com/dagomar-degroot-the-frigid-golden-age-climate-change-the-little-ice-age-and-the-dutch-republic-1560-1720-cambridge-up-2018]
- John L. Brooke, *Climate Change and the Course of Global History: A Rough Journey* (Cambridge, 2014), described as the first global study by a historian to integrate earth-system climate science with the material history of humanity. [source: https://history.osu.edu/publications/climate-change-and-course-global-history-rough-journey]
- The "Year Without a Summer" (1816): the April 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora (Sumbawa, Dutch East Indies), VEI 7, the largest in at least 1,300 years, cut average global temperatures by about 0.4–0.7 °C; European summer 1816 was the coldest on record between 1766 and 2000; crop failures, food riots and famine across the Northern Hemisphere. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_Without_a_Summer]

## Anthropogenic climate change: the science in brief
- 1856: Eunice Newton Foote showed CO2 has a strong heat-trapping effect ("An atmosphere of that gas would give to our earth a high temperature"). From 1859 John Tyndall showed water vapour, methane and CO2 absorb radiated heat and proposed that changes in them may have caused past climate changes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change]
- Charles David Keeling (Scripps) began regular CO2 measurements at Mauna Loa in March 1958, producing the Keeling Curve. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keeling_Curve]
- James Hansen's 1988 US Senate testimony popularised "global warming". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change]
- 2016–2025 averaged 1.26 °C [1.13–1.36] above the 1850–1900 baseline; 2024 was the warmest year on record at about +1.60 °C. The IPCC uses 1750 as the "pre-industrial" baseline for greenhouse gases. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene]

## The Anthropocene debate
- Antecedents: Antonio Stoppani's "anthropozoic era" (1873). Eugene F. Stoermer used "Anthropocene" in the 1980s; Paul Crutzen and Stoermer published "The 'Anthropocene'" in the IGBP *Global Change Newsletter* in May 2000; Crutzen's "Geology of Mankind" appeared in *Nature* in 2002. Crutzen proposed the Industrial Revolution (late 18th century) as the start. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene_Working_Group]
- Crutzen shared the 1995 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland for work on stratospheric ozone. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_depletion]
- Candidate start dates: early farming c. 8,000 years ago (William Ruddiman; contested); 1610 Orbis spike (Lewis and Maslin 2015); Industrial Revolution / 1784 steam engine; Trinity test 16 July 1945; 1950s bomb radionuclides / Great Acceleration. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene]
- The Anthropocene Working Group (AWG) was established in 2009 under the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy (SQS) of the International Commission on Stratigraphy (ICS). In May 2019 it voted 29–4 to treat the Anthropocene as a formal unit with a mid-20th-century start. On 11 July 2023 it proposed Crawford Lake, Ontario, Canada as the "golden spike" site, with a start in 1952 marked by plutonium. J. R. McNeill and Naomi Oreskes were AWG members. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene_Working_Group]
- In March 2024 (vote on 4 March) the SQS rejected the proposal, 12 against, 4 for, 2 abstentions (about 66% against). The IUGS (with the ICS) confirmed the rejection in a statement of 20 March 2024 and rejected a procedural challenge. Reasons: the very recent start date and shallow sedimentary record, and the problem of fixing one date for changes that began at different times in different places (diachronous). No new proposal may be submitted for at least ten years. AWG chair Colin Waters called the result "very disappointing". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene; https://adn.com/nation-world/2024/03/05/have-humans-started-a-new-geologic-epoch-scientists-say-no; https://climateandcapitalism.com/2024/03/21/top-geology-body-denies-appeal-rejects-anthropocene-proposal]
- An alternative proposal (2021, expanded 2022) treats the Anthropocene as a geological "event" rather than an epoch. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene]
- J. R. McNeill (with Peter Engelke), *The Great Acceleration: An Environmental History of the Anthropocene Since 1945* (2016). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._R._McNeill]
- Dipesh Chakrabarty (born 1948, Kolkata; University of Chicago; author of *Provincializing Europe*), "The Climate of History: Four Theses", *Critical Inquiry* 35:2 (Winter 2009), pp. 197–222. Thesis 1 is that anthropogenic climate change collapses the old humanist distinction between natural history and human history, because humans have become a geophysical force. He developed this in *The Climate of History in a Planetary Age* (2021). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipesh_Chakrabarty; https://intersections.ssrc.org/citations/the-climate-of-history-four-theses/; https://eurozine.com/the-climate-of-history-four-theses]
- "Capitalocene": coined in 2009 by the Swedish human ecologist Andreas Malm (then a doctoral student at Lund), spread to Donna Haraway and Jason W. Moore. Malm dates the problem to fossil capital's rise in British cotton after c. 1825–1830s; Moore to the "long seventeenth century". Their objection: "Anthropocene" blames humanity in general, when not all humans are equally responsible. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalocene; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene]
- Jason W. Moore, *Capitalism in the Web of Life: Ecology and the Accumulation of Capital* (Verso, 2015): capitalism is a way of organising nature that depends on "Cheap Nature" (cheap labour, food, energy, raw materials) taken from frontiers. [source: https://blackwells.co.uk/bookshop/product/Capitalism-in-the-Web-of-Life-by-Jason-W-Moore/9781781689011; https://antipodeonline.org/2016/03/09/capitalism-in-the-web-of-life/]
- Kathryn Yusoff, *A Billion Black Anthropocenes or None* (University of Minnesota Press, Forerunners series, 2018): geology's "grammar" underpinned extractive economies of colonialism and slavery; the Anthropocene's universal "we" erases Black and brown suffering. Janae Davis and others propose "Plantationocene". [source: https://manifold.umn.edu/projects/a-billion-black-anthropocenes-or-none; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene]

## Extinctions
- Late Pleistocene: most of the world's megafauna (species of 45 kg or more) went extinct; major extinctions in Australia–New Guinea (Sahul) from about 50,000 years ago and in the Americas about 13,000 years ago, coinciding with the arrival of modern humans; Africa lost far fewer. Causes debated: human "overkill", climate change, or both. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Pleistocene_extinctions]
- Island extinctions: the moa of New Zealand disappeared in the mid-15th century after Polynesian (Māori) settlement in the late 13th century (hunting and habitat loss); the dodo of Mauritius died out in the mid-to-late 17th century (habitat loss, hunting, introduced mammals). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene_extinction]
- Passenger pigeon: perhaps 3–5 billion at peak, possibly the most numerous bird on Earth; Arlie Schorger estimated 25–40% of US land birds. Native peoples hunted it (the Seneca made offerings before hunting juveniles; the Wendat linked it to the Feast of the Dead). Railroads and the telegraph (1860s) commercialised the hunt; the last large nesting, at Petoskey, Michigan, in 1878, saw 50,000 birds killed a day for nearly five months; about 180 million acres of eastern forest were cleared for farming 1850–1910. Last fully authenticated wild bird: shot near Oakford, Illinois, 12 March 1901. The last of the species, Martha, died at the Cincinnati Zoo on 1 September 1914. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passenger_pigeon; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martha_(passenger_pigeon)]
- The "sixth extinction" / Holocene extinction: current extinction rates estimated at 100 to 1,000 times background rates. The 2019 IPBES Global Assessment estimated about one million species at risk of extinction within decades; ranked drivers: land and sea-use change, direct exploitation, climate change, pollution, invasive species. The 2022 *Living Planet Report* found average vertebrate population declines of almost 70% since 1970. Whether this is yet a true "mass extinction" is debated (Stuart Pimm: we are "on the edge" of it). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene_extinction]

## Energy as history's motor
- Werner Sombart called the pre-industrial era a "wooden age"; Joachim Radkau (*Wood: A History*, Eng. 2012) argues the "wood brake" gave stability; the German idea of *Holznot* (wood famine) is debated, with some historians since the 1980s treating it partly as a myth used to justify state forestry. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holznot]
- London switched widely to coal for home hearths in the late 16th and early 17th centuries; in 1700 five-sixths of the world's coal was mined in Britain; Britain would have run out of watermill sites by the 1830s without coal. Northern Song China (960–1127) had already used coal on a large scale for iron in the 11th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- Kenneth Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence: China, Europe, and the Making of the Modern World Economy* (Princeton, 2000): Britain escaped ecological constraints through conveniently located coal and New World resources; China's large coal deposits lay in the northwest, far from the Yangzi delta core. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Pomeranz]
- Vaclav Smil's account: world energy moved from biomass to coal to oil. Fossil fuels matched traditional biomass globally only in the late 1890s (summaries give 1890s–1900); the 19th century was still largely a wooden era; oil overtook coal in the 1960s (one summary says 1964, unconfirmed); traditional biomass still supplied about 5% of world primary energy in 2020. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transition; https://energyskeptic.com/2026/vaclav-smil-why-energy-transitions-take-such-a-long-time/]
- Jean-Baptiste Fressoz's counter-argument: "energy transition" was a politicians' term for a future goal (popularised after the 1973 oil crisis; Jimmy Carter used it in 1977), and historically energy sources were added, not replaced: more wood was burned in the 19th century even as coal grew. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transition]
- Andreas Malm (*Fossil Capital*, 2016): British cotton masters turned from water to steam after the 1820s not because water ran out but because steam let them locate mills in towns and control labour. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalocene]
- Nuclear: in the 1970s–80s nuclear power gained large shares in some countries (France and Slovakia still get over half their electricity from it); deployment slowed after the late 1990s; accidents at Three Mile Island (1979) and Chernobyl (1986) dented demand. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transition]
- Coal today: about a quarter of world primary energy and over a third of electricity in 2020; China mines almost half the world's coal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal]

## Disease environments (McNeill)
- J. R. McNeill (born 6 October 1954, Chicago; Georgetown since 1985; son of William H. McNeill). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._R._McNeill]
- *Something New Under the Sun: An Environmental History of the Twentieth-Century World* (2000) argues that 20th-century human activity changed environments on an unprecedented scale (World History Association Book Prize 2001). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._R._McNeill]
- *Mosquito Empires: Ecology and War in the Greater Caribbean, 1620–1914* (2010; AHA Beveridge Award): the shift to sugar plantations widened the scope for yellow fever and malaria; "differential resistance" between locally seasoned populations and newly arrived European troops helps explain why Spain held its Caribbean colonies against rivals for so long and why imperial powers lost mainland empires in the revolutionary wars of the late 18th and early 19th centuries. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._R._McNeill]

## Deforestation, soils and the Dust Bowl
- The Dust Bowl struck the southern Plains in the 1930s, centred on the Texas and Oklahoma panhandles, affecting about 100 million acres. Causes: severe drought (waves in 1934, 1936, 1939–40) plus the deep ploughing of the shortgrass prairie, removing deep-rooted grasses; mechanisation (gasoline tractors, combines) and wartime wheat prices encouraged the conversion. Homestead Acts of 1862 (160 acres), Kinkaid Act 1904 and Enlarged Homestead Act 1909 had drawn settlers onto semi-arid land west of the 100th meridian. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_Bowl]
- "Black Sunday", 14 April 1935: a huge dust storm swept the Plains. A May 1934 storm deposited about 12 million pounds of dust on Chicago. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_Bowl]
- About 3.5 million people moved out of the Plains states between 1930 and 1940; many migrated to California, but most migrants moved shorter distances within states. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_Bowl]
- Response: Soil Erosion Service under Hugh Hammond Bennett (August 1933), reorganised as the Soil Conservation Service (1935); Soil Conservation and Domestic Allotment Act (1936); the Civilian Conservation Corps planted the Great Plains Shelterbelt of over 200 million trees from Canada to Abilene, Texas; contour ploughing, strip farming, terracing promoted. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_Bowl]
- Cultural record: Steinbeck's *The Grapes of Wrath* (1939), Woody Guthrie's *Dust Bowl Ballads*, Dorothea Lange's *Migrant Mother* (1936). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_Bowl]
- Worster's interpretation: the Dust Bowl was produced by American capitalist culture, which set out to dominate and exploit the land; revisiting in the mid-1970s, he judged that capital-intensive agribusiness (deep wells into the aquifer, irrigation, chemicals) showed farmers had "learned nothing". Paul (Mathew Paul) Bonnifield's *The Dust Bowl* (1979) instead saw "the triumph of the human spirit in its capacity to endure and overcome hardships and reverses." [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_Bowl; https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/greatplainsquarterly/1915]
- Cronon, "A Place for Stories: Nature, History, and Narrative", *Journal of American History* 78:4 (March 1992), pp. 1347–1376, used these two 1979 books, which share sources and most facts yet reach opposite conclusions, to show that environmental history is shaped by narrative choices (where a story begins and ends). [source: https://williamcronon.net/courses/469/handouts/469-periodizing-landscape-change.html; https://typeset.io/pdf/a-place-for-stories-nature-history-and-narrative-21wszhxpvp.pdf]
- A 2023 *Journal of Economic History* study found large, lasting effects on farmland but modest effects on average wage incomes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_Bowl]

## Regulation and the state: conservation v. preservation, parks, laws, treaties
- Gifford Pinchot (11 August 1865 – 4 October 1946), Yale 1889, head of the Division of Forestry from 1898 and first chief of the US Forest Service (1 February 1905 – 7 January 1910, when he was fired in the Pinchot–Ballinger controversy); later governor of Pennsylvania. His conservation was utilitarian and Progressive: scientific management of resources for use; he supported damming Hetch Hetchy. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gifford_Pinchot; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hetch_Hetchy]
- The 1 February 1905 letter from Agriculture Secretary James Wilson to Pinchot (reportedly drafted by Pinchot) directs that conflicts be decided by "the greatest good for the greatest number in the long run". [source: https://evergreenmagazine.com/felt-necessities-2/; https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090114151349/http://www.fs.fed.us/aboutus/history/chiefs/pinchot.shtml]
- John Muir (21 April 1838, Dunbar, Scotland – 24 December 1914), emigrated to Wisconsin in 1849; helped get Yosemite National Park established (1 October 1890); first meeting of the Sierra Club 28 May 1892, Muir its president. A preservationist. He met Pinchot in 1896; they broke in 1897 over Pinchot's support for sheep grazing in forest reserves, splitting the movement into "preservationists" (Muir) and "conservationists" (Pinchot). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Muir; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hetch_Hetchy]
- Hetch Hetchy: Miwok and Paiute peoples used the valley seasonally for over 6,000 years. San Francisco sought it as a reservoir; Congress passed the Raker Act, signed by Woodrow Wilson, in December 1913 (passed 7 December 1913); O'Shaughnessy Dam completed 1923. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hetch_Hetchy]
- In 2020 the Sierra Club acknowledged racist remarks by Muir and links of some associates to eugenics; others (e.g. Aaron Mair, first Black Sierra Club president) called that account "revisionist". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Muir]
- Yellowstone was signed into law by Ulysses S. Grant on 1 March 1872, widely considered the world's first national park. Native Americans had used the area for at least 11,000 years; in the 1870s–80s tribes were effectively excluded; the Tukudeka ("Sheepeaters", Eastern Shoshone) had been the only year-round residents. Earlier: Hot Springs, Arkansas (1832) and Yosemite Valley grant to California (1864). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellowstone_National_Park; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_park]
- Other early parks: Royal National Park, New South Wales (26 April 1879); Banff, Canada (1885); Tongariro, New Zealand (1887); Albert (now Virunga) National Park, 1925, Africa's first (WP says "Congo Free State", but that ended in 1908; by 1925 it was the Belgian Congo); Kruger, South Africa (1926); Nahuel Huapi, Argentina (1934). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_park]
- National Environmental Policy Act signed by Nixon on 1 January 1970 (environmental impact statements; created the Council on Environmental Quality). EPA proposed by Nixon 9 July 1970; began operating 2 December 1970; William Ruckelshaus first administrator. One early EPA action: phasing lead out of gasoline from 1971. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Environmental_Protection_Agency; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_Air_Act_(United_States)]
- Clean Air Act: first enacted 1963 (signed by Lyndon Johnson 17 December 1963); the 1970 amendments, usually what "the Clean Air Act" means, created National Ambient Air Quality Standards; 1990 amendments added an acid-rain emissions-trading programme. An EPA study (2011) estimated the 1990 amendments' benefits at about $2 trillion a year by 2020 against about $60 billion in costs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_Air_Act_(United_States)]
- Clean Water Act: the Federal Water Pollution Control Act of 1948, rewritten in the 1972 amendments; Nixon vetoed it on 17 October 1972 and Congress overrode the veto on 18 October 1972. The Cuyahoga River in Cleveland, which caught fire at least 14 times, burned on 22 June 1969, spurring national outcry. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_Water_Act; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuyahoga_River]
- Ozone: Rowland and Molina (1974) warned that CFCs could destroy stratospheric ozone; British Antarctic Survey scientists Farman, Gardiner and Shanklin reported the Antarctic ozone hole in *Nature* in May 1985. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_depletion]
- Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer: agreed 16 September 1987, in force 1 January 1989; ratified by 198 parties, the first universally ratified treaty in UN history; Article 5 gave developing countries extra time. Kofi Annan called it "perhaps the single most successful international agreement to date". The ozone layer is projected to return to 1980 levels around 2040 for most of the world and 2066 over Antarctica. The 2016 Kigali Amendment extended it to HFCs (greenhouse gases). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal_Protocol]
- UN Conference on the Human Environment, Stockholm, 5–16 June 1972, created UNEP, headquartered in Nairobi, the first UN agency based in a developing country; Indira Gandhi's speech linked environmental management to poverty alleviation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Conference_on_the_Human_Environment]

## Environmentalism: Silent Spring to Earth Day
- Rachel Carson (27 May 1907 – 14 April 1964), marine biologist; *The Sea Around Us* (1951) won the National Book Award. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Carson]
- *Silent Spring* was serialised in *The New Yorker* from 16 June 1962 and published by Houghton Mifflin on 27 September 1962. Impetus: a January 1958 letter by Olga Owens Huckins about birds killed by DDT spraying at Duxbury, Massachusetts; also the USDA's 1957 fire-ant spraying. Title from Keats's "La Belle Dame sans Merci" ("And no birds sing"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_Spring]
- Chemical companies, including DuPont and Velsicol (which threatened legal action), attacked the book; American Cyanamid's Robert White-Stevens said following Carson would "return to the Dark Ages". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_Spring]
- Carson never called for an outright DDT ban; she warned that overuse would breed resistance. The US banned DDT for agricultural use in 1972; it was never banned for anti-malarial use. Claims that Carson caused millions of malaria deaths have been widely rebutted. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_Spring]
- Santa Barbara oil spill, 28 January 1969: over 3 million US gallons spilled, killing more than 10,000 seabirds, dolphins, seals and sea lions. Roderick Nash wrote the Santa Barbara "Declaration of Environmental Rights" (read 28 January 1970). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_Day]
- First Earth Day, 22 April 1970, initiated by Senator Gaylord Nelson (Wisconsin), national coordinator Denis Hayes; an estimated 20 million Americans took part. In 1990 Hayes took it international: 200 million people in 141 countries. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_Day]

## Global perspectives
### India
- The Chipko ("hugging") movement in the Uttarakhand Himalaya opposed commercial logging. Chandi Prasad Bhatt founded Dasholi Gram Swarajya Sangh at Gopeshwar in 1964. First direct action at Mandal, 24 April 1973 (villagers drove off loggers). At Reni on 25 March 1974, with the men away, Gaura Devi, head of the village women's council (*Mahila Mangal Dal*), led 27 women who hugged trees and kept an all-night vigil until loggers left. In 1980 Prime Minister Indira Gandhi imposed a 15-year ban on felling in the Himalayan forests. Leaders included Bhatt (Magsaysay Award 1982) and Sunderlal Bahuguna. Women's leading role makes Chipko a reference point for ecofeminism, though how far this reflected Chipko ideology is debated. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chipko_movement]
- Ramachandra Guha (born 29 April 1958, Dehradun; PhD IIM Calcutta on the social history of forestry in Uttarakhand). *The Unquiet Woods: Ecological Change and Peasant Resistance in the Himalaya* (1989) treats Chipko as part of a long tradition of peasant resistance to state forestry. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramachandra_Guha]
- Madhav Gadgil and Ramachandra Guha, *This Fissured Land: An Ecological History of India* (Oxford University Press, Delhi, 1992; UC Press 1993; revised 2013). [source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bulletin-of-the-school-of-oriental-and-african-studies/article/gadgilmadhav-and-guharamachandra-this-fissuted-land-an-ecological-history-of-india-xiv-274-pp-delhi-etc-oxford-university-press-1992-1295/84EC9C70FC47B3FFDCC9D60A3C5F43A3]
- Guha, "Radical American Environmentalism and Wilderness Preservation: A Third World Critique", *Environmental Ethics* 11:1 (Spring 1989), pp. 71–83: deep ecology is rooted in American history and inappropriate for the Third World; setting aside wilderness (e.g. tiger reserves) has moved resources from the poor to the rich. Reprinted in Guha and Joan Martínez-Alier, *Varieties of Environmentalism* (1997), which popularised "environmentalism of the poor". [source: https://users.manchester.edu/Facstaff/SSNaragon/Online/texts/425/Guha%20(1989),%20Radical%20American%20Environmentalism.pdf; https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/mono/10.4324/9781315070766-6/chapter-5-radical-american-environmentalism-wilderness-preservation-third-world-critique-ramachandra-guha-martinez-alier]
- Bhopal, 3 December 1984: methyl isocyanate leaked from the Union Carbide India Limited pesticide plant; over 500,000 people exposed; at least 3,787 deaths officially, over 16,000 claimed; widely considered the world's worst industrial disaster. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhopal_disaster]

### China and East Asia
- Mark Elvin (18 August 1938 – 6 December 2023), *The Retreat of the Elephants: An Environmental History of China* (Yale University Press, 2004): covers about 3,000 years of written record, tracing the southward retreat of wild elephants as forests were cleared and land farmed, water control, and case studies of Jiaxing, Guizhou and Zunhua. Reviewers (e.g. Vaclav Smil) note it is more a set of essays than a chronological history. Elvin is also known for the "high-level equilibrium trap" (*The Pattern of the Chinese Past*, 1973). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Elvin; https://www.americanscientist.org/node/1645; https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/84506]
- Ling Zhang (Boston College), *The River, the Plain, and the State: An Environmental Drama in Northern Song China, 1048–1128* (Cambridge, 2016): the Yellow River's breach of 1048 turned the Hebei plain into a shifting delta for 80 years; against Karl Wittfogel's "hydraulic mode of production" she proposes a "hydraulic mode of consumption" in which the river, plain and state drained the region's resources. [source: https://cambridge.org/core/books/river-the-plain-and-the-state/ED09F46598B7065EAAD46BAE8A3A4C2C; https://china.usc.edu/node/21762]
- Minamata disease, discovered in 1956 in Minamata, Japan, was caused by methylmercury in wastewater from the Chisso Corporation's factory, discharged 1932–1968; by March 2001, 2,265 people had been officially recognised as victims. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minamata_disease]

### Africa
- James Fairhead and Melissa Leach, *Misreading the African Landscape: Society and Ecology in a Forest-Savanna Mosaic* (Cambridge, African Studies Series 90, 1996): in Kissidougou prefecture, Guinea, the round "forest islands" around villages, treated by officials since French occupation in 1893 as relics of a vanished forest, were in fact created and enlarged by villagers (Kissi and Kuranko); population growth there meant more forest, not less; a century of policy rested on misreading. A classic critique of declensionist assumptions. [source: https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/handle/10919/66166; https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/misreading-the-african-landscape/towards-a-new-forestsavanna-ecology-and-history/E4C4991DB5E1233E7A2A8ED2A5F96FB6]
- Wangari Maathai founded the Green Belt Movement in Kenya in 1977 (with the National Council of Women of Kenya); more than 51 million trees planted; Maathai received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Belt_Movement]
- Further Africa literature (from WP bibliography): James McCann, *Green Land, Brown Land, Black Land: An Environmental History of Africa, 1800–1990* (1999); Nancy Jacobs, *Environment, Power and Injustice: A South African History* (2003); Jonathan Adams and Thomas McShane, *The Myth of Wild Africa* (1996); William Beinart and Lotte Hughes on empire and environment. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history]

## Environmental justice and slow violence
- Warren County, North Carolina, 1982: the state sited a PCB landfill in a mainly African American county; protests over six weeks led to over 550 arrests, often taken as the launch of the US environmental justice movement. The United Church of Christ's Commission for Racial Justice then studied the siting of hazardous waste by race (*Toxic Wastes and Race*, 1987). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_County_PCB_Landfill; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_justice]
- First National People of Color Environmental Leadership Summit, Washington DC, 1991 (about 1,100 people, including delegates from Latin America and Africa); Executive Order 12898 on environmental justice signed by Bill Clinton in 1994. Robert D. Bullard is a leading scholar. Export of hazardous waste to the Global South surged in the 1980s–90s. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_justice]
- Rob Nixon (South African-born scholar) coined "slow violence" in *Slow Violence and the Environmentalism of the Poor* (Harvard University Press, 2011): "a violence that occurs gradually and out of sight, a violence of delayed destruction that is dispersed across time and space, an attritional violence that is typically not viewed as violence at all". The poor are its main casualties. It builds on Johan Galtung's "structural violence" (1969). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slow_violence; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rob_Nixon]

## Debates
- **Declension narratives and their critics.** Environmental histories tend to be "declensionist", stories of decline under human pressure, a form of the "lost golden age" narrative; critics also charge "presentism". Cronon's "A Place for Stories" (1992) showed how two Dust Bowl books on the same evidence reached opposite stories (Worster: capitalist culture caused disaster; Bonnifield: human endurance triumphed). Fairhead and Leach (1996) showed that an assumed story of deforestation in Guinea was the reverse of the truth; Degroot (2018) shows societies adapting and profiting. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history; https://williamcronon.net/courses/469/handouts/469-periodizing-landscape-change.html; https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/handle/10919/66166]
- **Should the field be activist?** Environmental history "frequently promoted a moral and political agenda although it steadily became a more scholarly enterprise"; strong advocacy can compromise objectivity. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history]
- **Environmental determinism.** Some environmental historians stress that history is "inevitably guided by forces that are not of human origin" (Hughes); critics warn against climate determinism. Use the Syria drought debate (Kelley et al. 2015 v. Selby et al. 2017; already in the Modern Syria chapter) and the regional, uneven Little Ice Age as cautions. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age]
- **Wilderness and the global South.** Cronon (1995) called pristine wilderness a fantasy; Guha (1989) argued wilderness preservation and deep ecology export an American ideal that displaces poor rural people; Yellowstone excluded Native peoples. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Cronon; https://users.manchester.edu/Facstaff/SSNaragon/Online/texts/425/Guha%20(1989),%20Radical%20American%20Environmentalism.pdf; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellowstone_National_Park]
- **Conservation v. preservation.** Pinchot's wise use for "the greatest good ... in the long run" against Muir's preservation of nature for its own sake; Hetch Hetchy (1913) as the test case. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Muir; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gifford_Pinchot]
- **Anthropocene: when, and whose?** Start dates range from early farming to 1610 to c. 1800 to 1945/1950s; formal epoch rejected in 2024. Capitalocene (Malm, Moore), Plantationocene and Yusoff argue the "anthropos" hides unequal responsibility; Chakrabarty argues climate forces historians to think of humans also as a species and geophysical force. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalocene]
- **Energy transitions or energy additions?** Smil's sequence of transitions (biomass, coal, oil) v. Fressoz's argument that new sources were added on top of old ones. Malm v. resource-scarcity explanations of the turn to coal; Pomeranz on coal and colonies as Britain's way out of ecological limits; the *Holznot* debate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transition; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holznot; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- **Megafaunal extinction.** Human overkill v. climate change v. both; the Americas' extinctions were nearly simultaneous (within about 3,000 years), Eurasia's staggered over tens of thousands. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Pleistocene_extinctions]
- **Crosby and biological explanations.** Crosby's biological account displaced racial-superiority explanations of European expansion, but it can minimise human agency, violence and colonial policy (a critique made of "virgin soil" arguments); the writer should present this as a debate, not cite a specific critic unverified here. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_Imperialism_(book)]
- **The DDT ban and malaria.** Anti-environmentalist groups blame Carson for malaria deaths; critics call the claim easily refuted because DDT was never banned for malaria control. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_Spring]
