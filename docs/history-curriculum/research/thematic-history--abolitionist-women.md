## Enslaved women's own resistance (frame the chapter with these)
- Nanny of the Maroons (c.1686 – c.1760), leader of Jamaica's Windward Maroons; most of what is known comes from Maroon oral tradition, which makes her an Asante; little textual evidence exists. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanny_of_the_Maroons]
- The British made peace with the Windward Maroons by treaty on 20 April 1740; a separate 1740 land patent granted "Nanny and the people now residing with her and their heirs ... a certain parcel of Land containing five hundred acres in the parish of Portland" (New Nanny Town, later Moore Town). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanny_of_the_Maroons]
- Caveat for the writer: under the treaties the Maroons agreed not to harbour new runaways and to help catch them for bounties, so Nanny was a fighter for her community's freedom, not an abolitionist in the 19th-century sense. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanny_of_the_Maroons]
- A 1733 entry in the Journal of the Assembly of Jamaica rewards a "Black Shot" for "having killed Nanny, the rebels old obeah woman" — yet a Nanny received the 1740 land grant; whether this is the same woman is unclear. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanny_of_the_Maroons]
- Jamaica declared Nanny a National Hero in 1975 (its only female national hero); she is on the J$500 note. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanny_of_the_Maroons]
- Elizabeth Freeman ("Mum Bett", c.1744 – 28 December 1829), enslaved in Sheffield, Massachusetts, by John Ashley; after hearing the 1780 Massachusetts Constitution ("All men are born free and equal...") she sought out lawyer Theodore Sedgwick. *Brom and Bett v. Ashley* was heard in August 1781 by the County Court of Common Pleas at Great Barrington; the jury found for her, awarding thirty shillings damages; Ashley dropped his appeal. The case was cited in the Quock Walker cases, which ended slavery in Massachusetts. (Note: Wikipedia's lead wrongly implies the Supreme Judicial Court decided her case; it was the county court.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Freeman]
- Freeman's words as reported by Catharine Sedgwick (a later, second-hand account): "I'm not a dumb critter; won't the law give me my freedom?" [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Freeman]
- Saint-Domingue/Haiti: Sanité (Suzanne) Bélair (c.1781 – 5 October 1802), a free-born woman (affranchie) of Verrettes, sergeant then lieutenant in Toussaint Louverture's forces during the fight against the Leclerc expedition; captured with her husband Charles Bélair and executed; on the 10-gourde note (2004); featured in a 2023 Panthéon (Paris) exhibition. The story that she refused a blindfold and won a soldier's death by firing squad rather than beheading is "said" (tradition). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanit%C3%A9_B%C3%A9lair]
- Other Haitian women: Marie-Jeanne Lamartinière fought at Crête-à-Pierrot (1802); Cécile Fatiman, a *mambo*, is remembered (tradition) for the ceremony before the 1791 rising; Philippe Girard, "Rebelles with a Cause: Women in the Haitian War of Independence, 1802–1804", *Gender & History* (2009). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_the_Haitian_Revolution]
- Mary Prince (born c.1788 in Bermuda, died after 1833): sold several times; in 1815 sold to John Adams Wood of Antigua; brought to London in 1828; left the Woods' household; Wood refused to free her, so she could not return to her husband in Antigua without re-enslavement. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Prince]
- *The History of Mary Prince* (1831) was dictated to Susanna Strickland (later Moodie) in Thomas Pringle's household (Pringle was secretary of the Anti-Slavery Society); the first account of the life of a Black enslaved woman published in Britain; three printings in its first year; attacked by James MacQueen in *Blackwood's*; two libel cases followed and she testified in both (1833). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Prince]
- Ellen Craft (1826–1891) escaped from Macon, Georgia, in December 1848 with her husband William by passing as a white planter with William as her servant; they fled to England after the Fugitive Slave Act and published *Running a Thousand Miles for Freedom* (1860). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellen_and_William_Craft]

## Britain: pioneers, boycotts, ladies' associations, petitions
- White British women took part in the movement from the 1780s: supporting societies, organising boycotts of slave-grown produce (especially sugar) and writing anti-slavery literature (Midgley 1992). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_Kingdom]
- 1791 sugar boycott: triggered by frustration at Parliament's delays; William Fox's *An Address to the People of Great Britain, on the Propriety of Refraining from the Use of West India Sugar and Rum* (1791) went through 26 editions in under a year, with over 200,000 copies (Free-produce article says over a quarter million) in Britain and the US. Women, who could not vote, could promote and join a boycott. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Fox_(pamphleteer) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free-produce_movement]
- Size of the boycott: Clarkson claimed some 300,000 (families, in one account; people, in another) by the end of 1791; another estimate gives 400,000 people at its height. Give "300,000–400,000". It faded as the French Revolution turned violent in 1792. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free-produce_movement ; https://www.quakersintheworld.org/quakers-in-action/153 ; https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/rest/bitstreams/174609/retrieve]
- 1820s: an 1820s sugar bowl advertised "East India Sugar not made by Slaves" (British Museum). Amelia Opie published *The Black Man's Lament, or, How to Make Sugar* (1826). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free-produce_movement ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amelia_Opie]
- Elizabeth Heyrick (née Coltman; 4 December 1769 – 18 October 1831), Leicester Quaker (joined Friends after being a Methodist), widowed 1797; wrote more than twenty books and pamphlets (also on animal cruelty — she bought a bull at Bonsall in 1809 to stop a bull-baiting — prisons, wages, war). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Heyrick]
- In 1824 she published *Immediate, Not Gradual Abolition; or, An Inquiry into the Shortest, Safest, and Most Effectual Means of Getting Rid of West Indian Slavery*, rejecting the gradualism of Wilberforce and others; circulated widely in Britain and the US; appealed to the public and consumers rather than government. Garrison later praised her in a Glasgow speech. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Heyrick]
- With Susanna Watts she canvassed Leicester in 1824 for a boycott of West Indian sugar; by June 1825 almost a quarter of Leicester's population had stopped using it. She also wrote *An Appeal to the Hearts and Consciences of British Women* (1828). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Heyrick]
- First women's anti-slavery society: the Birmingham Ladies' Society for the Relief of Negro Slaves, founded 8 April 1825 by Lucy Townsend with Mary Lloyd as joint secretaries; members included Heyrick, Sophia Sturge and Sarah Wedgwood; renamed Female Society for Birmingham c.1830; its archive was added to UNESCO's UK Memory of the World register in 2026. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Ladies_Society_for_the_Relief_of_Negro_Slaves ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy_Townsend]
- 73 female anti-slavery societies were created in Britain in 1825–33, about 20 under Birmingham's influence; Midgley: they "marked the change from abolition as an individual woman's commitment, to antislavery as a collective female endeavour". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_Kingdom]
- The Sheffield Female Anti-Slavery Society (1825; Mary Anne Rawson a founder) was the first anti-slavery society in Britain to campaign for immediate rather than gradual emancipation. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Anne_Rawson ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy_Townsend]
- 1830: the Birmingham women resolved to pay their annual £50 donation to the national Anti-Slavery Society only once it dropped "gradual" from its title; the national society soon dropped gradualism. Sources differ on dates (April or May 1830) and on who proposed it (Heyrick's influence; Sarah Wedgwood); describe it as a conditional donation, not a "threat". [source: https://spartacus-educational.com/Female_Society.htm ; https://spartacus-educational.com/REheyrick.htm]
- 1833 national women's petition: organised with the London Female Anti-Slavery Society (Anne Knight credited); reported at 298,785 women's signatures (other sources "nearly 300,000", one says 187,000); described (Midgley, via review) as the largest single anti-slavery petition ever presented to Parliament. Use "nearly 300,000". [source: https://spartacus-educational.com/REslaveryW.htm ; https://www.gloucestershire.gov.uk/media/0fllmkqs/notes_on_women_abolitionists_1_-48448.pdf ; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/179242]
- Earlier mass petitions (men and women): 1792, 400,000 signatures; 1814, 1,375,000. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_Kingdom]
- After 1834, a women's petition of about 700,000 signatures to Queen Victoria against apprenticeship is described as the largest in the movement's history (single source, Spartacus — hedge). [source: https://spartacus-educational.com/REslaveryW.htm]
- Anne Knight (1786–1862) attended the 1840 convention, produced what is thought the first leaflet for women's suffrage (1847) and formed the first UK women's suffrage organisation, in Sheffield (1851). Elizabeth Pease (Nichol) founded the Darlington Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Knight ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Pease_Nichol]
- "Am I not a woman and a sister?": the male Wedgwood motto ("Am I not a man and a brother?", 1787) was turned female by British abolitionists in the 1820s; in 1830 the US paper *Genius of Universal Emancipation* printed the female image; Elizabeth Margaret Chandler popularised it; it became the PFASS seal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain%27t_I_a_Woman%3F ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Female_Anti-Slavery_Society]

## United States: organisers and orators
- Maria W. Stewart (née Miller; 1803 – 17 December 1879), free Black woman born in Hartford, orphaned at five, indentured to a minister's family; widowed 1829 and denied her inheritance; influenced by David Walker's *Appeal* (1829). Garrison's *Liberator* printed her *Religion and the Pure Principles of Morality* (1831) and her four lectures of 1832–33. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_W._Stewart]
- Her first lecture was on 21 September 1832 at Franklin Hall, Boston; she spoke to mixed audiences of men and women, Black and white; her public career ended with a farewell address in Boston, 21 September 1833. She is "widely recognized" as the first American-born woman to speak publicly on abolition and women's rights (hedge: "among the first"; the Wikipedia article itself has contradictory dates for her last lecture — 27 February 1833 at the African Masonic Hall was her last *lecture*, 21 September 1833 her farewell). Later head matron at Freedmen's Hospital, Washington. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_W._Stewart]
- Lydia Maria Child (née Francis; 11 February 1802 – 20 October 1880), of Medford, Massachusetts; popular author (*Hobomok*; *The Frugal Housewife*, 1829; the *Juvenile Miscellany*, from 1826, the first US children's monthly). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lydia_Maria_Child]
- Her *An Appeal in Favor of That Class of Americans Called Africans* (1833, published at her own expense) argued for immediate, uncompensated emancipation and against racial prejudice; T. W. Higginson called it "the first anti-slavery work ever printed in America in book form"; it cost her much of her readership (the *Miscellany* folded). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Appeal_in_Favor_of_that_Class_of_Americans_Called_Africans ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lydia_Maria_Child]
- Child helped fund the first anti-slavery fair (Boston, 1834); joined the AASS executive committee in 1839 and edited the *National Anti-Slavery Standard* 1840–43, becoming the first woman to edit a national political paper; in 1860 she edited Harriet Jacobs's *Incidents*. She also wrote "Over the River and Through the Wood" (1844). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lydia_Maria_Child]
- Philadelphia Female Anti-Slavery Society (PFASS): founded December 1833, days after the American Anti-Slavery Society's founding meeting in Philadelphia (which women could not join); founded by eighteen women including Lucretia Mott, Mary Ann M'Clintock, and the Black Forten women (Charlotte and daughters Margaretta, Sarah, Harriet); also Grace and Sarah Mapps Douglass and Hetty Reckless; racially integrated, rare at the time; dissolved March 1870 after the Fifteenth Amendment. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Female_Anti-Slavery_Society]
- PFASS work: petitions to Congress and the Pennsylvania legislature (1834–50: DC slavery, interstate trade, jury trials for fugitives), annual fairs, support for Sarah Mapps Douglass's school; Black members under Hetty Reckless aided fugitives with the Vigilance Association, which Mott thought outside the society's role — a race-inflected disagreement. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Female_Anti-Slavery_Society]
- Lucretia Mott (née Coffin; 3 January 1793 – 11 November 1880), born Nantucket; Quaker minister (recorded 1821); married James Mott 1811; her home was an Underground Railroad stop. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucretia_Mott]
- The Grimké sisters, Sarah (26 November 1792 – 23 December 1873) and Angelina (20 February 1805 – 26 October 1879), daughters of a slaveholding Charleston judge, moved north and became Quakers; considered the only notable white Southern women abolitionists. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angelina_Grimk%C3%A9 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Moore_Grimk%C3%A9]
- Angelina's *An Appeal to the Christian Women of the South* (1836) — the only such appeal by a Southern woman to Southern women; women had four duties: to read, pray, speak and act. In autumn 1836 the sisters were the only women at the AASS agents' training. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angelina_Grimk%C3%A9]
- 1837: their New England tour to mixed ("promiscuous") audiences provoked the Massachusetts Congregational ministers' *Pastoral Letter*; Angelina answered Catharine Beecher (*Letters to Catharine Beecher*, 1838); Sarah wrote *Letters on the Equality of the Sexes and the Condition of Woman* (1838, addressed to Mary S. Parker of the Boston FASS). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angelina_Grimk%C3%A9 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Moore_Grimk%C3%A9]
- February 1838: Angelina addressed a committee of the Massachusetts legislature, the first woman in the US to address a legislative body, defending women's right to petition. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angelina_Grimk%C3%A9]
- 1839: the sisters and Theodore Weld published *American Slavery As It Is*, which Stowe drew on for *Uncle Tom's Cabin*. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angelina_Grimk%C3%A9 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncle_Tom%27s_Cabin]
- Anti-Slavery Convention of American Women, New York, 9–12 May 1837: about 175 women from ten states and twenty societies (71 delegates); Mary S. Parker president, Mott, Child, Sarah Grimké, Grace Douglass among vice-presidents; only five Black women attended; women's antislavery petitions more than doubled in 1837. Ann D. Gordon calls it the first convention at which women discussed women's rights. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Slavery_Convention_of_American_Women]
- Second convention, Pennsylvania Hall, Philadelphia, from 15 May 1838 (203 delegates). Angelina married Weld there on 14 May; on the evening of 16 May she spoke for an hour while a mob broke windows; the women left arm in arm, Black and white; on 17 May a mob (some 10,000 reported) burned the hall, three days after it opened; the mayor had refused protection and a grand jury exonerated the rioters. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania_Hall_(Philadelphia) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Slavery_Convention_of_American_Women]
- The "woman question" split the American movement in 1839–40: when Abby Kelley was elected to the AASS business committee (1840), conservatives left; the Tappans' American and Foreign Anti-Slavery Society did not admit women. Kelley was in 1843 the first woman in America to speak at a national political convention (Liberty Party, Buffalo). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abby_Kelley_Foster ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_States]
- US petitions: under the House "gag rule" (1836–1844; Pinckney Resolutions passed 26 May 1836) more than 1,000 petitions with 130,000 signatures were tabled unread; women's signatures were a large share (Susan Zaeske, *Signatures of Citizenship*, studies women's petitions 1831–65: deferential in 1835, openly claiming a political right by the 1840s). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gag_rule_(United_States) ; https://uncpress.org/book/9780807854266/signatures-of-citizenship]
- Women's Loyal National League, founded 14 May 1863 by Elizabeth Cady Stanton (president) and Susan B. Anthony (secretary): nearly 400,000 signatures for a constitutional amendment abolishing slavery — the largest US petition to that date (about one in 24 Northern adults); Charles Sumner had two Black men carry the first 100,000 names into the Senate. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_Loyal_National_League]
- Other figures for cross-reference: Mary Ann Shadd (1823–1893) founded the *Provincial Freeman* in Ontario in 1853, the first Black woman publisher in North America; Sarah Parker Remond (1826–1894) lectured in Britain from 1858 and later became a physician in Florence. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Ann_Shadd ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Parker_Remond]

## Black abolitionist women: Truth, Tubman, Harper, Jacobs
- Sojourner Truth (born Isabella Bomefree, c.1797 – 26 November 1883), enslaved in Ulster County, New York; Dutch was her first language; walked to freedom with her infant daughter Sophia late in 1826 ("I did not run off ... I walked off"); New York's emancipation completed 4 July 1827; took the name Sojourner Truth in 1843. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sojourner_Truth]
- The Akron speech: delivered 29 May 1851 at the Woman's Rights Convention, Akron, Ohio; brief reports in the *New York Tribune* (6 June) and *Liberator*; full transcript by Marius Robinson, the convention's recording secretary, in the *Anti-Slavery Bugle*, 21 June 1851, which he went over with Truth. The question "Ain't I a Woman?" does not appear in it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain%27t_I_a_Woman%3F]
- Frances Dana Gage published a very different version in May 1863, in Southern dialect, adding the refrain, the claim that she could "bear de lash", and that she had borne thirteen children most sold away (Truth had about five, one sold); Gage's version was reprinted 1875, 1881, 1889 and became standard until Nell Irvin Painter's 1996 biography. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain%27t_I_a_Woman%3F]
- Gage also portrayed a hostile audience; other eyewitness reports describe Truth warmly received. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain%27t_I_a_Woman%3F]
- Harriet Tubman (born Araminta Ross, c. March 1822 – 10 March 1913), Dorchester County, Maryland; severe adolescent head injury; escaped with her brothers Ben and Henry on 17 September 1849; in 1849 she reached Philadelphia. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriet_Tubman]
- From 1851 to 1862 she made about 13 expeditions rescuing some 70 people, and gave instructions to 50–60 more who escaped; called "Moses"; never captured; the "$40,000 reward" is a legend (manufactured figure). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriet_Tubman]
- Combahee Ferry raid, 1–2 June 1863, with Col. James Montgomery and about 150 Black soldiers of the 2nd South Carolina: freed more than 700 (Tubman article) / more than 750 (raid article). She is widely credited as the first woman to lead an armed military operation in the US. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriet_Tubman ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raid_on_Combahee_Ferry]
- Frances Seward sold Tubman a farm in Auburn/Fleming, New York, in 1859; Tubman was later active for women's suffrage. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriet_Tubman]
- Frances Ellen Watkins Harper (24 September 1825 – 22 February 1911), born free in Baltimore, orphaned at three; first book *Forest Leaves* (1845); *Poems on Miscellaneous Subjects* (1854) a commercial success; lived with William Still's family (1851); joined the AASS as a lecturer in 1853, first anti-slavery speech 1854; refused segregated seating on a Philadelphia streetcar in 1858; "Bury Me in a Free Land" (1858, *Anti-Slavery Bugle*); "The Two Offers" (1859), first short story published by a Black woman; *Iola Leroy* (1892); co-founded the National Association of Colored Women (1896). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Ellen_Watkins_Harper]
- Harriet Jacobs (1813 [or 1815] – 7 March 1897), born enslaved in Edenton, North Carolina; harassed by Dr James Norcom ("Dr Flint"); from 1835 hid for about seven years in a crawlspace under her grandmother's roof; escaped by boat to Philadelphia in 1842. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriet_Jacobs]
- She first asked Stowe to write her story and was rebuffed; wrote it herself 1853–58; *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl, Written by Herself* (1861, Thayer & Eldridge, edited by Lydia Maria Child), pseudonym "Linda Brent"; long dismissed as a novel by Child (e.g. Blassingame) until Jean Fagan Yellin's documentary research in the 1980s established Jacobs's authorship and its autobiographical truth. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriet_Jacobs ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidents_in_the_Life_of_a_Slave_Girl]

## Harriet Beecher Stowe and *Uncle Tom's Cabin*
- Stowe (14 June 1811 – 1 July 1896), born Litchfield, Connecticut, daughter of Lyman Beecher; lived in Cincinnati from 1832 across the river from slave Kentucky. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriet_Beecher_Stowe]
- Written in response to the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850; serialised in *The National Era* from 5 June 1851 to 1 April 1852 (40 weeks); book published 20 March 1852 by John P. Jewett (first run 5,000; 3,000 sold the first day); 300,000 copies sold in the US in the first year; London edition May 1852 sold 200,000, with over 1.5 million copies (mostly pirated) circulating in Britain within a few years; translated into 20 languages by 1857. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncle_Tom%27s_Cabin ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriet_Beecher_Stowe]
- The best-selling novel of the 19th century in the US, second only to the Bible among books. Sources include Josiah Henson's narrative (1849) and *American Slavery As It Is*; *A Key to Uncle Tom's Cabin* (1853) answered critics (though she read many cited works only afterwards). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncle_Tom%27s_Cabin]
- Stowe met Lincoln on 25 November 1862; the "little woman who wrote the book that started this great war" line is reported only later by her son and is likely apocryphal. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriet_Beecher_Stowe ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncle_Tom%27s_Cabin]
- Modern criticism: its racial stereotypes and passive Tom shaped American images of Black people. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncle_Tom%27s_Cabin]

## From exclusion to women's rights: 1840 to Seneca Falls and the split
- World's Anti-Slavery Convention, Exeter Hall, London, opened 12 June 1840, organised by the British and Foreign Anti-Slavery Society (founded 1839); a second circular (February 1840) limited it to "gentlemen". Official delegate list: 493 names. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Anti-Slavery_Convention]
- The Garrisonian AASS sent Mott and Charles Lenox Remond; the Massachusetts and Pennsylvania societies sent women (Abigail Kimber, Elizabeth Neall, Mary Grew, Sarah Pugh). After a day of debate on Wendell Phillips's motion, women were seated in the gallery, unable to speak or vote; Garrison, Remond, N. P. Rogers and William Adams sat with them. Mott was one of six (some sources: seven) women delegates. Stanton attended as a delegate's wife on honeymoon. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Anti-Slavery_Convention ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucretia_Mott ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_Falls_Convention]
- In Haydon's painting of the convention, the women (Pease, Rawson, Knight, Opie, Lady Byron, Mary Clarkson and others) are at the far right; Haydon disliked Mott's views and gave her no prominent place. Lucy Townsend was left out. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Anne_Rawson ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucretia_Mott ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy_Townsend]
- Seneca Falls: planned over tea at Jane Hunt's house, Waterloo, 9 July 1848 (Mott, Martha Coffin Wright, Mary Ann M'Clintock, Stanton, Hunt — Stanton the only non-Quaker); held 19–20 July 1848 at the Wesleyan Chapel; about 300 attended; the Declaration of Sentiments (modelled on the Declaration of Independence: "all men and women are created equal") was signed by 100 — 68 women and 32 men. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_Falls_Convention ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_Sentiments]
- The suffrage resolution, added by Stanton, was the most contested — Mott urged its removal; Frederick Douglass, the only African American present, argued for it and it passed. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_Falls_Convention]
- Caveat: Stanton promoted Seneca Falls as the movement's beginning (in *History of Woman Suffrage*); the 1837 women's anti-slavery convention already discussed women's rights. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_Falls_Convention ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Slavery_Convention_of_American_Women]
- American Equal Rights Association founded 1866 (Eleventh National Women's Rights Convention, New York, 10 May 1866) for suffrage "irrespective of race, color or sex"; split over the Fifteenth Amendment (passed Congress February 1869, ratified 1870), which barred racial but not sex discrimination in voting. Harper, Douglass and Abby Kelley supported the amendment; Stanton and Anthony opposed it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Equal_Rights_Association ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abby_Kelley_Foster]
- Stanton and Anthony allied in Kansas (1867) with the racist Democrat George Francis Train and wrote of "Patrick and Sambo and Hans and Yung Tung"; the 1869 AERA meeting ended the association; Stanton and Anthony founded the NWSA two days later; Lucy Stone, Julia Ward Howe and others the AWSA in November 1869 (Harper helped found the AWSA); they merged as NAWSA in 1890. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Equal_Rights_Association ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Ellen_Watkins_Harper]

## Beyond the Anglo-American world
- France: Olympe de Gouges (1748–1793), one of France's earliest public opponents of slavery: *Réflexions sur les hommes nègres* (1788); her play *Zamore et Mirza* staged at the Comédie-Française in December 1789 as *L'Esclavage des Noirs* (published with a preface 1792). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympe_de_Gouges]
- Brazil: Maria Firmina dos Reis (São Luís, Maranhão; born 11 March 1822 — Wikipedia's infobox also implies 1825, baptised 1825 — died 11 November 1917), daughter of an enslaved woman's family, schoolteacher 1847–1881; *Úrsula* (1859), considered the first Brazilian abolitionist novel and among the first novels by a Brazilian woman; she is considered Brazil's first Black woman novelist; "A escrava" (1887); founded a free mixed school. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Firmina_dos_Reis]
- Brazil: after Ceará's abolition (25 March 1884) abolitionist societies multiplied; Ave Libertas, an all-women society in Recife (Pernambuco), founded 20 April 1884 (formally inaugurated 8 September 1884) by the seamstress-designer Leonor Porto (who freed the people she still held); it bought manumissions, freeing 200 captives in its first year, through bazaars, theatre benefits and press work; it had about 60 members by 1887 (secondary summary). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ave_Libertas_Society ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_Brazil]
- Celso Castilho's *Slave Emancipation and Transformations in Brazilian Political Citizenship* (University of Pittsburgh Press, 2016) is among the first books to take seriously the role of white and Black women in Brazilian abolitionism (Recife); with Camillia Cowling he showed that women, enslaved and free, were the main beneficiaries of 1880s emancipation funds. [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/736695/pdf]
- Brazil's Golden Law (13 May 1888) was signed by Princess Isabel as regent; Brazil's Black movement contests the image of abolition as her gift (cross-refer to the Abolition chapter). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_Brazil]
- Cuba: Ana Betancourt (born 14 December 1832, Camagüey) at Guáimaro in 1869 linked the freeing of the enslaved to women's emancipation (already in the published Latin America chapter). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ana_Betancourt]

## Debates
- Marginal helpers or the movement's base? Older accounts treated women's societies as auxiliaries "of marginal interest"; Midgley (1992) for Britain and Jeffrey (1998) for the US argue women's money, petitions, fairs and boycotts were its foundation; Midgley also notes women's self-imposed limits (they denied challenging "separate spheres"). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy_Townsend ; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/179242 ; https://www.uncpress.org/book/9780807847411/the-great-silent-army-of-abolitionism]
- Who drove immediatism? In Britain, women (Heyrick 1824; Sheffield 1825; Birmingham's 1830 £50 condition) pushed the national society off gradualism before most male leaders; David Brion Davis's classic 1962 essay traced immediatism's emergence more broadly. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Heyrick ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Fox_(pamphleteer)]
- Abolition as the cradle of feminism: Ira V. Brown called the PFASS a "cradle of feminism"; newer work (Shirley Yee, Janice Sumler-Lewis, Julie Winch) stresses free Black women's own community activism and their more militant agenda (aid to fugitives) inside mixed societies. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Female_Anti-Slavery_Society]
- The Seneca Falls origin story is Stanton's; Ann D. Gordon and others point to the 1837 convention and the longer abolitionist roots. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Slavery_Convention_of_American_Women ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_Falls_Convention]
- Race inside the alliance: Stanton's and Anthony's racist rhetoric in 1867–69 versus Harper, Truth and Douglass; Martha S. Jones (*Vanguard*, 2020) centres Black women's insistence on rights for all. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Equal_Rights_Association ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain%27t_I_a_Woman%3F]
- Voice and authorship: Black women's words were often mediated by white editors — Prince (Strickland/Pringle), Truth (Robinson and Gage), Jacobs (Child; dismissed as fiction until Yellin). Painter (1996) shows how Gage made the "Ain't I a Woman?" symbol. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Prince ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain%27t_I_a_Woman%3F ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidents_in_the_Life_of_a_Slave_Girl]
- Counterpoint — women as enslavers: Jones-Rogers (2019) shows Southern white women owned, bought, sold and brutally managed enslaved people (about 40% of 18th-century South Carolina bills of sale had a female buyer or seller), treating slaveholding as a route to wealth and independence; her findings support Thavolia Glymph's claim that enslaved people faced much violence from mistresses. This should temper any story of women as naturally anti-slavery; the Grimkés themselves came from a slaveholding household, and Leonor Porto in Recife freed people she owned. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/They_Were_Her_Property ; https://mississippitoday.org/2019/05/11/they-were-her-property-book-reveals-how-white-women-took-part-in-and-profited-from-slaverys-brutality/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ave_Libertas_Society]
- Uncle Tom's Cabin: "incalculable" influence (Tompkins) versus a vehicle of lasting racial stereotypes; its role in "causing" the war is overstated (Lincoln quotation apocryphal). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncle_Tom%27s_Cabin]
- Brazil: Castilho (2016) recovers women's central part in 1880s abolitionism, against a historiography led by Nabuco, Patrocínio and Rebouças. [source: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/736695/pdf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_Brazil]
