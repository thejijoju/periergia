## 1. Background and the fall of the shogunate (brief; Tokugawa chapter has the detail)
- Samurai families were about 5–6% of roughly 30 million people in the 19th century (1.5–1.8 million); roughly 1 in 50 samurai families was "upper samurai", the rest split between middle and lower ranks. [source: MEI]
- At the time it was called the *Goisshin* ("Honourable Restoration"); *Meiji Ishin* is sometimes translated "Meiji Reformation". [source: MEI]
- The bakufu ran an annual gold deficit by 1800, over half a million *ryō* by 1837. Satō Nobuhiro claimed that by 1827 at least 30% of farmers had lost land to wealthier neighbours. [source: MEI]
- News of China's defeat in the First Opium War (Treaty of Nanking 1842) spread among the daimyō and was taken as a severe military and cultural disaster. [source: MEI]
- Ii Naosuke's Ansei Purge punished over a hundred political enemies, eight killed; Ii was assassinated in 1860 (Sakuradamon Incident, 24 March 1860). Yoshida Shōin, a *shishi* ("man of spirit") teacher, was caught in the purge. [source: MEI; OKU]
- 1863: the court issued an order to "expel the barbarians"; Chōshū fired on foreign ships (Shimonoseki campaign); Britain bombarded Kagoshima (Satsuma) in August 1863 after the killing of a British merchant who crossed Shimazu Hisamitsu's procession. Both domains' failure to repel the West shifted them from *jōi* (expulsion) to *fukoku kyōhei* ("rich nation, strong army"). [source: MEI]
- The phrase *fukoku kyōhei* had been used by the Mito scholar Fujita Yūkoku. [source: MEI]
- Chōshū militias (*shotai*) such as Takasugi Shinsaku's *Kiheitai* mixed samurai, townsmen and peasants; the Kiheitai began 300 strong. [source: MEI; BOS]
- France (minister Léon Roches) backed the Tokugawa; Britain (Harry Parkes) increasingly favoured Satsuma and Chōshū. [source: MEI]
- The Satsuma–Chōshū (Satchō) alliance was formalised on 7 March 1866, brokered by Sakamoto Ryōma of Tosa, between Kido Takayoshi and Saigō Takamori. Sakamoto was assassinated in Kyoto in late 1867. [source: MEI]
- Emperor Kōmei died in early 1867 (MEI says February 1867); Mutsuhito, born 3 November 1852, became emperor on 30 January 1867 per EMP's lead (EMP's narrative gives a formal ascension ceremony on 13 February 1867). He was 14. [source: MEI; EMP] Caveat: the dates differ between article sections; "early 1867, aged 14" is safe.
- On 8 November 1867 the last shogun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu (15th shogun), returned administrative power to the court; he resigned the title of shogun on 19 November. [source: MEI]
- In the early hours of 3 January 1868 Satsuma and allied troops seized the Imperial Palace and a decree restored imperial rule (*ōsei fukko*); Iwakura Tomomi, Saigō and Ōkubo organised the coup. [source: MEI]
- The 3 January 1868 proclamation to foreign rulers: "We shall henceforward exercise supreme authority in all the internal and external affairs of the country." [source: MEI]

## 2. The Boshin War (1868–69)
- Dates: 27 January 1868 – 27 June 1869. Infobox totals: about 120,000 imperial vs 150,000 shogunate troops; total about 8,200 killed and 5,000+ wounded. The text elsewhere says about 120,000 troops mobilised altogether with roughly 3,500 known casualties in open fighting "but much more during terrorist attacks". Figures conflict; say "several thousand killed". [source: BOS]
- It has been romanticised as a "bloodless revolution"; BOS stresses it was violent. [source: BOS]
- Battle of Toba–Fushimi, 27 January 1868, near Kyoto: about 15,000 shogunate troops (some French-trained, including the Shinsengumi) were beaten by Satsuma–Chōshū forces outnumbered about 3:1 but armed with Armstrong guns, Minié rifles and Gatling guns. Yoshinobu fled to Edo by sea (aboard the *Kaiyō Maru*). [source: BOS; MEI]
- The shogunate had a French military mission (arrived January 1867); Napoleon III gave Yoshinobu 2,000 Chassepot rifles; the shogunate navy (eight steam warships) was the strongest in Asia; Yokosuka arsenal built 1865 by French engineer Léonce Verny. [source: BOS]
- Edo surrendered without a battle after negotiations between Saigō Takamori and Katsu Kaishū (April–May 1868); diehards (Shōgitai) were defeated at the Battle of Ueno, 4 July 1868. [source: BOS; MEI]
- Northern Alliance (*Ōuetsu Reppan Dōmei*) of northern domains around Aizu and Sendai formed May 1868. [source: BOS]
- Battle of Aizu: siege of Tsuruga (Aizuwakamatsu) Castle from 6 October 1868; Aizu surrendered 6 November 1868. Aizu's samurai population was sent to camps in the far north and the domain ceased to exist. [source: AIZU]
- Byakkotai (White Tiger Unit): about 305 Aizu boys aged 16–17. Twenty of them, cut off, saw smoke over the castle town, wrongly believed the castle had fallen and committed suicide on Iimori Hill; one, Iinuma Sadakichi, survived. In 1928 Mussolini donated a column from Pompeii for their graves. [source: BYA; AIZU]
- Enomoto Takeaki took eight warships and about 2,000 men north with French advisers including Jules Brunet; the Republic of Ezo was proclaimed in Hokkaido on 27 January 1869, with an election limited to samurai (Enomoto elected); it fell after the Battle of Hakodate (Goryōkaku), the republic ending 27 June 1869. [source: BOS; EZO]
- The imperial navy used the French-built ironclad *Kōtetsu* (ex-CSS *Stonewall*); at Miyako Bay (March 1869) a Gatling gun repelled samurai boarders. [source: BOS]
- Enomoto was briefly imprisoned, freed in 1872, and later became ambassador to Russia and a Meiji minister. Yoshinobu was pardoned in 1869 and retired to Shizuoka. [source: EZO; BOS; MEI]
- Edo was renamed Tokyo in 1868 and the emperor moved there in 1869. Caveat: the exact date given varies (MEI 3 September; EMP 19 September; BOS 26 October). The era name Meiji ("enlightened rule") was adopted in 1868, and from then on there was one era name per reign. [source: MEI; EMP; BOS]

## 3. Abolishing the domains and the samurai
- 1869 "return of the registers" (*hanseki hōkan*): led by Kido Takayoshi; Satsuma, Chōshū, Tosa and Hizen surrendered their domains to the emperor first; between 25 July and 2 August 1869 the daimyō of 260 other domains followed; only 14 had to be ordered. Daimyō became non-hereditary governors keeping 10% of tax revenue. [source: HAN; SEI]
- The *kazoku* peerage (1869) merged court nobles and daimyō; samurai were reclassified as *shizoku* (gentry) and *sotsu* (foot soldiers). [source: SEI; HAN]
- August 1871 (*haihan-chiken*): daimyō summoned in Tokyo were told, without consultation, that domains were abolished. HAN: 261 surviving domains became 3 urban prefectures (*fu*) and 302 prefectures (*ken*), cut to 3 + 72 the next year and 3 + 44 by 1888. MEI phrases it as "302 domains reorganised into 72 prefectures" (January 1872). [source: HAN; MEI]
- The state absorbed domain debts and converted domain paper money at face value. [source: HAN]
- Samurai stipends: optional commutation into government bonds 1874; compulsory 1876. HAN: ex-daimyō stipends became bonds worth five years' stipend at 5% interest, redeemed by annual lottery over 30 years. [source: MEI; HAN]
- Dampatsurei edict of 1871 made men of the samurai class cut the topknot. The Burakumin Emancipation Edict of 1871 abolished outcast status ("new commoners", *shin heimin*). [source: MEI; BLT]
- Conscription Ordinance, January 1873. Caveat: sources disagree on terms. MEI: four years from age 21 then three in reserve; BLT: from age 20, three years active, then reserves. Wealthy men could buy exemption. [source: MEI; BLT]
- Blood tax riots, spring 1873: the ordinance translated the French *impôt du sang* as "blood tax", and rumours spread that officials would literally take blood. Riots in at least 10 prefectures; targets included government offices, new schools, police stations and newly emancipated burakumin. More than 60,000 arrested (29,000+ in Okayama alone); 15 ringleaders executed. [source: BLT]
- Most ex-samurai did not rebel; many became officials, teachers, police and officers. [source: MEI]
- The Tokyo police were heavily ex-samurai; the militarised police (*Rasotsu*) grew from 3,000 (1871) to 18,000 (1877). [source: MEI; SAT]

## 4. The Iwakura Mission (1871–73)
- Proposed by the Dutch-American missionary Guido Verbeck in a "Brief Sketch" to Ōkuma Shigenobu (1869). [source: IWA]
- Led by Iwakura Tomomi as ambassador plenipotentiary, with vice-ambassadors Ōkubo Toshimichi, Kido Takayoshi, Itō Hirobumi (four vice-ambassadors in all; IWA names only these three). 48 officials and scholars plus 53 students and attendants; about 100 sailed. [source: IWA]
- Three aims: recognition of the restored imperial government; preliminary renegotiation of the unequal treaties; study of Western institutions. Treaty revision failed. [source: IWA]
- Sailed from Yokohama 23 December 1871 on the SS *America*; San Francisco 15 January 1872; Washington 29 February 1872; Liverpool 17 August 1872; audience with Queen Victoria at Windsor, 5 December 1872; visited Manchester, Glasgow, Newcastle (Armstrong's Elswick works), Sheffield, Saltaire and more; continental Europe in the first half of 1873; returned via Egypt, Aden, Ceylon, Singapore, Saigon, Hong Kong and Shanghai; back in Yokohama 13 September 1873. [source: IWA]
- Five girls stayed in the US to study, including Tsuda Umeko (IWA text says 6 years old; a caption says 9, probably Japanese age-reckoning), who in 1900 founded the school that became Tsuda University, and Yamakawa Sutematsu (later Ōyama Sutematsu). [source: IWA]
- Kume Kunitake was the official diarist; his 5-volume account was published in 1878. [source: IWA]
- Makino Nobuaki, a student member: domain abolition and the Iwakura Mission "must be cited as the most important events that built the foundation of our state after the Restoration." [source: HAN]

## 5. *Seikanron* (1873) and the Korea question
- 1869: Japan's letter to Korea used the character 皇 for the Japanese emperor, which Koreans reserved for the Chinese emperor; Korea refused to receive the envoy. [source: SEI; K76]
- While the Iwakura group was abroad, Saigō argued for confronting Korea and offered to go as envoy himself; orthodox accounts say he invited his own assassination as a *casus belli* (SEI notes revisionists dispute his motives). Returning mission members (Ōkubo, Iwakura) blocked it in October 1873. Saigō, Itagaki, Etō Shimpei, Gotō Shōjirō resigned. Revisionists argue the split was about when and how, not whether. [source: SEI; FPR]

## 6. The Satsuma Rebellion and Saigō Takamori
- Saigō Takamori: 23 January 1828 – 24 September 1877. Ōkubo Toshimichi: 1830 – 14 May 1878, assassinated by disaffected samurai. Kido Takayoshi (1833 – 26 May 1877) died of illness during the war. [source: SAI; OKU; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kido_Takayoshi]
- Earlier revolts: Saga Rebellion 1874 (Etō Shimpei), a Chōshū rebellion 1876, Shinpūren 1876. [source: BOS; SAT]
- Saigō founded private academies (*Shi-gakkō*) in Kagoshima in 1874, with 132 branches combining classics and weapons training; by late 1876 Satsuma had effectively seceded. [source: SAT]
- Seinan ("Southwestern") War, 29 January – 24 September 1877. Government: 51,800 army, 5,054 Imperial Guard, 18,000 police; rebels: about 12,000 at the start, swelling to about 20,000 at Kumamoto. Government killed and wounded about 15,000. [source: SAT]
- Siege of Kumamoto Castle (from 19–22 February 1877): Tani Tateki held with 3,800 soldiers and 600 police; relieved 12–14 April. Tabaruzaka: 4,000+ killed or wounded each side (flagged "citation needed"). [source: SAT]
- Final stand at Shiroyama, Kagoshima, 24 September 1877, with about 500 men (only 40 alive by 6 a.m.). Saigō was shot in the hip/abdomen. The popular story says he committed seppuku with Beppu Shinsuke as second; the autopsy showed no seppuku wounds, so he was likely beheaded by Beppu after being shot. Mark Ravina (2010 article "The Apocryphal Suicide of Saigō Takamori") examines this legend. [source: SAT; BOS bibliography]
- Ravina prefers "War of the Southwest" to "Satsuma Rebellion", seeing it as closer to a civil war. [source: SAT]
- Emperor Meiji pardoned Saigō posthumously on 22 February 1889; statue in Ueno Park, Tokyo. The 2003 film *The Last Samurai* merges Boshin and Satsuma events; its title coinciding with Ravina's 2004 biography was "purely coincidence". [source: SAT; RAV]
- Cost: the war drove the government off the gold standard into paper money and then near bankruptcy, leading to sale of state enterprises. See NOT VERIFIED for the yen figure. [source: SAT]

## 7. Land tax reform and peasant protest
- Land Tax Reform (*chiso kaisei*), announced 1873, completed by 1880: a uniform money tax of 3% of assessed land value, paid by the landowner, replacing rice taxes on the cultivator; land titles created private ownership. The state, not the farmer, was shielded from harvest fluctuation. [source: LTR]
- A land tax reform department (1875) imposed prefectural quotas and altered valuations, causing riots (Ise, Makabe revolts). In January 1877 the rate was lowered from 3% to 2.5%. [source: LTR; SAT]
- MEI: land tax took about 30% of harvests, double to seven times European rates by net agricultural output; Qing China's land tax was about 2%. Peasants bore the main cost of industrialisation. [source: MEI] (Treat as an estimate.)
- Matsukata Masayoshi (1835–1924), finance minister from 1881 (15 of 20 years 1881–1901), founded the Bank of Japan in 1882; his "Matsukata deflation" crashed rice and silk prices and many small landholders lost land. Tenancy reached over 40% in some areas. [source: MAT; FPR]
- 1884 saw roughly sixty riots; farmers' total debt was estimated at about 200 million yen. [source: CHI]
- Chichibu Incident: began 31 October 1884 (CHI's lead says "November 1884"), Saitama; a self-styled "Revolutionary Army" of 5,000 to over 10,000 seized the district office, declared "Year One of Freedom and Self-Government", sought debt moratoria and destroyed debt records; crushed in about ten days by police and army. Convictions: CHI says nearly 3,000 (300 felonies; seven death sentences, five hanged February 1885); FPR says over 4,000. Largest popular uprising of the Meiji period. Historian Irokawa Daikichi argued its leaders held the Liberal Party's revolutionary ideology. Jansen: a "Tokugawa-style uprising that was dealt with in non-Tokugawa ways." [source: CHI; FPR]
- Tenants paid over half their crop as rent; families sent daughters to textile mills or sold them into prostitution. [source: CHI]

## 8. Freedom and People's Rights Movement (*Jiyū Minken Undō*)
- Began with the Tosa Memorial, 17 January 1874, by Itagaki Taisuke, Gotō Shōjirō, Etō Shimpei and others, calling for an elected assembly; it said power lay "with the officials alone". [source: FPR]
- Itagaki: "Saigō fights the government with arms, but we will fight it with people's rights." [source: FPR]
- Societies: Risshisha (Tosa, April 1874, named after the translation of Samuel Smiles's *Self-Help*); Aikokusha (1875, revived 1878). By November 1880, commoners were 53% of delegates. [source: FPR]
- Repression: press and libel laws 1875; Regulations for Public Meetings, April 1880. Concessions: Genrōin and prefectural assemblies (1878). [source: FPR]
- Political crisis of 1881: Ōkuma's call for a British-style cabinet plus the Hokkaido Colonisation Commission asset scandal led to Ōkuma's ouster; Imperial Rescript of 12 October 1881 promised a national assembly in 1890. [source: FPR]
- Parties: Jiyūtō (Liberal Party, October 1881, Itagaki; inspired by Rousseau, whose *Social Contract* Nakae Chōmin translated; rural landlord base); Rikken Kaishintō (March 1882, Ōkuma; British-model; urban elites, Keiō, Mitsubishi). Jiyūtō dissolved 29 October 1884. [source: FPR]
- Itagaki was stabbed at Gifu on 6 April 1882; reported words: "Itagaki may die, but liberty will not!" [source: ITA]
- The Itsukaichi draft constitution (1881), found in a village storehouse, showed commoner constitutional thinking. [source: FPR]
- Women: Kishida Toshiko (1863–1901), merchant's daughter and former court tutor to Empress Haruko, lectured for the Jiyūtō from 1882; her "Daughters in Boxes" speech (12 October 1883) led to arrest and a fine for speaking without a permit. Fukuda (Kageyama) Hideko, inspired by Kishida in 1882, joined the radical 1885 Osaka Incident plot, was dubbed "Japan's Joan of Arc", and in 1907 founded *Sekai Fujin*, the first Japanese socialist women's newspaper. [source: KIS; FUK]
- Laws of 1890 and 1900 barred women from political meetings/organisations. "Good wife, wise mother" (*ryōsai kenbo*) coined by Nakamura Masanao, 16 March 1875. [source: GWWM]

## 9. The 1889 Constitution
- Itō Hirobumi (16 October 1841 – 26 October 1909) went to Europe in March 1882 for about a year and a half; studied with Rudolf von Gneist (Berlin) and Lorenz von Stein (Vienna, from August 1882). US model rejected as too liberal. [source: ITO; MC]
- Cabinet system 1885 (Itō first PM); Privy Council 1888. Drafters: Inoue Kowashi, Kaneko Kentarō, Itō Miyoji, with German advisers; drafted in secret. [source: MC]
- Promulgated 11 February 1889 (National Foundation Day); in force 29 November 1890, when the first Diet met; replaced 3 May 1947. 76 articles in 7 chapters. [source: MC]
- Art. 1: "The Empire of Japan shall be reigned over and governed by a line of Emperors unbroken for ages eternal." Art. 3: "The Emperor is sacred and inviolable." Art. 11: emperor commands army and navy (later read as military independence from cabinet). Art. 28: freedom of religion "within limits not prejudicial to peace and order". [source: MC]
- House of Peers + elected House of Representatives; vote for men paying at least 15 yen in tax, about 1.1% of the population. First election July 1890. Universal male suffrage 1925. [source: MC; ITO]
- First parliamentary government in Asia. Model for Ethiopia's 1931 constitution (Tekle Hawariat; Ethiopian reformers called "Japanizers"). [source: MC]
- Ōkuma lost his right leg to a nationalist's bomb in 1889 over treaty-revision terms. [source: OKM]

## 10. Industrialisation, zaibatsu, Tomioka and women workers
- Raw silk was the main export; in 1862 raw silk and cocoons were 86% of exports. Silk output (tons/yr): 1868–72: 1,026 (exports 646); 1909–14: 12,460 (exports 9,462). Japan overtook China in silk exports. [source: TOM; MEI]
- Coal: 0.6 Mt (1875) → 21.3 Mt (1913). Steamships: 26 (1873) → 1,514 (1913). Rail: 18 mi (1872) → about 7,100 mi by the end of the series. [source: MEI]
- First railway Tokyo–Yokohama 1872; 17 private railways nationalised 1906–07. [source: RAIL]
- Tomioka Silk Mill (Gunma): French engineer Paul Brunat; opened 4 November 1872 with 300 reels (France and Italy's largest had up to 150). Rumours that foreigners drank workers' blood (red wine) deterred recruits; manager Odaka Atsutada enlisted his own daughter. 404 workers by January 1873, mostly samurai daughters; 556 by April 1873. Initial regime: 8-hour days, Sundays off. Sold to Mitsui in 1893 for 121,460 yen; hours rose to 12 in summer. UNESCO World Heritage 2014. [source: TOM]
- Wada Ei (1857–1929), samurai daughter from Matsushiro, wrote the *Tomioka Diary*. [source: WADA]
- Female share of workforce: Tsurumi reports women 62% of private-factory workforce in 1900 and 71% a decade later (via secondary summary only — see NOT VERIFIED). Tsurumi's *Factory Girls* draws on workers' own songs. [source: https://press.princeton.edu/node/27528]
- State-built factories, mines and shipyards sold to well-connected entrepreneurs (1880s). [source: MEI; SAT]
- Zaibatsu: family holding company + bank + subsidiaries. "Big Four": Mitsui, Sumitomo (Edo roots), Mitsubishi, Yasuda (Meiji origins). Term common only after WWI. [source: ZAI]
- Mitsubishi: Iwasaki Yatarō (1835–1885) of Tosa; Tsukumo Trading 1870, Mitsubishi 1873; ships from the 1874 Taiwan expedition given to it; carried troops against Satsuma 1877; Takashima mine 1881, Nagasaki Shipyard 1884, Hashima 1890. [source: IWS; MBI]
- Mitsui: Echigoya roots (1673); Mitsui Bank 1 July 1876, Japan's first private bank. [source: MTS]
- Shibusawa Eiichi (1840–1931), "father of Japanese capitalism"; First National Bank 1873; on the 10,000 yen note from 3 July 2024. [source: SHI]
- Yahata (Yawata) Steel Works, est. 1896; first blast furnace 5 February 1901; by 1912 80% of pig iron. [source: YAH]
- Ashio copper mine pollution (1880s on): Japan's first major pollution disaster; Tanaka Shōzō (elected 1890) led protest; February 1907 miners' riot. [source: ASH; TAN]
- High Treason Incident: 24 sentenced to death 18 January 1911, 12 commuted; 11 executed 24 January, Kanno Sugako the next day. [source: HTI]

## 11. Revising the unequal treaties
- 1858 treaties: extraterritoriality and no tariff autonomy. [source: UNE]
- Rokumeikan (Josiah Conder) opened 28 November 1883 under Inoue Kaoru to impress Westerners. [source: ROK]
- Normanton (24 October 1886): British crew escaped, all Japanese passengers died; consular court acquitted the captain, later given three months. Fuelled demands for revision. [source: NOR]
- Anglo-Japanese Treaty of Commerce and Navigation, 16 July 1894 (Mutsu Munemitsu): ended extraterritoriality from 17 July 1899. [source: AJT; MUT]
- Tariff autonomy restored 1911 (Komura Jutarō). [source: KOM]

## 12. Japan's own empire — Taiwan, Korea
### Ganghwa 1876
- Un'yō incident 20 September 1875: 35 Korean soldiers killed. Treaty 26 February 1876 (Kuroda Kiyotaka / Shin Heon): Art. 1 "an independent state enjoying the same sovereign rights as does Japan" (undercutting Qing suzerainty); ports Busan, Incheon, Wonsan; Art. 10 extraterritoriality. Korea's first unequal treaty; consciously modelled on Perry. [source: K76]
### 1874 Taiwan expedition and Ryukyu
- Mudan incident December 1871: 54 Ryukyuan sailors killed by Paiwan; Japan used it to invade southern Taiwan (May 1874, about 3,600 troops under Saigō Jūdō) and later annex Ryukyu (1879). [source: MUD; TWN]
### Korea 1882–1910
- Queen Min (Empress Myeongseong, 1851–1895) killed 8 October 1895 (Eulmi Incident) in Gyeongbokgung by over fifty Japanese agents organised by Minister Miura Gorō. [source: MYE; RJW]
- Gojong fled to the Russian legation 11 February 1896; proclaimed the Korean Empire 13 October 1897. [source: GOJ]
- Eulsa Treaty, 17–18 November 1905, under Itō with troops around the palace; five ministers ("Five Eulsa Traitors") approved; Gojong refused consent. Min Yeong-hwan's suicide 30 November; Chang Chi-yŏn's editorial "I Wail Bitterly Today" (20 November 1905): "integrity of a nation which has stood for 4,000 years... rights and freedom of twenty million people." Itō first Resident-General 1906. [source: K05]
- Hague emissary affair 1907; Gojong forced to abdicate 1907; Korean army disbanded 1 August 1907. [source: GOJ; ITO; RIG]
- Righteous armies 1907–1910: Japanese official statistics give 2,851 clashes, about 16,700–17,000 Korean dead. [source: RIG] (Article quality uneven.)
- An Jung-geun (1879–1910) shot Itō at Harbin 26 October 1909; cited fifteen crimes; executed at Lüshun 26 March 1910; unfinished *Treatise on Peace in the East*. [source: AJG; ITO]
- Annexation treaty 22 August 1910, effective 29 August (Ye Wanyong, Terauchi Masatake). Koreans: *Gyeongsul Gukchi* (national humiliation). 1965: "already null and void." [source: K10]
### Taiwan from 1895
- Ceded at Shimonoseki 1895; Japan's first colony, a "model colony". Taipei fell 7 June 1895. Yunlin Massacre June 1896 (6,000 killed); 1898–1902 some 12,000 "bandit-rebels" killed. The Diet debated selling Taiwan to France (1897). Kodama Gentarō and Gotō Shinpei from 1898; *hoko* system. Tapani (1915): over 1,400 killed. Musha 1930 (beyond period). "Industry for Japan, agriculture for Taiwan." [source: TWN; TAP]

## 13. Sino-Japanese and Russo-Japanese wars and global echo
- First Sino-Japanese War 1894–95: shifted East Asian dominance from China to Japan. [source: SJW]
- Russo-Japanese War: 8 February 1904 – 5 September 1905; Port Arthur attack before declaration; Port Arthur fell January 1905; Mukden 20 February – 10 March 1905 (~half a million men; ~90,000 Russian casualties); Tsushima 27–28 May 1905 (Russia lost 8 battleships, 5,000+ men; Japan 3 torpedo boats, 116 men). Dead: Russia 80,378–86,100; Japan 43,300–71,453. [source: RJW]
- Jacob Schiff loans of $200 million. [source: RJW]
- Treaty of Portsmouth 5 September 1905 (Roosevelt; Nobel); Korea, Liaodong lease, South Manchuria Railway, south Sakhalin; no indemnity. Hibiya riots 5–7 September 1905: 17 killed, 2,000+ arrested, martial law. [source: POR; HIB; RJW]
- Global echo: Sun Yat-sen ("defeat of the West by the East"); African Americans (Joseph Bryant); W. E. B. Du Bois; Vietnamese, Indonesians, Indians, Filipinos, Ottomans, Persians; 1905 Russian Revolution; "Yellow Peril" fears. [source: RJW]
- Caveat: "first defeat of a European power by a non-European state" — Ethiopia defeated Italy at Adwa, 1 March 1896. Say "first in a modern great-power war". [source: ADWA]

## 14. Emperor Meiji
- Died 29 July 1912 (1852–1912). Role debated; Donald Keene notes contradictions. [source: EMP]

- EMP: Meiji "kept no diary, wrote almost no letters" and left very few photographs (flagged citation needed); his biographer Donald Keene (*Emperor of Japan: Meiji and His World, 1852–1912*, 2002) notes that accounts of his boyhood contradict each other. Western-style imperial portraits (e.g., Takahashi Yuichi's 1880 portrait) were part of the new monarchy's image. [source: EMP; MEI]

## 15. Additional context for the Sino-Japanese War section (Japanese/Korean side)
- First Sino-Japanese War: Japan fielded 357,100 in total, China 240,616 (infobox); Japan about 35,000 killed or wounded. Before the war, Korea's own reformers pursued *tongdo sŏgi* ("Eastern ways and Western machines"), a self-strengthening programme; Japanese leaders believed Korea needed Meiji-style reform, which also served Japanese interests. [source: SJW]
- The defeat shifted regional dominance from China to Japan and helped trigger the reform and revolutionary movements of Kang Youwei and Sun Yat-sen. [source: SJW]
- Mutsu Munemitsu (1844–1897) negotiated at Shimonoseki; after a Japanese fanatic shot Li Hongzhang, Japan reduced its indemnity claim and Mutsu remarked, "Li's misfortune is the good fortune of the Great Ch'ing Empire." [source: MUT]
- Taiwan: Japanese forces landed near Keelung on 29 May 1895; Liu Yongfu's Black Flags resisted in the south; contemporary reports said over 100,000 troops were needed and the occupation "was not accomplished without much cruelty". [source: TWN]

## Debates
- **Restoration, revolution or reform?** Contemporaries called it *Goisshin*; it is also translated "Meiji Reformation". W. G. Beasley argues that until victory the Satchō leaders sought to displace those in power rather than change institutions, and that the outcome was a "centralised absolutism"; Jansen stresses how the shogunate's authority eroded as domain bureaucrats used military force. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration]
- **The Marxist "debate on Japanese capitalism" (1927–37):** the Kōza-ha ("Symposium" faction, named after the 7-volume *Symposium on the History of the Development of Japanese Capitalism*, Iwanami 1932–33) held that the Meiji state was absolutist, resting on semi-feudal agriculture (high rents, poor tenants), so Japan needed a two-stage revolution (the Comintern line of the 1927 and 1932 Theses). The Rōnō-ha (Labour-Farmer faction, led by Yamakawa Hitoshi) called the Restoration a bourgeois revolution and the emperor system a bourgeois monarchy with insignificant feudal vestiges. Hoston (1986) is the standard study; state repression ended open debate in the 1930s, but it shaped postwar Japanese scholarship and left-wing party programmes. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism_and_the_Crisis_of_Development_in_Prewar_Japan; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koza_school]
- **E. Herbert Norman** (1909–1957), Canadian diplomat-historian born in Japan, argued in *Japan's Emergence as a Modern State* (1940) that persisting feudal class relations explained repression at home and imperial expansion abroad, an interpretation close to the Kōza-ha. Accused during the Red Scare, he died by suicide in Cairo in April 1957. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._Herbert_Norman]
- **Modernisation theory:** the Conference on Modern Japan's Hakone meeting (late August – early September 1960; Ford Foundation-funded; participants included John W. Hall and Edwin Reischauer from the US and Maruyama Masao from Japan) framed Meiji Japan as a successful non-Western modernisation and a model for others, in implicit rivalry with Marxist accounts. Japanese critics included Inoue Kiyoshi (1963). Jansen later recalled the theory as an "intellectual mushroom cloud". Attendance figures in the sources differ. [source: WebSearch results: https://doshisha.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/28010/files/r005000040014.pdf; https://dijtokyo.org/event/hakone-conference-revisited-modernization-and-the-civil-society-school-in-postwar-japan] (Secondary summaries; keep wording general.)
- **People's history:** Irokawa Daikichi (*The Culture of the Meiji Period*) and others recovered commoner political thought (the Itsukaichi draft constitution; the Chichibu leaders' ideology) against both the oligarch-centred and the purely economic readings. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chichibu_incident; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_and_People%27s_Rights_Movement]
- **Carol Gluck:** treats late-Meiji ideology (1890–1912) as a process built by many hands and often unreliable local purveyors, not a single state-imposed system. By the end of the era the emperor had become a symbol of the Meiji achievement of "civilisation", and success-and-striving ideologies ran against the official family-state. Atsuko Hirai's review criticised omissions (socialism, colonialism); Jansen praised it as the best book on Meiji ideology. [source: WebSearch summaries: https://scholarcommons.sc.edu/hist_facpub/128; https://ahorbinski.dreamwidth.org/54593.html] (Secondary; phrase cautiously.)
- **Andrew Gordon:** *Labor and Imperial Democracy in Prewar Japan* (1991; Fairbank Prize 1992) reads riots from the 1905 Hibiya riots onward, labour disputes and unions as an "imperial democracy" movement, democratic demands framed in loyalty to emperor and empire, rooted in the Meiji project. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Gordon_(historian); https://www.overdrive.com/media/2066391/labor-and-imperial-democracy-in-prewar-japan]
- **Mark Ravina:** rejects the clean Tokugawa/Meiji and tradition/modernity split. He stresses Tokugawa innovation and Meiji conservatism, and places 1868 among the nation-states of the long 19th century that sought international recognition while celebrating their own uniqueness. Reviewers dispute how new this is (JAS reviewer; an exchange with Frederick Dickinson in *Pacific Affairs*). Ravina also challenges the Saigō seppuku legend and the "Satsuma Rebellion" label. [source: WebSearch summary: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/734144; https://pacificaffairs.ubc.ca/?p=12402; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satsuma_Rebellion]
- **Seikanron:** orthodox historians see an invade/don't-invade split in 1873; revisionists see a dispute over timing plus a power struggle between caretaker and Iwakura groups, citing Japan's own gunboat diplomacy in Korea in 1875–76. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seikanron]
- **Legality of Korea's protectorate and annexation:** most Korean scholars hold that the 1905 and 1910 treaties were void from the start (coercion; Gojong's and Sunjong's lack of consent). Japanese studies argue Gojong consented. The 1965 Japan–ROK Basic Relations Treaty fudged it with "already null and void". At the 2001 Harvard conferences, Western international-law scholars largely rejected the illegality claim (per Kan Kimura). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan%E2%80%93Korea_Treaty_of_1905; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan%E2%80%93Korea_Treaty_of_1910]
- **Colonial modernity vs exploitation (Korea, Taiwan):** Korean nationalist historiography frames 1910–45 as repression and resistance; Shin and Robinson (1999) emphasise how modernity was produced and mobilised for colonial domination. In Taiwan the "model colony" image (infrastructure, Gotō Shinpei's reforms) stands against the record of massacre and resistance (Yunlin 1896, Tapani 1915) and a colonial economy of "industry for Japan, agriculture for Taiwan". [source: https://cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-asian-studies/article/abs/colonial-modernity-in-korea-edited-by-giwook-shin-and-michael-robinson-cambridge-mass-harvard-university-press-1999-harvard-east-asian-monographs-184harvardhallym-series-on-korean-studies-xiii-466-pp-4950-cloth/11B53DCE911FEDA4EB8A86489DB57A2C; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_under_Japanese_rule]
- **Who paid for modernisation:** MEI (summarising scholarship) holds that peasants bore the cost through land taxes far above European and Qing rates; the Kōza-ha emphasised semi-feudal tenancy; others point to rural dynamism already under way in late Tokugawa. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism_and_the_Crisis_of_Development_in_Prewar_Japan]
