## What the field is (definitions and scope)
- "World history" or "global history" as a field examines history from a global perspective; it is distinct from comparative history, which compares multiple cultures/nations but not on a global scale. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_history_(field)]
- World historians are described as working with two focal points: integration (how processes drew the world's peoples together) and difference (how patterns reveal the diversity of human experience) (Hare and Wells, *History Teacher*, 2015). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_history_(field)]
- The field sets itself against two structures of professional history-writing: (1) using today's nation-states to set the boundaries and agendas of studies of the past; (2) a deep legacy of Eurocentric assumptions. It studies networks, connections and systems crossing linguistic, cultural and national borders. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_history_(field)]
- Jerry H. Bentley (2011) observed that "the term *world history* has never been a clear signifier with a stable referent"; usage overlaps with universal history, comparative history, global history, Big History, macro history and transnational history. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_history_(field)]
- As a distinct academic field, world history is traced to the United States in the 1960s, quickening in the 1980s; it has often displaced "Western Civilization" courses in American curricula. Wikipedia's article itself carries a "Globalize … United States" tag (i.e., its account is US-centred) — the writer should note the field has strong German, British, Indian, Japanese and other centres (Conrad, Osterhammel in Germany; Subrahmanyam, Chakrabarty from India; Bayly in Britain). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_history_(field)]
- Transnational history: Akira Iriye: "transnational history may be defined as the study of movements and forces that have cut across national boundaries". Ian Tyrrell pioneered the approach in American studies in 1991; Iriye and Pierre-Yves Saunier define it in terms of "connections and circulations" between societies in the modern age. It foregrounds non-state actors. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transnational_history]
- Histoire croisée ("entangled"/"crossed" history): Michael Werner and Bénédicte Zimmermann, "Penser l'histoire croisée: entre empirie et réflexivité", *Annales HSS* 58:1 (Jan–Feb 2003), 7–36; English version "Beyond Comparison: Histoire Croisée and the Challenge of Reflexivity", *History and Theory* 45:1 (Feb 2006), 30–50. It attends both to real intercrossings in the object of study and to how researchers themselves cross scales, categories and viewpoints. [source: WebSearch results incl. https://historyandtheory.org/superlatives/wernerzimmermann and https://shs.cairn.info/article/E_ANNA_581_0007?lang=en]
- "Connected histories": Sanjay Subrahmanyam, "Connected Histories: Notes towards a Reconfiguration of Early Modern Eurasia", *Modern Asian Studies* 31:3 (1997), 735–762, in a special issue edited by Victor Lieberman; essays later collected in *Explorations in Connected History* (2005). [source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/modern-asian-studies/article/connected-histories-notes-towards-a-reconfiguration-of-early-modern-eurasia1/994952F5EFC854AFA7CF0D7DB6A88925 (via WebSearch); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanjay_Subrahmanyam]
- Subrahmanyam: born 21 May 1961, New Delhi; PhD Delhi School of Economics (1987); at UCLA since 2004; Chair in Early Modern Global History at the Collège de France (elected 2013); first Infosys Prize in Humanities (2012); Dan David Prize 2019 jointly with Kenneth Pomeranz. His work "is associated with the concept of 'connected histories'". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanjay_Subrahmanyam]
- Sebastian Conrad, *What Is Global History?* (Princeton University Press, 2016) is widely used as the introduction to the field; translated into fourteen languages; Conrad is at the Free University of Berlin and since 2012 directs its joint MA in Global History with Humboldt University. Reviewers summarise his view that global history is both a subject of study and a distinct way/methodology of looking at history (paraphrase; exact sentence not verified). One Cambridge review record dates the book 2015 — use 2016. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastian_Conrad; WebSearch]
- Microhistory began in Italy in the 1970s (Carlo Ginzburg, Giovanni Levi); Ginzburg's *The Cheese and the Worms* (1976) on the miller Menocchio is "probably the most popular and widely read work of microhistory". Since the 2010s "global microhistory" combines microhistorical focus with transregional/global perspectives. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microhistory; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cheese_and_the_Worms]
- Big History: David Christian (born 1946, Brooklyn; grew up in Nigeria and England) began teaching the first Big History course in 1989 at Macquarie University, Sydney, covering history from the Big Bang to the present; his *Maps of Time* won the World History Association Book Prize (2005). Bill Gates funded a high-school version (pilot in 2011 in 50 schools). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Christian_(historian); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_History]

## Precursors: universal histories from many traditions
- Herodotus (c. 484–c. 425 BC), from Halicarnassus (now Bodrum, Turkey), then under Persian control; his *Histories* include ethnographic and geographic material on many peoples, notably Egyptians. Thucydides rejected this all-embracing approach for a focused monograph on the Athens–Sparta war. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_history_(field)]
- Sima Qian's *Shiji* (*Records of the Grand Historian*), written late 2nd–early 1st century BC in the Western Han, building on his father Sima Tan's work; covers some 2,500 years from the Yellow Emperor to Emperor Wu; first of the Twenty-Four Histories; describes the world as known to the Western Han. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiji]
- Rashid al-Din (1247–1318), born into a Persian Jewish family in Hamadan province, converted to Islam around age 30, vizier of the Ilkhan Ghazan, executed 1318 after being charged with poisoning Öljaitü. His *Jāmiʿ al-tawārīkh* ("Compendium of Chronicles"), early 14th century, in Persian and Arabic, covers peoples from China to Europe and has been called "the first world history". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashid_al-Din_Hamadani; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jami%27_al-tawarikh]
- Ibn Khaldun (27 May 1332 – 17 March 1406) wrote the *Muqaddimah* in 1377 as the introduction to his planned world history, the *Kitab al-ʿIbar*; he analysed the rise and fall of dynasties, nomadic versus sedentary life and *asabiyyah* (social solidarity). Toynbee called the *Muqaddimah* "the greatest work of its kind". (The World history (field) article gives his death as 1409 — an error; use 1406.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Khaldun; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muqaddimah]
- Hegel's *Lectures on the Philosophy of World History*, given at Berlin in 1822, 1828 and 1830, published posthumously in 1837 (ed. Eduard Gans): world history moves from Asia to Greece and Rome to northern Europe; he treated India and China as "stationary". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lectures_on_the_Philosophy_of_World_History; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurocentrism]
- Oswald Spengler, *The Decline of the West* (*Der Untergang des Abendlandes*), vol. 1 1918, vol. 2 1922 (definitive edition 1923); treated cultures as organisms with lifespans; warmly received by some intellectuals in China, India and Latin America who hoped European empires would collapse. (World history (field) article says 1919–1922 — use 1918/1922.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Decline_of_the_West; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_history_(field)]
- Arnold J. Toynbee, *A Study of History*, 12 volumes, 1934–1961 (vols I–III 1934, IV–VI 1939, VII–X 1954, XI atlas 1959, XII *Reconsiderations* 1961); traced 19 or 21 civilizations through genesis, growth, time of troubles, universal state and disintegration. (Some sources say "ten volumes 1936–1954"; the 1934–1961 list is the precise one.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Study_of_History]
- Other early-20th-century bestsellers: H. G. Wells, *The Outline of History* (1918 per Wikipedia, i.e., 1919–20 serial — treat date loosely), van Loon, *The Story of Mankind* (1921). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_history_(field)]

## McNeill and the founding of modern world history
- William H. McNeill (31 Oct 1917, Vancouver – 8 July 2016), taught at the University of Chicago 1947–1987; *The Rise of the West: A History of the Human Community* (University of Chicago Press, 1963) won the 1964 National Book Award in History and Biography. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_H._McNeill]
- McNeill conceived the book as a student in 1936 to counter Spengler and Toynbee; the title deliberately contrasts with *Decline of the West*. Against discrete civilizations, he stressed contact and diffusion of techniques and ideas across the Eurasian "ecumene" as the motor of change. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rise_of_the_West]
- In "*The Rise of the West* after Twenty-Five Years", *Journal of World History* 1:1 (Spring 1990), 1–21, McNeill reflected that the book should be seen as "an expression of the postwar imperial mood in the United States" and a "form of intellectual imperialism" (wording as quoted by Wikipedia; primary text not accessible). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rise_of_the_West]
- Also: *Plagues and Peoples* (1976), early major work on disease in history; *The Human Web* (2003) with his son J. R. McNeill; AHA president 1985. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_H._McNeill]
- Marshall Hodgson (born 1922), *The Venture of Islam* (posthumous, 3 vols), coined "Islamicate"; *Rethinking World History* (1993, ed. Edmund Burke III) critiqued Eurocentric historiography and set Europe's rise in wider Eurasian dynamics (e.g., Song Chinese innovations). Published chapter on Early Modern overview already cites Hodgson & McNeill's "gunpowder empires". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Hodgson]

## Institutions
- World History Association founded 1982 (affiliated with the AHA since 1983). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_History_Association]
- *Journal of World History* established 1990 by Jerry H. Bentley at the University of Hawaiʻi as the WHA's official journal; twice yearly at first, quarterly from 2003. Bentley (1949–2012). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_World_History; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_H._Bentley]
- *Journal of Global History* established 2006, Cambridge University Press, co-sponsored by the LSE; roots in a seminar Patrick K. O'Brien created at the Institute of Historical Research in 1990. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Global_History]
- AP World History course launched in the 2001–2002 school year (US). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_History_Association]

## World-systems analysis and its challengers
- Immanuel Wallerstein (born 28 Sept 1930, New York; died 31 Aug 2019); *The Modern World-System*, four volumes, 1974–2011; vol. 1 (1974) largely written at the Center for Advanced Study in the Behavioral Sciences (Stanford). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Wallerstein]
- Core idea: a world-system is "a unit with a single division of labor and multiple cultural systems" (Wallerstein, 1974, as quoted). The capitalist world-economy, originating in the "long" 16th century (c. 1450–1640) in western Europe and the Americas, divides into core, semi-periphery and periphery; hegemony passed from the Netherlands to the UK to the US. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World-systems_theory]
- Wallerstein named Frantz Fanon, Fernand Braudel and Ilya Prigogine as his greatest influences; roots also in dependency theory (Andre Gunder Frank's "development of underdevelopment") and the Annales school. Other world-systems thinkers include Samir Amin, Giovanni Arrighi, Oliver Cox, Janet Abu-Lughod. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Wallerstein; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World-systems_theory]
- Janet Abu-Lughod (1928–2013), *Before European Hegemony: The World System A.D. 1250–1350* (Oxford University Press, 1989; Wikipedia lists a 1991 printing): a pre-modern world system spanning Eurasia existed in the 13th century, linking Champagne fairs, the Mongol Empire, the Mamluk Sultanate and India; the "rise of the West" was enabled by the collapse of that earlier system, not by features internal to Europe. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janet_Abu-Lughod; WebSearch catalog record https://search.worldcat.org/oclc/18462916]
- Andre Gunder Frank (born Andreas Frank, Berlin, 24 Feb 1929 – 23 April 2005): *Capitalism and Underdevelopment in Latin America* (1967) a founding dependency text; *ReOrient: Global Economy in the Asian Age* (University of California Press, 1998) argued the world economy c. 1400–1800 was Asia-centred (China at its centre), Europe used American silver to buy into it, and the "rise of the West" was a late, perhaps temporary, episode. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andre_Gunder_Frank; WebSearch publisher descriptions]
- Eric Wolf, *Europe and the People Without History* (1982): global history tracing connections among peoples usually treated as discrete; differs from world-systems theory in seeing Europe's growth until the late 18th century as tributary rather than capitalist; "primitive" phenomena are "products of the encounter between the West and the Rest". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe_and_the_People_Without_History]
- Sidney Mintz criticised world-systems determinism ("The So-Called World-System: Local Initiative and Local Response", 1977): larger forces were always met by local responses. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Mintz]

## The Great Divergence
- The term "Great Divergence" was coined by Samuel P. Huntington in 1996 and popularised by Kenneth Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence: China, Europe, and the Making of the Modern World Economy* (Princeton University Press, 2000); Eric Jones's *The European Miracle* (1981) had popularised "European miracle". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- Pomeranz (born 4 Nov 1958), PhD Yale 1988 under Jonathan Spence; long at UC Irvine, now University of Chicago; AHA president 2013–14; book won the John K. Fairbank Prize and was joint winner of the WHA book prize for 2000. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Pomeranz; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Divergence_(book)]
- Argument: the most advanced regions of Europe and Asia (England, the Yangzi Delta) were comparable until about 1800 and faced similar land constraints; Britain pulled ahead through accessible coal and colonies (New World land). Associated with the "California School" (Pomeranz, R. Bin Wong and others). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Divergence_(book)]
- R. Bin Wong (Wang Guobin), UCLA: *China Transformed: Historical Change and the Limits of European Experience* (Cornell, 1997); with Jean-Laurent Rosenthal, *Before and Beyond Divergence: The Politics of Economic Change in China and Europe* (Harvard, 2011). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Bin_Wong; WebSearch]
- Critics/alternatives: Philip C. C. Huang, "Development or involution in eighteenth-century Britain and China?" *Journal of Asian Studies* 61:2 (2002), 501–538, answered by Pomeranz, "Facts are stubborn things" (*JAS* 62:1, 2003). Robert C. Allen (born 1947) found 18th-century London/Amsterdam labourers could buy 4–6 times a subsistence basket vs roughly subsistence in Beijing and Delhi (1725–1825), and argues in *The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective* (2009) for a high-wage, cheap-coal explanation. Stephen Broadberry: English silver wages three times the Yangzi Delta's by 1600; divergence c. 1700 (Broadberry & Zhai). Jack Goldstone (2021 review): divergence only after 1750 or even 1800. Prasannan Parthasarathi, *Why Europe Grew Rich and Asia Did Not* (2011), extends comparison to South India and criticises Pomeranz's ecological emphasis. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Allen_(economic_historian); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_C._C._Huang; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Pomeranz; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Divergence_(book)]
- Frank's own review of Pomeranz appeared in *Journal of Asian Studies* 60:1 (2001). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]

## Eurocentrism and postcolonial critiques
- The term "Eurocentrism" as the name of an ideology was coined in the 1970s by the Egyptian Marxian economist Samir Amin (1931–2018; born Cairo); English usage recorded by 1979; his book *L'eurocentrisme* 1988 (English *Eurocentrism*, 1989). (Amin's own Wikipedia article says he "introduced" the term in 1988 — the sources conflict; safest: "Amin, who developed the concept from the 1970s and published *Eurocentrism* in 1988".) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurocentrism; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samir_Amin]
- Edward Said (1935–2003), *Orientalism* (1978): Western scholarship on the "Orient" was bound up with imperial power. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientalism_(book); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Said]
- Dipesh Chakrabarty (born 1948, Kolkata), University of Chicago; *Provincializing Europe: Postcolonial Thought and Historical Difference* (2000); Toynbee Prize 2014; later "The Climate of History: Four Theses" (2009). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipesh_Chakrabarty]
- J. M. Blaut, *The Colonizer's Model of the World* (1993) and *Eight Eurocentric Historians* (2000). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurocentrism]
- Spengler himself presented his book as rejecting the Eurocentric ancient–medieval–modern scheme. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Decline_of_the_West]

## Big syntheses of the modern world
- C. A. Bayly (18 May 1945 – 18 April 2015), Vere Harmsworth Professor at Cambridge 1992–2013: *The Birth of the Modern World, 1780–1914: Global Connections and Comparisons* (Blackwell, 2004); argues the world was more "globalised" than commonly thought and rejects European "exceptionalism"; final chapter "Contested uniformity and universal complexity revisited". First posthumous winner of the Toynbee Prize (2016). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Bayly; WebSearch]
- Jürgen Osterhammel (born 1952, Wipperfürth; University of Konstanz): *Die Verwandlung der Welt* (C. H. Beck, 2009); English *The Transformation of the World: A Global History of the Nineteenth Century*, trans. Patrick Camiller (Princeton, 2014), xxii + 1,167 pp. Leibniz Prize 2010; Toynbee Prize 2017; Balzan Prize for Global History 2018. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%BCrgen_Osterhammel; WebSearch catalog/review records]

## Commodities as global threads
- Sidney Mintz (16 Nov 1922 – 27 Dec 2015), anthropologist; fieldwork among sugar-cane workers in Puerto Rico (from 1948), later Jamaica and Haiti; taught at Yale, then Johns Hopkins. *Sweetness and Power: The Place of Sugar in Modern History* (Viking, New York, 1985) — "one of the most influential publications in cultural anthropology and food studies". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Mintz]
- Mintz's argument (per reviews): sugar, a crop grown by enslaved people in Europe's tropical colonies, went from aristocratic luxury to working-class staple in Britain; cheap sugar (often in sweetened tea) supplied calories to industrial workers, linking Caribbean slavery to English factory life. Some scholars question the working-class consumption part. [source: WebSearch incl. https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/a/sidney-w-mintz/sweetness-and-power-the-place-of-sugar-in-moder]
- Sven Beckert (born 1965; Harvard), *Empire of Cotton: A Global History* (Knopf, 2014): won the 2015 Bancroft Prize, Pulitzer finalist; Peer Vries called it "a must read for every historian interested in global history, but in my view it is better as a story on cotton than as an analysis of capitalism." His sequel *Capitalism: A Global History* (2025). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sven_Beckert]
- Beckert's "war capitalism": slavery, expropriation of Indigenous peoples, imperial expansion, armed trade and entrepreneurs' assertion of sovereignty over people and land — the precondition of industrial capitalism; reviewers call the term loosely defined. [source: WebSearch, e.g. https://reason.com/2016/07/24/cotton-coercion-and-capitalism/]
- Silver (worked example): the Manila galleons ran between Manila and Acapulco 1565–1815, after Andrés de Urdaneta found the eastward return route (*tornaviaje*) in 1565; they carried Asian (mostly Chinese) goods for American silver; Ming China ran on silver, valued there at about double its price elsewhere. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manila_galleon; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_silver_trade_from_the_16th_to_19th_centuries]
- Dennis O. Flynn and Arturo Giráldez, "Born with a 'Silver Spoon': The Origin of World Trade in 1571", *Journal of World History* 6:2 (1995), 201–221: global trade began with the founding of Manila as a Spanish entrepôt in 1571, when all important populated continents began exchanging products continuously. [source: WebSearch catalog record https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_38839981]
- Potosí found 1545; mercury amalgamation multiplied Potosí output sixfold 1575–1590; Bolivia and Mexico produced about 80% of world silver 1500–1800, about 30% of it ending in China (Wikipedia figure; published chapters say "a third or more" — compatible). Japan also exported much silver to China in the late 16th–early 17th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_silver_trade_from_the_16th_to_19th_centuries]

## Oceans as units of analysis
- Fernand Braudel (1902–1985), *The Mediterranean* (1949) — the model for sea-centred history; *Civilization and Capitalism, 15th–18th Century* (3 vols, 1967–1979). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernand_Braudel]
- Atlantic history: studies the Americas, Europe and Africa as a common sphere after the 16th century; Robert R. Palmer's *The Age of the Democratic Revolution* (1959, 1964) pioneered it, following C. L. R. James's 1930s linking of the French and Haitian revolutions; Bernard Bailyn's Harvard seminar (annual from 1995; 366 young historians to 2010) and *Atlantic History: Concepts and Contours* (2005). Critics: "imperial history under another name"; Caroline Dodds Pennock notes thousands of Native Americans crossed the Atlantic in the 16th century. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_history]
- Paul Gilroy (born 1956), *The Black Atlantic: Modernity and Double Consciousness* (1993): a transatlantic black culture mixing African, American, British and Caribbean elements. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Black_Atlantic]
- Indian Ocean: K. N. Chaudhuri, *Trade and Civilisation in the Indian Ocean: An Economic History from the Rise of Islam to 1750* (Cambridge, 1985) and *Asia before Europe* (1990), commissioned in 1980 as a Braudel-style history of the Indian Ocean. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirti_N._Chaudhuri]
- Indian Ocean trade ran on seasonal monsoon winds, known and exploited by Ptolemaic and Roman Egyptian traders. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_trade]
- Sunil Amrith (born 1979, Nairobi, of Indian parents; raised in Singapore; Yale): *Crossing the Bay of Bengal: The Furies of Nature and the Fortunes of Migrants* (Harvard, 2013; AHA John F. Richards Prize 2014); *Unruly Waters* (2018); *The Burning Earth* (2024; British Academy Book Prize 2025). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunil_Amrith]
- Sujit Sivasundaram (Sri Lankan-born, Cambridge), *Waves Across the South* (HarperCollins, 2020): revolutions and empires in the Indian Ocean and Pacific seen from the sea; British Academy Book Prize 2021. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sujit_Sivasundaram]
- Pacific: Epeli Hauʻofa (7 Dec 1939 – 11 Jan 2009), Tongan-Fijian anthropologist at the University of the South Pacific; essay "Our Sea of Islands" (1993, in *A New Oceania: Rediscovering Our Sea of Islands*) argued Pacific Islanders were connected rather than separated by the sea — a reframing from "islands in a far sea" to "a sea of islands". Matt K. Matsuda, *Pacific Worlds: A History of Seas, Peoples, and Cultures* (Cambridge, 2012). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epeli_Hau%CA%BBofa; WebSearch]

## Mobilities: disease, migration, ideas
- Alfred W. Crosby, *The Columbian Exchange: Biological and Cultural Consequences of 1492* (1972) coined the term and helped found environmental history. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Columbian_Exchange]
- McNeill, *Plagues and Peoples* (1976) on disease in history. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_H._McNeill]
- Philip D. Curtin, *The Atlantic Slave Trade: A Census* (1969) estimated 9,566,000 Africans imported to the Americas; current estimates about 12–12.8 million embarked. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_D._Curtin]
- Amrith's work on migration across the Bay of Bengal (above) is a ready example of migration as a global thread.

## Global microhistory and lives across empires
- Natalie Zemon Davis (8 Nov 1928 – 21 Oct 2023), *Trickster Travels: A Sixteenth-Century Muslim Between Worlds* (Hill & Wang, 2006), on al-Hasan al-Wazzan ("Leo Africanus"), seeing his writings as showing "the possibility of communication and curiosity in a world divided by violence" (quoted in Wikipedia). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natalie_Zemon_Davis]
- Leo Africanus: born c. 1494 in Granada, raised in Fez; visited Timbuktu c. 1510; captured by Spanish corsairs in 1518, presented to Pope Leo X and baptised; wrote *Cosmographia et geographia de Affrica* (1526), published by Ramusio as *Descrittione dell'Africa* (1550). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Africanus]
- Linda Colley, *The Ordeal of Elizabeth Marsh: A Woman in World History* (2007; a NYT best book of 2007); also *Captives: Britain, Empire and the World 1600–1850* (2002). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linda_Colley]
- John-Paul Ghobrial (ed.), *Global History and Microhistory*, *Past & Present* 242, Supplement 14 (Nov 2019), introduction "Seeing the World like a Microhistorian" (pp. 1–22); open access; Jan de Vries criticised the genre for giving undue prominence to "unusually cosmopolitan individuals" (wording as reported in a secondary summary). [source: WebSearch incl. https://global.history.ox.ac.uk/article/john-paul-ghobrial-global-history-and-microhistory-supplement-volume-past-and-present]

## Critiques of global history (2017–2018)
- Jeremy Adelman (born 1960, Canadian; Princeton 1992–2023; now directs the Global History Lab at Cambridge) published "What is global history now?" in *Aeon* on 2 March 2017. He argued the field's relevance had been tied to globalisation and its boosters; criticised the belief that all history must become transnational or global and "borderless, do-good storytelling" (phrase as quoted by commentators); asked how historians can treat separation "as we do connection"; and called for a global history as attentive to disunity as to unity. Commentators report he called global history a narrative fit for the "now-defunct Clinton Global Initiative". The essay was written after the Brexit vote and Trump's election (context per commentators). [source: WebSearch incl. https://www.cambridge.org/core/blog/2018/02/15/the-futures-of-global-history; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Adelman]

## Debates
- **Unit of analysis**: nation-state vs world-system (Wallerstein), oceans (Braudel, Chaudhuri, Hauʻofa, Bailyn), commodities (Mintz, Beckert), individuals (Davis, Colley, global microhistory). [sources above]
- **When did the modern world system begin?** Wallerstein: the long 16th century in western Europe; Abu-Lughod: an earlier 13th-century Eurasian system; Frank: an Asia-centred system before 1800 (and, with Gills, a 5,000-year system); Flynn & Giráldez: world trade born 1571 with Manila; Wolf: tributary, not capitalist, before the late 18th century. [sources above]
- **Great Divergence**: California School (Pomeranz, Wong, also Frank, Parthasarathi) — late divergence c. 1800, coal and colonies; vs Huang (involution), Allen (high wages + cheap coal, earlier northwestern European wage lead), Broadberry (early divergence by 1600/1700), Landes (culture), Goldstone (after 1750–1800). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Divergence]
- **Connection vs comparison**: McNeill's diffusion/contact model; Subrahmanyam's "connected histories" (against comparing sealed civilizational units); Werner & Zimmermann's histoire croisée; Rosenthal & Wong's balanced comparison of China and Europe. [sources above]
- **Eurocentrism smuggled back**: McNeill's own 1990 self-criticism; Wikipedia notes world-systems and dependency theory "cemented the centrality of the 'West' in world history" (quoted from a source in the article); Chakrabarty and Amin on Europe as the measure; reviewers' doubts about Bayly and Osterhammel; the field's US/European institutional centre. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rise_of_the_West; others above]
- **The immobile and the disconnected**: Adelman (2017) — global history mirrored globalisation's winners and neglected those left behind; Drayton & Motadel (2018) — the field already studies disconnection and is needed against nationalist myth; de Vries — global microhistory overweights cosmopolitan individuals. [sources above]
- **Big History**: welcomed (Gates-funded school course) but contested; University of Queensland removed its big-history elements from its "Global History" course in 2020. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_History]
