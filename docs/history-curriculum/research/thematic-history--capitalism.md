## 1. The word and its definitions
- In French, Étienne Clavier wrote of *capitalistes* in 1788. The first recorded English use of "capitalist" is by Arthur Young in *Travels in France* (1792). David Ricardo's *Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* (1817) refers to "the capitalist" many times. [source: CAP]
- The first use of "capitalism" in its modern sense is attributed to Louis Blanc in 1850. He wrote: "What I call 'capitalism' that is to say the appropriation of capital by some to the exclusion of others". Proudhon followed in 1861. [source: CAP]
- In English, according to the OED, the word "capitalism" first appears in 1854, in Thackeray's novel *The Newcomes*, where it meant "having ownership of capital". [source: CAP]
- Marx wrote of "capital", "capitalist" and the "capitalist mode of production" in *Das Kapital* (vol. I, 1867). The same Wikipedia article says both that he "did not use the form capitalism" and that he used it "less frequently than 'capitalist mode of production'". Safe wording: Marx rarely or never used the noun; his term was the "capitalist mode of production". [source: CAP]
- Werner Sombart's *Der moderne Kapitalismus* appeared in three volumes from 1902 to 1927 (vol. 1 1902, vol. 2 1916). It divided capitalism into early, high and late stages, with "late capitalism" beginning with the First World War. Wikipedia credits Sombart with coining "late capitalism" and "creative destruction". On "creative destruction", hedge: the phrase is generally associated with Schumpeter (1942). In 1903 Sombart became an associate editor of the *Archiv für Sozialwissenschaft und Sozialpolitik* alongside Max Weber and Edgar Jaffé. [source: SOM]
- Jürgen Kocka (born 1941, Haindorf, Sudetenland), a leader of the Bielefeld school, wrote *Capitalism: A Short History* (Princeton University Press, 2016; translated by Jeremiah Riemer from a German original of 2013/2014; sources differ on the year). [source: KOC; https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/DAF25ADE5A265E93C091B413BFBBD64F/core-reader]
- Kocka's working definition: "I propose a working definition of capitalism that emphasizes decentralization, commodification, and accumulation as basic" characteristics. He treats it as an ideal type. A reviewer asked whether this is "rather a list of associated characteristics than a genuine definition". [source: WebSearch, Cambridge Core review of Kocka, https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/DAF25ADE5A265E93C091B413BFBBD64F/core-reader] (Quote only the fragment given.)
- A standard modern definition is an economic system based on private ownership of the means of production and their use for profit. [source: CAP]

## 2. Weber, Braudel and the "where and why" question
- **Weber.** *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* was published as essays in the *Archiv* in 1904 and 1905. Talcott Parsons first translated it into English in 1930. Weber argued that the Protestant, especially Calvinist, ethic (a "calling", predestination anxiety, worldly asceticism) helped capitalism develop in northern Europe. He illustrated the "spirit of capitalism" with Benjamin Franklin's writings. [source: PE]
- Critics of Weber: Davide Cantoni (2015) found no effect of Protestantism on growth in German cities from 1300 to 1900. Sascha Becker and Ludger Woessmann argue that Protestant literacy explains the gaps Weber observed. Others note that the Catholic minority in Weber's Germany was largely Polish, and discriminated against. [source: PE]
- **Braudel.** Fernand Braudel (24 August 1902 – 27 November 1985) wrote *Civilisation matérielle, économie et capitalisme, XVe–XVIIIe siècle*. Volume 1, *Les structures du quotidien*, appeared in 1967 (English 1973). Volumes 2 (*Les jeux de l'échange*) and 3 (*Le temps du monde*) appeared in 1979. The English edition, *Civilization and Capitalism, 15th–18th Century*, was translated by Siân Reynolds. Also *Afterthoughts on Material Civilization and Capitalism* (lectures, 1977). [source: BRA]
- Braudel's three levels: the ground floor is "material life" (everyday subsistence production and consumption), then the "market economy", then, at "the highest level of all", "capitalism". [source: WebSearch summaries incl. https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1984/11/22/the-long-march/ and https://riseofthewest.net/thinkers/braudel03.htm] The ground-floor term varies in summaries ("material life" or "everyday economic life"). Describe the scheme; do not quote further.
- Braudel argued that free-market exchange and capitalism are to some degree opposed. The market means transparent public transactions among many equal competitors. Capitalism means a few participants using capital, private deals, control of information and limits on competition to control the market. [source: CAP] The label "anti-market" comes from secondary commentary. Do not put it in quotation marks as Braudel's own words.
- Braudel saw finance capitalism emerging earlier, among the Genoese in the sixteenth century and the Dutch in the seventeenth and eighteenth. Giovanni Arrighi extended this into recurring cycles of financial expansion. [source: CAP]

## 3. Merchant capitalism beyond Europe
- **Song China (960–1279).** The economy moved from Tang command structures to extensive use of markets. Wikipedia says national income was "around three times that of 12th century Europe" (treat as an estimate). The period saw cash crops (tea, sugar, mulberry, indigo), regional specialisation, a national market, merchants trading as far as East Africa, and coal used as industrial fuel. The state kept monopolies on tea, salt and gunpowder ingredients. [source: SONG]
- *Jiaozi*: private Sichuan merchants (Chengdu) first issued them. Sixteen Sichuanese merchant houses then formed a note bank. The government took over, founding the Jiaozi office in 1023 and issuing the first official notes in 1024. Numismatists regard these as the first paper money. Overissue caused inflation, and in 1105 Huizong replaced the *jiaozi* with the *qianyin*. [source: JZ]
- Joint-stock-like pooling (*douniu*: many shareholders, managing merchants, profit-sharing) is recorded in Tang and Song China. Other early share companies: Stora Kopparberg (a share transfer documented in 1288) and the Bazacle milling company of Toulouse (shares traded around 1350). [source: JSC]
- **Islamic partnerships.** The Muslim *qirad* (developed from pre-Islamic Arabian caravan trade) is closest to the Italian *commenda* of about the thirteenth century. In the *qirad* the travelling partner bore no capital risk. The *commenda*'s origins are debated: Babylonian, Greco-Roman, Byzantine, Jewish (*'isqa*) and Muslim antecedents are all proposed. [source: COM] (The published Islamic Commercial Revolution chapter already covers the *qirad*/*mudaraba* and Udovitch: cross-refer.)
- **Indian Ocean.** Monsoon-driven trade linked the Red Sea, the Gulf, India, Southeast Asia, China and East Africa. Gujaratis carried Moluccan spices and Chinese silk in exchange for textiles. Calicut was the hub of pepper exports to the Red Sea and Europe. [source: IOT]
- The *hundi*, a medieval Indian bill of exchange and credit instrument, was used for remittance, credit and trade payments. [source: HUN]
- **The Maghribi traders debate.** Avner Greif (born 1955, Stanford) argued that eleventh-century Jewish "Maghribi" traders of the Cairo Geniza enforced contracts through a reputation-based "coalition" (collective punishment of cheats). He contrasted them with the Genoese, who relied more on formal, individualist legal institutions; see *Institutions and the Path to the Modern Economy: Lessons from Medieval Trade* (2006). [source: GRF; https://web.stanford.edu/~avner/greif-debate.html]
- Critics: Jeremy Edwards and Sheilagh Ogilvie, "Contract Enforcement, Institutions, and Social Capital: the Maghribi Traders Reappraised" (*Economic History Review*, 2012; working paper 2008). They argue that no example Greif cites proves a coalition existed, and that the traders also used courts. Historian Jessica Goldberg, working from the Geniza letters, is cited among those finding little evidence of coalition boycotts. Greif replied (2008, 2012). Lisa Bernstein (2017) argues that reputation still mattered. The debate is unresolved. [source: WebSearch: https://web.stanford.edu/~avner/greif-debate.html ; https://ehs.org.uk/article/contract-enforcement-institutions-and-social-capital-the-maghribi-traders-reappraised1/ ; https://nplblog.law.harvard.edu/2017/11/15/hls-private-law-workshop-lisa-bernstein-revisiting-the-maghribi-traders-again/]
- **"Sprouts of capitalism" (China).** Mainland Chinese historians identified features of the late Ming and early Qing economies (16th–18th centuries) as "sprouts" of an indigenous capitalism. The term first appears in *The Chinese Revolution and the Chinese Communist Party*, edited by Mao in 1939: "As China's feudal society had developed a commodity economy, and so carried within itself the seeds of capitalism, China would of herself have developed slowly into a capitalist society even without the impact of foreign capitalism." Shang Yue and others developed it in the 1950s (essay collections 1957, 1960). It was denounced in the Anti-Rightist campaign and the Cultural Revolution. Wu Chengming's multi-volume history (1985) took it up again. Korean nationalist historiography adopted the idea. Mori Masao (1980) said the work "failed to produce satisfactory theoretical results, though it uncovered a wealth of historical facts". [source: SPR] A good non-Western historiographical case.
- Proto-industrialisation (Franklin Mendels, dissertation 1969, article 1972: rural linen in Flanders). Later historians found parallels in India, China, Japan and the Muslim world. [source: PROTO]
- Jairus Banaji, *A Brief History of Commercial Capitalism* (Haymarket, 2020): commercial capitalism reorganised production on a world scale long before industrial capitalism, from Byzantine and Italian merchants through the Muslim trading world to the Dutch and English companies. It argues that capitalism has always combined free and unfree labour. [source: WebSearch: https://www.haymarketbooks.org/books/1510-a-brief-history-of-commercial-capitalism ; https://revsoc21.uk/2021/02/21/review-of-jairus-banaji-a-brief-history-of-commercial-capitalism/]

## 4. The transition: enclosure, agrarian capitalism, the Brenner debate
- England's first parliamentary enclosure act was in 1604. Between 1604 and 1914 there were more than 5,200 enclosure bills, covering about 6.8 million acres, roughly one-fifth of England's area. By the 1750s, enclosure by act of Parliament had become the usual method. [source: ENC]
- Enclosure was a demand or grievance in Kett's Rebellion (1549) and the Midland Revolt / Captain Pouch revolts (1604–07). Thomas More's *Utopia* (1516) has sheep that "devour men". [source: ENC] (If quoting More, use only the phrase "devour men", from the English translation Wikipedia gives.)
- E. P. Thompson: enclosure "was a plain enough case of class robbery". [source: HOC; ENC; Thompson, *The Making of the English Working Class*, Penguin 1991, p. 217] Revisionists answer that "the impact of eighteenth and nineteenth century enclosure has been grossly exaggerated", and that better-off peasants often took part in it. [source: HOC]
- Marx's "so-called primitive accumulation" (*Capital* vol. I, Part 8, chapter 26 onward) answered Smith's "previous accumulation". Marx prefixed the German *ursprünglich* with *sogenannte* ("so-called"). His story is violent expropriation, not thrift: "This primitive accumulation plays in Political Economy about the same part as original sin in theology." [source: PA]
- David Harvey's "accumulation by dispossession" treats primitive accumulation as ongoing: privatisation, financialisation, the management of crises and state redistribution. Silvia Federici extends Marx to the subjugation of women's bodies and reproductive labour, enforced through the witch-hunts. [source: PA; ABD]
- **Brenner debate.** Robert Brenner, "Agrarian Class Structure and Economic Development in Pre-Industrial Europe", *Past & Present* 70 (February 1976). He argued that class structure ("social-property relations"), not population or trade, decided long-run outcomes. The same demographic collapse after the Black Death ended serfdom in the west but brought a "second serfdom" east of the Elbe. Between 1500 and 1750 England developed agrarian capitalism while France reinforced small peasant property. In England, landlords kept most of the land and leased it competitively. The result was the triad of landlord, capitalist tenant farmer and wage labourer, with tenants forced by the market to specialise and improve. [source: BD]
- Respondents: M. M. Postan, John Hatcher and Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie defended the demographic (neo-Malthusian) model. Guy Bois offered a rival Marxist account (a "falling rate of feudal levy") and called Brenner's approach "political Marxism". T. J. Byres, following Rodney Hilton, made rich peasants, not landlords, the "prime movers". The collected papers appeared as *The Brenner Debate* (1985). [source: BD]
- It followed the Dobb–Sweezy debate of the 1950s: Maurice Dobb located the "prime mover" in internal class conflict; Paul Sweezy in the growth of trade. [source: BD]
- Ellen Meiksins Wood (born New York, 12 April 1942) developed the "political Marxist" view in *The Origin of Capitalism* (1999; expanded edition 2002), which saw capitalism originating in the English countryside. [source: EMW] (Year from her bibliography and a 1999 interview; treat 1999 as first edition.)
- Polanyi: "not until 1834 was a competitive labor market established in England, hence industrial capitalism as a social system cannot be said to have existed before that date". [source: HOC]

## 5. Companies, exchanges and the Dutch moment
- **VOC.** Established 20 March 1602 by the States General, merging existing companies, with a 21-year monopoly on Asian trade and capital of 6,440,200 guilders. Any Dutch citizen could buy shares, which were traded on open secondary markets that became the Amsterdam stock exchange. Its charter allowed it to build forts, keep armies and make treaties. It is "one of the first joint-stock companies" and sometimes called the first multinational. [source: VOC]
- Between 1602 and 1796 the VOC sent nearly one million Europeans to Asia on 4,785 ships, against the English company's 2,690 ships. It paid dividends averaging about 18% of capital a year for almost 200 years. It went bankrupt and was dissolved in 1799. [source: VOC]
- Nuance: according to Padgett and Powell (2012), cited by Wikipedia, the VOC became the first intercontinental "corporation" with "locked in" capital and limited liability in **1612**, not 1602. [source: JSC] (See conflicts.)
- English East India Company chartered 31 December 1600 (published chapter); the Muscovy Company received a royal charter in 1555. [source: JSC; HOC]
- Bank of Amsterdam (Wisselbank) established 31 January 1609. Some describe it as the first true central bank; that view is not universal. It went into liquidation in 1819. [source: BOA]
- Tulip mania peaked and collapsed in February 1637. Charles Mackay's *Extraordinary Popular Delusions* (1841) made it famous. Many modern scholars, including Anne Goldgar, think it was far less destructive than Mackay claimed. [source: TUL]
- Braudel on Weber: "Amsterdam copied Venice, as London would subsequently copy Amsterdam, and as New York would one day copy London." [source: PE, citing Braudel, *Afterthoughts on Material Civilization and Capitalism*]
- Mercantilism: a state-backed system of monopolies, bullionism and colonies, articulated by Thomas Mun and Josiah Child in England and by Colbert (in office 1665–1683) in France. Smith attacked it in 1776. [source: HOC]

## 6. Wallerstein and the world-system
- Immanuel Wallerstein (born New York 1930, died 31 August 2019). *The Modern World-System* appeared in four volumes from 1974 to 2011. Core, semi-periphery and periphery; hegemony passed from the Netherlands to Britain to the United States. From 1976 he headed the Fernand Braudel Center at Binghamton. He named Fanon, Braudel and Prigogine as his greatest influences. [source: WAL; WST]
- Critics and rivals: too economistic and state-centric; positivists call it unfalsifiable. Andre Gunder Frank pushed an Afro-Eurasian world system back to the 4th millennium BCE. Janet Abu-Lughod (thirteenth-century system) and Giovanni Arrighi are other world-systems contributors. [source: WST] (The published Global History chapter already sets out Wallerstein, Abu-Lughod and the Great Divergence; cross-refer.)

## 7. Slavery, colonial extraction and "war capitalism"
- Eric Williams, *Capitalism and Slavery* (published in the US, 1944; British publishers refused it). It grew from his Oxford D.Phil. C. L. R. James gave a statement of the thesis in English in 1938. Seymour Drescher (1987): "If one criterion of a classic is its ability to reorient our most basic way of viewing an object or a concept, Eric Williams's study supremely passes that test." Kenneth Morgan calls it "perhaps the most influential book written in the twentieth century on the history of slavery". [source: CAS]
- Sven Beckert (Harvard; co-director of the Program on the Study of Capitalism), *Empire of Cotton: A Global History* (2014): 2015 Bancroft Prize, Pulitzer finalist. "War capitalism". *Capitalism: A Global History* (2025). [source: SB]
- Reviews of Beckert's *Capitalism: A Global History* (2025) describe the book this way:
  - It argues capitalism was born global rather than spreading out from Europe.
  - It opens around 1150 with the merchants of Aden in Yemen.
  - It treats the last third of the nineteenth century as "the most monumental turning point in the global history of capitalism".
  - It sees capitalism as man-made, always resisted, and imposed by violence and law.
  - Critics call it light on theory.
  [source: WebSearch: https://www.historytoday.com/archive/review/capitalism-global-history-sven-beckert-review ; https://jacobin.com/2025/12/beckert-capitalism-global-history-review] Publisher not confirmed. The quoted phrase comes from review summaries; check the book before quoting it.
- Marx on colonial origins (vol. I, ch. 31, "The Genesis of the Industrial Capitalist"). In the standard English translation, the discovery of American gold and silver, the enslavement and entombment in mines of the Indigenous population, the conquest of the East Indies and Africa turned into "a warren for the commercial hunting of black-skins" signalled "the rosy dawn of the era of capitalist production". [source: WebSearch: https://historyofeconomicthought.mcmaster.ca/marx/cap1/chap31] Translations vary; use the short phrase "rosy dawn of the era of capitalist production" only.
- The published Slavery and Atlantic Slave Trade chapters cover Williams, Drescher, Davis, Beckles, Baptist, Olmstead and Tomich's "second slavery" in full. Cross-refer; do not repeat.

## 8. Industrial capitalism, class formation and labour
- E. P. Thompson, *The Making of the English Working Class* (Victor Gollancz, 1963; revised Pelican edition 1968). Thompson was ranked 30th on the Modern Library's 100 best non-fiction books of the twentieth century and second only to Braudel in a 2011 *History Today* poll of historians. The preface's "enormous condescension of posterity" is already quoted in published chapters. [source: MEWC; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._P._Thompson]
- Fordism: Gramsci popularised the term in "Americanism and Fordism" (1934, *Prison Notebooks*). It meant the moving assembly line, standardisation and the mass market. [source: FORD]
- Beverly Silver, *Forces of Labor: Workers' Movements and Globalization since 1870* (2003): as car production moved from Detroit and Flint to Turin, then to Brazil, South Africa and South Korea in search of cheap, disciplined labour, it recreated militant labour movements in each new place. Strategic strikes ran from Flint (1936–37) to Turin (1969–70) to São Paulo and Ulsan in the 1980s. [source: WebSearch: https://libcom.org/library/aufheben-review-beverley-silvers-forces-labor] (Secondary summaries; the slogan "where capital goes, conflict follows" is NOT verified, see below.)
- **Deindustrialisation.** The US Rust Belt (formerly the Steel or Factory Belt) declined from the 1950s–60s onward. Its cities include Pittsburgh, Detroit, Cleveland, Buffalo and Baltimore. Bethlehem Steel ended most operations in 1982, went bankrupt in 2001 and dissolved in 2003. [source: RUST; DEIND]
- Some economists find that much of the fall in manufacturing's share of GDP in rich countries (1995–2014) reflects relative prices: services became dearer than goods. [source: DEIND]
- Dani Rodrik, "Premature Deindustrialization", *Journal of Economic Growth* 21:1 (March 2016), pp. 1–33 (NBER w20935, 2015). Developing countries now reach peak industrialisation (by employment or output share) at much lower incomes than early industrialisers. Latin America has been especially hard hit, while Asian manufactures exporters have been largely insulated. Causes: globalisation and labour-saving technology. Rodrik credits the term to Dasgupta and Singh (2006). [source: WebSearch: https://drodrik.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/premature-deindustrialization ; https://www.nber.org/papers/w20935]

## 9. The labour capitalism doesn't count
- "Informal income opportunities": Keith Hart, Ghana study of 1971 (published 1973), and the ILO's Kenya report of 1972. Hernando de Soto, *El otro sendero* (1986; English *The Other Path*, 1989) blamed over-regulation. [source: INF]
- Informal employment as a share of non-agricultural employment: 58.7% in the Middle East and North Africa, 64.6% in Latin America, 79.4% in Asia and 80.4% in sub-Saharan Africa. Above 90% in India and many sub-Saharan countries if agriculture is included. Home-based workers are the most numerous; street vendors the most visible. (No date given; older ILO data.) [source: INF]
- ILO global figures: 2 billion workers, 61% of employed people (2016 estimate). About 58% (58.2%), nearly 2 billion, in the 2023 update using 2019 data; 89% in low-income countries. A 2026 ILO report (secondary coverage only) gives more than 2.1 billion of 3.6 billion workers, about 60%. [source: WebSearch: https://www.wiego.org/?p=32772 ; https://janataweekly.org/more-than-2-1-billion-of-worlds-3-6-billion-workers-are-in-the-informal-economy/] Safest: "about 2 billion people, close to three in five of the world's workers (ILO)".
- **Online gig work.** World Bank, *Working Without Borders: The Promise and Peril of Online Gig Work* (2023): 154 to 435 million online gig workers, 4.4–12.5% of the global labour force. The low figure is registered platform workers (web data); the high one includes secondary and marginal workers (survey). [source: WebSearch: https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/ebc4a7e2-85c6-467b-8713-e2d77e954c6c]
- US: 16% of adults had earned gig income as of 2021 (Pew). [source: GIG]
- **Wages for Housework.** Started in 1972 by Mariarosa Dalla Costa, Silvia Federici, Brigitte Galtier and Selma James. Dalla Costa and James, *The Power of Women and the Subversion of the Community*, opened the "domestic labour debate". Lotta Femminista operated in Padua, the Power of Women Collective in London and Bristol, and Federici's Wages for Housework Committee in Brooklyn (1975). Black Women for Wages for Housework (Margaret Prescod, Wilmette Brown) and Wages Due Lesbians formed in 1974–76. [source: WFH]
- Federici (born Parma, April 1942) published the pamphlet *Wages Against Housework* in 1975 (Power of Women Collective and Falling Wall Press; first edition April 1975). Its argument: domestic work is presented as love rather than work. She taught at the University of Port Harcourt, Nigeria, 1984–86. *Caliban and the Witch: Women, the Body and Primitive Accumulation* (2004). [source: FED; WFH; WebSearch https://catalogue.maydayrooms.org/items/show/340]
- Oxfam and the Institute for Women's Policy Research value unpaid care work at nearly $11 trillion a year. [source: WFH]
- In India in the 2020s several states made unconditional cash transfers to women, justified in some states as wages for housework. As of 2025 the BBC estimated 118 million women in twelve states were receiving them. [source: WFH] A useful global, present-day link.

## 10. Gold standard, first globalisation, collapse
- Britain slid onto a de facto gold standard in 1717, when Isaac Newton at the Mint overvalued gold, and formalised it after the Great Recoinage of 1816. Until 1850 only Britain and a few colonies were on gold. [source: GS]
- The international classical gold standard ran from 1873 to 1914. Germany switched from silver to the gold mark (laws of 4 December 1871 and 9 July 1873), funded by the 5 billion francs France paid after 1871. Others followed: the US effectively in 1879 (Eichengreen; formally in 1900), India's rupee pegged in 1893, Japan in 1897 (on reserves won in the 1894–95 war with China), Russia in 1897, the Philippines in 1903 and Mexico in 1905. Japan saw gold as vital for access to Western capital. [source: GS]
- In 1925 Britain returned to gold at the pre-war $4.86, Churchill's decision as Chancellor; critics link it to unemployment and the 1926 General Strike. [source: GS]
- Keynes, *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919), pictured the pre-1914 "inhabitant of London" ordering "by telephone, sipping his morning tea, the various products of the whole earth". [source: GLOB] (The Globalization chapter already uses this passage; cross-refer.)
- Great Depression (1929–39): world GDP fell an estimated 15% in 1929–32 and US GDP about 30%. International trade fell by more than 50% (in value: see KNOWN_ISSUES). US unemployment reached 25%; in some countries it reached 33%. [source: GD] The Interwar chapter covers the Depression, including Chile and Brazil; cross-refer.
- Soviet alternative: NEP adopted at the 10th Party Congress (March 1921) and abandoned in 1928; five-year plans from 1928. The first three plans' growth (1928–40) coincided with the Depression elsewhere. [source: NEP; SOV] Lenin used "state capitalism" positively in 1918 and under the NEP. [source: STC]

## 11. Keynes, Bretton Woods and embedded liberalism
- Keynes (5 June 1883 – 21 April 1946): *A Tract on Monetary Reform* (1923), *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (early 1936). "Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren" was a lecture published in 1930, collected in 1931; it foresaw a world rich enough that the love of money would lose its hold. Keynes popularised the phrase "technological unemployment", calling it "only a temporary phase of maladjustment". [source: JMK; TU]
- Bretton Woods: the United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference, 1–22 July 1944, at the Mount Washington Hotel, New Hampshire, with 730 delegates from 44 Allied nations. Keynes's International Clearing Union and "bancor" lost to Harry Dexter White's more conservative plan. [source: BWC; JMK]
- "Embedded liberalism": John Ruggie (1982), building on Polanyi. It was a compromise between open trade and domestic stability, embodied in Bretton Woods. Growth in the 1950s–60s was "more rapid than before or since"; the system broke down in the 1970s. [source: EL; GT]
- 15 August 1971: Nixon suspended dollar–gold convertibility (with a wage-price freeze and an import surcharge). Floating rates had de facto replaced Bretton Woods by 1973. [source: NIX] The Neoliberal Age chapter covers this; say "effectively ended".

## 12. The neoliberal turn, China, and 2008
- Hayek (8 May 1899 – 23 March 1992): "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945), on prices as signals carrying dispersed knowledge. Shared the 1974 economics Nobel with Gunnar Myrdal. [source: FH] *The Road to Serfdom*, Mont Pèlerin, Thatcher and Reagan are all in the Neoliberal Age chapter; cross-refer.
- "Washington Consensus": John Williamson, 1989, a ten-point package. [source: WC]
- "Socialism with Chinese characteristics": first used by Deng Xiaoping on 1 September 1982 at the 12th Party Congress: "We must integrate the universal truth of Marxism with the concrete realities of China, and blaze a path of our own and build Socialism with Chinese Characteristics." It rests on the doctrine of the "primary stage of socialism". [source: SCC]
- Reform and opening (from December 1978): decollectivisation; special economic zones (Shenzhen, later Pudong); the dual-track price system; the 1985 easing of price controls. Stock exchanges at Shanghai and Shenzhen (1990); WTO entry in 2001. Extreme poverty fell by 800 million between 1978 and 2018; China became the leading manufacturing power. The state kept monopolies over the "commanding heights" (banking, petroleum). [source: ROU] The China Today chapter covers this; cross-refer.
- Isabella Weber (born 1987; University of Massachusetts Amherst), *How China Escaped Shock Therapy: The Market Reform Debate*. She argues that gradual dual-track pricing, not a big bang, shaped China's reform. [source: IW] (Publication year not confirmed; see NOT VERIFIED.)
- State capitalism: Engels (1880) saw state ownership as a final stage of capitalism, not its abolition. Lenin and Bukharin applied the idea to wartime economies. Commentators apply the label to China and Singapore today. [source: STC]
- **2008.** The subprime crisis began in early 2007. Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy on 15 September 2008, the largest in US history. Congress passed the $700 billion TARP on 3 October. About 8.7 million US jobs were lost, and unemployment went from 5% (2007) to 10% (October 2009). Global losses exceeded $2 trillion. [source: GFC] The US recession ran from December 2007 to June 2009. 2009 was the only global recession year by the IMF definition. China, India and Indonesia kept growing substantially. [source: GR]
- Financialisation: Greta Krippner's definition, "pattern of accumulation in which profit making occurs increasingly through financial channels rather than through trade and commodity production". Gerald Epstein (2005): "the increasing importance of financial markets, financial motives, financial institutions, and financial elites". [source: FIN]
- Adam Tooze, *Crashed: How a Decade of Financial Crises Changed the World* (2018). [source: TOOZE]

## 13. Varieties and the developmental state
- Peter A. Hall and David Soskice (eds), *Varieties of Capitalism: The Institutional Foundations of Comparative Advantage* (2001). They distinguish liberal market economies (US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Ireland) from coordinated market economies (Germany, Japan, Sweden, Austria; France is listed too in Wikipedia's summary). A further type, "Mediterranean capitalism", was later identified. Colin Crouch criticised the scheme: "actors seem to exist in an iron cage of institutions". Others add "dependent" and "hierarchical" market economies outside the West. [source: VOCAP]
- Chalmers Johnson (1931 – 20 November 2010), *MITI and the Japanese Miracle* (Stanford University Press, 1982), coined "developmental state": a state focused on development, with far-sighted bureaucrats (MITI) steering industry. Alice Amsden extended the analysis to South Korea and other late industrialisers ("getting prices wrong"). Singapore under the PAP is another case. [source: CJ; DEV]

## 14. Platform and digital capitalism
- Nick Srnicek coined "platform capitalism" in his book of that name (Polity). Google, Facebook, Apple, Microsoft, Uber, Airbnb and Amazon operate as platforms for others' business. [source: PLAT; SRN] (Wikipedia's citation is 2017; the book is often dated 2016. Say 2016–17 or omit the year.)
- Shoshana Zuboff, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism: The Fight for a Human Future at the New Frontier of Power* (Profile Books, 2019). Google and Facebook invented "a new logic of accumulation" from behavioural data. Vincent Mosco used "surveillance capitalism" in 2014. [source: SURV; ZUB] The Digital Age chapter gives Zuboff and Doctorow's reply; cross-refer.

## 15. Inequality and Piketty
- Thomas Piketty (born 7 May 1971, Clichy), *Le Capital au XXIe siècle* (Seuil, 2013: Wikipedia gives August in one place and September in another). The English translation by Arthur Goldhammer (Belknap/Harvard) appeared in March or April 2014 (both given). It reached no. 1 on the NYT hardcover non-fiction list on 18 May 2014 and sold over 2.5 million copies by the end of 2017. [source: C21]
- Thesis: when the rate of return on capital (r) exceeds growth (g) over the long run, wealth concentrates. The wars, the Depression and policy reversed inequality between 1930 and 1975; the world is returning to "patrimonial capitalism". Remedy: a progressive global wealth tax of up to 2% a year and top income tax rates up to 80%, which Piketty himself called "politically impossible". In late 2014 he said r > g was not the only or main tool for explaining inequality. [source: C21]
- Critics:
  - Lawrence Summers: diminishing returns to capital.
  - Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson: institutions matter more, using the cases of Sweden and South Africa.
  - Matthew Rognlie (2015): depreciation, and housing drives most of the rise in capital's share.
  - Chris Giles (*Financial Times*, 23 May 2014): "unexplained errors" in the wealth data; Piketty rebutted.
  - Martin Wolf: the book assumes inequality matters without explaining why.
  [source: C21]
- Admirers: Paul Krugman, "the most important economics book of the year—and maybe of the decade". Branko Milanović: "one of the watershed books in economic thinking". [source: C21]
- Piketty's later books: *Capital and Ideology* (2019) and *A Brief History of Equality* (2022). [source: TP]
- World Inequality Reports: the first (*WIR 2018*) was released 14 December 2017 by Alvaredo, Chancel, Piketty, Saez and Zucman; the second came out in 2021 (*WIR 2022*). [source: WID]
- **World Inequality Report 2026** (third edition), published 9 December 2025 by the World Inequality Lab (Paris School of Economics). Coordinators reported as Lucas Chancel, Ricardo Gómez-Carrera, Rowaida Moshrif and Thomas Piketty. Findings as reported by press and summaries:
  - The richest 10% receive about 53% of global income and own about three-quarters (~75%) of global wealth.
  - The bottom half holds about 2% of wealth and gets under 10% of income (some outlets say about 8%).
  - The top 1% hold about 37% of wealth.
  - The richest 0.001% hold about three times the wealth of the poorest half.
  - The top 10% account for 77% of carbon emissions and the poorest half for 3%.
  [source: WebSearch: https://aljazeera.com/news/2025/12/10/where-in-the-world-are-wealth-and-income-most-unequal ; https://www.storyboard18.com/how-it-works/worlds-richest-10-take-over-half-of-global-income-says-world-inequality-report-87155.htm ; https://dce.visionias.in/news-today/2025-12-11/economics-(indian-economy)/world-inequality-report-2026-released] The report's own site (wir2026.wid.world) could not be reached from this session. Use the round figures (53% income, ~75% wealth, ~2% for the bottom half), attributed to "the World Inequality Report 2026", and avoid the finer numbers.

## 16. The contested future
- **Climate.** Andreas Malm (born 1977, Lund) coined "Capitalocene" in 2009. *Fossil Capital* (Verso, 2016; Deutscher Memorial Prize) argues that cotton masters switched to steam for profit and control of labour. Malm dates the climate crisis from fossil capital after about 1830. Jason W. Moore's "Cheap Nature" and capitalist world-ecology date it, according to Wikipedia, to the "long seventeenth century (c. 1550–1700)" (see conflicts). [source: CAPC; MALM]
- Weber's own fossil-fuel image: the modern economic order will determine lives "until the last ton of fossilized coal is burnt" (Parsons translation). [source: PE]
- **Degrowth.** It criticises GDP growth as a measure of progress. Hickel and Kallis argue that green growth lacks empirical support, because absolute decoupling is not happening fast enough for 1.5–2 °C; the European Environmental Bureau (2021) agreed. Critics (e.g. Rikard Warlenius) say the concept is vague, ignores rebound effects, has little political support and would deepen poverty. Alternatives: green growth and "agrowth". [source: DG]
- **Deglobalisation.** The first wave ran from 1914 to 1945 (Smoot–Hawley 1930, imperial preference). A second wave dates from 2008 (the US–China trade war of 2018–20, COVID-19 supply shocks). [source: DEGL] The Globalization chapter covers this; cross-refer. Treat events of 2025–26 cautiously.
- **Automation and AI.** Frey and Osborne (Oxford Martin School, 2013) estimated that 47% of US jobs were at high risk of automation; Bruegel (2014) gave 54% for the EU-28. Critics say such studies ignore new jobs. Debates about technological unemployment peaked in the 1930s and 1960s and faded without being settled. [source: TU]

## Debates
- **What is capitalism, and when did it start?** Three broad answers:
  - Commerce and markets (Smith; Sweezy's "commercialisation model"; Braudel's and Arrighi's finance-led cycles; Banaji's commercial capitalism from the eleventh century).
  - Social-property relations and wage labour (Marx's "capitalist mode of production"; Brenner and Wood's English agrarian origin; Polanyi's 1834).
  - Culture (Weber's Protestant ethic, Sombart's "spirit").
  Kocka offers an eclectic working definition (decentralisation, commodification, accumulation). [source: CAP; BD; PE; KOC; Banaji refs above]
- **Brenner debate.** Class structure (Brenner) against demography (Postan, Le Roy Ladurie), against a rival Marxism (Bois), against peasant "prime movers" (Hilton, Byres). [source: BD]
- **Eurocentrism.** The "sprouts of capitalism" school in China and Korea; Song commercialisation; Islamic partnerships; Indian Ocean credit (*hundi*). Beckert (2025) argues capitalism was "born global", and the California School and the Great Divergence (Pomeranz 2000) answer European-exceptionalist accounts. Broadberry's wage data cut the other way, finding England ahead of the Yangzi delta in silver wages by 1600. [source: SPR; SONG; COM; HUN; GDIV; Beckert reviews]
- **Institutions and trust.** Greif's Maghribi "coalition" against Edwards and Ogilvie, and Goldberg; Bernstein's partial defence. [source: Maghribi refs above]
- **Market versus capitalism.** Braudel separated the transparent competitive market from monopolistic capitalism "at the top"; most economists treat them as one. [source: CAP]
- **Slavery and capitalism.** Williams's thesis and its critics (Drescher, Davis); Beckert's "war capitalism"; Baptist's thesis disputed by economic historians. This is covered in the published Slavery chapter; keep consistent with it. [source: CAS; SB]
- **Polanyi's double movement.** Society protects itself against disembedded markets. Ruggie applied it to the postwar order and Stiglitz to shock therapy. Critics: McCloskey and North on his history of markets; Bregman on the Speenhamland "myths"; others say he underplays class and power. [source: GT; EL]
- **The developmental state against neoliberal orthodoxy.** Johnson (MITI) and Amsden against the Washington Consensus. China's gradualism (Weber) against shock therapy. [source: CJ; DEV; WC; IW]
- **Varieties of capitalism.** LMEs against CMEs (Hall and Soskice); Crouch's "iron cage" critique; extensions to dependent and hierarchical market economies. [source: VOCAP]
- **Piketty.** r > g and patrimonial capitalism, against Summers, Acemoglu and Robinson, Rognlie (housing) and Giles (data). [source: C21]
- **Surveillance capitalism versus monopoly.** Zuboff's "new logic of accumulation" against Doctorow's view that the problem is monopoly, not mind control (covered in the Digital Age chapter). [source: SURV]
- **Deindustrialisation.** Rich-country manufacturing decline driven by relative prices and productivity (benign) or by trade and offshoring. Rodrik's "premature deindustrialisation" in the Global South. Silver's argument that labour militancy follows capital as it relocates. [source: DEIND; Rodrik; Silver refs]
- **Informality.** De Soto (regulation pushes people into informality; celebrate the informal entrepreneur) against critics of his methods and normative bias. [source: INF]
- **Wages for housework.** Recognition and payment of unpaid care against the worry that it would reinforce gender roles or commodify care. [source: WFH]
- **Climate.** Anthropocene against Capitalocene (Malm: fossil capital after 1830; Moore: earlier "Cheap Nature"). Green growth and decoupling against degrowth (Hickel, Kallis) and its critics. [source: CAPC; DG]
- **Automation.** High-risk estimates (Frey and Osborne 47%) against compensation effects and new jobs (Chapman). [source: TU]
