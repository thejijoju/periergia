## 1. Ireland: Home Rule and the Ulster crisis (brief; the Nationalism chapter has the 19th century)
- The Third Home Rule Bill was introduced by Asquith in April 1912. The Parliament Act 1911 meant the Lords could only delay it for up to two years. It received royal assent on 18 September 1914 together with the Suspensory Act, which postponed it for the duration of the war, "with the exclusion of some or all of Ulster still to be decided". [source: PI-IRL]
- Lord Randolph Churchill (1886): "the Orange card is the one to play". The slogan "Home Rule means Rome Rule" expressed the same fear. Protestant unionists feared industrial decline and religious persecution under a Catholic-dominated Irish government. [source: PI-IRL]
- The Ulster Covenant was signed on and before 28 September 1912 ("Ulster Day"): 237,368 men signed the Covenant and 234,046 women signed a separate Declaration, nearly 500,000 in all (PI-IRL says "more than 500,000"; use "nearly half a million"). Carson signed first at Belfast City Hall. [source: UC; PI-IRL]
- Unionists formed the Ulster Volunteers. The Larne gun-running (April 1914) landed 25,000 rifles and three million rounds from Germany. Nationalists formed the Irish Volunteers, who ran guns at Howth (July 1914). In the Curragh incident (20 March 1914) many senior British Army officers in Ireland threatened to resign rather than act against the Ulster Volunteers. "Ireland seemed to be on the brink of civil war." [source: PI-IRL]
- The 1911 census showed Catholic majorities in five of Ulster's nine counties. Two of them, Tyrone and Fermanagh, were later put into Northern Ireland. [source: PI-IRL]
- The number of excluded counties varied over time: nine (Gladstone era; Carson in 1912), three (Churchill, April 1912), four (Bonar Law), and six (Lloyd George, May 1916). The Ulster Unionist Council backed six in May 1920. [source: PI-IRL]
- Lloyd George to Carson, 29 May 1916: "We must make it clear that at the end of the provisional period Ulster does not, whether she wills it or not, merge in the rest of Ireland." Redmond had been told, by contrast, that exclusion would be temporary and that excluded counties could vote. "A vote was never held." [source: PI-IRL]

## 2. 1916, the War of Independence and the Government of Ireland Act
- The Easter Rising began on Easter Monday, 24 April 1916, and lasted six days. It was organised by the IRB's seven-man Military Council. The Irish Volunteers under Patrick Pearse took part, with James Connolly's Irish Citizen Army and about 200 women of Cumann na mBan. Pearse surrendered on Saturday 29 April. [source: ER]
- Deaths: 485 in all (Glasnevin Trust count). They were 260 civilians (about 54%), 143 British military and police, and 82 rebels, including the 16 executed. More than 2,600 were wounded. Most civilian casualties were caused by the British Army's artillery and machine guns. About 40 of the dead were children. [source: ER]
- About 3,500 people were taken prisoner and 1,800 sent to camps or prisons in Britain. Fourteen leaders were shot at Kilmainham Gaol between 3 and 12 May, including all seven signatories of the Proclamation. Sixteen were executed in all; Roger Casement was hanged at Pentonville on 3 August. Éamon de Valera escaped execution. [source: ER]
- The Proclamation's wording on equal citizenship is quoted in the Quotations section. Note that "children of the nation" means all Irish people, not children. [source: PROC]
- In the December 1918 general election Sinn Féin won 73 of Ireland's 105 seats. Its MPs boycotted Westminster, formed Dáil Éireann and declared independence on 21 January 1919. [source: ER; IWI]
- The War of Independence ran from 21 January 1919 (Soloheadbeg ambush) to the truce of 11 July 1921. It was a guerrilla war between the IRA and the British Army, the RIC, the Black and Tans, the Auxiliaries and the Ulster Special Constabulary (USC). Bloody Sunday in Dublin, 21 November 1920: the IRA killed 14 British intelligence operatives, then the RIC fired on a Croke Park crowd and killed 14 civilians. Kilmichael (17 Auxiliaries killed) followed, and in December the centre of Cork was burnt by British forces. Total deaths were about 2,300: about 491 IRA, 936 British forces and police, and about 900 civilians. [source: IWI]
- In the north-east the conflict was sectarian. In summer 1920 loyalists drove 8,000 "disloyal" workers out of the Belfast shipyards, all Catholics or Protestant labour activists. From 1920 to 1922 more than 500 people were killed in what became Northern Ireland (TT gives 557, mostly Catholics), and more than 10,000 became refugees, most of them Catholics. The almost wholly Protestant USC carried out some reprisal killings of Catholics. [source: PI-IRL; NI; TT]
- The Long Committee (from September 1919) planned the legislation. It was unionist in outlook, and James Craig's circle were the only Irishmen consulted. The committee first proposed a nine-county Ulster, but most northern unionists wanted six counties for a safer majority. Remaining Ulster counties: Cavan 81.5% Catholic, Donegal 78.9%, Monaghan 74.7%. Charles Craig MP, 29 March 1920, put a nine-county Unionist majority at "about three or four" against about ten in a six-county parliament. [source: PI-IRL]
- The Government of Ireland Act 1920 passed the Commons on 11 November 1920, received royal assent in December 1920 and came into force on 3 May 1921 (this is the legal moment of partition). It created two home-rule parliaments within the UK, Northern Ireland (six counties) and Southern Ireland, plus a Council of Ireland. Elections were held on 24 May 1921. The Northern parliament first met on 7 June 1921 under James Craig, and George V opened it on 22 June. Sinn Féin treated the southern election as an election to the Dáil, and the Southern parliament met only once, attended by four unionists. [source: PI-IRL; GIA]
- In the 1921 election Fermanagh–Tyrone voted 54.7% nationalist to 45.3% unionist. Tyrone and Fermanagh county councils declared allegiance to the Dáil (November–December 1921) and were dissolved by the Northern government. [source: PI-IRL]
- Nationalist MP Thomas Harbison, 11 November 1920, said Ulster nationalists were to be "cut off from the rest of Ireland and thrown into eternal slavery by another system of jerrymandering". [source: PI-IRL]

## 3. The Treaty, the Boundary Commission and the Civil War
- The Anglo-Irish Treaty was signed on 6 December 1921 after negotiations from October 1921. The British side included Lloyd George, Churchill, Austen Chamberlain and Birkenhead; Griffith and Collins led the Irish side. It created the Irish Free State as a self-governing dominion, with an Oath of Allegiance including fidelity to George V. [source: PI-IRL; AIT]
- The Dáil approved it on 7 January 1922 by 64 to 57. De Valera resigned and Griffith replaced him (60 to 58). The main dispute was dominion status and the Oath rather than partition, but partition was "a significant matter for dissent". De Valera's alternative was "Document No. 2". [source: AIT; PI-IRL]
- Article 12 let Northern Ireland opt out. On 7 December 1922, the day after the Free State came into being, the Northern parliament voted to opt out. [source: PI-IRL; IBC]
- Article 12 also set up a Boundary Commission to fix the border "in accordance with the wishes of the inhabitants, so far as may be compatible with economic and geographic conditions". Free State leaders expected to gain Fermanagh, Tyrone, south Armagh, south Down and Derry City, leaving the North unviable. Griffith: "under clause twelve we'll get at least two of the six counties". [source: PI-IRL]
- The commission did not meet until November 1924. Its chair was Justice Richard Feetham (South Africa), with Eoin MacNeill for the Free State. Joseph R. Fisher, a Belfast unionist editor, was appointed by London for Northern Ireland after Belfast refused to name anyone. Feetham ruled out plebiscites. The report recommended only small transfers in both directions and was leaked to the *Morning Post* on 7 November 1925. MacNeill resigned. [source: IBC; PI-IRL]
- On 3 December 1925 the three governments (Cosgrave, Craig, Baldwin) suppressed the report and confirmed the existing border. The Free State was released from its share of the UK national debt (the British claim was £157 million). The Dáil approved by 71 to 20. The report was not published until 1969. De Valera said the Free State had "sold Ulster natives for four pound a head, to clear a debt we did not owe." [source: IBC; PI-IRL]
- The Civil War ran from 28 June 1922 (shelling of the anti-Treaty garrison in the Four Courts, with British-lent 18-pounders) to 24 May 1923 ("dump arms" order). The pro-Treaty National Army won, helped by British weapons. Collins was killed at Béal na Bláth in August 1922, ten days after Griffith's death from a brain haemorrhage. [source: ICW]
- Atrocity and execution: the Free State officially executed 81 Republican prisoners (Erskine Childers on 24 November 1922). After the killing of TD Seán Hales, four Republican leaders were shot on 8 December 1922. At Ballyseedy nine prisoners were tied to a landmine and eight died. About 12,000 Republicans were imprisoned. [source: ICW]
- Civil War deaths: the 2023 UCC Irish Civil War Fatalities Project counts 637 pro-Treaty and 426 anti-Treaty dead. Total combatant and civilian deaths are 1,426 in the Free State and 1,485 for the island. A 2012 study gave just under 2,000. Older figures of up to 4,000 are "generally accepted to be too high". Say "roughly 1,500–2,000 dead". [source: ICW]
- Legacy: Fine Gael descends from the pro-Treaty side; Fianna Fáil and Sinn Féin descend from the anti-Treaty side. [source: ICW]
- De Valera's 1937 Constitution claimed "the whole island of Ireland" as the national territory (Articles 2 and 3). The claim was removed by the Nineteenth Amendment in 1998 under the Good Friday Agreement. [source: PI-IRL]
- The heads of government north and south did not meet again until Lemass and O'Neill in 1965. [source: PI-IRL]

## 4. Northern Ireland as a sectarian state
- Unionist governments ruled without a break for 50 years (1921–72). Every prime minister and almost every minister belonged to the Orange Order, as did all but 11 of 149 UUP MPs. Almost all judges were Protestant. The RUC and the USC ("B-Specials") were almost wholly Protestant. Catholics were initially about 35% of the population. [source: NI; TT]
- The Special Powers Act 1922 allowed internment without trial, flogging, search without warrant and bans on publications and meetings. It "was used almost exclusively against nationalists". [source: TT]
- The Local Government Act (NI) 1922 allowed boundaries to be redrawn. This was used to gerrymander nationalist-majority Derry City, Enniskillen, Omagh, Armagh and other areas. Plural voting and a ratepayer franchise in local elections lasted until 1969. Discrimination in housing, public jobs and policing showed "a consistent and irrefutable pattern of deliberate discrimination against Catholics" (NI, quoting its source). Caveat (NI): some unionists argue that discrimination also had socio-economic and geographic causes, not only bigotry. [source: NI; PI-IRL]
- Craig's "Protestant Parliament and a Protestant State" (April 1934) is in the Quotations section. It is often misquoted as "a Protestant parliament for a Protestant people". He did say "a Protestant Government for a Protestant people" on 21 November 1934. [source: CRAIG]
- Craig privately in 1938: "In this island we cannot live always separated from one another... The change will not come in my time but it will come." [source: PI-IRL] (Writer: treat as reported, cited by PI-IRL.)
- The Ireland Act 1949 confirmed that partition would last as long as a majority in Northern Ireland wanted it, which Dublin called the "Unionist Veto". [source: PI-IRL]

## 5. The Troubles and the Good Friday Agreement
- NICRA (formed 1967) campaigned against discrimination in housing, jobs, policing and the franchise, modelled on the US civil rights movement. Caledon squatting protest, 20 June 1968: a house had been given to an unmarried 19-year-old Protestant secretary rather than to large Catholic families. On 5 October 1968 the RUC beat marchers in Derry on camera. [source: TT; NI]
- The August 1969 riots led to the deployment of British troops (Operation Banner, 14–15 August 1969), the British Army's longest operation. [source: TT]
- Internment was introduced in 1971; of the first 350 detainees none were Protestant. From 1971 to 1975, 1,981 people were interned: 1,874 Catholic/republican and 107 Protestant/loyalist. [source: TT]
- Bloody Sunday, Derry, 30 January 1972: soldiers of 1 Para shot 26 unarmed civilians. Thirteen died that day and a fourteenth months later. The Widgery Tribunal was widely called a whitewash. The Saville Inquiry (set up 1998, reported 2010) found the shootings "unjustified" and "unjustifiable". In 2025 "Soldier F" was tried and found not guilty. [source: BS; TT]
- **Death toll (give as a range):** CAIN counts 3,532 killed between 1969 and 2001 (3,489 up to 1998). *Lost Lives* (2006 edition) counts 3,720 between 1966 and 2006. NI's narrative says 3,254, with over 50,000 casualties. Use "about 3,500 (counts range from roughly 3,250 to 3,700)". By the CAIN breakdown, about 52% of the dead were civilians, 32% security forces and 16% paramilitaries. Republicans caused about 60% of deaths, loyalists 30% and security forces 10%. Loyalists killed 48% of the civilians, republicans 39% and the security forces 10%. [source: TT; NI]
- The Good Friday (Belfast) Agreement was signed on 10 April 1998 by the British and Irish governments and eight Northern Ireland parties. Talks were chaired by US envoy George Mitchell, with Blair, Ahern and Mo Mowlam. The DUP was the only major party to oppose it. It brought power-sharing, the principle of consent, North–South bodies and police reform. "Constructive ambiguity" postponed decommissioning. [source: GFA]
- Referendums on 22 May 1998: in Northern Ireland 71% voted yes on an 81% turnout (almost all Catholics, but only 57% of Protestants); in the Republic 94% voted yes on a 56% turnout. The UK and Irish governments' treaty came into force on 2 December 1999. [source: GFA]
- The IRA announced the end of its campaign in July 2005 and completed decommissioning on 26 September 2005. Paisley (DUP) and McGuinness (Sinn Féin) led a power-sharing executive from May 2007. [source: GFA]
- The 1973 border poll was boycotted by nationalists (57% turnout). Brexit revived the border question through the "Irish Sea border". [source: PI-IRL; GFA]

## 6. India: the road to 1947
- In 1939 Viceroy Linlithgow declared war on India's behalf without consulting Indian leaders, and the Congress ministries resigned. The League celebrated "Deliverance Day" and backed the war. [source: PI]
- **Lahore Resolution** (later called the Pakistan Resolution), adopted on 23 March 1940 at the League's session in Lahore (22–24 March, Iqbal Park), presented by A. K. Fazlul Huq. It called for "independent states" in the Muslim-majority north-western and eastern zones. The name "Pakistan" had been coined by Choudhary Rahmat Ali in 1933. [source: LR; PI]
- Jinnah's Lahore speech set out the two-nation theory; the wording is in the Quotations section. [source: TNT]
- **Cripps Mission**, late March 1942 (Cripps landed in Delhi on 22 March). It offered dominion status after the war, with a clause that no province would be forced into the dominion. The League rejected it as falling short of Pakistan, and Congress rejected it partly because of that clause. US pressure (Lend-Lease) lay behind it. [source: CM; PI]
- **Quit India**: launched at the AICC's Bombay session on 8 August 1942 (Gowalia Tank Maidan), with Gandhi's call to "Do or Die". The leaders were jailed until 1945, which left the League free to organise. Jinnah: "The war which nobody welcomed proved to be a blessing in disguise." British count: 1,028 killed; Congress estimates 4,000–10,000. [source: QI; PI] (On the number arrested, see the conflict note in the overlap section above.)
- Attlee's Labour government (1945) decided to end the Raj. In early 1947 Britain announced it would transfer power no later than June 1948. [source: PI]
- **1946 elections**: Congress won 923 of 1,585 provincial seats and 90–91% of the general (non-Muslim) seats or vote. The League won 425–429 of 492 Muslim seats (about 87%), including 113 of 119 in Bengal, and every Muslim seat in the central assembly. The franchise was property-restricted. [source: E46; PI]
- **Cabinet Mission** (1946) proposed a three-group federation with a weak centre. The League accepted; Nehru's speech of 10 July 1946 "effectively torpedoed" the plan (PI, quoting its source). [source: PI; CAB]
- **Direct Action Day**, 16 August 1946. Jinnah had warned: "We will either have a divided India or a destroyed India." In the Great Calcutta Killing more than 4,000 died and 100,000 were left homeless within 72 hours (infobox 4,000–10,000, Hindus and Muslims). It was followed by Noakhali (October 1946, Muslims attacking Hindus), Bihar (October–November 1946, Hindus attacking Muslims), Garhmukteshwar, and Rawalpindi (March 1947, Muslims attacking Hindus and Sikhs). The historian Yasmin Khan says Chief Minister Suhrawardy "if he did not explicitly incite violence certainly gave the crowd the impression that they could act with impunity". [source: DAD; PI]
- Mountbatten was appointed the last viceroy, arriving in March 1947, with a deadline of 30 June 1948 and instructions to avoid partition. He concluded that partition was needed for a quicker transfer of power. **The 3 June Plan**: at a press conference on 3 June 1947 he announced partition and brought independence forward to mid-August 1947. Punjab and Bengal assemblies would vote on dividing their provinces, and the NWFP and Sylhet would hold referendums. Separate independence for Bengal was ruled out. The Indian Independence Act passed on 18 July 1947. [source: PI; RL; IIA]
- Mountbatten "had accepted the post as Viceroy on the condition of an early deadline". [source: RL]
- Patel to the AICC: "the choice is between one division and many divisions… de facto Pakistan already exists in the Punjab and Bengal… I would prefer a de jure Pakistan". [source: PI]
- Pakistan came into being on 14 August 1947 with Jinnah as Governor-General, and India on 15 August with Nehru as Prime Minister. Mountbatten stayed on as India's Governor-General until June 1948. [source: PI]

## 7. The Radcliffe Line
- Sir Cyril Radcliffe, a London barrister who "had never visited India", arrived on 8 July 1947 and "was given just five weeks". He chaired both boundary commissions (Punjab and Bengal), each with two Congress and two League nominees. The judges split two-and-two on all major issues, leaving Radcliffe to decide. [source: RL; PI]
- The awards were finished just before independence but published on 17 August 1947, two days after it. Representatives were given two hours to study copies on 16 August. Radcliffe left on Independence Day and "destroyed all his papers before he left India". [source: RL]
- Disputes: Gurdaspur (bare Muslim majority of about 50–51%) was split, with three tehsils going to India. Radcliffe's stated reason was the canal headworks serving Amritsar. Pakistanis allege that Mountbatten manipulated the award to give India a land route to Kashmir. Shereen Ilahi notes that the route ran through Hindu-majority Pathankot, which was not in dispute. Mountbatten and Radcliffe denied any manipulation. In Bengal, Muslim-majority Murshidabad and Malda went to India, and Hindu-majority Khulna and the Chittagong Hill Tracts went to Pakistan. [source: RL; PI]
- Auden's poem "Partition" (written 1966; in *City Without Walls*, 1969) is about Radcliffe; its opening line is in the Quotations section. [source: WebSearch summary of litcharts.com/poetry/w-h-auden/partition and others]

## 8. Violence and migration (give ranges; align with the British Empire chapter)
- Displacement: **12–20 million**; deaths: **200,000 to 2 million** (Talbot and Singh 2009: "figures ranging from 200,000 to 2 million"). [source: PI]
- About 14.5 million crossed the borders. The 1951 censuses counted 7,226,600 displaced persons in Pakistan and 7,295,870 in India. [source: PI]
- Punjab: about 12 million migrated, about 6.5 million Muslims to West Punjab and 4.7 million Hindus and Sikhs to East Punjab. "Virtually no Muslim survived in East Punjab (except in Malerkotla and Nuh) and virtually no Hindu or Sikh survived in West Punjab (except in Rahim Yar Khan and Bahawalpur)." Bengal: about 3.3 million migrated (2.6 million Hindus to India, 0.7 million Muslims to East Pakistan), and migration from East Bengal continued for years. [source: PI]
- Demographic studies estimate 2.2 million, or 2.3–3.2 million, people "missing" in Punjab. [source: PI]
- Competing tolls: West Punjab's governor Sir Francis Mudie estimated that 500,000 Muslims died trying to reach his province. The British High Commissioner in Karachi put the full total at 800,000. Lawrence James says this "makes nonsense of the claim by Mountbatten and his partisans that only 200,000 were killed". Richard Symonds: at the lowest estimate half a million dead and twelve million homeless. [source: PI]
- Talbot and Singh describe the violence as unprecedented in "scale and level of brutality", "designed to cleanse an existing generation and prevent its future reproduction"; some scholars question the term genocide, and some call the Punjab violence "retributive genocide". [source: PI]
- Population exchange had not been planned. An organised transfer was arranged only in Punjab. Ambedkar and Patel favoured a full exchange. [source: PI]
- Master Tara Singh's sword-flourish outside the Punjab Assembly on 3 March 1947 is cited by Ishtiaq Ahmed as provoking violence against Sikhs and Hindus in northern Punjab. "Yet, at the end of that year, more Muslims had been killed in East Punjab than Hindus and Sikhs together in West Punjab" (Ahmed). [source: PI]

## 9. Women and abduction
- India claimed that 33,000 Hindu and Sikh women had been abducted; Pakistan claimed 50,000 Muslim women. (Match the British Empire chapter.) [source: PI; VAW]
- Overall estimates run from 75,000 to 100,000 women kidnapped and raped (VAW lead). Andrew Major estimates 40,000–45,000 in Punjab. Butalia gives 25,000–29,000 Hindu and Sikh women against 12,000–15,000 Muslim women (as reported by VAW). Present these as estimates that differ. [source: VAW]
- Recovery: by 1949 about 12,000 women had been recovered in India and 6,000 in Pakistan. By 1954 the totals were 20,728 Muslim women recovered from India and 9,032 Hindu and Sikh women from Pakistan. Many refused to "return", fearing their families would not accept them. [source: PI; VAW]
- Systematic violence against women began in the Rawalpindi massacres of March 1947. At Thoha Khalsa (Rawalpindi district) "many Sikh women chose to drown themselves in a well". (Butalia's book discusses this case; the number of women is NOT verified here.) [source: VAW; THO]
- Some scholars note "positive" side effects. Refugee women in Bengal took paid work and entered politics, and in Punjab partition "narrowed the physical spaces and enlarged the social spaces available to women". [source: PI]

## 10. Kashmir and 1971
- Jammu and Kashmir was a Muslim-majority princely state with a Hindu ruler, Maharaja Hari Singh. After the Poonch rebellion in its western districts, Azad Kashmir was proclaimed on 24 October 1947, and Pakistani tribal militias invaded. In Jammu, a massacre of Muslims (September–October 1947) was carried out by Dogra state troops, local Hindus including RSS members, and refugees; the Maharaja was "implicated in some instances". [source: KC]
- On Mountbatten's insistence India required accession before sending troops. The Maharaja signed the Instrument of Accession on 26 October 1947, accepted the next day, on the provisional basis of a later "reference to the people". Jinnah said the accession was obtained by "fraud and violence". [source: KC]
- UN Security Council Resolution 47 (21 April 1948) called for a ceasefire and a plebiscite. The plebiscite was never held. The first war ended in a UN-mediated ceasefire line, which became the Line of Control under the Simla Agreement (1972). Wars followed in 1965, 1971 and 1999 (Kargil). An insurgency began in 1989; the state government counted 43,460 killed in 21 years (2011 figure). India revoked Article 370 in August 2019. Today India controls about 55% of the land, Pakistan about 30% and China about 15%. [source: KC]
- 1971: Operation Searchlight began on 25 March 1971 after the Awami League's election victory was ignored. India entered the war on 3 December 1971, and Pakistan surrendered at Dhaka on 16 December 1971. About 10 million refugees fled to India. Civilian deaths are estimated at 300,000 to 3,000,000. This was "a later partition of Pakistan, and not of India" (PI). [source: BLW; PI]

## 11. Palestine: Mandate, revolt, Peel (brief; the First World War and Interwar chapters cover the background)
- Balfour Declaration, 2 November 1917 (see First World War chapter): "a national home for the Jewish people", with the proviso protecting "the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities". [source: research/20th-century-history--first-world-war.md, BALF]
- The Mandate took formal effect on 29 September 1923. At its start the population was about 11% Jewish, 9% Christian Arab and 78% Muslim Arab (1922 census 757,182). A British estimate of 1918 gave 700,000 Arabs and 56,000 Jews. [source: MP; UNPP]
- In 1946 there were 1,076,783 Muslims, 608,225 Jews, 145,063 Christians and 15,488 others; Jews were about a third of the population. [source: UNPP]
- The Arab revolt of 1936–39 left "more than 5000 Arabs, 400 Jews, and 200 British" dead, and "10% of the adult Arab male population was killed, wounded, imprisoned, or exiled". (The Interwar chapter uses Walid Khalidi's 5,032; consistent.) [source: MP]
- **Peel Commission** (Palestine Royal Commission), report of 7 July 1937: the first official recommendation of partition. It proposed a small Jewish state (Galilee, the Jezreel Valley and the coastal plain), an Arab state joined to Transjordan, and a British mandatory enclave for Jerusalem with a corridor to Jaffa. Transfer would be "in the last resort … compulsory". It cited the 1923 Greek–Turkish exchange, with 225,000 Arabs in the Jewish area against 1,250 Jews in the Arab area. The Arab Higher Committee rejected it. The Zionist Congress rejected the specific borders but empowered its leaders to negotiate; Weizmann and Ben-Gurion backed it as a basis. The Woodhead Commission (1938) found partition impracticable. [source: PEEL; UNPP]
- The 1939 White Paper limited Jewish immigration and land purchase and promised a state within ten years. [source: MP]
- The Irgun bombed the King David Hotel (British headquarters) in 1946, killing 92 people (MP; the commonly cited figure is 91, so say "more than 90"). In February 1947 Britain referred Palestine to the UN; in September 1947 it said the Mandate would end at midnight on 14 May 1948. [source: MP; UNPP]

## 12. The UN partition plan (Resolution 181)
- UNSCOP drafted the plan (3 September 1947). The Palestinian Arab leadership boycotted UNSCOP, while Jewish organisations collaborated with it. [source: UNPP]
- The General Assembly adopted it as **Resolution 181 (II) on 29 November 1947: 33 for, 13 against, 10 abstentions, 1 absent** (Thailand). It needed a two-thirds majority; the vote was postponed by three days on 26 November. [source: UNPP]
- For included the United States, the Soviet Union and France. Against: Afghanistan, Cuba, Egypt, Greece, India, Iran, Iraq, Lebanon, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Syria, Turkey and Yemen. Abstaining: the United Kingdom, China, Ethiopia, Yugoslavia, Argentina, Chile, Colombia, El Salvador, Honduras and Mexico. [source: UNPP]
- Proposed shares: the Jewish state 15,264 km² (56.47%), the Arab state 11,592 km² (42.88%), and a Jerusalem–Bethlehem international zone (*corpus separatum*) of 176 km² (0.65%), with economic union. Proposed populations (UNSCOP): the Jewish state 498,000 Jews and 407,000 Arabs; the Arab state about 725,000 Arabs and 10,000 Jews. Arabs were about two-thirds of the population of Palestine overall and, by the Arab side's figure, owned 94% of the land. [source: UNPP]
- Reactions: the Jewish Agency accepted the plan despite dissatisfaction with the borders. Ben-Gurion called the borders "bad from a military and political point of view". The Irgun (Begin) and Lehi rejected it. The Arab Higher Committee, the Arab League and the Arab states rejected it as violating self-determination and walked out, accompanied by the Indian and Pakistani delegates. [source: UNPP]
- Debate over Zionist acceptance: Simha Flapan, de Waart and Jerome Slater argue that acceptance was tactical. Benny Morris says the movement, "except for its fringes", accepted it. [source: UNPP]

## 13. The 1948 war, the Nakba and Deir Yassin
- The war ran from 30 November 1947 to the 1949 armistices (W48 gives an end date of 10 March 1949; the last armistice, with Syria, was signed on 20 July 1949). Phase one was a civil war in Mandatory Palestine. Phase two began on 14 May 1948, when Ben-Gurion declared the State of Israel; the Mandate expired at midnight on 14/15 May, and Arab armies (Egypt, Transjordan's Arab Legion, Iraq, Syria and Lebanon) invaded on 15 May. [source: W48; MP]
- Armistices in 1949: Egypt on 24 February, Lebanon on 23 March, Transjordan on 3 April and Syria on 20 July. Israel held about 78% of Mandatory Palestine, 22 points more than the plan allotted. Transjordan took the West Bank (and annexed it) and Egypt the Gaza Strip. The lines became the "Green Line". [source: W48]
- Losses: Israel lost about 6,373 (about 1% of its population; about 4,000 soldiers). Morris gives 5,700–5,800. Arab losses are uncertain: estimates run from 4,000 soldiers of Egypt, Jordan and Syria to about 15,000. Aref al-Aref gives 13,000 Palestinian dead. [source: W48; EXP]
- **Refugees:** "more than 700,000" Palestinians, about half of Mandatory Palestine's Arab population and about 80% of the Arabs of what became Israel, were expelled or fled. The UN Conciliation Commission estimated 711,000 refugees in September 1949; EXP's infobox says 750,000+. Use "about 700,000–750,000". About 160,000 Arabs remained in Israel, a quarter of them internally displaced ("present absentees"). [source: EXP; W48]
- Before 15 May: Morris estimates 250,000–300,000 had already left, Tessler 300,000 and Pappé 380,000. [source: EXP]
- Villages depopulated or destroyed: 400–600 (EXP). Counts: Morris 369, Walid Khalidi (*All That Remains*) 418, Abu Sitta 531. [source: EXP]
- Expulsion from Lydda and Ramle (Operation Dani, July 1948): about 60,000 residents. In late April 1948 Haifa's Arab population fell to about 4,000 by mid-May. [source: W48; EXP]
- **Deir Yassin**, 9 April 1948: the Irgun and Lehi, supported by the Haganah and Palmach, killed "at least 107" villagers, including women and children, though the village had agreed a non-aggression pact. For decades 254 was believed; "present scholarship puts the death toll at around 110". The news spread fear and accelerated flight. The Haganah and the Jewish Agency condemned it, and the Agency sent an apology to King Abdullah, who rejected it. The reprisal on 13 April was the Hadassah medical convoy massacre: 78 Jews killed, most of them medical staff. [source: DY]
- UN General Assembly Resolution 194 (11 December 1948) said refugees "wishing to return to their homes and live at peace with their neighbours should be permitted to do so at the earliest practicable date", with compensation for others. [source: EXP]
- The term: the first post-1948 use of *nakba* ("catastrophe") is attributed to Constantin Zureiq, *Ma'na an-Nakba* (*The Meaning of the Disaster*, 1948). Palestinians mark Nakba Day on 15 May. Israel's 2011 "Nakba Law" denies state funding to institutions that commemorate it. In Israel the war is the War of Independence. [source: NAK; W48]
- **Jewish departures from Arab and Muslim countries:** about 900,000 Jews "migrated, fled, or were expelled" from Muslim-majority countries in the 20th century, mainly from 1948 to the early 1970s. About 650,000 (72%) settled in Israel. W48: in the three years after the war "over 260,000" came from Arab states, and about 700,000 Jews in all immigrated to Israel in those three years. Examples: the Farhud in Baghdad in 1941 (180 killed); about 120,000 left Iraq in 1950–51 (Operation Ezra and Nehemiah) on condition of giving up citizenship; anti-Jewish riots at Oujda and Jerada (June 1948, 44 killed); Yemen's Operation Magic Carpet (1949–50); 130,000 of Algeria's 140,000 Jews went to France in 1961–62. Push factors were antisemitic violence and expulsion; pull factors were Zionism, economic prospects and the 1944 "One Million Plan". [source: JEX; W48]
- The "refugee" framing is itself debated. Those who see the exodus as analogous to the Nakba stress push factors, and those who reject the analogy stress pull factors. Iraqi-born Avi Shlaim: "nobody expelled us from Iraq". Yehouda Shenhav calls the analogy "unfounded, immoral". Proponents (e.g., Ben Dror Yemini) speak of a "Jewish Nakba". [source: JEX]

## 14. Cyprus (brief comparison)
- Britain took over administration in 1878 and annexed Cyprus in 1914; it became a crown colony in 1925. EOKA's armed campaign for *enosis* (union with Greece) ran from 1 April 1955 (387 British servicemen and personnel killed). The colonial authorities created a solely Turkish Cypriot reserve police force to fight EOKA, which heightened intercommunal tension. The Turkish Cypriot TMT pursued *taksim* ("partition", from Arabic *taqsīm*). [source: CYP]
- Independence came on 16 August 1960, with Britain, Greece and Turkey as guarantors. "Bloody Christmas", 21 December 1963, and its aftermath killed 364 Turkish Cypriots and 174 Greek Cypriots. A UN force and the Nicosia "Green Line" followed. [source: CYP]
- After the Greek-junta-backed coup of July 1974, Turkey invaded on 20 July 1974 and held 36% of the island by 16 August. About 160,000 Greek Cypriots fled or were expelled from the north (the Cyprus government reported 200,000 refugees in all), and Turkish Cypriots moved north. In the Annan Plan referendums of 24 April 2004 Turkish Cypriots voted yes by almost two to one and Greek Cypriots no by about three to one. Cyprus joined the EU in 2004 still divided. [source: CYP]

## 15. Memory and oral history
- The 1947 Partition Archive was founded by Guneeta Singh Bhalla, who began recording interviews in the San Francisco Bay Area in 2010 (formalised 2011). It is inspired by the Hiroshima Peace Memorial and Holocaust memorials. It had nearly 9,700 interviews by June 2021 (PI) and "over 12,000" from 14 countries by June 2025 (PA). It crowd-sources "Citizen Historians" and works with Stanford University Libraries and, in South Asia, Ashoka, Habib, LUMS, Guru Nanak Dev and Delhi universities. Its book *10,000 Memories* appeared in 2024. [source: PA; PI]
- The Partition Museum in Amritsar opened in August 2017. Kavita Puri's *Partition Voices* (2019) records British South Asian survivors. India declared 14 August "Partition Horrors Remembrance Day" in 2021, which Congress criticised as politically motivated. [source: PI]
- **Urvashi Butalia** (born 1952, Ambala) co-founded Kali for Women (1984), India's first feminist press, with Ritu Menon, and founded Zubaan (2003). *The Other Side of Silence: Voices from the Partition of India* (Viking/Penguin India 1998; Duke 2000) draws on more than seventy interviews and begins with her own family. It centres women, children and Dalits and the violence against women, including killings by their own families. It won the Oral History Association book award (2001) and the Nikkei Asia Prize for Culture (2003). [source: UB; WebSearch summary of dukeupress.edu/the-other-side-of-silence and Penguin/WorldCat records]
- Palestinian memory: Walid Khalidi (ed.), *All That Remains* (1992), documents the depopulated villages. [source: EXP]

## Debates
- **Partition as Britain's exit strategy / "divide and rule".** Fraser (1984) treats partition as a British method of conflict resolution that failed three times. Dubnov and Robson (2019) treat it as a twentieth-century imperial idea circulating among the same officials and intellectuals (e.g., Reginald Coupland) rather than a natural answer to ethnic conflict. On India, Talbot and Singh say partition was neither "the inevitable end of the so-called British 'divide and rule' policy" nor of Hindu–Muslim difference. Venkat Dhulipala rejects "divide and rule" as the cause and stresses the popular vision of Pakistan as a "New Medina". Another view (*The Shadow of the Great Game*, Narendra Singh Sarila) says the British establishment wanted partition for strategic reasons. [source: PI; WebSearch summaries cited in Reading list]
- **Haste: Mountbatten's timetable.** Stanley Wolpert blames British haste for the scale of the slaughter. Independence came before the boundaries were known, leaving order to the new states. Lawrence James and others argue Mountbatten had no option but to "cut and run" given the looming civil war and Britain's exhausted resources. [source: PI]
- **Who wanted partition? Jinnah and the "sole spokesman".** Ayesha Jalal and H. M. Seervai argue that Jinnah used the Pakistan demand as a bargaining counter for Muslim political rights within India and that Congress's refusal to share power produced partition. Pakistani historiography treats Pakistan as the realisation of the two-nation theory; in India Jinnah is often seen as "the demon who divided the land" (Akbar Ahmed). Joya Chatterji shows that Hindu *bhadralok* and the Bengal Congress campaigned for Bengal's partition in 1947, preferring division to Muslim-majority rule. [source: JIN; AJ; WebSearch summary of Bengal Divided reviews]
- **History "from below".** Butalia, Menon and Bhasin, and the oral-history archives shift the focus from high politics to women, Dalits and families, and to silences inside families. [source: UB; PI]
- **The causes of the Palestinian exodus.** (a) The older Israeli account says Arab leaders ordered or encouraged flight; Morris's work found this was not the main cause. (b) Benny Morris (*Birth*, 1987/88; *Revisited*, 2004): the exodus was "in large part a response to deliberate expulsions and violence" rather than Arab orders. There was "transfer thinking" in Zionism, but no master plan; Plan D gave commanders "carte blanche" for expulsions. In ECP's paraphrase, the result was "of war, not intent". Later he said it was "a variety of ethnic cleansing". In a 2004 *Haaretz* interview he argued that "there are circumstances in history that justify ethnic cleansing". (c) Ilan Pappé (*The Ethnic Cleansing of Palestine*, 2006): a deliberate, planned ethnic cleansing with Plan Dalet as its "blueprint". Walid Khalidi (1961, 1988) earlier called Plan Dalet a "master plan for the conquest of Palestine". (d) Yoav Gelber and David Tal read Plan D as defensive, aimed at securing roads and the partition borders. Efraim Karsh (*Palestine Betrayed*, 2010) argues that the catastrophe was largely of the Arab leadership's own making. (e) Rashid Khalidi (*The Iron Cage*, 2006) examines Palestinian leadership failures as well as British and Zionist obstruction. *The Hundred Years' War on Palestine* (2020) frames the century as settler colonialism backed by Britain and then the US. EXP (citing Slater) reports that "most scholarship today agrees that expulsions and violence, and the fear thereof, were the primary causes". [source: BM; IP; ECP; PD; EXP; RK; HYW; WebSearch summary of Karsh reviews]
- **The New Historians.** Morris coined the term in 1988 for himself, Pappé, Avi Shlaim and Simha Flapan, who used Israeli archives opened under the thirty-year rule. Anita Shapira criticised their meagre use of Arab sources, and Karsh accused them of selective reading. Their findings entered Israeli school textbooks in 1999. [source: NH; BM]
- **Ireland: inevitable or imposed?** Nationalists saw partition as an arbitrary division against the will of the island's majority, with a gerrymandered unionist majority (TT). Unionists saw it as self-determination for a distinct Ulster Protestant people. Townshend's book is read by some (Gallagher, LRB) as treating partition as nearly inevitable and rooted in religious difference, while History Today reports his view that federalism was beyond British political capacity and partition the only workable answer. The Boundary Commission is widely seen as a "fudge". [source: TT; WebSearch summary of Townshend reviews]
- **Population transfer as solution.** Peel (1937) cited the Greek–Turkish exchange of 1923 and envisaged transfer "in the last resort ... compulsory". Punjab's transfer was organised only after violence. The comparison of the Nakba and the Jewish exodus from Arab lands is itself contested. [source: PEEL; PI; JEX]
