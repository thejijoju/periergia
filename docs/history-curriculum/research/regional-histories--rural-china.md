## 1. The late-imperial peasant economy and the involution debate
- The Ming-Qing chapter already presents the debate: Pomeranz's *The Great Divergence* (Princeton, 2000) argued the Yangzi Delta was comparable with England until about 1800, with coal and colonies making the difference; Philip C. C. Huang answered with "involution" — ever more family labour in cash crops and household sidelines raised total output while returns per workday fell, "growth without development". Cross-refer rather than retell. [source: MQ; GDB]
- Philip C. C. Huang (UCLA 1966–2004; founding editor of the journal *Modern China*) wrote *The Peasant Economy and Social Change in North China* (Stanford, 1985) and *The Peasant Family and Rural Development in the Yangzi Delta, 1350–1988* (Stanford, 1990), and criticised Pomeranz in "Development or Involution in Eighteenth-Century Britain and China?" (*Journal of Asian Studies* 61:2, 2002). [source: PH; https://sup.org/books/cite/?id=2761]
- Huang's 1990 book calls the process "involutionary commercialization" and "involutionary growth": ever-increasing labour input per unit of land, expanded total output, diminishing marginal returns per workday. [source: Stanford UP description via WebSearch, https://sup.org/books/cite/?id=2761]
- Loren Brandt, *Commercialization and Agricultural Development: Central and Eastern China, 1870–1937* (Cambridge UP, 1989). Brandt (with Thomas Rawski) is grouped with scholars who argue for substantial growth in agricultural output and rural incomes in the late 19th–early 20th century, the point of tension with Huang; Ramon Myers set Brandt, David Faure and Rawski against Huang's involution in a *Journal of Asian Studies* exchange. CAVEAT: whether Brandt claimed rising *labour productivity* specifically was not verified — write "rising output and incomes from commercialisation". [source: WebSearch summaries, https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-asian-studies/article/abs/chinese-economic-history-and-development-a-note-on-the-myershuang-exchange/9F8C1265F3407755A885DADD919968B0 ; https://www.theportobellobookshop.com/9780521022866]
- Huang later wrote that Pomeranz had come to agree on declining average returns to labour from the shift to cotton, the disagreement lying elsewhere (e.g. a "fertilizer revolution"). [source: WebSearch summary of Huang's JAS exchange, https://en.lishiyushehui.cn/article/download/302 — secondary; use cautiously]
- Kenneth Pomeranz's earlier rural book: *The Making of a Hinterland: State, Society, and Economy in Inland North China, 1853–1937* (University of California Press, 1993). It studies "Huang-Yun" (western Shandong with parts of Zhili and Henan, some 15 million people by the early 20th century), which slid from core to periphery after the Yellow River changed course in 1853; its theme is the state's abandonment of the hinterland, with chapters on local finance, farming, transport, taxation and popular protest. [source: https://www.ucpress.edu/flyer/books/the-making-of-a-hinterland/hardcover ; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/396410/summary]

## 2. Rural crisis in the Republican era
- Warlord armies: about half a million soldiers in 1916, tripling by 1922 and tripling again by 1924 (published Modern China chapter; cross-refer). [source: MC]
- Banditry grew with warlord armies; "by 1930, the total bandit population was estimated to be 20 million" (Wikipedia, attribution to a named scholar not confirmed — attribute as "one estimate"). [source: BAN]
- Phil Billingsley, *Bandits in Republican China* (Stanford UP, 1988), is the standard English study. The bandit "White Wolf" (Bai Lang) terrorised central China from autumn 1913 until he was killed on 5 August 1914, commanding more than 20,000 men at his peak across five provinces. [source: https://www.sup.org/books/asian-studies/bandits-republican-china ; https://thechinaproject.com/2022/10/12/white-wolf-the-great-bandit-of-early-republican-china/]
- The Red Spear Society: a 1920s rural self-defence movement of smallholders and tenants in Henan, Hebei and Shandong, defending villages against bandits, warlords and tax collectors (later Communists or Japanese); members used protective magic like the earlier Boxers; the CCP suppressed such "Hui-Dao-Men" societies in a 1953 campaign. Elizabeth Perry's *Rebels and Revolutionaries in North China, 1845–1945* (1980) is the key study. [source: RSS]
- Famines covered in Modern China (cross-refer): Henan 1942–43, estimates ~700,000 (Garnaut) to 2–3 million (Eastman). [source: MC]

## 3. Fei Xiaotong
- Fei Xiaotong (2 November 1910, Wujiang, Suzhou – 24 April 2005, Beijing), anthropologist-sociologist; studied at Yenching and Tsinghua; first fieldwork in Guangxi ended when he was injured in a tiger trap and his wife and co-researcher Wang Tonghui drowned going for help. [source: FEI]
- He studied at the LSE under Bronisław Malinowski (1936–38); his PhD thesis on Kaixiangong village (Wujiang, Jiangsu) became *Peasant Life in China: A Field Study of Country Life in the Yangtze Valley* (London: Routledge, 1939), with a preface by Malinowski. [source: FEI]
- With Zhang Zhiyi he wrote *Earthbound China: A Study of Rural Economy in Yunnan* (Chicago, 1945); *China's Gentry: Essays in Rural-Urban Relations* (Chicago, 1953). [source: FEI]
- *From the Soil* (*Xiangtu Zhongguo*, 乡土中国): lecture notes and essays for the journal *Shiji Pinglun*; English translation by Gary Hamilton and Wang Zheng, *From the Soil: The Foundations of Chinese Society* (University of California Press, 1992). Publication date: 1947 per the book article; 1948 (Shanghai: Guancha) per the Fei article — say "1947–48" or "late 1940s". [source: SOIL; FEI]
- Main ideas: Chinese people as "inseparable from the soil"; society organised as concentric ripples spreading from a pebble dropped in water, with the self at the centre — the "differential mode of association" (*chaxu geju*), contrasted with the Western "organizational mode of association" (*tuanti geju*); a "two-track" politics of centre and locality. [source: SOIL; https://china-europa-forum.net/bdfdoc-160_en.html]
- *From the Soil* was banned on the mainland after 1949 (Fei's works branded "rightist"), restricted in Taiwan, available only in Hong Kong until the 1980s. [source: SOIL]
- Fei was denounced as a "big Rightist" in the Anti-Rightist Campaign (1957); sociology was abolished as "bourgeois pseudo-science"; he was rehabilitated in 1980, sat as a judge at the Gang of Four trial, and led the restoration of sociology. His influence is thought to have helped persuade the government to promote rural industry ("small towns") in the 1980s. [source: FEI]

## 4. The Rural Reconstruction Movement
- Started in the 1920s by Y. C. James Yen, Liang Shuming and others; a "middle way" between the Nationalist state and Communist revolution; "hundreds" of village projects; after 1931 the Nanjing government gave qualified support; work was curtailed by war and lack of resources in the mid-to-late 1930s. [source: RRM]
- James Yen (Yan Yangchu), born Bazhong, Sichuan, 1890 (he gave 1893), died New York 17 January 1990; worked with Chinese labourers in France in WWI; Yale graduate 1918; in 1923 co-founded the National Association of Mass Education Movements with Liang Qichao, Hu Shih and Tao Xingzhi; its literacy campaigns attracted more than five million students. [source: YEN]
- Yen's Ding Xian (Ting Hsien) experiment, Hebei, 1926–1937, about 200 miles south of Beijing: People's Schools coordinating hybrid pigs, cooperatives, village drama and village health workers. [source: YEN; DZ]
- In 1948 Yen persuaded the US Congress to fund the Sino-American Joint Commission on Rural Reconstruction (JCRR), which carried out the largest pre-1949 land reform project on the mainland and then moved to Taiwan, where it helped lay the rural foundation for growth. After 1949 Yen was labelled a "henchman of American imperialism"; welcomed back to China in 1985. [source: RRM; YEN]
- Liang Shuming (18 October 1893 – 23 June 1988), philosopher; ran rural reconstruction in Zouping County, Shandong, 1931–37, appointed by the warlord-governor Han Fuju; trained 800 people for schools across Shandong (1931–33); founded the Shandong Rural Reconstruction Institute; helped found the China Democratic League. [source: LSM]
- Liang argued Chinese rural society could not be divided neatly by class, since one lineage held both "haves" and "have-nots"; class struggle would set kinsmen against each other. [source: LSM]
- Rural reconstruction pioneered village health workers in the 1930s, a precedent for barefoot doctors. [source: BFD]
- Legacy: from around 2002–03 a "New Rural Reconstruction" movement led by Wen Tiejun (Renmin University) revived the name; in July 2003 Qiu Jiansheng founded the James Yen Institute for Rural Reconstruction in Dingzhou. Wen Tiejun began popularising the term *sannong wenti* in the late 1980s. [source: NRR; YEN]

## 5. The CCP's peasant strategy and the Hunan Report
- Mao's *Report on an Investigation of the Peasant Movement in Hunan*, based on 32 days of investigation around Changsha in early 1927, published in *Zhongyang Fukan* on 28 March 1927. [source: HUNAN; matches MAO]
- It went against the party leadership's worker-centred line (Chen Duxiu) and endorsed peasant violence; it listed "Fourteen Great Achievements" of the peasant associations, and described peasants' subordination to political, clan and religious authority, with women additionally dominated by husbands. [source: HUNAN]
- Mao learned from Peng Pai, who organised the Hailufeng Soviet in Guangdong; Mao co-led the Peasant Movement Training Institute. [source: HUNAN]
- Maurice Meisner saw two "heresies": bypassing the urban proletariat and relying on peasant initiative. Roy Hofheinz Jr.: Mao's novelty was insisting that only the poorest peasants could turn things upside down. [source: HUNAN]
- Revolution from the countryside and Mao's strategy belong to the Maoism chapter; cross-refer. [source: MAO]

## 6. Land reform (1946–1953)
- Landlord status was defined (in theory) by the share of income from exploitation rather than labour; landlords' land was confiscated through struggle meetings and "speaking bitterness". [source: LR]
- Deng Yingchao (1947): "women function as great mobilizers when they speak bitterness" (already used in MC). Land titles went to adult household members, raising women's economic independence. [source: LR; MC]
- Outline Land Law, October 1947 (from a National Land Conference July–September 1947). William Hinton said it "played as important a role as the Emancipation Proclamation in the American Civil War". [source: LR]
- By the CCP's victory, more than half the population in Communist areas had taken part and over 25 million hectares had been redistributed (Brian DeMare). [source: LR; matches MC]
- Land Reform Law, summer 1950: officially more lenient; landlords kept commercial assets; the "five big properties" (land, draft animals, excess grain, tools, surplus housing) redistributed; middle peasants' land protected and a "rich peasant economy" preserved. Extended to all Han farming areas 1950–52; largely complete by 1952. [source: LR]
- Hu Yaobang had to explain that "annihilating" the landlord class meant seizing property, not killing landlords. [source: LR]
- Outcome: about 43% of cultivated land redistributed to about 60% of the rural population; land taken from over ten million landlords (DeMare); middle peasants gained most. In Hinton's "Long Bow" (Zhangzhuangcun, *Fanshen*), middle peasants ended at 90% of people owning 90.8% of land (Walter Scheidel's example). [source: LR]
- Deaths (attributed ranges, include the parallel campaign against counter-revolutionaries, 1949–53): total range 200,000–5,000,000; Benedict Stavis (1978) 200,000–800,000 in land reform; Twitchett/Fairbank/MacFarquhar (*Cambridge History of China*, 1987) 1–2 million executed; J. A. G. Roberts 200,000–2,000,000; Mao (1957) 700,000 killed 1950–52; Zhou Enlai to Edgar Snow 830,000 "destroyed" before 1954; Meisner 2 million executed 1950–52; Lee Feigon 2–5 million. Consistent with MC ("200,000 to 5 million", land reform plus counter-revolutionaries) and MAO (CHC 1–2 million; Stavis 200,000–800,000). [source: LR; MC; MAO]
- Landlord retaliation: the Nationalist-backed "Homecoming Legion" of dispossessed landlords killed and extorted villagers during the civil war. [source: LR]

## 7. Collectivisation and the people's communes
- By June 1956 over 60% of rural households were in higher-level producers' cooperatives; members earned work points. (MAO: mutual aid teams, then cooperatives from 1953, higher cooperatives from 1956 of 100–300 families.) [source: COM; MAO]
- Liang Shuming's 1953 clash with Mao: Mao quoted him as saying "the workers are up in the ninth heaven whereas the peasants are down in the ninth hell", and denounced him at the Central People's Government Council (September 1953). [source: https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-5/mswv5_35.htm]
- 1958: Xushui (Hebei) publicised as a commune; Mao declared "People's communes are good"; ~26,000 communes formed, averaging 4,500 hectares, 24,000 people and 5,200 households (MAO says about 25,000 averaging 5,000 households — see Conflicts). Communes ran mess halls, abolished private plots, and at first offered "free supply". Communes lasted 1958–1983. [source: COM; MAO]
- Retreat: accounting unit devolved to the brigade (late 1960) and production team (1962); private plots, sidelines and small livestock restored ("Three Freedoms"); the team stayed the accounting unit until decollectivisation, 1979–82. [source: COM]
- Communes extracted roughly half a labourer's income in work-point valuation to invest in irrigation, mechanisation and land reclamation. [source: COM]
- Learn from Dazhai (from 1963): the Shanxi village under Chen Yonggui, a model of self-reliant terracing; Chen joined the Politburo in 1973, was a vice premier, and was removed from the State Council in 1980; Dazhai became a figure of ridicule under reform. [source: DZAI]
- Socialist Education Movement ("Four Cleanups"), 1963–1965, against "reactionary elements" in rural cadres. [source: SEM]

## 8. The Great Leap famine (cross-refer to Maoism; do not retell)
- Ranges as in MAO: 15 to 45 million or more; most recent scholarly estimates ~30–45 million (Banister/Ashton ~30m; Cao Shuji 32.5m; Yang Jisheng 36m; Dikötter ≥45m). [source: GCF; MAO; MC]
- The famine was overwhelmingly rural: urban hukou holders received fixed rations; Wikipedia states "an estimated 95% or higher of all deaths occurred among rural hukou holders" (unattributed estimate — use as "by one estimate"). [source: HK]
- Xinyang (Henan): people starved at the doors of grain warehouses; in Guangshan county one-third of people died (Xinhua reporter Lu Baoguo to Yang Jisheng); the Xinyang post office confiscated 1,200 letters begging for help. [source: GCF]
- Local leadership mattered: radical Anhui under Zeng Xisheng vs. Jiangxi, whose leaders quietly limited the Leap (Manning and Wemheuer). Wan Li later said Anhui had 3–4 million "abnormal" deaths. [source: GCF]
- Xin Meng, Nancy Qian and Pierre Yared: more deaths where more food per head was produced; inflexible procurement explains at least half of mortality. [source: GCF]
- Liu Shaoqi, after 44 days in Hunan villages (April–May 1961), judged the famine "three parts natural disaster, seven parts man-made"; he repeated the 30/70 attribution at the Seven Thousand Cadres Conference (January 1962). [source: GCF]
- Ralph Thaxton's Da Fo village study: food *output* did not collapse, but availability did, through state appropriation; villagers survived by covert "contention" (crop theft, eating green crops). [source: GCF; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/china-quarterly/article/catastrophe-and-contention-in-rural-china-maos-great-leap-forward-famine-and-the-origins-of-righteous-resistance-in-da-fo-village-ralph-a-thaxton-cambridge-cambridge-university-press-2008-xxii-383-pp-1799-2799-isbn-9780521722308/E079555DF914C471940736D1F687D53D]
- Xiaogang itself: 67 of 120 villagers died, 1958–60; a quarter of Fengyang County's population died. [source: XG; matches CT]

## 9. Hukou and the rural–urban divide
- Hukou Registration Regulation signed 9 January 1958, dividing citizens into agricultural (rural, *nongmin*) and non-agricultural (urban) hukou; status conversion quotas of 0.15–0.2% a year. [source: HK; CT says "formalised in 1958"]
- In 1949 about 89% lived in rural areas (~484 million vs ~58 million urban). Urban hukou carried rations, housing, jobs, pensions; rural hukou holders had to provide for themselves. Kam Wing Chan: the system "effectively forbade the peasantry to exit agriculture". [source: HK]
- Sulamith Heins Potter and Jack Potter (1990) called the rural–urban distinction "caste-like". [source: HK]
- The World Bank's Shahid Yusuf credits hukou with channelling migration and avoiding large slums (already in CT). [source: HK; CT]
- Reforms: 1984 document on peasants settling in towns; 1985 rules on transient population; 2014 State Council plan to abolish the agricultural/non-agricultural distinction and grant urban hukou to 100 million by 2020, keeping strict limits on cities over 5 million; July 2024 plan to remove settlement limits in cities under 3 million. [source: HK]
- Many migrants do not want urban hukou, as rural hukou carries land rights. [source: HK]
- Sun Zhigang, a 27-year-old university-educated migrant from Hubei, died after a beating in a Guangzhou custody-and-repatriation centre on 20 March 2003, detained for lacking a temporary residence permit; the *Southern Metropolis Daily* exposed the case and the C&R system was abolished. [source: SZ; MIG]

## 10. Barefoot doctors
- Mao's "June 26 Directive" (1965) attacked urban bias in medicine (80–90% of people lived in the countryside); the programme became national policy in 1968 via the *Red Flag* journal; target one barefoot doctor per 1,000 people; Rural Cooperative Medical System stations at brigade level. [source: BFD; COM]
- Training: three to six months; mix of Western and Chinese medicine, vaccination, sanitation, deliveries; each village required a female doctor. [source: BFD]
- Abolished with the communes (1981); the term replaced by "village doctors" in 1985; RCMS coverage fell from 90% to 4.8% by 1984. [source: BFD]
- Influence: the 1978 Alma-Ata Declaration on primary health care drew some inspiration from it. New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme from 2003; over 90% of rural residents covered by 2010. [source: BFD]
- Mobo Gao (*Gao Village*) argues rural people gained from barefoot doctors and village schools (already in CR). [source: CR; https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/90595]

## 11. Household responsibility system and Xiaogang (the "founding legend")
- December 1978, Xiaogang (Fengyang County, Anhui): eighteen farmers, led by Yen Jingchang (Yan Jinchang), secretly agreed to divide collective land into family plots, deliver a quota and keep the surplus, and to raise the children of anyone sentenced to death until 18. The next harvest exceeded the previous five combined; per capita income rose from 22 to 400 yuan; grain 90,000 kg in 1979 (Wikipedia, figures from local accounts). [source: XG; HRS]
- The story's authenticity is disputed: a *Journal of Agrarian Change* article calls it "the legendary story" and cites a dispute over authenticity (Chen and Zeng, 2004); NPR (2012) found farmers' and officials' accounts diverge. Keep CT's framing: "the founding legend". [source: https://en.lishiyushehui.cn/article/download/332 ; https://www.npr.org/sections/money/2012/01/20/145360447/the-secret-document-that-transformed-ch]
- Third Plenum (December 1978) allowed contracting to groups but still banned contracting to households (*baochan daohu*); Hua Guofeng (1979) allowed it for poor remote households; Anhui's Zhou Yueli reported by end-1979 that 10% of teams had household contracting; Deng Xiaoping (May 1980) backed Fengyang and Feixi. [source: HRS]
- 1982 "No. 1 Document" officially established the system; "total victory" in 1983; communes dismantled from 1983, land stayed collectively owned. By 1984 Zhao Ziyang recalled farmers had more grain than they could sell. [source: HRS; CT]
- Mechanism: higher state purchase prices, fewer products under state monopoly, rural markets restored. [source: HRS]
- Precedents: household contracting in Yongjia, Wenzhou (1956) and *sanzi yibao* in the early 1960s. [source: HRS; GCF]

## 12. Township and village enterprises
- Rooted in commune and brigade enterprises; 1.5 million in 1978; term officially used March 1984; over 12 million by 1985. Employment 28 million (1978) to a peak of 135 million (1996); output 49 billion yuan (1978) to 1.8 trillion yuan (1992). [source: TVE]
- "TVE" denotes location, not ownership; many private firms "wore a red hat" by registering as collectives (Yasheng Huang; Wong). [source: TVE]
- Deng Xiaoping (1987) said TVEs appeared "out of nowhere". [source: TVE]
- Decline from mid-1990s: competition, credit squeeze, official preference for foreign firms; widespread privatisation; World Bank (1997) attributed ~60% of China's air and water pollution to TVEs. [source: TVE]

## 13. "Three rural problems" (sannong), taxes and the 2006 abolition
- *Sannong*: agriculture (*nongye*), villages (*nongcun*), peasants (*nongmin*); theorised by Wen Tiejun from the late 1980s; prioritised by Hu Jintao and Wen Jiabao. [source: SAN; NRR]
- Li Changping, township party secretary in Qipan, Hubei, wrote to Premier Zhu Rongji in March 2000: "农民真苦，农村真穷，农业真危险" ("the peasants' lives are truly bitter, the countryside truly poor, agriculture truly in danger"); published in *Southern Weekend* (24 August 2000), whose readers made him Person of the Year; his book *I Told the Premier the Truth* (2002). The Guardian (2002) called him "China's most famous advocate for peasant rights". [source: LCP; https://www.boaoforum.org/ljnh/en/2016anyjjb/23251.htm ; https://www.aisixiang.com/data/1869.html]
- Chen Guidi and Wu Chuntao's reportage *An Investigation of China's Peasantry* (Jan 2004) on tax extortion and violence against peasants in Anhui was banned in March 2004 after selling more than 250,000 copies; English edition *Will the Boat Sink the Water?* (PublicAffairs, 2006). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_the_Boat_Sink_the_Water ; https://pen.org/will-the-boat-sink-the-water/]
- Agricultural tax abolition: Wen Jiabao announced (March 2005) nationwide exemption in 2006; the NPC Standing Committee on 29 December 2005 annulled the 1958 Agricultural Tax Regulations effective 1 January 2006. AP called it a "2,600-year-old" tax (other sources "over 2,000 years"). The tax was 41% of state revenue in 1949 and under 1% by the early 2000s. [source: https://covid-19.chinadaily.com.cn/english/doc/2005-12/29/content_507775.htm ; https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/chn149780.pdf ; WebSearch summary]
- After 2006 local governments relied increasingly on land sales (link to CT on land finance). [source: WK; CT]
- "Building a New Socialist Countryside" campaign under Hu Jintao (from 2006). [source: HU; TPA; NRR]

## 14. Migrant workers and left-behind children
- NBS migrant worker monitoring: 252.78 million (2011); 277.5 million (2015; 36% of the workforce); 299.73 million (2024, +2.2 million, average age 43.2, 31.6% over 50); 301.15 million (2025, +1.42 million, average age 43.3, ~32% over 50, average monthly income 5,075 yuan). NBS definition: rural-registered people in non-farm work for six months or more a year. [source: MIG; https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202505/20/WS682c743ba310a04af22c08c0.html ; https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-05-05/china-rural-migrant-workforce-ages-as-shift-to-services-weighs-on-pay-102440700.html ; matches CT "almost 300 million in 2024"]
- Rural-urban migrants: 8.9 million (1989) to 23.0 million (1994) (Zhao and Sicular); an estimated 174 million moved 1978–1999. [source: MIG]
- Women were most of the early export-zone workers (CT). [source: CT]
- Left-behind children: 22.9 million (2000 census); 61 million (2010; 37.7% of rural children); UNICEF 2018: about 69 million left behind by one or both parents (~30% of rural children); 52.86% living with neither parent. [source: HK; LBC; matches CT]
- Bijie, Guizhou: in 2015 four left-behind siblings drank pesticide (Wikipedia: "attempted suicide"; widely reported as dying); Bijie had 260,000 left-behind children. In 2012 five left-behind boys died of fumes after lighting a fire in a rubbish bin. [source: LBC]
- Ministry of Civil Affairs set up a left-behind-children office in 2016. [source: LBC]

## 15. Land seizures and protest
- Sun Liping (Tsinghua) estimated up to 180,000 "mass incidents" in 2010 (~490 a day); last official count 87,000 (2005). Yu Jianrong (CASS) wrote in 2010 that land disputes lay behind 65% of rural disturbances. [source: https://chinadigitaltimes.net/2011/09/unrest-grows-as-economy-booms/ ; WK]
- CAS figure cited on Wikipedia: 50 million displaced farmers by end-2011, about 3 million a year. Land finance: FT (2011) 40% of local revenue from land sales; CT gives 38% in 2019. [source: WK; CT]
- Wukan (Lufeng, Guangdong; ~20,000 people): protests began 21–23 September 2011 over land sold to developers without compensation ("give us back our farmland"); representative Xue Jinbo died in police custody in December; villagers expelled officials; police siege from 14 December; Guangdong party chief Wang Yang conceded; 1 and 11 February 2012 secret-ballot elections (6,500+ voters) made protest leader Lin Zuluan village party chief. [source: WK]
- In June 2016 Lin was arrested after a letter on land seizures; he confessed to bribery on state TV; in December 2016 nine villagers were sentenced to two to ten years. [source: WK]

## 16. Poverty alleviation and rural revitalisation (attributed claims)
- World Bank: China's $1.90 poverty rate fell from 88% (1981) to 0.7% (2015); CT: close to 800 million left extreme poverty over four decades (World Bank). [source: POV; CT]
- National line raised from 1,274 to 2,300 yuan (November 2011), leaving 128 million under it. Targeted Poverty Alleviation (*jingzhun fupin*): Xi's visit to Shibadong village, Xiangxi, Hunan, November 2013; formally adopted 2014; household registry; cadres paired with poor households; "first secretaries" sent to villages; 2015 decision to lift 70 million out by 2020. [source: TPA]
- 23 November 2020: government announced absolute poverty eliminated by its 2,300-yuan (2010 prices; ~4,000 yuan in 2020) line. On 25 February 2021 Xi declared "complete victory": 98.99 million rural poor lifted out in eight years; all 832 poor counties and 128,000 villages removed from the list. [source: POV; https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2021/2/25/chinas-xi-declares-complete-victory-against-rural-poverty]
- Caveats: the World Bank's line for upper-middle-income countries ($5.50) is higher — CT: about 13% still poor at it; academics report one-off transfers to push households over the line. Premier Li Keqiang, 28 May 2020: 600 million people had a monthly income of only about 1,000 yuan; most of the lowest-income group are rural. [source: TPA; CT; https://subsites.chinadaily.com.cn/en/2020-05/29/c_541881.htm ; https://www.caixinglobal.com/2020-06-06/opinion-china-has-600-million-people-with-monthly-income-less-than-141-is-that-true-101564071.html]
- Rural revitalisation: announced by Xi at the 19th Party Congress (October 2017); targets of "decisive progress" by 2035 and full revitalisation by 2050; Rural Revitalization Promotion Law adopted 29 April 2021, in force 1 June 2021 (74 articles). [source: https://subsites.chinadaily.com.cn/npc/2021-04/29/c_875840.htm ; https://www.china-briefing.com/news/chinas-rural-revitalization-strategy-opportunities-for-investment/]

## 17. Peasant agency: the historians
- Kate Xiao Zhou, *How the Farmers Changed China: Power of the People* (Westview, 1996): farmers, not leaders, drove decollectivisation and reform, through a movement that was "spontaneous, unorganized, leaderless, non-ideological, apolitical". [source: https://search.worldcat.org/title/33818920 ; https://cato.org/cato-journal/fall-1996/how-farmers-changed-china-power-people-kate-xiao-zhou]
- Yan Yunxiang (UCLA), fieldwork in Xiajia village, Heilongjiang: *The Flow of Gifts* (1996), *Private Life under Socialism* (2003), *The Individualization of Chinese Society* (2009): rise of the individual and the conjugal family, decline of patriarchal hierarchy. [source: YYX]
- Ralph A. Thaxton Jr., *Catastrophe and Contention in Rural China* (Cambridge, 2008), built on some 400 interviews over 20 years in Da Fo, Henan: covert resistance and long-delayed "righteous" retaliation in the reform era. [source: Cambridge review links in §8]
- Jacob Eyferth, *Eating Rice from Bamboo Roots: The Social History of a Community of Handicraft Papermakers in Rural Sichuan, 1920–2000* (Harvard Asia Center, 2009): revolution as redistribution of skill and technical control, from villages to cities and from men to women. [source: https://fairbank.fas.harvard.edu/research/publications/eating-rice-from-bamboo-roots-the-social-history-of-a-community-of-handicraft-papermakers-in-rural-sichuan-1920-2000]
- Kenneth Pomeranz (*The Making of a Hinterland*, 1993): state abandonment shaped peripheral rural North China. [source: §1]
- Mobo Gao, *Gao Village* (Hong Kong UP/Hurst 1999; Hawaii 2007): an insider's 40-year account of his Jiangxi home village; Mao era "not quite so bad", reform gains "not as unambiguous". [source: https://asianreviewofbooks.com/content/gao-village-revisited-the-life-of-rural-people-in-contemporary-china-by-mobo-cf-gao/ ; https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/90595]

## Debates
- **Involution vs. growth**: Huang (falling returns per workday) vs. Pomeranz (comparable living standards to ~1800) and Brandt/Rawski (commercialisation raised output and incomes, 1870–1937). [source: §1]
- **Why the CCP won the villages**: land reform as decisive mobilisation (DeMare) vs. nationalism/war; Liang Shuming's remark that Chiang's "greatest contribution was to make the CCP successful" (MC). [source: LR; MC]
- **Who made reform**: Kate Zhou and Yasheng Huang (rural, bottom-up, private) vs. accounts crediting Deng and provincial leaders such as Wan Li (Anhui) and Zhao Ziyang (Sichuan); Xiaogang as myth vs. history. [source: §11; CT]
- **Mao-era rural gains**: Mobo Gao and Lin Chun (barefoot doctors, schools, human capital) vs. famine, extraction and hukou "caste". [source: CR; CT; HK]
- **Hukou**: prevented slums (Yusuf) vs. institutionalised inequality (Chan; Potters). [source: HK]
- **Poverty "victory"**: official national-line claims vs. higher international lines, sustainability doubts and Li Keqiang's 600 million. [source: §16]
- **Peasant agency under socialism**: Thaxton's resistance, Yan's individualisation, Eyferth's transfer of skill. [source: §17]
