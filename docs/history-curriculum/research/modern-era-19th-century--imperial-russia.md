## 1. The empire in numbers (frame)
- The 1897 census (Russia's first and only imperial census) recorded 125,640,021 people, making Russia the world's third most populous state after the British and Qing empires. [source: CEN]
- The census asked mother tongue, not ethnicity. "Great Russian" speakers were 44.31% of the population. Officials counted Ukrainian ("Little Russian") and Belarusian ("White Russian") as Russian dialects. [source: CEN]
- Religion (1897): Orthodox 69.3%; Muslims 11.1% (13.9 million); Roman Catholics 9.1%; Jews 4.2% (5.2 million); Lutherans 2.8%; Old Believers 1.8%. [source: RE]
- Treaties of Aigun (1858) and Peking (1860) took the Amur region and the future Primorsky Krai (site of Vladivostok) from a Qing China weakened by the Taiping war. Russia sold Alaska to the US in 1867 for $7.2 million (11 million roubles). [source: RE; AL2]

## 2. The Decembrists (1825)
- Alexander I died suddenly on 1 December 1825 (19 November OS). His brother Konstantin had privately renounced the throne two years earlier, so the next brother, Nicholas, was heir. The confusion over the oath gave the conspirators their chance. [source: DEC]
- Roots: Union of Salvation (1816), later Northern Society (St Petersburg) and Southern Society (Tulchin, Ukraine; led by Pavel Pestel). Pestel's programme was more Jacobin and centralising; the Society of United Slavs wanted a democratic federation of Slavic peoples. [source: DEC]
- On 26 December 1825 (14 December OS) officers led about 3,000 soldiers into Senate Square, refusing to swear to Nicholas I and proclaiming loyalty to Konstantin. About 9,000 loyal troops faced them. Their designated leader, Prince Trubetskoy, failed to appear. Kakhovsky fatally shot Governor-General Miloradovich. At day's end Nicholas ordered grapeshot; rebels fleeing over the frozen Neva were fired on and many drowned. [source: DEC]
- Southern revolt: the Chernigov Regiment under Sergei Muravyov-Apostol rose on 10 January 1826 (29 December 1825 OS) and was crushed within 5 days. [source: DEC]
- Five leaders were hanged on 25 July 1826 (13 July OS): Pestel, Ryleyev, Kakhovsky, Sergei Muravyov-Apostol, Bestuzhev-Ryumin. 121 were tried (61 Northern, 37 Southern, 23 United Slavs); many others were exiled to Siberian hard labour (Nerchinsk mines, Chita, Petrovsky Zavod). "Soldier-Decembrists" received thousands of lashes and walked to Siberia in chains. [source: DEC]
- Wives such as Maria Volkonskaya followed husbands into Siberian exile; "Dekabristka" became a Russian byword for wifely devotion. Survivors were amnestied on 26 August 1856 by Alexander II. [source: DEC]
- Legacy: Herzen put profiles of the five executed on the cover of his *Polar Star*; Nekrasov wrote a long poem on the wives; Tolstoy's planned Decembrist novel evolved into *War and Peace*. [source: DEC]
- Hosking (via OAN): "In one sense, the reign of Nicholas I was a protracted epilogue to the Decembrist rising." [source: OAN]

## 3. Nicholas I (r. 1825–1855): "Orthodoxy, Autocracy, and Nationality"
- Born 6 July 1796; reigned from 1 December 1825; died 2 March 1855 of pneumonia during the Crimean War. Also King of Poland (crowned in Warsaw May 1829, the only Russian monarch so crowned) and Grand Duke of Finland. [source: NIC1]
- The Third Section of His Majesty's Own Chancellery (political police) under Alexander Benckendorff: 300 gendarmes and 16 office staff; informers and mail interception. [source: NIC1]
- Sergey Uvarov, Minister of Education, proposed the triad in a circular of 2 April 1833 (date per Riasanovsky): education "in the joint spirit of Orthodoxy, Autocracy and Nationality" (*Pravoslavie, samoderzhavie, narodnost'*). Also called "Official Nationality"; critics saw it as a call for Russification. Hosking calls it Russia's first statewide political ideology since the 16th century. [source: OAN]
- Ended local autonomies: Bessarabia 1828, Poland 1830/1832, the Jewish *qahal* 1843. Finland kept its autonomy, partly because Finnish soldiers helped crush the Polish rising. [source: NIC1]
- Jewish conscription edict of 26 August 1827: Jewish boys served 25 years from age 18; many were taken earlier, from 12, into Cantonist schools, and some were pressured to convert. In 1851 the Jewish population was 2.4 million. [source: NIC1]
- Army of about one million men in a population of 60–70 million. [source: NIC1]
- 1849: helped the Habsburgs crush the Hungarian revolution (cross-ref Nationalism chapter). [source: NIC1]
- Russo-Persian War 1826–28 (Treaty of Turkmenchay): Persia ceded its last Caucasian territories (modern Armenia and the rest of Azerbaijan; capture of Erivan by Paskevich 1827) and granted extraterritoriality to Russians. Russo-Turkish War 1828–29. [source: NIC1]
- Censorship and universities: 3,600 university students in 1854, 1,000 fewer than in 1848. Seton-Watson: "the intellectual atmosphere remained oppressive until the end of the reign." Kiev University founded 1834. [source: NIC1]
- Pyotr Chaadayev wrote eight *Philosophical Letters* (1826–31, in French) indicting Russia's backwardness; after one was published he was officially declared insane and placed under medical supervision. [source: CHA]
- Despite repression, Pushkin, Gogol, Turgenev and others brought Russian literature international stature. [source: NIC1]

## 4. The Crimean War (1853–1856)
- Dates 16 October 1853 – 30 March 1856. Proximate cause: Nicholas I demanded protection of the Ottoman Orthodox; Russia occupied the Danubian Principalities in July 1853; the Ottomans declared war in October. Most historians see the Holy Places dispute as a pretext. [source: CRW]
- Sinop, 30 November 1853: the Russian fleet destroyed an Ottoman squadron; Western press called it the "massacre of Sinope". Britain and France declared war 28 March 1854; Sardinia joined. [source: CRW]
- Allies landed in Crimea September 1854: Alma (Sept 1854), Balaclava (25 October 1854; Charge of the Light Brigade, 278 casualties of about 700), Inkerman. Siege of Sevastopol; Admiral Nakhimov killed 30 June 1855; Sevastopol fell in September 1855 after the French took the Malakoff (final bombardment and assault from 5 September). [source: CRW]
- Also fought in the Caucasus (Kars, surrendered to Muravyov 1855), the Baltic (Bomarsund on Åland destroyed August 1854; Sveaborg bombarded August 1855), the White Sea and Kamchatka (Allied landing at Petropavlovsk beaten off, September 1854). [source: CRW]
- Casualties (contested): Russian dead estimated 450,000–630,000, the vast majority from disease (one calculation: only 1 in 6 Russian deaths from combat). France 95,615 dead; Britain 22,182; Sardinia 2,166; Ottoman estimates range from 45,400 to 400,000. Disease killed more than combat on all sides. [source: CRW]
- Nearly 200,000 Crimean Tatars displaced (Mara Kozelsky, *Slavic Review* 2008). [source: CRW]
- Russian weakness: under 1,600 km of railway in the whole empire, none near Crimea; about 8,000 km of paved roads. [source: CRW]
- Treaty of Paris, 30 March 1856: the Black Sea neutralised and closed to warships, no arsenals on its coast; Russia returned Kars and southern Bessarabia. Russia renounced the Black Sea clauses in 1871 (Treaty of London). The Declaration of Paris outlawed privateering. [source: CRW; TOP]
- Media war: W. H. Russell's reports in *The Times* and Roger Fenton's photographs. The telegraph reached the Black Sea coast in late 1854, and an undersea cable to Crimea in April 1855 brought news to London in hours. Tolstoy served at Sevastopol and wrote *Sevastopol Sketches*. [source: CRW]
- Florence Nightingale reached Scutari (Selimiye Barracks, Üsküdar) in early November 1854. Mary Seacole, a Jamaican-born nurse and businesswoman (1805–1881), went in 1855 and ran the "British Hotel" for sick and convalescent officers. Recent commentators argue that Nightingale's wartime achievements were exaggerated by the press, though her later work professionalising nursing is not disputed. [source: NIG; SEA]
- Grand Duke Konstantin Nikolayevich after the defeat: "We cannot deceive ourselves any longer; we must say that we are both weaker and poorer than the first-class powers, and furthermore poorer not only in material terms but in mental resources, especially in matters of administration." [source: CRW, citing Lieven]
- Mikhail Pogodin's 1853 memorandum to Nicholas I complained of Western double standards (France takes Algeria, England annexes Indian principalities, yet Russian moves "disturb the balance of power"); Nicholas wrote on it: "That is the whole point." Figes uses it to show Russian resentment of the West. [source: NIC1; CRW]

## 5. Emancipation (1861) and the Great Reforms
(Core emancipation facts are in the Abolition chapter; see overlap note.)
- 1857 census: 23.1 million privately owned serfs out of 62.5 million (37.7%). Pipes: about 38%. State peasants were a similar number (about 23.1 million in the official figures). [source: SER; EMA]
- By 1859 a third of noble estates and two-thirds of their serfs were mortgaged to state or noble banks (Figes). [source: EMA]
- Terms: two-year transition; landlords kept "cut-off" lands (*otrezki*); the state advanced 75% of the price to landlords, and peasants repaid the state with interest over 49 years. Land was held through the commune (*mir*). Typically a peasant could buy about half the land he had worked, or take a quarter free (the "pauper's allotment"). Redemption became compulsory in 1881, payments were cut 13% in 1883 and cancelled in 1907. [source: EMA; SER]
- In many places peasants refused to believe the manifesto was genuine, and troops dispersed crowds (Eric Wolf). [source: EMA]
- In Congress Poland the 1864 emancipation, imposed after the January Uprising to weaken the *szlachta*, was more generous than the Russian one. Peasants there got larger plots and paid market price only once, and 90% of the serfs who got larger plots were in Congress Poland. [source: AL2; SER; JAN]
- Zemstvos (1864): elected district and provincial councils with limited taxing power, spending on schools and medicine. Weighted to nobles: 40% peasant quota at district level, none at provincial level. None in the Baltic, the nine western (former Polish) provinces and most of the borderlands. 34 provincial zemstvos before 1905. Town dumas 1870. Noble share of zemstvo seats rose from 55% (1886) to 72% (1903) after Alexander III's 1890 law. [source: AL2; ZEM]
- Judicial reform 1864: open trials, irremovable judges, juries, justices of the peace. [source: AL2]
- Military reform (Dmitry Milyutin, war minister 1861–1881): universal conscription from 1 January 1874 for all classes including nobles. MIL: service of 6 years from age 21 instead of 25 years. Fifteen military districts; corporal punishment and branding abolished. [source: AL2; MIL]
- Educated Jews (secondary-school graduates) and some other categories could live outside the Pale under Alexander II. [source: AL2]
- After Karakozov's attempt (April 1866), Alexander's government became more conservative (Dmitry Tolstoy at Education). [source: AL2]
- Finland: Alexander II reconvened the Diet in 1863 (first since 1809), raised Finnish to equal status with Swedish (decree of 30 July 1863), and allowed its own currency (markka). He is still remembered as "the Good Tsar" in Finland; his statue stands in Helsinki's Senate Square. [source: AL2; FIN]
- Russo-Turkish War 1877–78: independence of Romania, Serbia and Montenegro and an autonomous Bulgaria; Alexander is "Tsar Liberator" in Bulgaria (Sofia monument 1907). [source: AL2]
- Markevich and Zhuravskaya (*American Economic Review*, 2018): abolition brought "substantial increases in agricultural productivity, industrial output, and peasants' nutrition". [source: SER]

## 6. Intelligentsia, populism, terror and 1881
- Alexander Herzen founded the Free Russian Press in London (1853) and with Nikolay Ogarev published *Kolokol* ("The Bell"), smuggled into Russia. [source: HER]
- Nikolay Chernyshevsky wrote *What Is to Be Done?* in the Peter and Paul Fortress (1863). Its ascetic revolutionary Rakhmetov inspired a generation; Lenin used the title. Mock execution, penal servitude 1864–72, exile in Vilyuisk, Siberia, 1872–83. [source: CHE]
- Sergei Nechaev (son of former serfs) wrote the *Catechism of a Revolutionary* and had a comrade, Ivan Ivanov, murdered (1869), the case behind Dostoevsky's *Demons*. [source: NEC]
- "Going to the People", 1874: thousands of students went to the villages, inspired by Lavrov (patient propaganda) and Bakunin (immediate revolt). Kropotkin called it "the mad summer of 1874". Peasants were suspicious, some angered by attacks on the tsar. More than a thousand were arrested by autumn 1874; many waited 3–4 years for trial (the Trial of the 193, 1877–78, where sympathetic juries acquitted many). [source: GTP; AL2]
- Vera Zasulich shot the St Petersburg governor Trepov on 5 February 1878 (24 January OS) after he had a political prisoner, Bogolyubov, flogged. A jury acquitted her on 12 April 1878 (31 March OS), and crowds carried her through the streets. [source: ZAS]
- Land and Liberty (*Zemlya i Volya*) split on 15 August 1879: *Narodnaya Volya* ("People's Will", terror) vs *Chorny Peredel* ("Black Repartition", Plekhanov, propaganda; later Russian Marxism). The NV Executive Committee "sentenced" Alexander II to death on 25–26 August 1879. Fewer than 50 people ever sat on the Executive Committee, men and women together. Jews were well represented, especially among women; about a third of the party's women were Jewish. [source: NV; ASS]
- Earlier attempts: Karakozov 1866; Berezowski (Paris, 1867); Soloviev 1879; the attempt to blow up the imperial train; Stepan Khalturin's Winter Palace bomb, 5 February 1880, killing 11. [source: AL2; ASS]
- Loris-Melikov headed a Supreme Executive Commission from February 1880. His proposals for limited consultative representation were approved by Alexander shortly before his death and cancelled by Alexander III on Pobedonostsev's advice. [source: AL2; AL3]
- Assassination, 13 March 1881 (1 March OS), Catherine Canal, St Petersburg. Planned chiefly by Andrei Zhelyabov; after his arrest two days earlier, Sophia Perovskaya directed it, signalling with a handkerchief. Nikolai Rysakov's bomb damaged the carriage; when the tsar got out, Ignacy Hryniewiecki (a Pole) threw the fatal bomb and died that evening. A Cossack and a 14-year-old delivery boy were also killed. Bombs made by Nikolai Kibalchich. [source: ASS]
- Zhelyabov, Perovskaya, Kibalchich, Timofey Mikhailov and Rysakov were hanged on 15 April 1881. Hesya Helfman's sentence was deferred for pregnancy and commuted; she died in January 1882. Perovskaya was the first woman in Russia executed for a political crime. Vera Figner was arrested in 1883 and served 20 years. [source: ASS; PER]
- Church of the Savior on Blood built on the spot, 1883–1907. [source: ASS]
- Aleksandr Ulyanov, Lenin's elder brother, was among five hanged in May 1887 for a plot to kill Alexander III. [source: AL3]
- Later parties: RSDLP, first congress 1898 (Minsk); split into Bolsheviks (Lenin) and Mensheviks (Martov) at the 1903 congress. Socialist Revolutionaries founded 1902; their Combat Organisation killed interior ministers Sipyagin (1902) and Plehve (1904) and Grand Duke Sergei (1905). The Jewish Labour Bund was founded in Vilna on 7 October 1897; women were more than a third of its members. [source: RSD; SR; BUND]

## 7. Alexander III (r. 1881–1894): counter-reform
- Reigned 13 March 1881 – 1 November 1894. Domestic "counter-reforms"; known as the "Tsar-Peacemaker" because Russia fought no major war. Franco-Russian alliance 1892/94. [source: AL3]
- Konstantin Pobedonostsev, Chief Procurator of the Holy Synod 1880–1905 and tutor to Alexander III and Nicholas II, denounced parliaments, juries, a free press and secular education as alien, and called democracy "the insupportable dictatorship of vulgar crowd". [source: POB]
- Land captains (*zemskie nachalniki*, 1889), appointed from the local nobility, supervised peasant communes; 1890 zemstvo law increased noble weight. [source: AL3; ZEM]
- Famine of 1891–92: deaths are disputed. Some historians estimate 375,000–400,000, mostly from disease. Others argue the excess mortality of 1892 is explained by cholera (about 300,000 deaths). Tolstoy organised relief. The opposition attacked late grain-export bans; Mikhail Davydov calls "hungry exports" a myth. [source: FAM]

## 8. Russification
- After the Crimean defeat and the 1863 Polish rising, Russification intensified to forestall separatism. [source: RUS]
- Poland: after 1864 Polish banned in public places; in the 1880s in schools and offices of Congress Poland. Secret Polish teaching (the Flying University); by Russian estimates about a third of Congress Poland's inhabitants took part in clandestine teaching by the early 1900s. Vilnius University closed 1832. [source: RUS]
- Lithuania: Governor-General Mikhail Muravyov ("the Hangman", 1863–65) closed Polish and Lithuanian schools. The Lithuanian press ban (Latin-script printing prohibited) lasted 1865–1904, defeated by book smuggling. Martial law in Lithuania introduced in 1863 lasted 40 years. [source: RUS; AL2]
- Ukraine: the Valuev Circular of 18 (30) July 1863 banned religious and educational books in Ukrainian (belles-lettres allowed), quoting the Kiev Censorship Committee that "a separate Little Russian language never existed, does not exist, and shall not exist". The Ems Ukaz, signed by Alexander II at Bad Ems on 30 May 1876 (18 May OS), banned Ukrainian printing and imports except reprints of old documents; Ukrainian theatre restricted in 1884. Eased only in 1905; 34 Ukrainian newspapers founded in 1905 alone; a Ukrainian Club of 44 deputies in the First Duma. [source: VAL; EMS; R05]
- Taras Shevchenko (9 March 1814 – 10 March 1861), born a serf; his freedom bought for 2,500 roubles on 5 May 1838 (painter Bryullov's circle); *Kobzar* (1840) founded modern Ukrainian literature. For links to the Brotherhood of Saints Cyril and Methodius he was exiled as a private soldier to the Orenburg garrison (Orsk) in Central Asia, freed under Alexander II, barred from Ukraine, and died in St Petersburg days after the emancipation manifesto. [source: SHE]
- Baltic: 14 September 1885 ukaz made Russian mandatory for Baltic officials; extended to municipal proceedings in 1889; Russian the language of instruction by the early 1890s. [source: RUS]
- Alexander III promoted Russian-language schooling in Polish, Baltic-German and Finnish areas. [source: AL3]
- Finland: Nicholas II's February Manifesto of 15 February 1899 (pushed through the Senate under Governor-General Bobrikov's threats) asserted imperial authority over Finnish law. A petition of 523,000 names (the "Great Petition") and *Pro Finlandia* (1,050 foreign signatories) answered it. Russian co-official 1900. Bobrikov received dictatorial powers in April 1903 and was assassinated by Eugen Schauman in June 1904. Finns call 1899–1905 and 1908–17 the "times of oppression" (*sortokaudet*). [source: RFIN; FIN]
- Georgia: Kartli-Kakheti annexed 1801; the Georgian Church's autocephaly abolished in 1811 (made an exarchate of the Russian Church); a noble plot of 1832 against Russian rule was discovered; Guria revolt 1841. [source: GEO]
- Theodore Weeks (via R05): officials "never succeeded in coming up with a legal definition of 'Pole'", making identity a function of birth. [source: R05]

## 9. Jews, the Pale and pogroms
- The Pale of Settlement was created under Catherine II in 1791. At its height it held over five million Jews, about 40% of the world's Jews; it was abolished by the Provisional Government on 20 March 1917. Some two million Jews emigrated from it 1881–1914, mainly to the US. [source: PALE]
- Jews were about 4% of the empire's population (4.2% in 1897). [source: R05; RE]
- 1881–82: more than 200 anti-Jewish riots, mainly in Ukraine (Yelizavetgrad, 15–16 April 1881 OS / 27 April NS; Kiev, the worst of 1881; Warsaw; Odessa). One assassin (Hesya Helfman) was Jewish, and her role was exaggerated. At least 40 Jews killed April–December 1881; another count gives 25 Jews and 25 rioters killed in 259 pogroms in 1881–83. Thousands of homes destroyed. Jewish press called them "Storms in the South" (*sufot ba-negev*). Jewish students organised self-defence in Odessa. [source: POG]
- May Laws (Temporary Regulations), 15 May 1882 (3 May OS), proposed by Interior Minister Ignatyev: no new Jewish settlement outside towns and shtetls, no land leases outside towns, no Sunday trading. Temporary, but lasted more than 30 years. Educational quotas (10% in the Pale, 5% outside, 3% in the capitals) from 1886/87. About 20,000 Jews expelled from Moscow in 1891. US President Benjamin Harrison protested to Congress on 9 December 1891. [source: MAY; PALE]
- Kishinev, April 1903: after blood-libel agitation in Pavel Krushevan's *Bessarabets*, 47–49 Jews killed, about 500 wounded, more than 1,500 homes destroyed; international outcry; Bialik's poem "In the City of Slaughter"; Tolstoy and Gorky blamed the government. *The Times* published a dispatch purportedly from Plehve ordering officials not to stop rioters — it was a forgery. [source: KIS; POG]
- 1903–06 wave: an estimated 2,000 Jews dead. After the October Manifesto pogroms broke out in 660 towns; at least 400 Jews (and 100 non-Jews) killed in Odessa; Black Hundreds incited violence. Nicholas II privately admired the mobs. [source: POG; NIC2]

## 10. Poland: 1830–31 and 1863–64
(Nationalism chapter covers the outline; add these details.)
- November Uprising: began 29 November 1830 when cadets under Piotr Wysocki attacked the Belweder; Grand Duke Konstantin escaped. Ended 21 October 1831. Spread to Lithuania, Belarus and Right-Bank Ukraine (Emilia Plater fought in Lithuania). The Sejm deposed Nicholas as king in 1831. The Organic Statute (1832) ended Polish autonomy. Thousands left in the "Great Emigration". Forces: about 150,000 Polish vs 180,000–200,000 Russian. [source: NOV; NIC1]
- January Uprising: 22 January 1863 – 18 June 1864 (last fighter into 1865); guerrilla war across Poland, Lithuania, Belarus, Latvia and Ukraine. Romuald Traugutt led from 17 October 1863 and was hanged at the Warsaw Citadel on 5 August 1864. Prussia (Bismarck) concluded a military convention with Russia to help suppress the rising, which provoked protests from other governments. Ruthenian peasants seldom joined; some helped Russian forces. [source: JAN]
- Repression figures differ: official Russian data give 396 executed and 18,672 exiled to Siberia; another passage gives 128 hanged under Muravyov in Lithuania and 9,423 exiled; over 60,000 imprisoned and deported altogether; Norman Davies gives 80,000 deported, "the single largest deportation in Russian history". 1,660 estates confiscated in Poland and 1,794 in Lithuania. [source: JAN]
- Alexander II to the Polish nobility, 1856: "Gentlemen, let us have no dreams!" (JAN renders it "forget any dreams"). [source: AL2; JAN]

## 11. The Caucasus: Shamil and the Circassians
- Caucasian War: 1817 – 21 May 1864 (OS; 2 June NS). Commanders Yermolov (1816–27), Vorontsov (1844–53), Baryatinsky (1853–56). Pushkin wrote *The Prisoner of the Caucasus* about it; Tolstoy fought there. [source: CAW]
- Imam Shamil (26 June 1797 – 4 February 1871), an Avar from Gimry, Naqshbandi Sufi; third imam of the Caucasian Imamate from 1834 after Ghazi Muhammad (killed 1832) and Hamzat Bek. Survived the siege of Akhulgo (June–August 1839). Destroyed Vorontsov's column at Dargo (1845). Surrendered in 1859 (at Gunib, to Baryatinsky's army of about 250,000); exiled to Kaluga, later Kiev; allowed to make the hajj in 1869, met Emir Abdelkader at Mecca, and died in Medina in 1871. [source: SHA; CAW] Caveat: CAW says Shamil was "captured by Dmitry Milyutin"; MIL says Milyutin "took part in the capture". Say the army under Baryatinsky.
- Hadji Murad, Shamil's lieutenant, is the subject of Tolstoy's novella *Hadji Murat*. (The article exists; novella attribution standard but cross-check if used.) [source: CAW]
- In 1857 Dmitry Milyutin proposed mass expulsion of the Circassians. Charles King (*The Ghost of Freedom*) summarises Milyutin's view: "eliminating the Circassians was to be an end in itself – to cleanse the land of hostile elements". Alexander II endorsed it. [source: AL2; CIR]
- Alexander II's rescript of 24 June 1861 (as quoted from Natho, *Circassian History*): "A few years of persistent efforts remain to utterly force out the hostile mountaineers from the fertile countries they occupy and settle on the latter a Russian Christian population forever." [source: AL2] Caveat: secondary quotation via a Circassian-diaspora author; attribute carefully or paraphrase.
- General Nikolay Yevdokimov ordered the clearance of the western Caucasus from October 1862: villages and food burned, survivors driven to the Black Sea coast. Russian field reports called it *ochishchenie* ("cleansing"). Victory parade at Qbaada (Krasnaya Polyana, near Sochi) on 21 May 1864. [source: CIR]
- Refugee numbers vary: Russian/Soviet estimates of those leaving 1858–64 range from about 312,000 to 493,000 (Volkova, Dzidzariia, Berzhe, Main Staff 418,000); Ottoman records show over a million Caucasus immigrants by 1879. Deaths: Walter Richmond estimates at least 600,000 dead; Shenfield "hardly fewer than one million, and may well have been closer to one-and-a-half million". Many died in camps at Trabzon, Samsun and Varna (in Samsun up to 110,000 refugees, over 200 deaths a day at times). The 1897 census counted about 150,000 Circassians left in the region. [source: CIR; Richmond via https://www.everand.com/book/395139379/The-Circassian-Genocide] Use ranges, not one figure.
- The Circassian name for it is *Tsitsekun*. The Duma rejected a 2005 Circassian petition for recognition in 2006. Recognised as genocide by Kabardino-Balkaria (1992), Adygea (1994), Georgia (20 May 2011) and Ukraine (9 January 2025); Russia rejects the term. [source: CIR]
- Kabarda's population fell sharply in the early war years; sources differ (350,000 → 50,000 by 1818; or 200,000 in 1790 → 30,000 in 1830). [source: CAW] Use only as "fell by most of its population, by different estimates".
- Circassian diaspora settled across the Ottoman Balkans, Anatolia and the Levant (Jordan, Syria); Circassians later appear in the Egyptian elite (cross-ref Nationalism chapter's "Turco-Circassian" officers). [source: CIR; Nationalism notes]

## 12. Central Asia
- Kazakh steppe: the Junior Jüz accepted Russian protection in 1731; Russia claimed suzerainty over all three jüzes but did not control them for a century. The 1822 *Regulations for the Siberian Kirgiz* made Kazakhs *inorodtsy*. [source: RCA]
- Kenesary Qasymuly (1802–1847), grandson of Ablai Khan, last khan of the Kazakhs, led a revolt from 1837 against Russian forts and Cossack raids. [source: KEN; RCA]
- Russian settlers on Kazakh land drove many Kazakhs over the border into China (19th c.). [source: RUS]
- Failed winter expedition against Khiva, 1839–40 (Perovsky): camels died en masse. Russian captives in Khiva were a pretext; Richmond Shakespear led 416 Russian slaves to the Caspian in 1840. [source: RCA]
- Ak-Mechet (Kokand) taken 1853; Fort Vernoye (Almaty) 1854; Pishpek (Bishkek) 1862; Turkestan city 1864 (Veryovkin bombarded the Yasavi mausoleum). [source: RCA]
- Tashkent: Mikhail Chernyayev besieged it 9 May – 17 June 1865, exceeding vague instructions (Gorchakov had wanted an independent city-state); the Kokand regent Alimqul was killed. Turkestan Governor-Generalship created July 1867 under Konstantin von Kaufman, at Tashkent. Samarkand surrendered May 1868; Bukhara defeated at Zerabulak 2 June 1868 and became a vassal (treaty 5 July 1868). [source: TAS; RCA]
- Khiva conquered 1873 and made a protectorate; Kokand rebelled in 1875 and was annexed in 1876 (Skobelev). [source: RCA]
- Turkmen: Teke Turkmen repulsed Lomakin at Geok Tepe (1879). Skobelev besieged it December 1880 – January 1881. RCA: at least 14,000 Tekes killed. Skobelev's report: 6,500 bodies buried inside, 8,000 "of both sexes" killed in the pursuit. GEOK also says all males who failed to escape were killed and about 5,000 women and children spared, and gives a Teke loss estimate of 20,000. Transcaspian Oblast annexed May 1881; Merv submitted 1884 (occupied 16 March 1884); Panjdeh crisis with Britain/Afghanistan 1885. [source: RCA; GEOK]
- "Great Game": RCA judges that Britain did little to stop the conquest except near Afghanistan; Morrison's 2020 book firmly rejects the Great Game framing (reviewers). Afghan border demarcated 1886 and 1893; Anglo-Russian Entente 1907. [source: RCA; Morrison search]
- In 1897 Russians were only 4% of the population in four of the five Central Asian oblasts, living mostly in European quarters of the main towns. [source: RE]
- Andijan uprising, 29 May 1898 (17 May OS): about 1,500 men under the Naqshbandi sheikh Dukchi Ishan (Muhammad Ali Madali) attacked the Russian barracks; 22 Russian soldiers killed; 18 executed including the leader; 546 arrested. Most rebels were Kyrgyz. [source: AND]

## 13. Siberia and Indigenous peoples
- Mikhail Speransky, governor-general of Siberia from 1819, drafted the 1822 Statute on the Administration of Inorodtsy, a legal category for non-Slavic subjects (above all Siberian, Central Asian and Far Eastern peoples). It brought varying protections (exemption from military service, tax relief, protection of grazing land, self-administration); the term grew pejorative. Some peoples, such as the Chukchi, were classed as only "incompletely dependent". [source: INO; SPE]
- Earlier colonisation was catastrophic: e.g. the Yakut population fell an estimated 70% between 1642 and 1682, mostly from smallpox; about 100,000 of 150,000 Itelmen and Koryaks died after 1697 (Kamchatka). By the 1820s almost all Yakuts were nominally Orthodox while keeping older practices; gold discoveries and later the Trans-Siberian brought more Russians; Yakut literature rose in the late 19th century. [source: YAK; IPS]
- In the 19th century Siberian regionalists (*oblastniki*) acknowledged that natives had suffered violence "of almost genocidal proportions" from colonisation. [source: IPS]
- Russian America: Russian traders took Aleut women and children hostage to force men to hunt sea otters and foxes; the Russian-American Company governed much of the North Pacific fur trade in the 1820s; Alaska sold 1867. [source: ALE; AL2]
- Exile and *katorga*: after the 1847 penal law, exile and hard labour became common punishments for national rebels; Chekhov visited the Sakhalin penal colony in 1891 and wrote *Sakhalin Island*. [source: KAT]
- Trans-Siberian migration: about four million peasants settled in Siberia 1906–14 (just after this chapter's end; mention as consequence). [source: TSR]

## 14. Muslim reformers: Gasprinsky and the Jadids
- Ismail Gasprinsky (Gaspıralı; 20 March 1851 – 24 September 1914), Crimean Tatar. Founded the newspaper *Tercüman* ("Interpreter"; GAS gives 1883, ran to 1918), read across Turkic regions of the empire and in Egypt and Iran. Opened the first "new method" (*usul-i jadid*) school in Crimea in 1884, teaching reading phonetically in the mother tongue plus geography, history, mathematics and science. Promoted a common simplified Turkic literary language intelligible "by the boatman of the Bosphorus and by the camel driver of Kashgar". Started *Alem-i Nisvan* (Women's World), edited by his daughter Şefiqa. [source: GAS; JAD]
- Gasprinsky, *Russian Muslims* (1881): "Our ignorance is the main reason for our backward condition… we must learn European ideas from European sources." [source: GAS]
- Jadids called themselves "progressives"; they clashed with traditionalist ulama (*qadimists*). Adeeb Khalid sees the conflict as a struggle over cultural authority within Central Asian society. Mahmudkhoja Behbudiy brought the ideas to Turkestan; Abdurauf Fitrat in Bukhara. Jadid schools banned corporal punishment and encouraged girls (few attended). 166 new Tatar-language periodicals 1905–17; the first Turkic-language newspaper in Turkestan only after 1905. [source: JAD]
- Tsarist "Russo-native" schools from 1884 in Turkestan reached few: under 300 Turkic students in Russian higher primary schools in 1916. [source: JAD]
- Gasprinsky co-founded the Union of Muslims (*Ittifaq al-Muslimin*), created in the 1905 era. [source: GAS]

## 15. Witte, industrialisation and the Trans-Siberian
(Numbers in Second Industrial Revolution chapter; add these.)
- Sergei Witte (29 June 1849 – 13 March 1915), from a Lutheran Baltic German family; schooled in Tiflis; a railway manager (Odessa Railways, Southwestern Railways). Finance minister August 1892 – 1903; state alcohol monopoly 1895; 1897 law limiting working hours; negotiated the Chinese Eastern Railway across Manchuria. Framed the October Manifesto and became first chairman of the Council of Ministers (prime minister) on 20 October 1905 (OS). Led the Russian delegation at Portsmouth (1905); later styled Count Witte. [source: WIT]
- Trans-Siberian: imperial rescript 9 March 1891; Tsarevich Nicholas inaugurated construction in Vladivostok on 19 May 1891. Built 1891–1916; Circum-Baikal section completed 1904 (until then, ferries and winter sledges across Lake Baikal); Amur line 1916. Moscow–Vladivostok 9,289 km. French finance (Hottinguer) helped. [source: TSR; NIC2]
- The Chelyabinsk tariff break (1896) protected central Russian grain from cheap Siberian grain. [source: TSR]

## 16. Nicholas II, the war with Japan and 1905
- Nicholas II reigned from 1894. Khodynka Field disaster at the coronation festivities, 30 May 1896, seen as an ill omen. Attacked by a policeman at Ōtsu, Japan, in 1891; Rotem Kowner argues he came to regard the Japanese as weak and inferior. [source: NIC2]
- Russia leased Liaodong and Port Arthur in 1898 and took timber concessions on the Yalu; Japan offered to recognise Russian dominance in Manchuria for a free hand in Korea. Gojong of Korea pursued neutrality. War 8 February 1904 – 5 September 1905. The Baltic Fleet sailed about seven months and 18,000 nautical miles before Tsushima (27–28 May 1905: 5,045 Russian dead, 6,016 captured). [source: RJW; TSU] Further detail in the Meiji chapter.
- Bloody Sunday, 22 January 1905 (9 January OS): Father Georgy Gapon's Assembly of Russian Factory Workers marched with a petition after a Putilov strike had spread to about 150,000 workers in 382 factories. The tsar was at Tsarskoye Selo. Deaths: officials 96 (another official figure 132); infobox 143–234; anti-government sources over 4,000; moderate estimates around 1,000 killed or wounded. About 414,000 struck in January. Gapon was killed in April 1906 (by SRs who suspected him of police links; detail not confirmed here — say "killed in 1906"). [source: BS; GAP]
- Nicholas's diary that evening: "Difficult day! In St. Petersburg there were serious disturbances due to the desire of workers to get to the Winter Palace. The troops had to shoot in different places of the city, there were many dead and wounded. Lord, how painful and bad!" (NIC2's translation from the State Archive text; it warns that Massie's translation is not authentic.) [source: NIC2]
- Potemkin mutiny: began 27 June 1905 off Tendra Spit over borscht made from maggoty meat; ship sailed to Odessa under a red flag; crew surrendered at Constanța, Romania; basis of Eisenstein's 1925 film. [source: POT]
- Ivanovo-Voznesensk ("the Russian Manchester") textile strike, May–June 1905: elected mill delegates formed an early soviet; 40,000 workers assembled on 13 May. [source: R05]
- Peasant uprisings from summer 1905, seizing land and tools. [source: R05]
- October 1905 general strike; St Petersburg Soviet. October Manifesto signed 30 October 1905 (17 October OS), drafted by Witte and Obolensky: civil liberties, a legislative Duma, wider franchise. Nicholas regretted it ("sick with shame", per R05, flagged citation needed — do not quote). [source: R05; NIC2]
- The "Days of Freedom" (17 October to early December): over 400 meetings in Moscow in four weeks; at least 67 unions in Moscow and 58 in St Petersburg; Kadets, Octobrists, Union of the Russian People formed. [source: R05]
- Moscow armed uprising, December 1905: crushed by the Semyonovsky Regiment with artillery; about a thousand dead by 18 December (5 December OS). [source: R05]
- Casualty/repression figures (contested): Kovalevsky told the Duma that by April 1906 more than 14,000 had been executed and 75,000 imprisoned; Brian Taylor says deaths were in the "thousands" (one source over 13,000). R05 infobox: 15,000 revolutionaries killed. Revolutionaries killed 7,293 people (2,640 officials) between 1906 and 1909. [source: R05]
- Fundamental Laws of 24 April 1906 (R05; three days before the Duma met) kept the tsar's control of ministers, army, foreign policy; State Council made an upper house (20 February 1906). First Duma opened 27 April 1906 and was dissolved after 73 days; Stolypin replaced Witte. Revolution's end usually dated to the coup of 16 June 1907. [source: R05]
- Borderlands in 1905: Congress Poland — 93.2% of industrial workers struck in 1905; Łódź insurrection June 1905; Rosa Luxemburg said Poland "in 1905 marched at the head of the Russian Revolution"; some Polish historians call it a fourth uprising. Finland — general strike 30 October – 6 November 1905; the 1906 parliament with universal suffrage made Finland the first European territory where women could stand for election. Estonia — troops killed 94 at a Tallinn market meeting (29 October 1905 NS); about 160 manors looted, about 400 killed by the army. Latvia — troops killed 73 in Riga on 26 January 1905; elected parish bodies in 94% of parishes; war between Latvian peasants and Baltic German nobles. Georgia — the Gurian Republic (peasant self-rule). Ukraine — the press revival noted above. [source: R05; FIN; GEO]
- Lenin later: "Without the 'dress rehearsal' of 1905, the victory of the October Revolution in 1917 would have been impossible." — *"Left-Wing" Communism: An Infantile Disorder* (1920), ch. 3. [source: https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1920/lwc/ch03.htm (via search result)]

## Debates
- **Was Russia an empire or a nation-state in the making?** Hosking: the needs of the empire "impeded the formation of a nation" (HOS; review summaries of *Russia: People and Empire*). Kappeler insists on a multiethnic history rather than a Russian one. RUS notes imperial officials claimed Russification of Ukrainians and Belarusians was "organic consolidation" of an "all-Russian" nation, contested by Ukrainian and Belarusian nationalists. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Hosking; https://www.wilsonquarterly.com/quarterly/undefined/russia-people-and-empire; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russification]
- **Circassian genocide or wartime expulsion?** Circassian organisations, Richmond, Shenfield and others use "genocide" (95–97% killed or deported; 600,000 to 1.5 million dead). Georgia (2011) and Ukraine (2025) recognise it; Russia rejects the term, and Yeltsin (1994) called Circassian resistance legitimate but did not accept tsarist guilt for genocide. Figures rest on fragmentary Russian and Ottoman data (Alan Fisher: refugees left "without passing any official border point"). The writer should name the debate and give ranges. Note the main Wikipedia article is strongly advocacy-toned; prefer Richmond's estimates and say they are estimates. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circassian_genocide]
- **Were pogroms state-organised?** Long believed so; Hans Rogger, I. Michael Aronson and John Klier found no evidence for central organisation of the 1881–82 wave, though authorities often tolerated violence and discriminatory laws fed it. For 1903–06 some historians (Radzinsky) argue many were incited by local officials and the Okhrana; the Plehve "dispatch" in *The Times* was a forgery; Nicholas II privately approved of the mobs. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pogroms_in_the_Russian_Empire; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kishinev_pogrom; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_II]
- **Did emancipation work?** Traditional view: peasants got too little land, heavy redemption payments, and the commune trapped them (EMA; SER). Markevich & Zhuravskaya (2018) find gains in productivity, output and nutrition. Gerschenkron's model of state-led late industrialisation (Second Industrial Revolution chapter) treats the commune as a brake. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serfdom_in_Russia; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emancipation_reform_of_1861]
- **The 1891–92 famine:** 375,000–400,000 deaths vs. the view that cholera explains the excess mortality; Davydov calls "hungry exports" a myth. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_famine_of_1891%E2%80%931892]
- **The Crimean War's causes:** most historians see the Holy Places as a pretext; A. J. P. Taylor: "fought for the sake of Europe rather than for the Eastern question; it was fought against Russia, not in favour of Turkey". Figes stresses religion and Russian resentment of Western double standards (Pogodin). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War]
- **The Great Game:** older accounts frame Central Asia as an Anglo-Russian contest; RCA and Morrison (2020) argue Britain did little to prevent the conquest and that local frontier dynamics and ambitious commanders (Chernyayev at Tashkent, against Gorchakov's line) drove it. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_conquest_of_Central_Asia; https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/russian-conquest-of-central-asia/60AD9B4DF95196BEA2F8EF67AF0CB8BB]
- **Jadids:** Khalid reads Jadid–qadimist conflict as a struggle over cultural authority; Jadids combined Islamic reform and anti-colonial critique (JAD notes Gasprinsky promoted anti-Russian activism, though he also worked within imperial institutions — treat carefully). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jadid]
- **Nightingale and Seacole:** recent writers argue Nightingale's Crimean achievements were exaggerated by contemporary media; Seacole's recognition grew from the 1980s (posthumous Jamaican Order of Merit, 1990). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Seacole]
- **1905 as "dress rehearsal" or a missed constitutional turn:** Lenin's teleology vs. the view that the October Manifesto and Duma created a limited constitutional monarchy that the Fundamental Laws then hollowed out (R05). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution_of_1905]
