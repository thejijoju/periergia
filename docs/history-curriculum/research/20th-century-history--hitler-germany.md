## 1. Revolution, 1918–19
- The revolution began on 29 October 1918 with a sailors' mutiny (Wilhelmshaven, then Kiel) against orders to sail out against the British fleet; workers' and soldiers' councils spread across Germany in early November and took power "with little resistance or bloodshed". By 7 November it had reached Lübeck, Bremen, Hamburg, Cologne and Munich, where Kurt Eisner (USPD) proclaimed the People's State of Bavaria on 8 November. [source: REV]
- 9 November 1918: Chancellor Max von Baden announced, without authorisation, that the Kaiser had abdicated and handed the chancellorship to Friedrich Ebert (SPD), which "was not allowed under the constitution". SPD deputy Philipp Scheidemann proclaimed a republic from a Reichstag window to forestall Karl Liebknecht, who proclaimed a socialist republic a few hours later; Ebert "stormed angrily" at Scheidemann because he believed a national assembly should decide. Wilhelm II left for the Netherlands the next day; formal abdication 28 November. [source: REV]
- 10 November: the Council of the People's Deputies (three SPD, three USPD) became the provisional government; Ebert kept most of the imperial officer corps, administration and judiciary. The same evening the secret Ebert–Groener pact: Groener promised the army's support, Ebert promised not to change the army's command structure. [source: REV]
- 12 November proclamation: lifted censorship, amnesty for political prisoners, freedoms of association, assembly and press; promised direct, secret, equal elections. [source: REV]
- Ebert told returning troops on 10 December "No enemy overcame you" — words that "would help give rise to the stab-in-the-back myth". [source: REV]
- USPD left the government on 29 December 1918 after the "Christmas crisis"; Gustav Noske took over military affairs. KPD founded at the turn of the year; Rosa Luxemburg's programme (31 December) said the communists could never take power without the clear will of the majority, and she wanted to take part in the elections, but a boycott motion passed 62 to 23. [source: REV]
- The January 1919 "Spartacist uprising" began with the dismissal of the Berlin police chief Emil Eichhorn (4 January); KPD members were a minority among the demonstrators; it was crushed by troops and Freikorps. On 15 January 1919 Luxemburg and Liebknecht were captured, handed to the Guards Cavalry Rifle Division (Captain Waldemar Pabst), clubbed and shot; Luxemburg's body was thrown into the Landwehr Canal and found only on 1 July. The perpetrators largely went unpunished. [source: REV]
- Historian Eberhard Kolb called the January revolt "the revolution's Battle of the Marne". Most modern historians think a Bolshevik-style council government was "all but impossible"; the historical debate is over whether the SPD needed to rely so heavily on the old army and Freikorps. Kurt Tucholsky (1928) accused Ebert and Noske of betraying the revolution. [source: REV]
- Short-lived soviet republics in Bavaria, Bremen and Würzburg in spring 1919 were violently suppressed. [source: REV]
- 19 January 1919 election to the National Assembly, "in which women for the first time had equal voting rights with men": SPD 38%, USPD 7.6%, KPD abstained; Weimar Coalition = SPD, Centre, German Democratic Party (DDP). Assembly met at Weimar from 6 February; Ebert elected president 11 February. [source: REV; WCON]
- Scheidemann resigned rather than sign Versailles: "What hand should not wither that puts itself and us in these fetters?" The Assembly accepted the treaty on 16 June 1919 under Allied pressure; Gustav Bauer (SPD) replaced him. [source: REV]

## 2. The constitution and Article 48
- Adopted 11 August 1919, in force three days later; 181 articles; initial draft by the liberal lawyer Hugo Preuss, based largely on the Frankfurt Constitution of 1849. [source: WCON; REV]
- Reichstag elected by all Germans over 20, by proportional representation with no threshold; a seat for every 60,000 votes. In July 1932, 14 parties won seats (62 received votes); the People's Justice Party won a seat with 40,800 votes out of over 37 million. Critics of the "PR caused it" argument note the Nazi rise "can be attributed only to the sentiment of voters" (flagged citation needed in part). [source: WCON; E28]
- President elected for seven years, could dissolve the Reichstag, appointed and dismissed the chancellor (even one who kept the Reichstag's confidence). WCON calls the office a "replacement monarch"; WR calls it an *Ersatzkaiser*. No constructive vote of no confidence. [source: WCON; WR]
- Article 48: in a serious threat to public safety and order the president could take measures, including armed force, and suspend listed rights (Arts 114, 115, 117, 118, 123, 124, 153: personal liberty, home, post, expression, assembly, association, property). Decrees needed a chancellor's or minister's countersignature (Art. 50); the Reichstag had to be informed and could revoke them by simple majority. The text did not define the emergency or expressly grant legislative power. [source: WCON; A48]
- Usage: Ebert invoked it 136 times, "by agreement with Parliament"; Hindenburg used it 109 times in three years, "largely as a way to bypass parliament". WCON: invoked 205 times before Hitler became chancellor. Article 48 was also used four times to depose state governments, all left-wing (e.g. Saxony 1923). [source: A48; WCON; WR]
- The collapse of parliamentary lawmaking in numbers: Reichstag laws vs Article 48 decrees — 1930: 98 laws, 5 decrees (94 sitting days); 1931: 34 laws, 44 decrees (41 days); 1932: 5 laws, 60 decrees (13 days). [source: A48 table] (Good material for a short table.)
- Bracher's "25/48/53 formula": presidential cabinets governed through the articles allowing the president to dissolve the Reichstag (25), issue emergency decrees (48) and appoint the chancellor (53). [source: BRA]
- Women in the constitution: equal civil rights "in principle" (Art. 109); right to vote. [source: WCON; WNG]
- The 1949 Basic Law answered Weimar: a constructive vote of no confidence, a weak president, and the listed basic rights made non-suspendable. [source: WR; A48]
- Legacy abroad: South Korea's first constitution was based on it; India's emergency-suspension language drew on it. [source: WCON]

## 3. Early crises, 1919–23
- Kapp Putsch, 13 March 1920 (Wolfgang Kapp, General Walther von Lüttwitz, Marinebrigade Ehrhardt), triggered by the order to disband Freikorps; the government fled Berlin, but the coup collapsed in days in the face of a general strike and the civil service's refusal to cooperate. [source: KAPP]
- Political murders by the right-wing Organisation Consul: Matthias Erzberger (August 1921), Walther Rathenau (22 June 1922). [source: WR; HYP]
- Stab-in-the-back myth: an "antisemitic and anti-communist conspiracy theory"; on 18 November 1919 Hindenburg and Ludendorff testified to the National Assembly's inquiry committee in civilian clothes; reviews misrepresenting British General Frederick Maurice's book spread the phrase; Nazi propaganda called Weimar's founders the "November criminals". [source: STAB]
- Ruhr occupation by France and Belgium: 11 January 1923 – 25 August 1925, after repeated defaults (Germany had failed for the 34th time in 36 months to deliver in-kind coal); passive resistance financed by printing money; about 137 German civilians killed (est.). Stresemann ended passive resistance in September 1923. [source: RUHR; HYP; WR]
- Hyperinflation: war debts of 156 billion marks by 1918; 320 marks per dollar in mid-1922; 7,400 by December 1922; 4.2 trillion (4,210,500,000,000) per dollar by November 1923. Cost-of-living index 41 (June 1922) to 685 (December 1922). [source: HYP]
- Causes are debated: German officials blamed reparations; British and French experts, supported by later study of Chancellery records, argued stabilisation was deliberately delayed in hope of reparations relief; historians agree that printing money for Ruhr strikers fed it. [source: HYP]
- Winners and losers: savings wiped out, but debtors gained; Hugo Stinnes, the "Inflation King", controlled 1,535 businesses with 2,890 plants by 1924. [source: WR]
- Stabilisation: Rentenmark (mid-November 1923; see conflicts) at 1 Rentenmark = 1 trillion paper marks; Hjalmar Schacht currency commissioner from 12 November 1923; Reichsmark in 1924. [source: HYP; WR]
- 1923 also saw the Rhenish Republic secessionist movement (21 October) and communist participation in governments in Saxony and Thuringia, ended by a *Reichsexekution* in Saxony. [source: WR]

## 4. The Beer Hall Putsch and Mein Kampf
- Hitler joined the German Workers' Party (DAP) on 12 September 1919, sent by Captain Karl Mayr of the army's propaganda department; Austrian-born (Braunau am Inn, 20 April 1889). [source: BHP; AH]
- 8–9 November 1923, Munich, inspired by Mussolini's March on Rome: Hitler, with about 600 SA men, burst into the Bürgerbräukeller during a speech by state commissioner Gustav von Kahr and fired a shot into the ceiling. Next day about 2,000 marched to the Feldherrnhalle; 15 Nazis, four police officers and one bystander were killed. [source: BHP]
- Trial 26 February – 1 April 1924 (24 days); Hitler's opening speech nearly four hours; Ludendorff acquitted; Hitler sentenced to five years' *Festungshaft* (fortress confinement), the mildest form, eligible for parole after six months; Judge Georg Neithardt refused to deport him to Austria because he "thinks and feels like a German". Released 20 December 1924 after about nine months. [source: BHP]
- Afterwards he sought power "strictly legal[ly]". [source: BHP, citing Kershaw and Evans]
- *Mein Kampf*: written at Landsberg (BHP says he dictated it to Emil Maurice and Rudolf Hess; MK says he wrote it — use "wrote, with help from Hess and Maurice"); vol. 1 published 18 July 1925, vol. 2 1926. It called for *Lebensraum* in the East and treated the forged *Protocols of the Elders of Zion* as authentic. By 1933 about 240,000 copies had been sold. [source: MK; BHP]
- An Arabic translation appeared in Egypt in 1937. [source: MK]
- Ludendorff stood for president in March 1925 for the Nazis: 280,000 votes (1.1%). Party membership at end of 1928: 130,000. [source: RISE]

## 5. "Golden Twenties"
- 1924–29 relative stability: Dawes Plan; Locarno (1925) settled the western borders; Germany joined the League 1926; Stresemann (10 May 1878 – 3 October 1929) won the 1926 Nobel Peace Prize. Young Plan: average 2 billion Reichsmarks a year over 59 years. [source: WR; STR]
- Culture: Bauhaus, cabaret, jazz, the Americanised "new woman". [source: WR]
- Ebert died in early 1925 aged 54; in the 1925 presidential election Hindenburg, candidate of the monarchist right who had not stood in the first round, won with 48%. Took office 12 May 1925. [source: WR; WCON; HIN]

## 6. Depression and the Nazi electoral rise
- American loans dried up after October 1929; the 1931 European banking crisis worsened it. Unemployment: about two million in 1926; 4 million in 1930; about 6 million in 1932. [source: WR]
- Hermann Müller's grand coalition collapsed on 27 March 1930 over unemployment-insurance costs; Hindenburg appointed Heinrich Brüning (Centre) on 29 March 1930 at Schleicher's instigation — the first "presidential cabinet". [source: WR]
- Brüning's austerity: spending cuts, tax rises, mandated wage cuts, cuts to unemployment, sickness and pension benefits; an internal devaluation forcing prices and wages down about 20%. Consensus: his policies made the crisis worse and helped the Nazis. [source: WR]
- Nazi Reichstag results (votes, share, seats):
  - 20 May 1928: 810,127 votes, about 2.6% (computed from the results table; WR implies the same, "five times" less than 1930), 12 seats. Turnout 75.6%. [source: E28; WR]
  - 14 September 1930: 6,379,672, 18.3%, 107 seats (of 577); second party; KPD 13.1%, 77 seats; SPD 24.5%, 143. Turnout 82%. [source: E30]
  - 31 July 1932: 13,745,680, 37.3%, 230 seats (of 608), largest party — their "high-water mark in a free election"; Nazis and KPD together held a majority of seats. Turnout 84.1%. [source: EJ32; WR]
  - 6 November 1932: 11,737,021, 33.1%, 196 seats (−34; two million fewer votes); KPD 16.9%, 100 seats. Last free and fair election. Turnout 80.6%. [source: EN32; WR]
  - 5 March 1933 (after the fire, under terror): 17,277,180, 43.9%, 288 seats (of 647); with the DNVP (8.0%, 52) a bare majority; KPD still won 12.3% and 81 seats. Turnout 88.7%. In Prussia 50,000 SS, SA and Stahlhelm men served as auxiliary police. [source: EM33; WR]
- Presidential election 1932: first round 13 March — Hindenburg 49.6%, Hitler about 30% (11.3 million votes), Thälmann (KPD), Duesterberg (Stahlhelm); run-off 10 April — Hindenburg 53.05% (19.36 million), Hitler 36.77% (13.42 million), Thälmann 10.16%. The SPD backed Hindenburg. [source: PRES]
- Party growth: membership 293,000 (September 1930) to almost 1.5 million by end of 1932; Goebbels ran propaganda from 1930; business funding started in 1932. [source: EJ32]
- SA and SS banned 13 April 1932; Papen lifted the ban 16 June. [source: RISE; WR]
- Papen ("Cabinet of Barons", chancellor 30 May – 17 November 1932 per WR; HIN dates Brüning's resignation 1 June) deposed the SPD-led Prussian government on 20 July 1932 (*Preußenschlag*); Prussia held 61% of the population. Bracher argued that after the Prussian coup democrats lost their fighting spirit. [source: WR; BRA]
- 13 August 1932: Hindenburg refused Hitler the chancellorship; Hitler refused the vice-chancellorship ("playing second fiddle"). [source: WR; RISE]
- Schleicher chancellor 3 December 1932; his public-works programme, "often wrongly attributed to Hitler", created 2 million jobs by July 1933 (Turner). [source: WR]
- Hindenburg, on first meeting Hitler (October 1931), privately called him "that Austrian corporal" or "that Bohemian corporal". [source: HIN]

## 7. 30 January 1933 — the backroom deal
- Papen, now Schleicher's enemy, persuaded Hindenburg to appoint Hitler in a coalition with the DNVP that would "rein in" Hitler; Hindenburg refused Schleicher a dissolution decree (28 January); amid rumours that Schleicher would move troops, Hindenburg appointed Hitler on 30 January 1933. [source: WR; PAP]
- The cabinet had only three Nazis (Hitler; Wilhelm Frick, Interior; Hermann Göring, minister without portfolio and Prussian interior minister); eight conservatives including Hugenberg; Papen vice-chancellor and Reich Commissioner for Prussia, with the right to attend every Hitler–Hindenburg meeting. [source: PAP; RISE; HIN]
- The *Industrielleneingabe*: a letter from 22 industry, finance and agriculture figures asking Hindenburg to appoint Hitler (November 1932). [source: RISE]
- SA and SS torchlight parades through Berlin that night. [source: RISE]

## 8. Reichstag fire and Reichstag Fire Decree
- 4 February 1933 decree already allowed bans on meetings and publications. [source: RFD]
- Fire on the evening of 27 February 1933 (alarm shortly after 9 p.m.); Marinus van der Lubbe, a Dutch council communist (not a KPD member), arrested inside. [source: RF; WR]
- Decree of 28 February 1933 ("for the Protection of People and State") under Article 48 suspended the listed rights "until further notice"; it stayed in force until 1945 and "thousands" of Hitler's decrees rested on it. Mass arrests of communists, including all communist Reichstag deputies. [source: RFD; RF; WCON; A48]
- Leipzig trial 21 September – 23 December 1933: Dimitrov and the other communists acquitted; van der Lubbe beheaded 10 January 1934; posthumously pardoned by Germany in 2008. [source: RF]
- Who set the fire: Kershaw (1998) said nearly all historians agreed van der Lubbe acted alone; Hett (2014) argues the single-arsonist thesis (Fritz Tobias, 1964) rests on flawed work, and a 1955 affidavit by SA man Hans-Martin Lennings, published in 2019, says his squad brought van der Lubbe to a Reichstag already burning. Evans calls Nazi-plot theories conspiracy theory. Present as open. [source: RF]
- Potsdam Day, 21 March 1933: ceremony at the Garrison Church re-opening the Reichstag. [source: POT]

## 9. The Enabling Act
- 23 March 1933, Kroll Opera House, with SA men inside and outside the chamber. It let the cabinet make laws, including ones deviating from the constitution, for four years (to 1 April 1937); renewed 1937 and 1939. KPD deputies excluded; 26 SPD deputies arrested or in hiding. Passed 444 to 94 (see conflicts), only the SPD against; even with all SPD present it would have passed. The Centre Party voted for it after Ludwig Kaas got promised written guarantees that were never received. The Reichsrat assented unanimously the same evening. [source: ENA; NG]
- Otto Wels (SPD) was the only speaker against. [source: ENA; WELS] (Quotations below.)
- The constitution was never repealed but became a dead letter; the Reichsrat was abolished 14 February 1934 in breach of the Act itself. [source: WCON]

## 10. Gleichschaltung ("coordination")
- States: Nazis held only four of 17 Länder in January 1933; Provisional Law (31 March 1933) dissolved state parliaments; Second Law (7 April) installed Reich governors. [source: GL]
- Civil service law, 7 April 1933: removed "non-Aryan" and politically unreliable officials (including teachers, professors, judges); an 11 April decree defined "non-Aryan" as anyone with one Jewish parent or grandparent. [source: GL; NG]
- 1 April 1933 national boycott of Jewish businesses. 10 May 1933 nationwide book burnings. [source: NG]
- Trade unions: 1 May made the "Day of National Labour"; on 2 May SA occupied union offices, seized funds, arrested leaders; German Labour Front (DAF, Robert Ley) founded 10 May; Trustees of Labour (19 May) ended collective bargaining. [source: GL; NG]
- Parties: KPD effectively banned from 6 March; SPD banned 22 June; Law Against the Formation of New Parties, 14 July 1933. [source: GL]
- Culture and press: Reich Chamber of Culture (September 1933); by 1939 over two-thirds of newspapers and magazines owned directly by the Propaganda Ministry; some 6,500 modern artworks removed from museums. [source: NG]
- Youth: Hitler Youth membership compulsory from 25 March 1939; League of German Girls (BDM) for girls 14–18. [source: NG; WNG]
- Church: Reichskonkordat negotiated by Papen in 1933; *Mit brennender Sorge* (1937). [source: PAP; NG]

## 11. Terror apparatus
- Dachau opened 22 March 1933 for political opponents (communists, social democrats); Theodor Eicke commandant from 26 June 1933, his rules the model for later camps; at least 25,613 murdered in the main camp and nearly 10,000 in subcamps (memorial site documents 32,000; website 41,500). [source: DAC; KZ]
- Early camps 1933–34: Jane Caplan estimates about 50,000 prisoners, arrests perhaps over 100,000; about 80% KPD members and 10% SPD; about 70 camps. By mid-1935 only five camps with 4,000 prisoners, while 100,000 were in ordinary jails, a quarter for political offences. [source: KZ]
- Ernst Thälmann (KPD) arrested 1933, over eleven years in solitary confinement, shot at Buchenwald on 18 August 1944 after Himmler noted "Thälmann must be executed". [source: THA]
- Gestapo: about 32,000 employees (1944 estimate); many towns had fewer than 50 officers (Stettin and Frankfurt am Main had 41 together in 1939; Düsseldorf office 281 men for 4 million people; Lower Franconia 22 officers for 840,000). Gellately: it depended on citizens' denunciations. [source: GES]

## 12. Night of the Long Knives, 30 June – 2 July 1934
- Purge of the SA leadership (Ernst Röhm) and other enemies, by SS/SD and Gestapo (Himmler, Heydrich) and Göring's police; Hitler wanted to reassure the army, which feared Röhm's plan to fold it into the SA, and to end talk of a "second revolution". Victims included Röhm (shot 1 July after refusing to kill himself), Gregor Strasser, Kurt von Schleicher and his wife, Ferdinand von Bredow, and Gustav von Kahr; Papen's aides were targeted after his Marburg speech of 17 June 1934. At least 85 killed (estimates up to 700–1,000); over 1,000 arrested. [source: NLK]
- Cabinet law of 3 July 1934 (drafted by Justice Minister Gürtner) declared the killings "legal as acts of self-defence by the State". Hindenburg's congratulatory telegram was, Göring later admitted, never seen by him. The army "almost unanimously applauded". [source: NLK]
- After the purge, persecution of gay men became a police priority. [source: GAY]
- Hindenburg died 2 August 1934; by a law of the previous day the presidency merged with the chancellorship ("Führer und Reichskanzler"); soldiers and officials swore an oath of unconditional obedience to Hitler personally; approved by about 90% in the plebiscite of 19 August 1934. [source: NG]

## 13. Consent, economy, rearmament
- Six million unemployed in 1933; fewer than a million by 1937, partly by pushing women out of work, by conscription and rearmament; real wages fell about 25% (1933–38); the working week rose from 43 hours (1933) to 47 (1939). [source: NG; RES]
- Rearmament: Schacht approved 35 billion Reichsmarks over eight years in June 1933; financed by deficits and Mefo bills; 1933–39 revenue 62 billion RM vs spending over 101 billion (up to 60% rearmament); military spending 10% of GNP by 1936. Four-Year Plan under Göring (1936); Hitler's memorandum demanded the economy "be fit for war within four years". Conscription violated Versailles. [source: ENG]
- Business: banks and other state holdings privatised; Tooze calls business leaders "willing partners in the destruction of political pluralism in Germany". [source: ENG]
- Strength Through Joy; the KdF-wagen (later the Volkswagen) prototype shown 17 February 1939. [source: NG]
- *Volksgemeinschaft* ("people's community") as goal. [source: NG]
- Overy argues the image of Speer's efficient total-war economy is "a myth" resting on the Strategic Bombing Survey. [source: ENG]

## 14. Racial law, sterilisation and "euthanasia"
- Law for the Prevention of Hereditarily Diseased Offspring, 14 July 1933 (in force January 1934): compulsory sterilisation ordered by "Hereditary Health Courts" (a judge and two doctors) for conditions many of which were not genetic; drafted in 1932 under Weimar, closely resembling Harry Laughlin's American "Model Eugenical Sterilization Law". 1934: 84,600 cases, 62,400 sterilisations; 1935: 88,100 cases, 71,700. An estimated 360,000 sterilised 1933–39. [source: STE; T4]
- Nuremberg Laws, 15 September 1935 (the only Reichstag session outside Berlin, passed unanimously): Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honour (no marriage or sex between Jews and Germans; no German women under 45 employed in Jewish households); Reich Citizenship Law (citizenship only for "German or related blood"). Decree of 14 November 1935 defined who was a Jew; on 26 November the laws were extended to "Gypsies, Negroes, and their bastards". Prosecutions delayed until after the 1936 Olympics. [source: NUR; ROM]
- Jews were under 1% of the population in 1933; by 1939 about 250,000 of Germany's 437,000 Jews had emigrated (figure for the base year unclear in source); the Reich Flight Tax took up to 90% of assets. [source: NG; NUR]
- Kristallnacht, 9–10 November 1938: pretext the shooting of diplomat Ernst vom Rath (7 November, died 9 November) by Herschel Grynszpan, 17, whose family had been expelled to Poland; Goebbels's speech that night; deaths, synagogues and shops — see conflicts; about 30,000 Jewish men sent to camps; Jews made to pay a 1 billion Reichsmark "atonement" fine. Goebbels blamed the "healthy instincts" of the people. Kershaw found most Bavarians disapproved of the violence. [source: KN; KER]
- Aktion T4: Hitler's "euthanasia" authorisation, signed October 1939, backdated to 1 September 1939, to Karl Brandt and Philipp Bouhler; named after Tiergartenstraße 4; children's programme from August 1939; gassing tested at Fort VII, Poznań, October 1939; six killing centres including Grafeneck, Brandenburg, Hartheim, Sonnenstein, Bernburg, Hadamar; about 70,000 killed by August 1941; total 275,000–300,000 by 1945 (about 200,000 in Germany and Austria). T4 personnel later ran Operation Reinhard death camps. [source: T4]
- Protest: Bishop Clemens von Galen of Münster preached against the killings (sermons of July–August 1941, printed illegally); Evans calls this the "strongest, most explicit and most widespread protest movement against any Nazi policy". Hitler suspended T4 on 24 August 1941, but killing continued in decentralised form until 1945. Galen had also preached in 1935 linking heresy with the "sins of the Jews" (nuance for the writer). [source: T4; GAL]

## 15. Other persecuted groups (brief; the Holocaust chapter has detail)
- Roma and Sinti: Central Office for Combatting Gypsies existed from 1929 (Weimar); Robert Ritter's research unit (1936); internment camps (*Zigeunerlager*) from 1935; Himmler's December 1938 "Gypsy plague" order; 16 December 1942 order for deportation to Auschwitz, where about 21,000 of 23,000 died. Estimates of the total killed: 250,000–500,000 (USHMM cites 220,000–500,000). West Germany recognised the genocide only in 1982. [source: ROM; NG]
- Gay men: Paragraph 175 predated the Nazis (1871); the Institute for Sexual Science was raided 6 May 1933; 1935 amendment broadened the offence; an estimated 100,000 arrested, about 50,000 sentenced by civil courts, 6,400–7,000 by military courts, 5,000–6,000 sent to camps, with a death rate estimated at 60%. The 1935 version remained in West German law until 1969; Paragraph 175 was abolished only on 10 March 1994. [source: GAY; P175]
- Black Germans: in 1937 a Gestapo commission sterilised an estimated 500 children of German mothers and African soldiers of the French occupation (the "Rhineland bastards"), including girls as young as eleven. In 1940 about 120,000 French African soldiers were captured; some German units massacred black prisoners; roughly half of French colonial POWs did not survive captivity. [source: BLK]
- Jehovah's Witnesses, Freemasons, "asocials", and others were also imprisoned. [source: NG]

## 16. Women in the Nazi state
- Nazi ideal of wife and mother; slogan "Kinder, Küche, Kirche" (borrowed from Wilhelmine Germany); women barred from becoming judges and prosecutors (1936) and pushed out of medicine and politics. [source: WNG]
- Marriage loans (law of 1 June 1933): interest-free loans of 1,000 RM to "Aryan" newlyweds, initially on condition the wife stopped work; one quarter forgiven for each child; the work condition was dropped by 1937; by 1939, 42% of couples got them. [source: ML]
- Cross of Honour of the German Mother, instituted 16 December 1938, first awarded May 1939: bronze for four children, silver for six, gold for eight. The number of families with four or more children fell 5% between 1935 and 1940. [source: MC; NG]
- NS-Frauenschaft led by Gertrud Scholtz-Klink; Lebensborn homes (1935). [source: WNG; NG]
- Female employment was about 15 million in both 1939 and 1944 (weak source; one secondary). [source: WNG]
- Historiography: Gisela Bock (*When Biology Became Destiny*, 1984 chapter) stressed women as victims; Claudia Koonz (*Mothers in the Fatherland*, 1987): "Far from being impressionable or innocent, women made possible State murder in the name of interests that they defined as maternal"; Bock called Koonz's work "anti-feminist". Wendy Lower: thirteen million women, a third of the female population, were active in a Nazi organisation. Women also served as camp guards (Ravensbrück, opened May 1939, was the women's camp). [source: WNG]
- Postwar: *Trümmerfrauen* (rubble women); over two million women raped in the Soviet zone. [source: WNG]

## 17. Resistance
- Workers and the left: SPD and KPD kept underground networks after 1933 but could not cooperate; the Gestapo infiltrated them and arrest rates were high; they won recruits among industrial workers resentful of labour discipline; the exiled SPD in Prague published accurate reports (Sopade). They "were able to achieve little" beyond survival and short strikes. Julius Leber (SPD) arrested 4 July 1944 trying to link SPD and KPD networks. [source: RES]
- East and West German memory: the West canonised the White Rose and 20 July; the East mythologised communist resistance. [source: RES]
- Detlev Peukert: "everyday resistance"; Broszat: *Resistenz* (institutions' "immunity"). [source: RES; BRO]
- Georg Elser, a carpenter loosely linked to the KPD, planted a bomb in the Bürgerbräukeller; Hitler left 13 minutes before it exploded on 8 November 1939, killing seven or eight; Elser was executed at Dachau in April 1945. [source: RES]
- White Rose (Munich): Hans and Sophie Scholl, Christoph Probst, Alexander Schmorell, Willi Graf, Professor Kurt Huber. Six leaflets from 27 June 1942, about 15,000 copies in all; the second leaflet denounced the murder of Jews. Arrested 18 February 1943 after Sophie threw leaflets into the university atrium and the caretaker Jakob Schmid called the Gestapo; tried by Roland Freisler's People's Court and executed with Probst by guillotine at Stadelheim on 22 February 1943. The RAF dropped the sixth leaflet over Germany in 1943 as "The Manifesto of the Students of Munich". [source: WRO; SOS]
- Rosenstrasse protest, February–March 1943: non-Jewish wives protested the detention of about 1,800 Jewish husbands — "the only mass public demonstration by Germans in the Third Reich against the deportation of Jews". [source: ROS; RES]
- 20 July 1944: Claus von Stauffenberg's bomb at the Wolf's Lair; Hitler slightly hurt; Operation Valkyrie coup collapsed within hours; Stauffenberg and others shot that night. Over 7,000 arrested, 4,980 executed, about 200 of them conspirators. Earlier attempts by Henning von Tresckow (bomb on Hitler's plane, March 1943). [source: J20]
- Debate on the plotters: many were conservative nationalists, not democrats; Christian Gerlach showed Tresckow and others knew of mass murder from 1941; Mommsen agreed their main concern was the military situation; Peter Hoffmann and Danny Orbach dispute Gerlach. [source: J20]
- Kreisau Circle (Helmuth James von Moltke) opposed killing Hitler. Edelweiss Pirates: youth groups of 14–18-year-olds from the late 1930s. [source: J20; RES]

## 18. War, war economy and forced labour
- About 12 million people taken from almost twenty countries, about two-thirds from Central and Eastern Europe; about 15 million passed through forced labour counting turnover. In late summer 1944 German records listed 7.6 million foreign civilian workers and POWs in Germany, a fifth to a quarter of the workforce (see conflicts). 2.8–3 million *Ostarbeiter* wore an "OST" badge and lived under guard. Polish workers fell under the discriminatory "Polish decrees"; Jan Gross estimates "no more than 15 percent" of Polish workers volunteered. [source: FL]
- Compensation came only with the German fund of 2000, which paid over €4.37 billion to nearly 1.7 million surviving victims. [source: FL]
- Hitler's 30 January 1939 "prophecy" in the Reichstag (see Quotations) — the bridge to the Holocaust chapter. [source: FID]
- Genocide in one paragraph only: see overview and the Holocaust chapter.

## 19. The end, 1945
- 19 March 1945: Hitler said the German people "had proven to be the weaker" and ordered the destruction of German industrial infrastructure (the "Nero Decree"; the name is not in the text read). [source: AH]
- 20 April: last trip to the surface, decorating Hitler Youth boys. 22 April: "everything is lost". Married Eva Braun on the night of 28–29 April; shot himself on 30 April 1945 with Soviet troops about 500 metres away; Braun took cyanide; bodies burned in the Chancellery garden. Dönitz became head of state; Goebbels and his wife Magda killed themselves on the evening of 1 May (the fate of their children is not in the text read; check before mentioning). Berlin surrendered 2 May. [source: AH]
- Unconditional surrender signed at Reims on 7 May and, because the USSR did not recognise that, again at Karlshorst, Berlin, at 22:43 CET on 8 May 1945 (Keitel for the Wehrmacht; Zhukov, Tedder). [source: GIS]
- Nuremberg trials November 1945 – October 1946: NG says 23 top officials were tried, all but three convicted, twelve sentenced to death. (The defendant count differs between sources depending on who is counted; say "two dozen" or check before using a number.) [source: NG]
- Some 11 million displaced persons freed in 1945; repatriation: 5.2 million to the USSR, 1.6 million to Poland, 1.5 million to France. [source: FL]

## Debates
- **Why did Weimar fail?** "Democracy without democrats"; WR groups causes as economic, institutional and individual (Weitz). Institutional: Article 48, the strong president, PR without threshold, no constructive no-confidence vote. Individual: Brüning's austerity (consensus: harmful), Papen's Prussian coup, the Papen–Hindenburg deal. Bracher (*Die Auflösung der Weimarer Republik*, 1955) argued collapse was not inevitable and dated the beginning of the end to presidential government in 1930 — the "25/48/53 formula"; his Brüning interpretation, against Werner Conze, was supported by Brüning's posthumous memoirs (1970). Hett (2018): conservative elites' attempts to co-opt Hitler. [source: WR; BRA; WCON; Hett search sources]
- **Sonderweg ("special path").** Wehler (Bielefeld School): Germany modernised economically in the 1860s–70s but not politically; the Prussian rural elite kept control of army, diplomacy and civil service ("partial modernisation"; with Fritz Fischer from the 1960s). Blackbourn and Eley, *Mythen deutscher Geschichtsschreibung* (1980), English *The Peculiarities of German History* (1984): there is no "normal" path; the German bourgeoisie dominated social and cultural life; the thesis creates an "exaggerated linear continuity between the nineteenth century and the 1930s". Nipperdey (1975 review) said Wehler made the elites too united; Kocka replied that the Sonderweg may not explain Nazism's rise but still explains Weimar's failure. [source: SON; WEH]
- **Intentionalists vs functionalists (structure of the regime).** Terms coined by Timothy Mason, "Intention and Explanation" (1981), as "the straight road" vs "the twisted road to Auschwitz". Intentionalists (Bracher, Hillgruber, Jäckel, Hildebrand; Dawidowicz) stress Hitler's ideology and will; Bracher saw Nazi Germany as totalitarian, with antisemitism Hitler's "one basic principle ... deeply, blindly and ruthlessly". Functionalists/structuralists: Broszat, *Der Staat Hitlers* (1969; *The Hitler State*, 1981) — a welter of competing institutions; Hans Mommsen — Hitler a "weak dictator" and "cumulative radicalisation". **The debate over the decision for genocide belongs to the Holocaust chapter; here use it to explain how the regime worked.** [source: FID; BRA; BRO; MOM]
- **Kershaw's synthesis: "working towards the Führer".** Rejects Mommsen's "weak dictator" but accepts Hitler's limited role in day-to-day government; agencies competed to anticipate Hitler's wishes; Hitler's power was Weberian charismatic authority (Stalin's bureaucratic); essay in *Contemporary European History* 2:2 (1993), and *Hubris* (1998). Kulka praised it as combining the strengths of both schools. [source: KER; WIL]
- **Consent and coercion.** Gellately, *The Gestapo and German Society* (1990) and *Backing Hitler* (2001): the Gestapo was small and relied on denunciations; most Germans came to back the regime; the camps and Gestapo were publicised, not hidden. Critic Eric Johnson (H-German): a wider sample of cases would alter some conclusions. Kershaw's Bavarian studies (*The "Hitler Myth"*, 1980 German; *Popular Opinion and Political Dissent*, 1983): popular indifference to the Jews' fate; Kulka preferred "passive complicity"; Kater said Kershaw downplayed popular antisemitism. [source: GEL; GES; KER; WebSearch: https://reviews.history.ac.uk/review/293/print; https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780198205609]
- **Goldhagen.** *Hitler's Willing Executioners* (1996): a unique German "eliminationist antisemitism" made ordinary Germans willing killers; written against Browning's *Ordinary Men* (1992) on the same Police Battalion 101. A "publishing phenomenon" with a "mostly scathing" reception among historians; Hilberg called it "totally wrong about everything"; Yehuda Bauer said the thesis fitted Romania better than Germany; Democracy Prize 1997. [source: HWE]
- **Aly.** *Hitlers Volksstaat* (2005; *Hitler's Beneficiaries*): a "convenience dictatorship" kept support through social mobility, redistributive taxes and welfare, paid for by plunder of Jews and occupied Europe. Tooze rejected it (2005 Cambridge debate); Marc Buggeln found Nazi taxation less progressive than Britain's or America's; Evans: "economic reductionism". [source: ALY]
- **The 20 July plotters** — patriots or conservative nationalists who cared mainly about defeat? (Gerlach, Mommsen vs Hoffmann, Orbach). [source: J20]
- **Who burned the Reichstag?** (Tobias/Mommsen/Kershaw vs Hofer, Hett.) [source: RF]
- **Women: victims or agents?** (Bock vs Koonz; Lower.) [source: WNG]
- **Hyperinflation's cause** (reparations vs deliberate policy). [source: HYP]
- **Was the economy geared for total war only after 1942?** (Overy vs the older Speer story; Tooze critical of Speer.) [source: ENG]
- **Resistance definitions** (Widerstand vs Peukert's "everyday resistance" and Broszat's *Resistenz*; Cold War memories in East and West). [source: RES; BRO]
