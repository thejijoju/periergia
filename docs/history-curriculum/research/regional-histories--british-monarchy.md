## 1. Before 1688: the long background (keep brief)
- Following Viking raids and settlement in the ninth century, Wessex emerged as the dominant English kingdom; Alfred the Great took the title "King of the Anglo-Saxons"; his grandson Æthelstan was the first king to rule a unitary kingdom roughly matching England's present borders. [source: MON]
- William of Normandy's invaders landed at Pevensey on 28 September 1066 and defeated Harold at the Battle of Hastings on 14 October 1066; the Domesday survey was completed by 1086. [source: NC]
- Magna Carta was sealed by King John at Runnymede on 15 June 1215; Pope Innocent III annulled it, leading to the First Barons' War; it was reissued in 1216, 1217 (when it got the name "Magna Carta") and 1225 (in exchange for new taxes). Three clauses of the original survive in the 1297 version and remain law in England and Wales. Edward Coke invoked it in the early 17th century against the divine right of kings, on what Wikipedia calls a "badly flawed" historical account. [source: MC]
- Henry III agreed to summon a parliament in 1265 during Simon de Montfort's rebellion; under Edward III Parliament came to be divided into two Houses. [source: MON]
- The Wars of the Roses are dated 22 May 1455 to 16 June 1487; Richard III was killed at Bosworth in 1485 and Henry Tudor became Henry VII. [source: WOR; MON]
- The Act of Supremacy 1534 made Henry VIII and his successors "Supreme Head" of the Church of England in place of the Pope; the 1558 Act made Elizabeth I "Supreme Governor", a title the British monarch still holds. [source: AOSup]
- 1603: James VI of Scotland became James I of England (Union of the Crowns); in 1604 he was the first to style himself "King of Great Britain". The two kingdoms stayed separate. [source: MON]
- Charles I ruled without Parliament from 1629 to 1640; the English Civil War began in 1642. A High Court of Justice of 59 commissioners convicted him, and he was executed outside the Banqueting House, Whitehall, on 30 January 1649. The monarchy was abolished; Cromwell became Lord Protector in 1653 (refusing the title of king); the monarchy was restored in 1660. [source: MON; EXC; TRC]
- The Exclusion Crisis (1679–81) over the Catholic James, Duke of York, produced the first parties: "Petitioners" became Whigs, "Abhorrers" became Tories. [source: MON; GR]

## 2. The Stuarts, the Royal African Company and the slave trade
- The Royal African Company was established in 1660 by the House of Stuart and City of London merchants; it was overseen by James, Duke of York (later James II), and Charles II granted it a monopoly of English trade with Africa. Its predecessor, the Company of Royal Adventurers Trading into Africa, was chartered on 18 December 1660. Its first aim was gold (the Gambia), but it quickly traded enslaved people. [source: RAC]
- Historians estimate the RAC shipped more enslaved Africans to the Americas than any other company. Between 1672 and 1731 company-invested ships carried 186,748 enslaved people on 652 voyages to English colonies, of whom 16,077 died en route; the predecessor company (1662–1672) carried 27,489 (670 died). [source: RAC]
- Many enslaved people were branded "DoY" for the Duke of York; others were branded "RAC" on their chests. [source: RAC]
- The RAC's elephant-and-castle emblem appeared on guineas (e.g. a 1686 guinea under the bust of James II). [source: RAC]
- Edward Colston (Deputy Governor 1689–90) transferred much of his shareholding to William III in early 1689. William III ended the monopoly in 1697 under parliamentary pressure (note: some sources say 1698); the company survived until 1752. [source: RAC]
- Charles III has opened the Royal Collection and Royal Archives to researchers studying British slavery. [source: SIB]

## 3. 1688–1714: the constitutional bargain
- James II became king in February 1685 with wide support; the birth of his son James Francis Edward on 10 June 1688 raised the prospect of a Catholic dynasty, prompting the "Invitation to William" (signed by the "Immortal Seven"). William landed in Devon (Torbay/Brixham) on 5 November 1688; James went into exile in France on 23 December. [source: GR; MON]
- The Declaration of Right was presented to William and Mary in February 1689, treating James as having abdicated; its statutory form, the Bill of Rights, received royal assent on 16 December 1689. It set out regular parliaments, free elections, parliamentary privilege, no taxation without Parliament, a ban on cruel and unusual punishments; it required future monarchs to be Protestant. The Claim of Right Act 1689 applied in Scotland. [source: BOR; MON]
- The settlement was not bloodless everywhere: James's supporters (Jacobites) fought the Williamite War in Ireland, accompanied by a Jacobite rising in Scotland in 1689. [source: GR]
- The Act of Settlement 1701 excluded Catholics and settled the succession on Sophia, Electress of Hanover, and her Protestant heirs. The Acts of Union 1707 united England and Scotland as Great Britain. In 1714 Sophia's son George I succeeded Anne. [source: MON; AOS]
- Royal assent has always been granted since 1708 (the last refusal was 1708). [source: MON]
- Under George I power shifted to ministers, especially Robert Walpole (in office 3 April 1721 – 11 February 1742), often called the first prime minister though the title was not then in use. [source: MON; RW]
- In 1760 George III surrendered the Crown's hereditary revenues (including Crown Estate profits) in return for a Civil List fixed by Parliament; this lasted until 2012. [source: MON; SGA]
- In 1834 William IV dismissed Melbourne and appointed Peel, but Peel lost the election and the king had to recall Melbourne; after the Reform Act 1832 the monarch "was no longer able to interfere effectively with parliamentary power". [source: MON]

## 4. Bagehot's "dignified" crown (1867)
- Walter Bagehot (3 February 1826 – 24 March 1877) serialised *The English Constitution* in *The Fortnightly Review* from 15 May 1865 to 1 January 1867; it was published as a book by Chapman & Hall in 1867, the year of the Second Reform Act; a second edition with a new introduction appeared in 1872. [source: EC; WB]
- Bagehot distinguished the "dignified" parts of the constitution (which win reverence, chiefly the monarch and the Lords) from the "efficient" parts (Cabinet and Commons, which govern). See Quotations for exact wording. [source: EC; MON; Standard Ebooks text via search, https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/walter-bagehot/the-english-constitution/text/chapter-1]
- He summed up the monarch's role as "the right to be consulted, the right to encourage, the right to warn". George V copied out these three rights in 1894 (Peter Hennessy's account, as reported in search results). [source: EC; https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/87644 via search]

## 5. Victoria and the imperial monarchy
- Victoria (24 May 1819 – 22 January 1901) reigned from 20 June 1837 for 63 years and 216 days. Married Albert 10 February 1840; Albert died of diagnosed typhoid on 14 December 1861. Her seclusion earned her the nickname "widow of Windsor" and republicanism temporarily gained strength; her popularity later recovered. Her nine children married across Europe ("grandmother of Europe"). [source: QV; MON]
- In the 1870s Charles Dilke and Charles Bradlaugh called for a republic on the American or French model. [source: REP]
- Victoria's coronation (28 June 1838) drew some 400,000 visitors by the new railways, but inside the Abbey there was "a good deal of mishap and confusion" from lack of rehearsal; Chartists organised anti-monarchist demonstrations in northern manufacturing towns. Historian Roy Strong: "the ceremony of 1838 was the last of the botched coronations". [source: CQV]
- Victoria began talking in 1871 about becoming Empress (partly because her daughter, married to the German heir, would outrank her). The Royal Titles Act 1876 (39 & 40 Vict. c. 10) was pushed by Disraeli and opposed by Gladstone, who thought calling the Queen an Empress "despotic and un-British"; it made her Empress of India only (sign-manual "Victoria Regina et Imperatrix" for India). Wikipedia gives her Empress title from 1 May 1876. The Act was repealed in 1958. [source: RTA; QV]
- The "Proclamation Durbar", from 1 January 1877, was attended by Viceroy Lytton with maharajas, nawabs and intellectuals; it was largely an official event, unlike the mass durbars of 1903 and 1911. [source: DD]
- Golden Jubilee, 20–21 June 1887: the first official celebrations were in India, from February (to avoid summer heat); Indian princes were invited; an Indian cavalry contingent escorted the Queen; she engaged two Indian Muslim waiters, one of them Abdul Karim. [source: GJ]
- Abdul Karim (1863 – 20 April 1909), "the Munshi", taught Victoria Hindustani; her physician Reid noted in 1890 she was "taking Hindustani lessons" twice daily. After her death Edward VII sent him back to India and ordered his correspondence with Victoria confiscated and destroyed. Shrabani Basu used Victoria's Urdu diaries for *Victoria & Abdul* (2010). [source: AK; SB]
- Diamond Jubilee, 22 June 1897 (she had passed George III as longest-reigning on 23 September 1896). Joseph Chamberlain proposed a "Festival of the British Empire" for a monarch ruling "over 450 million people"; troops from Canada, India, Africa and the South Pacific marched; eleven colonial prime ministers attended. In Dublin, James Connolly's Irish Republican Socialist Party led a mock funeral procession. [source: DJ]
- Coronations began to incorporate the empire only in 1902 (dominion premiers, Indian princes); in 1911 the Abbey procession included the banners of the dominions and India. [source: CBM]
- Delhi durbars were held in 1877, 1903 and 1911; only the 1911 durbar was attended by a reigning sovereign (George V and Mary), on 12 December 1911 at Coronation Park, with seating for 10,000 officials and 50,000 local people; George V wore the new Imperial Crown of India (6,100 diamonds). He announced the move of the capital from Calcutta to Delhi and the annulment of the partition of Bengal. Next day a *jharokha darshan* (balcony appearance) at the Red Fort, a custom of the Mughal emperors, drew "half a million or more". [source: DD]
- At the 1911 durbar Maharaja Sayajirao Gaekwad III of Baroda approached without his jewellery, made a simple bow and turned his back when leaving (BBC called it a "daring act of anti-colonial dissent"). [source: DD]

## 6. The world wars and the Windsors
- Edward VII (1901) was the first monarch of the House of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha. On 17 July 1917 George V's proclamation renamed the royal house "Windsor", amid anti-German feeling after Gotha bomber raids and a few months after his cousin Nicholas II's abdication (15 March 1917). Wilhelm II joked about "The Merry Wives of Saxe-Coburg-Gotha". [source: HW]
- When H. G. Wells wrote of Britain's "alien and uninspiring court", George V replied: "I may be uninspiring, but I'll be damned if I'm alien." [source: GV]
- The British government offered asylum to the Romanovs in 1917, but George V thought their presence would be inappropriate amid fears of revolution; the family was killed by the Bolsheviks in 1918. [source: GV]
- George V gave the first royal Christmas broadcast on the radio on 25 December 1932; it became annual. [source: GV]
- Balfour Declaration 1926 and Statute of Westminster (royal assent 11 December 1931): the dominions became equal, the single monarch reigning separately in each. [source: MON; SOW]
- Second World War: Buckingham Palace was bombed during the Blitz while the King and Queen were in residence; the Queen said "I am glad we have been bombed. It makes me feel we can look the East End in the face." [source: G6]
- George VI was the last Emperor of India (title formally relinquished June 1948) and, after the London Declaration (1949), the first Head of the Commonwealth. [source: G6; MON]

## 7. The abdication crisis (1936)
- Edward VIII reigned 20 January – 11 December 1936 (326 days; never crowned). He wished to marry the twice-divorced American Wallis Simpson; Baldwin told him the marriage would be unacceptable; options (marriage with her as queen; a morganatic marriage; abdication) were put to the dominion prime ministers, most of whom agreed there was "no alternative to course (3)". [source: ABD; HW]
- The instrument of abdication was signed 10 December 1936 and given legal force by His Majesty's Declaration of Abdication Act on 11 December. South Africa dated it from 10 December; the Irish Free State recognised it on 12 December (via the External Relations Act) after using the crisis to strip the Crown from its constitution, so abdication took effect on three different dates across the Commonwealth. [source: ABD]
- Radio broadcast: "I have found it impossible to carry the heavy burden of responsibility and to discharge my duties as king as I would wish to do without the help and support of the woman I love." [source: E8]
- As king he had opposed sanctions on Fascist Italy after its invasion of Ethiopia and refused to receive the deposed Emperor Haile Selassie. As Duke of Windsor he married Wallis in France on 3 June 1937 and in October 1937 met Hitler at Obersalzberg against government advice. [source: ABD]

## 8. Elizabeth II (1952–2022)
- Born 21 April 1926; reigned 6 February 1952 – 8 September 2022 (70 years 214 days, longest of any British monarch); died at Balmoral aged 96. Queen of 32 sovereign states during her lifetime, 15 realms at her death. [source: E2]
- On her 21st birthday (21 April 1947), during the royal tour of southern Africa, she broadcast: "I declare before you all that my whole life, whether it be long or short, shall be devoted to your service and the service of our great imperial family to which we all belong." (Written by Dermot Morrah of *The Times*.) [source: E2]
- She learned of her accession in Kenya, at Sagana Lodge after a night at Treetops Hotel, on 6 February 1952. [source: E2]
- Coronation, 2 June 1953: the first British coronation fully televised (Churchill opposed; she insisted). 27 million in Britain watched live on BBC television (CE2); E2 says "more than 20 million" British viewers and that TV licences doubled to 3 million. Worldwide audience estimated at 277 million; about 8,000 guests; 129 nations and territories represented; Queen Sālote of Tonga rode in an open carriage in the rain. [source: CE2; E2]
- 1961: toured Cyprus, India, Pakistan, Nepal and Iran; visited Ghana despite fears for her safety, hosted by President Nkrumah "who had replaced her as head of state". [source: E2]
- 1960s–70s: more than 20 countries gained independence "as part of a planned transition"; in 1965 Ian Smith declared Rhodesian independence with Elizabeth as "Queen of Rhodesia"; she formally dismissed him; the regime survived over a decade. [source: E2]
- 1975: Australian Governor-General John Kerr dismissed Prime Minister Whitlam; Speaker Gordon Scholes appealed to the Queen to reverse it. [source: E2; RAU]
- 1983: angered when the US invaded Grenada, one of her realms, without informing her. 1986: *Sunday Times* reports (20 July) that she was worried by Thatcher's policies and refusal of sanctions on apartheid South Africa; Brian Mulroney called her a "behind the scenes force" in ending apartheid. [source: E2]
- 24 November 1992, Guildhall: called 1992 her *annus horribilis* (separations of Andrew and Charles, Anne's divorce, eggs thrown in Dresden, the Windsor Castle fire). Two days later John Major announced she would pay income tax from 1993 and the civil list would be cut. [source: E2; AH; MON]
- 1999: opened the new National Assembly for Wales (May) and Scottish Parliament (July) as part of devolution. [source: E2]
- 2011: first state visit to the Republic of Ireland by a British monarch (invited by President McAleese). [source: E2]
- 2014 Scottish independence referendum: she told a woman outside Balmoral she hoped people would think "very carefully". [source: E2]
- Funeral: lay in state in Westminster Hall 14–19 September 2022, about 250,000 filed past; the 19 September 2022 state funeral was the first in Britain since Churchill's in 1965. [source: DFE]
- In Hong Kong a pro-Beijing paper attacked mourners "indulging in this fantasy that they are subjects of the British Empire"; a harmonica player was arrested under a colonial-era sedition law. India declared a day of national mourning. [source: DFE]

## 9. Diana and the "near-death of deference"
- Diana, Dodi Fayed and driver Henri Paul died after the crash in the Pont de l'Alma tunnel, Paris, at 00:23 on 31 August 1997; the 2008 British inquest (after Operation Paget) returned unlawful killing through the grossly negligent driving of Paul and the pursuing paparazzi. [source: DIA]
- The royal family stayed at Balmoral; the failure to fly a flag at half-mast over Buckingham Palace caused public dismay (protocol: the Royal Standard flies only when the monarch is in residence and never at half-mast). As a compromise the Union Flag flew at half-mast on the funeral day. Under pressure the Queen returned and gave a live broadcast on 5 September 1997 speaking "as a grandmother". [source: E2; DIA]
- More than one million bouquets were left at Kensington Palace. The funeral (6 September) was watched by an estimated 31.5 million in Britain and around 2.5 billion worldwide (estimate). [source: DIA]
- Tony Blair's tribute called her "the People's Princess" (newspaper headline "Shocked Blair Salutes 'The People's Princess'", *The Age*, 1 September 1997, cited in DIA). [source: DIA]
- In the 1990s republicanism grew partly due to negative publicity (e.g. after Diana's death); yet polls 2002–07 showed 70–80% support for continuing the monarchy. [source: MON]

## 10. The Commonwealth and decolonisation
- London Declaration, 28 April 1949, drafted by V. K. Krishna Menon (Nehru's adviser) and Sir Norman Brook: India could stay in the Commonwealth as a republic, accepting the King "as a symbol of the free association of its independent member nations and as such the Head of the Commonwealth". It became the precedent for all others. [source: LD; CW]
- Head of the Commonwealth is not inherited as of right; CHOGM agreed in 2018 that Charles would succeed, which he did on 8 September 2022. It gives no constitutional role. [source: HOC; MON]
- The Commonwealth has 56 members; Mozambique (1995) was the first with no constitutional link to the empire; Rwanda joined 2009; Togo and Gabon in 2022. [source: CW]
- Philip Murphy's *Monarchy and the End of Empire* (2013) studies the House of Windsor's role in the postwar Commonwealth (cited in CW and Commonwealth_realm). [source: CW]
- Charles III is King of the UK and 14 other realms: Antigua and Barbuda, Australia, the Bahamas, Belize, Canada, Grenada, Jamaica, New Zealand, Papua New Guinea, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Saint Lucia, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, Solomon Islands, Tuvalu. [source: C3]

## 11. Indigenous treaty relationships with the Crown
- Royal Proclamation of 7 October 1763 (George III): only the Crown could purchase land from First Nations; regarded by First Nations as their "Magna Carta" or "Indian bill of rights"; recognised in Canada's Constitution Act 1982, partly thanks to the Indigenous "Constitution Express" movement (1980–82). John Borrows argues it and the Treaty of Niagara (1764) undercut Crown claims to sovereignty over First Nations. [source: RP63; MOC]
- First Nations, Inuit and Métis generally see their treaties as made with the continuing Crown, not the cabinet of the day, to last "as long as the sun shines, grass grows, and rivers flow"; Indigenous leaders have met royalty on treaty business since 1710. [source: MOC]
- The eleven Numbered Treaties (1871–1921; Treaties 1–7 in 1871–77, 8–11 in 1899–1921) were signed between First Nations and the reigning monarch (Victoria, Edward VII or George V); there is "a significant difference" between the written texts and First Nations' oral understanding. [source: NT]
- Treaty of Waitangi, first signed 6 February 1840 by William Hobson and Māori chiefs; around 530–540 Māori, at least 13 of them women, signed the Māori text. Article one: Māori text grants *kawanatanga* (governance), English text cedes "all the rights and powers of sovereignty". Rangatira may have seen it as power-sharing; the Crown as cession. Waitangi Tribunal (1975) gives extra weight to the Māori text. (British Empire chapter already covers; cross-refer.) [source: TOW]
- The Kīngitanga (Māori King movement) began in the 1850s partly to negotiate with the Governor and Queen "on equal footing". [source: TOW]

## 12. Republicanism in the realms
- Australia, 6 November 1999: two-question referendum; the republic model (president appointed by a two-thirds majority of Parliament, endorsed by the February 1998 Constitutional Convention) lost 54.87% No; no state voted Yes (Yes ranged 37.44% in Queensland to 49.84% in Victoria); turnout 95.10%. "Yes" led by Malcolm Turnbull; "No" by Kerry Jones, who attacked the model and "elites". Many direct-election republicans voted No. [source: AUS99]
- Some Aboriginal Australians were sceptical of a republic: former Senator Neville Bonner said a republican president would not "care one jot more for my people". [source: RAU]
- Albanese created an Assistant Minister for the Republic (1 June 2022 – abolished 28 July 2024); after the Voice referendum failed (2023) a republic referendum was deprioritised, and in September 2025 Albanese ruled one out while he is PM. [source: RAU]
- Lidia Thorpe (Senator) in August 2022 added "the colonising" to her oath of allegiance to "Her Majesty Elizabeth the Second" and was made to retake it; on 21 October 2024 in Canberra she heckled Charles, accusing him of genocide and shouting "you are not my king" and "this is not your land"; Ngunnawal Elder Aunty Violet Sheridan called this "disrespectful"; the Senate censured Thorpe. [source: LT; RT24]
- Barbados, independent 30 November 1966, became a parliamentary republic on 30 November 2021; Governor-General Dame Sandra Mason was elected first president on 20 October 2021. Mason's 2020 Throne Speech: "[t]he time has come to fully leave our colonial past behind". Charles (as Prince of Wales) attended the ceremonies. No referendum was held (a 2008 referendum plan had lapsed). [source: BAR; C3]
- Jamaica: both main parties favour a republic. Michael Manley (PNP, from 1972) made the first moves; in June 2022 the government aimed for a republic by the 2025 election, but this was retracted in March 2025. Entrenched clauses need two-thirds of both houses plus a referendum. P. J. Patterson resigned from the UK Privy Council in April 2022. [source: JAM]
- March 2022 Caribbean tour by William and Catherine: Jamaican Advocates Network protested near the British High Commission demanding an apology and reparations for slavery; William expressed "profound sorrow" over slavery, which "should never have happened" and "forever stains our history". The Bahamas National Reparations Committee and Antigua's Reparations Support Commission wrote open letters; a Wessex visit to Grenada was postponed. [source: PJ]
- Charles (as Prince of Wales) at CHOGM, Kigali, 24 June 2022: he could not "describe the depths of my personal sorrow at the suffering of so many" (Reuters wording in multiple outlets). [source: https://www.aa.com.tr/en/africa/prince-charles-in-rwanda-expresses-personal-sorrow-over-slavery-/2621666 ; https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/2022-06-24-prince-charles-tells-of-personal-sorrow-at-slavery]

## 13. Britain: devolution, Windrush, republicanism and cost
- The British Nationality Act 1948 gave citizenship of the UK and Colonies (with right of settlement) to British subjects born in colonies; between 1948 and 1970 nearly half a million people moved from the Caribbean to Britain (the "Windrush generation", after the *Empire Windrush*, 1948). The 2018 Windrush scandal: people wrongly detained, denied rights, and in at least 83 cases wrongly deported; Amber Rudd resigned April 2018; the 2020 Wendy Williams review found the Home Office showed "ignorance and thoughtlessness". [source: WS]
- The 1998 Good Friday Agreement created a power-sharing Northern Ireland Assembly; Scotland and Wales got devolved legislatures opened in 1999. [source: DEV; E2]
- Republic (pressure group): founded by a small group in London in 1983, relaunched 2006; chief executive Graham Smith. On coronation day, 6 May 2023, the Metropolitan Police arrested a reported 52 people including Republic members; Smith was held nearly 16 hours. [source: RPG]
- British Social Attitudes (NatCen): from 1983 to 2012 those wanting abolition ranged 3–11%; in 2023 those saying the monarchy was "very important" fell to 29%, an all-time low (55% very or quite important; 54% in data released April 2024). [source: REP]
- Polls: YouGov January 2023, 64% for continuing the monarchy (MON). ## 15. The anthem's double life
- "God Save the King": author unknown; first published version resembling it in 1744 (*Thesaurus Musicus*); published in *The Gentleman's Magazine* in October 1745 "as sung at both Playhouses" (Drury Lane, Covent Garden) in support of George II after the Jacobite victory at Prestonpans. Its official status comes from custom, not proclamation or statute. [source: GSK]
- Percy Scholes recommended "traditional" as the attribution and rejected Henry Carey's claim. [source: GSK]
- The tune served as Prussia's anthem "Heil dir im Siegerkranz" (1795–1918) and the German Emperor's (1871–1918), Russia's imperial anthem (1816–33), Switzerland's until 1961; it is still Liechtenstein's anthem and the US song "My Country, 'Tis of Thee". It is one of New Zealand's two national anthems and the royal anthem of Australia, Canada and others. [source: GSK]
- Sex Pistols' "God Save the Queen", released 27 May 1977 (Virgin; earlier A&M pressing) during the Silver Jubilee; banned by the BBC and IBA-regulated stations (Radio 2's controller: "in gross bad taste"). It equated the Queen with a "fascist regime" and sang "there is no future in England's dreaming". It reached No. 1 on the *NME* chart but No. 2 on the official UK chart behind Rod Stewart, amid claims of chart rigging (Virgin Megastore sales excluded; WH Smith displayed a blank line at No. 2). On 7 June 1977, Jubilee holiday, the band played it from a boat called the *Queen Elizabeth* on the Thames near Westminster. Paul Cook said "it wasn't written specifically for the Queen's Jubilee". [source: SP]

## Debates
- **Whig history.** Herbert Butterfield, *The Whig Interpretation of History* (1931), attacked history "with reference to the present"; Macaulay's account of 1688 is the classic Whig story of a consensual, bloodless triumph of limited monarchy. The monarchy's story (Magna Carta to 1688 to Bagehot) is especially prone to this. [source: WH; GR]
- **1688.** Whig (Macaulay, Burke) consensual revolution vs a Dutch invasion (J. R. Jones: "the first and arguably the only decisive phase of the Nine Years' War"; John Childs; Jonathan Israel) vs Steven Pincus, *1688: The First Modern Revolution* (2009), who sees a violent, divisive revolution against James's French-style state-building. [source: GR]
- **Invented tradition.** Hobsbawm: many traditions that "appear or claim to be old are often quite recent in origin and sometimes invented". Cannadine argues royal ceremonial, badly performed in the early nineteenth century, became expertly staged from the late nineteenth century in an age of imperial rivalry and mass media, and its meaning shifted again after 1953 with decline and television (summary from search results; wording not verified, paraphrase only). Strong's "last of the botched coronations" (1838) supports the point. Cohn applies the idea to the Raj's durbars. [source: IT; CQV; Cambridge chapter page via search]
- **Ornamentalism vs Orientalism.** Cannadine argues class, rank and status mattered more than race in the empire, with the crown at the top; critics such as Richard Gott called it "a romantic and postmodernist version suitable for the New Labour era". [source: ORN]
- **Constitutional monarchy as working institution.** Bogdanor (*The Monarchy and the Constitution*) and others treat the crown as a functioning, neutral constitutional safeguard. Bogdanor argued George V played "a crucial and active role" in forming the 1931 National Government; Philip Williamson disputes this. [source: VB; GV]
- **Republican critique.** Tom Nairn's *The Enchanted Glass* (1988), "one of the earliest serious modern investigations into the British monarchy from an abolitionist perspective", called Britain "Ukania". [source: TN]
- **Welfare monarchy.** Prochaska argues royal patronage of charity, developed from the Victorian era, made the crown a "half-way house between state and society"; Bogdanor (reviewing) agreed civic functions were as crucial as constitutional ones; Cannadine reviewed it in the LRB (1995). [source: search results above]
- **Managed retreat?** Elizabeth II's Wikipedia article describes "a planned transition to self-government" for more than 20 countries; the published British Empire and Decolonization chapters (Mau Mau, Elkins) supply the counter-evidence of violence. Murphy's 2013 book is the scholarly treatment. [source: E2; CW]
- **Slavery and the crown.** Stuart royals chartered and governed the RAC (DoY branding). Campaigners in Jamaica, the Bahamas, Antigua and Grenada asked for apology and reparations (2022); royals have expressed "sorrow" but not formally apologised. [source: RAC; PJ; C3]
