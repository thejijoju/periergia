## 1. Gorbachev, glasnost and perestroika (1985–1988)
- Gorbachev was elected general secretary by the Politburo on 11 March 1985, just over four hours after Konstantin Chernenko died aged 73; Gorbachev, 54, was the Politburo's youngest member. His initial goal was to revive the stagnating economy, which he came to believe required political and social reform. [DSU; MG]
- *Glasnost* ("openness/transparency") was an old Russian word (used under Alexander II for open trials) that Gorbachev made a political slogan in the mid-1980s. *Perestroika* means "restructuring"; the term was coined by the sociologist Tatyana Zaslavskaya; its purported goal was not to end the planned economy but to make socialism work more efficiently; it added to shortages and is often blamed for the rise of nationalism in the republics. Critics after Alexander Zinoviev called it "katastroika". [GL; PER]
- Economic steps: 1987 Law on State Enterprise (more say for work collectives); 1988 Law on Cooperatives legalised "socialist cooperatives" that functioned as private firms. [PRU]
- 23 December 1986: Andrei Sakharov returned to Moscow after almost seven years of internal exile (in Gorky), after a personal phone call from Gorbachev. 7 February 1987: dozens of political prisoners freed, the first group release since the mid-1950s thaw. [DSU]
- Yeltsin: made Moscow party chief 23 December 1985; his 27 October 1987 Central Committee speech attacking the slow pace of reform (Gorbachev accused him of "political immaturity") circulated in samizdat; he was fired as Moscow party chief 11 November 1987 — the start of his rebranding as a rebel. [DSU]
- 1 July 1988, 19th Party Conference: Gorbachev won backing for a new elected legislature, the Congress of People's Deputies; elections set for 26 March 1989. 29 November 1988: the USSR stopped jamming all foreign radio stations. [DSU]
- March 1989 elections: 5,074 candidates on the final list, about 85% party members; the Congress's first session (25 May–9 June 1989) was broadcast live and uncensored and "transfixed the population"; Sakharov was elected; Yeltsin formed the first opposition, the Inter-Regional Deputies Group. [DSU]
- 7 December 1988, UN General Assembly: Gorbachev announced unilateral cuts of 500,000 in Soviet armed forces within two years, including withdrawals from Eastern Europe, and said "Freedom of choice is a universal principle to which there should be no exceptions" (Wilson Center archival translation; other translations: "should allow for no exceptions"). Chief of staff Sergei Akhromeyev, who opposed the cuts, resigned the same day. [source: WebSearch: https://digitalarchive.wilsoncenter.org/document/116224; https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB261/; MG (caption: "deep unilateral cuts in Soviet military forces in Eastern Europe")]
- Chernobyl: Gorbachev said the USSR spent 18 billion roubles on containment and decontamination; in a 2006 *Japan Times* piece he wrote that Chernobyl, "even more than my launch of perestroika, was perhaps the real cause of the collapse of the Soviet Union." [R89; DSU]

## 2. The end of the Brezhnev Doctrine
- The Brezhnev Doctrine justified the 1968 invasion of Czechoslovakia; Gorbachev repudiated it in the late 1980s. [BRZ]
- 7 July 1989 (R89's date), speaking to the 23-nation Council of Europe: "Any interference in domestic affairs and any attempts to restrict the sovereignty of states—friends, allies or any others—are inadmissible" — an implicit renunciation of force, without naming the Brezhnev Doctrine. [R89]
- "Sinatra Doctrine": coined 25 October 1989 in Helsinki by Foreign Ministry spokesman Gennadi Gerasimov, after a speech by Eduard Shevardnadze two days earlier: "We now have the Frank Sinatra doctrine. He has a song, 'I Did It My Way'. So every country decides on its own which road to take." Asked whether that included rejecting communist parties: "political structures must be decided by the people who live there." SIN stresses it was "retrospective": Poland had already elected a non-communist government a month earlier. [SIN]
- When East Germany asked for Soviet troops in 1989, Soviet forces in Eastern Europe were under strict instructions not to intervene and stayed in barracks. [R89; BRZ]
- Gorbachev's warning in East Berlin (6–7 October 1989, GDR 40th anniversary) is rendered in German as "Wer zu spät kommt, den bestraft das Leben" ("The one who comes too late is punished by life") — wording is a later rendering, see Quotations. [R89]

## 3. The Soviet periphery moves first (1986–1989)
- **Kazakhstan, Jeltoqsan:** protests 16/17–19 December 1986 in Alma-Ata after Gorbachev replaced the Kazakh party leader Dinmukhamed Kunaev with Gennady Kolbin, a Russian who had never lived or worked in Kazakhstan; spread to Shymkent, Taldykorgan, Karaganda. Death toll disputed: Kazakh SSR government said 2 deaths; sources cited by the US Library of Congress say at least 200; some accounts over 1,000; the writer Mukhtar Shakhanov said a KGB officer testified to 168 killed. Some 5,000 arrested per one estimate. In June 1989 Kolbin was replaced by Nursultan Nazarbayev. [JEL; DSU]
- **Latvia:** 14 June 1987, about 5,000 laid flowers at Riga's Freedom Monument for the 1941 deportations (organised by Helsinki-86, "the first openly anti-Communist organization in the Soviet Union"); 23 August 1987 rallies in all three Baltic capitals on the Molotov–Ribbentrop anniversary. [DSU; SING]
- **Crimean Tatars:** 25 July 1987, 300 demonstrated near the Kremlin for the right to return to their homeland, from which they were deported in 1944. [DSU]
- **Singing Revolution:** 1987–1991; the name was coined by Estonian artist-activist Heinz Valk after spontaneous mass night-time singing at the Tallinn Song Festival Grounds on 10–11 June 1988. Estonia's environmental "Phosphorite War" (from February 1987) was an early mobilisation. Popular fronts: Estonia (April 1988), Latvia (June 1988), Lithuania's Sąjūdis (May 1988). [SING; DSU]
- 16 November 1988: the Estonian Supreme Soviet declared sovereignty (Estonian law to take precedence over Soviet law) — the first such declaration. [DSU; R89]
- **Armenia and Karabakh:** 20 February 1988 the Nagorno-Karabakh regional soviet voted to join Armenia; demonstrations in Yerevan grew from 30,000 (20 Feb) to close to a million by 25 February — "more than a quarter of Armenia's population" and "the first of the large, peaceful public demonstrations" of communism's end (DSU). Sumgait pogrom against Armenians, February 1988: 32 dead (official Soviet), 200+ (Armenian sources). In autumn 1988 almost all of Armenia's 200,000 Azerbaijanis were expelled, over 100 killed. Spitak earthquake 7 December 1988 killed an estimated 25,000–50,000; Gorbachev had the Karabakh Committee arrested on 11 December. [DSU; SUM]
- **First Nagorno-Karabakh War** (February 1988–May 1994): about 724,000 Azerbaijanis and 300,000–500,000 Armenians displaced; military dead roughly 6,000 Armenian side and 11,557 (official) to 25,000–30,000 Azerbaijani side; infobox gives c. 16,000 Azerbaijani and 4,000 Armenian civilian deaths (treat as rough). [NK]
- **Black January, Baku:** night of 19–20 January 1990, 26,000 Soviet troops entered Baku; Azerbaijan's official figure 147 civilians killed; other sources 133–137, unofficial up to 300; Yazov said force was meant to stop the Popular Front taking power. [BJ; DSU]
- **Georgia, 9 April 1989:** troops with batons and sharpened spades, plus CN and CS gas, dispersed a pro-independence demonstration on Rustaveli Avenue; 21 killed (19 in the crush, 17 of them women; a 16-year-old girl was beaten to death). The Sobchak commission investigated. 9 April is now National Unity Day. [TBI]
- **Abkhazia:** 16 July 1989 Sukhumi riot, 18 dead — start of the Georgian–Abkhaz conflict. [DSU]
- **Central Asia:** Fergana massacre, 4–11 June 1989, Uzbeks attacked Meskhetian Turks, about 100 killed; Islam Karimov installed. Zhanaozen riots in Kazakhstan, June 1989. [DSU]
- **Miners:** Kuzbass coal strike from 10/11 July 1989, joined by the Donbas and Vorkuta; ended 24–27 July with concessions; the Independent Union of Miners (July 1990) was the USSR's first independent trade union. [DSU]
- **Ukraine and Belarus:** 26 April 1988 march in Kyiv for the Chernobyl anniversary ("Openness and Democracy to the End"); 17 July 1988, 10,000 Greek Catholics at Zarvanytsia, the largest gathering since the church was outlawed in 1946; Belarusian Popular Front (1988) under Zianon Pazniak, discoverer of the Kurapaty NKVD mass graves near Minsk. [DSU]

## 4. Poland: Solidarity, the Round Table, June 1989
- Solidarity was founded August 1980 at the Lenin Shipyard, Gdańsk — the first independent trade union in a Warsaw Pact country recognised by the state; membership peaked at about 10 million in September 1981, one-third of the working-age population. [SOL]
- The August 1980 strike began on 14 August in defence of crane operator Anna Walentynowicz, fired on 7 August five months before retirement; she and Alina Pienkowska turned a bread-and-butter strike into a solidarity strike. [SOL]
- Lech Wałęsa (born 29 September 1943), an electrician at the Lenin Shipyard, fired 1976; led the August 1980 Gdańsk Agreement; Nobel Peace Prize 1983 (received by his wife); president 1990–95, the first Polish president elected by popular vote. [WAL; SOL]
- Martial law declared by General Wojciech Jaruzelski 13 December 1981; Solidarity suspended and leaders interned; it survived underground with Church support and "substantial financial support from the Vatican and the United States" (SOL). [R89; SOL]
- Strike waves April–May and from 15 August 1988 (July Manifesto mine, Jastrzębie-Zdrój; Gdańsk Shipyard 22 August); 31 August 1988 Wałęsa invited to Warsaw for talks. [R89]
- Round Table: began 6 February 1989 in Warsaw, co-chaired by Wałęsa and interior minister Czesław Kiszczak; agreement in early April legalised Solidarity and set partly free elections. [PRT; R89]
- Elections 4 June 1989 (second round 18 June) — the same day as the Tiananmen massacre in Beijing. 161 of 460 Sejm seats (35%) open to free contest, all won by Solidarity's Citizens' Committee; 99 of 100 seats in a recreated, fully elected Senate (the other went to an independent). First-round turnout 62.7%. [PEL; R89]
- 24 August 1989: Tadeusz Mazowiecki became the first non-communist prime minister in the Eastern Bloc (378 votes for, 4 against, 41 abstentions); new government approved 13 September. The Soviet Union "voiced no protest". [R89]
- Wałęsa elected president in two rounds, 25 November and 9 December 1990; first fully free parliamentary elections 27 October 1991; last Russian troops left 18 September 1993. [R89]

## 5. Hungary opens the border
- Kádár replaced 23 May 1988 (Károly Grósz); Miklós Németh PM 24 November 1988. 28/29 January 1989 Imre Pozsgay declared 1956 a "popular uprising". Round Table 22 April–18 September 1989. [R89]
- 2 May 1989 Hungary began dismantling its 240 km border fence with Austria (partly because the government could not afford hard-currency spare parts). 27 June 1989: foreign ministers Gyula Horn and Alois Mock symbolically cut the fence. [R89; PIC]
- 16 June 1989: reburial of Imre Nagy (executed 1958) before at least 100,000 in Budapest. [R89]
- **Pan-European Picnic**, 19 August 1989, near Sopron: a border gate opened for three hours as a symbolic gesture; idea from Ferenc Mészáros (Hungarian Democratic Forum) and Otto von Habsburg; patrons Habsburg and Pozsgay saw it as a test of Gorbachev's reaction. More than 600 East Germans crossed; border guards under Árpád Bella did not intervene. Németh later: "no knocking on my door by the Soviet ambassador, no telephone calls from Moscow." Hungary fully opened the border to East Germans on 11 September 1989. [PIC]
- Communist party refounded as Hungarian Socialist Party 7 October 1989; republic proclaimed 23 October 1989; free elections 24 March 1990 (R89's wording; first round was 25 March per common accounts — not checked); Soviet troops left 19 June 1991. [R89]

## 6. East Germany: exodus, Leipzig, the Wall
- By end of September 1989 more than 30,000 East Germans had escaped west via Hungary; thousands camped in West Germany's Prague embassy. [R89]
- Leipzig Monday demonstrations grew out of peace prayers at St Nicholas Church held since 20 September 1982; the first demonstration, 4 September 1989, was organised by two young women, Katrin Hattenhauer and Gesine Oltmanns ("For an open country with free people"). [MON]
- 9 October 1989: the state massed some 8,000 police and armed units; rumours of a "Chinese solution"; an appeal for calm recorded by six prominent citizens initiated by conductor Kurt Masur (including local party members) was broadcast; some 70,000 marched (MON narrative: "more than 40,000") and no one fired. Chant: "Wir sind das Volk!" ("We are the people!"). 16 October 120,000; 23 October 300,000. Honecker deposed 18 October (Egon Krenz). [MON; R89]
- 4 November 1989: Alexanderplatz demonstration in East Berlin, about half a million — the largest in GDR history. [R89]
- 9 November 1989: at a press conference from 18:00, Günter Schabowski read a new travel regulation; asked when it took effect he said "As far as I know, it takes effect immediately, without delay" (18:53). Crowds gathered; at 22:45 (or 23:30) Harald Jäger, commander at Bornholmer Straße, opened the crossing. [FBW]
- Bulgaria: Todor Zhivkov ousted by his Politburo 10 November 1989, the day after the Wall opened; eco-protests in Sofia preceded it; Union of Democratic Forces formed 7 December 1989; round table 3 January–14 May 1990; renamed Bulgarian Socialist Party won the June 1990 free elections. [R89]
- Malta summit, Bush and Gorbachev, 2–3 December 1989 — sometimes taken as the end of the Cold War, though "many historians" prefer 26 December 1991. [R89]

## 7. Czechoslovakia: Charter 77 and the Velvet Revolution
- Charter 77, published 6 January 1977 with 242 first signatories, partly prompted by the arrest of the rock band the Plastic People of the Universe; it invoked the Helsinki Final Act (1975) and UN covenants; about 1,900 signatories by 1989. Signatories suffered dismissal, exile, imprisonment. [C77]
- Václav Havel, playwright; *The Power of the Powerless* (1978) on citizens forced to "live within a lie"; longest imprisonment May 1979–February 1983 (*Letters to Olga*); worked in the Trutnov brewery after 1968. [HAV]
- 17 November 1989: a student march (International Students' Day, 50th anniversary of the Nazi murder of Jan Opletal), about 15,000 by 16:00, was beaten by riot police on Národní Street. A false report that a student, "Martin Šmíd", had been killed (spread by Radio Free Europe) fuelled protests. Crowds of 200,000 (19 Nov), about half a million (20 Nov), 800,000 at Letná (25 Nov); two-hour general strike 27 November; party leadership resigned 24 November; party gave up monopoly 28 November. Protesters jingled keys. 568 injured, no deaths. [VEL; R89]
- Husák appointed a largely non-communist government 10 December and resigned; Dubček speaker 28 December; Havel elected president unanimously 29 December 1989; free elections June 1990 (Civic Forum and Slovakia's Public Against Violence won); last Soviet troops left 27 June 1991; peaceful split into Czech Republic and Slovakia 1 January 1993. [VEL; HAV; R89]

## 8. Romania: the violent exception
- Timișoara, 16 December 1989: Hungarian-minority protest against the eviction of Reformed pastor László Tőkés became a citywide revolt; Ceaușescu left for Iran on 18 December; workers bussed in from Oltenia to crush protests joined them. [ROM; R89]
- 21 December: Ceaușescu's Bucharest rally, broadcast live, turned into booing; shooting followed. 22 December: defence minister Vasile Milea died (2005 investigation: suicide); army switched sides; Ceaușescus fled by helicopter from the Central Committee roof; captured near Târgoviște; tried by a drumhead military tribunal (charges included genocide) and shot on 25 December 1989 — the last people executed in Romania. [ROM; R89]
- **Death toll (range):** 1,104 killed (162 during 16–22 December, 942 after the National Salvation Front took power, much of it in confused "terrorist" fighting) and 3,352 wounded; official figure 689; infobox range 689–1,290; FSN officials in January 1990 claimed up to 7,000. Contemporary media reported national tolls up to 60,000–70,000 and a Timișoara "mass grave", later shown to be false; Timișoara's own toll was about 60–73. ROM: no academic evidence that loyalist Securitate units fought after 22 December. Romania was the only 1989 revolution in which the leader was executed. [ROM; R89; WebSearch: https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/world/europe/ro-sr-10.htm; https://www.wilsoncenter.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/publication/e-dossier5.pdf]
- Ion Iliescu (ex-communist) led the FSN; elections 20 May 1990; June 1990 Mineriad: over 10,000 Jiu Valley miners called in against student protesters; officially 7 dead. Romania joined NATO 2004, EU 2007. [R89; ROM]

## 9. Albania, Mongolia, China and beyond (global frame)
- Albania: Hoxha died 11 April 1985; revolts from Shkodra 1989; travel freedom 1990; first free elections March 1991; communists lost 22 March 1992. [R89]
- Mongolia: Mongolian Democratic Union announced by Tsakhiagiin Elbegdorj 10 December 1989; protesters used the traditional Mongolian script and Chinggis Khaan imagery; 100,000+ rally 4 March 1990; Politburo resigned 9 March 1990; free elections 29 July 1990 (the former ruling party won). [R89]
- China: Tiananmen protests 15 April–4 June 1989; Gorbachev visited Beijing 15 May; crackdown 3–4 June; death toll unknown. [R89]
- Beyond Europe, Ethiopia, Angola, Benin, Congo-Brazzaville, Mozambique, Somalia and South Yemen abandoned Marxism–Leninism; communist parties kept a monopoly only in China, Cuba, Laos, North Korea and Vietnam. [R89]

## 10. German reunification (1990)
- 28 November 1989 Kohl's Ten-Point Programme; 18 March 1990 first free East German elections (alliance favouring unification won); monetary union treaty 18 May, effective 1 July 1990; Unification Treaty 31 August 1990; accession under Article 23 of the Basic Law; reunification 3 October 1990. [GER; 2P4; R89]
- Two Plus Four Treaty signed in Moscow 12 September 1990 (the two Germanies plus US, USSR, UK, France): Four Powers renounced their rights; full sovereignty when it entered into force 15 March 1991; German forces capped at 370,000; no foreign troops, nuclear weapons or carriers in former GDR; Oder–Neisse border confirmed (German–Polish Border Treaty 14 November 1990). Last Russian troops left at end of August 1994. [2P4]
- Opposition: Thatcher ("We defeated the Germans twice! And now they're back!", December 1989); Mitterrand wary; Andreotti quoted Mauriac: "I love Germany so much that I prefer to see two of them." Gorbachev in January 1990 accepted unification but not NATO membership; in May 1990 (Washington) he accepted Germany's right to choose alliances; in July told Kohl the Soviets would not oppose NATO membership. [GER; MG]
- Aftermath: April 1993 Allensbach survey — only 22% of West Germans and 11% of East Germans felt they were one nation; deindustrialisation and unemployment in the east; wages in the east rose from 35% to 74% of western levels 1990–95. [GER]

## 11. The Baltic drive and 1990–91
- 23 August 1989 Baltic Way: up to two million people, 675 km chain Vilnius–Riga–Tallinn on the 50th anniversary of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact; in December 1989 the Congress of People's Deputies condemned the Pact's secret protocols (Yakovlev Commission). [BW; DSU]
- Lithuanian Communist Party split from the CPSU 7 December 1989 (a first). Sąjūdis won 36 of 42 Lithuanian seats in March 1989. Gorbachev's visit to Vilnius 11–13 January 1990 met a rally of about 250,000. [DSU]
- 11 March 1990: Lithuania's Supreme Council under Vytautas Landsbergis declared restored independence (Act of 11 March), the first Soviet republic to do so; Moscow imposed an economic blockade (April–late June 1990). Latvia declared restoration with a transition period 4 May 1990. [LTA; DSU; JAN]
- 1990 republican elections: the CPSU lost in Lithuania, Moldova, Estonia, Latvia and Georgia; 7 February 1990 the CPSU gave up its monopoly; Article 6 repealed in March 1990 when Gorbachev was elected Soviet president by the Congress (1,329 for, 495 against). [DSU; MG]
- Russia: Yeltsin chair of the RSFSR Supreme Soviet 29 May 1990; Russian sovereignty declaration 12 June 1990; Yeltsin quit the party 12 July 1990; elected Russian president 12 June 1991 with 57% (Ryzhkov 16%). [DSU]
- 13 January 1991, Vilnius TV tower: Soviet troops and KGB Alpha Group killed 14 unarmed civilians (one more died of a heart attack). Riga "Barricades": six deaths in following days. 31 July 1991 Medininkai: OMON killed seven Lithuanian border guards. [JAN; DSU]
- Referendums: Lithuania 9 February 1991 (93.2% for independence); Estonia 3 March (77.8%); Georgia 31 March (99.5%, turnout 90.6%) and declaration of independence 9 April 1991. Iceland recognised Lithuania 12 February 1991. [DSU]
- Union referendum 17 March 1991: about 77% for a "reformed" union in the nine republics that took part; Baltic states, Armenia, Georgia, Moldova boycotted. [DSU; MG]

## 12. The August coup and the end of the USSR
- New Union Treaty due to be signed 20 August 1991, strongly backed by Central Asian republics. 19 August: the State Committee on the State of Emergency (Yanayev, Pavlov, Yazov, Kryuchkov, Pugo and others) put Gorbachev under house arrest at Foros, Crimea; Yeltsin spoke from a tank outside the White House; troops refused to storm; coup collapsed 21 August. [DSU; COUP; MG]
- Three young men killed in a tunnel clash 21 August — Dmitry Komar, Vladimir Usov, Ilya Krichevsky — posthumously made Heroes of the Soviet Union; Pugo and Akhromeyev died by suicide. Dzerzhinsky statue at the Lubyanka removed night of 24 August. [COUP]
- Estonia declared independence 20 August, Latvia full independence 21 August 1991; USSR recognised Baltic independence 6 September; UN admission 17 September. [SING; DSU]
- 24 August: Gorbachev resigned as general secretary; Ukraine declared independence; 29 August CPSU activity suspended; Yeltsin banned the party in Russia 6 November. [DSU]
- 1 December 1991: Ukraine's referendum — about 91% for independence (DSU), a "fatal blow" Gorbachev had not expected. 8 December: Yeltsin, Kravchuk and Shushkevich signed the Belovezha Accords in western Belarus, declaring the USSR no longer existed "as a subject of international law and a geopolitical reality" and creating the CIS; Gorbachev called it unconstitutional. [DSU; MG]
- 16 December: Kazakhstan the last republic to declare independence. 21 December: Alma-Ata Protocol, 11 of 12 remaining republics (all but Georgia). 25 December: Gorbachev resigned on television ("I hereby discontinue my activities at the post of President of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics"); Soviet flag lowered 19:35 Moscow time, Russian tricolour raised. 26 December: the Soviet of the Republics voted the USSR out of existence. Russia took the Soviet UN Security Council seat. Georgia joined the CIS in December 1993 and left 2008. [DSU; AAP; MG; CIS]
- Central Asian leaders largely stayed in power: Nazarbayev (to 2019), Karimov (to 2016), Niyazov (to 2006); Tajikistan slid into civil war. [R89]
- Post-Soviet wars: Karabakh, Transnistria, South Ossetia, Abkhazia, Chechnya, Tajikistan. [R89]

## 13. Shock therapy and transitions
- Poland: inflation reached 639.6% in 1989; foreign debt about $42 billion. Balcerowicz plan presented on TV 6 October 1989; 11 laws signed 31 December 1989, effective 1 January 1990; Jeffrey Sachs advised. Results: hyperinflation stopped, shortages ended; GDP fell about 9.8% and 7% in two years; unemployment 0.3% (Jan 1990) → 6.5% (end 1990) → 16.9% (July 1994), later 20.7% (Feb 2003); 1,675 industrial plants liquidated; by 2008 GNP 77% above 1989; no quarter of negative growth in 2009. [ST; BAL]
- Russia: Gaidar's reform began 2 January 1992 with price liberalisation; inflation 2,509% (1992), 840% (1993), 215% (1994), 131% (1995). Voucher privatisation 1992–94 under Chubais: 12,000–14,000 medium and large firms privatised by mid-1994, about 40 million shareholders; assets concentrated in insiders and oligarchs ("prikhvatizatsiya", grab-itization). 1998 financial crisis. GDP fell roughly 40–50% in the 1990s (DSU table: Russia −40% 1990–2000; YEL: "fell by 50%"). [ECR; PRU; YEL; DSU]
- GDP falls 1990–2000 (DSU table): Georgia 78%, Armenia 63%, Moldova 63%, Azerbaijan 60%, Ukraine 59%, Latvia 51%, Kyrgyzstan 50%, Tajikistan 50%, Turkmenistan 48%, Lithuania 44%, Kazakhstan 41%, Russia 40%, Belarus 35%, Estonia 35%. [DSU]
- Mortality: Russian male life expectancy 64.2 (1989) → 57.4 (1994); total 69.7 → 63.9; deaths 1.58 million (1989) → 2.30 million (1994); deaths exceeded births from 1992 ("Russian cross"), until 2013. Causes debated: alcohol (Korotayev/Khaltourina), stress, the collapse itself vs reform policies. Estimates of excess deaths: Steven Rosefielde (2001) 3.4 million premature deaths in Russia 1990–98. [DEM; RX; DSU]
- Poverty in Russia from about 2% (1987–88) to 50% (1993–95) at <$4/day (ST); half below the poverty line by 1997 (ECR). [ST; ECR]
- Shock therapy sustained in Poland, Czech Republic, Slovakia and the Baltic states; adopted then aborted in Russia, Albania, North Macedonia, Bulgaria, Kyrgyzstan. [ST]

## 14. NATO and EU enlargement
- Visegrád Group formed February 1991 (Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia) to seek EU and NATO integration. Partnership for Peace (1994) a compromise. 1993: Wałęsa campaigned for NATO entry; Yeltsin reportedly said Poland's membership was no threat, then retracted, writing that enlargement would "violate the spirit" of the Two Plus Four treaty. NATO–Russia Founding Act May 1997. Open letter of 40+ experts (incl. Nunn, Bradley, Nitze, McNamara) opposed enlargement. [NATO]
- Joined NATO: Czech Republic, Hungary, Poland 12 March 1999; Bulgaria, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia 29 March 2004; Albania, Croatia 2009; Montenegro 2017; North Macedonia 2020; Finland 2023; Sweden 2024. [NATO; 2P4]
- EU: 1 May 2004 the largest enlargement — Cyprus, Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovakia, Slovenia; Bulgaria and Romania 2007. [EU04]

## 15. Yugoslavia's violent breakup
- Milošević rose to power in Serbia in 1987, led the "anti-bureaucratic revolution" (1988–89 rallies that toppled the Vojvodina and Montenegro leaderships), curbed the autonomous provinces including Kosovo in 1989. Died of a heart attack in his ICTY cell 11 March 2006 before verdict. [MIL; KOS]
- Slovenia and Croatia declared independence 25 June 1991; Ten-Day War in Slovenia 27 June–7 July 1991 (Brioni Accords), about 70 killed. [TDW; CRO; YW]
- Croatia 1991–95: Vukovar destroyed (autumn 1991); Republic of Serbian Krajina held over a quarter of Croatia; Operation Storm 4/5–8 August 1995, after which 150,000–200,000 Serbs fled; about 22,000 dead (15,000 Croats, 7,000 Serbs) (YW); "over 20,000" (CRO). [CRO; YW]
- Bosnia 1992–95: referendum 29 Feb–1 March 1992 (Bosniaks 44%, Serbs 31–32.5%, Croats 17%); war from 6 April 1992; Bosnian Serbs under Karadžić, backed by Milošević and the JNA, seized about 70% of the territory with ethnic cleansing; camps including rape camps (Foča, Višegrad). Dead 97,207–102,622 (64,036 Bosniaks, 24,905 Serbs, 7,788 Croats); 2.2 million displaced; 12,000–50,000 women raped, mainly by Serb forces. [BOS; YW]
- Siege of Sarajevo 5 April 1992–29 February 1996, 1,425 days, the longest siege of a capital in modern warfare; about 14,000 killed. [SAR; YW]
- **Srebrenica**, July 1995: more than 8,000 Bosniak men and boys killed by Bosnian Serb forces under Ratko Mladić (and the Scorpions); list of missing/killed has 8,372 names; 25,000–30,000 women, children and elderly forcibly removed; 370 lightly armed Dutch UN troops in a declared "safe area"; ruled genocide by the ICTY Appeals Chamber (2004) and the International Court of Justice (2007); Dutch government resigned 2002; UN day of commemoration 11 July (designated May 2024). [SRE]
- Dayton: initialled 21 November 1995 at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base, Ohio; signed in Paris 14 December 1995; Bosnia as one state of two entities (Republika Srpska and the Federation). [DAY; BOS]
- Kosovo War 28 February 1998–11 June 1999: KLA vs Yugoslav forces; mass expulsions from 20 March 1999; NATO bombing from 24 March 1999 without UN Security Council approval, at least 488 Yugoslav civilian deaths (HRW 488–527); Kumanovo Agreement 9 June. Deaths 13,535 (10,812 Albanians, 2,197 Serbs). [KOS; YW]
- Totals: 130,000 (Humanitarian Law Center) to 140,000 (International Center for Transitional Justice) dead in the Yugoslav Wars; about 2.4 million refugees and 2 million internally displaced. [YW]
- ICTY: created by UN Security Council Resolution 827, 25 May 1993; The Hague, 1993–2017; 161 indicted, 90 convicted and sentenced; Mladić convicted 22 November 2017; closed 31 December 2017. [ICTY]

## Debates
- **Who/what ended the Cold War and the USSR.** DSU sorts explanations into *intentionalist* (Archie Brown: Gorbachev drove events 1985–88 and later; Breslauer's "man of the events"; Kotz and Weir: elites spurred nationalism and capitalism for their own benefit) and *structuralist* (Edward Walker: Soviet nationality policy built conscious nations and a federal myth that eased secession). Brown's *Human Factor*: Western economic and military strength did not force Soviet defeat; leadership, power and ideas — chiefly in Moscow — ended the Cold War (critics thought *The Gorbachev Factor* over-credited him). Triumphalist view: Reagan's buildup, SDI and pressure (incl. oil prices) broke the USSR; Raymond Garthoff argued Reagan's confrontation probably delayed the end by two to three years. Kotkin/Gross: 1989 was an implosion of the communist establishment, "civil society" too small to topple it (NYRB critic: stridently revisionist; *JCWS* review: dubious in parts but convincing overall). Kenney: grassroots movements predated glasnost. Zubok: Gorbachev's reforms drained state resources and empowered separatism; not inevitable. Plokhy: challenges the "American victory" story — Bush wanted to preserve the USSR (Kyiv speech 1 August 1991, "Chicken Kiev"); Ukraine's 1 December referendum and the Russia–Ukraine leaders ended it. Xi Jinping's view: "historical nihilism" and loss of party control of the army destroyed the CPSU. [DSU; WebSearch: https://www.politics.ox.ac.uk/publication/human-factor-gorbachev-reagan-and-thatcher-and-end-cold-war; https://www.spectator.com.au/?p=10637885; https://americanforeignrelations.com/A-D/Cold-War-Termination.html; https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2009/11/05/1989/; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/534419/pdf; https://www.themoscowtimes.com/archive/book-review-plokhys-the-last-empire-is-the-best-account-of-the-soviet-collapse-yet; https://spectator.com/article/it-s-not-just-putin-who-misses-the-soviet-empire-president-bush-did-too/]
- **The NATO "not one inch" dispute.** Baker to Gorbachev, 9 February 1990: Baker's notes and Moscow's record contain "not one inch eastward" (in the German context). No written or treaty promise; the Two Plus Four treaty mentions no other country. Gorbachev (2014) and Shevardnadze said the assurance concerned only East Germany and was kept; Gorbachev and Yeltsin felt later enlargement violated its spirit. Blanton and Savranskaya (National Security Archive, 2017): documents show talks were "not at all narrowly limited" to East Germany and Soviet complaints were "founded" in memcons. Hannes Adomeit (2018): exploratory talk ≠ commitments. Trachtenberg (2021): claims "by no means baseless ... But the Soviets were not deliberately misled." Sarotte (*Not One Inch*, 2021): betrayal claims untrue in law but with "psychological truth". Shifrinson's 2022 find: West German diplomat Jürgen Chrobog, March 1991 memo, "we would not expand NATO beyond the Elbe" (possibly meaning the Oder). [2P4; MG]
- **Shock therapy.** Supporters: Poland as the success story; Russia's failure due to incomplete application (Sachs resigned, complained of too little Western aid). Critics (Stiglitz; Ghodsee and Orenstein; Isabella Weber): Russia's mortality rise was the worst in peacetime for an industrialised country; poverty, inequality, corruption rose. Counterpoints in ST: the Soviet economy was already collapsing and inter-republic trade broke down; Russia's rushed voucher privatisation was a domestic political choice to create a pro-Yeltsin capitalist class; Poland privatised more slowly and saw less inequality; some say Poland was actually the most gradualist. Mortality debate: policy vs alcohol vs collapse itself. [ST; RX; ECR]
- **Yugoslavia.** Causes: nationalism and unresolved ethnic tension vs elite manipulation (Milošević's Serbian nationalism replacing communism; UN 1994 report: Serb aim was "Greater Serbia", also "Greater Croatia" ambitions). Kosovo bombing remains controversial (no UN mandate). ICTY criticisms: some sentences too light; statistical studies (Hashimoto and Gray) suggest Serbs more likely convicted and given longer sentences, results not conclusive. [YW; KOS; ICTY]
- **When did the Cold War end?** Malta (3 December 1989), German unification (3 October 1990), or the Soviet dissolution (26 December 1991). [R89; MG]
