## Defining slavery: Patterson and Finley
- Orlando Patterson (Jamaican-American historical sociologist) published *Slavery and Social Death: A Comparative Study* in 1982 (Harvard University Press). [source: W:Orlando_Patterson; W:Social_death]
- Patterson defines slavery as "the permanent, violent domination of natally alienated and generally dishonored persons" (quoted in Vincent Brown's *American Historical Review* article; another source places it at p. 13). [source: https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/vbrown/files/brown-socialdeath.pdf; https://firstthings.com/?p=69038]
- Patterson's core argument: slavery originated, or was conceived as originating, as a substitute for (usually violent) death, commonly of a war captive. The captive was spared physical death but suffered "social death": loss of name, kin and honour, and total dependence on the master. He deliberately does not define slavery primarily as property; critics objected that wives and children could also be property in law. [source: W:Social_death; https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/orlando-patterson-3/slavery-and-social-death-a-comparative-study/]
- Patterson treats palace eunuchs (Byzantium, China) as the "ultimate slaves": trusted and powerful yet despised. This is useful for the mamluk/Janissary paradox. [source: W:Social_death]
- In 2018 Patterson added a new preface describing social death as a "slaveholder ideology" and responding to Afropessimist scholars (Frank B. Wilderson III, Saidiya Hartman and others) who use the concept. [source: W:Social_death]
- Moses I. Finley (1912–1986), an American-born classicist who moved to Cambridge after being dismissed from Rutgers in the McCarthy era, wrote *Ancient Slavery and Modern Ideology* (1980; expanded edition, edited by Brent D. Shaw, 1998). [source: W:Moses_Finley]
- Finley distinguished "societies with slaves", where slaves were few and economically marginal, from "slave societies", where slaves were a large share of the population and the elite's main source of income. He named only five "genuine" slave societies: classical Greece and Roman Italy in antiquity, and the US South, the Caribbean and Brazil in the modern era. [source: WebSearch summary of Finley's *Ancient Slavery and Modern Ideology* and of a BMCR review (BMCR 2019.09.22), https://glc.yale.edu/sites/default/files/pdf/ancient_slavery_and_modern_ideology.pdf; https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/?p=44286. These pages could not be opened here, so the summary was not checked against Finley's text: paraphrase it and do not quote it]
- Critics of Finley's five-society list call it too short and its criteria rigid and arbitrary. The edited volume *What is a Slave Society?* (reviewed BMCR 2019.09.22) questions it, citing cases such as Joseon Korea, the Sokoto Caliphate and some Pacific Northwest peoples, where slaves were also a large share of the population (see below). [source: https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/?p=44286 via WebSearch summary; the case examples come from the Korean, African and Native American sections below]

## Ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt
- The Laws of Hammurabi (Hammurabi ruled c. 1792–1750 BCE, middle chronology) distinguish three kinds of person: *awīlum* (free), *muškēnum* (a dependent or commoner class; its meaning is contested) and *wardum*/*amtum* (male/female slave). [source: W:Code_of_Hammurabi]
- The code's final law (282): if a slave says to his master "You are not my master", the master shall prove he is his slave and "cut off his ear". Laws 278–281 cover warranties on slave sales and buying slaves abroad; laws 15–20 cover harbouring fugitive slaves. [source: W:Code_of_Hammurabi]
- In Egypt, slavery existed at least from the Old Kingdom. Egyptian terms (*bꜣk*, *ḥm*; *sqr-ꜥnḫ*, "living prisoner") do not map neatly onto "slave", and scholars distinguish chattel slavery, bonded labour and forced (corvée) labour. [source: W:Slavery_in_ancient_Egypt]
- Egyptologists agree that the Great Pyramids were **not** built by slaves (workers' tombs found near Giza in 2010). [source: W:Slavery_in_ancient_Egypt]

## Ancient Greece
- Numbers are uncertain. Athens probably had the largest slave population, perhaps up to 80,000 in the 6th–5th centuries BCE (three or four per household on average). The lowest estimate, for Demosthenes' time, is about 20,000. A census under Demetrius of Phalerum (317–307 BCE) reportedly counted 21,000 citizens, 10,000 metics and 400,000 slaves, a figure many scholars doubt. [source: W:Slavery_in_ancient_Greece]
- Thucydides reports more than 20,000 slaves deserting Athens during the Spartan occupation of Decelea (Peloponnesian War). [source: W:Slavery_in_ancient_Greece]
- Slaves worked in the Laurion silver mines: the general Nicias leased out 1,000 slaves there. [source: W:Slavery_in_ancient_Greece]
- Sparta's helots were a state-owned, land-bound population that formed the majority there. Historians often liken them to serfs rather than chattel slaves. [source: W:Slavery_in_ancient_Greece]
- Aristotle's *Politics* (Book I) argues that some people are slaves "by nature", while others are slaves only by law or convention. He calls the slave "a piece of property" and "a tool for action". Scholars note inconsistency in his account, and the Stoics (Seneca, Letter 47) disagreed. [source: W:Natural_slavery]
- Aristotle's argument had a long afterlife. At the 1550 Valladolid debate Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda called Amerindians "natural slaves", and Bartolomé de las Casas denied that the category applied to them. [source: W:Natural_slavery]

## Rome: a slave society
- Estimates (attributed): about 20–30 per cent of Italy's population was enslaved by the end of the 1st century BCE, at least 1–2 million people. One study (Kyle Harper) estimates just under 5 million slaves in the whole empire in 260–425 CE, about 10–15 per cent of 50–60 million people. Keith Bradley warns that "None of these figures is capable of proof." [source: W:Slavery_in_ancient_Rome]
- Walter Scheidel (2005) argues that about three-quarters of slaves in Italy were born into slavery, so imports were lower than the higher estimates assume. [source: W:Slavery_in_ancient_Rome]
- Roman law: "Slaves are either born or made" (*servi aut nascuntur aut fiunt*), through war, piracy, kidnapping or child abandonment. [source: W:Slavery_in_ancient_Rome]
- In 7 CE Augustus imposed a 2 per cent tax on slave purchases. Its estimated yield of about 5 million sesterces a year may imply about 250,000 sales a year. Delos and Ephesus were major slave markets. [source: W:Slavery_in_ancient_Rome]
- Third Servile War (Spartacus), 73–71 BCE: about 70 gladiators escaped a school at Capua. Ancient figures for the rebels reach some 120,000 men, women and children (infobox, including non-combatants). Crassus destroyed the army in 71 BCE. Ancient sources disagree about the rebels' aim: Plutarch says escape over the Alps, while Appian and Florus say an attack on Rome. Pompey killed about 5,000 fugitives. The figure of 6,000 crucified by Crassus appears in the article's infobox and is flagged there as needing a citation. [source: W:Third_Servile_War] (The published Rome chapter says six thousand crucified along the Via Appia; see Conflicts.)
- Freed slaves of Roman citizens became citizens, though with some restrictions. [source: published Rome chapter, consistent with W:Slavery_in_ancient_Rome]

## China, India, Korea
- China: slaves were classed among the *jianmin* ("base people"). The Qin (221–206 BCE) enslaved the families of offenders and used large numbers of state slaves on roads, canals and land reclamation. [source: W:Slavery_in_China]
- Han dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE): Emperor Gaozu freed agricultural workers enslaved in the Warring States period. Han law capped holdings at 200 slaves for kings and dukes, 100 for an imperial princess and 30 for other officials. [source: W:Slavery_in_China]
- Wang Mang (r. 9–23 CE) abolished slavery in 9 CE as part of radical land reform. It was reinstated in 12 CE. [source: W:Slavery_in_China]
- Slavery declined during the Song commercial boom. The Qing held about two million slaves at the conquest, then gradually turned slaves and bondservants into peasants. Slavery in China was nominally abolished in 1910. Hong Xiuquan's Taiping regime abolished slavery in its territory in the 1850s–60s. [source: W:Slavery_in_China]
- Wikipedia's article says large-scale slavery like that of classical Greece and Rome did not exist in ancient China. [source: W:Slavery_in_China]
- India: the meaning of Vedic *dāsa*/*dāsyu* is disputed: enemy, servant or slave. Upinder Singh says the Rig Veda knows slavery through war and debt. Scott Levi says the association of Vedic *dasa* with "slave" is "problematic". The *Arthashastra*, *Manusmriti* and *Mahabharata* show slavery established by the start of the common era. [source: W:Slavery_in_India; W:Dasa]
- Megasthenes (4th century BCE) claimed there was no slavery in Maurya India, but Ashoka's edicts mention obligations to slaves and hired workers. [source: W:Slavery_in_India]
- Slavery was prohibited in East India Company territories by the Indian Slavery Act 1843, in French India in 1848, in British India in 1861 (Indian Penal Code) and in Portuguese India in 1876. [source: W:Slavery_in_India] (The published Abolition chapter cites Bartle Frere's later estimate of 8–9 million enslaved in 1841; cross-refer.)
- Korea: *nobi* (Goryeo and Joseon) could be bought, sold or gifted, and the status was hereditary. Wikipedia says they were more than 30 per cent of the population at the peak in mid-Joseon. Before Joseon, slaves and serfs were no more than about 10 per cent. Most government *nobi* were freed in 1801, and the system was abolished in 1894. [source: W:Nobi; W:Slavery_in_Korea]
- Whether *nobi* were slaves is debated. James Palais called Joseon a "slave society". Others treat out-resident *nobi* as near-serfs or tenants. The Korean word for a chattel slave is *noye*. [source: W:Nobi; W:Slavery_in_Korea; consistent with the published Pre-modern Korea chapter]
- In 1426 King Sejong granted female government *nobi* 100 days' maternity leave, lengthened by a month before birth in 1430. In 1434 their husbands received 30 days' paternity leave. [source: W:Nobi]

## The Islamic world and military slavery
- Islamic law forbade enslaving Muslims and protected non-Muslims under Muslim rule, so slaves came from beyond the frontiers. (Covered in detail in the published medieval chapter; cross-refer.)
- *Mamluk* means "one who is owned". Large-scale slave armies began under the Abbasid caliph al-Muʿtaṣim (r. 833–842), who moved his capital to Samarra partly because of clashes between his Turkish *ghilman* and the people of Baghdad. Recent historians distinguish the Samarra *ghilman* system from the later mamluk system. Military slavery lasted from the 830s to 1811 (Muhammad Ali's massacre of Egypt's mamluks). [source: W:Mamluk]
- The Mamluk sultanate ruled Egypt and Syria 1250–1517 (see the published medieval chapter).
- Janissaries: an Ottoman elite infantry corps (traditionally from 1363; formed under Murad I or Orhan). From the 1380s it was recruited through the *devshirme*, a levy of Christian boys, mostly from the Balkans, who were converted and trained. Strength grew from about 1,000 (1400) to 37,627 (1609) and about 135,000 (1826). Mahmud II abolished the corps on 15 June 1826 (the "Auspicious Incident"). [source: W:Janissary]
- The *devshirme* also produced many grand viziers from the 15th to the 17th century. Muslims were allowed into its posts from 1594, and recruiting Christians effectively stopped by 1648. An attempt to revive it in 1703 failed, and it was abolished early in Ahmed III's reign. [source: W:Devshirme] (The Janissary article dates the levy's abolition to 1648; give "mid-17th century, with a failed revival in 1703".)
- Zanj Rebellion, 869–883: a revolt near Basra, led by Ali ibn Muhammad, of enslaved and freed Africans who had been put to work clearing nitrous topsoil and in the salt flats of southern Iraq. "Zanj" here probably means Africans generally, not only East Africans. The rebels took al-Ubulla (870) and Basra (September 871, sacked). The Abbasid regent al-Muwaffaq besieged their capital al-Mukhtara from 881, and it fell in 883. [source: W:Zanj_Rebellion]
- Zanj casualties: al-Masʿudi gives a "moderate" figure of 500,000 dead but calls it "empty conjecture". Modern historians treat the contemporary figures as gross exaggerations. Do not give a death toll. [source: W:Zanj_Rebellion]
- The Crimean Khanate exported about 2 million captives from Muscovy/Russia and Poland-Lithuania to the Ottoman world in 1500–1700 (estimate by Dariusz Kołodziejczyk). [source: W:History_of_slavery]

## Trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trades
- Paul Lovejoy estimates about 6 million people taken across the Sahara between 650 and 1500. A further 1.4 million are estimated for the 17th–18th centuries and 1.2 million for the 19th (Segal). [source: W:Trans-Saharan_slave_trade]
- Totals for Black Africans taken to the Arab world from the mid-7th century to the 20th century range from 10 to 18 million (Segal). [source: W:Trans-Saharan_slave_trade]
- Tidiane N'Diaye and Olivier Pétré-Grenouilleau estimate 8 million taken from East Africa (7th century to 1920) and 9 million across the Sahara. William Gervase Clarence-Smith notes that estimating the Indian Ocean trade has been controversial. [source: W:Indian_Ocean_slave_trade] (Matches the published Migration and Diaspora chapter. The Africa to 1860 chapter calls the 8 million figure "high-end ... contested", so keep it attributed.)
- Gustav Nachtigal estimated that three or four captives died or escaped for every one who reached the Saharan market. The Ottoman *firman* of 1857 abolished the trade in Tripoli, but it continued at least into the 1890s. [source: W:Trans-Saharan_slave_trade]
- Wikipedia's estimates chart for the 1800s shows about 4.0 million Atlantic, 1.2 million trans-Saharan and 0.9 million Indian Ocean exports. The East African trade grew enormously in the 19th century. [source: W:Indian_Ocean_slave_trade]

## Barbary corsairs and the Mediterranean
- Robert C. Davis, *Christian Slaves, Muslim Masters: White Slavery in the Mediterranean, the Barbary Coast, and Italy, 1500–1800* (2003), estimates that 1 million to 1.25 million Europeans were taken to Tunis, Algiers and Tripoli between the early 16th and mid-18th centuries. He extrapolates by assuming a roughly constant capture rate over 250 years. [source: W:Barbary_slave_trade]
- Contested: Peter Earle says Davis may have extrapolated from the peak period (1580–1680), and that his figures "sound a bit dodgy". John Wright cautions that all such estimates are back-calculations. Contemporary observers estimated about 35,000 European slaves held at any one time in the 17th century. [source: W:Barbary_slave_trade]
- Most captives were seamen, many of them Italian. Raids reached Ireland (the 1631 Sack of Baltimore, led by Jan Janszoon/Murad Reis) and Iceland. England lost 466 merchant ships to Barbary corsairs in 1609–1616. [source: W:Barbary_slave_trade]
- After the 1816 Anglo-Dutch bombardment of Algiers, the Dey agreed to end Christian slavery. [source: W:Barbary_slave_trade]
- Keep proportion: Christians also enslaved Muslims in Mediterranean galleys (Davis frames it as a mutual conflict). The Barbary theme is sometimes used polemically to relativise the Atlantic trade. [source: W:Barbary_slave_trade]

## Medieval Europe: slavery and serfdom
- Etymology: English "slave" comes via Old French *esclave* from Byzantine Greek *sklábos* / Late Latin *sclavus*, from the name for the Slavs, because so many Slavs were enslaved in the 8th–9th centuries. Wikipedia calls this the "widespread view" but notes it "has been disputed since the 19th century". [source: W:Slavery] (The published medieval chapter states it without caveat; see Conflicts.)
- About 10 per cent of the people recorded in England's Domesday Book (1086) were slaves. [source: W:Slavery_in_medieval_Europe]
- Verdun was a centre where captives, many of them Slavs, were castrated for sale as eunuchs to al-Andalus. Genoese Caffa in Crimea was a major Black Sea slave market. [source: W:Slavery_in_medieval_Europe]
- Serfs, unlike slaves, generally could not be bought or sold individually, though they might be sold with the land. Russian *kholops* were slaves, and by the late Middle Ages had become serfs. [source: W:Serfdom]
- A "second serfdom" spread in central and eastern Europe in the early modern period. Serfdom was abolished in Habsburg lands by the 1781 Serfdom Patent (second step 1848), in Prussia in 1807, and in Russia in 1861 (state serfs 1866). The UN's 1956 Supplementary Convention classes serfdom as a practice similar to slavery. [source: W:Serfdom]
- Some 150,000–200,000 Roma were enslaved over five centuries in the Romanian principalities (abolition is usually dated 1856; Wikipedia's History of slavery says 1864). [source: W:History_of_slavery] (Mention only with care; the date is uncertain in the source.)

## Indigenous American slaveries
- Some Native American peoples held war captives as slaves before and after contact. Among some Pacific Northwest peoples about a quarter of the population were slaves, and the status was hereditary. [source: W:Slavery_among_Native_Americans_in_the_United_States]
- Andrés Reséndez, *The Other Slavery: The Uncovered Story of Indian Enslavement in America* (Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, 2016), estimates that 2.5 to 5 million Indigenous people were enslaved in the Americas (1492–1900, per the published Afro-Indigenous chapter). It won the 2017 Bancroft Prize and was a National Book Award finalist. Most of the enslaved were women and children, and labour was often extracted under other names (encomienda, repartimiento, missions, debt peonage). [source: W:The_Other_Slavery]
- Spain's New Laws of 1542 restricted the encomienda (it now ended on the holder's death); key provisions were repealed in 1545. [source: W:Encomienda]
- Alan Gallay estimates that 24,000 to 51,000 Native Americans were exported through Carolina ports in 1670–1715, more than half of them from Spanish Florida. The trade helped trigger the Yamasee War (1715). [source: W:Slavery_among_Native_Americans_in_the_United_States; W:Indian_slave_trade_in_the_American_Southeast]

## The Atlantic system: scale and the Middle Passage
- SlaveVoyages (Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database): about 12.5 million captives embarked from Africa and about 10.7 million survived the voyage (Richard Anderson et al. 2013 citing the database). [source: W:Atlantic_slave_trade] (Matches the published chapters exactly.)
- About 2 million (about 14 per cent) died in the Middle Passage. Rates were much higher on some voyages, and crew mortality of about 20 per cent was expected. [source: W:Middle_Passage] (The published Atlantic chapter gives "12 to 15 per cent", which is consistent.)
- Before 1820 perhaps three times as many enslaved Africans crossed the Atlantic as Europeans (Eltis). [source: W:Middle_Passage]
- Philip D. Curtin's *The Atlantic Slave Trade: A Census* (1969) gave about 9,566,000 imported to the Americas, one of the first systematic estimates. He later wrote *The Rise and Fall of the Plantation Complex* (1990), the source of the term "plantation complex". [source: W:Philip_D._Curtin]
- Britain carried about 2.53 million Africans in 1640–1807 (Lovejoy figures quoted in the article; treat as indicative). [source: W:History_of_slavery]
- The Lagos (Portugal) slave market opened in 1444. [source: W:History_of_slavery]

## Racialization and the law of slavery
- Barbados Slave Code, 1661 ("An Act for the better ordering and governing of Negroes"): the first comprehensive English colonial slave act. Its preamble aimed to "protect them [slaves] as we do men's other goods and Chattels". It was re-enacted in 1676, 1682 and 1688 and served as a model elsewhere. [source: W:Barbados_Slave_Code]
- *Partus sequitur ventrem* ("that which is born follows the womb"): Virginia, 1662. Children took their mother's status, so children of enslaved women, including those fathered by enslavers, were born enslaved. The doctrine was derived from Roman civil law. [source: W:Partus_sequitur_ventrem]
- Virginia passed a comprehensive slave code in 1705. Louis XIV's Code Noir (1685) applied to the French colonies (Saint-Domingue 1687, Louisiana 1724) and stressed religious rules. [source: W:Slave_codes]
- The published medieval chapter already covers the "curse of Ham" and Iberian law (*Siete Partidas*); cross-refer.

## Law and life under slavery
- Slave codes typically restricted marriage (enslaved people's marriages were not legally protected), movement and assembly, and in some places education. [source: W:Slave_codes]
- Anti-literacy laws: South Carolina passed the first in 1740, after the Stono Rebellion (1739), with a £100 fine and six months' prison for teaching enslaved people to write. Seven Southern states passed such laws between 1740 and 1834. Wikipedia says the US is the only country known to have had anti-literacy laws. [source: W:Anti-literacy_laws_in_the_United_States]
- Religion: enslaved Africans brought Islam, Catholicism (e.g. from Kongo) and African religions. In the US many met in secret "hush harbors" and "invisible churches" beyond white supervision. [source: W:Slavery_in_the_United_States] (Omar ibn Said, Malê revolt: cross-refer to the published Atlantic and Abolition chapters.)
- Family separation: the domestic slave trade moved about one million enslaved people from the Upper South to the Deep South, breaking up families (see below). [source: W:Slavery_in_the_United_States]

## The United States in its hemispheric web
- US enslaved population: 697,681 in 1790; 3,953,760 in 1860 (census). [source: W:Slavery_in_the_United_States]
- The Constitution barred a federal ban on importing slaves until 1808. Congress banned imports from 1 January 1808 (Act of 1807), but smuggling continued (e.g. the *Wanderer*, 1858). [source: W:Slavery_in_the_United_States]
- The cotton gin (1793) made short-staple upland cotton profitable. Through the domestic trade, about one million enslaved people were forcibly moved from the Upper South to the Deep South, often called a "second Middle Passage". [source: W:Slavery_in_the_United_States]
- Louisiana Purchase (1803, about $15 million): France's failure to suppress the revolution in Saint-Domingue, together with the prospect of renewed war with Britain, led Napoleon to sell. [source: W:Louisiana_Purchase]
- Indian Removal Act: passed by the Senate on 24 April 1830 and the House on 26 May 1830 (signed 28 May, per the published Native American Studies chapter). Economic historians (Carlson and Roberts) link its passage to land interests and slavery. [source: W:Indian_Removal_Act] (The published Native American Studies chapter covers removal, the Trail of Tears and the more than 4,000 enslaved people taken west by the Five Tribes; cross-refer.)
- The Haitian Revolution's shockwaves in the US: Gabriel's conspiracy (Virginia, 1800), the German Coast uprising (Louisiana, 1811, the largest revolt in US history), Denmark Vesey's conspiracy (Charleston, 1822) and Nat Turner's rebellion (Virginia, 1831). [source: W:Slavery_in_the_United_States]
- Haiti and antislavery outward: Pétion's republic gave military and financial aid to Simón Bolívar, which proved critical to the liberation of New Granada. Dessalines offered citizenship and help to the enslaved of Martinique and Guadeloupe. [source: W:Haiti; W:Haitian_Revolution]
- Haiti's indemnity: in 1825 France demanded 150 million francs for recognition. It was reduced in 1838 to 60 million for the remainder (90 million in all). A 2022 *New York Times* analysis estimates about 112 million francs actually paid (about $560 million in 2022 money), with linked loans finally paid off in 1947. [source: W:Haitian_independence_debt]
- "Second slavery": Dale Tomich defined the concept in 1988 to describe the new 19th-century zones of mass slave commodity production tied to industrialisation: cotton in the US South, sugar in Cuba and coffee in Brazil. He edited *The Politics of the Second Slavery* (2016). [source: W:Second_Slavery; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/684058 via WebSearch]
- Cuba became the world's largest sugar producer by the mid-19th century, doubling production every ten years between 1830 and 1860 (Wikipedia's article is flagged "essay-like", so use with care). [source: W:Second_Slavery]
- By 1860 cotton was almost 60 per cent of US exports, and the South grew about two-thirds of the world's cotton (published American Civil War chapter; cross-refer, do not re-verify).

## Abolitions and emancipations
- Britain: the Slave Trade Act 1807 received royal assent on 25 March 1807 and took effect on 1 May 1807 (Commons vote 283–16). The Slavery Abolition Act 1833 received royal assent on 28 August 1833 and took effect on 1 August 1834. About 800,000 enslaved people were freed; territories of the East India Company were excluded. Owners received £20 million in compensation, financed partly by a £15 million loan from Nathan Mayer Rothschild and Moses Montefiore (1835). Claims that taxpayers finished repaying it only in 2015 rest on a Treasury FOI response. [source: W:Slave_Trade_Act_1807; W:Slavery_Abolition_Act_1833]
- United States: the Thirteenth Amendment passed the Senate on 8 April 1864 and the House on 31 January 1865, and was ratified on 6 December 1865 (proclaimed 18 December). [source: W:Thirteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution]
- Brazil: the Lei Áurea (Law No. 3,353) was signed by Princess Isabel on 13 May 1888. Art. 1: "É declarada extinta desde a data desta lei a escravidão no Brasil" ("From the date of this law, slavery is declared extinct in Brazil"). Owners received no compensation. [source: W:Lei_Áurea]
- Mauritania: abolished slavery by presidential decree in 1981, the last country in the world to do so, but passed no criminal law. A 2007 law allowed slaveholders to be prosecuted. Estimates (Global Slavery Index): 155,600, or 4 per cent of the population, in 2014; 90,000, or 2.1 per cent, in 2018. Hereditary servitude persists, especially among the Haratin. [source: W:Slavery_in_Mauritania]
- Other dates are in the published Abolition chapter (France 1794/1848, Netherlands 1863, Cuba 1886, Ottoman Empire, Zanzibar, Northern Nigeria 1936). Cross-refer rather than relist.

## Slavery within Africa
- Paul E. Lovejoy (Canadian historian, York University), *Transformations in Slavery: A History of Slavery in Africa* (Cambridge University Press, 1983; 2nd ed. 2000; 3rd ed. 2011). [source: W:History_of_slavery (bibliography, "Cambridge UP, 1983"); W:Paul_Lovejoy]
- Estimated shares of enslaved people (from Wikipedia's Slavery in Africa, which cites an encyclopedia): about a third of the population in Senegambia (1300–1900) and in the early Sahelian states of Ghana, Mali, Segou and Songhai; a third among Asante and Yoruba; about a third in Kanem and perhaps 40 per cent in Bornu; one- to two-thirds in the Fulani jihad states; at least half in the 19th-century Sokoto Caliphate. Hugh Clapperton thought half of Kano's population was enslaved in 1824. [source: W:Slavery_in_Africa]
- When British rule was imposed on northern Nigeria, about 2 to 2.5 million people were enslaved in the former Sokoto Caliphate area. [source: W:Slavery_in_Africa]
- As the Atlantic trade closed, enslaved labour within Africa expanded into export production (the published Abolition chapter covers this, with Klein and Lovejoy/Hogendorn).

## Eric Williams and the capitalism debate
- Eric Williams, *Capitalism and Slavery* (published in the US in 1944; British publishers refused it, and André Deutsch published a UK edition in 1964). It grew from his Oxford D.Phil. thesis, on a topic suggested by C. L. R. James. [source: W:Capitalism_and_Slavery]
- Williams's arguments: profits from slavery and the triangular trade helped finance Britain's industrial revolution; abolition followed the economic decline of the West Indian sugar economy (the "decline thesis", drawing on Lowell Ragatz) and rising free-trade interests; and the self-emancipation of rebels from Haiti to Jamaica (1831) helped end slavery. [source: W:Capitalism_and_Slavery]
- Reception: Seymour Drescher, his leading critic, still called it a classic (1987). Kenneth Morgan (ODNB) calls it "perhaps the most influential book written" on the subject (quotation truncated in source; do not quote). David Brion Davis wrote that the thesis "has now been wholly discredited by other scholars". Hilary Beckles (2004) noted "the fundamental academic respect" it enjoys in the Caribbean. Ryden (2012) finds support for a profitability crisis. [source: W:Capitalism_and_Slavery; W:Eric_Williams]
- "New history of capitalism": Edward E. Baptist, *The Half Has Never Been Told: Slavery and the Making of American Capitalism* (2014), argues that rising cotton-picking productivity was driven by torture (the "pushing system") and that slavery was central to American capitalism. [source: W:Edward_E._Baptist]
- Critics: Alan Olmstead (*Journal of Economic History*) called it "flawed beyond repair", attributing productivity gains mainly to improved cotton seed varieties and accusing Baptist of overstating cotton's share of the economy. Eric Hilt wrote that "much of its economic analysis is so flawed that it undermines the credibility of the book". See also Olmstead and Rhode, "Cotton, slavery, and the new history of capitalism", *Explorations in Economic History* 67 (2018). Eric Foner and Daina Ramey Berry praised the book. [source: W:Edward_E._Baptist; W:History_of_capitalism]
- *The Economist*'s 2014 review ("Almost all the blacks in his book are victims, almost all the whites villains. This is not history; it is advocacy.") was withdrawn with an apology after an outcry. [source: W:Edward_E._Baptist]
- Related works: Sven Beckert, *Empire of Cotton: A Global History* (2014; Bancroft Prize 2015). Beckert and Seth Rockman (eds.), *Slavery's Capitalism* (2016). Walter Johnson, *River of Dark Dreams: Slavery and Empire in the Cotton Kingdom* (2013), who rejects the "new history of capitalism" label and places himself in W. E. B. Du Bois's tradition. [source: W:Sven_Beckert; W:History_of_capitalism; W:Walter_Johnson_(historian)]

## Afterlives: sharecropping, convict leasing, Jim Crow
- Sharecropping spread in the Reconstruction era (1865–1877): the cropper paid the owner a share of the crop, typically one-half to two-thirds, and the crop-lien system kept many in debt. [source: W:Sharecropping]
- The Thirteenth Amendment exempts "punishment for crime". Black Codes and vagrancy laws fed convict leasing, which was mostly applied to Black men. Douglas A. Blackmon (*Slavery by Another Name*) describes it as a distinct form of bondage. It was phased out after Attorney General Francis Biddle's Circular No. 3591 (12 December 1941). [source: W:Convict_leasing; W:Thirteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution]
- Jim Crow: segregation from the 1870s, upheld in *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896, "separate but equal"). Federal troops withdrew after the Compromise of 1877. Most Jim Crow laws were overturned by the Civil Rights Act 1964 and the Voting Rights Act 1965. [source: W:Jim_Crow_laws] (Modern America chapter: Great Migration of about 6 million, 1910–1970; cross-refer.)

## Reparations
- CARICOM Reparations Commission created September 2013. Hilary Beckles (UWI) urged action in a 2013 lecture in Georgetown, Guyana, on the 250th anniversary of the Berbice revolt. [source: W:Reparations_for_slavery]
- The CARICOM Ten Point Plan for Reparatory Justice was unanimously approved by CARICOM heads in March 2014 (11 March per one source). Its points include: a full formal apology; repatriation; an Indigenous peoples' development programme; cultural institutions; the public health crisis; illiteracy eradication/education; an African knowledge programme; psychological rehabilitation; technology transfer; and debt cancellation. [source: W:Reparations_for_slavery; WebSearch summaries of https://caricom.org/?p=19846 and https://www.derechos.org/intlaw/doc/crc10point.html — the exact wording of each point could not be opened; give the list in general terms]
- An updated CARICOM reparations manifesto was reportedly presented in Accra in 2026; reports conflict on dates and details (see NOT VERIFIED).
- Brattle Group report (June 2023, presented at the University of the West Indies, Mona; commissioned by the American Society of International Law with UWI's Centre for Reparation Research; led by former ICJ judge Patrick Robinson): reparations for transatlantic chattel slavery, covering harms during and after slavery in 31 countries, estimated at about $107.8 trillion (Wikipedia: "more than 100 trillion dollars"), with the UK's share about $24 trillion. These are advocacy estimates that no government has accepted. [source: W:Reparations_for_slavery; WebSearch summaries of https://ibw21.org/reparations/trillions-owed-in-reparations-for-slavery-says-report/ and https://www.soas.ac.uk/about/blogs/why-britain-cant-invest-its-way-out-reparations-debate]
- Netherlands: Prime Minister Mark Rutte apologised on behalf of the government on 19 December 2022, calling slavery a crime against humanity, with a €200 million fund for awareness and education but no compensation. King Willem-Alexander apologised on 1 July 2023 (Keti Koti) at the national slavery monument in Oosterpark, Amsterdam, asking forgiveness for the "crystal-clear lack of action" by the House of Orange. [source: W:Dutch_2022_apologies_for_slavery; https://www.upi.com/Top_News/World-News/2023/07/01/netherlands-king-willem-alexander-acknowledges-apologizes-for-slavery/9831688234558/; https://www.iamexpat.nl/expat-info/dutch-expat-news/keti-koti-2023-why-did-dutch-king-apologise-slavery]
- US: John Conyers introduced H.R. 40 (a commission to study reparations) every year from 1989 to 2017. Ta-Nehisi Coates, "The Case for Reparations", *The Atlantic*, June 2014. [source: W:Reparations_for_slavery]
- UK descendants: Laura Trevelyan's family apologised in Grenada in February 2023 and funded education; in April 2023 she co-founded Heirs of Slavery. Barbados's task force has pressed the MP Richard Drax over the Drax Hall estate. [source: W:Reparations_for_slavery]

## Modern slavery and human trafficking
- *Global Estimates of Modern Slavery* (ILO, Walk Free and IOM; published 12 September 2022, reference year 2021): 49.6 million people in modern slavery on any given day, 27.6 million in forced labour and 22 million in forced marriage. More than 12 million were children, and the total was about 10 million higher than in the previous (2017) estimates. [source: https://www.walkfree.org/reports/global-estimates-of-modern-slavery-2022/; https://www.ilo.org/publications/global-estimates-modern-slavery-forced-labour-and-forced-marriage-1 (via WebSearch)]
- Walk Free's definition of "modern slavery" covers forced labour, forced or servile marriage, debt bondage, forced commercial sexual exploitation, human trafficking, slavery-like practices and the sale and exploitation of children. The 2018 Global Slavery Index estimated 40.3 million (71 per cent female). [source: W:Slavery_in_the_21st_century]
- North Korea has the highest estimated prevalence (2023 GSI: about 2.7 million, over 10 per cent of the population). [source: W:Slavery_in_Korea]
- The Palermo Protocol (UN, 2000) is the main international definition of trafficking. [source: W:Human_trafficking]
- Critiques: the Global Slavery Index was criticised for extrapolating from survey countries to others (2013–2016 editions; Guth et al. 2014 question its replicability and validity), though the 2018 methodology improved. Julia O'Connell Davidson, *Modern Slavery: The Margins of Freedom* (2015), argues that the term blurs slavery with labour, debt, marriage and migration, and serves immigration control. Her critics (e.g. Rahila Gupta) say she "defines slavery out of existence". [source: W:Global_Slavery_Index; https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/modern-slavery-the-margins-of-freedom/; https://www.opendemocracy.net/en/defining-modern-slavery-out-of-existence-who-benefits/ (via WebSearch)]

## Debates
- **Definition.** Property (legal ownership) or social death (Patterson)? Vincent Brown ("Social Death and Political Life in the Study of Slavery", *AHR*, 2009) warns that "social death" can erase the enslaved's own political and social lives. Patterson's 2018 preface calls social death a "slaveholder ideology", meaning the masters' aim rather than a description of the slaves' inner life. [source: W:Social_death; https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/vbrown/files/brown-socialdeath.pdf]
- **Slave societies.** Finley's five "genuine slave societies" against critics who add Joseon Korea (Palais, against the view that *nobi* were near-serfs), the Sokoto Caliphate and others (*What is a Slave Society?*, reviewed BMCR 2019). [source: W:Nobi; W:Slavery_in_Africa; https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/?p=44286 via WebSearch]
- **Comparing trades.** Estimates for the trans-Saharan, Red Sea and Indian Ocean trades (Lovejoy; N'Diaye and Pétré-Grenouilleau; Segal, 10–18 million) are far less certain than the Atlantic figures and are sometimes used polemically. The Barbary figures (Davis) are similarly contested. [source: W:Trans-Saharan_slave_trade; W:Indian_Ocean_slave_trade; W:Barbary_slave_trade]
- **Williams thesis.** Williams (profits financed industry; abolition followed decline) against Drescher's *Econocide* (1977) and David Brion Davis ("wholly discredited"), with partial revival by Ryden and Inikori and strong Caribbean support (Beckles, Carrington). [source: W:Capitalism_and_Slavery; W:Eric_Williams; W:History_of_capitalism]
- **New history of capitalism.** Baptist, Beckert and Johnson (slavery central to modern capitalism) against economic historians Olmstead, Rhode and Hilt (productivity came from seed technology; the numbers are mishandled). [source: W:Edward_E._Baptist; W:History_of_capitalism]
- **Second slavery.** Tomich: 19th-century slavery in the US South, Cuba and Brazil was not a relic but was remade by industrial capitalism. [source: W:Second_Slavery; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/684058]
- **Indigenous slavery.** Reséndez argues that enslavement, not disease alone, drove much of the Indigenous population collapse, especially in the Caribbean. This is debated alongside the epidemiological view (see the published Conquest of the Americas chapter). [source: W:The_Other_Slavery]
- **Reparations.** For: CARICOM's ten-point plan, the Brattle/ASIL estimates, Coates. Critics question legal liability across generations (e.g. Kevin D. Williamson's reply to Coates). National apologies (the Netherlands 2022/2023) have avoided compensation. [source: W:Reparations_for_slavery]
- **"Modern slavery."** Walk Free and the ILO use a broad umbrella term. O'Connell Davidson and other critics argue that it conflates distinct abuses and serves border control. Method critiques target the Global Slavery Index's extrapolation. [source: W:Global_Slavery_Index; https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/modern-slavery-the-margins-of-freedom/]
