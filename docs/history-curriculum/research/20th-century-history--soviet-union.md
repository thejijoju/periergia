## 0. The Soviet Union as a multinational state (frame)
- More than 100 distinct ethnic groups; population estimated at 293 million in 1991. A 1990 estimate: Russians 50.78%, Ukrainians 15.45%, Uzbeks 5.84%. [source: USSR] Point for the writer: Russians were only just a majority by the end.
- Ethnic Russians were overrepresented in the leadership though the institutions were formally ethnically neutral; there were non-Russian leaders, Stalin (a Georgian) among them. [source: USSR]
- Stalin was born Ioseb Besarionis dze Jughashvili in Gori, Georgia (then Tiflis Governorate, Russian Empire), 18 December 1878; died 5 March 1953. [source: STA]
- All fifteen republics became independent states in 1991; all but the three Baltic states joined the CIS. [source: USSR]

## 1. 1917: two revolutions
- Calendar caveat: Russia used the Julian (Old Style, O.S.) calendar until 1918. The February Revolution began on 23 February O.S. = 8 March N.S. 1917, in Petrograd, with protests against food rationing joined by women marking International Women's Day; the October Revolution began on 25 October O.S. = 7 November N.S. 1917. [source: FEB; OCT]
- Nicholas II, stranded on his train at Pskov, abdicated on 2 March O.S. 1917, ending Romanov rule; Grand Duke Michael declined the crown the next day unless a Constituent Assembly chose him. [source: FEB]
- "Dual power": the self-proclaimed Provisional Government (from members of the old Duma) shared power with the Petrograd Soviet of workers' and soldiers' deputies. [source: FEB]
- The Bolsheviks won support with "peace, land, and bread" and "All power to the Soviets"; General Kornilov's failed coup (September 1917) boosted them. [source: RR; RCW]
- The Winter Palace, seat of the Provisional Government, was captured in the night of 25–26 October O.S.; the initial assault on Petrograd was largely without casualties. [source: OCT; RCW]
- Constituent Assembly elections (November 1917): Bolsheviks won 175 of 715 seats; the Socialist Revolutionaries 370. The Assembly met for one day in January 1918, rejected the Soviet decrees on peace and land, and was dissolved. [source: OCT; LEN]
- The Cheka (secret police) was created by Lenin's decree in December 1917. [source: OCT; RR]
- Treaty of Brest-Litovsk with Germany, March 1918. Finland, Lithuania (December 1917), Latvia and Ukraine (January 1918) and others declared independence as the empire fell apart. [source: RR; LEN]

## 2. Civil War, Red Terror, War Communism (1917/18–1921/22)
- Casualties: an estimated 7 to 12 million, mostly civilians (Mawdsley); some put deaths at up to 10 million. Andrea Graziosi: about 16 million excess deaths 1914–1922 (4–5 million military), the great majority from starvation, typhus, epidemics, Spanish flu and the 1921–22 famine; the victims of Red and White terror "a few hundred thousand". About 3 million died of typhus during the war. One to two million "White émigrés" fled. [source: RCW]
- The war's main fighting ended 1920 (evacuation of Crimea, November 1920); Vladivostok captured October 1922. [source: RCW]
- Red Terror, launched after the 30 August 1918 assassination of Petrograd Cheka chief Moisei Uritsky and the same-day attempt on Lenin by the SR Fanny Kaplan. Mainstream estimates of deaths range from 50,000 to 600,000 (RT infobox); RCW: estimates from about 50,000 to 140,000 or 200,000 executions, "most estimations" about 100,000 (Lincoln). Present as a wide, contested range. [source: RT; RCW]
- White Terror: about 300,000 killed (Erlikhman). About 100,000 Jews were killed in pogroms in Ukraine (Kenez). [source: RCW]
- War Communism (from June 1918): nationalisation of industry, a state grain monopoly and forced requisitioning (*prodrazvyorstka*). By 1921 heavy industry output was about 20% of 1913; cultivated land 62% of pre-war area; the harvest about 37% of normal. [source: WC; RCW]
- Famine of 1921–22 (Volga and Ural regions, including Tatarstan): about 5 million dead (RCW elsewhere cites a range of 3–10 million). Herbert Hoover's American Relief Administration and Fridtjof Nansen's relief effort fed over 10 million people a day at the peak; Nansen won the 1922 Nobel Peace Prize partly for this. [source: FAM21; RCW]
- Peasant war: the Tambov Rebellion (1920–21, led by the SR Alexander Antonov; suppressed by Tukhachevsky) — around 100,000 arrested and 15,000 killed. Kronstadt sailors rose for sixteen days in March 1921, demanding freely elected soviets, free speech and free trade; crushed. [source: TAM; KRON]
- Central Asia: the Basmachi resistance grew out of the 1916 revolt against conscription; the Bolsheviks stormed Kokand (seat of the Turkestan autonomous government) in February 1918, massacring up to 25,000 people. Enver Pasha joined after November 1921 and died in August 1922; resistance declined in the mid-1920s after Red Army campaigns and concessions on economic and Islamic practice. [source: BAS]
- Poland: the Polish–Soviet War ended with the Peace of Riga (March 1921), dividing Belarusian and Ukrainian lands between Poland and Soviet Russia. [source: RCW]

## 3. NEP, the founding of the Union, korenizatsiia (1921–1928)
- NEP: adopted at the 10th Party Congress; decree of 21 March 1921 replaced grain requisitioning with a tax in kind (*prodnalog*), letting peasants trade surplus. Lenin proposed it as a temporary expedient. Private traders were "NEPmen". Abandoned with Stalin's "Great Break" in 1928 and phased out 1928–31. [source: NEP]
- Treaty and Declaration on the Creation of the USSR approved 30 December 1922 by delegations of the Russian SFSR, Ukrainian SSR, Byelorussian SSR and the Transcaucasian SFSR (Georgia, Armenia, Azerbaijan). By 1940 the Union had 16 republics (15 after the Karelo-Finnish SSR was downgraded in 1956 — the 1956 detail NOT VERIFIED here; say "fifteen by the end"). [source: TRE; USSR]
- Georgian Affair: in his 30 December 1922 notes on the "nationalities issue", Lenin accused Dzerzhinsky, Ordzhonikidze and Stalin of "Great Russian chauvinism" over their treatment of the Georgian Bolsheviks. [source: LT; LEN]
- Lenin's "Testament" (dictated December 1922–January 1923) warned about Stalin's power and, in a postscript, recommended his removal as General Secretary (quotation below). Lenin died 21 January 1924. [source: LT; LEN]
- *Korenizatsiia* ("indigenisation" / "nativisation", from *korennoi narod*, "native people"): formalised April 1923 (12th Party Congress). It meant schooling and administration in the republic's language; promotion of the titular nationality into local government, bureaucracy and *nomenklatura*; national soviets and national districts for minorities; creating alphabets so national languages could be taught. It aimed to undo Russian domination where Russians were not a majority, and included criticism of Russian culture and alphabet as tsarist Russification. At the 1923 congress Stalin named two dangers: "Great Power chauvinism" and "local nationalism". [source: KOR]
- Terry Martin (Harvard), *The Affirmative Action Empire* (2001): the Soviet state created national territories, dozens of official national languages and "the world's first mass 'affirmative action' programs"; in the 1930s Stalin, responding to a perceived threat to Soviet unity, re-established the Russians as the leading nationality and deported "enemy nations". Chapters include "Linguistic Ukrainization, 1923–1932", "The National Interpretation of the 1933 Famine", "Ethnic Cleansing and Enemy Nations", "The Reemergence of the Russians". [source: WebSearch summaries: https://reviews.history.ac.uk/review/278; https://biblioteca.izt.uam.mx/vufind/Record/musev2_52766/TOC]
- Reversal: between 1933 and 1938 korenizatsiia was not formally repealed but stopped being enforced, and the leaderships of national republics were purged; from 1937 the central press praised Russian language and culture. [source: KOR]
- Ukraine: Mykola Skrypnyk, a Bolshevik who led Ukrainisation as Ukraine's Commissar of Education (1927–33), killed himself on 7 July 1933 when the policy was reversed, rather than recant at a show trial. The 1930 show trial of the "Union for the Liberation of Ukraine" convicted 45 intellectuals. The "Executed Renaissance" — the Ukrainian literary flowering of the 1920s cut down in the 1930s — was named in 1959 by Jerzy Giedroyc for Yuriy Lavrinenko's anthology. Mykola Khvylovy urged Ukrainian literature to turn away from Moscow (literary discussion 1925–28). [source: SKR; ER]

## 4. Stalin's revolution from above: plans, collectivisation, dekulakisation
- First Five-Year Plan, October 1928 – December 1932 (declared complete early); new industrial centres at Magnitogorsk, the Dnieper and Nizhny Novgorod. [source: FYP]
- Magnitogorsk ("magnetic mountain", southern Urals) was built in the 1930s as a one-industry steel town modelled on Gary, Indiana. [source: MAG]
- Stakhanovite movement from 1935 (second plan), modelled on the coal-miner Alexei Stakhanov's "mythic productivity". [source: STAK]
- Collectivisation: November 1929 Central Committee plenum decided on nationwide accelerated collectivisation (kolkhozes and sovkhozes). About 7.5% of peasant households were collectivised in October 1929, 52.7% by February 1930; about 90% of agriculture by 1936. Peasants slaughtered livestock rather than surrender it. [source: COL]
- Stalin's article "Dizzy with Success" (*Pravda*, 2 March 1930) blamed local excesses and briefly slowed the drive (quotation below). [source: COL]
- Women led many riots (*bab'i bunty*), fuelled by rumours that children would be "socialised", women's hair exported, and a "common blanket" imposed (Lynne Viola, 1986). [source: COL]
- Dekulakisation: Stalin announced the "liquidation of the kulaks as a class" on 27 December 1929; Politburo resolution 30 January 1930. Only about 1% of peasants employed labourers. Between 2.1 and 2.3 million people were deported to special settlements 1930–33 (Werth); 20,000–30,000 executed by troikas; Hildermeier puts deaths (excluding the famine) at 530,000–600,000. POP gives conservative figures of 1,679,528–1,803,392 deported (Viola). [source: DEK; POP]

## 5. The famines of 1930–33 (use the Interwar chapter's ranges)
### Ukraine: the Holodomor
- Recent scholarship: **3.5–5 million** victims in Soviet Ukraine, 1932–33 (a 2003 joint UN statement by 25 countries said 7–10 million). Scholars agree the famine was man-made; whether it was genocide is disputed (see Debates). [source: HOL; HGQ]
- Law of Spikelets (decree "On the Protection of Socialist Property"), 7 August 1932: prosecution even for gleaning grain from fields. [source: HOL]
- Blacklists ("black boards"), formalised by a 20 November 1932 decree: blacklisted farms/villages had loans called in, stores closed, food and livestock confiscated, trade cut off. In Ukraine 37 of 392 districts and at least 400 collective farms were blacklisted (over half the farms in Dnipropetrovsk oblast); Cossack villages in the Volga and Kuban (Russia) were also blacklisted. Some blacklisted areas in Kharkiv had death rates over 40%. [source: HOL]
- 22 January 1933: Stalin signed a secret directive restricting peasant travel from Ukraine and the Kuban; Kotkin argues the sealing was to stop famine-borne disease, others read it as deliberate. [source: HOL]
- In 2008 the Russian State Duma condemned the Soviet regime "that has neglected the lives of people for the achievement of economic and political goals". Since 2006 Ukraine and 33 other UN member states, the European Parliament and 35 of 50 US states have recognised the Holodomor as genocide. (Counts move; say "dozens of states".) The capitalised term *Holodomor* (death by hunger) became standard around 2003. [source: HOL]
- Reporting and denial: Welsh journalist Gareth Jones reported the famine in the West under his own name in March 1933 (press release in Berlin, 29 March 1933); *The New York Times*' Walter Duranty echoed Soviet denials. [source: GJ; HDEN; HOL]
### Kazakhstan: the *Asharshylyq*
- 1930–33 (began late 1930, a year before the worst of the Ukrainian famine): **1.3–2.3 million** died; **38–42% of all Kazakhs** perished, the highest proportion of any group; Kazakhs fell from about 60% to 38% of the republic's population and the republic's population by more than a third. 665,000 to 1.1 million refugees; thousands were shot fleeing to China; survivors fled to China, Afghanistan and elsewhere. [source: KAZ]
- Causes: collectivisation and the forced sedentarisation of nomadic herders; excessive grain and livestock quotas; blacklisting. Party boss Filipp Goloshchekin called for a "Small October" in the steppe. [source: KAZ]
- A Kazakh parliamentary commission chaired by historian Manash Kozybayev called it genocide; others see it as part of the wider Soviet famine driven by industrialisation and collectivisation rather than a targeted national campaign. Sarah Cameron (*The Hungry Steppe*, 2018) ties it to the Soviet project of turning nomads into a modern Soviet nation. [source: KAZ; Cameron book pages below]

## 6. The Gulag
- GULAG established 25 April 1930 (OGPU order); run by OGPU, then NKVD (1934–46), then MVD; formally abolished 25 January 1960. [source: GUL]
- About 14 million passed through the camps and 4 million through the colonies, 1930–53 (consistent with the overview's "some 18 million"). Deaths: "tentative consensus" roughly 1.5–1.7 million (lead says about 1.6 million) died in detention or soon after release; disputed estimates run from over 2.7 million to 6 million. [source: GUL]
- The camp population peaked at 1.5 million in 1941, fell during the war and reached its maximum by 1953 (camps plus colonies were over 1.6 million in 1939). In the winter of 1941 a quarter of the Gulag population died of starvation; 516,841 prisoners died in camps in 1941–43. [source: GUL]
- Showcase project: the White Sea–Baltic Canal; in 1933 Gorky led 120 writers and artists there, and 36 wrote a propaganda book (1934) that was destroyed in 1937. [source: GUL]
- After Stalin's death a mass amnesty (1953) freed non-political prisoners and those with sentences up to five years. [source: GUL]
- Kengir (Steplag, Kazakhstan) uprising, 1954: after 40 days of prisoner self-rule it was crushed with tanks on 26 June; former prisoners say 500–700 were killed or wounded, officials only a few dozen killed. The camp held about 5,200 political prisoners with a women's section. [source: KEN]

## 7. The Great Terror (1936–38)
- Also "the Year of '37" and *Yezhovshchina* (after NKVD chief Nikolai Yezhov); peak September 1936 – August 1938. Followed the murder of Sergei Kirov (1934, by Leonid Nikolaev; exact day not checked; whether Stalin arranged it is debated). Three Moscow show trials 1936–38 (Zinoviev, Kamenev, Bukharin); the Red Army high command purged, including Marshal Tukhachevsky. Stalin turned on the NKVD in 1938; Yagoda and Yezhov were executed. [source: GP]
- NKVD Order No. 00447 (30 July 1937; signed by Yezhov, approved by the Politburo), "on the operation to repress former kulaks, criminals and other anti-Soviet elements": regional troikas; Category I (shooting) and Category II (Gulag); quotas per region (e.g. Byelorussian SSR 2,000 Cat. I and 10,000 Cat. II), later exceeded by orders of magnitude. The "kulak operation" arrested 669,929 and executed 376,202 — over half of all known executions. [source: O447; GP]
- National operations: the Polish Operation (143,810 arrests, 111,091 executions; Snyder: at least 85,000 ethnic Poles) was the model for operations against Finns, Latvians, Estonians, Bulgarians, Afghans, Iranians, Greeks and Chinese; second only to the kulak operation in victims. [source: GP]
- Official figure of verifiable executions 1937–38: **681,692**. Total deaths estimated at 700,000–1.2 million (Ellman's 950,000–1.2 million includes deaths in detention and soon after release). [source: GP]

## 8. Deportations of peoples
- Total: internal forced migrations affected at least 6 million people (Werth, Polian); Werth puts overall deaths at about 1–1.5 million; Pohl ~390,000 deaths in kulak resettlement and up to 400,000 among those deported to forced settlements in the 1940s. [source: POP]
- **Koreans, 1937**: nearly 172,000 Soviet Koreans moved from the Far East to Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, on the pretext of stopping "the infiltration of Japanese espionage"; decree of 21 August 1937; first train 1 September 1937; 16,500–50,000 died. [source: KORD]
- **Volga Germans, 1941**: decree of 28 August 1941; the whole population of the Volga German ASSR (and Saratov and Stalingrad oblasts) deported from 3 September to Siberia and Kazakhstan; the autonomous republic abolished; able-bodied men 15–55 and later women 16–45 forced into the "labour army" (*Trudarmee*). 28 August is a day of mourning for Russian Germans. (No reliable total in the source.) [source: VG]
- **Kalmyks, 1943**: Operation Ulusy, 28–31 December 1943: 93,139 Kalmyks (26,359 families) sent in cattle wagons to Siberia; ~1,400 died in transit; >13,000 dead or missing within two years (83,688 registered in early 1945); 16,000+ deaths overall, a 17% mortality rate. Returned 1957. [source: KAL]
- **Chechens and Ingush, 1944** (*Aardakh*, "the Exodus"; Operation Lentil): 23 February 1944, ordered by Beria with Stalin's approval; 496,000 deported to Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan; at least a quarter died (archives: over 100,000 deaths in round-up, transit and early exile; Chechen sources claim 400,000). Return from 1957. The European Parliament called it genocide in 2004. [source: CHE]
- **Crimean Tatars, 1944** (*Sürgünlik*, "exile"): 18–20 May 1944; at least 191,044 (other sources 423,100) deported in cattle trains mainly to the Uzbek SSR — including Communist Party members and Red Army soldiers; nearly 8,000 died in transit and tens of thousands in exile. On 14 November 1989 the USSR Supreme Soviet declared the deportations a crime. Recognised as genocide by Ukraine and others (Canada 2019). [source: CT]
- Others also deported (Karachays, Balkars, Meskhetian Turks, etc.) — names appear in POP but not checked in detail; mention only generically ("other peoples of the Caucasus").
- Mustafa Dzhemilev (Crimean Tatar), deported as a six-month-old in May 1944, grew up in Uzbekistan, became a dissident and made the longest hunger strike in human-rights history: 303 days, survived by force-feeding. [source: DZH]

## 9. The war (brief — Second World War chapter has detail)
- Katyn: on 5 March 1940 Stalin, Molotov, Kaganovich, Voroshilov, Mikoyan and Kalinin signed an order to execute 25,700 Polish "nationalists and counterrevolutionaries"; nearly 22,000 (21,857) Polish officers, police, border guards and intelligentsia were shot in April–May 1940. Soviet responsibility was confirmed by investigations from 1990. [source: KAT]
- Stalingrad: most commonly dated from 23 August 1942 (infobox 17 July) to 2 February 1943; total dead 1.1 to 3 million+. [source: STG]
- War dead: use the WWII chapter's "about 27 million". [source: overlap]
- 1946–47 famine (drought, high prices): from several hundred thousand to 2 million dead; Ukraine ~300,000, Moldavia ~100,000. [source: FAM46]

## 10. Postwar Stalinism (1945–53)
- Zhdanov Doctrine (1946): Central Committee resolution against the journals *Zvezda* and *Leningrad* for publishing Anna Akhmatova and Mikhail Zoshchenko. [source: ZHD]
- Destruction of the Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee and the campaign against "rootless cosmopolitans" (late 1940s). Doctors' Plot: on 13 January 1953 nine eminent Moscow doctors, mostly Jewish, were accused of plotting to poison Soviet leaders; after Stalin's death (5 March 1953) Beria's ministry exonerated them on 31 March; *Pravda* announced it 6 April. [source: DOC]

## 11. The satellite empire
- Warsaw Pact: signed May 1955 by the USSR and seven Eastern Bloc states. Albania de facto expelled 1961 and formally left 1968; Romania by the early 1960s acted as an "independent" non-Soviet member. [source: WP]
- **East Germany, 16–17 June 1953**: began with East Berlin construction workers striking against raised work quotas (16 June); next day over one million people in about 700 localities; put down with Soviet forces; 55–125+ killed. Before it, Ulbricht's "accelerated construction of socialism" (July 1952) drove mass flight (*Republikflucht*). West Germany kept 17 June as a holiday until 1990. [source: EG53]
- **Hungary, 23 October – 4/10 November 1956**: students in Budapest began it; Imre Nagy's government disbanded the ÁVH (28 October) and declared withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact (1 November); Soviet forces attacked 4 November, fighting ended 10 November. About 2,500 Hungarians killed and 20,000 wounded (lead says 2,000) and ~700 Soviet soldiers; 200,000 Hungarians fled, mostly to Austria. Nagy was executed after a secret trial in June 1958. [source: HUN]
- **Czechoslovakia, 1968**: the Prague Spring began 5 January 1968 when Alexander Dubček became party First Secretary; on 20–21 August 1968 about 250,000 troops of the USSR, Poland, Bulgaria and Hungary (rising to about 500,000; "Operation Danube") invaded; Romania and Albania refused; East German troops were held back. 137 Czechoslovaks were killed. [source: CZ68; PS]
- Brezhnev Doctrine: first set out in Sergei Kovalev's *Pravda* article "Sovereignty and the International Obligations of Socialist Countries" (26 September 1968); superseded in 1989 by the "Sinatra Doctrine". [source: BD]

## 12. Khrushchev: Secret Speech, Thaw, limits
- Secret Speech ("On the Cult of Personality and Its Consequences"): delivered after midnight on 25 February 1956 to a closed session of the 20th Party Congress; about four hours; no stenographic record; denounced Stalin's personality cult and repressions and cited Lenin's Testament. Copies were mailed around the USSR from 5 March; a Polish translation reached the West; *The New York Times* printed the text in June 1956; not published in the USSR until 1989. Pro-Stalin demonstrations in Tbilisi, 4–10 March 1956. [source: SS; KHR]
- The Thaw: named after Ilya Ehrenburg's 1954 novel *The Thaw*; Stalin's body removed from the Lenin Mausoleum (1961); Brezhnev ended the Thaw. [source: THAW]
- Solzhenitsyn's *One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich*, published in *Novy Mir* in November 1962 with editor Aleksandr Tvardovsky's support and Khrushchev's approval — the first published account of the camps. [source: IVAN; SOL]
- Deported peoples (Chechens, Ingush, Kalmyks, etc.) allowed home in 1957; Crimean Tatars and Volga Germans were not. [source: CHE; KAL; CT]
- Virgin Lands (from 1954): plan to plough 13 million hectares by 1956 in western Siberia and northern Kazakhstan; 300,000 Komsomol volunteers in summer 1954; changed Kazakhstan's demography; the Kazakh party leaders Shayakhmetov and Afonov opposed it because the land was Kazakh pasture; caused severe soil erosion. [source: VL]
- Novocherkassk, 2 June 1962: after meat prices rose 30% and butter 25% (from 1 June), troops fired on striking workers; officially 23–24 dead, other sources 26 killed and 87 badly wounded; over 100 convicted, seven executed. [source: NOV]
- Khrushchev on his October 1964 removal: "...Could anyone have dreamed of telling Stalin that he didn't suit us anymore and suggesting he retire? ... The fear is gone, and we can talk as equals. That's my contribution." (Taubman). [source: KHR]

## 13. Science, space and superpower (brief; Cold War chapter has arms race)
- Sputnik 1 launched 4 October 1957; transmitted for three weeks; triggered the American "Sputnik crisis" and the space race. [source: SPU]
- Yuri Gagarin (9 March 1934 – 27 March 1968): first person in space, Vostok 1, 12 April 1961, one orbit in 108 minutes, from Baikonur (in Kazakhstan). [source: GAG]
- Andrei Sakharov played a key role in the first Soviet megaton hydrogen bomb ("Sakharov's Third Idea") before becoming a dissident. [source: SAK]

## 14. Brezhnev, stagnation and Central Asia
- "Era of Stagnation" (*zastoi*): a term coined by Gorbachev for Brezhnev's rule (1964–82); began with growth, but industrial growth slowed in the 1970s as heavy and arms industry were prioritised over consumer goods. Gorbachev called it "the lowest stage of socialism". [source: STAG]
- Cotton ("white gold"): the Amu Darya and Syr Darya were diverted to irrigate Central Asian cotton; by 1988 Uzbekistan was the world's largest cotton exporter; heavy agrochemical use (including dioxins). [source: ARAL]
- Aral Sea: once the world's fourth-largest lake (68,000 km²), it began shrinking in the 1960s; by 2007 it was 10% of its original size in four lakes; the eastern basin of the South Aral dried completely by August 2014; fishing industry devastated (Karakalpakstan, Kyzylorda). Ban Ki-moon (2010): "one of the planet's worst environmental disasters". Kazakhstan's Kokaral dam (2005) has partly revived the North Aral. [source: ARAL]
- Uzbek cotton scandal: Sharof Rashidov (First Secretary from 15 March 1959) received about four billion roubles for non-existent cotton; Andropov's anti-corruption drive (1983) and the Gdlyan–Ivanov investigation followed. [source: UZC] (Many Uzbeks saw the "cotton affair" as an anti-Uzbek campaign — NOT VERIFIED here.)

## 15. Dissidents
- Dissidents faced prosecution, exile abroad with loss of citizenship, the psychiatric hospital (*psikhushka*) or the labour camp. The 1975 Helsinki Accords inspired Helsinki monitoring groups; 50 members of Soviet Helsinki Groups were imprisoned. [source: DIS]
- Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn: arrested by SMERSH in February 1945 for criticising Stalin in letters; eight years in camps (incl. Ekibastuz, Kazakhstan) then exile; Nobel Prize in Literature 1970 "for the ethical force with which he has pursued the indispensable traditions of Russian literature"; *The Gulag Archipelago* first published in Russian by YMCA-Press, Paris, 1973 (after the KGB seized a copy; the typist who had hidden it was found hanged 3 August 1973); stripped of citizenship and flown to West Germany 1974; Vermont from 1976; *Archipelago* published in *Novy Mir* 1989; returned 27 May 1994. [source: SOL; GA]
- Andrei Sakharov (21 May 1921 – 14 December 1989): Nobel Peace Prize 1975; arrested 22 January 1980 after protesting the Afghan invasion and exiled to Gorky (closed to foreigners); his wife Yelena Bonner sentenced to exile there in 1984; Gorbachev phoned him on 19 December 1986 to say they could return. [source: SAK]
- Vasily Grossman's *Life and Fate* (1959) — the KGB raided his flat; *Everything Flows* was also censored. [source: GROS]
- Non-Russian dissent: Crimean Tatar return movement (Dzhemilev); Ukrainian and Baltic Helsinki activity (general only; specifics NOT VERIFIED).

## 16. Afghanistan, 1979–89
- Soviet troops entered in December 1979; Operation Storm-333 assaulted Tajbeg Palace and killed Hafizullah Amin. Withdrawal completed 15 February 1989. [source: AFG]
- Soviet dead: 14,453 (9,511 killed in combat). Afghan dead: about 1–3 million (of 13.5 million in 1979); millions fled, mostly to Pakistan and Iran. Scorched-earth tactics, landmines, destruction of irrigation. [source: AFG]

## 17. Chernobyl, 26 April 1986
- Reactor 4 exploded near Pripyat, Ukrainian SSR. Two killed by the explosion, 28 of 134 workers with acute radiation sickness died within three months. 10 km zone after 36 hours (~49,000 evacuated), later 30 km (~68,000 more). Radiation alarms at Sweden's Forsmark plant on 28 April; the government acknowledged the disaster publicly only two days after the explosion. About 6,000 childhood thyroid cancers by 2005 (15 fatal). Long-term deaths: up to 4,000 in Ukraine, Belarus and Russia (UN) to 16,000 across Europe. One of two INES level-7 accidents (with Fukushima). [source: CHER]

## 18. Gorbachev and the end (brief)
- Gorbachev (2 March 1931 – 30 August 2022) elected general secretary 11 March 1985; glasnost ("openness") and perestroika ("restructuring"); the party's monopoly (Article 6) removed 14 March 1990; elected (unopposed) first and only president of the USSR; Nobel Peace Prize 1990. [source: GOR; GLAS]
- National revolts from the periphery: Jeltoqsan, Alma-Ata, 16–19 December 1986, after Kazakh First Secretary Kunaev was replaced by the Russian Gennady Kolbin (officially two deaths; Mukhtar Shakhanov claimed a KGB officer testified to 168; ~5,000 arrested). Nagorno-Karabakh regional soviet voted to join Armenia 20 February 1988. Tbilisi, 9 April 1989: soldiers killed 21 (mostly women) at a pro-independence demonstration. Baltic Way, 23 August 1989 (50th anniversary of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact): about two million people formed a 675 km chain across Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania. Black January, Baku, 19–20 January 1990: Azerbaijani official figure 147 civilians killed. Lithuania first declared independence, March 1990. [source: JEL; DISS; TBI; BW; BJ]
- August 1991 coup failed; 8 December 1991 Belovezha Accords (Russia, Belarus, Ukraine) declared the USSR at an end and founded the CIS; Gorbachev resigned 25 December; the USSR formally dissolved 26 December 1991. [source: DISS]
- Legacy: Putin in 2005 called the dissolution "the greatest geopolitical catastrophe of the 20th century". Memorial (founded during the Soviet collapse to document Stalinist crimes) was shut down by Russian courts in December 2021 and shared the 2022 Nobel Peace Prize. [source: DISS; MEM]

## Debates
- **Totalitarian vs revisionist schools.** Cold War Sovietology was dominated by the "totalitarian model" (Carl Friedrich, 1953/1956: official ideology, control of weapons and media, terror, single mass party "usually under a single leader"; Hannah Arendt). Traditionalists such as Richard Pipes (*The Russian Revolution*, 1990; head of the CIA's 1976 "Team B"; Reagan NSC 1981–82) and Robert Conquest (*The Great Terror*, 1968; *The Harvest of Sorrow*, 1986) stressed ideology, Lenin–Stalin continuity and Stalin's personal will; Conquest estimated some 20 million deaths from Stalin's purges. From the 1960s–70s "revisionists" — Sheila Fitzpatrick ("history from below"; she "almost singlehandedly created the field of Soviet social history" — Manning) and J. Arch Getty (*Origins of the Great Purges*, 1985: a chaotic system, institutions escaping the centre, Stalin reacting ad hoc) — stressed society, social mobility and the popular side of 1917, and a break between Lenin and Stalin. Traditionalists called revisionists apologists who downplayed terror; Peter Kenez said Pipes wrote Soviet history "as a prosecutor"; after 1991 Pipes said revisionist work was "as unreadable as [it was] irrelevant". Fitzpatrick and Geyer (*Beyond Totalitarianism*, 2009) question the term itself. [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totalitarianism; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Arch_Getty; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheila_Fitzpatrick; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Pipes; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Conquest]
- **The archival revolution after 1991.** Opened archives gave hard numbers: 681,692 recorded executions in 1937–38; Gulag throughput and deaths (~1.5–1.7 million); NKVD operational orders such as 00447. Conquest claimed vindication (the "I told you so" title anecdote is "famously said" — use as anecdote only); Getty, Rittersporn and Zemskov (*American Historical Review*, October 1993) and Wheatcroft argued the archives did not support Conquest's higher totals. Fitzpatrick notes it was revisionist "archive rats" who mined the new archives most. Result: both the scale (mass, centrally ordered, quota-driven killing) and the lower-than-Conquest totals are now accepted; the overview chapter already says the archives "ended the older Western debate about scale". [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Conquest; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Purge; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulag; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Arch_Getty]
- **Kotkin's *Magnetic Mountain* (1995).** Using Magnitogorsk, Kotkin treats Stalinism as a "civilization" — a new way of life defined against capitalism — and shows how ordinary people learned to "speak Bolshevik", adopting the identities the regime offered; beyond both "totalitarian" and "revisionist" (often called "post-revisionist"). Anna Krylova (*Contemporary European History*, 2014) criticises the framework for leaving Soviet subjects only a "Bolshevik" language. ("Post-revisionist" label from general usage — keep it hedged.) [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Kotkin; WebSearch summaries: https://sites.bu.edu/revolutionaryrussia/files/2013/09/HI-446-Magnetic-Mountain.pdf; https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/6b7e028e-1696-4825-81c5-f9ace53f7fa3/content]
- **Empire or not?** The USSR was formally a federation of national republics that promoted (then curbed) national cultures. Terry Martin's "affirmative action empire" captures the paradox: a state that built nations while ruling them. Francine Hirsch's *Empire of Nations* (2005) shows ethnographers classifying peoples to make the Union. Mark Beissinger ("Soviet Empire as 'Family Resemblance'", *Slavic Review* 65:2, 2006) treats "empire" as a family-resemblance concept, arguing the line between multinational empire and multinational state is blurry and that empire is partly a matter of how subjects come to see authority; Martin's 2002 essay "The Soviet Union as Empire: Salvaging a Dubious Analytical Category" is the counterpoint usually cited. Plokhy argues the Union ended because Ukraine and Russia left it. Non-Russian experiences — famine, deportation, Russification from the late 1930s, Baltic annexation, Central Asian cotton — are the strongest "empire" evidence; the late-Soviet promotion of national elites (Kunaev, Rashidov) the strongest counter-evidence. [source: WebSearch summary: https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/soviet-empire-as-family-resemblance/; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francine_Hirsch; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serhii_Plokhy; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korenizatsiia]
- **Was the Holodomor genocide?** Consensus that the famine was man-made. For genocide: Raphael Lemkin (1953), James Mace, Norman Naimark, Stanislav Kulchytsky (famine turned into an instrument against Ukrainians from late 1932); Snyder thinks it meets the Convention's criteria but prefers "mass killing"; Applebaum sees it as planned to undermine Ukrainian identity and calls genocide a "proxy debate" about Ukraine's right to exist. Against or hedged: R. W. Davies and Stephen Wheatcroft, Kotkin, Getty — no intent to kill Ukrainians as such, but Stalinist policy and incompetence; Michael Ellman: a crime against humanity, not genocide; Conquest (2004) moved toward "consciously abetting it". Political: recognised as genocide by Ukraine, the European Parliament and dozens of states; Russia's Duma (2008) condemned the regime but rejects the genocide label (latter part implicit). The Kazakh famine has a parallel, less internationalised debate (Kozybayev commission vs Cameron and others). [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holodomor; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holodomor_genocide_question; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakh_famine_of_1930%E2%80%931933]
- **Continuity or rupture (Lenin→Stalin)** — already flagged as unresolved in the overview; Lenin's Testament and the Georgian Affair are the classic evidence for rupture; the Red Terror and the Cheka for continuity. [source: overview chapter; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenin%27s_Testament; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Terror]
