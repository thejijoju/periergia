## 1. The League of Nations and collective security
- Founded 10 January 1920 by the Paris Peace Conference; ceased operations 18 April 1946 (final session in Geneva, delegates of 34 nations), components passing to the UN. Official languages French and English. [LON]
- Structure: Assembly (all members), Council, permanent Secretariat in Geneva; two "wings", the Permanent Court of International Justice and the International Labour Organization (ILO, created 1919 under Part XIII of Versailles). Council began with four permanent members: UK, France, Italy, Japan. [LON]
- The US never joined: Senate Republicans under Henry Cabot Lodge wanted reservations (only Congress could take the US to war); Wilson won the Nobel Peace Prize (October 1919). [LON]
- Membership: 42 founding members (November 1920); peak of 58 members from 28 September 1934 to 23 February 1935; 63 countries in all, never simultaneously. Brazil was the first founding member to withdraw (June 1926); Germany admitted 8 September 1926; Japan and Germany left in 1933, Italy 1937, Spain 1939; USSR joined 18 September 1934 and was expelled 14 December 1939 for invading Finland. [LON; MEM]
- **Non-European members** (MEM table): founding or 1920 members included India (British Raj — not a self-governing state), China (16 July 1920), Japan (10 January 1920), Siam (10 January 1920) and Liberia (30 June 1920). Later: Abyssinia/Ethiopia 28 September 1923; Turkey 18 July 1932; Iraq 3 October 1932 (first mandate to become independent); Afghanistan 27 September 1934; Egypt 26 May 1937 (the last state ever to join). Argentina walked out in December 1920 after its resolution to admit all sovereign states failed, resuming participation in September 1933. [MEM; LON]
- Ethiopia's admission (1923) was secured by Ras Tafari (later Haile Selassie) promising to eradicate slavery. [HS]
- Achievements in the 1920s: Åland Islands (1921, to Finland), Upper Silesia partition, Albania's borders, Mosul (to Iraq, 1926), the Greek–Bulgarian border incident of 1925; failures/partial: Corfu 1923 (Mussolini occupied the island 31 August 1923; the matter was taken to the Conference of Ambassadors at Mussolini's insistence), Vilnius. [LON]
- Humanitarian work: Nansen's Commission for Refugees (27 June 1921), which helped 425,000 POWs home within two years and created the Nansen passport for stateless people; Slavery Commission (1926 Slavery Convention); opium control; Tanganyika railway death rate cut from 55% to 4%; Committee on the legal status of women. [LON]
- Liberia: after accusations of forced labour (including on the Firestone rubber plantation), a commission appointed jointly by the League, the US and Liberia reported in 1930 that slavery and forced labour existed and implicated officials; President Charles D. B. King and his vice-president resigned. [LON]
- Geneva Protocol of 1925 banned poison gas in war (LON); Italy later used gas in Ethiopia in violation of it (IEW).
- Kellogg–Briand Pact (Pact of Paris), signed 27 August 1928 in Paris; renounced war "as an instrument of national policy"; in force 24 July 1929. Original signatories included India, the Irish Free State, Japan, South Africa, the US. [KBP]
- Scholarly consensus (LON): although the League failed in its main goal, it extended the rule of law, gave a voice to smaller nations, fostered financial stabilisation in Central Europe in the 1920s, raised awareness of epidemics, slavery, child labour, "colonial tyranny" and refugees, and put colonial powers "under international observation" via mandates. [LON]
- Stanley Baldwin to the Commons, 23 June 1936: collective security "failed ultimately because of the reluctance of nearly all the nations in Europe to proceed to what I might call military sanctions"; see Quotations. [LON]

## 2. The mandate system
- Established by Article 22 of the Covenant (signed 28 June 1919, in force 10 January 1920); territories "not yet able to stand by themselves under the strenuous conditions of the modern world"; tutelage "entrusted to advanced nations"; administration as a "sacred trust of civilisation". [MAN]
- A compromise between Jan Smuts (who wanted annexation) and Wilson (trusteeship). [MAN]
- Classes: **A** (ex-Ottoman: Syria, Lebanon, Palestine, Transjordan, Iraq — provisionally recognised as independent nations subject to "administrative advice and assistance"); **B** (ex-German Central/West Africa: British and French Togoland, British and French Cameroon, Ruanda-Urundi, Tanganyika); **C** (South West Africa; Pacific: Japanese South Seas Mandate, New Guinea, Nauru, Western Samoa) to be administered "as integral portions" of the mandatory's territory. [MAN]
- Fourteen mandates among seven mandatory powers: UK, South Africa, France, Belgium, New Zealand, Australia and Japan. Iraq (League member 3 October 1932) was the only one independent before WWII. Mandatories reported annually to the Permanent Mandates Commission; no independence deadlines were set and the PMC lacked executive power. [LON; MAN]
- Susan Pedersen: colonial administration in the mandates did not differ substantially from colonial rule elsewhere; the difference was mainly in how the powers spoke about them. [MAN]
- Pedersen, *The Guardians*: the PMC received petitions (from Syrians, Western Samoans and others); petitions rarely won redress but had other important effects; reviewers note "more than 3,000 petitions" over the commission's life (one secondary source only). [WebSearch: https://www.timeshighereducation.com/book-review-the-guardians-the-league-of-nations-and-the-crisis-of-empire-by-susan-pedersen; https://dissentmagazine.org/online_articles/booked-league-of-nations-susan-pedersen/]

## 3. Resistance in the mandates and North Africa
- Iraq: the 1920 Iraqi Revolt against the proposed British Mandate of Mesopotamia brought Sunnis and Shias together; Faisal (ejected from Syria by the French in July 1920) was made king of Iraq in 1921; RAF Iraq Command (1922) used "air control". Independence and League membership 1932, following a general strike of July 1931 (PAL). [IRQ; PAL]
- Syria: the Great Syrian Revolt, 1925–1927, a general uprising in the State of Syria and Greater Lebanon against French mandate policies; led by Sultan al-Atrash (Druze). [SYR] (Detail on the bombardment of Damascus not checked — see NOT VERIFIED.)
- Palestine: the Great Revolt, 1936–1939, sought independence and an end to British support for Zionism (Jewish immigration, land sales). Arab general strike April–October 1936. Peel Commission (Lord Peel arrived 11 November 1936; report 7 July 1937) recommended partition into a small Jewish state, a residual mandatory area and a larger Arab state linked to Transjordan. The revolt is credited with forcing the White Paper of 1939 (binational state within ten years), which the Permanent Mandates Commission held inconsistent with the mandate. [PAL]
- Casualties (PAL): official British figures — more than 2,000 Arabs killed in combat by army and police, 108 hanged, 961 killed in "gang and terrorist activities"; Walid Khalidi's analysis: 5,032 Arab dead (3,832 killed by the British, 1,200 in intracommunal violence) and 14,760 wounded. By one estimate, "ten percent of the adult male Palestinian Arab population between 20 and 60 was killed, wounded, imprisoned or exiled." [PAL]
- Jewish immigration peaked in 1935. An Arab Women's Congress met in Jerusalem in 1929 (200 participants). [PAL]
- Morocco: the Rif War (1921–1926), an anti-colonial war between Berber tribes of the Rif and Spain, joined by France in 1924. [RIF] (Details — Annual, the Republic of the Rif — not checked here.)

## 4. Reconstruction and the "Roaring Twenties" (brief; Germany is context only)
- Dawes Plan (1924): staggered reparations schedule, a large loan to stabilise the German currency, end of the Ruhr occupation; brief recovery in the late 1920s at the price of heavy reliance on foreign capital; superseded by the Young Plan (1929). [DAW]
- Statute of Westminster, 11 December 1931, embodied the equality of the Dominions set out in the Balfour Declaration of 1926. [SOW]
- "Jazz age": in popular use before 1920; F. Scott Fitzgerald popularised it with *Tales of the Jazz Age* (1922). [JA]
- Harlem Renaissance: African-American cultural movement of the 1920s–30s, then called the "New Negro Movement" after Alain Locke's 1925 anthology *The New Negro*. [HR]
- 1929 opened with good prospects in the US; Wall Street had a nine-year run, the Dow peaking at 381.17 on 3 September 1929. [WSC; GD]

## 5. The Great Depression worldwide
### The crash and its spread
- Black Thursday 24 October 1929 (market lost 11% at the opening bell); Black Monday 28 October (Dow −12.82%); Black Tuesday 29 October (Dow −11.73%; some 16 million shares traded). The Dow did not regain its September 1929 peak until 23 November 1954. [WSC]
- Worldwide GDP fell an estimated 15% (1929–32); US GDP −30%; international trade fell by more than 50%; unemployment in some countries reached 33%; crop prices fell by up to 60%; "areas dependent on primary sector industries suffered the most". [GD]
- US: unemployment 25% by 1933; about one-third of farmers lost their land; 9,000 of 25,000 banks failed. [GD]
- Smoot–Hawley Tariff Act signed by Hoover 17 June 1930, raising tariffs on over 20,000 imported goods; a petition of 1,028 economists (eventually over 1,250) urged a veto; Canada and others retaliated; by September 1929 the administration had protest notes from 23 trading partners. In a 1995 survey two-thirds of US economic historians agreed it at least worsened the Depression. [SH; GD]
- Mid-1931 crisis began with the collapse of Vienna's Creditanstalt (May 1931). Britain left gold in September 1931; Japan and Scandinavia followed in 1931; the US and Italy stayed into 1932–33; the "gold bloc" (France, Poland, Belgium, Switzerland) until 1935–36. The earlier a country left gold, the earlier it recovered (Eichengreen and others). China, on a silver standard, almost avoided the Depression. [GD]
- Bernanke (2002, to Friedman and Schwartz): see Quotations. [GD]

### Latin America
- League of Nations reports named Chile (GD says "the country hardest hit") / Chile, Peru and Bolivia (GDLA) as the worst hit. [GD; GDLA]
- Chile: 80% of government revenue came from copper and nitrate exports; exports fell from US$279m (1929) to US$35m (1932); GDP by 1932 under half of 1929 (GD) — GDLA gives real GDP index 100 → 67, a conflict; say "collapsed". 50,000 nitrate workers unemployed by 1932. [GD; GDLA]
- Peru: exports fell from US$132m (1929) to US$38m (1932); suspended foreign-debt payments March 1931. [GDLA]
- Brazil: coffee exports fell 50% (1929–32); in 1931 the government had thousands of coffee sacks dumped into the sea and bought and burned crops to hold up prices. [GDLA]
- Brazil's Revolution of 1930 replaced Washington Luís with Getúlio Vargas (military coup in Rio, 24 October 1930), ending the São Paulo–Minas "café com leite" oligarchy and beginning the Vargas Era; coffee prices had collapsed after the 1929 crash. [BR30]
- Cuba: sugar was 80–90% of agricultural production; prices fell to 1.47 cents/lb. Mexico was hit less (exports only 12% of GDP vs 30% Chile, 27% Argentina), and recovered through import substitution; Mexican silver benefited from the US Silver Purchase Act of 1934. [GDLA]
- Argentina: Britain's beef quota led to the Roca–Runciman Treaty; economy back to 1929 levels by 1935. Latin American exports to the US fell from $1.2bn (1929) to $335m (1933). The Depression pushed governments towards local industry, protection and welfare institutions (import-substitution industrialisation, e.g. Chile's CORFO, 1939). [GD]

### Africa
- Colonial Africa: falling commodity prices hit agriculture; sisal in Kenya and Tanganyika; colonial budgets and infrastructure projects cut. Belgian Congo: peanut prices fell from 125 to 25 centimes; employment in Katanga fell 70%; the wage labour force shrank by 72,000; Léopoldville's population fell by a third. [GD]
- South Africa: the 1931 Carnegie Commission found nearly one-third of Afrikaners lived as paupers; relief programmes focused on the white population. [GD]
- Nigeria, Women's War (Igbo *Ogu Umunwanyi*), November 1929: sparked 18 November at Oloko by a census dispute (Nwanyeruwa: "Was your widowed mother counted?"), amid fears that direct taxation (imposed on men 1928) would extend to women and the 1929 crash's effect on women's trade; protests against warrant chiefs; "sitting on a man" tactics; 16 Native Courts attacked; infobox: about 10,000 women, 55 killed. AWW: "the first major revolt by women in West Africa". [AWW]
- Gold Coast cocoa hold-up, 1937–38: from November 1937, Gold Coast farmers withheld cocoa and boycotted European firms after a buying pool (eventually 13 firms, 94% of purchases, led by Cadbury and the United Africa Company) set low prices; the Nowell Commission (announced February 1938) reported in 1938; suspension of the buying agreements ended the hold-up. [WebSearch: https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1938/feb/23/west-africa-cocoa-trade-commission; https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1939/feb/01/west-africa-cocoa-marketing; https://nvdatabase.swarthmore.edu/node/4767] (Secondary; keep wording general.)

### Asia
- India: debated how far it was affected; jute and coal hard hit; otherwise "fairly stable". [GD]
- Burma: Saya San (Galon) rebellion, from 22 December 1930 in Tharrawaddy; the Depression had a devastating impact on rice prices (Burma's chief export). By end 1932 over 1,000 rebels killed and 9,000 surrendered or captured; Saya San and 125 others hanged; nearly 1,400 imprisoned or transported. [SAYA]
- Siam: the Depression contributed to the end of absolute monarchy in the revolution of 1932. [GD]
- Vietnam: Depression-era hardship and heavy taxes fed the Nghệ-Tĩnh soviets (§9). [NT]
- China: largely unaffected thanks to the silver standard until the US Silver Purchase Act (1934) drained silver; fiat currency (*fabi*) reform 3 November 1935. [GD]
- Japan: the Shōwa financial crisis of 1927 (bank runs; 37 banks failed, including the Bank of Taiwan, and the Suzuki Shoten) strengthened the big zaibatsu banks. [SFC]
- Japan returned to gold in December 1930 and left in December 1931 (GD table). Economy shrank 8% in 1929–31. Takahashi Korekiyo (1854–1936), finance minister, abandoned gold (December 1931), devalued the yen and ran Bank-of-Japan-financed deficits ("Keynesian" before Keynes); Japan was out of the depression by 1933; industrial production doubled in the 1930s; textiles displaced British textiles in export markets. When he tried to cut military spending (from 1934) he was assassinated in the February 26 incident, 1936. [GD; TAK]
- Rural Japan: the early-1930s farm slump combined collapsing raw-silk prices (US demand), a rice price fall after a bumper 1930 harvest, and Tōhoku crop failures in 1931 and 1934; one summary puts the raw silk price drop 1929–31 at over 50%. Contemporary reports speak of farm families selling daughters (figures unreliable). [WebSearch: https://ch.u-tokyo.ac.jp/ocw-files/lecture_files/eco_04/12/notes/en/CEHJ-12.pdf (University of Tokyo lecture notes); https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/314875/files/21_1.pdf]
- Manchuria was widely promoted as Japan's economic "lifeline" against the Depression, which made the conquest popular. [MCK]
- Japanese government policy encouraged households, especially housewives, to cut consumption. [GD]
- Turkey turned towards statist economic policy. [GD]

### Responses
- New Deal (from 1933): "3 R's" (relief, recovery, reform); Emergency Banking Act; deposit insurance (1933 Banking Act); Second New Deal 1935–38 (WPA, National Labor Relations Act 1935, Social Security); unemployment still above 20% in 1935 and about 15% in 1940; Roosevelt "never completely gave up trying to balance the budget". When FDR took office (4 March 1933) farm income had fallen by over 50% since 1929. New Deal coalition included African Americans. [ND; GD]
- Recovery began in most countries in 1933; rearmament stimulated European economies 1937–39 (UK unemployment down to 1.5 million by 1937). [GD]

## 6. Fascist Italy
- Squadristi (Blackshirts) attacked socialists from 1919, broke strikes (August 1920 Milan general strike), seized towns in the Po Valley 1920–22. [ROR]
- 24 October 1922, Naples rally of 60,000: "Our program is simple: we want to rule Italy." March on Rome: Facta's request for a state of siege refused by King Victor Emmanuel III; the march numbered fewer than 30,000; Mussolini (who stayed in Milan, then came to Rome) was asked to form a cabinet 29 October and appointed prime minister 30 October 1922; Blackshirts paraded 31 October. Big industrialists (Confindustria) had met him beforehand. Mussolini reached power "legally" under the Statuto Albertino. [ROR]
- Acerbo Law; April 1924 election: the National List won 65% and all its 356 candidates were elected amid violence. Giacomo Matteotti (Unitary Socialist Party secretary) denounced the fraud on 30 May 1924; kidnapped and killed by a squad led by Amerigo Dumini on 10 June 1924; opposition's Aventine Secession (from 27 June). On 3 January 1925 Mussolini told the Chamber he took responsibility for squadrist violence (without naming Matteotti); generally regarded as the start of the open dictatorship. [MAT; BM]
- 1925: the party declared the state "totalitarian" — a term first used pejoratively by liberal opponents. OVRA secret police 1927; strikes outlawed. Mussolini to the Chamber, 26 May 1927: "Everything in the State, nothing outside the State, nothing against the State." *The Doctrine of Fascism* (1932) ghostwritten by Giovanni Gentile under Mussolini's name. [IF; BM]
- Lateran Treaty 1929 (11 February) with the Vatican; Pius XI called Mussolini "the Man of Providence". [BM]
- Corporatism: employer and worker syndicates linked under the state as a "Third Position". [IF]
- Colonial violence: "Pacification" of Libya ordered November 1929 against resistance led by Omar Mukhtar; over half of Cyrenaica's population confined in 15 concentration camps; Angelo Del Boca estimated 40,000–70,000 Libyan dead. IF: deaths of about one-tenth of Libya's population in the fascist era. [BM; IF]
- Racial laws in force 11 November 1938 (*Corriere della Sera* front page). [IF]

## 7. The Soviet Union, 1921–1939 (brief; separate Soviet chapter)
- NEP proposed by Lenin in 1921 as a temporary expedient (private small and medium firms, "NEPmen"); abandoned with Stalin's "Great Break" in 1928, phased out 1928–31. [NEP]
- Holodomor, Soviet Ukraine 1932–33: recent scholarship estimates 3.5–5 million deaths (a 2003 UN statement by 25 countries said 7–10 million). [HOL]
- Kazakh famine (*Asharshylyq*) 1930–33: estimated 1.3–2.3 million died; 38–42% of all Kazakhs perished, the highest proportion of any group; Kazakhs fell from about 60% to 38% of the republic's population; caused by collectivisation and forced sedentarisation of nomads. [KAZ]
- Great Purge 1936–39 (peak September 1936 – August 1938 under Yezhov), after Kirov's assassination (1934); Moscow show trials from August 1936 (Zinoviev, Kamenev; Bukharin later); Red Army high command purged; deaths estimated 700,000 to 1.2 million. [PUR]

## 8. India: Gandhi's campaigns
- Non-cooperation movement 1920–22; suspended by Gandhi after Chauri Chaura (United Provinces), 4 February 1922, where after police fired and killed three, a crowd burned the police post and killed 22 or 23 policemen. 225 tried; 170 initially sentenced to death (9 January 1923). [CHAURI; SALT]
- Bardoli Satyagraha 1928. [SALT]
- At midnight on 31 December 1929 Congress raised the flag at Lahore; declaration of *Purna Swaraj* (complete self-rule) issued 26 January 1930. [SALT]
- Salt tax: 1882 Salt Act gave Britain a salt monopoly; the tax was 8.2% of Raj tax revenue and fell hardest on the poorest. Irwin to London: "At present, the prospect of a salt campaign does not keep me awake at night." *The Statesman*: "It is difficult not to laugh..." [SALT]
- Gandhi's letter of 2 March 1930 to Irwin listed eleven demands. Salt March: 12 March – 6 April 1930, 24 days; Gandhi and 78 satyagrahis from Sabarmati Ashram to Dandi (Gujarat), 385 km. At Dandi on 6 April he made illegal salt. Over 60,000 people arrested by the end of the month (April 1930). Gandhi arrested at midnight 4–5 May 1930. [SALT]
- Women: 1930 civil disobedience was the first time women became mass participants; Sarojini Naidu led the Dharasana Salt Works satyagraha after the arrests of Abbas Tyabji (76) and Kasturba Gandhi; United Press correspondent Webb Miller's report of the beatings appeared in 1,350 newspapers. [SALT]
- Peshawar, 23 April 1930: Khudai Khidmatgar (trained nonviolent volunteers under the Pashtun Muslim leader Ghaffar Khan, said to number 50,000) fired on in the Qissa Khwani Bazaar; an estimated 200–250 killed. [SALT]
- C. Rajagopalachari led a parallel Vedaranyam salt march on the east coast. [SALT]
- *Time* named Gandhi its 1930 Man of the Year. Gandhi–Irwin Pact (early 1931) followed; Second Round Table Conference late 1931. The campaign brought no immediate dominion status or major concessions and attracted little Muslim support; satyagraha ended as official Congress policy in 1934. [SALT]
- Repression was presided over by Ramsay MacDonald's Labour government (Secretary of State Wedgwood Benn). [SALT]

## 9. Egypt, Indonesia, Vietnam
### Egypt after 1919 (1919 itself is in the WWI notes)
- Unilateral Declaration of Egyptian Independence, 28 February 1922: Britain reserved four areas — foreign relations (UDEI wording: "foreign relations, communications, the military, and Sudan"); the reserved points were "only removed" by the 1936 treaty per British archives (UDEI); 1923 constitution. Caroline Elkins: the declaration did not entail sovereignty. [UDEI; EGY]
- Anglo-Egyptian Treaty signed in London 26 August 1936: British troops withdrawn except 10,000 (plus auxiliaries) guarding the Suez Canal zone; 20-year term. Egypt joined the League 26 May 1937. [AE36; MEM]
- Huda Sha'arawi (1879–1947): led elite women in the 1919 protests; on return from the International Woman Suffrage Alliance congress in Rome she removed her veil in public (HUDA places it after her husband's death in 1922 and her return from the Rome congress; exact year not stated — see NOT VERIFIED); founded the Egyptian Feminist Union in 1923. [HUDA]
### Indonesia
- Sukarno founded the PNI (Indonesian National Association, 4 July 1927; renamed Indonesian National Party, May 1928) from his Bandung study club; aim: independence, secular unity across ethnic groups. [PNI; SUK]

### Vietnam

## 13. Ethiopia 1935–36 and the League's failure
- Walwal (Welwel) incident, December 1934, in the Ogaden at an Italian fort "well beyond" the 1928 treaty border. [IEW]
- 3 October 1935: Italian forces under De Bono crossed the Mareb from Eritrea without a declaration of war (with Graziani from Somalia); Adwa taken 6 October. On 14 October De Bono proclaimed the end of slavery in Ethiopia. Ethiopian Christmas Offensive, December 1935. Mussolini authorised gas (Badoglio, 28 December 1935); the air force dropped 330 tons of mustard gas over four months, violating the 1925 Geneva Protocol; Red Cross hospitals attacked. Amba Aradam (February 1936), Maychew and Lake Ashenge (March–April 1936). [IEW; HSS]
- Haile Selassie left Addis Ababa by train for Djibouti 2 May 1936; Badoglio entered Addis Ababa 5 May; 9 May 1936 Mussolini proclaimed the Italian Empire to a crowd of 200,000 and annexed Ethiopia (Victor Emmanuel III as emperor). [IEW]
- Casualties: Ethiopian military ~70,000 killed (infobox); Italian 8,231 killed (3,731 Italians, 4,500 askaris); total Ethiopian deaths 250,000–270,000 (Traverso; Del Boca) — "hundreds of thousands" of civilians died over the occupation per IEW lead. Resistance (*Arbegnoch*) continued until liberation in 1941 (Haile Selassie re-entered Addis Ababa 5 May 1941). [IEW; HSS]
- League sanctions approved 18 November 1935 by 50 states (Italy against; Austria, Hungary, Albania abstained); they did not include oil and did not close the Suez Canal; lifted 4 July 1936. US Neutrality Acts embargo on arms to both sides plus a "moral embargo" on Italy. [LON; HSS]
- Hoare–Laval Pact (December 1935) proposed partition of Ethiopia (APP: conceding two-thirds); leaked; public outcry forced Hoare and Laval to resign. [LON; APP]
- Haile Selassie addressed the Assembly in Geneva on 30 June 1936 (first head of state to do so), in Amharic; Italian journalists jeered (Romanian delegate Titulescu shouted "To the door with the savages!"); he denounced gas attacks. His motion not to recognise the conquest was rejected 23 against, 1 for, 25 abstentions. In 1937 only six nations did not recognise the occupation: China, New Zealand, the USSR, Republican Spain, Mexico and the US. Italy left the League (announced 11 December 1937). *Time* Man of the Year for 1935. He lived in exile at Fairfield House, Bath, 1936–41. [HSS; HS]
- Britain and France avoided harsh sanctions partly fearing to drive Mussolini into Hitler's arms. [LON]

## 14. The Spanish Civil War, 1936–1939
- 17 July 1936 (Morocco) – 1 April 1939 (Franco declared victory). Popular Front narrowly won February 1936 election; conspiracy led by General Mola; Franco named supreme leader 1 October 1936. [SCW]
- At the coup, the Nationalist zone held 11 million of Spain's 25 million people; Nationalists had about 60,000 of the peninsular army plus the 35,000-strong Army of Africa (Moroccan *Regulares* and Legion), airlifted from Tetouan to Seville in German planes. [SCW]
- Foreign intervention: Italy's Corpo Truppe Volontarie (70,000–75,000), Germany's Condor Legion (16,000), despite Germany signing the Non-Intervention agreement in September 1936. USSR the Republic's only major arms source (634–806 aircraft, 331–362 tanks). Mexico refused non-intervention: $2m aid, 20,000 rifles, and sanctuary for some 50,000 Republican refugees. [SCW]
- International Brigades organised by the Comintern: some 32,000 volunteers in all, never more than 18,000 at once; Negrín announced their withdrawal at the League on 21 September 1938; killed estimates 3,615–5,000. Volunteers included Latin Americans (Cubans, Argentines, Mexicans) and, in the Sixth of February Battalion, Moroccans, Algerians, Syrians, Iraqis, Egyptians, Chinese, Japanese, Indians and Filipinos. [IB]
- Guernica, 26 April 1937: bombed by the Condor Legion and Italian Aviazione Legionaria at Franco's behest; Basque government reported 1,654 killed at the time; local historians identified 126, later revised to 153. Picasso's *Guernica*. [GUE]
- Deaths: highly disputed. Violent deaths by category: combat 100,000–700,000; rearguard terror 103,000–235,000; air raids 10,000–15,000. Demographers' excess deaths 1936–39: 346,000 (540,000 for 1936–42). Nationalist executions: Preston minimum 130,000; Beevor ~200,000; Republican zone killings 38,000–70,000 (most common ~50,000). [SCW]
- Women got the vote under the Second Republic in 1931; revoked 1939, restored 1977. [TWS]

## 16. The road to 1939 (Germany only as context)
- Rhineland remilitarised 7 March 1936; Anschluss 12 March 1938. [RHI; ANS]

## Debates
- **Why did the League fail?** Contemporary verdicts blamed great-power self-interest (Samuel Flagg Bemis: failure not because the US stayed out but because great powers applied sanctions only when it suited them, and democracy had collapsed over half the world) and unpreparedness (Mowat). Since archives opened, historians (e.g., Patricia Clavin on economic work; Pedersen on mandates) stress its lasting technical, humanitarian and normative legacy. [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations]
- **Mandates: new order or old empire?** Pedersen: administration in the mandates differed little from ordinary colonial rule; the PMC served neither self-determination nor real accountability, but petitions and publicity gave colonised peoples a stage and constrained the powers' language. Getachew: interwar internationalism institutionalised racial hierarchy; anticolonial thinkers responded with their own "worldmaking". [MAN; reviews cited above; Princeton page]
- **Causes of the Depression:** Monetarists (Friedman and Schwartz: the Fed let the money supply collapse) vs Keynesians (collapse of demand/investment); economists are "almost evenly split"; Eichengreen and others: the gold standard was the transmission mechanism and leaving it predicted recovery; Kindleberger: no hegemon able and willing to stabilise the system ("In 1929, the British couldn't and the United States wouldn't" — Eichengreen's summary). Smoot–Hawley consensus: worsened it. [GD; Kindleberger sources]
- **What was fascism?** Paxton treats fascism by what fascists did, as a process in which movements gained power through alliance with conservative elites (consistent with the overview); Gentile's official doctrine (1932) proclaimed the totalitarian state. Historians contrast Italy's relatively "mild" domestic terror (Knickerbocker 1941) with extreme colonial violence in Libya and Ethiopia. [IF; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anatomy_of_Fascism]
- **Matteotti's murder:** historians debate whether Mussolini ordered it; his 3 January 1925 speech assuming responsibility for squadrist violence is generally seen as the start of the dictatorship. [MAT]
- **Appeasement — orthodox / revisionist / counter-revisionist:**
  - Orthodox: *Guilty Men* by "Cato" (Michael Foot, Frank Owen, Peter Howard; July 1940) attacked fifteen public figures; after 1939 consensus blamed appeasement. [GM; APP]
  - Early dissent: John F. Kennedy, *Why England Slept* (1940 Harvard thesis): Britain and France were unprepared. [APP]
  - A. J. P. Taylor, *The Origins of the Second World War* (1961): Hitler had goals but no blueprint, was an opportunist like earlier German statesmen (Stresemann), and appeasers were rational men "doing their best"; widely attacked on publication; he had not read *Mein Kampf* before writing it (Kathleen Burk). Paul Kennedy and Martin Gilbert: "a choice of evils". [APP; TOR]
  - Counter-revisionist: R. A. C. Parker, *Chamberlain and Appeasement* (1993): appeasement was not chiefly about buying time; Chamberlain rejected a realistic alternative (a firm Anglo-French alliance / "Grand Alliance"), later developed in *Churchill and Appeasement* (2000); critics call the Grand Alliance a mirage since Stalin would not fight for Czechoslovakia. [Spectator reviews 1993, 2000: https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/30th-october-1993/34/deep-searched-with-sourcey-book; https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/22nd-july-2000/32/was-his-war-really-necessary]
  - "Post-revisionist": Frank McDonough — appeasement a viable crisis-management strategy, poorly implemented. (APP calls this both "counter-revisionist" and "post revisionist" — the labels are used inconsistently; describe positions rather than labels.) James P. Levy defends the appeasers against hindsight. [APP]
  - Overy: with Wheatcroft, *The Road to War* (1989) reconstructs each power's concerns without hindsight; in the late-1980s *Past and Present* debate Overy rejected Timothy Mason's thesis that a domestic economic crisis forced Hitler into a "flight into war" in 1939, arguing Hitler was not shown to have been told of the crisis, the regime could repress unrest, and officials believed they could master rearmament's problems. (Overy's specific verdict on British appeasement — e.g., imperial overstretch — was NOT confirmed; see below.) [OWW; Penguin page]
- **Spain as "rehearsal":** Hugh Thomas argued the coup could have been crushed quickly if the government had armed the workers. Death tolls remain politically contested (Preston ≥130,000 Nationalist executions; Beevor ~200,000; Ruiz, Seidman lower/other figures). [SCW]
- **Long March: epic or myth?** Distance (25,000 vs 20,000 *li*) and episodes such as Luding Bridge are disputed (Jung Chang and Jon Halliday, *Mao: The Unknown Story*, 2005, is in LM's bibliography; the specific claims were not checked). [LM]
- **Nanjing decade: "golden" or failed?** The nickname "Golden Decade" vs the record of one-party tutelage, factionalism, secret police (Dai Li) and incomplete rural reform. [NJD]
- **Salt March's results:** no immediate dominion status or major concessions and little Muslim support (SALT), vs Nehru's view that its real importance lay in transforming the "village masses". [SALT]
- **Négritude's founders:** canonically Césaire, Senghor and Damas; recent scholarship restores Paulette and Jane Nardal. [NEG]
